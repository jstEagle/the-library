## 11. The Consulate Burns

Petra smelled it before she heard it — a sweetness in the fog, like caramel left too long in a pan, and beneath that something older and fouler, wet wool and char and the particular stink of paper surrendering to flame.

She was already awake. She had been awake since four, lying in her narrow bed under the eaves of Casa Herrera with the pouch's contents turning over in her mind like stones in a riverbed, when the first bell began to ring. Not the harbor bells, not the church bells marking the hour, but the urgent, arrhythmic clanging of the volunteer brigade on Cerro Alegre, and then another bell answering from below, and then the whistles, dozens of them, shrill against the fog.

She dressed in the dark without thinking about it — the discipline of years compressed into ninety seconds — and was down the stairs before Doña Inés had finished lighting the lamp in the hall.

"The consulate," the landlady said. Her hands were steady on the lamp but her voice was not. "Look at the sky."

There was no sky. There was only the fog, and inside the fog a glow, low and orange, pulsing like something breathing, somewhere down toward the Plaza Sotomayor where the Baltic consulate kept its rooms above a shipping agent's office, two floors of dark wood and tall windows and a flag that had flown for a republic which, as of three weeks ago, no longer existed to claim it.

* * *

They went down together, because there was no stopping anyone in Valparaíso from going down; the whole city moved toward fires the way water moves toward a drain. The streets were already filling — sailors in their shore clothes, women in shawls, children carried half-asleep on hips, the whole population of the cerros pouring downward through the fog with the terrible festive energy of people who have seen many fires and will see many more. A man sold coffee from a cart at the corner as though this were any other morning.

The heat reached them a block away. It pressed against their faces like an open oven door, and the fog around the burning building had turned the color of a wound, glowing from within, and the consulate's tall windows were gone, and through the empty frames they could see the fire standing up inside the rooms, upright and almost dignified, consuming the filing cabinets one by one.

"They won't save it," Tomás Reyes said beside her.

She hadn't heard him arrive. He stood with his hands in his coat pockets, watching the flames with the flat professional attention he gave to everything — ships, weather, other people's faces.

"The walls are adobe," Petra said. "The stone won't burn."

"The stone won't. Everything inside it is already dead." He nodded at the roofline, where the tiles were beginning to shift and slide. "Look at how it burns. Fast, even, all at once. That's not a lamp knocked over near curtains. That's accelerant, poured along the corridors." He said it quietly, without drama, the way he might report the height of a swell. "Someone wanted those papers gone, and wanted them gone thoroughly."

Petra felt the cold move through her body in a slow wave, from the crown of her head downward, as if someone had poured ice water over her hair.

Because she had needed those papers. Three nights ago she had stood across the street from the consulate in the fog, counting the lights in the windows, memorizing the comings and goings of the caretaker staff — a chargé d'affaires who no longer had a country to be charged for, a clerk, a night watchman — planning, in the patient way she planned everything, how she would present herself as a seamstress called to alter a uniform, how she would get ten minutes alone with the archive room, how she would confirm what the pouch had told her: names, dates, regimental numbers, the paper trail of the atrocity her government had ordered and buried. The consulate's records were the second half of the proof. The pouch was testimony; the archive was corroboration. Together they would have been unanswerable.

Now the archive was a column of smoke rising straight up through the fog until it vanished, going wherever dead countries go.

"Are you all right?" Tomás asked.

"I'm cold," she said, which was true, and let him believe it was only the fog.

* * *

By seven the fire was out and the crowd had thinned to the incurious and the professional. Two carabineros stood at the cordon looking bored. The chargé d'affaires — a small, precise man named Kalns whom Petra had seen twice from a distance, once at the customs house and once entering the consulate with a briefcase chained to his wrist — sat on an overturned crate across the street, wrapped in a blanket that someone had brought him, staring at the black window frames of his former embassy with the expression of a man attending his own funeral.

Doña Inés, who had known everyone worth knowing in this port for forty years, marched up to him with a flask of coffee laced with aguardiente and no introduction whatsoever.

"Drink this, señor. You look like a man who has just watched his mother die."

"In a sense," Kalns said, in careful Spanish, "I have." He drank. His hands shook. Then his eyes came up, and they were not the eyes of a broken man; they were quick, and they moved across the crowd behind Doña Inés with a searching, cataloguing attention that made the back of Petra's neck prickle. "You are from the boarding house on the calle de la Merced. Casa Herrera. I know the house. My predecessor lodged there in the old days." He paused. "Several of your residents have been asking after our archives lately."

The fog seemed to close in around Petra's ears.

"Have they?" Doña Inés said comfortably. "Refugees ask after everything. Papers are life to people like us. You should understand that better than anyone this morning."

"Yes," Kalns said. "One understands." But his gaze had found Petra, and rested on her for exactly one second longer than courtesy required — long enough to register her face, her coat, the mended seam at her shoulder — before moving on. "A seamstress, I think. You altered a uniform for our watchman last month. Good work. The man spoke well of you."

"He was kind to say so."

"The watchman," Kalns added, still in that mild, exhausted voice, "has not been seen since last night. He locked up at eleven. By midnight the building was burning. The police are interested in finding him." He rose, folding the blanket with unnecessary neatness. "Everyone is interested in finding someone, these days. It is the great occupation of the port." He inclined his head to Doña Inés. "Thank you for the coffee, señora. When your residents ask after papers again, tell them the papers are ash. All of them. Every page, every file, every record of anything my country ever did or failed to do. Ash." He said the word twice more under his breath as he walked away, like a prayer, or a sentence being served.

* * *

The walk back up the hill took twenty minutes and Petra spent all of them doing arithmetic.

Who knew she had been asking after the archives? Not Kalns himself — she had never approached him directly; that would have been amateur work. She had asked questions sideways, through Emil Kolar, whose job at the Czech legation gave him reason to attend consular functions, through a Chilean shipping clerk who owed Tomás favors, through the harmless, endless gossip of a port full of men waiting for ships that wouldn't sail. Each thread had seemed thin enough. Each thread had seemed safe.

But threads run both ways. If she could trace a line from herself to the consulate, someone else could trace it backward. And the fire itself — Tomás had said accelerant, poured along the corridors, thorough, deliberate. Someone had burned the archive on purpose. Someone who wanted the same documents destroyed that she wanted confirmed. Which meant whoever it was knew what the documents were. Which meant whoever it was knew about the pouch, or the mission, or her.

Or — and this thought arrived with its own separate, colder dread — the fire had nothing to do with her at all. Victors' historians did not travel with matches, but they traveled with influence, and Herr Vogel had been in the city nine days now, courteous and patient, buying lunches for minor officials and requesting, always requesting, access to the records of the smaller consulates. Perhaps the fire was simply housekeeping. Empires tidied up. Dead nations' paperwork was an embarrassment to the living arrangement of Europe, and there were men whose entire careers consisted of making embarrassments disappear.

Either way, the result was identical: the corroboration was gone. What remained was the pouch, sewn into nothing now, decoded and hidden in the lining of a dress pattern in the bottom of her trunk, and her own memory, and her own word. Testimony without evidence. A voice crying in a port at the end of the world.

And someone, possibly, watching her.

* * *

Casa Herrera at midmorning had the atmosphere of a struck bell that hadn't stopped humming.

Emil Kolar was in the dining room when she came in, sitting at the long table with a cup of tea he wasn't drinking, his long clerk's fingers laced together so tightly the knuckles had gone white. He looked up when she entered and looked away again immediately, which was unlike him; Emil's habit was to meet every glance with an apologetic half-smile, the smile of a man perpetually asking pardon for taking up space.

"You've heard," he said.

"The whole city has heard."

"They're saying—" He stopped. Started again. "The police were here. While you were out. Two of them. Asking Doña Inés whether any of the residents had connections to the consulate."

"What did she tell them?"

"That we are a house of nobodies. Sailors, clerks, a pianist, a seamstress." He attempted the half-smile; it didn't hold. "She told them the truth, which is worse than a lie, because the truth is that none of us has any business at all, and yet here we all are, and one of us was seen near the consulate on Tuesday."

Petra kept her face still. "Seen by whom?"

"A neighbor. An old woman on the corner who watches everything. She told the police a woman in a gray coat stood across the street from the consulate Tuesday night for the better part of an hour." Emil's eyes came up at last, and they were frightened, and they were sorry, and they were — Petra understood with a sensation like a floor tilting — apologetic. "It was you, wasn't it. Tuesday. In the gray coat."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs Marta Ozola was playing scales, the same ascending figure over and over, a pianist's habit of filling silence with order.

"And if it was me?" Petra said carefully.

"Then you should know that the police will come back, and next time they'll ask each of us separately, and separately is where people like me—" He stopped himself, but too late; the sentence had escaped whole. *People like me break.* He put his face in his hands. "God forgive me. I'm not threatening you. I'm telling you what I'm afraid of. There's a difference. I need there to be a difference."

"There's a difference," Petra said quietly, and meant it, and hated how much she understood him.

She climbed the stairs past Marta's scales, past the landing window where the fog was lifting off the bay and the burned consulate was visible as a raw black gap in the row of facades along the plaza, a missing tooth in the harbor's smile. In her room she sat on the edge of the bed and forced herself through the inventory, item by item, the way she'd been trained: What is lost? The archive. What remains? The pouch, decoded, copied onto a dress pattern, the original cipher pages dissolved in water two weeks ago and poured down the drain. Who knows? Possibly Kalns, possibly the police, possibly a watcher unknown. What is the next move?

The training offered no answer for the next part, the part that had crept into her chest on the walk home and settled there like the fog itself: *someone burned the archive before I could reach it, and I don't know who, and until I know who, every face in this house is a question.*

Tomás. Did Tomás count? He had read the fire like a professional. He noticed everything. He had caught her counting departures in the first week, and instead of reporting it he had brought her coffee and said nothing, which was either loyalty or surveillance, and she could no longer tell the difference, and the inability to tell the difference was itself a kind of wound.

Marta, playing scales above her — Marta, whose brother had been in one of the minority regiments, the regiments the pouch accused. Marta, who had a right to know what the pouch said, and a motive to want it never said.

Emil, downstairs, terrified, transparent, breaking.

Even Doña Inés, who sheltered everyone and therefore had reason to fear what any one of them might bring down upon the rest.

*This is what isolation does,* she thought. *This is the design of it. Burn the archive, scatter the couriers, close the passes, and the courier turns on the people around her, because a spy alone is a spy already defeated.* Somewhere, perhaps, her handler — orphaned, unreachable, his address a bakery now — would have approved. The thought made her feel ill.

A knock at the door. She was on her feet with her heart hammering before her mind caught up, and that, too, told her something about the state she was in.

It was Doña Inés, holding a folded note on cheap paper. "A boy brought this. He wouldn't say who sent him. Paid with a coin that had a hole drilled through it." She held it out and studied Petra's face with her old, unfooled eyes. "Whatever it is, niña, remember that this house has stood through worse than whatever it is. And remember that I ask no questions I don't already know the answers to, which is the only way to keep a house like this alive."

The note was one line, in Spanish, in a round commercial hand:

*The watchman did not run. Ask the pilot what happens to men who see too much in this harbor.*

Petra read it three times. Outside, the fog was burning off, and the sun came through the landing window and lay across the floorboards in a long bright bar, ordinary and warm, and down in the dining room Emil had begun to cry quietly, and above her Marta had stopped playing scales and started, softly, something else — a melody Petra didn't recognize, in a minor key, that sounded like a country remembering itself.

She folded the note into her sleeve and went downstairs to find Tomás Reyes.
