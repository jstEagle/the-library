## 4. The Ledger in the Type Cases

The morning of the second day of the requisition, Edmund came down the stairs before dawn because he could not sleep and because there was no sense pretending otherwise. The house above the shop had always been warm in a way that had nothing to do with the stove — his father's snoring through the wall like distant surf, Marta's cat asleep on the landing, the smell of yesterday's bread — but warmth was a thing you noticed only when it began to leave. Now every familiar sound seemed to be counting itself. He lay awake cataloguing them until cataloguing became unbearable, and then he dressed by feel and took the lamp down into the shop.

The requisition order still hung in his mind the way wet paper hangs on a wall, sagging, refusing to dry flat. *All civil registers, ledgers of account, rolls of parishioners, and books of record to be surrendered within three days.* Three days. One already gone. And somewhere in this town, presumably in the magistrate's strongroom or the parish chest, those books sat waiting to be gathered up like schoolchildren at the end of recess. It did not concern him. That was what he told himself as he lit the lamp and set it on the composing stone: it did not concern him, because the Oyelaran print shop kept no registers. They printed other people's records; they were not required to *be* one.

He had come down, if he was honest, for the Baskerville.

The job was small — funeral notices for old Widow Kessel's husband, forty copies, paid for in eggs — and Widow Kessel had asked, with the particular ferocity widows reserve for typography, that it be set in the same face as her wedding announcements twenty years ago. That face was Baskerville. Edmund knew the case. Third shelf, left bank, between the long primer and the small pica. He had set a playbill from it in October.

Except when he pulled the case out into the lamplight, the compartment where the capital Q lived held nothing but dust and two dead moths, and the lowercase ran thin as a winter congregation. He tilted the case toward the light. Half the font was simply gone — not worn, not scattered, but gone, the compartments clean-swept, as though the letters had been called away.

"Father," he said to the empty shop, out of habit, and then remembered his father was upstairs and asleep, and said it again anyway, quieter, because some words are for saying whether anyone hears.

It was not so strange. Augustin Oyelaran moved type the way other men moved furniture — without apology, without record, and usually without telling anyone. A font borrowed for a job and never returned was practically a family tradition. Edmund climbed the step-ladder and checked the cases on the upper shelves, sliding each one out an inch and tilting it, reading the metal gleam the way a farmer reads cloud. Long primer. Small pica. Bourgeois. The Baskerville was nowhere, and neither, now that he started looking properly, were certain others. The 14-point Caslon they used for broadside headers: missing. The fat-face display sort they'd cut last spring for the carnival bills: missing. A pattern assembled itself slowly, the way a picture comes up in a developing bath — not scattered loss but *withdrawal*, deliberate, a hand removing things.

His father had been printing something. Something large, in several sizes.

Edmund stood in the middle of the shop with the lamp raised and let that thought sit. Two nights ago his father had burned something in the stove after midnight. Yesterday he had gone pale as unsized paper at the sight of the requisition notice, and had spent the day in the back room doing nothing visible at all. Edmund had told himself the burning was correspondence. Old letters. Nothing.

But his father did not burn old letters. His father was the kind of man who mended book spines with thread and paste rather than let a volume die, who had once walked four miles through sleet to return a misprinted hymnal to the pastor personally, with apologies. Burning paper was against his religion the way usury was against a banker's.

Edmund went to the stove first. Cold ashes, raked flat — too flat, raked with intention, the way a man smooths a grave. He crouched and sifted with his fingers and found nothing but ash, fine and grey and complete. Whatever had been burned had been burned thoroughly, torn small first. He wiped his hands on his apron and felt, absurdly, guilty, as though he had been going through a stranger's pockets.

Then he went back to the type cases.

Because here was the thing about a print shop that no one who hadn't worked one ever understood: it was not a room full of objects. It was a room full of *positions*. Every case had its shelf, every shelf its number, every tray its slot in the frame, and the whole apparatus stood balanced like masonry — you could pull any stone out and the arch held, but you could always tell when a stone had been moved, because the dust told you, and the weight told you, and the fit of things told you. Edmund had grown up inside this balance the way a fish grows up inside water. He did not think about it. But it spoke to him constantly, and this morning it was speaking in a voice he didn't recognize.

He began at the bottom of the left bank and worked upward, pulling each case fully out, checking behind it. Behind the fourth case, the wall showed a rectangle of cleaner wood, sharp-edged, like a picture removed from a wall. He stood very still.

There was more space behind the cases than there should have been. The shelving was built against the north wall, solid, and the depth of a type case was a fixed thing — he had measured it often enough as a boy, playing at geometry while his father corrected proofs. But the gap back here ran deeper than a case. Deeper by the width of a large book. Deeper by the width of several.

He put the lamp close and looked, and saw that the shelf board itself was hinged — a strip of leather along one edge, worn dark with handling, invisible unless you knew to look, which Edmund had never known to look, in nineteen years of living above this shop.

He should have called his father. Later, in the terrible accounting he would keep of this night, he would return to that moment again and again: there was a version of events in which he climbed the stairs and woke Augustin and said *I found it, whatever it is, I found it*, and everything after happened differently, or happened the same but with company. But his hand was already on the shelf board, because his body had decided before his mind convened, and his mind — his tired, cold, three-days'-deadline mind — wanted only to know.

The board swung out on the hinge without a sound. Behind it, in a cavity lined with oiled cloth, lay a stack of books.

Not pamphlets. Books. Ledgers — real ones, bound in calf, brass-cornered, the kind the town clerk chained to his desk. There were four of them, and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth like a relic, a fifth that was larger and older, its spine broken and resewn twice, its cover stamped with the town arms: a bridge over water, and the motto Edmund had been reciting since childhood. *Flumen Testatur* — the river bears witness.

The master ledger. The register of Vessengard itself. Every name in the town, entered at birth, annotated at marriage, closed at death; every ration entitlement from the granary fund, every debt owed to the common purse, every widow's pension, every conscription exemption, every plot and parcel and grudge recorded across ninety years in six different clerks' handwriting. The book that said who you were. The army wanted it within three days, on pain of hanging, and it was sitting behind the small pica in his father's print shop.

Edmund did not touch it at first. He knelt there with the lamp trembling slightly in his hand — he could see the tremor in the light on the wall, a small dishonest flicker — and understood, in the slow way dread arrives, not as lightning but as floodwater rising around your boots, that his father was going to hang.

The thought came with no drama at all. That was the awful part. It arrived the way arithmetic arrives: two plus two. The order posted in the square named the penalty. His father had hidden the book. Hiding was not surrendering. Therefore. He could see it with terrible clarity — the scaffold they would build in the market square, probably where the maypole stood in summer, and his father's grey head above the crowd, and Marta — and himself, standing where? In the crowd, wearing his face?

"No," he said aloud, to nobody, and put the lamp down, and lifted the master ledger out of its nest with both hands.

It was heavier than it looked. Ninety years of names make a weight; he felt it in his wrists, in his shoulders, settling into him like something poured. He carried it to the composing stone and laid it down and opened it, because he could not help himself, because it was the town and he had never seen the town opened like a book before, though of course it had been open all along, all his life, and he had simply never been the one holding it.

The pages had that soft nap of well-handled paper. The earliest entries were in iron-gall ink gone brown as dried blood. He turned forward through decades — harvest accounts, the flood year, the cholera summer where the death entries crowded together like people pressing toward a door. He found his own birth entry, third from the top of a page in the year '29, in his grandfather's upright hand: *Edmund, son of Augustin Oyelaran, printer.* Someone — his mother, he realized, with a lurch; his mother, dead nine years — had drawn a small star beside it in pencil. He stared at the star for longer than he meant to.

He found Marta's entry, and beside it, in a later clerk's hand, the note of her marriage license fee — unpaid, marked *waived*, and underneath in fainter pencil someone had written *girl has troubles enough*, and Edmund's throat tightened, because he had never known that, never known she'd been spared the fee or why, and here it was, preserved in official ink, the town's kindnesses and cruelties both flattened into ledger-line.

He found the ration column for the current year, and his eye ran down it the way it ran down a galley of proof, automatically, catching errors — and stopped. The entries thinned toward the river streets. Whole households absent. The Kessels missing. The ferryman Halvard missing. Half the tanners' row, gone from the book as though the river had taken them early. He frowned, turned back a page, turned forward again. No. Not an error. An omission, systematic, recent, in fresh brown-black ink: the poorest quarters of Vessengard simply not entered among those entitled to grain. As if they had ceased to exist. As if the book had quietly agreed, in advance, with whatever the army intended.

*Everything is administrative,* the captain's sort of voice would say. *Merely a matter of record.*

Edmund closed the ledger and stood with his palms flat on its cover and felt the weight of it transfer from his hands into his chest, where it settled and stayed. This was what his father had been hiding. Not paper — *people*. Every name the army wanted was a person the army wanted unrecorded, uncounted, unowed. And his father, gentle, meticulous, hymnal-mending Augustin, had looked at the requisition notice and committed treason in the space between one breath and the next, without consulting his son, without consulting anyone, and had then gone upstairs and slept, or lain awake, Edmund didn't know which, while the book sat twelve feet below his bed behind the small pica.

Three days. Two now. If the soldiers searched — and soldiers who posted hanging notices were soldiers who searched — they would find it, because Edmund had found it in ten minutes with a missing font for a reason, and trained officers were better at finding things than sleepy compositors. His father would hang. Or worse, his father would hang and the book would go into an army wagon and roll east, and ninety years of Vessengard would become a line item in someone else's inventory, and the omitted families would stay omitted forever, officially, permanently, their absence ratified in captured ink.

Unless it crossed the river. The thought arrived unbidden and he examined it the way you examine a coin found on the street — turning it over, suspicious of it even as you pocket it. Across the river was the free bank, the committee men, the smugglers' paths through the marsh. Everyone said so; everyone said a great many things in taverns. If the ledger crossed the river, it stopped being evidence against his father and became testimony against the requisition. It became what its motto promised. The river bears witness.

And how exactly did a book weighing as much as a sleeping child cross a frozen river patrolled by grey coats? Through Halvard, presumably. At a price. Everything lately seemed to come to price, and Edmund disliked — distrusted — the speed with which his mind reached for figures, as if survival were a job of costing to be estimated. He pushed the thought down. It rose again immediately, patient, like ice under snow.

First things. He rewrapped the ledger — no. He stopped. He did not rewrap it. Instead he carried it back to the cavity, set it in place atop the others, closed the hinged board, slid the type case home, and stepped back and looked at the wall. Nothing. Perfectly nothing. Dust undisturbed except for the single line of his own knee-print in the floor's grey felt, which he scrubbed out with his palm until the floor was uniformly innocent.

Then he stood in the middle of the shop, breathing the familiar smells — ink and solvent and cold iron — none of which smelled familiar anymore, everything smelling instead like concealment, like the inside of a secret he now inhabited.

He thought about the missing Baskerville, and laughed once, short and awful, with no humor in it at all. His father hadn't misplaced anything. He had emptied the cases to make the cavity plausible — no, to fill the space, to give the wall a reason for its extra depth. The withdrawn fonts were camouflage. Every vanished letter was a soldier's searching hand, given something to find that wasn't the truth. His father had been composing this deception in metal for God knew how long, setting it up one empty compartment at a time, the most patient piece of typesetting of his career, and its text read: *nothing here, nothing here, move along.*

Edmund banked the lamp low and climbed the stairs and stood a moment outside his parents' old room, where his father now slept alone, listening. The surf-sound of snoring came steadily through the door. Ordinary. Unremarkable. A man asleep above his own gallows.

He lay down afterward and did not sleep. Above the workbench hung the framed motto his father had printed as an apprentice and never taken down — *the river bears witness* — and in the dark Edmund recited it to himself like a rope thrown to a drowning man, and gripped it, and found it did not hold his weight. Because witness required a reader. Witness required that somebody live to testify. And downstairs, behind the type cases, lay the only book in the world that could say who deserved saving — and it could not walk, could not swim, could not cross the ice by itself.

Somewhere in that arithmetic, he understood dimly, was a berth, and a name to fill it, and the beginning of a choice he did not yet know he was making.

At noon the knock came — three blows, unhurried, official — and Marta's face appeared white at the top of the stairs, and a voice from the street announced that Captain Voss of the Requisition would inspect the premises of Oyelaran & Son that afternoon, and thanked them, courteously, for their cooperation.

* * *

They had four hours.

Augustin received the news without surprise, which frightened Edmund more than panic would have. His father simply nodded, finished the proof he was correcting, wiped his pen, and said, "Then we tidy," as if expecting the bishop to lunch. He moved through the shop straightening stacks of paper, squaring the furniture, wiping the press — and Edmund watched him and understood that the tidiness was performance, that his father had rehearsed this afternoon in his head, perhaps for days. Only once did Augustin pause, standing near the north shelving, his back to his son, one hand resting on the frame of the small pica case the way a man rests a hand on a horse he must not spook.

"The Baskerville's gone missing," Edmund said carefully. "Third shelf."

"Mm," said his father. "Borrowed, I expect." He did not turn around. "Type travels, in this trade. You'll learn that."

"I've begun to learn it."

His father's hand tightened on the case frame — a small motion, quickly mastered. Then the knock came again, hours early, and the door opened on grey coats and frost-smoke and a tall officer unwinding a scarf with the unhurried grace of a man entering his own library, and Captain Voss smiled at them both and said, "Master Oyelaran. I'm told you keep the finest fonts on this stretch of river. Show me everything."
