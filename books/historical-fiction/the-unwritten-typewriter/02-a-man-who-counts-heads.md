## 2. A Man Who Counts Heads

The supply boat came on the third Thursday of September, which was late by two days and early by nobody's reckoning, because the supply boat kept its own calendar the way the sea keeps its own opinions.

Juniper saw her first as a smudge against the grey, standing in the lamp room with his morning tea going cold in his hand. He had learned to read boats the way other men read weather: the *Petrel* sat low in the water, which meant flour and lamp oil and probably a crate of those waxed oranges the mainlanders thought islanders couldn't live without. She was riding awkward, though — listing a degree to port, correcting, listing again. Someone aboard was walking around when they oughtn't be.

"Sit down, whoever you are," he muttered at the smudge. "You'll have us all over."

He went down the hundred and nine steps — he had counted them once during a long winter and never stopped counting since; it was a habit like biting his nails, except tidier — and out along the causeway path to the landing stage, where the gulls were already assembling in their usual mob, screaming their usual nonsense about fish that did not exist.

The *Petrel* bumped against the tires hung along the pilings, and a deckhand threw the line, and Juniper caught it out of habit and hauled, and there she was: crates, barrels, mail sack, and one passenger.

The passenger was not expected.

He was a small man, neat as a pinned moth, wearing a coat too good for this coast and boots already regretting it. He had a leather satchel across his chest and a hat with a feather in it that the wind took an immediate personal interest in. He stepped onto the landing stage, looked up at the lighthouse towering white against the scudding clouds, and beamed.

"Well," he said, in the voice of a man who has practiced being delighted, "it's taller than the paperwork suggests."

"Everything is," said Juniper.

The man laughed as though this were wit of the first order, and Juniper felt the small internal sigh he reserved for visitors. Nine years of keeping a light will teach a man to measure people quickly: fishermen, inspectors, the occasional lost yachtsman weeping into his moustache. This one registered as *harmless*, which was a category Juniper trusted least of all. Harmless men were the ones who asked questions.

"I'm looking for the keeper of the light," the man said, producing a card from somewhere inside his coat with the flourish of a magician. "Aldous Wren. Census of the Northern Districts, Ministry of — well, several ministries, honestly, they can't agree whose fault I am."

Juniper took the card. It said, in very tidy print, exactly what the man had just said, minus the joke.

"Census," Juniper repeated.

"Every soul on every rock between here and the Headlands," Wren said cheerfully. "Names, ages, occupations, marriages. The government wishes to know you exist."

"The government has managed without knowing for some years."

"All the more reason." Wren smiled. He had a face built for smiling — round, ruddy, with eyebrows that seemed to operate independently, like two small dogs who had heard something. "I'm told the keeper here is a Mrs. Ashgrove. Widow."

The wind chose that moment to shove past them both toward the open sea, and Juniper let the silence stretch just long enough to seem like wind-deafness rather than calculation.

"You're speaking to him," he said. "Her. The keeper. J. Ashgrove."

"J," said Wren, making a note before anyone had given him anything to note, which Juniper would think about later and wince at. "And the J stands for?"

"Juniper."

"A tree," said Wren, pleased. "My grandmother was called Hazel. There was a fashion for it, I think. Everyone my grandmother's age is a shrub."

* * *

They got the crates up to the store room first, because cargo waits for no census. Juniper worked and Wren worked beside him, badly but enthusiastically, and by the time the last barrel of lamp oil was stowed, Wren had learned — through no effort of concealment on Juniper's part — that the island had one goat (bad-tempered), one rowboat (leaking), and one keeper (not offering further details).

Wren talked the whole time. That was the thing about him. He talked the way rain falls — steadily, from all directions, getting into everything. He talked about the crossing ("I have never in my life been so intimately acquainted with a bucket"), about the Ministry ("a building where enthusiasm goes to be embalmed"), about the oranges ("do you want these? No? More for me"). And threaded through all of it, so lightly you could almost miss it, were questions.

How long had the keeper been here? Nine years. A long time alone. Some prefer it. And the light — oil or electric? Oil, still; the mainland kept promising electricity the way it promised roads. And had there been many keepers before?

"One family," Juniper said. "Mine."

"Ah." Wren stacked a crate with the care of a man stacking his own opinions. "Ashgroves."

"Ashgroves."

"And how far back does the name go?"

"Farther than the records," said Juniper, which was true, and also a door closing.

Wren accepted the closed door with a nod and opened another one instead. "Lunch?" he said. "I find census work goes better fed. I've bread, cheese, and a tin of something labeled in a language I don't speak, which usually means herring."

* * *

In the kitchen, the kettle did its work, and Wren unpacked his lunch with the ceremony of a bishop laying out relics, and Juniper found himself — against all policy — sitting across the table from a stranger for the first time in eleven months.

It was, he admitted privately, not entirely unpleasant. The kitchen had gone quiet in a way rooms do when only one person breathes in them. Wren's chatter filled it back up, the way filling a cistern fills a house. He ate three of the waxed oranges and apologized for none of them.

"So," Wren said eventually, wiping his hands, and produced from the satchel a ledger bound in green cloth and a pencil sharpened within an inch of its life. "The form. It takes ten minutes if we're lucky and an hour if either of us is interesting."

"I'm not interesting."

"No one ever is," Wren agreed solemnly. "That's what makes the job bearable." He licked the pencil. "Name."

"J. Ashgrove."

"Full name, please. The form is very insistent. It says *full name* twice, and the second time it's underlined."

Juniper looked at the kettle. The kettle offered nothing.

"Juniper Ashgrove," he said.

"Marvellous. Age?"

"Fifty-one."

"Fifty-one. Occupation: keeper of the light. Marital status—" Wren's pencil hovered. "The parish record lists a marriage. Maren Ashgrove, née Voss. Married... let me see, I copied the entry at the district office..." He turned a page in his own notes, and Juniper watched him turn it and could not look away, the way one cannot look away from a cart tipping. "Eleventh of May, 1922. Is that right?"

"Yes," said Juniper. Too fast. He heard it land wrong even as he said it — a half-beat early, the answer of a man reciting rather than remembering.

Wren's pencil did not move. His eyebrows, however, exchanged a glance.

"Twelve years ago," Wren said mildly. "You'll forgive me — most widows and widowers carry the date like a stone in the pocket. They say it slow. You said it quick."

"It's been a long time."

"Twelve years," Wren agreed. "Or — hm." He frowned at his notes with genuine, untheatrical confusion, the kind that made Juniper's stomach drop a full fathom. "The parish register gives 1922. But the marriage banns — I cross-check banns, it's a habit, I apologize, everyone finds it tedious — the banns were read in March of 1921. Which would make the wedding..." He counted on his fingers, visibly, like a schoolboy. "Thirteen years. Nearly fourteen."

The clock in the corner ticked. The sea hit the rocks below in its patient, enormous way.

"Banns are often misdated," Juniper said.

"They are," Wren agreed readily. "Clerks are human. I once found a baptism recorded before the birth, which I choose to believe was a miracle rather than sloth." He smiled, and then, gently, without any cruelty in it at all: "But you'd know your own anniversary."

"Would I?" said Juniper, and heard the crack in his own voice like ice giving way underfoot.

It was a small thing, the silence that followed. Three seconds, perhaps four. But Juniper had spent nine years learning silences the way sailors learn skies, and this one had weight in it. Wren sat quite still, pencil resting on the page, and looked at Juniper with those independent eyebrows now both raised in concert, and behind the cheerfulness there was something else — not suspicion exactly, or not yet. Interest. The interest of a man who counts heads for a living and has just found one head too many.

"Maren," Wren said softly. "That's her name. Maren Ashgrove. Died — the winter of '25, the registers say. Drowned."

"Drowned," Juniper said. The word came out flat and finished, a coin paid over.

"That's the year the *Ellen May* went down off the point, isn't it? Terrible business. Half the district talked of nothing else for a month." Wren's voice stayed light, but his eyes didn't. "You'd have been keeping this light then. You'd have seen her go out, if she was on that boat."

"She wasn't on that boat."

"No," Wren said. "No, I don't expect she was. Her boat was smaller. Found on the rocks below the cliff path, I read. Alone." He paused. "That must have been — I'm sorry. I'm asking you to unwrap a wound so the government can count it. That's a rotten thing my job makes me do."

For a moment — one treacherous moment — Juniper nearly told him. Not all of it, not the ribbon, not Tomas, not the name that wasn't his. Just the shape of it. Because the man had kind eyes, and kindness after nine years is a dangerous ration, and grief left too long in the dark grows teeth.

Then he remembered Inspector Corvane's face at the last inspection, smooth as poured concrete, and the memory shut the door again.

"She drowned," Juniper said. "I keep her light. Write down whatever the form requires."

Wren held his gaze a moment longer. Then he wrote. The pencil scratched, small and busy, and Juniper watched the letters appear upside down on the page — *J. Ashgrove, widow, 51, keeper* — and hated how neatly they fit.

"There," Wren said at last, closing the ledger with a snap. "One soul counted." He stood, stretched, and began repacking his satchel, chattering again as though the last five minutes had been weather. "Now — where am I sleeping? Because the *Petrel* won't be back for eleven days, and I've a dozen more households on this stretch of coast, and the harbor master swears there's an inn, and the harbor master, I suspect, swears many things."

"Eleven days," Juniper said.

"Eleven days. Then the last boat before winter, and after that it's just you, the goat, and the gulls until spring." Wren paused at the door, satchel on his hip, and gave Juniper a smile that was entirely warm and entirely watchful, the two things somehow occupying the same face without contradiction. "I do hope we'll get on, Mrs.—"

He stopped. Corrected himself with visible care, the way a man steps back from a stair edge in the dark.

"*Keeper* Ashgrove."

The title hung in the kitchen air between them, and Juniper heard — or imagined he heard, and would spend the next eleven days deciding which — the half-second pause before *keeper*, the tiny hitch of a man choosing a word rather than using one.

"The boathouse has a cot," Juniper said. "Mind the goat."

* * *

That night, Juniper climbed to the lamp room and lit the light, and while the great lens gathered itself up and began its slow rotation, sending its blade of brightness out over the black water, he stood at the glass and looked down at the boathouse, where a candle burned in the window.

Aldous Wren was writing. Juniper could see the small bent shadow of him over the desk, the scratch of a pen too faint to hear at this distance but easy enough to imagine. Writing his report, no doubt. *Island visited. One soul counted. Keeper cooperative.*

Or writing something else.

Juniper turned back to the lamp. Below, the sea kept its own ledger — patient, complete, and unreadable — and somewhere beneath it lay the true date of a wedding, and a drowned woman, and a brother who had walked into a customs office nine years ago and never walked out.

Eleven days, the census man had said. Eleven days until the last boat.

Juniper wound the clockwork, checked the oil, and signed the log:

*Wind SW, freshening. Light steady. J. Ashgrove, widow.*

His hand hesitated, just slightly, on the final word.

Outside, the storm the barometer had been promising all day arrived like a debt collector, and the tower leaned into it, and the light went on answering.
