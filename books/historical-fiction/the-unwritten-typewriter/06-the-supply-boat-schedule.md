## 6. The Supply Boat Schedule

Aldous Wren had counted heads in four hundred and eleven households that autumn, and he could say with some authority that the northern islands attracted two kinds of people: those who wanted to be left alone, and those who wanted everyone else to leave them alone. Keeper Juniper Ashgrove — or whoever he was — belonged to a third category entirely. He wanted to be left alone so thoroughly that he had become someone else to do it.

Wren sat at the lighthouse kitchen table on the fourth morning of his stay, ostensibly because the weather was too foul for the census boat to make the crossing back, and actually because he had not been able to stop thinking about the ribbon.

The storm had settled into a long gray argument with the island. Rain came sideways off the sea and tried the shutters like a debt collector. Inside, the kitchen was warm and smelled of peat smoke and strong tea and, faintly, of lamp oil, which Wren suspected had soaked into the very stones of the place over decades of faithful service.

Juniper stood at the stove with his back half-turned, which Wren had learned was his preferred way of holding a conversation — angled toward escape, like a man perpetually one step from a door.

"Eleven days," Wren said.

"Until what?"

"The supply boat. The last one before winter." Wren turned his teacup slowly on its saucer. "I checked the schedule board at the harbor office before I came out. The *Merrow's Gift*. She leaves the mainland on the twenty-third, calls here on the twenty-fourth, weather permitting, and then she's gone until spring. After that, this island is a paragraph in an atlas."

Juniper set down the kettle with exaggerated care, as though it might be listening. "You've a remarkable memory for timetables."

"It's the census man in me. Names, dates, numbers of pigs per household. My head is a filing cabinet with legs." He smiled. Juniper did not smile back, but then, Juniper rarely did. Wren had catalogued his expressions over four days the way he'd catalogue livestock: there was the flat look, the wary look, the look of a man doing arithmetic about how far it was to the cliff path. This morning he wore the arithmetic one.

"And Corvane," Wren went on, before the silence could set like concrete, "will be aboard."

That got a reaction. Small — a stillness in the shoulders, a fraction of a degree — but Wren had spent years noticing what people failed to say. It was the whole trade, really. The census asked questions; the answers lived in the pauses.

"You know him, then," Juniper said.

"I've met him. Twice. Once at a function where he told a joke about fishmongers that made three aldermen laugh out of terror, and once across a desk, when he reviewed my district figures and found nothing wrong, which I took as a compliment until I understood that finding nothing wrong is his profession." Wren leaned forward. "He inspects the winter boat personally every year. Everyone knows it. It's practically a festival. The harbor master polishes brass, the crew shaves, and Inspector Corvane walks the deck with his little notebook, and God help any crate whose paperwork has a smudge on it."

"Why would he bother with an island of nine people?"

"Because it's the one crossing no one can bribe their way around. Because it's habit. And because—" Wren hesitated, choosing the next words the way a man chooses footholds on wet rock, "—because I think he keeps lists. Of names. Of things that don't add up. And I think your name is on one of them, keeper, and mine may soon be joining it."

The stove ticked. Rain worked at the glass. Juniper finally sat down across from him, and Wren noted it as an event worth recording: the man had chosen the table over the door.

"Say what you came here to say," Juniper said. "You've been circling it since Tuesday."

*Since the ribbon,* Wren thought. Since he'd stood in the boathouse with that old typewriter open under his fingers and read fragment after fragment of manifest numbers inked into the cloth — numbers he knew, numbers he had audited six years ago from the other end, numbers that had been declared lost in a records fire that had conveniently destroyed only the pages that mattered. He had walked back up to the house that evening feeling as if he'd found a coin from a country that no longer existed.

"Very well." He folded his hands. "The ribbon is evidence. Real evidence — the kind that can't be burned, because it's already been written. Manifest numbers, dates, initials. If the right people saw it — naval inspectors, the press, anyone outside Corvane's reach — it would unravel him. But it has to get off this island, past three checkpoints, onto the mainland, into hands that aren't his. And the last chance is the *Merrow's Gift*, eleven days from now, with Corvane himself standing on her deck."

"So you want to smuggle it."

"I want to *carry* it," Wren said. "Smuggling implies guilt. Carrying evidence implies citizenship."

"There's a difference?"

"In theory, several miles of legal distinction. In practice—" he spread his hands, "—we'll find out."

"No."

It came without heat, without drama, just flat and final as a slammed drawer. Juniper rose and began stacking dishes that didn't need stacking. "The answer is no. You'll take the boat, you'll finish your census on the mainland, and you'll forget what you saw in my boathouse. That typewriter is junk. That ribbon is rag. There's nothing on this island but a light and a lonely man who talks to gulls."

"Keeper—"

"*Mister Wren.*" Juniper set the stack down hard enough to rattle. For a moment something moved behind his eyes that wasn't anger at all — something older and wetter, like water under ice. "Nine years I have kept my head down and my lamp lit and my mouth shut. Nine years. Do you know what happens to people on this coast who carry evidence? They go out on the water and they don't come back. I buried one already. I am not going to be the reason another grave gets dug."

Wren watched him. He didn't argue — arguing with a man in that state was like arguing with weather. Instead he said, quietly: "Your wife's brother."

The dishes stopped.

"Tomas Ashgrove," Wren said. "Customs clerk, Port Ellery. Disappeared in the autumn of '25. Verdict: drowned, body unrecovered. I filed the census follow-up myself, two years later. Widow Ashgrove, née — well. You'd know better than I what she was née."

"You're a long way past counting heads, Mister Wren."

"Yes," Wren agreed. "I noticed that around the time I started lying to my own office about how long I needed to stay here."

They looked at each other across the table, across the tea and the rain and nine years of careful silence, and Wren felt the strange vertigo of the moment when two separate lonelinesses recognize each other. He had come to this island to count one man. He had instead found the exact weight of everything he himself had been avoiding since the year of the fire in the records office.

"The boat comes in eleven days," he said again, gently. "I'm not asking you to decide today. I'm asking you to decide by Tuesday."

"I've decided today."

"Then decide again Tuesday. It's a free country. Well — it's a country. The freedom varies by postcode."

Something flickered at the corner of Juniper's mouth. It might have been the ghost of a smile, or a muscle spasm brought on by fury; with him it was genuinely difficult to tell. He picked up his oilskin off the peg by the door.

"I've got a lamp to trim," he said, and went out into the rain.

* * *

Wren spent the afternoon doing what he did best: making lists.

He wrote them in the back of his census ledger, in the cramped shorthand he used for marginal notes, because a ledger full of household counts was the one document on the island no one would ever bother to steal. *Boat: Merrow's Gift, 24th. Inspection: Corvane, personal. Checkpoints: harbor master (drunk), toll bridge clerk (nervous), quay inspector (Corvane). Assets: one ribbon, approx. 40 inches, inked both sides. Liabilities: one keeper, stubborn as sea rock.*

He was on his third cup of tea and second list when he heard the engine.

It came low across the water, not the friendly stutter of the mail launch but a heavier sound, purposeful, the sound of a boat with a job to do. Wren went to the window and pulled the curtain back a finger's width.

A patrol launch, gray-painted, government number stenciled on the bow, was coming around the southern point. Not passing. Circling. It slowed as it came abeam of the lighthouse, wallowed in the swell, and held there — dead in the water, close enough that Wren could see a man at the rail with binoculars, and see the binoculars seeing the house.

He counted. It was a habit; he counted everything. One minute. Two. Three. Four. A launch didn't hold station off a lighthouse for four minutes unless someone aboard was reading something — a chart, a list, a description. Five minutes. The man with the binoculars lowered them, spoke to someone below, raised them again.

Wren let the curtain fall and stood in the dim kitchen with his heart going like a telegraph key. He thought: *they're checking whether the widow is home.* He thought: *Corvane's list.* He thought of the logbook downstairs, signed *J. Ashgrove, widow* in a hand that had grown steadier and more practiced every year, and of how much easier it is to catch a lie when you already suspect one.

The launch's engine coughed, caught, and swung away north, trailing a line of white that the rain erased almost immediately. Gone. But not gone the way weather goes. Gone the way a creditor goes — satisfied, for now, with having been seen.

When Juniper came back at dusk, salt-crusted and dripping, Wren was waiting at the table with two cups poured and the ledger closed between them.

"Patrol launch," Wren said. "Circled us for five minutes this afternoon. Man at the glasses. They looked at the house, then at the tower, then at the house again."

Juniper stood in the doorway with water running off his oilskin onto the flags, and Wren watched the arithmetic happen behind his eyes — the same arithmetic, Wren guessed, that had been running for nine years, only now the sums were changing.

"They've never done that before?" Wren asked.

"Not in daylight. Not twice in a season." Juniper hung the oilskin on its peg with great precision, as if the coat were the last orderly thing left in the world. He came to the table but did not sit. "If they take the logbook, they take the signatures. If they take the signatures—"

"They take you. And then the ribbon doesn't matter, because the man who could explain it will be answering questions somewhere with no windows." Wren pushed the second cup toward him. "So here's the ugly truth, keeper, and I'll only say it once. Staying silent isn't safe anymore. That option closed this afternoon, somewhere around minute four. The only question left is whether that ribbon leaves this island in our pockets or in Corvane's evidence bag — and whether we're standing on the boat when it does."

Juniper looked at the tea. He looked at the window, where the rain had thinned to a fine silver drift and the first star was fighting through over the water, right where the *Merrow's Gift* would appear in eleven days. When he spoke, his voice was quiet and stripped of all its walls.

"Maren sewed," he said, apparently from nowhere. "She could put a stitch through anything — canvas, leather, the hide of a boot. She used to say there wasn't a thing made that couldn't be taken apart and made to hold something else." His jaw worked. "Lamp wick coils are packed in tarred canvas. Nobody unpacks wick. Nobody touches it. It smells of oil and it burns your fingers and harbor men hate it."

Wren kept very still, the way one keeps still near a bird that has decided, against all odds, to land.

"That's not a yes," Juniper said.

"It's the most beautiful maybe I've ever heard."

"Don't make me regret it." He sat down at last, and took the cup, and wrapped both hands around it as though it were something small and warm in a cold world. "Eleven days. We do it your way, census man. But if it goes wrong — if anyone else drowns because of what's written on that rag —"

"Then we'll drown them properly in paperwork afterward," Wren said. "It's the one weapon I'm armed with."

And Juniper Ashgrove laughed — a short, rusty, astonished sound, as if the noise had surprised him more than anyone — and outside the rain went on falling on the light and the island and the long dark water, and somewhere north, unseen, the winter was already loading itself onto a boat.
