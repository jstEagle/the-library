## 7. The Ledger Beneath

The fever came for Matron Sorrel on a Tuesday, the way fevers always do — without ceremony, without regard for the schedule of the dying or the workload of the living.

Kestrel knew it first by her hands. He had watched those hands for three years now, through triage and amputation and the long quiet hours of holding men down while the surgeons worked, and he had learned to read them the way other people read faces. Sorrel's hands were never still. Even at rest they moved — folding gauze, straightening a pillow, tapping out some private arithmetic against her thigh. That morning, wrapping a corporal's burned forearm, they faltered. The bandage went on crooked. She noticed it before he did, and she looked at her own fingers as if they had betrayed her in a language she'd once been fluent in.

"Late night with the reports," she said.

"You were asleep when I checked."

"I was resting my eyes." She tied off the wrap, and it was neat enough, but it took her two tries. "Go see to the fever ward. I have it from here."

He had it from there. That was the trouble. Kestrel went, because she was his superior and because arguing with Sorrel was like arguing with weather, but all through the afternoon he kept one ear turned toward the supply room where she worked, listening for the sound of her hands stopping.

They stopped at dusk.

He found her sitting on the floor between the shelves of rolled bandages, her back against the shelving uprights, her cap askew, looking up at him with an expression of furious surprise — as though the fever were a subordinate who had overstepped.

"Don't make a production," she said. Her voice already had gravel in it. "Help me up. Put me in my own cot. And Kestrel—" she caught his sleeve as he bent, and her grip was still strong, grip like a man who'd spent thirty years hauling soldiers back from the edge of things, "—don't put me in the fever ward. I've seen what we do to people in the fever ward. I'd rather die among the linens."

"You're not going to die."

"No," she agreed, mildly. "Probably not today."

* * *

By midnight she was raving.

It came on fast after that, the way swamp fevers did — one hour shivering under three blankets, the next burning so hot that the sweat dried on her skin before it could bead. Kestrel sat with her in the small private alcove behind the supply room that served as her quarters, a room barely wider than a cot and a chair, smelling of carbolic and camphor and, beneath it, faintly, of lavender, which she denied using and which Juniper swore she'd seen her sprinkle on her pillow every Sunday.

Juniper wanted to sit vigil too. Kestrel sent her to bed, and she went, but he heard her stop outside the canvas partition and stand there a long moment, her small feet visible in the lamplight gap beneath, before she padded away. In the morning there would be contraband sugar appearing at Sorrel's bedside from sources Kestrel would pretend not to investigate. He knew the girl. He knew how she loved. It was one of the things that frightened him most about her.

Sorrel surfaced from the delirium around two in the morning, suddenly, completely, the way she did everything. Her eyes found him in the dark and focused with terrible clarity.

"How long?"

"Since midnight. You were talking."

"To whom?"

"The quartermaster. A colonel. Someone named Pell." He hesitated. "A girl called Lark."

Her face changed. Not much. Sorrel's face was a ledger itself, entries made and struck through with equal discipline. But something opened behind her eyes and then closed again, quick as a door in wind.

"Lark," she said. "Yes. She would be there. She's always there, this time of year." She turned her head on the pillow and looked at the ceiling of the alcove, at the water stains that mapped some private geography only she could read. "How many names are in it now, Kestrel?"

He went cold. "In what?"

"Don't." The word came out cracked but iron underneath. "Don't insult us both. I caught you in March. Under the floorboard by the stove. You thought I didn't see you put it back." She coughed, wet and deep, and waved off the cup he reached for. "I've been keeping count of your counting since spring. Forty-one names as of last week. You write smaller than I did."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still between them. Outside, somewhere across the compound, a mule complained and a sentry told it to shut up, and the war went on being the war, indifferent and enormous, while in a linen closet two keepers of forbidden books regarded each other across four feet of dark.

"You never said anything," Kestrel managed.

"What was there to say? 'Stop doing the thing I did for eleven years'?" She laughed, and it turned into another cough, worse, and he held the cup to her lips despite her waving, and she drank because her body wanted what her pride refused. When she could speak again her voice had dropped half its register. "Where is it now?"

"Under the floorboard. Where you said."

"It moves. You move it. Vasseur's clerks will search eventually — they search everywhere eventually, it's the whole personality type. Move it weekly. Never the same board twice in a month." She recited it like drill. Then, quieter: "And when I'm well, we'll speak properly. There are things about the keeping of it that no one should learn alone."

"When you're well," he said firmly. "You'll tell me then."

She smiled. It was the worst thing he'd seen all year, that smile — patient, knowing, fond, and absolutely certain of something he wasn't ready to know.

* * *

The fever took her voice on the third day.

Not all at once. It went the way voices go in field hospitals — first the volume, then the music of it, until what remained was a whisper that cost her visibly, each word purchased at the price of a shudder. Dr. Halloway wanted to sedate her fully. Sorrel refused sedation with a look that made the doctor, a man who had held beating hearts in his hands, step back from the cot.

"She wants to tell you something," Halloway said to Kestrel in the corridor, rubbing his eyes. "She's been waiting for you since dawn. Whatever it is, hear it. I don't like her odds if this turns tonight."

Kestrel sat beside her in the grey light of early morning. Rain had started against the canvas roof, soft and ceaseless, the sound of the whole world wringing itself out.

"Sorrel. I'm here."

Her hand came out from under the blanket and found his wrist — not his hand, his wrist, where she could feel his pulse, because Sorrel trusted pulses more than words. Her skin was furnace-hot and dry.

"My trunk," she whispered. "Bottom. False bottom. Lift the tray."

"Yes."

"There's a book. Blue. Leather gone soft at the corners, like yours will be, someday, if you live that long." A breath, drawn with effort, released with more. "Eleven years. Two wars. Four hundred and six names." Her fingers tightened on his wrist. "Four hundred and six, Kestrel. I counted them twice a year, every year, like prayer. Some of them I never even saw die. Reports came in, and the names weren't in the reports, and I wrote them anyway. That's the whole vocation. That's the entire religion."

"Why are you telling me now?" His voice came out strange. "You're going to recover. We'll—"

"Hush." No force in it. All the force in the world anyway. "Ledgers outlive their keepers. Say it back."

"Ledgers outlive their keepers."

"That's not comfort. Don't take it as comfort. It's a warning and an instruction." Her eyes were bright with fever and something else, something older and harder. "The book doesn't care that you're tired. The book doesn't care that you're afraid. The book only cares whether the names get written or don't. Everything else — the danger, the treason, the tribunal they'll build for you someday — that's just weather. The book is the climate." She paused, gathering breath like a woman gathering stones. "I meant to burn mine in '19. After Lark. I had it in the stove. I stood there with the door open for an hour."

"What stopped you?"

"Her mother wrote to me. Asking why the army said her daughter died of pneumonia when Lark's own last letter said she was wounded and being moved." Sorrel's whisper frayed. "One letter. One mother asking one honest question. And I understood that the book wasn't mine to burn. It never belonged to me. I was just the hand it was using." Her grip tightened almost painfully. "Do you understand? You think you keep the ledger. You don't. It keeps you. And when you're dust, it goes on wanting, and someone else's hand will shake above the page the way ours shook, and God help them, they'll need to know it can be done. That someone did it before. That the hands survive it. Mostly."

"Mostly," he repeated.

"I'm still here," she said. "That counts as surviving. Lower your standards accordingly."

Despite everything — the fever, the fear, the rain — he laughed, and her mouth twitched, satisfied, and for a moment she was entirely herself, the woman who had taught him to tie a tourniquet one-handed in the dark and to lie to officers with a straight spine.

Then the moment passed, and she sagged, and her whisper dropped to almost nothing, and he leaned close to catch it.

"Trunk. Bottom. Take it tonight. Keep it separate from yours — never together, never in the same hiding place, that's the first rule and the last rule. Two books, two graves. If they find one, the other must live." Her eyes closed. Opened. Fixed on him with the last of her clarity. "And Kestrel. If it comes to choosing — the book or yourself—"

"Don't say it."

"—the book," she finished, and her hand fell away from his wrist, and her breathing went shallow and rapid, and within minutes the fever had her again, and she was calling for Lark, and for a colonel long dead, and for someone named Amory whom Kestrel had never heard her mention in three years.

He sat with her until the rain eased toward dawn. Then he went to her trunk, knelt, and lifted the tray.

The blue book lay beneath a folded uniform and a bundle of letters tied with string. Its leather had gone soft at the corners exactly as she'd said, worn to velvet by eleven years of hands. He didn't open it. Not yet. Opening it felt like reading someone's mail, or touching a relic, or stepping onto ground where something had been buried alive.

Instead he wrapped it in a spare shirt, carried it across the compound in the grey drizzle, and slid it beneath a floorboard on the far side of the ward — a board he had never used, in a corner he rarely passed, so that the two ledgers now slept twenty paces apart, two graves, as instructed.

When he straightened, Juniper was standing in the doorway with a basin of water, watching him with her quick dark eyes.

"Matron's worse," she said. Not a question.

"Worse," he agreed.

"I brought the good cloth for her forehead." Juniper set down the basin. She looked at the floorboard near his boot, then deliberately at his face, and said nothing at all — which was its own kind of entry in a ledger Kestrel hadn't known the girl was keeping.

That evening, word came down from command that Colonel Vasseur had arrived at Greyhollow Station ahead of schedule, and that record discipline would begin immediately, and that all irregular files were to be surrendered for review by week's end.

Kestrel stood in the ward doorway with the rain starting again, twenty paces from one hidden book and forty-one names, and understood that the warning had not been about fever at all.
