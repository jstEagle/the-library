## 10. The Question Too Many

The rain had stopped by the time Elias Wren decided he was done being polite.

For six days he had played the census taker. He had sat in the records tent with its leaking stove and its smell of carbolic and wet wool, and he had asked his mandated questions in his mandated order: name, age, parish, next of kin, date of admission, disposition. He had filled in the columns of the census forms in his small, even hand, and he had smiled at the clerks, and he had thanked Matron Sorrel for her patience through her coughing fits, and he had watched Kestrel Dravenmoor move through the ward like a man carrying water in both hands — careful, level, never spilling.

Six days of arithmetic that didn't add up.

Elias had been counting since he was nine years old. His father said it was a sickness; his mother said it was a gift; Tomas, four years younger and forever losing things, said it was just what Elias did instead of talking. Counting was how Elias loved the world. He counted the stairs to their flat in Marrowgate — fourteen, then eleven after the bombing took the top two. He counted the buttons on Tomas's first uniform the day he marched out — nine, one already hanging by a thread, and Elias had sewn it himself at the door because there wasn't time for anything better than a button.

Tomas Wren. Private, 3rd Battalion. Reported *transferred to auxiliary station* in a letter so thin you could read through it. No station named. No date of transfer. No grave. A boy of nineteen who could not find his own boots in the dark and who had once walked into a canal because he was reading while walking, and now the entire army of the republic could not find him either.

*Transferred.* Elias had learned what that word meant the way you learn anything in wartime: by asking it too many times until someone's face changed.

He had the evidence now. It hadn't been hard; that was the obscene part. Greyhollow's morning reports were kept in a cabinet whose lock had been broken since before the war, and the admissions ledger — the real one, the one the nurses used — lived on the ward desk where anyone with eyes could read it. For three nights Elias had copied figures by lamplight while the rain hammered the canvas roof and told himself he was still doing his job.

The numbers were simple enough for a child. In the last eleven months, Greyhollow's recorded admissions totaled 1,847. Its recorded dispositions — discharged, transferred, died — totaled 1,612. That left 235 men admitted and unaccounted for. Not missing in action. Not lost in transit. *Unaccounted*, here, in a station that had no cemetery large enough for them and no paperwork to say they'd ever drawn breath past its gates.

And the morning reports — the ones that went up the chain to command — were worse than incomplete. They were edited. Elias had found the seams. Whole entries scored out in a different ink, a heavier hand, dates rewritten so that men who died on the ninth died instead on the eleventh, at some other station, under some other command's pen. Three privates struck from a single Tuesday in March as though a clerk had simply grown tired of writing.

Someone at Greyhollow was making the war smaller than it was. And someone else at Greyhollow — Elias would have bet his brother's name on it — was keeping count of the difference.

He found Kestrel at the laundry line behind the ward tents, near dusk, pinning bandages with the economy of a man who had done ten thousand of them and hated every one slightly less than he hated wasting cloth. The sky over the ridge was the color of a bruise going yellow at the edge. Somewhere east, very far off, the guns had started again, a sound like a giant turning over in his sleep.

"Mr. Dravenmoor."

Kestrel didn't startle. That was the first thing Elias had noticed about him, six days ago, and it still held: nothing startled him, not shells, not orders, not a stranger saying his name in the half-dark. He finished the pin he was setting before he turned.

"Census taker." Not unkind. Not warm, either. "Come to count my clothespins? There are forty-one. I'll save you the trouble."

"I've been counting other things."

Something moved behind Kestrel's eyes — quick, controlled, gone. "Have you."

"One thousand eight hundred forty-seven admissions," Elias said. "One thousand six hundred twelve accounted dispositions. Two hundred thirty-five men who came through your gate and vanished from the record like smoke through gauze." He let the numbers sit between them the way you set a stone on a table. "I checked my sums four times. I'd be grateful if you'd check them too. You strike me as a man who checks."

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. Beyond him, down the slope, the ward lamps were coming on one by one, little yellow eyes opening in the blue dark. A transport of wounded was expected tonight; you could feel the station holding its breath the way it always did before the trucks came, everyone moving a half-step faster without admitting it.

"You should take those figures to Colonel Vasseur," Kestrel said. "Record discipline is his passion. He's having files burned over it."

"He's having files burned *because* of it."

"Then you two have something in common after all." Kestrel reached past him for another bandage, and the conversation was supposed to be over; Elias could feel the door closing, polite and padded, the way doors closed all through this war.

He didn't move out of the way.

"There's a name I came to ask about," Elias said. "It isn't on any list. It isn't in your admissions ledger, or your discharge book, or the morning reports, or the burial register, which is short by the same two hundred thirty-five, give or take. Private Tomas Wren. Third Battalion. Nineteen years old. Transferred to an auxiliary station — yours, according to a routing slip I paid a drunk quartermaster in Vellmark two months' wages to find." His voice stayed level. He was proud of that, later, that it stayed level. "He'd have arrived in the winter. February, maybe early March. He was afraid of the dark and he hummed when he was nervous and he had a button missing off his left cuff that I sewed on myself the morning he left, badly, because I was crying and pretending I wasn't."

The wind moved the line of bandages. They swayed like a row of pale ghosts deciding whether to speak.

Kestrel had gone very still. Not the stillness of a man with nothing to hide — Elias knew that stillness too, had catalogued it across a hundred interviews. This was the stillness of a man standing at the edge of something, weighing it.

"Say the date again," Kestrel said quietly.

"February. The convoy came up from Vellmark on the ninth. He'd have been on it."

"The ninth." Kestrel closed his eyes, and Elias watched his lips move — not words, exactly. Numbers. The man was counting. Even now, even here, he was counting, and Elias felt something crack open in his chest, because he recognized it. It was how *he* prayed.

When Kestrel opened his eyes, they were wet, and he did not apologize for it, and somehow that was worse than tears.

"Come with me," he said.

* * *

They did not go to the records tent. They went to the ward — long, low, lamplit, smelling of iodine and the ghost of every fever that had ever passed through it — and then past it, to the narrow room behind the dispensary where the stores were kept and where, Elias understood dimly, a man could talk without the walls having ears. Kestrel lit no lamp. The window gave them a grey square of dying light, and that was all he seemed to want.

"Do you know what happens to a man here when he dies?" Kestrel asked. His voice had changed — lower, stripped of the ward-floor steadiness. "Officially?"

"He dies," Elias said. "It's written down."

"It's written down if it's convenient." Kestrel leaned against the shelving, arms folded, as if holding himself together at the seams. "If the convoy's full, if the report's already sealed, if the colonel needs his casualty figures to come in under quota so the battalion upstream doesn't get audited — then a man dies twice. Once in the cot. Once in the ledger. And the second death is the one that follows him home. Or doesn't." He looked at Elias, and there was no deflection left in his face at all. "There are men buried on the north slope with no markers. There are men sent forward to the depot hospital dead and entered as living, so the numbers would balance somewhere they'd never be checked. And there are names that appear nowhere at all. Not because we don't know them. Because knowing them is dangerous."

Elias's mouth had gone dry. "Tomas Wren," he said. "February. The ninth."

"I remember the ninth of February." Kestrel's jaw worked. "I remember every man off that convoy, because that was the night the fever ward overflowed and we put cots in the corridor, and I made a promise to myself about that night that I intend to keep if it kills me, and it may." He straightened. "I can't tell you what happened to your brother. I want to say that plainly, so you understand I'm not hiding a body behind a kindness. But I will tell you this—" and here his voice caught, actually caught, a splinter in the machinery of it, "—if Tomas Wren came through Greyhollow, then Tomas Wren has a place where his name is written down. Written properly. With his battalion and his date and whatever we knew of him, so that someday, someone looking — someone like you — could find him."

The room tilted. Elias gripped the edge of the dispensary table and the wood was cold and real and the world kept tilting anyway.

"You keep a ledger," he said. It wasn't a question. It had never been a question; it had been the shape under all his questions for six days, the thing he'd been circling like a bird over water. "You keep a second ledger. Of the ones they cut out."

Kestrel said nothing. The silence was the most honest answer Elias had gotten in two years of asking.

"Show me." His voice came out rough. "Please. I have spent twenty-two months reading letters that say *transferred*. My mother sets a place at the table. She's stopped setting it, and then she starts again, and I cannot — I need to see his name in ink. Written by a hand that knew he existed. Even if it only says he died. *Especially* if it only says he died. A man shouldn't get to vanish just because the war needed him to."

"Elias." Kestrel said his name like a warning and a plea at once. "That ledger, if it exists, is treason. Vasseur has made it treason. If he finds it, it burns, and the man who kept it goes in front of a tribunal, and possibly the wall. If I show it to you, you're not a census taker anymore. You're a witness. Witnesses get transported. Witnesses get—"

"My brother got *nothing*," Elias said, and heard his own voice break the level at last, finally, gloriously. "No marker. No letter. No lie, even — they couldn't be bothered to lie well. Two hundred and thirty-five men got nothing, Mr. Dravenmoor, and you're standing there telling me the only place in this whole rotten province where they exist is under your floorboards, and you want me to weigh that against *danger*?" He laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and short. "I walked into a war zone with a forged commission to ask one question too many. Do you think I don't know what I am now?"

For a moment neither of them moved. Down the hill, the first truck of the evening convoy ground through the gate, and the station exhaled into motion around them — stretchers, lanterns, someone calling for hot water — the great machine swallowing another load of boys and beginning its arithmetic again.

Kestrel crossed the room. He knelt at the corner where the shelving met the floor, and his fingers found a board's edge with the certainty of long practice, and Elias's heart slammed so hard he could hear it.

But Kestrel paused there, kneeling, his hand flat on the wood, and looked up.

"Not tonight," he said. "Tonight there's a convoy coming in and forty men who need me, and if I open that floor with the station awake, we both hang. Tomorrow. Before the audit — Vasseur's announced one, you'll have seen the notice. Before he seals everything and ships us out. I'll show you every page." His eyes held Elias's, fierce and exhausted and, underneath it, something that looked unbearably like relief — the look of a man who has carried a weight alone so long he'd forgotten it had a shape. "And if your brother is in it — Elias, if he's in it — you'll be the first person to read his name who loved him. I swear that on every man in the book."

Elias nodded. He couldn't speak. He stood in the grey light with his hands shaking and thought: *twenty-two months, and tomorrow.*

Kestrel rose, replaced the board so cleanly the seam vanished, and was already turning toward the noise of the convoy, the nurse reassembling over the man. At the door he stopped.

"Wren," he said. "Your brother. What was he humming? When he was nervous."

Elias blinked. "A folk song. From Marrowgate. About a miller's daughter. Badly. He couldn't carry a tune in a bucket."

Something flickered across Kestrel's face — recognition, or its shadow, or maybe only grief reaching forward to meet grief halfway. He didn't say. He said, "Get some sleep, census taker. Tomorrow you'll want a steady hand."

Then he was gone into the lamplight and the shouting and the wheels, and Elias stood alone in the dispensary, listening to the convoy unload the war, counting the seconds until morning.

There were, he noted distantly, fourteen hours.

He had counted shorter nights.
