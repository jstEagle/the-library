## 15. The Berth

The manifest arrived at dusk, folded twice and smelling of the transport office — coal smoke and cheap ink. Sergeant Halloran brought it himself, which meant it mattered. He set it on the dispensary counter without ceremony, the way you'd set down a dead man's effects.

"Last transport out of Greyhollow," he said. "Forty berths. Thirty-nine assigned by seniority and medical priority." He tapped the paper once with a blunt finger. "One berth unassigned. Commandant says it goes to whoever the matron designates. In writing. Her authorization or nobody."

He said *the matron* the way people had started saying it lately — carefully, in past tense, as though Sorrel were already a document rather than a woman. She hadn't left her cot in six days. The fever had taken her voice down to a rasp and her mind down to a place where she called Juniper by another name and asked for a sister who had been dead thirty years.

"I'll see it gets to her," I said.

Halloran looked at me a moment longer than the sentence required. He was a fair man, Halloran, in the way that men who follow orders have to be fair to sleep at night. "Matron Sorrel saved my brother at Marrow Ford," he said. "Whatever she writes, I'll countersign it myself."

Then he left, and I stood alone in the dispensary with the manifest and the lamplight and the arithmetic starting up in my head like a loom.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the whole point of everything I've done under these floorboards, and because a man who keeps two sets of books eventually has to account to himself.

I did not take the manifest to Sorrel.

I told myself I would. I carried it down the corridor to the fever ward, and I stood in the doorway with the paper in my hand, and I watched Juniper sit vigil beside the matron's cot with a wet cloth in her small hands, dabbing at a forehead that burned like a stove door. Juniper had tucked her hair back with a scrap of string. She was humming — something tuneless, half a lullaby, half just breath — the way you hum when silence would break you.

Sorrel's eyes opened. They found me in the doorway with terrible accuracy, the way they always had, even now, even swimming in fever.

"The manifest?" she rasped.

"It can wait until morning."

"No." Her hand came off the blanket, trembling, and pointed at the paper. "Read it to me. My eyes swim."

So I read it. Forty berths. Thirty-nine names. And the line at the bottom: *Remaining berth to be designated by the station matron under her hand and seal.*

Sorrel listened with her eyes closed. When I finished, she was quiet so long I thought she'd gone under again. Then:

"You know what I'd write."

It wasn't a question. Of course she knew. Of course I knew. There was only one name either of us could put on that line, and it wasn't mine, and we both understood without saying it that it wasn't hers either — because Sorrel had not walked more than ten steps in a week, and a berth spent on a dying woman was a berth wasted, and Sorrel had never wasted anything in her life, not thread, not morphine, not grief.

"Juniper," she said. "You'll write Juniper Vale. Under my hand."

"Your hand means your writing. Your seal. Vasseur will check both."

"My seal is in the second drawer." She tried to lift her head; failed; lay back with the effort of it. "And my writing—" A sound came out of her that might have been a laugh if fevers allowed laughter. "My writing, Kestrel. You've seen my writing every day for four years. You've entered my dosages, transcribed my orders, signed my requisitions when my hands shook too badly. You've been forging me for years. You're better at being me than I am."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly what it is." Her eyes opened again, and there was something in them I hadn't seen since before the fever — the old iron, the woman who had run this station through three bombardments and an epidemic and the winter the supply lines died. "Listen to me. I am going to say this once, while I still have the mind to say it, and then I'm going to forget I said it, and you're going to let me forget. You will put that child on that train. You will not ask her permission, because she will refuse, and she will refuse wrong. She is twelve years old and she has already buried one family. She will not bury a second from inside a burning city."

"Sorrel—"

"And you will not put your own name on that line." Her voice cracked on it, went thin. "Don't think I don't see it sitting behind your face. The arithmetic. One berth, three of you, and you've already decided the answer is you stay and play the martyr with the census man." She drew a breath that whistled. "I didn't spend forty years in uniform to watch you die of nobility. Nobility is just pride wearing a nurse's apron."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere down the ward a man moaned in his sleep and someone shushed him.

"Promise me," she said.

And here is where honesty costs. Because I promised her. I took her hand — those hands, the hands that had taught me to tie a tourniquet, to hold a screaming man's face and tell him the truth gently — and I said, "I promise, Matron."

And then I went back to the dispensary, and I sat down with the manifest and her seal, and I forged her authorization for Juniper Vale, and I did not put my own name anywhere on the page, and I told no one. Not Sorrel, who believed she had won. Not Juniper, who would have refused. Not Elias, who would have argued. No one.

That is the chapter. That is the whole of it. A promise kept and broken in the same hour, depending on how you read the words.

* * *

The forgery itself took less time than I expected, and that frightened me more than the act.

I spread Sorrel's old requisitions across the counter — a year of them, her hand declining through them like a patient declining, strong strokes in spring, wavering lines by autumn, the letters leaning harder and harder as though blown by a wind only she could feel. I studied the way she made her R's, open at the top like a bird's beak. The way her T crossed low. The way, in the last months, she had stopped dotting her I's with any force, leaving them like pinpricks.

Then I wrote: *Designation of remaining berth: JUNIPER VALE, ward of this station. By order and hand of Matron E. Sorrel.* And beneath it, her signature — the late signature, the shaky one, because a shaky signature would be true to her condition and therefore beyond suspicion — and pressed her seal into the wax I'd softened over the lamp.

The seal came down clean. The wax held the impression of the greyhollow crest, the little hound mid-leap, and I looked at it and felt nothing in my hands and everything in my chest, which is how guilt works, I've learned. It never lives where the crime is committed. It lives everywhere else.

Because here is what I couldn't stop seeing, pressing the seal into the wax: Sorrel's hands doing it. Sorrel's actual hands, if her body had let her rise from that cot, would have done exactly this, written exactly this, sealed exactly this. The forgery was faithful. That was the horror of it. I hadn't invented her will; I had merely lent it my fingers. And yet the law — Vasseur's law, the new law of record discipline, discrepancies treated as treason — did not care whose will it was. It cared whose hand. And the hand was mine.

I had spent four years writing dead men's names into a hidden book, believing that ink could be a form of justice. Now I had written a living girl's name into an official one, knowing the ink was a lie, and I could not tell you which entry weighed more. Maybe that's the lesson of ledgers. Every line costs somebody something. The only question is whether you're willing to look at the price column.

* * *

I hid the manifest where I hide everything — under the third floorboard, beside the ledger — and then I couldn't stand it, and took it out again, and put it instead inside my shirt, against my skin, where I could feel it with every breath. Paper warms to your body temperature after an hour. After that you can't tell where the document ends and you begin. I've thought about that a great deal since.

The evening shift passed in its usual litany. Dressings changed. Morphine measured to the drop. A boy from the 9th Fusiliers cried for his mother and I held his hand and lied to him about morning, and all the while the manifest rode against my sternum like a second heart, and I caught myself doing the arithmetic again, the cold kind, the kind Sorrel had named in me and tried to kill.

*One berth. Four candidates, if you count Elias, and I count Elias, God help me.*

Juniper: twelve years old, orphaned twice over — once by the shelling of Aldenmere, once more slowly by this place becoming the only home she had. She scrubs bandages until her knuckles split and never complains, because complaining, she learned early, is a luxury rationed like sugar. If Greyhollow burns, she burns with it, unless someone spends the berth.

Elias: a target. Vasseur's audit has him marked — the census taker who asks one question too many, whose questions are treason-adjacent now, whose presence at Greyhollow is a liability to everyone including himself. He has orders to leave on the same transport. His berth is his own; he doesn't need mine. But he'll give it up. I know him well enough now to know he'll step aside for Juniper without hesitation, and then he'll be stranded here with me, and part of me — the shameful part, the part I keep below the floorboards with everything else — wants that. Wants him here. Wants the tribunal to come and find us side by side, because if the truth is going to cost us, I find I cannot imagine paying it alone.

Me: I could take the berth. Write my own name. Board the last transport with the ledger sewn into my coat and testify in some safe capital, safe and alive and useless, carrying the names forward like a courier carries dispatches. That is the defensible choice. That is the choice duty's arithmetic recommends.

But the names are not dispatches. They are not cargo. A ledger carried away from the ground where its dead fell is just paper. Here, read aloud, in front of the men who falsified the reports and the staff who suspected and said nothing — here it is testimony. Here it is a mirror held up to Vasseur's clean books. If I leave, the ledger leaves with me, and Vasseur burns Greyhollow's records behind us, and thirty-nine berths' worth of survivors carry away their memories, and memory fades, and memory can be contradicted, but a ledger read aloud before witnesses — that becomes fact. That becomes the kind of thing tribunals must reckon with because too many people heard it to pretend otherwise.

So the arithmetic resolves, the way it always resolves, cold and clean and cruel: Juniper rides. Elias rides, whether he likes it or not — I'll put him on that train myself if I have to. Sorrel stays; she could not travel and would not, and we both know her war ends in this ward, among her patients, and there are worse rooms to die in than one you filled with your own mercy.

And I stay. Not because staying is noble. Sorrel's right — nobility is pride in an apron. I stay because the work isn't finished. The ledger has blank pages left, and the audit is coming, and someone has to stand in front of Vasseur's clean books and make them dirty with the truth.

* * *

Near midnight I checked on Juniper. She'd fallen asleep in the chair beside Sorrel's cot, upright, chin on chest, the wet cloth dried out in her lap. I lifted her — she weighs almost nothing, twelve years old and rations short — and carried her to her cot in the linen room, and she stirred and murmured something I didn't catch, and gripped my sleeve for a moment before letting go.

I stood there in the dark listening to her breathe, and I thought: *she doesn't know.* Tomorrow or the day after, when the manifests post, she'll see her name on the transport list and she'll come to me furious, and she'll say she won't go, and she'll mean it with her whole fierce heart, and none of it will matter, because the authorization bears Sorrel's seal and Halloran's countersignature and no twelve-year-old overrides a matron's designation. She will hate me for a week. Perhaps a month. Perhaps, if I die here, forever — and that is the part that sits in my chest like swallowed stone. That the last thing she might carry of me is anger. That my final gift arrives wrapped in betrayal.

But she'll be alive to hate me. Alive on a train heading west, with two ledgers in her kit if I do this right — mine and Sorrel's, the hidden book and the inherited one, the whole archive of the unnamed — and somewhere ahead of her a coastal town and a life that isn't measured in cots. She'll understand someday. Or she won't, and she'll live anyway, and either outcome beats the alternative by every measure I know.

I tucked the blanket to her chin. On the shelf above her cot sat her collection — a chipped blue button, a sparrow's feather, a tin soldier missing one arm, the small treasury of a child who owns nothing and hoards everything. I touched the feather once, lightly, the way you touch things you're saying goodbye to.

Then I went back down the corridor, past the sleeping ward, past the dispensary, to the loose floorboard, and I lifted it, and I took out the ledger, and I turned to a fresh page.

At the top, in my careful hand, I wrote the date. Beneath it, the first entry of the night: not a dead man's name this time, but a living girl's, and the words *berth secured — cost: withheld from bearer.* Because the ledger should tell the truth, even the parts that indict me. Especially those.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Through the canvas I could hear the rail yard: couplings clanking, an engine building pressure somewhere in the dark, the war packing itself up to move west and leave us the bill.

Two days, maybe three, until the last transport. Two days, maybe three, until Vasseur's audit reached my desk and my floorboards and my forged seal.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark holding the pen, and far down the corridor, in the fever ward, I heard Juniper begin to sing to Sorrel — that same tuneless half-lullaby — and I closed my eyes, and I memorized the sound, the way I memorized every name, because I had already decided what I was willing to lose, and I wanted to be sure I knew exactly what it was.
