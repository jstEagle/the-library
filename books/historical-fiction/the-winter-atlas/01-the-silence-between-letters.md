## 1. The Silence Between Letters

The type was cold before dawn, and Oscar loved it best that way.

She sat at the composing stone with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, the lamp turned low because Papa said lamplight wasted money and the city said lamplight invited attention, and she lifted each piece of type from the case with the sureness of long habit. *B*, upper left. *R*, two rows down. Her fingers moved without consulting her mind, and her mind was free to wander where it pleased — which these nights meant the taverns below, where the murmur had changed its tune.

The print shop occupied the second floor of a narrow house on Binder's Lane, and beneath it, wedged into the cellar like a secret the building kept even from itself, was the Half Moon tavern. On ordinary evenings its noise rose through the floorboards as a pleasant rumble — dice, argument, a fiddle missing half its strings. But for three weeks now the sound had been different. Quieter. Closer. Men leaning in instead of leaning back. When forty voices drop to twenty, the silence between them grows teeth.

Oscar set another line: *the winter roads shall be opened to all* — no, that wasn't right, that was the atlas copy, not tonight's job. Tonight was funeral notices and a handbill for Herr Vogel's dry goods, honest work, forgettable work. She broke the line apart with a brass rule and began again.

"Boy." Papa's voice from the back room, where he did the accounts by a stove that gave more smoke than heat. "Is the Vogel bill locked up?"

"Chase is full. Waiting on the last quatrain."

A grunt. In this shop, a grunt could mean approval, doubt, or indigestion; after seventeen years of listening, Oscar had learned to hear all three.

She was Oscar Zhou to everyone on Binder's Lane — to the chandler who sold them tallow, to the night watchman who nodded as she carried bundles to the ferry office, to the guild clerk who had entered her, aged nine, into the apprentice rolls with a bored flick of his pen. A boy named Oscar, small for his years, quiet, clever with his hands. The binding on her chest was the first thing she put on every morning and the last thing she took off at night, and somewhere in those seventeen years the ritual had stopped feeling like disguise and started feeling like dressing. That was the part nobody warned you about: that a lie worn daily becomes a skin, and a skin becomes a self, until you cannot say which one is the costume.

She did not regret it. That was the strange thing. There were girls in this city who ground flour and hauled water and would never in their lives be handed a galley of type and told, *make it sing*. Oscar had been handed one at eight years old, when her mother was still alive, and Mei Zhou had looked at her daughter over the composing stone and said nothing at all — which, in their family, was the loudest possible blessing.

Her mother. Three years dead now, and still the shop smelled faintly of the jasmine oil she'd rubbed into her hands to keep the ink from cracking her skin. Still, when Oscar sorted the job cases, her fingers found the compartments her mother had reorganized against all guild convention — punctuation where the numerals should be, numerals where the swash letters lived — and only Oscar knew the system, because only Oscar had learned it at her mother's knee, in Chinese first and then in the trade tongue, the letters explained twice over like twins with different names.

Papa had never remarried. Papa had never once, in three years, spoken his wife's name aloud inside these walls. That silence had a shape too, and Oscar had learned to move around it the way she moved around the press — carefully, knowing exactly where the machinery could catch her.

Below, the tavern door banged open and let in a gust of street cold and a voice too loud for the new quiet. Oscar paused, type stick balanced, and listened without meaning to.

"—telling you, they've taken the toll ledgers. All of them. Every crossing on the north—"

"Drink your beer and lower your voice."

"—I'm saying what I'm saying! If the colonel's men can close a road, they can close a shop—"

The door swallowed the rest. Oscar sat very still. The lamp flame leaned in a draft and righted itself.

Colonel Reiner. Everyone on Binder's Lane knew the name the way sailors know a storm front. He commanded the garrison on the far bank, and in the year since his appointment the checkpoints had multiplied along the river bridges like mold on bread — first two, then four, then the tollhouse at Miller's Bend where carts were emptied to their axles and reloaded by soldiers' hands. Trade slowed. Paper got expensive. And paper, Oscar thought, looking around at the shop that was made entirely of paper — paper in bales, paper in quires, paper in drawers and galleys and stacks against every wall — paper was suddenly a thing that could get a man hanged.

Because it wasn't just the handbills anymore. It was the broadsides that appeared overnight pasted to the customs house, printed from plates nobody could find. It was the pamphlets that circulated hand to hand with no colophon, no printer's mark, no name — which was itself a kind of signature, because only someone very good would dare print without a mark, and the city had perhaps four someones that good, and one of them worked above the Half Moon.

Not Papa. Papa printed almanacs and funeral notices and the occasional shipping manifest, and if his politics leaned anywhere it was toward the politics of a man with a daughter to feed. But Mama had had friends. Mama had had friends who came late, who spoke softly, who left parcels wrapped in oilcloth that Papa carried to the cellar and never discussed. Oscar had been fourteen and told to sweep the stairs while the visitors climbed them, and she had swept the same three steps for an hour, listening to everything.

That was the other silence in the house, the one under Papa's silence about Mama: whatever Mei Zhou had been besides a compositor's wife, it had gone into the ground with her, unconfessed, and Papa intended to keep it there. Once, drunk on plum wine at New Year, he had begun — "Your mother, she believed—" — and then stopped as though the sentence were a bridge with the middle planks missing, and never tried again.

Oscar finished the Vogel bill, locked the chase, and wiped the stone. Through the window, the river showed itself in slices between the warehouses: black water, white rim of ice, the far bank lamps of the garrison burning later than usual. Four lights where there were usually two.

She pulled her coat from its peg, wound the wool scarf high over the binding at her throat, and went down the outside stair into the cold.

* * *

The Half Moon's cellar was warm with bodies and tallow smoke, and Marta the keeper saw Oscar come in and did not see her — that was the arrangement, unspoken, going back years. Boys bought beer for their fathers. Boys carried messages. Boys were furniture with ears.

She wove to the corner table where old Anselm sat every night with the same half liter and the same newspaper, reading it so slowly it might have been scripture. Anselm had been a compositor himself once, before his eyes went, and he treated Oscar with the particular courtesy one craftsman pays another.

"Sit, boy. You look like your father's ledger."

"I feel like it."

He slid the newspaper across. "Page four. Read me the shipping notices. My eyes are done for the night."

His eyes were fine. His voice, however, needed carrying, and page four was where the shipping notices ran — and where, folded into the routine columns, certain sentences appeared that meant nothing to a customs officer and everything to the men drinking quietly around them. *The winter roads shall be opened to all.* *Cargo consigned to friends requires no manifest.* Oscar read them in her flattest apprentice monotone while Anselm nodded along, and around them the tavern listened through the medium of not listening at all.

This was the world she belonged to. She knew that with a certainty that frightened her. Not the conspiracy, maybe — or maybe yes, maybe she'd been inside it since childhood without ever being shown the door — but this: the lamplit rooms where words were freight, the men and women who understood that ink was not merely ink but a lever, the whole underground economy of the said and unsaid. She loved it the way you love a house you grew up in, including its cracks.

And she knew how houses fall.

Three tables away, a man she didn't know sat alone, drinking little, watching much. His boots were garrison boots. He wore a laborer's coat over them, badly — the coat was right but the shoulders were wrong, and the boots were wrong entirely, and Oscar had spent her life learning to read people the way she read type, letter by letter, and this man spelled *soldier* in a hand that thought itself disguised.

Anselm followed her glance and did not follow it visibly. "New face," he murmured. "Been coming five nights."

"Five," Oscar said. "He drinks the same beer. Never finishes it."

"A man who doesn't drink doesn't want a loose tongue." Anselm turned back to his newspaper. "Finish the notices, boy. Then go home by the lane, not the alley. And tell your father—" he paused, choosing, "—tell him the ice on the river will break early this year. He'll know what it means."

Oscar did not know what it meant. That was the trouble with the language everyone around her spoke fluently; she'd been raised in it but only ever taught the household words.

She finished the notices, left coin for the beer she hadn't drunk, and climbed back into the cold. The lane was empty except for snow beginning to fall, fat and slow, the kind of snow that meant business. Above her, the shop window glowed with Papa's single lamp, and his shadow crossed it once, bent over the account books, and did not cross again.

Tell him the ice will break early. She stood in the falling snow and looked up at the lit window — her home, her trade, her whole careful life stacked in paper and silence — and felt, for no reason she could name, the way one feels on a bridge when the railing hums underfoot: nothing has broken yet, but something upstream is moving.

In the morning, she would tell him. In the morning everything would be ordinary — the stove, the type cases, the Vogel bill delivered, the day's honest and forgettable work.

It was the last ordinary morning for a long time. But she couldn't have known that standing in the snow, only that the tavern behind her had gone quieter than she'd ever heard it, and that somewhere in the city a courier was already riding, and that the silence between letters — the silence her family had lived inside for seventeen years — was about to be broken by the sound of a seal cracking open.
