## 2. A Letter Under Seal

The courier came at the hour when the light through the shop's front windows turned the color of weak tea, and Oscar Zhou was elbow-deep in a forme of type she had been setting since before dawn.

She heard him first — the knock of a horse's iron shoes on the cobbles outside the print shop, too deliberate for a dray cart, too unhurried for anything urgent. Then the bell above the door gave its small brass complaint, and cold air poured in with the smell of the river and of leather that had been ridden hard through frost.

Oscar did not look up at once. That was the first rule her father had taught her, back when she was still small enough to stand on a crate to reach the composing stick: *In this trade, we are furniture until spoken to.* A compositor who watched every customer too closely was a compositor who remembered faces, and faces were dangerous things to keep.

"Delivery for the master of the house." The voice was clipped, official, the vowels of a man who had been taught his diction by the state rather than by his mother. "Signature required."

Only then did she raise her head — just enough, chin down, shoulders rounded into the boyish stoop she had worn for six years now, since the winter they'd buried Mama and Papa had cut her hair himself with the shop shears and said, *from today you are my son, and God forgive us both.*

"I'm the son," she said. Her voice sat low where it always sat, in the cellar of her throat. "Papa's at the press."

The courier was young, red-cheeked from the cold, with the grey-and-black livery of the Governor's postal service buttoned to the collar. He held out a letter, and Oscar saw at once that it was wrong in every particular that mattered.

It was not paper-wrapped like an invoice or a book order. It was sealed — wax, deep black, stamped with the double-headed eagle of the provincial chancellery. And it was addressed not to *Zhou & Son, Printers* as business letters were, but in a fine clerk's hand directly to *Master Wen Zhou, proprietor,* with a second line beneath that made her stomach drop before she could even read it whole:

*By order of the Office of Internal Security.*

"We don't get much from that office," she said, and heard how carefully flat her own voice came out. Six years of practice. Six years of being no one in particular, a thin boy with ink-stained fingers who asked no questions and kept no opinions.

"No," the courier agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't." He held out the receipt book. "Name."

"Oscar Zhou."

He wrote it without interest. She signed beneath it, watching her own hand stay steady, and only when the door had closed behind him and the hoofbeats had faded toward the Custom House did she allow herself to look properly at the letter.

*Internal Security.*

Below, muffled through the floorboards, the tavern called the Drowned Bell was already filling for the midday hour. She could hear the low tide-murmur of voices, a scrap of song, someone laughing too loudly. For two years now the talk down there had been turning — slowly, then less slowly — from grain prices to grievances, from grievances to names. She set some of those names herself, in pamphlets that arrived after midnight and left before dawn, printed on the second press that officially did not exist. She had never asked whose hands carried them out of the city. She had never wanted to know. Not-knowing was a kind of armor; she had sewn it herself, stitch by stitch, and worn it so long she'd forgotten it was there.

The letter weighed almost nothing. It might have been a page of blank stock.

"Papa," she called down the stairwell, and her voice cracked on the second syllable despite everything.

* * *

Wen Zhou came up wiping his hands on a rag, ink to the second knuckle as always, a big slow-moving man who seemed built out of the same oak as the presses he tended. He was smiling the way he smiled when a job went well — and then he saw the seal, and the smile died not quickly but entirely, all at once, like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

"Where did this come from?"

"A courier. Chancellery seal. I signed for it." She held it out. "Papa, I can open it if—"

"No." He took it from her hand with a strange gentleness, as though it might go off. He turned it over once. Twice. His thumb found the wax eagle and rested there, and Oscar watched her father — the most deliberate man in a city of deliberate men, a man who locked up the shop each night in the same sequence, who checked the banked fire twice — watch that seal for the length of ten full breaths.

Then he said, quietly, "Fetch me the knife. The bone-handled one. And then go mind the press."

"Papa—"

"*Mind the press*, Oscar."

But she did not go. She fetched the knife because he would only ask again, and she stood by the window with her arms crossed while he slit the wax in one clean stroke, and she read the letter upside down across the desk from him, because six years of setting type meant she could read anything inverted, mirrored, backwards, sideways — reading was her trade, and no father alive could stop her doing it upside down.

There were three paragraphs. She got most of them before he finished the first.

*...it has been represented to this office that the establishment known as Zhou & Son has served, whether wittingly or otherwise, as a point of relay in the conveyance of seditious correspondence...*

*...the names of certain persons connected to the late Madame Lian Zhou are of particular interest...*

*...you will present yourself to the Office of Internal Security within three days to answer these representations, failing which measures will be taken...*

Her mother's name. Sitting in a government letter like a coin dropped in a gutter.

Papa read it through once, fast. Then again, slow, his lips moving slightly. Then he folded it along its original creases with the precision of a man folding a shroud, and he reached for the lamp.

"Papa. Don't."

"The stove is right there."

"You'll want to read it again tomorrow. You'll want to know exactly what it says when you're standing in front of them." She heard herself speaking in the tone she used with apprentices, calm and instructional, and hated it, and kept using it. "Burning it changes nothing. They keep copies. They *always* keep copies — we've printed enough of their notices to know how their offices work."

Her father looked at her then, really looked, and something passed over his face that she had never seen there before and never wanted to see: he looked *old*. Not tired-old, not wise-old. Old the way a building looks when the foundation shifts.

"They named your mother," he said.

"I know. I read it upside down."

A ghost of something moved at the corner of his mouth — pride, absurdly, at that — and then drowned. He set the letter down on the desk instead of feeding it to the stove, smoothing it flat with both palms as though it were a proof sheet he intended to correct.

"Three days," he said.

"Two and a half. It's past noon."

"Don't be clever."

"I'm never clever. I'm a dull boy who sets type." But her hands, she noticed distantly, had begun to shake, and she pressed them flat against her apron the way she pressed proofs, evenly, with the whole weight of her arms.

Downstairs, the Drowned Bell roared suddenly — laughter, or an argument, impossible to tell through the floor. Somewhere in that noise were men who talked of uprising, men whose pamphlets passed through her hands in the dark, men whose names might at this very moment be sitting in a file drawer in the Office of Internal Security beside a letter bearing her family's address. And somewhere in the city, other letters like this one were arriving at other shops, other houses, other doors, and the people opening them were learning what Oscar was learning now: that silence, which had felt like safety, was in fact just the sound of the trap before it springs.

* * *

They did not burn the letter. Instead her father did something worse, something that frightened her more than flame would have: he unlocked the bottom drawer of his desk — the drawer she had been forbidden since childhood, the drawer she had privately decided years ago contained debt papers or love letters or nothing at all — and took out a small iron key on a length of black cord.

"Oscar." He wound the cord around the key twice, three times, and pressed it into her palm, closing her fingers over it with both of his big ink-dark hands. "If anyone comes for me — soldiers, clerks, anyone with a chancellery seal — you take the stairs behind the paper store, you go down to the river, and you do not come back for the shop. Do you hear me? Not for the shop. Not for the presses. Not for me."

"What's in the drawer, Papa?"

His jaw worked. Above them the winter light was failing already, the short day sliding toward early dark, and the lamp threw his shadow huge against the wall of standing type cases, taller than any man.

"There are things your mother kept," he said finally. "Things I promised her I'd keep too. I thought—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I thought keeping them hidden was the same as keeping us safe. I was wrong about that. Maybe I've been wrong about it for a long time."

The key lay in her palm, small and cold and heavier than any piece of metal had a right to be.

"And the letter?" she asked. "What do we do about the letter?"

Her father looked at the creased paper on his desk, at the black wax lying in two halves like a broken heart, and for a long moment he said nothing at all. In six years of shared silences — the silence of her name, the silence of the second press, the silence of the trunk at the foot of her mother's old bed that neither of them had ever opened — Oscar had learned to read his quiet the way she read type: by touch, in the dark, letter by letter.

This silence spelled *fear*. And beneath the fear, faint as a watermark, something else.

"Nothing," he said at last. "We do nothing. We set tomorrow's job, we eat supper, we sleep. We are printers, and printers print, and that is all we have ever done."

But his eyes went, as he said it, to the forbidden drawer — and Oscar stood there holding the key, understanding with terrible clarity that her father had just lied to her for the first time in her life, and that whatever her dead mother had kept hidden in that drawer was the reason a government courier had known their address.

That night she waited until his lamp went dark. Then she lit her own stub of candle, knelt at the drawer, and turned the key.

Inside lay a bundle of letters tied in her mother's handwriting, a pistol wrapped in oilcloth — and beneath them, flat and heavy, half of an engraved copper plate, its etched lines glinting in the candlelight like frost on a window.

At its edge, in tiny italic script, were words she knew better than her own heartbeat, because she had set them herself a hundred times:

*The Winter Atlas — Plate VII of XIV.*
