## 2. What the Case Held

The weight channel of a master clock is a shaft cut through the tower's spine, four meters deep and barely wider than a man's shoulders, and it is cleaned once a decade if the guild remembers, which the guild rarely does. Mara Almeida stood at its lip with a lantern hooked to her belt and a length of rope coiled over one shoulder, and she looked down into a darkness that smelled of machine oil, pigeon droppings, and something older — dust that had settled out of the air of 1911 and never been disturbed since.

She had been working since dawn. The master clock had stopped three days ago, at 4:17 by the observatory's own reckoning, and the city had noticed the way a body notices a missing heartbeat: not at once, but as a low unease. The tower stood at the river's elbow, and from its gallery you could see both banks — the cobbled quarter with its guild halls and its shuttered cafés on one side, the flat, poorer streets on the other, and between them the Winter Bridge, gray with frost, carrying its thin traffic of soldiers and people who pretended not to be refugees. In eleven days, men in good coats would sign a document in the record chamber below and decide which nation owned which side of that river. Everyone in the city was doing the arithmetic of their own lives against that date. Mara was doing the arithmetic of a clock.

The fault, she had decided, was in the weight line. The going train ran clean; she had cleaned it, oiled it, reset the escapement with the small brass feelers her uncle Tomas had made her when she was twelve. But the striking train had been dragging for weeks before the stop — a heaviness in the fall of the great weight, a hesitation like a man climbing stairs with a stone in his boot. Something in the channel. Something fouling the wire.

She tied the rope to the beam, tested it twice, and lowered herself into the shaft.

The lantern threw her shadow huge and wavering up the brick. The channel was colder than the room above, a well of trapped winter, and her breath came out in small ghosts. She descended hand over hand, boots braced against the wall, until she reached the shelf where the weight hung — a drum of lead the size of a child's coffin, motionless now, its wire slack. She unhooked the weight and set it on the shelf beside her, and the space it left was a black doorway in the wall.

The fouling, when she found it, was not rust and not a bird's nest.

It was a bundle, wrapped in oilcloth, wired to the channel wall with three turns of soft iron wire — wire that had been twisted by hand, she saw at once, by someone standing exactly where she stood, working quickly, in bad light. The oilcloth was stiff with age but sound. Someone had meant this to last, and someone had meant it to be found, or at least not to be lost; there was a contradiction in the thing that made her hands slow.

She cut the wire with her nippers. The bundle came away heavier than it looked.

* * *

She did not open it in the shaft. She climbed out with it inside her coat, against her ribs, and it was only in the clock room, with the dead mechanism looming over her and the frost ferns on the windows letting in a thin gray light, that she unwrapped it on the bench where she did her finest work.

Inside the oilcloth were two things.

The first was a journal, small, leather-bound, water-stained at one corner. The second was a letter, sealed, and it was the letter that stopped her breath.

It was sealed with wax — good wax, the dark red the commission used — and pressed into the wax were two seals side by side. The first she recognized from her apprenticeship studies: the border-survey office, the crossed calipers over the river bend, the motto ringed around it. The second was not official at all. It was a maker's mark. A gear with eight teeth, and inside the gear, an A.

Almeida. Her family's mark, struck into brass watch cases for three generations, hanging in the window of the workshop on Kova Street, worn on a chain around her mother's neck. Here it was, stamped into the wax of a sealed letter hidden inside the observatory's master clock, beside the seal of the office that had surveyed the border the delegates would soon sign.

She turned the letter over. On the back, in a small, precise hand, was written: *For the record chamber, when the clock is opened. A. — 1911.*

A. Her uncle's initial. Or her grandfather's. Or her mother's.

The clock room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the city made its small cold sounds — a tram, a dog, a man calling across a courtyard — and none of it reached her. She sat down on the bench stool because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

The machinery of time, she thought. Eight years the letter had hung in the dark beside the falling weight, riding up and down, up and down, as the clock struck the hours over the river — struck them, she now understood, with that dragging hesitation she had come to cure. The clock had not been failing. The clock had been carrying something. Every hesitation she had diagnosed as wear was the weight of this paper, this wax, this name, slowing the honest fall of the hours.

She picked up the journal instead. It seemed safer, somehow, than the letter — a dead man's notebook rather than a living secret — and she opened it to the water-stained pages and began, without deciding to, to read.

* * *

*14 September 1911. — The commission dines at the Almeida workshop tonight. I am not invited, which tells me everything. You do not feed the instrument; you feed the man who signs the instrument's readings.*

*My name is Aurel Kess. I am the junior surveyor of the border commission, and I am writing this because I have begun to be afraid, and a man who is afraid should write things down where they cannot be revised.*

*The river has moved. Not the river itself — the river is where God and the floods put it. The map has moved. There is a version of the survey that shows the channel where it was in 1904, before the flood cut the new bend, and that version puts four villages — Vranov, Stibor, the mill settlement, the little one past the mill whose name I can never keep straight — on the eastern side. On the other side. The version I measured puts them here, where they have always been, among people who speak as they speak and bury their dead where their dead lie.*

*The old version is the one being copied out fair for the delegates. I have seen the fair copy. I have held it. The ink is not yet a month old and it is dressed up to look eight years old — tea-stained, dried by a stove, the paper creased and re-creased to suggest handling. Someone has spent real skill on this. It is good work. That is the worst part. It is work done with love, the way a clockmaker loves a movement, and it is a lie from the first stroke to the last.*

*19 September. — The seals. I watched them take an impression of the survey office seal from a document that crossed my desk in July, and I watched the wax — good wax, the dark red — and I understood that I was watching the border be drawn twice: once in the field, once in a warm room by men who would never be cold in their lives.*

*There is a name that keeps appearing. Not the general's, not the commissioner's. A craftsman's name. The one who made the seals, who aged the paper, who did the work no gentleman could do. The others will be pardoned or promoted; that is how these things go. The craftsman will be the one they bury if it ever surfaces. I have written the name in the back of this journal and then crossed it out, and then written it again, because I cannot decide whether writing it protects me or kills me.*

*22 September. — I have made a copy of what matters. Not the whole dossier — I could never move it — but the letter that ties the fair copy to the men who ordered it. Signatures, dates, the instruction to use the 1904 line. I have sealed it with the true seal and the craftsman's mark together, so that no one can claim one without the other. I am going to hide it where only a certain kind of person will ever find it: someone with clean hands and patient hands, someone who opens the things the rest of the city only lives inside.*

*I am putting it in the clock.*

*Let them hang a lie over the river for a few years. Paper waits. Paper is patient. Someday the mechanism will need cleaning, and the man or woman on the rope will be someone who reads, someone who knows a maker's mark from a survey seal, and then the letter will come down out of the dark and do its work.*

*I may not survive to see it. I have been followed twice this week. If this journal reaches anyone, let it reach them with the letter. The journal is the hand; the letter is what the hand holds. One without the other is a rumor. Together they are a verdict.*

*23 September. — Last entry. They have posted me to the far garrison. It is a courtesy, the kind of courtesy that comes before a courtesy of a different kind. I leave the journal in the channel too, wired beside the letter, because a sealed letter alone can be dismissed — a forgery of a forgery, someone will say — but a surveyor's hand, day by day, describing the crime as it was committed, that is harder to wave away. I have done what I can. The rest is in the hands of whoever the clock chooses.*

*The clock chooses slowly. That is its nature. But it chooses.*

* * *

Mara read the last page twice. Then she sat for a long time with the journal closed under her palm, listening to her own heartbeat, which seemed suddenly loud and unreliable in the silence of the stopped clock.

The name crossed out and rewritten in the back of the journal — she turned to it. The ink was brown with age, the strokes dug deep, a man pressing hard enough to hurt the paper. She read it once. She did not read it aloud. She closed the journal gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

Almeida.

Not Tomas. The hand was not her uncle's — she knew his writing the way she knew his tread on the workshop stairs, quick and slanted, always in a hurry to finish. This was slower, rounder, an older hand. Her grandfather's, then. The grandfather who had died when she was six, of whom she kept one memory: being lifted up to see the inside of the observatory clock, the gears turning like a small brass heaven, his beard smelling of oil and tobacco. *Everything honest is made by hand, Marushka,* he had said, or she had decided he said, or the memory had grown that way in the retelling, the way memories do, polishing themselves into lies.

She looked at the letter. The wax held the two seals side by side — the crossed calipers, the eight-toothed gear — and she understood now that the pairing was the whole message. The survey office and the craftsman. The order and the hand. Bound together in one impression, undeniable, the way a movement is bound to its case.

Outside the frosted windows the light had changed; it was past noon. Below her, the city went on waiting for the signing, eleven days out, its streets full of people who did not know that the border they would live or die by had been drawn twice, and that the second drawing was hidden four meters down a shaft in the dark, and that she — Mara Almeida, apprentice clockmaker, seventeen years old, trusted with the tower keys because her family's name was trusted — was the one the clock had chosen.

She thought of her uncle Tomas's absences, the nights he came home with river mud on his boots and no errand he would name. She thought of her mother's locked drawer, and the way the whole house went quiet around it, the way silences in her family were not empty but load-bearing, holding something up, the way this wire had held the bundle to the wall for eight years.

She thought: *if I put it back, wire it up again, say the channel was clean, the fault was wear — no one would ever know. The clock would run. The city would sign. The villages would be erased by ink, and my family would stay whole, and I would go on being the girl who fixed the clock.*

The thought lasted four seconds. She was ashamed of it for the rest of her life, and grateful for it too, because in those four seconds she had measured the true weight of the thing in her hands, the way you weigh a movement before you decide what case will hold it.

She wrapped the letter and the journal back in the oilcloth — not to hide them, but because her hands wanted something familiar to do — and she stood in the clock room of the observatory with the dead mechanism towering over her and the whole divided city spread out below the frost ferns, and she understood that she was no longer only repairing a clock.

Somewhere down on Kova Street, her uncle was winding his watch.

Mara put the bundle inside her coat, against her ribs, where it lay like a second heart, and she picked up her tools, and she began to plan how to carry the truth past the men who had paid her family to bury it — starting with the one man in the city who had authenticated the original survey, and who now swept floors for a living: the dismissed archivist, Elias Varga.
