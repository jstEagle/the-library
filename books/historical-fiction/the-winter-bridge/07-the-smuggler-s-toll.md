## 7. The Smuggler's Toll

Elias Varga knew the river the way a priest knows his liturgy — by repetition, by rote, by the small heresies no one else noticed. He had crossed it every day for eleven years, first as an archivist with a leather satchel and a municipal seal swinging from his watch chain, and now as nothing at all, a man whose name had been struck from the rolls of the city he still loved. The river did not care. The river froze in December and thawed in March and between those months it held its opinions under ice.

He found Ivka where he had been told to find her: not at the ferry landing, which was watched, but two hundred meters downstream where the willows grew out of the embankment like the hair of drowned men. She was kneeling on the ice with an auger, boring through forty centimeters of frozen river as casually as a housewife boring into dough. Steam rose off her shoulders. Her hands were bare.

"You're Varga," she said without looking up. "The one they dismissed."

"I'm the one they dismissed."

"Same thing." She finished the hole. Black water welled up in it, breathing, alive, smelling of mud and winter. She set a weighted line into the gap and began marking the spot with a broken branch. "You want something moved across. That's what everyone wants this month. Everyone wants something moved before the signing, and after the signing nobody wants anything at all except to forget who helped them."

Elias crouched beside her. Up close she was younger than her voice — thirty perhaps, maybe less, the river having taken its toll in lines around her eyes. A scar ran along her jaw like a stitch. "I need a person moved," he said. "Two people. And a crate."

"Crates sink or float. People do both." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were the color of the water in the hole — dark, quick, holding light only briefly. "What's in the crate?"

"Papers."

"Papers." She said it the way other women said *rats*. "Papers are the worst cargo on this river. Brandy floats. Tobacco burns. Papers make soldiers dig holes in the ice looking for them, and then I have no route, and then my family has no bread. Who wrote these papers?"

"A dead surveyor. And several living forgers."

Ivka sat back on her heels. Somewhere upstream, muffled by ice and distance, a garrison bugle sounded the hour — four o'clock, though the sky had already gone the color of dirty pewter. The days were shrinking toward the solstice, and the whole city seemed to be shrinking with them, huddled against the coming signature that would decide, with a penstroke, which side of the line every street and stove and grave would belong to.

"The Almeida girl," Ivka said. "The clockmaker's niece. They say she's been asking after river crossings. Asking badly."

"She asks honestly. It's not the same as badly."

"It's worse." Ivka stood, brushing ice crystals from her skirt. Her boots were wrapped in sacking tied with wire — a smuggler's trick, silent on frozen stone. "Honest people get caught. Dishonest ones pay me. Which is your friend?"

"Honest," Elias said. "Completely. That's why she needs you."

* * *

Mara came down to the willows at dusk, and she hated it before she arrived.

She hated the smell of the river in winter — iron and rot beneath the clean white lie of snow. She hated the way the city's chimneys leaned over the embankment like conspirators. Most of all she hated needing anyone, because needing someone meant handing them a lever, and levers were how families like hers got broken. She had spent three days learning this lesson about herself, ever since Elias had told her there was exactly one person who could move a crate past three checkpoints, and that person was a ferry-woman who worked under the ice.

Ivka was waiting in the shadow of the willows, and Mara saw immediately that she had been measured. It happened in half a second — the flick of dark eyes over her coat, her boots, her guild pin, the calluses on her right hand that marked her as a craftsman and not a clerk. Being measured made her spine stiffen.

"You're the clockmaker's apprentice," Ivka said.

"Journeyman's track. Nearly done."

"On the master clock? The observatory one?"

"Yes."

Something moved behind Ivka's face — not warmth, exactly, but recognition, the look of a woman finding a familiar tool in an unfamiliar place. "My father wound the church clock in our village. Before." She let the word sit there. *Before* was doing heavy work all along this river; everyone used it; no one defined it. "Then you'll understand me when I say this: clocks are honest because someone makes them so. Rivers too. I keep the river honest. Forty centimeters of ice, and under it, everything flows where I say."

"And what does it cost to make things flow where you say?"

Ivka smiled without any pleasure in it. "There it is. The workshop voice. You price things quickly, clockmaker. Good. I hate haggling with amateurs." She turned and walked along the bank, and Mara followed, and Elias trailed behind them both like a man attending his own funeral.

They stopped where a rowboat lay overturned, half-buried in snow, its hull furred with frost. Ivka put her boot on the gunwale.

"My price," she said. "Not money. Money I can take from anyone, and money can be traced, and traced money hangs people. My price is this: when we cross, whatever is in that crate — you read it aloud. To me. On the ice, in the open, word for word. Names, dates, seals, everything."

Mara felt the cold go through her coat as if the coat had been removed. "That's not a price. That's a risk."

"That's the price." Ivka's voice didn't rise at all, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "Do you know what happens on that bridge next week? Delegates come in motorcars. Generals with clean gloves sign papers in a warm room, and the line comes down somewhere near the old mill, and eleven villages go to bed in one country and wake up in another. Eleven villages, clockmaker. And not one soul in them will know what was traded, or for what, or whose name is on the trade. Because paper goes up, and paper comes down, and the people under the paper never see it." She tapped the overturned boat with her boot. "I move contraband under the ice for men who own warehouses. I know exactly what I am. But I will not carry a document across this river blind — not this document, not this week. If power is trading away my side of the water, I will hear it read out loud, in the open air, where the river can witness it. That's my fee. Pay it or find another ferry."

"There is no other ferry," Elias said quietly. "We've looked."

"Then you've already paid," Ivka said. "You just don't know it yet."

* * *

They stood around the auger hole while the light died, and Mara argued with herself in the silence the way she argued with a stubborn movement — patiently, turning the problem over, looking for the tooth that would catch.

Reading the letter aloud meant speaking the names into the air. It meant her family's crime leaving the sealed world of paper and becoming sound, breath, weather — something that could not be taken back. Every instinct her uncle had ever taught her said: *what stays hidden stays safe.* Tomas had built his life on that principle the way she built escapements, tooth by tooth, tolerance by tolerance.

But standing here, on the ice, above the black breathing water, she thought of the letter's weight channel, the way the dead surveyor's journal had interleaved with the forgery, page against page, truth against lie. Documents wanted witnesses. That was their whole nature. A record with no reader was just paper rotting in the dark — and hadn't that been the plan all along? Fifteen years in a weight channel, darkness doing the work of consent.

"All right," she said.

Ivka raised an eyebrow.

"You'll have your reading. On the crossing. Word for word." Mara pulled her mittens tighter, buying a moment with the motion. "But understand what you're asking for. What's in that letter doesn't just condemn my family. It redraws the river. If you hear it, you can't unhear it, and if you're caught knowing it—"

"I've been catchable my whole life," Ivka said. "So has everyone I love. That's rather the point." She crouched and pulled the rowboat free of the snow with a strength that surprised Mara — the hull came up easily, as if the boat weighed nothing, as if the woman had been lifting rivers all her life. "We cross Thursday, before dawn. There's a customs shift change at five, and the ice road past the second sandbar holds until the thaw. Your crate rides in the false bottom. You two ride as my hired hands — a widow's porters, taking her late husband's tools to market. Grief is good cover. No one searches grief."

"And the checkpoints?" Elias asked.

"Checkpoints are doors," Ivka said. "Every door has a man in front of it, and every man has a boredom, a hunger, or a fear. We bring whichever one opens." She looked at Mara, and for a moment the hard ledger of her face went briefly, terribly human. "Your uncle's name opened doors for fifteen years, clockmaker. Time someone's name opened one the other way."

Mara flinched. Elias put a warning hand toward her arm; she shook it off.

"You know who I am. You knew before we came."

"I know everyone on this river. That's my trade, same as gears are yours." Ivka flipped the rowboat onto her shoulder with a grunt. "Don't mistake knowing for judging. I judge one thing: what a person does once they know what their family built. That test hasn't finished with you yet."

She walked off along the bank with the boat riding her shoulder like a yoke, sacking-wrapped boots silent on the frozen stones, and the last light caught the scar on her jaw and made it shine like a seam of silver.

* * *

They walked back through the city separately, as arranged, and Mara took the long way along the embankment because she could not yet bear the workshop's warm yellow windows and the smell of brass polish and her mother's supper waiting behind a door she would have to lie through.

The river lay beside her, black and patient under its ice. In the holes Ivka had bored, the water breathed and shifted, keeping its own counsel. She thought about what the ferry-woman had said — *that test hasn't finished with you yet* — and felt the truth of it settle into her chest like a cold coin.

Her family had forged a border. Her uncle had hidden it inside time itself, inside the great patient heart of the observatory clock, where it ticked away the years above the city like a conscience in reverse. And now the secret had fallen to her — to her hands, her craft, her choice — and the first person she'd had to trust with it was a stranger who ferried contraband under ice and named her fee in truths instead of coins.

It should have felt like ruin. Instead, walking the frozen embankment with the gas lamps stuttering on behind her, Mara felt something she had no proper name for. Not hope. Hope was too clean a word for it. It was closer to the feeling of a mechanism beginning, very slowly, to turn — the great weight starting its fall, the escape wheel releasing its first tooth, everything set in motion and irreversible.

Thursday. Before dawn. The ice road past the second sandbar.

And somewhere across the river, in a warm room full of clean gloves, the delegates were already arriving — which meant that somewhere closer than that, her uncle Tomas had begun, in his quiet and terrible way, to ask questions of his own.
