## 8. Guild of Small Gears

The workshop smelled of brass polish and lamp oil, the way it had smelled every evening of Mara's life, and it was this — not her mother's face, not the question she knew was coming — that made lying feel like a betrayal of physics. Some things were constants. The lathe's hum in memory even when silent. The smell of oil worked into wood until the bench itself had become an instrument. Her mother standing at the window with a cloth in her hands, polishing glass that did not need polishing.

Mara came through the door at half past six, which was correct, which was expected, and hung her coat on the peg where her coat always hung. Under her arm she carried the day's work: two carriage clocks due to a hotelier on the Corso, and beneath them, wrapped in the same flannel, the pendulum crate's remaining fittings — innocent-looking things, a suspension spring, a set of regulating nuts, nothing that would interest anyone who opened her bag. She had spent the walk from Ivka's ferry landing rehearsing simplicity. Simplicity was the whole art of it. A lie, like a mechanism, failed most often from too many parts.

"You're late." Her mother said it without turning from the window.

"Four minutes." Mara set the carriage clocks on the bench and began unwrapping them with the unhurried motions of a woman with nothing else in her bag. "The tram stopped at the garrison gate. They're checking papers now for everyone going east."

"Are they." Her mother turned then, and the cloth went into her apron pocket, folded small, the way she folded everything. She was a narrow woman, gray going on white at the temples, with Tomas's same deep-set eyes and none of his warmth in them — or rather, Mara thought, with all his warmth banked down like a fire raked over for the night, still burning somewhere underneath where no one could see it. "And what did they want with you?"

"My guild card. Everyone's guild card." Mara opened the first carriage clock's case and tilted the movement into the lamplight. A balance staff worn at one pivot; she could hear the roughness before she saw it. "They looked at my photograph for a long time. As if I might have grown a different nose since March."

Her mother made a sound that was almost a laugh. Almost. It died somewhere in her chest, and she crossed the room and put her hand briefly on Mara's shoulder — a touch so light it might have been checking for fever — and went through to the kitchen to put water on.

That was the shape of their evenings: work at the bench, tea at the table, talk that moved along grooves worn smooth by fifteen years of careful use. Talk about the hotelier's clocks. About flour prices. About whether the frost would take the last of the leeks in the courtyard box. Never about the river, except as weather. Never about the signing, except as inconvenience. Never about Uncle Tomas, except as *your uncle will be pleased with the Verdi commission* or *your uncle says the observatory job reflects well on the family*.

Mara fitted a new pivot into the balance staff, cutting it down with gravers until it ran true, and thought: *I have been apprenticed to silence longer than I have been apprenticed to clockmaking.* The realization arrived without drama, the way a worn part announces itself — not by breaking but by a roughness in the sound, a note that had always been there and only now became audible because everything else had gone quiet enough to hear it.

* * *

The kettle sang in the kitchen. Steam fogged the little window over the sink, and beyond the fog the city went on making its small corrupt noises: a cart horse on the cobbles, a drunk singing three streets over, the garrison bugle sounding the retreat hour. Somewhere east, across the frozen river, refugees were burning furniture they couldn't drag behind them, and here the bugle played on schedule, because schedules were what held a city together when its borders wouldn't hold.

Mara carried the tea through and set her mother's cup at her elbow without being asked, and her mother said thank you without looking up from the mending, and between them passed the entire vocabulary of a household that had learned to speak in objects instead of words. A cup set down meant *I am here*. A sleeve darned meant *you are provided for*. A door left open exactly a hand's width meant *I know something is wrong and I am choosing, tonight, not to ask*.

Tonight the door stood a hand's width open.

"The Verdi commission," her mother said, apropos of nothing. "Your uncle wants you to take the lead on the striking train. He says your escapement work is better than his now."

"He's generous."

"He's accurate." Her mother bit her thread and looked up, and for a moment her eyes were very direct, very gray, the eyes of a woman who had spent a war keeping books straight while men lied in ledgers. "He also says you've been to see him less. Since the observatory job."

The lie assembled itself in Mara's hands before her mind finished approving it, the way a trained hand catches a falling tool. "The observatory job is why. There's more wear in that movement than anyone guessed. I've been sleeping at the tower some nights — there's no point walking home at two in the morning with the curfew coming."

"Sleeping at the tower." Her mother repeated it flatly, testing the weight of it, the way Mara tested a suspected counterfeit coin against her teeth.

"The master clock has to run for the signing. Everyone will listen for it. If it strikes wrong during the ceremony—" Mara let the sentence hang, because the sentence was true, and a lie built on a true beam would bear weight better than any fabrication. That was the craft of it. You built deception the way you built a bridge: anchored at both ends in fact.

Her mother looked at her a long moment. Then she bent back to her mending. "Take a blanket from the cedar chest," she said. "The tower will be cold."

And that was the worst of it — not suspicion, but accommodation. Her mother handed her the lie like a key handed across a counter, no questions asked, and Mara understood suddenly that questions were a currency this household had stopped minting years ago. You did not ask where Tomas went at midnight. You did not ask what was in the locked drawer. You did not ask why a survey team had dined at this table in 1904 and never again. You took the blanket from the cedar chest and you let the silence do its work, because the silence kept everyone safe, and safety was the only inheritance on offer.

"I will," Mara said, and heard how easily it came, and hated the ease more than any struggle would have taught her.

* * *

She worked late at the bench because working was the only honest thing left in the house. The second carriage clock wanted a mainspring, and she stripped it and cleaned it and reassembled it under the lamp, and the small bright sounds of the work — the click of the click-spring, the whisper of the mainspring coiling — steadied her the way prayer steadied other people.

Through the wall, the guild hall's evening meeting broke up around nine. She heard the doors, the boots, the voices of masters calling goodnights to one another in the alley. Master Kolarik's laugh, big and easy. Old Ferenc arguing amiably with someone about the new tariff schedule. These were men she had known since childhood, men who had checked her first attempts at filing and told her kindly where her angles ran out. Men whose names — she had seen them now, in Elias Varga's copies, in the dead surveyor's journal — sat on documents that had moved a river on paper and drowned a village in ink.

At ten minutes past nine, three of them came up the outside stair together, stamping snow off their boots, because the workshop took overflow repair work from the guild and Tuesday was collection night. Master Kolarik first, broad and silver-bearded, then Ferenc, then young Master Dorn, who had once shown her how to bush a worn plate and had paid for her first loupe out of his own pocket when her mother couldn't.

"Little Mara." Kolarik filled the doorway the way he filled every doorway. "Still burning midnight oil? Your uncle says you'll ruin your eyes before you're twenty-five."

"Then he can inherit my clients," Mara said, and they laughed, and the laughter was warm and real and made her sick.

Dorn collected the guild's repairs from the shelf, ticking them off against his ledger. Ferenc leaned on the bench and watched her file, offering the unsolicited advice of forty years — *let the tool do it, girl, you fight the steel* — and she nodded and adjusted nothing, because her filing was fine and he knew it and they both knew he knew it, and this too was a groove worn smooth.

"The city's gone mad," Ferenc said comfortably. "Trams searched. Curfew notices on every corner. And for what? A line on a map drawn by men who'll be home for Christmas either way." He shook his head. "My grandfather fished that river. Now they tell me it may be foreign water. Well." He shrugged his great shoulders. "Paper doesn't catch fish."

*Paper doesn't catch fish.* Mara kept her eyes on her file and felt the letter's absence against her ribs like a missing tooth — it wasn't in the house, it was sewn into the pendulum crate at Ivka's landing, but she felt it anyway, the way you feel a wound under bandages. Paper didn't catch fish. Paper had moved the fish to another country's river. Paper had written eleven families out of existence in an afternoon, and the man who signed those pages was leaning on her bench complaining about tariffs.

"Will you go to the signing?" Dorn asked her, friendly. "On the bridge? Everyone's going."

"I'll be at the observatory," Mara said. "Someone has to make sure the clock strikes honestly."

The word was out before she could weigh it. *Honestly.* A hairline crack in the case, invisible unless you knew to look. Kolarik glanced at her — one beat, two — and then laughed and said something about idealists and apprentices, and the moment closed over like water over a dropped coin.

But she had seen the glance. And she filed it away with a craftsman's precision: Master Kolarik, glance, duration two seconds, cause unknown, disposition uncertain. She was learning to keep books on the men who kept books.

They left at half past nine, boots on the stairs, goodnights called down the alley. Through the window Mara watched them go — three solid citizens, collars up against the cold, and Dorn pausing to wind his pocket watch by the streetlamp before walking on. She watched his thumb turn the crown, deliberate, punctual, a man setting his timepiece against the city's time as though the city's time were a thing that could be trusted, as though time itself were neutral ground.

*Men who forged a border still wind their watches punctually.* The thought settled over her with a terrible calm. That was the discovery underneath all the others, worse than the forged seals, worse than her uncle's name in the margin: that corruption was not a fever, not a darkness, not anything that showed in the face. It was ordinary. It wound its watch. It gave a girl her first loupe. It complained about tariffs and worried about its eyes and laughed easily in doorways. Evil, if that was the word, kept excellent time.

* * *

Her mother came to the bench door at eleven, shawl over her nightdress, and stood watching her a moment in silence.

"It's done," Mara said, holding up the carriage clock. "Runs true. I'll deliver it tomorrow."

Her mother nodded. Then, instead of going to bed, she came and stood beside the bench and looked down at the movement, at the little train of wheels Mara had just set right, and her face in the lamplight was unreadable as a blank dial.

"When your father died," her mother said, "your uncle took this shop's debts onto himself. All of them. He was a journeyman then; he had nothing. He sold his tools — his good tools, the ones his own master left him — and he never spoke of it, and he paid off every creditor within the year." She touched the bench edge, lightly, the way she touched everything: as if confirming it was real. "Whatever you think you know about your uncle, remember what he has carried. Remember who carried it so you wouldn't have to."

"I know what he's carried," Mara said quietly.

"Do you." It was not quite a question. Her mother looked at her one moment longer — and there it was again, that directness, that gray level gaze, as though a message were being passed across a counter in code, each word a coin tested for weight — and then she wished her goodnight and went down the hall, and Mara heard the lock of the bedroom turn, soft as a breath.

The drawer was behind that lock. The letters were behind that lock. Her mother's knowledge, whatever shape it took, slept behind a lock no bigger than a thumbnail, and Mara stood in the lamplight with her heart going hard and thought: *tomorrow*. Tomorrow she would find the key or pick the lock, because she could not carry the crate across the river carrying this other weight too, unexamined. One truth at a time. One wheel at a time. That was how you rebuilt a movement that had been running false for years — you did not shake the whole mechanism at once. You removed one part, you studied it, you understood what it had been doing and why, and only then did you decide whether it could be made honest or had to be replaced.

She blew out the lamp. In the dark, the repaired carriage clock ticked on the shelf, keeping perfect time in a house full of crooked hours, and downstairs in the alley a garrison patrol went by, boots crunching the fresh snow, four men and a lantern, on schedule, punctual as sin.

Tomorrow the crate moved to the second checkpoint. Tomorrow her name — the respected name, the guild name, the name on the photograph the soldiers studied — would have to open a gate that ought to stay shut. She lay in the dark listening to the clock and understood, with the clarity of three in the morning, that she had already chosen. Not the letter. Not the truth. She had chosen the lie she'd told her mother's face, the first of its kind, smooth-running, well-oiled, and she could feel already how easily such mechanisms multiplied — how one small deception, properly fitted, invited the next, gear meshing into gear, until the whole train of them was turning and could not be stopped without breaking something.

Across the river, in a boathouse that smelled of tar and ice, a ferry-woman was sleeping with a rifle within reach of her hand, guarding a crate full of pendulum and paper. In a rented room above the chandlery, a disgraced archivist was copying names by candlelight, building the case he'd failed to build fifteen years ago. And here, in the house of the man who had forged the border, his niece lay awake learning to lie, and the clock struck twelve below her window — one clear note, slightly flat, the way it had been slightly flat for years, and no one in the city remarked on it anymore, because a wrong note sounded long enough becomes the note everyone expects.

Two days to the signing.
