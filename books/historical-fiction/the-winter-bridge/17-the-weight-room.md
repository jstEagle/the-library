## 17. The Weight Room

The weight room of the observatory tower was a shaft of stone and darkness that went down into the building's bones like a well. Mara had known it since she was nine years old, when her uncle first lowered her on a bosun's chair to clean the great driving weights — three iron cylinders, each the size of a man, each hung on a cable thick as her wrist, descending into their channels in the rock below. They were the clock's heart in a way no gear could be: all the intricate arithmetic above, every escapement and striking train, existed only to spend these weights slowly, meter by meter, week by week, converting gravity into time.

She came down the ladder now with the letter pressed flat against her chest, inside her coat, over her heart, and the cold of the stone came up through her boots like a warning.

Above her, through the floor hatch, she could hear the clock still striking wrong — the hour she had not yet corrected, the false count Tomas had fed into the mechanism, buying himself minutes the way other men bought bread. Seven strokes where there should have been ten. The sound fell down the shaft after her, pooling in the dark.

"Mara."

His voice came from below. He was already there. Of course he was already there; he had wound this clock for thirty years, and there was no part of this tower his feet did not know better than she did.

She climbed down the last rungs and turned.

The weight room was lit by a single lantern set in an iron bracket, and in its light her uncle stood among the hanging weights like a man standing among executed giants. He had his coat on — his good coat, the one he wore to guild functions — and in his right hand, held low but not carelessly, was the long assembly wrench they used for the great nuts of the winding drum. Iron, a meter long, weighted at the head.

"Tomas." She kept her voice level. She had learned that from him too: how a craftsman speaks when the work is going badly. Calm hands, calm voice, even as the metal fights you.

"You opened the clock," he said.

"I repaired the clock. That was my summons. You know it was."

"The case was sealed." He took one step, and the lantern light moved across his face, carving it. "There is a seal I put on that channel fifteen years ago, and it is broken, and the wax is in your pocket along with whatever else you took out of my clock."

*My clock.* She heard it and understood everything at once — that he had never thought of it as the city's clock, or the observatory's, or even the family's. It was his. He had built his silence into it, bolt by bolt, and for fifteen years the most honest machine in the city had been lying on his behalf.

"I know what's in the letter," she said.

Something crossed his face then — not surprise. Grief, maybe, arriving late, the way grief does in practical men. "Then you know what it cost," he said. "And you've come down here anyway. Into the one room where I am stronger than you."

"I've come down here because you'll listen better without witnesses."

He almost smiled. She saw it try to form and fail. "You always did think you knew me."

"I know you taught me that a mechanism tells you the truth if you're patient enough to read it. I know you made me check my work twice because *the work outlives the worker*. Your words. Every Sunday. You wrote them on the shop wall."

"The wall doesn't sign armistices," Tomas said. "Walls don't get asked what language a child will be beaten in. Sit down, Mara."

"There's nothing to sit on."

"Then stand," he said, "and listen, because I am only going to say this once, and then we are both going to do something we will regret for the rest of our lives, and I would prefer we regret only one thing between us."

* * *

Tomas Almeida had raised the wrench because his body had decided before his mind could vote.

That was the truth of it, and he held onto the truth of it the way a drowning man holds a spar. His hands had done it — the same hands that had filed her first pinions when she was small, that had guided hers over a file until the cut ran true, that had braided her hair once, badly, the winter her mother took to her bed and the shop went quiet — those hands had gone to the tool rack when he heard her boots on the tower stairs, and closed around the wrench, and now here he stood, fifty-eight years old, in the room where he had spent half his life alone with falling iron, holding a weapon against a girl who had his dead brother's chin and his own stubbornness and none of his sense.

He had imagined this moment, in the two days since he'd learned the seal was broken. He had imagined it as necessity. That was the word he'd used, walking the guild corridors, asking his careful questions: *necessity*. The family needed protecting. The family was the only country he had ever believed in, and countries, he had learned in the war, are preserved by men willing to do the arithmetic others refuse.

But arithmetic required numbers, and the number standing in front of him now was twenty-three years old and had freckles in winter and bit her lower lip exactly the way his wife had when she was concentrating, and no column of figures he had ever kept balanced had prepared him for the weight of that.

"Say it, then," Mara said. "Whatever you brought the wrench to prevent me from hearing. Say it."

So he said it. All of it, or nearly — the year the survey commission dined at their table, the general with the soft voice and the hard eyes, the night in the back room of the custom house when the map lay open under lamplight and a man named Ferenc laid the tracing film across it and said, *the river moves east, gentlemen, and everyone at this table becomes a landowner.* He told her about the seals — real seals, taken from a drunk archivist's unlocked cabinet, pressed into warm wax while the wine went round. He told her about the surveyor, Brenner, who had kept notes, who had grown a conscience too slowly, who had died of a fever in a wet spring that Tomas had never entirely stopped disbelieving.

"And the clock?" Her voice was very steady. Steadier than his. "Why the clock?"

"Because it was ours." He heard how it sounded and made himself finish. "Because I built the case, and I knew the channel, and because a man trusts the thing he made. Because every week I climbed those stairs to wind it, and every week I passed the weights, and I thought: *this is the price, and I am paying it steadily, like the clock pays out its line.* I thought if I did it regularly enough it would become ordinary. That's what craft is, Mara. Making the terrible repeatable."

"That's what a machine is," she said. "Craft is making the true repeatable. You taught me the difference. You just forgot which side of it you were standing on."

The wrench was heavy. He became aware of it again, its cold seeping through his glove, and he realized he had been holding it up — not threateningly, not yet, but up, the way a man holds a tool when the work has stalled — and that his daughter-niece-girl was looking at it, and at him, with an expression he had seen on her mother's face only once, the day she found the locked drawer and chose, visibly, deliberately, in front of him, not to ask.

"Put it down, Uncle."

"If I put it down—"

"You won't lose anything by putting it down that you won't lose forever by lifting it."

He looked at the wrench. It was a good tool. He had owned it longer than he had owned her. And he understood, with the terrible clarity that comes to men only at the exact moment it is useless, that the choice was not between using it and not using it. The choice had been made years ago, in a lamplit back room, and everything since — the winding, the sealing, the silences at dinner, the locked drawer upstairs where his wife kept her own share of the shame — everything had just been the long slow payout of the line, gravity doing what gravity does, and there was no gear train in the world that could wind it back.

His arms came down. The wrench head knocked softly against his thigh, once, twice, and then he set it on the stone ledge beside the winding drum, precisely, aligned parallel to the edge, because even now, even here, his hands wanted order.

Mara let out a breath that shook only a little.

"Now watch me," she said, and took the letter from inside her coat.

* * *

The letter was smaller than the room deserved. That was Mara's first thought, unfolding it flat on the winding-drum ledge under the lantern: that so much ruin should weigh so little. Four pages, a tracing fragment, seals gone brown as old blood. Above them the clock ticked on, spending its weights, indifferent.

"What are you doing," Tomas said. Not a question. A man watching a hand move toward a live wire.

"The record chamber," she said. "Below the dial. Where the strike counts are logged — where the observatory posts the official time for the whole city, every day, in the public ledger. The striking train passes through it. Everything the clock announces passes through that room."

"Yes," he said slowly. "I know what the record chamber is. I built the shelf the ledger sits on."

"Then you know that tomorrow morning, when the delegates sign, the chamber will be full of them. The commission keeps its papers there during the session — Elias confirmed it. The one room in this city where every faction agreed the record must be beyond tampering." She almost laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "They trusted your clock, Uncle. Everyone trusts your clock. That was always the beauty of it. So we'll give them something worth trusting."

"You hang that letter in the train," Tomas said, "and the weights will carry it up with the strike. The hammers' lift wires. It'll go into the chamber with the hour."

"With the true hour. I reset the count before I came down. Ten o'clock, struck honestly, for the first time in two days — and your secret rising on the same cables that announce it." She was working as she talked, and he recognized the mode, the craftsman's absorption, her fingers quick and sure: measuring the letter, folding it into a narrow packet, selecting from her apron pocket a length of fine brass wire and a clip of the kind used to hold timing slips to the lift wires. "You taught me this, too. Any load carried by the train must be balanced, or the strike goes false. So I balance it. Small packet, light wire, clear of the hammer arcs. It'll ride up clean and drop free in the chamber. I've calculated it four times."

"Mara." His voice cracked on her name. "Once it rises, nothing stops it. No one can reach the chamber before the delegates. You understand? There is no taking it back. They will hang men for this. They may hang me."

"I know."

"They will take the workshop. Your mother's name will be dragged through every tribunal in two provinces. The guild will strike you off before your journeyman's certificate is dry. Everything I built — everything I *stole*, yes, I know the word — everything will burn, and you will be standing in the ash with nothing but the truth, and the truth, girl, is a poor apprentice. It earns nothing. It feeds no one. It will not keep you warm."

"No," she said. "But it will keep thousands of people on the right side of a river. And it will keep the clock honest. And when I'm an old woman telling apprentices to check their work twice because the work outlives the worker, I'll be able to say it without choking."

She reached up to the lift wire.

And Tomas Almeida, who had come down the tower stairs ahead of her with a wrench in his coat, who had spent two days hunting the leak through the guild with cold methodical love, who had offered her the workshop and the legacy and the whole warm lie of the family name — Tomas watched his niece's hands rise toward the striking train and found that he could not move.

Not would not. Could not. It was the strangest paralysis of his life, standing in the one room where he was master, watching the one act that would destroy him, and being unable to lift a finger against it — because every finger he lifted would be lifted against her, and somewhere in the last quarter hour, among the falling weights, that arithmetic had finally refused to balance.

"You could stop me," she said quietly, threading the wire. "You still could. One shout. The garrison heard the curfew bell; they're sweeping the tower. One word and I'm arrested with contraband and you're a grieving uncle instead of a conspirator."

"Don't," he said.

"It's the smart play. You always taught me to find the smart play."

"*Don't.*"

His voice broke apart on the word, and in the lantern light she saw what it had cost him to climb down here at all — not to stop her, perhaps. Perhaps he had come down to be stopped. Perhaps some part of him had been climbing these stairs for fifteen years, waiting for someone to catch the weight before it landed.

She clipped the packet to the lift wire. Tested it with two fingers. Balanced. True.

Then she did the last thing he expected. She took his hand — the right one, the wrench hand, still cold from the iron — and pressed it flat against the cable, so that he felt it: the faint, patient tremor of the great weight far below, waiting, ready to fall, ready to carry.

"Do you feel it?" she said. "It's been falling for thirty years, Uncle. We just finally get to choose what it lifts on the way down."

Above them, faint through the stone, came boots on the tower stairs — many boots, descending fast, and the muffled voice of Lieutenant Brandt calling the observatory to open in the name of the provisional commission.

Mara stepped back from the train. The clock gathered itself. Somewhere overhead, gears that had lied for two days prepared to tell the truth, and the first hammer began to lift.

"Ten o'clock," she whispered. "Strike true."

The weights started to move.
