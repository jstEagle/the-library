## 3. Grey Uniforms at the Harbor

Mrs. Okafor had kept the town's books for nineteen years, and in all that time she had learned that a ledger tells the truth only when no one is watching it. She rose before dawn on the last Tuesday of November to make the pages honest—smoothing the ink where old Mr. Brandt had pressed too hard, correcting the spelling of the Halvorsen girls' names, which their father had never once managed to render the same way twice. The census was due to the county by the first of December, and she intended it to be perfect: every soul in Sker Point accounted for, every birth and death and marriage entered in her small, upright hand.

She did not know, as she warmed her fingers over the stove and blew the dust from the ledger's spine, that she would be the last person in the town to hold that book as its keeper. She did not know that by nightfall the names she tended like a garden would belong to the army.

The fog came in off the water at half past seven, thick as wool, and with it came the sound of engines—not the familiar chug of the supply boat *Petrel*, but something heavier, deeper, a sound the harbor had never made before. Mrs. Okafor stood at her window with her tea going cold and watched grey shapes resolve out of the fog: two trawlers flying the White Ensign, and behind them a steam pinnace riding low with soldiers packed shoulder to shoulder along its gunwales, their rifles standing between their knees like saplings planted in rows.

They came ashore at the stone quay in silence. That was the thing she would remember afterward—the silence of it. No officer shouting orders, no boots hammering the planks, just forty men stepping onto Sker Point's oldest stones as though they had always been there, as though the town had simply failed to notice them until now. The fog swallowed their breath. Their uniforms were the color of the sea in January, the color of the sky before snow, the color of nothing anyone could argue with.

Mrs. Okafor set down her cup. She took the ledger from its shelf, and then—she could not have said why—she put it back, and instead wrapped herself in her good coat, the one with the fox collar her husband had bought her in Hull before he died, and went out into the street.

By eight o'clock half the town had gathered at the harbor mouth, drawn the way people are drawn to any event that promises to change the shape of their lives while leaving them unable to stop it. They stood in knots of three and four, not speaking much, the fishermen with their caps in their hands, the women with shawls pulled tight. Ansel Iversen had come down from the lighthouse; she saw him standing apart, tall and still as his own tower, his face giving away nothing. Old Greta Lindqvist stood near him, her knuckles white around her walking stick. And somewhere in the crowd, though Mrs. Okafor would not see her until later, young Tomas Eriksen had positioned himself where he could see everything—which was, she thought later, very like Tomas.

Major Aldous Vane stepped off the pinnace last.

He was not what she expected. She had imagined someone loud, someone red-faced, a man who would need to fill the harbor with his voice the way the soldiers filled it with their numbers. Vane was none of these things. He was perhaps fifty, lean, with a face like a folded map—all creases and precision—and he walked the length of the quay reading the town the way she read her ledgers: thoroughly, without sentiment, missing nothing. His eyes went over the boats first, counting them. Then the fish sheds. Then the crowd, face by face, and Mrs. Okafor felt his gaze pass over her like a hand checking pockets.

He stopped at the harbormaster's office—a building of one room and a great deal of dust—and a sergeant nailed a paper to the door with four blows of a hammer that sounded, in the foggy quiet, like gunshots.

Then Major Vane turned to the town and read it aloud.

*By order of the Admiralty and under the Defence of the Realm Consolidation Act, the harbor of Sker Point, its moorings, vessels, sheds, and approaches are hereby requisitioned for His Majesty's service. All civilian traffic is suspended until further notice. All vessels will be registered, inspected, and subject to search. The inhabitants of Sker Point will furnish such assistance as may be required and will observe such regulations as shall be posted.*

He lowered the paper. He looked at them with an expression that was not unkind, which somehow made it worse.

"The war has come to your doorstep," he said. "I am sorry for the inconvenience. I am not sorry for the necessity."

Nobody answered. The fog dripped off the rigging of the *Maren*, Henrik Dahl's boat, and the sound of it was the only dissent in the harbor.

* * *

They came for the ledger at ten o'clock.

Mrs. Okafor had gone back to her office above the chandlery, telling herself that the requisition concerned boats and harbors, not books and names, and that whatever happened out there on the water, her pages were her own. She had the census open on her desk, the ink dry at last, when the knock came—two knocks, precise, spaced like the ticks of a good clock.

Major Vane did not wait to be invited fully in. He entered the way weather enters through a bad window: politely, inevitably. The sergeant stayed outside. Vane removed his cap, which surprised her, and set it on the corner of her desk, which surprised her more.

"Mrs. Okafor. The town clerk." He read the name off a list he carried. "You have kept the records here for some years."

"Nineteen."

"Then you'll appreciate efficiency." He held out his hand, palm up, an open request that was not a request at all. "The register of inhabitants. Births, deaths, marriages, residency. The full census."

She looked at his hand. It was a clean hand, a careful hand, with ink stains at the side of the middle finger that told her he wrote a great deal and wrote it himself.

"That book belongs to the parish," she said. "To the town. Those are our people in those pages. Their christenings. Their dead."

"And now they are also the Crown's people," Vane said, "in a war zone, on a coast the enemy mines and maps and watches. Names are how a nation knows itself, madam. In wartime, they are also how it survives." He did not move his hand. "I am not asking you to surrender anything that isn't already owed."

And there it was—the word *owed*, laid down flat between them like a coin on a counter, and both of them understanding that it was not a coin but a hook. Mrs. Okafor had spent nineteen years teaching this town that its debts were real, that its obligations were written down and honored. She could hardly begin refusing the principle now because she disliked the creditor.

But her hands would not move toward the book. She heard herself say, "Some of those families have German blood in them. Distant. Old marriages, old names. Lindqvist, Eriksen, Brandt—half the coast carries something like that if you go back far enough. What will you do with them?"

Vane regarded her for a long moment, and she had the uncomfortable sense of being entered into a column somewhere behind his eyes.

"What I do with information depends entirely on what the information does," he said. "A name is innocent until it isn't. You keep records, Mrs. Okafor. You know better than most that a fact harms no one. It is what men decide facts mean that does the harm." He paused. "I would rather have accurate pages than frightened guesses. Give me the book, and I promise you the town's story stays in a careful hand."

It was a good speech. She would think about it for days, turning it over, looking for the flaw the way she looked for a misposted entry, and finding it only in the fact that a man who spoke so beautifully about care had arrived with forty soldiers and a nail gun for his notices.

She gave him the ledger.

He received it with both hands, the way one receives a child or a relic, and that courtesy was the cruelest part. He turned to the flyleaf, where her predecessor had written *Sker Point Parish Register, 1861* in faded brown ink, and beneath it, in her own hand, added twenty years ago: *continued by Adaeze Okafor, clerk.* He read her name. Something moved across his face—recognition, perhaps, of another person who lived inside record-keeping—and then it closed again like a drawer.

"You will remain clerk," he said. "The army requires copies, extracts, verification. You will be summoned when needed and compensated for your time." He settled his cap back on his head. "You have done this work well. I can see that. Which is precisely why I need it."

After he left, she sat for a long time looking at the empty space on her shelf where the ledger had lived, a rectangle of unfaded wood, cleaner than everything around it, the way a scar is cleaner than skin.

* * *

By afternoon the harbor had changed its nature entirely.

Soldiers strung wire along the north pier. A sentry box appeared at the harbor mouth, built from fish-shed planks requisitioned within an hour of the notice being posted—Henrik Dahl's shed, taken apart board by board while Henrik stood watching with his arms folded, saying nothing, his jaw working like a man chewing on words too large to swallow. The fishing fleet was impounded at anchor under guard; the *Maren*'s crew stood on the quay watching a soldier paint a white registration number on their hull, and Mrs. Okafor saw old Søren Brandt turn away from the sight and walk home with his shoulders up around his ears.

The boats were the town's living, and the town understood immediately that its living had been conscripted. But it was not the boats that broke something open. It was smaller than that.

It was Mrs. Eriksen coming out of the bakery and finding a soldier copying down the names painted on the cottage doors. It was the notice pasted to the church door requiring all residents to report changes of address, guests, letters received from abroad. *Letters received from abroad.* Mrs. Okafor read that line twice, and as she read it she felt, rather than saw, the crowd around the church door rearrange itself—a half-step back here, a turned shoulder there, neighbors who had stood hip to hip against the cold suddenly standing slightly apart from one another, each privately calculating what the word *abroad* might cost, and whom.

Greta Lindqvist read the notice without expression. Then she took out her spectacles, polished them on her scarf, and read it again, slowly, the way one reads a diagnosis. Mrs. Okafor watched her and thought of Marta—Marta Lindqvist, married seven years now to a German naval officer, her letters arriving twice a year like rare birds, shown proudly at first and then, after August 1914, not mentioned at all. The whole town knew about Marta. The whole town had agreed, without ever discussing it, to stop knowing about Marta. That was how Sker Point survived things: it forgot on purpose, collectively, gently, the way a body forgets an old pain.

Now the army had arrived with a machine for remembering.

"They'll want to know who gets letters," Greta said quietly, to no one, to everyone, folding her spectacles away. "That's what this is. A net for letters."

No one answered her. That silence, too, was new. Before Tuesday, someone would have said *nonsense, Greta, we look after our own*. After Tuesday, everyone was busy wondering whether *we look after our own* was now a punishable sentence.

* * *

In the evening Mrs. Okafor was summoned to the harbormaster's office, which the army had already made its own—a map of the coast pinned over the harbormaster's dusty chart, a lamp turned up bright, the smell of pipe tobacco where no one had smoked pipes before. Two clerks in uniform sat at trestle tables making fair copies of her ledger, page by page, and seeing her own handwriting reproduced in military ink produced in her a feeling she could not immediately name. It was violation, yes. But underneath the violation was something more unsettling: pride. They were copying her work because it was good. Her nineteen years of careful entries had made the taking possible.

Major Vane stood at the map with a cup of tea—he drank tea, she noted absurdly, the ordinary human detail of it—and beckoned her over.

"Your records show two hundred and eleven souls resident in Sker Point," he said. "Is that current?"

"As of this morning."

"There are discrepancies I'd like resolved. Three men listed as fishermen whose boats are registered elsewhere. A widow listed at the Brandt cottage who died in March, unless your death register is wrong." He tapped the page. "And a household entry I find incomplete. Greta Lindqvist, resident alone. Yet I'm told she had a niece. Married. Living abroad." He said it lightly, conversationally, a man discussing weather. "Where does the niece appear in your records?"

Mrs. Okafor kept her face still. Nineteen years of practice.

"Marta Lindqvist left Sker Point in 1909 upon her marriage," she said. "She ceased to be a resident. The register records departures, Major. It doesn't follow the departed."

"Of course." He smiled, and it was a genuine smile, warm even, and she understood that this was the most dangerous thing about him—that he could be warm. "Does she write?"

"I wouldn't know."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't." He turned back to his map. "Thank you, Mrs. Okafor. You'll be called on again, I expect. Good night."

Walking home through streets already darkened by the new blackout regulations—curtains drawn, windows blind, the town hiding from the sky it had lived under openly for four hundred years—Mrs. Okafor passed the lighthouse and saw Ansel Iversen's light burning steady above the point, sweeping its slow white arm across the water, obedient to nothing yet except the old law of the sea.

She thought about the empty shelf in her office. She thought about Greta's face at the church door. She thought about the half-steps backward, the turned shoulders, the small cold distances opening between people who had shared herring nets and funerals and winters for generations.

And she thought, with a clerk's terrible clarity, that the ledger had never contained anything so dangerous as the truth, and that the truth was this: a town is only its trust, and today the army had come ashore and begun, very politely, very carefully, to count it.

Two days later, the summons came again. Not for her—for Greta Lindqvist. And Mrs. Okafor, watching the old woman walk up the quay alone toward the office with the nailed paper on its door, understood that the next entries in the history of Sker Point would be written in a hand none of them controlled.
