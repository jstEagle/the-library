## 11. Tomas Sees Too Much

The wind had turned in the night, swinging round from the northeast with a smell of iron and old ice, and by morning the whole harbor smelled of it—of cold metal, of the war that had come to sit among them like a guest who would not leave. Tomas Eriksen stood at the end of the pier in his army greatcoat, too big for him at the shoulders, the sleeves rolled twice, and watched the grey water heave against the pilings. His boots were new. That was the thing he kept coming back to, standing there in the wind: his boots were new, and they did not leak, and when he walked the sentry route along the quay at six each morning the stones no longer found the holes in his soles. Sixteen years of wet feet, ended by a uniform.

He was on his way to the signal station above the point when he saw the smoke.

It was not the lighthouse chimney—that stood where it always stood, its thread of pale woodsmoke rising straight as a plumb line in the still air between gusts. This was different. A darker smudge, low behind the keeper's cottage, in the lee of the coal shed where the wind could not carry it away before someone noticed. Smoke like that had a color to it. Paper smoke. He had seen paper burn before; every boy in Sker Point had burned something—a letter from a girl, a schoolbook page, a prayer he was ashamed of—and the smoke always came up thin and grey-blue and greedy, nothing like the fat white breath of a coal fire.

Tomas stopped at the foot of the path and told himself he was seeing nothing. He was good at that now. He had learned it from Major Vane, or rather he had learned its opposite from Major Vane and then taught himself the first part in self-defense: how to look at a thing and let it pass through you without catching. Vane looked at everything and caught everything. That was his trade. Tomas's trade, officially, was observation—the word made him feel important, *observation*, written in the major's own hand on the duty roster beside his name—but privately he had begun to understand that what the major actually paid for was not seeing but telling. The seeing anyone could do. The telling was the work.

He climbed the path anyway. He told himself it was because the signal station lay that way, because the route passed the cottage, because a soldier followed his route. All true. None of it the truth.

* * *

Ansel Iversen was kneeling at the mouth of the coal shed with a tin pail at his side, feeding papers into a small fire he had built against the shed wall, and he did not hear Tomas until the boy's boot crunched on the frozen cinder path. Then the keeper turned—not quickly, not guiltily, or so it seemed at first—and for one long moment the two of them regarded each other across the smoke, the man kneeling and the boy standing, the fire muttering between them like a third party with an opinion.

"Morning, Tomas."

"Morning, Mr. Iversen." His voice came out higher than he wanted. "Cold for burning things."

"It's never warm enough for burning things." Ansel fed another folded page into the flames. The paper caught, curled, went black at the edges, and for half a second before the heat took it Tomas saw handwriting—faint blue ink, a woman's hand, the letters small and slanted and foreign-looking, though he could not have said why they looked foreign except that they were not the blunt upright script of the town clerk's ledger, which was the only handwriting he had studied closely in his life.

His stomach did something complicated. It dropped and lifted at once, the way it did on the swing of the harbor crane, and he understood, in the space of that single burning page, exactly what he was looking at. Letters. Letters from across the water. Letters with German ink in them, in this winter, in this town, under this occupation, being destroyed by the one man whose light the navy now owned.

"You're burning letters," he said. Stupidly. As if the man might deny it, might laugh and say *old invoices, lad, accounts from the chandlery,* and give them both a door to walk through.

Ansel looked at him. The keeper's face was weathered down to almost nothing—grey eyes, grey stubble, the deep lines around his mouth that seven years of widowhood and thirty years of salt air had carved—but his eyes were not vague. They were terribly clear, and they were looking at Tomas the way a man looks at a scale he is waiting to see which way will tip.

"I am," Ansel said. "They were mine to burn."

"They're from Germany." The words left Tomas before he could weigh them, and he heard in his own voice the other voice, the one he had been practicing in the barracks mirror, the clipped official tone of the reports he dictated to Sergeant Hale while Hale wrote them down in longhand because Tomas's own hand was still a schoolboy's scrawl. *Subject observed. Subject approached. Subject admitted.* He heard himself becoming a report and hated it and could not stop it. "You know what the major said about correspondence. Anything from enemy territory has to be surrendered for inspection. It's—it's regulation nine. I wrote it down myself."

"I expect you did." Ansel put another letter on the fire. He did not hurry. That was the worst of it, Tomas thought later; the man did not hurry, did not glance over his shoulder toward the point where the soldiers' hut stood, did not do any of the things a guilty man does. He knelt in the cinders like a farmer planting, patient as winter, and fed the fire. "And what will you write about it?"

The question hung there. Below them the sea worked at the rocks with its slow patient hatred, and somewhere inland a dog barked twice and gave up, and the smoke rose straight up into the windless gap between gusts, a grey finger pointing at nothing, at everything.

"I'd have to report it," Tomas said. "If I saw it."

"But you haven't seen it yet. You're still deciding whether to see it." Ansel's voice was not unkind. That was somehow worse than if it had been angry. Anger he could have carried back to the station like a trophy, could have set before Vane: *he threatened me, sir, he tried to buy me.* Kindness left nothing to carry. "That's a hard place to stand, lad. Between seeing and saying. I wouldn't wish it on a dog."

"I'm not a dog."

"No," Ansel said. "You're not." He reached into the tin pail and took out the last letter—or the last that Tomas could see, though the pail still had weight to it, still shifted with a soft paper rustle when the keeper moved—and he held it a moment without putting it in the fire, and Tomas saw that his hands were not steady after all. The steadiness had been a coat he wore, and here at the end of it, with the last of whatever these letters were, the coat had slipped. "Her name was Marta Lindqvist," the keeper said, to the fire more than to the boy. "She grew up two streets from your grandmother's house. She used to swim off the shingle in June when the water was barely above freezing, and her mother would stand on the bank wrapped in a blanket shouting at her to come out, and she never came out until she was ready. That's the kind of person who writes these letters. Someone who doesn't come out until she's ready."

Tomas said nothing. The tide of memory was coming in fast and he could not stop it.

* * *

He was eleven. It was October, three years after his father drowned off the Herring Grounds, and his mother had gone quiet in the way she went quiet before the bad winters, sitting by the window with her hands in her lap as if her body were a house she had already moved out of. Tomas had gone down to the point to be alone with his anger, which was the only possession he owned outright, and the sea had been running high after two days of southwest gale, and he had gone too far out on the black rocks below the lighthouse to prove—to whom, to himself, to his dead father, to God, he had never settled the question—that he was not afraid of anything.

The wave took him without malice. That was the thing he remembered most clearly all these years after: the sea had not even bothered to be cruel. It simply reached up the rock like a landlord collecting rent and pulled him off, and the cold closed over his head like a door, and the world became green noise and pressure and the taste of his own blood where his chin struck stone.

Then a hand. A forearm like a hawser closing around his chest, and the terrible dragging lift, and air, and the flat grey sky, and Ansel Iversen's face above him, streaming, furious, afraid. The keeper had seen him from the gallery—had been watching the swell from the tower, as he watched it every day of his life, because watching was his trade before it was ever Tomas's—and had come down the rock path at a run no man his age should have managed. He had hauled Tomas up the shingle and laid him out and pressed the seawater out of him with both fists below the ribs, and when Tomas finally coughed and wept and shook, the keeper had sat down beside him in the wet gravel and put his coat around them both and said nothing at all for a long time.

Then, at last: "Your mother can't lose you too. Not yet. Not for a long time yet." And he had walked Tomas home through the dusk, holding his shoulder, and had told Mrs. Eriksen a version of events in which her son had slipped on the path and gotten a soaking, nothing worse, boys will be boys, and had refused the coin she pressed on him, and had gone back up to his light.

Tomas had never thanked him. He had meant to. He had composed the thanks a hundred times in his head on the walk to school, on the boats, in the years after, and every time the words had seemed too small for the debt, and so he had said nothing, and the nothing had grown a skin around itself and become permanent, the way silences do in that town. Everyone knew the story—Mrs. Okafor had entered it in some private ledger of hers, the ledger of things the town knew and did not say—and everyone pretended it had not happened, including, most thoroughly, Tomas.

Now he stood on the cinder path with a soldier's coat on his back and the memory of that arm around his chest, and the last letter went into the fire, and Ansel Iversen fed the flames until there was nothing but ash, and stirred the ash into the cinders with a length of iron rod until even the ash was gone.

"There," the keeper said quietly. "Now you haven't seen anything at all."

* * *

Tomas walked the rest of his route in a fog that had nothing to do with the weather. The signal station came and went; he logged the barometer, copied the wind readings, initialed the sheet. Sergeant Hale asked if anything was to report and Tomas heard himself say, "Nothing, sergeant," in a voice that sounded steady from the outside and felt, from the inside, like the moment after the wave takes you—green noise, pressure, the taste of blood.

*Nothing.* The word sat in him like a swallowed stone.

Because here was the arithmetic, and he could not make it come out. If he reported the burning, Ansel Iversen would be arrested by nightfall. He knew how it went; he had watched it go, four times now, with the fishermen and the shopkeeper Dahl and old Nils the netmender, men taken to the customs house and questioned behind a closed door while their families stood in the street. And a man who burned German letters in wartime would not be questioned and released. He would be charged. He might be shot—Vane had said as much at the first assembly, in that mild reasonable voice of his, explaining the regulations as if reading a recipe. And the town would learn that it was Tomas Eriksen who had told. The boy whose life the keeper had pulled out of the sea. They would not say it loudly. Nobody in Sker Point said anything loudly anymore. But it would enter Mrs. Okafor's silent ledger, and it would enter every kitchen, and his mother would live out her days in a town that knew her son had sold the man who saved him for a pair of boots and a coat and three shillings a week.

And if he did not report it—if he carried the silence instead—then he became what he had despised in everyone else since the uniforms came. A man who saw and said nothing. A keeper of secrets in a town full of them. Worse: if Vane ever learned that Tomas had seen and withheld—because Vane learned everything eventually, that was the whole terror of the man, his patience, his arithmetic—then it would not be the keeper who was ruined first. It would be the informant. Informants who withheld were worse than informants who failed. Vane had explained that too, gently, at the second interrogation, when Tomas had hesitated a half-second too long about the flickering light offshore. *A watchman who sleeps, we forgive. A watchman who lies, we cannot.*

There was no clean side of it. That was what nobody had told him about power when he had signed the papers and taken the shilling advance and bought the boots. He had thought informing would be like fishing: you saw the fish, you took the fish, you were paid. He had not understood that the net catches the one who hauls it. Every report he filed put another strand between himself and the town, and now the strands had grown fine enough to catch his own conscience, and he stood in the mesh of his own making and could not find the opening.

At noon he ate his ration in the station doorway and watched the lighthouse across the water, white and indifferent against the scudding clouds, its lamp dark in the daylight as lamps are, keeping whatever it keeps when it isn't shining. He thought about going over there. He thought about saying to Ansel Iversen, *I won't tell. But you have to promise me there's nothing else. No signals, no letters, nothing more*—and he knew even as he imagined it that the promise would be worthless, given or received, because a man who burns letters in the lee of his own coal shed is a man past promising.

In the afternoon the major sent for him.

Tomas climbed the hill to the requisitioned customs house with his heart going like a gull in a storm, rehearsing denials for questions that had not been asked. But Vane, when Tomas stood before the desk, was not looking at him. He was looking at the ledger—the old town census, Mrs. Okafor's careful columns of names—and turning pages with one finger, slowly, the way a man turns the pages of a book he has read many times and is reading again for pleasure. There were pencil marks in the margins now. Circles. Tomas could see, upside down, the shape of a name inside the nearest circle, and the name began with L.

"Eriksen," Vane said pleasantly, without looking up. "You walk the point route each morning. You have eyes the army pays for. Tell me—" and now he did look up, and his gaze was as mild and as bottomless as the water off the Herring Grounds— "in all your mornings, in all your walking, what is it you see at the lighthouse?"

Tomas opened his mouth. Behind his eyes: a tin pail, blue ink curling in the flames, an arm like a hawser, the taste of blood and cold. The silence stretched—one second, two, three—and he felt it stretching like ice under his boots, and somewhere beneath him he heard the first crack.

"Smoke," he heard himself say. "Chimney smoke, sir. Same as any morning."

Vane regarded him for a long, unhurried moment, the pencil turning slowly between his fingers, and then he smiled—the small, satisfied smile of a man who has just learned something important from what was not said—and looked back down at the ledger, and drew a fresh circle around the name that began with L.
