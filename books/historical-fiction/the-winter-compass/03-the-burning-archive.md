## 3. The Burning Archive

I had been a copyist for eleven years, and in all that time I had learned one law above every other: paper is patient. Paper waits. It does not argue, does not flatter, does not betray. A man can trust ink more than he trusts his own brother, because ink says only what it was made to say and holds it forever.

That night, I learned the second law: paper is also the most flammable thing in London.

* * *

It began with a smell — tallow, wet wool, the ordinary stink of a workshop at work — and then, beneath it, something that did not belong. Smoke where there should be no smoke. Beatrix lifted her head from the decoded charts spread across the long table, her pencil hovering over the soundings of Corren Sound, and said, "Do you smell—"

The door came in before she could finish.

Not opened. *Came in.* The lock had been old when I first crept through it two nights past, and it surrendered without ceremony, splintering along the jamb as three men filled the doorway with lantern-light behind them. They were not ruffians. That was the worst of it. They wore good coats, quiet boots, the patient faces of men paid by the month rather than by the job. Halloway's men. I knew it the way one knows weather before it breaks.

"The folio," the tall one said. Not a question. Not even particularly unkind.

Beatrix moved — I will give her that, she moved like a woman who had spent ten years learning exactly where everything in that room lived — but the tall man was faster, or perhaps simply better rehearsed. His hand closed on the master's letters, the originals, Josiah Quill's own hand with its peculiar hooked ascenders, still lying where we had been cross-referencing them against the marginalia not an hour before.

"No—" Beatrix's voice cracked across the workshop like a sounding-line snapping taut.

What happened next took less time than it takes to tell. The tall man drew a small tin case from his coat, and inside it, coals — carried hot, I realized, carried *deliberately*, which meant this raid had been planned to the last particular, meant not to seize evidence but to erase it. He fed the letters into an iron brazier by the cold hearth, all six of them, and struck a taper, and the fire took them with an eagerness that felt obscene.

I have watched ships burn. I stood on the shingle at Portsmouth as a boy and watched a hulk go up from keel to masthead, and I thought nothing could be worse than that — a whole life of oak and canvas and human labor given to flame in an afternoon. I was wrong. Watching a dead man's handwriting die was worse. Each letter curled and blackened and let go of its words one by one, and I found myself reading them as they burned — *the logs were altered before the court sat, Vane, you must copy them true, you must* — reading faster than the fire, as if speed could save them, as if memory were a kind of rescue.

"Take the charts," Beatrix said to me, very quietly, very steadily, and her steadiness frightened me more than the flames did. "Aldous. The charts. Now."

I swept the decoded sheets into my coat — Corren Sound, the soundings that proved the allied squadron had been steered onto the shoal, the whole architecture of the lie drawn in Beatrix's precise hand over her master's ghostly under-drawing. One letter alone escaped the brazier; whether by accident or by some failure of the tall man's thoroughness I never learned, but Beatrix snatched it from the table as we went, folding it into her bodice, and then we were out the back, through the mending-room, down the stair that smelled of size and linseed oil, into the frozen dark of Cordon Lane.

Behind us, the workshop began to burn in earnest.

* * *

London in January is a city of ice pretending to be a city of stone. Every surface wears a glaze; every breath hangs visible; every footfall is a negotiation. We ran anyway. We ran because the light in the workshop windows had gone orange, because voices were rising in the street, because staying still was dying.

We went east, away from the river, away from Halloway's territory in the great houses above the Strand. Beatrix knew the lanes as she knew coastlines — by their hazards, their depths, their turning-points — and she pulled me through a maze of courts and alleys with a certainty that made me ashamed of every map I had ever copied secondhand. Twice we heard boots behind us and pressed ourselves into a doorway while watchmen passed, their lanterns swinging haloes through the freezing fog. Once a link-boy saw our faces and I put a coin in his hand and said, "You saw no one," and he looked at the coin, and at the smoke staining the sky behind us, and said, "Saw no one, sir," and I have blessed him silently every day since.

At the corner of Fetter Lane, breathless, our lungs full of broken glass, Beatrix stopped and pressed her back to a wall and laughed — a small, terrible sound, half sob.

"They're burning it," she said. "Ten years of my life is burning right now, and he didn't even stay to watch."

"He doesn't need to watch," I said. "He has historians."

She looked at me then, and in the light of someone else's fire reflected off the ice-slick cobbles, I saw the thing settle in her face — not despair, not yet, but its raw ore. "Then we get there first."

* * *

The Reverend Nathaniel Croft kept a narrow house behind the naval chapel off Crutched Friars, and he answered our knocking at half past two in the morning in a nightshirt and a scholar's frown, holding a candle that trembled only slightly.

I had never met him. I knew him only as the one name Josiah Quill had ever trusted outside our conspiracy of two — written once, in cipher, in the margin of a chart: *if all else fails, Croft keeps a true ledger.* A chaplain who had served at sea, who had buried sailors, who had grown tired of burying questions alongside them.

He listened. That is the miracle I keep returning to. We stood dripping snowmelt in his parlor, Beatrix laying out the charts on his table among his sermons, her voice low and rapid and absolutely clear, and the old man listened the way a sounding-lead listens — all the way down. When she reached the end, when she named Sir Edmund Halloway aloud in a clergyman's parlor at three in the morning, he did not flinch.

"You understand what you are asking," he said. "If I enter this into the Admiralty record tonight, it cannot be quietly withdrawn tomorrow. Testimony, once filed, is a nail driven through the hull of a reputation. Whose? Possibly mine."

"Halloway's historians file their version at dawn," Beatrix said. "We've seen the drafts being prepared — Aldous copied one. By eight o'clock the archive will say the allied ships struck the shoal through navigational error, and Josiah Quill will be remembered as a madman who died making maps. After dawn, the truth becomes a rumor. Before dawn, it becomes a record."

Croft looked at the charts a long moment. Then he took up the surviving letter — the one letter that had escaped the fire — and read Josiah's hooked, urgent hand by candlelight, his lips moving slightly, and I watched sixty years of careful clerical caution lose, inch by inch, to something older and harder underneath it.

"I christened men on those ships," he said finally. "Corren Sound. I read the burial service forty-one times." He set the letter down. "Fetch me the record book."

* * *

It took until nearly five. Croft wrote in a fair, fast ecclesiastical hand, transcribing our testimony verbatim, questioning us both — dates, names, the sequence of the falsified logs, the provenance of each chart — with a precision that would have done the Admiralty's own examiners credit. Beatrix signed. I signed. Croft signed, sealed it with wax from his own desk, and wrote across the cover in letters meant to be read by anyone who might later try to lose it:

*Testimony concerning the Battle of Corren Sound, entered this fifth day of January, 1747, prior to any other filing.*

"There," he said, setting it down like a man setting down an anchor. "Now it exists in more than one place. Now it has weight."

Through his window, eastward, the sky had begun to turn the color of dirty pewter. Dawn was coming, and with it Halloway's historians, with their sanitized account and their tidy, murderous prose. Let them come. Their version would arrive to find a nail already driven through the hull.

But as we stood there, three people in a candlelit parlor with the truth finally fixed to paper, there came a hammering at the street door — hard, official, unhurried — and Croft's candle guttered in the draft as his housekeeper's footsteps hurried toward it.

"That will be the Admiralty's messenger," Croft said quietly, "come to summon whoever filed testimony to appear before the Board at nine o'clock." He paused. "Or it will be Halloway's men, come to make certain nothing was filed at all."

He crossed to the door and put his hand upon the latch, and did not lift it.
