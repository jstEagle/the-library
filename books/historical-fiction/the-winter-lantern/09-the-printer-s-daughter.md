## 9. The Printer's Daughter

I have been drunk for eleven years, which is a discipline like any other. It requires consistency. It requires that you never let a morning pass without honoring it, never let a grief catch you unprepared with your wits about you. Sobriety, in my experience, is far more dangerous. A sober man remembers things, and memory in this city is a currency that buys you nothing but trouble.

So understand what it cost me, that evening in March, to sit at my own workbench with nothing in my blood but bread and the dregs of yesterday's coffee, and to wait for my daughter — though the city knows her as my son, and the ledger knows her as no one at all — to come down from the type room.

I had sent the boy home early. Tomas, my journeyman, who has the observational skills of a fencepost and the loyalty of one too, which in this decade is the finest quality a man can have. I banked the stove myself. I turned the sign to *closed* at an hour when every honest printer in the district was still working, and I sat in the smell of ink and oil and cold iron, and I listened to my daughter's footsteps overhead, light as a woman's, careful as a forger's, and I thought: *Eleven years. Eleven years I have let her carry this alone, on the theory that a debt unpaid is a debt forgotten.*

The theory was wrong. Debts in this city are never forgotten. They are only transferred, like a lease, like a name on a census roll, from one pair of shoulders to another. And lately I had begun to feel the weight settling onto new shoulders — hers — and I found, to my considerable disgust, that I still had a father in me somewhere under all the brandy.

* * *

Let me tell you how it was, because she deserves the truth from someone, and I am the only one left who has it whole.

Her brother was called Emil. I say *was* the way you say a word you have kept in a drawer for a decade, handling it only in the dark. Emil Whitlock, born 1826, apprenticed at this press at nine years old, dead at twenty-one — no. Not dead. That's the lie I built, and it was a good lie, a printer's lie, set in solid type and distributed to every neighbor, every clerk, every census taker who ever darkened my door. *Died of the lung fever, winter of '47. Terrible thing. Young men go so fast.*

Emil did not die of the lung fever. Emil went up the river on a barge one night in October with three other young men and a crate of pamphlets that a man named Herr Kessler had asked me to print, and the barge was stopped at the customs house, and two of the four boys came back. Emil was not one of them.

They did not hang him. Hanging would have been a mercy, a clean entry in a record, a grave with a stone. What they did instead was worse. They *unmade* him. I went to the magistrate's office to inquire after my son and was told, with the particular blandness that this government reserves for its cruelties, that no such person had ever been registered in this district. No Emil Whitlock. No apprentice. No son. The clerk turned the ledger around and showed me the page with my own household on it — one child listed, a daughter, Ines, born 1830 — and my son's line was simply gone, scraped away as neatly as a compositor lifts a misprint. Somewhere in that building was a man with a knife and a stack of old censuses, and he had gone through my family's history and cut my boy out of it like a flaw in a sheet of paper.

I stood in that office and understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the moment of complete ruin, three things. First: they had not erased Emil to punish him. They had erased him to punish *me*, and to send a message to every printer on the street, and the message had been received. Second: the men who gave the order — Kessler's masters, the Committee for Records and Registration, the gray men with gray ledgers — knew my face and knew my shop and would be watching what I did next. And third: I had a daughter, fourteen years old, with her brother's hands and her brother's silence, and if I did not act that very night, they would come for her too. Not because she had done anything. Because she was mine, and mine was a name that had become a debt.

So I did the only thing a printer knows how to do. I altered the record.

It took me four nights. I did not simply strike Ines and leave a vacancy — vacancies attract questions, and questions attract clerks. No, I did it properly, the way we used to do it for the church registers when a parish priest wanted an inconvenient baptism softened. I made Ines into Emil. I went back through eleven years of censuses, guild rolls, school registries, baptismal books — God forgive me, the baptismal books — and I cut my daughter out of every one of them and set my son in her place, in her clothes, in her bed, in her future. I forged a death certificate for a boy who had never existed in that year and that fever, and I buried him in the parish ledger with a plot number and a fee paid in cash. And then I sat my daughter down at this very bench, with the type still warm from my hands, and I told her what she would have to become.

She was fourteen. She did not cry. She looked at me the way she looks at a line of type that won't justify — measuring, patient, already solving it — and she said, "The voice will be the hard part."

That is what she said. *The voice will be the hard part.* Not *why*, not *how long*, not the thousand things a child should say when her father tells her she must stop existing. She had understood immediately what I could not bring myself to say aloud: that this was not a disguise to be worn and shed, but a life to be lived, every day, in every doorway, for as long as the men in the gray ledgers held their grudge. And she did it. Eleven years. She bound herself flat and dropped her voice to a whisper and learned to walk like a boy and cut type better than Emil ever had, better than I do, if I am honest, which tonight I apparently must be — and she did it all without one word of reproach, which is the reproach that cuts deepest of all.

I drank because of it. That is not an excuse, it is an accounting. Every glass was a payment on a debt I could not face sober. A man can be a coward in installments.

* * *

She came down the stairs at half past eight, and I watched her do the thing she always does at the bottom step — the small hesitation, the shoulders squaring, the voice dropped into its lower register before she ever rounds the corner. Eleven years of that. Eleven years of my daughter rehearsing her own erasure at the top of a staircase.

"Father." The whisper. "The press in the back room needs — you've closed early."

"I've closed early," I agreed. "Sit down, Ines."

I had not said her name aloud in eleven years. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the Almighty keeps over the head of this one. I said it and her whole body flinched, and then went very still, the way a person goes still when they hear a floorboard creak above a room where no one should be.

"You're sober," she said.

"I am sober," I said. "Don't get used to it. It doesn't suit me. I look like a schoolmaster."

"You look terrible."

"I look *honest*. It's the same thing." I poured her the last of the coffee, cold, from the pot, because a ritual is a ritual, and I sat down across from her at the bench where I had taught two children to set their first letters, and I told her. All of it. Emil on the barge. The clerk with the scraped page. Kessler and the pamphlets and the debt, and the four nights of forgery, and the baptismal book, and the grave with no one in it. My voice held. I was almost proud of that.

She listened without moving. When I finished she was quiet a long moment, and then she said, in her own voice — her real voice, the one I had almost forgotten, low and dry and hers — "The men running the arrests now. The Committee. Is Kessler one of them?"

"He's worse than one of them. He's the one they trust. Kessler holds the paper on half the printers in this district, and I am the only one who knows what he did with Emil, because I'm the only one who ever asked. That's why the debt never died, Ines. A debt only dies when the creditor is afraid you'll tell someone what it was for. And I never told anyone. I drank instead. It was cheaper."

"And now?"

"Now the census man came to my door last week." I watched her face do something complicated. "Yes. Varga. He asked me about an apprentice who died of the lung fever, and he asked it twice, in two different ways, the way a man asks when he already knows the answer and wants to see if *you* do. And I lied to him the way I've lied to every census taker for eleven years, and he smiled at me and wrote something in his book, and I have been sober for three days trying to decide what he wrote."

"Father—"

"Let me finish. I'm only good for one speech per decade and I'm nearly through it." I reached into the drawer of the bench — the deep drawer, the one with the false bottom I built in '39 for hiding Kessler's pamphlets, because God has a sense of humor, He gives a man one talent for concealment and then a lifetime of reasons to use it — and I took out the key.

The second key. The one to the back room and the cellar beneath it, cut from the same blank as mine, that I had carried against my heart for eleven years like a stone I could not swallow and could not spit out. I had told myself I was keeping it for Emil. I had told myself a great many things.

I put it on the bench between us, on the grain of the wood, next to a nick Emil made with a chisel when he was ten.

"I always knew," I said. "I want you to understand that. Not suspected — *knew*. A father knows his own child's walk. Knew the first month, when you thought the binding and the whisper were enough. I let it go on because undoing it would have killed you, and because every day you lived it was a day the lie held, and because I am a coward, and because — " and here my voice did break, and I let it, because a man should pay something for eleven years of silence — "because you were so *good* at it, Ines. You were so good at disappearing that I told myself you had chosen it. That's the filthiest lie of all. No one chooses it. I chose it for you when you were fourteen years old, to save your life, and it was the right thing, and it was the worst thing I have ever done, and I have set type for both of those truths every day since and never once printed either of them."

She picked up the key. She did not take her eyes off me. Her hands — Emil's hands, my hands, hands that could set a page blind in the dark — closed around it, and I saw that she was trembling, very slightly, the way type trembles in a forme before the quoins are tightened.

"The men on the list," she said. "The arrests when the ice breaks. Varga's name is on it. He told me."

I had not known that. It landed in me like a quoinslip. "Then you're already in it. Whatever you've agreed to — don't tell me what, I don't want it in my head where a sober man can be made to give it up — you're already in it, and the shop is in it, and this key is the only part of it I can give you." I stood, because my legs wanted to stand, and I put my hand flat on the press, on the iron frame my grandfather had bought, and I said the last of it.

"Kessler's men came by this afternoon. Not for me. They measured the windows of the back room and asked when the press would next run a full edition. A press doesn't get measured, Ines. A press gets *counted*. They're deciding what this shop is worth to them, and when men like that decide a thing's worth, the answer is always the same — it's worth what they can take. The thaw is coming early this year. I can hear the river from my window at night, groaning like a man in his sleep. Whatever you and the census man are going to do, do it before the ice goes. And when you go down to that cellar — " I nodded at the floor, at the boards beneath the composing stone where a man keeps the things he cannot burn and cannot show — "remember that everything I buried down there, I buried for you. Not for Emil. Emil is the lie. You were always the truth of this shop. I just never had the courage to print it."

She stood. She put the key in her pocket, over her heart, which is where I had carried it, and I thought: *there, that's where it lived all along, that's where it was going.*

"Father," she said, and it was her own voice, and I let it be, "if they come for the shop —"

"Then they come for the shop. A press can be broken. A forme can be distributed, a page can be burned. But a thing that's been printed — " and here I smiled, the first time in eleven years that the smile and the feeling arrived together — "a thing that's been printed is loose in the world, daughter, and no Committee for Records and Registration has ever yet called back a page. Make them print you, Ines. Make them print you so many times they can never scrape you out again."

She went up the stairs. I listened to her footsteps, and this time she did not pause at the bottom step, and she did not square her shoulders, and her voice, when she spoke to Tomas at the door, was her own.

I sat down at the bench and looked at the nick in the wood that Emil made when he was ten, and I reached for the bottle, and I stopped, and I did not drink. Some debts you pay in brandy. This one, I am paying in mornings.

And then, through the window, I saw them: two men in gray coats across the street, not even pretending to pass by, standing under the lamp with a paper between them, and one of them tapping it with a finger, and both of them looking up at my windows.

The thaw was coming. The river knew it. And so, now, did I.
