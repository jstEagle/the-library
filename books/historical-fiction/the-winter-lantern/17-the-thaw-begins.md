## 17. The Thaw Begins

The river woke us at half past four in the morning with a sound like God clearing his throat.

I had been sleeping badly in the safehouse above the tavern—sleeping badly everywhere since the customs house, since Aurel was taken, since I learned that a page of my own name had been torn from our printing run and used as bait against us. Sleep came to me now the way a cat comes to a house it doesn't trust: briefly, suspiciously, and always ready to bolt. So when the ice began to speak, I was already awake to hear it.

It started as a groan, low and long, somewhere beneath the floorboards, beneath the tavern, beneath the frozen mud of the riverbank. Then it cracked—a report like a musket shot, then another, then a stuttering volley of them, as if the whole winter were being fired out of a gun at once. The tankard on the washstand rattled. Somewhere below, the tavern keeper's dog lost its mind entirely.

I sat up in the dark with my heart going like a press in full run, and I thought: *early*. The thaw wasn't due for another week at least. The old men who read the river the way priests read scripture had all agreed on that much. But rivers, I have learned, keep their own counsel. They are the one thing in this city that has never been required to file paperwork.

Across the room, Marta—the widow who ran the safehouse and pretended it was merely a room she rented to insomniacs—struck a match and lit the candle without bothering to ask what the noise was. She'd lived on this bank sixty years. Ice cracking was, for her, roughly as remarkable as sunrise.

"Early," I said anyway, because someone had to say it.

"Everything's early this year," she said, and there was something in her voice—not fear exactly, but the particular alertness of a woman recalculating. "You should look outside."

I went to the window, and that is when I saw the second thing, the thing that mattered more than any sound the river could make.

There was a paper nailed to the door of the cooper's shop across the lane.

* * *

I want to be precise about what I felt in that first moment, because memory has since dressed it up in better clothes than it wore at the time. What I felt first was not hope. What I felt first was terror—cold, complete, the animal kind—because I knew that typeface. I had cut those letters myself, or most of them, over three years of nights, my burin whispering through steel while my father drank himself stupid in the front room. A printer knows her own faces the way a mother knows her children: by the small flaws, the slightly crooked crossbar on the capital T, the descender on the g that runs a hair too long because I cut it the week my brother died and my hand was not steady.

That was my type. Nailed to a door across the lane, six feet above the mud, flapping in the wind off the breaking river.

And printed upon it, in columns I knew down to the last em-quad, were names. Names from the census ledger. Names of people who had been erased from the record so they could later be disappeared from the city without anyone being able to prove they'd existed at all. Our list. The list Aurel and I had set in metal night after night in the back room while my father snored two walls away. The list that was supposed to have been seized at the customs house along with everything else—along with Aurel.

Someone had printed more. Someone had distributed them. And whoever it was had done it overnight, across the whole river district, if that single sheet on the cooper's door was anything to go by.

Marta came to stand beside me at the window. She read the sheet for a long moment. I watched her lips move over the names.

"That's Ferenc Bakos," she said finally. "The schoolmaster. They took him in November. His wife's been telling everyone he went to visit his mother in Pest." She looked at me, and her eyes were old and sharp and missed nothing, which is why she ran a safehouse and not a bakery. "This says he never left. This says where they took him."

"Yes," I said.

"And you know about this."

"I know about the printing of it," I said. "Not this." I gestured at the door, the nail, the wind lifting the corner of the page like a hand trying to get her attention. "Ours was taken. Confiscated. My—" I stopped. I had been about to say *my partner*, which was true in a way that deserved a better word than the ones available. "The man I worked with was arrested at the customs house. The sheets were burned. I watched the smoke from the quay."

Marta considered this. Downstairs, the dog had achieved a state of theological ecstasy and showed no sign of descending from it.

"Well," she said at last, "then either the dead print very quickly in this city, or somebody kept back more plates than your man told you about." She turned from the window and began, with terrible calm, to dress. "Either way, girl, you'll want to be out of these rooms before the street fills up. Streets fill fast when the ice goes. And streets that fill fast"—she buttoned her collar—"are streets where nobody looks at anybody's face. Which cuts both ways."

She meant it kindly. It still landed like a stone in my chest. For eleven years I had survived by being unlooked-at, by moving through this city as a shadow wearing my dead brother's coat. Now the whole district was about to become a crowd of shadows, and I found—to my genuine surprise—that I hated the idea. That was new. That was Aurel's doing, I suspect, though I'd have bitten my tongue bloody before admitting it to him. He had this infuriating habit of looking directly at me when he spoke, as though I were a person whose face could be expected to do things. It had ruined me for invisibility somewhat.

I dressed. I took the second key my father had given me—the key to the shop, pressed into my palm on an evening when he was sober enough to mean it—and I went down through the tavern, past the dog, out into the blue-black cold of the earliest morning of the year.

* * *

The river district at dawn, with the ice going out, is a thing I will remember until I am dust, and I say that as someone who has already seen rather more history than she ordered.

The cracks had opened into leads of black water, and the whole surface of the river had begun to move—not flowing yet, but shifting, great plates of ice grinding against each other with a sound like millstones, like furniture dragged across heaven's floor. The ice near the banks heaved upward in ridges. Gulls had arrived from nowhere, screaming their opinions. And along the quay, emerging from doorways and alleys and cellar stairs, the people were coming out.

They came the way water finds a crack. First a few—the baker's boy, the fishwives, a cluster of dockworkers still in their nightshirts—and then dozens, and then it stopped being possible to count them. Nobody shouted. That was the eerie part, the part that made the hair rise on my arms. A crowd that shouts is a crowd performing. This crowd had gone quiet the way a fuse is quiet. They moved toward the bridges, all of them, drawn by whatever gravity governs such moments, and as they moved they read.

Because the papers were everywhere now. I saw them as I walked—nailed to doors, pasted to shutters, wedged under the iron knockers of houses whose owners would have wept to know what their front steps were displaying. At the corner of Salt Lane, a boy stood on a barrel nailing up another copy with the solemn efficiency of a town crier, and beside him a woman—I recognized her vaguely, a washerwoman with forearms like hawsers—was reading aloud to a knot of listeners, her finger tracking down the columns.

"—taken to the garrison on the ninth of December, transferred to the fortress at Segesvár, no trial recorded, no release recorded—" Her voice caught. "That's my sister's husband. That's József. We buried an empty box in March."

Nobody said anything. A man took off his hat. And then the washerwoman folded the paper with enormous care, as though it were a letter from the living, and put it inside her shawl, and the crowd moved on toward the bridge, and I stood there in the trampled snow with my heart doing something I didn't have a name for.

Here is what I understood, standing in that crowd of the named: we had planned to smuggle the truth out of the city like contraband, one coffin at a time, one checkpoint at a time, a handful of copies destined for printers abroad who would publish it months hence, safely, uselessly, after everyone on its pages was beyond help. That had been the plan. A good plan. A survivable plan.

But someone—someone with access to our plates, or to plates cut from our pages, someone who had watched the customs house burn our run and had simply gone home and printed again—someone had decided that truth delayed was truth denied, and had nailed it to every door in the district before dawn.

I stood in the crowd and I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me sideways, ragged, half sob, and the woman next to me gave me a look of sympathy, assuming, reasonably, that I had found a loved one on the list. In a sense I had. I had found myself—or the version of myself that existed in ink, the erased apprentice, the daughter struck from the census in 1841 so that her father's debts could stay hidden and his daughter could stay breathing. My page had been torn out and used to trap us. But the rest of the book had apparently decided it was done waiting for permission.

The rumor reached me in fragments as the crowd thickened. That the lists were public. That the garrison couldn't arrest everyone whose name was on a paper nailed to ten thousand doors. That the censors had sent runners out at dawn tearing the sheets down, and the runners had been followed by other citizens nailing them back up, and that this contest was ongoing and not going the authorities' way. That the magistrate had demanded to know how many copies existed and no one could tell him, because no one knew, because printing is the one magic in this world that multiplies what it touches.

"They're saying the bridges are packed," said a voice near me—a young man, a typesetter's apprentice by his ink-stained cuffs, and yes, I noticed the cuffs, sue me, some habits are load-bearing. "Saying the soldiers won't clear them. Saying some of the guards have families on the lists themselves."

"Guards have families," said the washerwoman grimly. "Guards have sisters' husbands too."

The crowd surged forward, and I let it carry me, and I will confess something here that I am not proud of: for one bright, shameful moment, standing in that river of the resurrected, I forgot Aurel entirely. I forgot the cell they were holding him in, forgot the interrogation, forgot that my freedom to stand in this crowd was purchased with his capture. Joy does that. It's a solvent. It dissolves the debts you owe even while it's dissolving the ones owed to you. For perhaps a hundred heartbeats I was simply a woman in a crowd watching a city come apart in the best possible way, watching names crawl back out of the ground where paperwork had buried them, and I thought: *we did this. Whatever else happens—we did this.*

Then I remembered, and the joy curdled into something more useful. Urgency.

Because if the lists were public, then the authorities were finished pretending this was routine. And if they were finished pretending, then the original ledger—the master copy, the one document that proved everything, the thing I had been carrying toward the river landing in pieces and hiding in flour barrels and false-bottomed crates for a week—had just become the most dangerous piece of paper in the city. To them. And therefore to me.

And Aurel was still in their hands.

* * *

I made it as far as the Bridge of Sighs—the first checkpoint, our coffin checkpoint, the place where a guard once told me I walked like a woman and I performed masculinity at him so flawlessly I wanted to weep afterward. The bridge was exactly as the rumors promised: packed shoulder to shoulder, a solid mass of citizens staring down at the black water running free between the ice pans, and above them, on the guard platform, a knot of soldiers looking distinctly uncertain about whether they were an occupation force or an audience.

A sergeant was shouting something about dispersal. The crowd received this announcement the way a river receives a fence post.

I was working my way along the rail, head down, thinking about the landing downstream, thinking about the third checkpoint and the guard who had held my torn page, thinking—God help me—about whether Aurel could be moved in chaos, whether chaos was a door or a wall, when a hand closed on my sleeve.

I nearly broke the hand on reflex. Eleven years of survival installs certain reflexes. But the grip was familiar, and the voice that came with it was hoarse and furious and alive, and I turned around and looked into the face of my father.

He was sober. That registered first, with the shock of a slap—he was *sober*, upright, shaved, wearing his good coat, the one with the brass buttons he hadn't worn since my mother's funeral. His eyes were red-rimmed but level. He looked older than he had a week ago and twenty years younger than he had a month ago, and behind him, half-hidden in the crowd, stood Marta's lodger, the quiet young man who mended nets and was, I had long suspected, rather more than that.

"Papa." My voice came out strange. "How did you—"

"The net-mender came for me at dawn." He nodded at the young man. "Said there were papers on the doors. Said I'd want to see." He swallowed. "Ines. I saw the type. On the cooper's door. I cut the mold for that T myself, the year you were born—I taught you to recut it when you were twelve, you kept shaving the serif too thin—"

"You knew it was mine."

"I know every letter you ever cut." He said it simply, without drama, the way he might have said *I know the smell of your ink*, and something in my chest that had been clenched for eleven years loosened by one degree. Then his grip tightened on my sleeve. "Listen to me. There are men at the shop. Soldiers, this morning—they came with a wagon for the press. Your press. They're stripping Whitlock & Son to the beams."

The name stung, absurdly, even now. Even today.

"Let them take it," I said. "The plates that matter aren't in the shop. Papa, the plates that matter haven't been in the shop for years—"

"I know where you buried them," he said. "I'm the one who dug the hole." He glanced at the soldiers on the platform, at the crowd, at the black water running under the bridge, and when he looked back at me his face did something I had never seen it do: it asked permission. "But there's a wagon at the shop right now, and a sergeant on it who was paid off by the same men who hold your friend Varga, and between the wagon and the river landing there is one empty hour, and one road, and one crowd that no one in this city can control anymore. Including them."

I stared at him. The ice boomed downstream, a long rolling thunder, and the crowd cheered it—actually cheered, hundreds of voices roaring at a river as though it were an army arriving to save them, and maybe it was.

"What are you proposing?" I asked.

My father, drunk for eleven years, invisible for eleven years, complicit for longer than either of us had ever said aloud, smiled at me with my mother's mouth.

"I'm proposing," he said, "that we commit one last act of forgery together, daughter. The wagon's manifest says it's carrying a printing press to be destroyed by order of the magistrate. I'd like to change what it actually carries. And I'd like my daughter—the real one, the one with the steady hand—to cut the orders that make it legal."

Below us, the last great pan of ice surrendered, split, and slid under the bridge, and the crowd's roar rose to meet it, and somewhere downstream at the customs landing a guard was holding a torn page with my name on it, waiting for me to come and be afraid.

I took my father's arm.

We went to work.
