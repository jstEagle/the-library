## 8. The Drowned Cargo

The archive smelled of tallow and cold stone, and Sera Lindqvist had always thought of that smell as safety. Tonight it smelled like a door about to be kicked in.

She had lit only two lamps — one for the worktable, one angled low toward the stairwell so that footsteps would break its light before they reached her. Her father's rule, learned in his knee: *a scholar guards her light like a sailor guards his fire.* Outside, the harbor groaned against the ice, a long slow complaint, as though the sea itself were turning over in its sleep and finding something lodged in its ribs.

The dive log lay open between them on the table, water-stained to the color of weak tea, its pages warped into shallow waves. Half a book. The spine had been broken off cleanly, as if someone had sawn it — no, not sawn, Sera thought, studying the edge again. *Torn while wet.* Someone had taken the second half into the water and it had never come back.

Mirabel Eriksen sat across from her with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, and for the first time since she'd known him — first from a distance, across a market argument over a mislabeled crate of Vhalen glass; then in the records room at midnight, two trespassers recognizing each other by lamplight — she saw the calluses. Thick, ridged, running along the pads of his fingers like the growth rings of a tree. Rope calluses. Salt calluses. A diver's hands on a clerk's body.

"You're staring," he said, without looking up.

"I'm observing. It's a professional habit."

"You're staring," he repeated, but there was no edge in it. He flexed his fingers once, the way a man does when an old ache speaks to him, and went back to the page.

The code had resisted them for two hours. It was not cipher in any sense her father's reference books would honor — no letter substitution, no transposition she could find. The entries read, on their face, like the dull poetry of a working boat: *wind north-northeast, water clear to nine fathoms, good light, hens fed, Mirabel has the cough again.* Her father's hand, she assumed — the elder Eriksen's — patient and unhurried, recording the weather of ordinary days.

"It's just a log," Mirabel had said an hour ago, and there had been something almost wounded in it, a boy's disappointment that his drowned father had left him nothing but weather.

But Sera had spent three years cataloguing dead men's papers, and she had learned that the dead rarely wrote *nothing*. They wrote *around* things. And a man who kept his hens' feeding schedule beside his fathom readings was a man who had trained himself to make everything look alike.

"It's the *evenness*," she said now, tapping the page. "Look. Every entry is the same length. Nearly to the line. A real log isn't like that — a real log runs long when the weather runs foul. Here, the storm days are the same as the calm days. He was fitting the truth into a fixed shape."

Mirabel looked up. In the lamplight his eyes were the gray of the harbor before thaw, and she watched him do what he evidently always did: check the arithmetic first. His lips moved slightly, counting lines.

"He wouldn't have learned that from diving," he said slowly.

"No. He'd have learned it from someone who wanted the log to survive an inspection." She pulled the ledger of Halvard's manifests toward her — the copies she'd made before the originals began to vanish, page by page, from her father's archive. "And here's the other half of the lesson. Look at the manifests for the *Sea-Wife*. Halvard's last four shipments. Grain, grain, grain, grain. Every entry the same length. Every entry the same clerk's hand. Grain is the most boring cargo in the world, Mirabel. Nobody writes it this neatly unless they're hiding something behind the neatness."

The wind pressed at the shutters. Somewhere below, in the archive's lower stacks, the building ticked and settled like a ship.

"Show me again," Mirabel said. "The fathoms."

She turned the log toward him. The entries ran in columns: date, wind, depth, bottom-condition. *Nine fathoms, sand. Nine fathoms, sand. Eleven fathoms, clay. Nine, sand. Twelve, rock.* And then, in the last week of the entries — the week before the storm, the week before the *Sea-Wife* went down with all hands and a hold full, supposedly, of Rostock wheat — the depths began to move.

"Fourteen fathoms," Sera said. "Fifteen. Sixteen, over gravel. Your father was a pearl man, yes? Coastal beds, shallow water?"

"Never deeper than ten. He said deeper than ten was working blind." Mirabel's voice had gone quiet. "These are harbor-mouth depths. The channel. Why would he be sounding the channel?"

"Because someone was paying him to." She let that sit. "A pearl diver doesn't survey a shipping channel for free, and he doesn't do it for grain. You sound a channel for a ship that needs to come in *quietly*. Without pilots. Without port authority. Without lights."

Mirabel sat back. For a long moment he didn't touch the page, as if it had grown hot. Then, with the deliberation she was beginning to recognize — the deliberation of a man who balanced ledgers, who believed that numbers did not lie even when men did — he began copying the depth readings onto a scrap of paper, and beside them, from memory, the soundings of the Vhalen channel as every harbor child learned them.

Sera watched the two columns grow and felt the floor of the evening tilt.

"They match," he said. "They match exactly. He wasn't diving for pearls those last weeks. He was — " Mirabel stopped. Started again. "He was marking a safe lane. Through the shoals. At night."

"For the *Sea-Wife*," Sera said.

"For the *Sea-Wife*."

* * *

They worked another hour, and the code began to give way the way ice gives way — not all at once but in rotten patches, one honest crack at a time.

It was Mirabel who found the key, and Sera would remember that, later, when it mattered. He had been reading the hen entries — *hens fed, hens fed, hen lost to fox* — with the flat patience of a man refusing to be defeated by poultry, when he suddenly went still.

"Sera. The hens."

"There are no hens on a dive boat."

"There are no hens on a dive boat," he agreed. "But there were hens at our house. My mother kept six. And when my father was home, he'd — " He stopped, and she saw him reach for a memory the way a man reaches into cold water. "He'd count them out loud. It was a game. He'd say the number wrong on purpose and I'd correct him. Six hens, and he'd say *seven*."

"Seven," Sera repeated. She was already turning pages, hunting. "Find me every entry where the number of hens is wrong."

There were eleven of them. Eleven entries in a half-year of weather and fathoms where the hens were miscounted — *seven hens, eight hens, seven, nine* — and once Mirabel saw the pattern, the rest came in a rush that left them both leaning over the table like conspirators over a dying man's will.

The wrong number was a flag. *This entry is not weather.* And the entry that followed the flag was never weather at all. The fathom readings became something else — a sequence. And when Sera matched the sequence against the tide tables for those weeks, against the moon, against the harbor's bell schedule which her father kept in a reference volume older than either of them — the numbers resolved into times.

"Meeting times," she whispered. "Night meetings. Here — *eight hens*, and the next day the entry says *fog, no work* — but eight means the second bell of the ebb, and there's a tide window at the second bell of the ebb when a shallow-draft ship can come over the shoal at Kolvik without a pilot. He was logging arrivals."

"Arrivals of what?"

She had the manifests open before he finished asking. "Halvard's grain ships. The *Sea-Wife* made six runs that season. Five of them are accounted for — grain in, grain sold, grain in the company's own records, I've checked them against the customs rolls." She turned the manifest copy around so he could see her own neat clerk's columns, the inheritance of a historian's daughter who had learned bookkeeping at the same age other girls learned embroidery. "The sixth run has no customs entry. No sale record. No warehouse receipt. It came in and it *disappeared*, Mirabel. Sixty tons of Rostock wheat that no mill in Vhalen ever milled."

The lamps flickered. Mirabel's face in the light had the stillness of deep water.

"Grain doesn't disappear," he said.

"No. But sixty tons of something else — something that arrives at night, over the shoals, guided by a diver paid to mark the channel — that disappears very well." Sera heard her own voice go flat and precise, the voice she used when feeling threatened the scholarship. "Arms, Mirabel. The coast towns have been buying them for two years — everyone knows it, nobody says it. Rostock gunsmiths, Ostergaard powder, shipped in ones and twos so no manifest ever looks wrong. And Halvard — Halvard, who owned half the grain trade and wanted to own the other half — Halvard was running them in under wheat."

Mirabel had gone very pale. "My father was diving for Halvard."

"Your father was diving for *someone*. Halvard's the ship. But look at who insured the *Sea-Wife*." She put the paper in front of him and let him read it himself, because she had learned that from her father too: never hand a man a conclusion when you can hand him a document. "Ostmark Marine. The Ostmark Trading Company. Your company, Mirabel. They underwrote the cargo. They underwrote the *ship*. And when the ship went down — "

"When the ship went down," Mirabel said slowly, "they paid the claim."

"They paid the claim," Sera agreed, "at full value. On a cargo of grain that never existed. Halvard's estate collected, the company wrote it off, and everyone was made whole — except the crew."

* * *

The revelation did not arrive all at once. That was the thing Sera would remember afterward — that it arrived the way drowning is said to arrive, in stages, each one colder than the last, each one easier to accept than to fight.

First came the arms. That was almost bearable; smuggling was Vhalen's second-oldest trade, and a magnate running guns under grain was a story with a shape she recognized, a villain with a face. Halvard had died in the autumn — drowned, officially, in a boating accident as tidy as any manifest — and dead men made convenient villains.

Then came the storm.

"It wasn't a storm," Mirabel said. He was holding the log's final pages, the ones written in a hand that had begun to press harder, the pen digging grooves into the paper. "Listen to this. *Hens fed. Wind rising. Tove says stay ashore.*" His voice caught on the name and steadied. "Tove. My father's dive partner. He's alive — he's on the breakwater, he drinks. But — *Tove says stay ashore*. Why would a dive partner tell him to stay ashore in a rising wind?"

"Because your father wasn't going out to dive." Sera had the tide tables and the harbor bell schedule and the last eleven entries all spread out now, a constellation of small dishonest numbers, and she could see the whole sky of it. "The *Sea-Wife* was due in on the night of the storm. The final run. The last of the arms. And your father had marked the channel — he was the only man who knew the safe lane over Kolvik shoal. If he went out that night, he went out to bring her in."

"Or to keep her out."

The words dropped into the room and neither of them moved.

"Read the last entry," Sera said.

Mirabel read it. His lips moved and no sound came, and then sound came. "*Nine hens. Wind full north. Water high. If the light is lit, she comes in. If the light is dark, she turns back.*" He looked up. "There was a beacon. A temporary light on the Kolvik stake — my mother has a lamp from it, I always thought it was just — " He stopped. Swallowed. "Someone was supposed to light the beacon to bring the ship in. Or leave it dark to turn her away."

"And the *Sea-Wife* wrecked on the shoal," Sera said, "in a storm that every record calls sudden, in a channel your father had already marked as safe. Mirabel — if the channel was marked, and the beacon was lit, the wreck doesn't happen. Ships don't hit water they've been guided around. So either the beacon was dark — "

"Or the soundings were wrong." Mirabel's voice had gone strange, distant, a man reading his own death warrant in a stranger's hand. "Someone moved the marks. Someone gave him false stakes to plant, or moved them after, so the safe lane led onto the shoal."

"And who benefits when a ship full of smuggled arms sinks with all hands?" Sera asked, and answered herself, because the answer had been sitting in her chest since the first missing page, cold and patient as the harbor ice. "The men who insured her. The men who'd been paid to carry her. The men who now had a dead magnate, a drowned crew who could tell no tales, and a claim they could pay themselves, out of their own coffers, and call it misfortune." She pressed her palms flat against the table to stop her hands from shaking. "The company didn't just bury the cargo, Mirabel. The company built the grave. They sank the *Sea-Wife* with her own secret in her hold, and then they wrote the weather report."

The lamp nearest the stair guttered. Outside, the ice boomed — a vast, cracking report, the sound of the harbor shifting, and Sera flinched, and saw that Mirabel had not. He sat with his father's log open in front of him and his diver's hands flat on the table, and he did not move at all.

* * *

Afterward, she could not say how long they sat there. The fire burned down to its red eye; the tallow candles needed trimming; the cold came up through the stone floor and into their boots, and neither of them shifted.

It was Mirabel who spoke first, and when he did, his voice was the voice of a clerk reading figures — steady, careful, each number placed like a stone in a wall.

"Say it plainly. All of it. I want to hear it in order."

Sera nodded, and gave it to him in order, because he deserved order; disorder was a luxury for people whose families were not in the account.

"Halvard ran arms under grain for at least one season, likely longer. Your father was hired — through someone, we don't yet know through whom — to survey and mark the night channel. The Ostmark Trading Company insured the runs, which means they knew the cargo; no underwriter writes a full-value policy on sixty tons of grain moving at night without papers without knowing exactly what's in the hold. On the final run, someone arranged the wreck — dark beacon, moved stakes, or both. The ship and crew were lost. Halvard collected the insurance, and within the season Halvard himself was dead, and the company's historians — " she gestured at the darkened stacks around them, at the whole quiet machinery of the archive — "began writing the version where the *Sea-Wife* was lost to weather and Halvard was a tragic figure and no one asks what happened to sixty tons of wheat."

"And my father." Mirabel's hands had not moved. "Where does my father sit in the order?"

Sera hesitated, and hated herself for hesitating, because she saw him see it.

"He's in the order twice," she said finally. "He's the man who marked the channel — which makes him either an instrument of the wreck or the one man who could have prevented it. And he's the man who kept this log — which makes him the only witness who wrote anything down." She turned the water-stained pages gently, the way she turned the archive's oldest folios. "Mirabel, I don't think your father sank the ship. I think the last entries read like a man who'd begun to suspect what the cargo was, and what the plan was, and was keeping a record in the only way that would survive an inspection. I think he went out that night to light the beacon himself, or to warn the ship off, or — " she stopped.

"Or they sent him out," Mirabel said, "because a diver can be lost in a storm and no one will ever call it murder."

The ice boomed again, farther out this time, a long rolling concussion that Sera felt in her teeth. Revelation, she thought, was not a door opening. It was the floor going out and finding, beneath the floor, not solid ground but deeper water, and beneath that water, more floors, going down and down into the dark. Every answer they had pulled from the log tonight had opened onto three questions. *Who hired her father? Who moved the stakes? Who inside the company signed the insurance, and who inside the company signed the weather?* And above all — the question that made her hands cold on the table's edge — *who knew that the Eriksen family had kept half a log for seven years, and had waited until now, until this winter, until a sealed letter addressed to 'the Eriksen household,' to reach for it?*

"Mirabel," she said. "The letter. The one your supervisor took. It named your family as Halvard's partners."

"Yes."

"If the company believed that, they'd have no reason to threaten you. Accomplices keep quiet to protect themselves." She watched his face as the arithmetic landed — she could see him doing it, line by line, the way he did everything. "The letter isn't an accusation, Mirabel. It's a *test*. They're watching what you do with what you know. Which means someone suspects the log exists. Which means someone suspects it survived the water. And the second half of it — " she touched the sawn, torn, drowned-away edge of the spine — "the second half is still down there, or it's in someone's drawer, and either way, whoever holds it holds the whole story."

Mirabel looked down at his hands — at the calluses that were his father's inheritance written into his skin — and when he raised his eyes they were no longer the gray of the harbor before thaw. They were the gray of it after, when the ice begins to move.

"Then we find the other half," he said. "And we find out who wrote that letter. Because Sera — " he laid one callused finger, very gently, on the final entry, on the nine hens and the full north wind and the light that was lit or not lit — "my father didn't drown in a storm. My father drowned in a *plan*. And plans are written by men with names."

Sera reached for a fresh sheet of paper and began, in her small exact hand, to write down the names they had — and stopped, because the first name that came, unbidden, was the name of the man who had signed the *Sea-Wife*'s policy in the company's own register.

Brannoch. Mirabel's factor. Mirabel's protector.

She looked up, and saw by his face that he had reached the same page at the same moment.

Outside, the harbor ice groaned, shifted, and began — very slowly, the way all doomed things begin — to break.
