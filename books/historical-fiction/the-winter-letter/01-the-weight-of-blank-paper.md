## 1. The Weight of Blank Paper

A woman carrying a bolt of linen is the most invisible creature in the Republic. I had tested this theory across four provinces and two hundred miles of frozen road, and Callowmere was to be its final proof.

I came down from the north road at dusk, when the light goes grey as dishwater and even a suspicious man's eyes grow lazy. The town sat along the river like something spilled and half-frozen there — slate roofs, chimneys breathing smoke into a sky the color of an old bruise, and beyond it all the river itself, locked under ice so thick the ferryman had given up his season and gone to drink somewhere warm. I had heard about that ice for three days. Every traveler on the road had opinions about it. *Thick as church marble. Thick as a miser's skull.* Nobody said what I was thinking, which was: thick enough to march an army over.

The bolt rode on my shoulder, forty pounds of linen with forty pounds of something else sewn into its heart. To any eye I was a stationer's widow — black shawl, plain grey dress, the particular stoop of a woman who has buried a husband and expects to bury more. Widowhood is the best disguise ever invented. It explains everything: why you travel alone, why you don't smile, why you flinch at sudden noise. Grief is a passport no one checks.

My name, for the purposes of the world, was Maren Ostrow. My real name I kept folded away like the last page of a letter you reread too often — Xanthe Underhill, courier, seven years silent, seven years careful. A name is a heavy thing to carry. That was the whole trouble. I was carrying two.

* * *

I read rooms the way other women read letters: quickly, hungrily, and with an eye for what's been crossed out. Seven years of silence will teach you that. Silence teaches you that a room tells you everything before anyone speaks — where the exits are, who counts the money twice, which chair faces the door. In the tavern at Harlow Cross I had learned in eleven minutes that the landlord watered the wine, his wife skimmed the till, and a revenue officer drank in the corner every Thursday pretending to be a wool merchant. I drank my one glass slowly and left before Thursday became Friday.

Callowmere's principal room was the inn called the Second Bell, and I read it standing in the doorway with snow melting off my boots.

Small room, low beams, clean rushes — someone here swept daily, which meant someone here cared, which meant either an honest house or a house very good at looking honest. A girl of perhaps ten fed sticks to the fire with the concentration of a bombadier. Two boys — twins by the matching stubborn chins — argued over a game of draughts with such violence that neither was actually playing. An old man sat by the window with a broken clock open in his lap like a patient on a table, and he never once looked up, though I later understood he missed nothing; deafness sharpens the eyes to a scalpel's edge.

And behind the bar, a broad woman with flour to her elbows and a face built for deciding things. She looked at me the way I looked at rooms. I felt myself being read, cover to cover, and had the uncomfortable sense she found the margins interesting.

"Room?" she said. "Or supper? Or both, because you look like both."

"Both," I said. "Maren Ostrow. Stationer's widow, out of Vell. I'm delivering linen downriver come thaw."

"Linen." Her eyes went to the bolt on my shoulder, weighed it professionally, and moved on. Either she noticed it was heavy for linen or she didn't, and if she did, she was polite enough — or clever enough — to say nothing. "Marta Voss. The Second Bell. We've one room left and it's cold, and the bed complains, but it doesn't lie."

"Neither do I," I said, and we both pretended that was true.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about invisibility: it is work. Full-time work, unpaid, with no holidays. You must be forgettable at exactly the right pitch — not so plain that you're memorable for being plain, not so pretty that eyes snag. You must have a story worn smooth as a river stone, no rough edges for questions to catch on. Dead husband, dead of fever, in Vell, three years past. Stationer's shop sold. Linen trade, small, honest, dull. Dull is armor. I had polished my dullness for years until it shone.

But that evening, sitting at the long table with a bowl of barley soup and the household of the Second Bell assembling around me piece by piece, I made an error. A veteran's error, which is the worst kind, because you should know better.

The schoolmaster — defrocked, though I didn't know the word yet, only that he had a scholar's stoop and ink-stained cuffs and talked like a man who'd been waiting years for an audience — asked me about paper. Of all things. Paper. Whether the mills upriver still made rag paper or had gone over to wood pulp, and whether the sizing held ink or let it feather, and I answered — I *answered*, fluently, precisely, with the enthusiasm of a woman discussing an old lover — because once, in another life, I had spent a year learning everything there was to know about paper. What it weighs. How it takes a seal. Which inks bleed through when held to candlelight, and which papers are made to be burned and which are made to survive fire.

He looked at me a moment too long. "You know your trade, Mistress Ostrow."

"I married it," I said, and gave him the widow's smile, the one that closes doors.

But inside, my heart was doing arithmetic. *Careless. Careless, careless.* Seven years of silence, undone by a question about rag content. I finished my soup and went up to my cold room and lay on the complaining bed and listened to the house settle, and told myself the truth I always told myself: they saw a widow. Widows know their husbands' trades. It's charming. It's nothing.

The bolt of linen leaned against the wall where I could see it from the bed. Forty pounds of blank paper, sewn into cloth, wearing a disguise just like mine. Names written on it — debtors, dissidents, children hidden in attics and cellars and haylofts, whole families who existed nowhere now except in my keeping. Somewhere in the Republic's records houses there were master ledgers that mirrored mine page for page, and those ledgers were about to fall into hands that believed order was a form of mercy, which is the most dangerous belief there is. Mine was the copy. Mine was, for some of those names, the only copy.

I had carried it this far by being no one. I fell asleep rehearsing how to be no one a little longer.

* * *

Morning came white and still, the kind of stillness that feels less like peace than like held breath. I was in the yard beating frost from the hem of my skirt — a widow may do her own mending; it's expected — when I heard the drums.

Not town drums. Not festival drums. These came from across the river, off the ice itself, carried flat and hard over the frozen surface the way sound travels over a lake in winter: thin, clear, wrong. Marching cadence. And beneath it, fainter, the creak and groan of many feet and wheels moving on ice, and a sound I knew from the war years and had hoped never to hear again — the high metallic complaint of gun-carriages taking a slope too fast.

I stood in the yard with my skirt in my fists and counted. Old habit. You count drums to count companies; you count companies to count men; you count men to count your chances, and the arithmetic is almost always ugly.

Marta Voss came out into the yard beside me, drying her hands on her apron. She cocked her head at the sound the way you'd listen to weather.

"They're early," she said.

"You were expecting them?"

"We're always expecting them. Expecting and ready are different animals." She squinted across the river toward the far bank, where — now that I followed her gaze — I could make out a dark line moving on the white, orderly as stitching. "Requisition column, by the pace of them. They'll want the granary tallies, the tax rolls, the records house. They'll want lists." She said the word *lists* the way other people say *plague*. "Colonel Rennick's people, if the reports hold. He does his requisitioning thoroughly."

"How thoroughly?"

"He requisitioned a village's bell once," Marta said. "Then the priest who complained. Then the village's opinion of him, which took longer." She turned and looked at me then, fully, and I had the sensation of a ledger being opened. "You'll be wanting to deliver that linen soon, Mistress Ostrow. Before the roads get complicated."

"It's only linen," I said.

"Mm," said Marta Voss, and went inside.

That was the moment I understood my situation with perfect clarity, the way you understand cold water the instant you're under it. For seven years I had been invisible, and invisibility had seemed a permanent condition, like being left-handed or unlucky. But it wasn't. It was a lease, and the army had come to terminate it. Every soldier crossing that ice was a day subtracted from my anonymity. When the requisition officers reached the records house and found the master ledger, they would begin hunting copies — and copies, in the end, meant couriers, and couriers meant me. The weight of blank paper in my room was about to become the heaviest thing in Callowmere.

I went back inside, up the stairs, and put my hand flat against the bolt of linen. Through the cloth I could feel the edges of the pages, hundreds of them, patient as sleeping birds.

"Hold on," I whispered, which was foolish — paper can't hear — but seven years alone teaches you to talk to the things that keep you alive.

Downstairs, the twins were arguing about whether the army would billet at the inn, the schoolmaster was pretending to read, and the clockmaker's hands had gone still over his broken clock, his head tilted toward the window and the sound coming over the ice.

Through the frosted glass, across the river, the dark line grew longer.

They were coming early. And I had not yet decided what I was willing to burn.
