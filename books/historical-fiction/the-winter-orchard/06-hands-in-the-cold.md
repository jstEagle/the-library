## 6. Hands in the Cold

The shears were older than I was, and Marta handled them like a living thing.

She'd brought them out of the tool shed wrapped in sacking, oiled and honed despite the cold, and when she pressed them into my hands that first morning in the orchard she said only: "Cut at an angle. The tree remembers where you've been careless."

I thought it was a saying. It took me three days to understand it was a warning.

We worked the south rows first, where the frost had split two of the old apple trunks down to the heartwood. Marta moved along the branches with a sureness that had nothing to do with strength — she was past sixty, her knuckles swollen like walnuts, her breath coming in short white plumes — but every cut she made was deliberate, placed, chosen. She pruned the way I had once been trained to write dispatches: nothing wasted, nothing accidental.

"Higher," she said, without looking up. "You're cutting for the fruit this year. Cut for ten years."

I adjusted my grip. "I don't know anything about trees."

"You know about keeping things alive." She snipped a water-sprout clean off. "That's most of it."

The snow squeaked under our boots. Somewhere behind us, in the house, Frau Lindqvist was arguing with the coal merchant, her voice carrying across the frozen garden with the clarity of a woman who had never once lost an argument. The boarding house held eleven souls now — a Latvian printer who coughed all night, two Estonian sisters who taught languages by correspondence, a Finnish clerk who had stopped speaking altogether sometime before Christmas. Exiles, all of them, the wreckage of small nations washed up on neutral shores. I had been among them nine days and had learned their routines the way I learned everything: silently, completely, and without being asked.

Marta had been here longest. Longer than Frau Lindqvist's stewardship, longer than the war, if the other boarders were to be believed. She had come with the estate's previous owner as a gardener and simply stayed when he died, tending an orchard nobody harvested anymore because there was no one left to sell apples to. The trees kept bearing anyway. That seemed to me the whole tragedy of the world in miniature.

"Your hands," Marta said suddenly.

I looked down. My palms were raw from the shears, the skin splitting at the base of the fingers where the metal bit. Courier's hands, Vask would have called them — hands that had carried things too long and too far.

"Gloves dull the feel," I said.

"Mm." She took my wrist, turned it over, examined the calluses with the frankness of a woman who had delivered babies and buried husbands and saw no reason to be delicate about bodies. Her thumb found the ridge of hard skin along my forefinger. "You didn't learn these holding a pen."

"No," I said.

She waited. It was a particular kind of waiting, patient and bottomless, and I recognized it because I used it myself — the silence you offer someone so they can decide how much truth they can afford. I gave her the version I'd prepared for the household, the one that cost the least.

"I worked in a government office. In Riga. Filing, mostly. Then the fighting came close and we burned the files and I left."

"Burned them yourself?"

"There wasn't time to wait for permission."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. Approval, maybe, or its cousin. She released my wrist and went back to the tree.

"My son filed things too," she said.

The words landed in the space between us and sat there. I kept cutting. Above us a crow crossed the pale sky, and the wind came off the strait smelling of salt and iron.

"He joined the army in the second year," Marta went on, conversational, terrible. "He was twenty-two. He wrote to me every week until the week he didn't." Snip. "They told me it was a battle near a river. They always say near a river. As if the river is the important part."

"What was his name?" I asked, because it seemed unforgivable not to ask.

"Jaanis." She said it the way you set something heavy down carefully so it doesn't break. "Jaanis Ozols. He had my hands. Big ones. He wanted to be a carpenter before they made him a soldier."

I knew the battle. Of course I knew the battle. I had stood in a freezing signals room while men read casualty lists aloud in flat voices, and I had memorized the names of the dead along with everything else, because Vask said a courier must carry grief as well as orders — *you cannot choose what matters, Beatrix, you can only choose what you do with it.* The engagement on the Dvina, October 1917. The last offensive. The one where Colonel Ilmari Vask committed three regiments to a crossing the intelligence had sworn was safe, and the intelligence had lied, or been wrong, or been sold, and the regiments went into the water and did not come out.

Ozols. I searched the memory-palace, the great cold hall of names I had built in 1913 and never torn down. Private Jaanis Ozols, 4th Regiment, listed missing on the third day, confirmed dead by divisional record in November. There was a face attached to it in the files — young, broad, a carpenter's jaw — and now there was a mother attached to it, standing four feet away from me with pruning shears in her swollen hands.

*Vask's last battle.* The phrase lived in my chest like a swallowed stone. The battle that broke him, they said afterward. The battle after which he was never the same, and then the republic fell anyway, and then he died — officially, in a bombing raid on Riga, in a grave I had visited, in a coat I now owned with a letter sewn into its lining summoning me to this very orchard at midwinter.

A letter written in his hand by someone who was not him.

"Say something," Marta said mildly. "You've gone strange."

"The cold does that," I said.

"It isn't the cold." She looked at me then, fully, her eyes gray and unhurried under the wool cap. "You've been strange since you came. You listen at doors without meaning to — no, don't apologize, it's a habit, like biting nails. You know which boarders take sugar in their coffee and which lie about taking sugar. You watched the road for two days before you asked anyone why there was a man watching it." She turned back to the tree. "I'm old, girl, not blind."

My heart was doing the thing Vask had trained out of it years ago, the leap and scramble, and I made it be still. "What do you want me to say?"

"Nothing. I want you to prune." Snip. A dead branch fell. "But I'll tell you something for free, since we're working together. This house keeps people. That's what it's for. Frau Lindqvist asks no questions because questions are dangerous and she has refugees to protect. The sisters upstairs ask no questions because they're afraid. The printer asks no questions because he's paid to forget things professionally." She wiped her nose on her sleeve. "I don't ask questions because I already know the answers, mostly, and the answers are always the same. Somebody's son is dead and somebody else is running."

The shears felt very heavy in my hand.

"And you?" I managed. "Why do you stay? You could have gone anywhere. Sweden. America."

"Where?" She gestured with the shears at the orchard, the gray sea beyond it, the whole ruined century. "These trees were planted by a man who is dead, for children who are scattered, in a country that isn't ours. If I leave, who cuts them?" She bent to gather the fallen branch, joints cracking, and added, quieter: "Besides. Jaanis would have hated it here. Too quiet. But he'd have hated more the idea of the trees going wild."

* * *

We broke at noon for bread and coffee in the potting shed, because the kitchen belonged to Frau Lindqvist's jurisdiction and the potting shed belonged to Marta, and there was a treaty between them older than any armistice. The shed smelled of earth and creosote and stored apples going soft in crates — a sweetness like something dying honorably. Marta poured coffee from a dented pot onto a spirit stove and we sat on upturned seed boxes, our boots nearly touching.

I had decided, somewhere between the third row of trees and the crow, to test her.

It was not a decision I was proud of. Vask had a rule about civilians: *you do not use them, you do not spend them, they are not currency.* But I had a forged letter in my coat lining, a dead colonel who might be alive, a brother whose handwriting I hadn't yet let myself think about properly, and a Danish officer on the road whose questions were too precise to be accident. I needed to know whether this house would shelter a fugitive or surrender one. And Marta was the spine of the house. Everyone knew it, including, I suspected, Frau Lindqvist.

So I did it the way Vask taught probing questions — sideways, buried in sympathy, deniable.

"Jaanis," I said. "In the army. Did he ever speak about his officers? Afterward, in letters?"

Marta blew on her coffee. "He spoke about one. The colonel. Vask." She pronounced it correctly, the Estonian way, and something in my chest pulled tight. "Jaanis worshipped him. Said he was the only officer who ate what the men ate and remembered the men's mothers' names." A dry sound, almost a laugh. "Including mine. Can you imagine? A colonel writing to say he was sorry, and using my name, and getting it right."

*I know,* I almost said. *I transcribed those letters. I carried some of them.* Because I had — condolence letters, in the weeks after the Dvina, dozens of them, dictated by Vask in a voice like gravel and copied out in my steadiest hand. He had insisted on getting the mothers' names right. He had spent a night drinking and then a morning composing, and he had said to me, hollow-eyed: *Names are the only thing we can give them that they can't lose again.*

"He blamed him, though," Marta said, and I looked up sharply. "At the end. His last letter — the one that came after the battle, from a hospital. He wrote that the colonel was a good man who was given bad maps." She stared into her cup. "Bad maps. That's what killed my son. Not bullets. Paper."

The shed was very quiet. The spirit stove hissed.

"Did he hate him for it?" I asked. Carefully. Sideways.

"No." She said it with finality, the way you state the weather. "Hate needs someone to blame. Jaanis understood there wasn't one man to blame. There was a whole machine." She refilled my cup without asking. "But I'll tell you what I decided, sitting in this shed, the winter they sent me his things. I decided that if that colonel was still alive anywhere — if he was hiding in a ditch or a cellar or a boat — I wouldn't be the one to point at him. Because pointing is what the machine does. And somebody has to stop doing what the machine does, even if it's just an old woman with an orchard."

The coffee scalded going down. I had come into the shed to test her loyalties and instead she had handed me, unprompted, unknowing, absolution in advance — for whatever I was carrying, whoever I might hide, whatever I intended to do on the ice behind the orchard that I had not yet let myself look at directly.

"You think he's alive," I said. It came out before I could weigh it. "Vask."

Marta looked at me for a long moment. Steam rose between us.

"I think dead men write letters," she said.

The floor of the world tilted slightly. I kept my face still — training holds even when your blood doesn't — but inside, everything was falling and rearranging itself, the way ice shifts on a strait.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"No," she agreed placidly, and drank her coffee. "You wouldn't. Being a discreet girl." She stood, groaning, and gathered the cups. "Finish yours. The east rows won't prune themselves, and the light goes early."

At the door she paused, her hand on the frame, her back to me.

"The coat you wear," she said. "The gray one with the torn pocket. It was his. I mended it for him myself, the winter before the Dvina, when he stayed here a week — he and another officer, passing through, quiet as thieves. He wore it walking these rows." She glanced back over her shoulder, and her eyes were not unkind. "So whatever's sewn into it, girl, or hidden in it, or written on it — you should know the pockets have been mended twice. Once by me. Once by someone who didn't know I'd already done it."

She went out into the snow.

I sat alone in the potting shed with the cooling coffee and the smell of dying apples, and I did not move for a long time. Twice mended. Which meant the letter had not been sewn in by Vask — Marta's stitching was the original, and the second seam, the clumsy one I'd taken for age, had been added later, after the coat had passed through other hands. Someone had opened the lining, put the letter in, and closed it again. Someone who had access to Vask's coat after Riga. Someone who knew the coat would find its way to me.

Someone who mended pockets badly.

Erik. The name rose in me and I pushed it down, hard, the way you push down a branch to see what's underneath — and underneath was everything I had refused to look at since the day I found the letter: my brother's careful, ambitious, adoring hand; the game he used to play in the barracks, copying the Colonel's capitals for laughs; the fact that Erik had been Vask's aide-de-camp, had access to his effects, had survived the fall of Riga when better men hadn't, and had known — he alone had known — that the coat was mine.

No. Not Erik. Anyone but Erik. There had to be another explanation, and I would find it, methodically, the way I'd been trained, and until I found it I would not —

Outside, the light was already failing over the east rows. Marta was waiting with the shears, patient as winter.

I drained the coffee, bitter and cold, and went out to work beside her, carrying what I carried.
