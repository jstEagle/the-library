## 7. The Forger's Mark

The letter lived inside the lining of her coat for six days before she took it out again.

She had told herself she was waiting. That was the professional word for it — *waiting* — as though the paper were a trap that needed time to go stale, as though the ink might lose its teeth if she left it alone long enough. But the truth was simpler and less flattering: she had been afraid of it. She, who had carried dispatches through checkpoints with soldiers' hands inches from the seam where they were sewn, who had recited eleven pages of cipher under a lamp while a man wrote down everything she said and looked for the lie in her face — she was afraid of one sheet of paper in her own coat.

Because if the letter was real, then Ilmari Vask had written it three weeks after the world said he was dead, and that meant either a miracle or a cruelty, and Beatrix Nystrom had stopped believing in miracles somewhere on the road out of Riga.

And if the letter was false —

She did not finish the thought. She made tea instead. She washed the boarding house's breakfast dishes for Fru Dahl, who paid her in coin and complaints in equal measure. She went out to the orchard in the grey afternoon and stood among the frozen apple trees with the pruning knife in her pocket and did not prune anything, because Marta had not come out today, and there was something about the orchard when it was empty that made thinking possible, and thinking was what she had been avoiding.

At four o'clock the light began to fail. The sky over the strait turned the color of pewter, and the first lamps came on in the village below, and Beatrix went up to her room, locked the door, and took the letter out of the coat.

* * *

She laid it flat on the table beneath the window, where the last of the daylight fell across it at an angle. Old habit. Vask had taught her that too: *Never read a document straight on. Light is an interrogator. Give it something to work with.*

The letter was short. It always looked shorter than it felt.

*To B.N. — If you are reading this, then the orchard has found you as I hoped it would. Come at midwinter. Come alone. There is an account to settle that only you can hear. You will know me by the old sign. — I.V.*

She had read it perhaps forty times since finding it sewn into the coat's shoulder seam, folded into a square no bigger than a communion wafer. Forty times, and every time the same two facts had stood up and refused to sit down. First: the hand was Vask's. Not similar to Vask's — *was* his, the narrow forward slant, the letters that leaned like men walking into wind, the peculiar way he crossed his t's low and leftward, a stroke like a fence rail. She had spent three years reading his orders, his marginalia, his corrections to her exercises. She knew that hand the way she knew her own reflection.

Second: Vask was dead. Shot at Cēsis, or drowned in the retreat, or taken east — the accounts disagreed, but they agreed on the essential point, which was that Ilmari Vask had ceased to exist in August of 1919, and this letter was dated the twenty-third of September.

A dead man does not write letters. Therefore the letter was forged. Therefore someone had gone to extraordinary lengths to forge it well — had studied his hand, had practiced, had known enough of his phrasing to produce *an account to settle that only you can hear*, which sounded exactly like him because it *was* him, it was a sentence he used, she had heard him say versions of it a dozen times in the training house outside Tartu, tapping the table with two fingers.

And therefore — this was the part she kept circling, the cold center — whoever had written it knew Vask intimately. Not from files. Not from captured papers. Intimately. Well enough to steal not just his handwriting but his voice.

There were perhaps five people alive who knew him that well. She was one. She had not written it.

She sat down at the table and made herself do the thing she had been avoiding for six days, which was to stop looking at the letter as a message and start looking at it as an object. A made thing. Something with a maker, and makers leave marks.

* * *

Vask's first lesson in forgery detection had not been about forgery at all. It had been about pride.

Tartu, spring of 1914. She was nineteen and certain she was clever, and she had produced — in an evening, with a borrowed pen — a passable imitation of a requisition order bearing the signature of a quartermaster major whose hand she'd studied for a week. She'd brought it to Vask the way a cat brings a bird to the doorstep. He'd held it up to the lamp, turned it once, twice, and set it down without expression.

"Very good," he said. "You'll be shot within the month."

"Sir?"

"You've signed your own name." He tapped the paper, once, near the bottom. "Not the letters. The pressure. Look." He turned it toward the light. "You bear down on your capitals the way a plowman bears down on the first furrow of the morning. All your strength goes into the beginning. Your small letters are careful because you're watching yourself by then, but the capitals — the capitals are where you still believe you're honest." He slid the paper back across the table. "Everyone has a letter like that. A letter where the hand stops pretending. Find yours, and kill it, and then find everyone else's, and never tell them you know."

She had spent that summer killing her own capital letters, training herself to enter each one at the same weight as the last stroke of the word before it, until writing became for her a continuous motion with no doors in it, no places where the self could slip through. It had worked. She had passed inspections in Helsinki, Stockholm, Copenhagen. Men with magnifying glasses and bored eyes had held her documents to the light and seen nothing but a tired woman traveling on someone else's passport.

And now she sat in a rented room in Denmark with a dead man's letter under a dying light, and she did the only thing left to do.

She stopped reading the words and started weighing them.

* * *

It took her an hour.

The daylight failed entirely and she lit the candle and moved it slowly along the lines, and she forced herself to be patient, to be the boring official she had spent years impersonating, to look at each letter as a stranger. The narrow slant — correct. The low leftward crossings — correct. The ink, a common iron-gall mix, aged convincingly; she touched the corner of the page and smelled the faint vinegar ghost of it, correct. Whoever had made this had not hurried. Whoever had made this had practiced for weeks, maybe months, had filled sheets and burned them, had learned to move their wrist the way another man's wrist moved.

Then she reached the salutation. *To B.N.*

Her own initials. Her breath did something complicated, and she made it stop, and she looked.

The *B* was perfect. The *N* was perfect. Both of them entered at the same even weight as the strokes around them, both of them lean and windblown, both of them Vask's exactly — so exactly that for a moment she thought she'd been wrong, that grief had made her see forgery where there was only miracle.

Then she moved the candle to the body of the letter, and found the *I* of *If you are reading this*, and the *C* of *Come at midwinter*, and the *I* of *I hoped*, and the *C* of *Come alone*, and she laid them side by side in her mind the way Vask had taught her to lay any two documents side by side, and she saw it.

The capitals in the body of the letter were heavier than the capitals in the salutation. Slightly. Barely. The difference between a hand pressing and a hand resting — the weight of a feather, the weight of nothing, invisible to anyone who hadn't spent a summer of her nineteenth year grinding her own instinct out of her fingers under a hard-mouthed colonel's eye.

But it was there, and once she saw it she could not unsee it, and worse — far worse — she recognized it.

Because it was *her* flaw. Or it had been. The plowman's capital, the first-furrow press, the strength going into the beginning. Vask had mocked her for it across a whole spring — *you write your capitals like you're breaking ground, Nystrom, are you farming or corresponding?* — until she killed it, until she trained it out of herself letter by letter, month by month.

Someone else had inherited it.

Someone who had sat in those same rooms in Tartu. Someone whose hand Vask had also corrected, also shaped, also watched for hours across a table — and here the deduction arrived all at once, the way ice lets go of a bank, the whole sheet of it shifting and groaning and going, and Beatrix sat very still in the candlelight with her hands flat on the table because if she didn't hold them down they would shake.

The list of people who knew Vask's hand intimately was five names long. But the list of people who had learned to forge it *from Vask himself* — who had been taught by him, corrected by him, drilled by him in the same spring she had — who would carry, buried in their muscles, the same fault he had flogged out of her and out of them, a fault that would resurface precisely when the forger's attention was divided, precisely when the mind was busy composing a message and the hand was left to its oldest habits —

The salutation was perfect because it was two letters, written with total concentration, the way she herself had written the first line of every forged document for three years, checking herself, holding herself. The body was longer. The body was where the writer forgot. The body was where the plowman's capital came home.

She knew three other people who had trained under Vask. Two were dead — she had seen the lists, she had memorized the lists, memorizing lists was what she was *for*. One was missing, unaccounted for since the retreat, presumed taken east.

And one —

No. She put her palm flat against the thought the way you press down a loose board. Not yet. An accusation built on a feather-weight of ink was worth nothing, less than nothing, it was a weapon that fired backward. She needed more. She needed to be wrong, actually, she realized; some stubborn animal part of her wanted with a physical ache to be wrong, to find another explanation, a stranger's explanation, a safe one.

But the letter offered none. She read it again, all of it, weighing every capital, and the pattern held, and the pattern had a shape, and the shape was familiar in the worst way — familiar the way a footprint in snow outside your own door is familiar.

*Come at midwinter. Come alone.*

Midwinter was nine days away. And she was here, at the orchard, exactly where the letter had told her to be — summoned by a dead man's hand to a place she would have come to anyway, for her aunt's sake, for her own — and it occurred to her, with a slow cold clarity, that she could no longer tell which of those reasons had truly brought her. That perhaps that had been the elegance of it. That the forger had understood something essential about her: that she could be led anywhere, so long as the path looked like her own idea.

*You will know me by the old sign.* What old sign? Vask had a hundred signs — countersigns, duress words, chalk marks, the whole grammar of a trade that was now stateless. But the letter named none of them. A real summons from Vask would have. He was precise to the point of ritual; he had never in his life written *the old sign* and trusted the reader to fill in the blank. He would have written the sign itself, or half of it, or a reference number. This letter was his voice performed by someone who knew the melodies but not the discipline underneath them.

Whoever wrote this wanted her at the orchard at midwinter. Alone. Expecting Vask. Trusting.

Or — the thought arrived sideways, unwelcome — wanting her to believe she'd been summoned, so that whatever she did next, she did it as a marked woman. So that when she went out to the ice road behind the orchard, or lingered at the fence after dark, or asked Marta questions about the back water, someone was watching and noting and reporting. A letter like this didn't have to bring its target anywhere. Sometimes it only had to make her move differently. Sometimes the trap wasn't the meeting place. Sometimes the trap was the suspicion itself — because a suspicious courier starts testing loyalties, and testing loyalties means revealing them.

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark and let the cold of the room climb into her bones, because the cold helped; the cold was honest.

Below, in the boarding house, floorboards creaked. Fru Dahl locking up. Somewhere down the road a dog, and beyond the dog the strait, black and salt-locked, holding its ice like a held breath. Nine days to midwinter. And tomorrow — she remembered with a small jolt — tomorrow Lieutenant Brandt was coming to supper. Fru Dahl had announced it at breakfast with the transparent delight of a woman who believed she was arranging a romance, and Beatrix had agreed because refusing would have been louder than accepting.

He would sit across the table from her with his careful Danish courtesy and his questions dressed as conversation, and she would have to perform the version of herself that had never found a letter in a coat lining, and she would have to do it while knowing — not suspecting, *knowing*, with the certainty of ink under lamplight — that the person who wrote that letter had learned their craft in the same rooms she had, from the same man, and was close enough to her life to know where her aunt had lived, and where her coat had hung, and how to reach into her past and sew a summons into its seams.

Five names. Two dead. One vanished east.

And one who had called her *B.N.* in a dead man's hand.

She folded the letter back into its square, sewed it into the lining with six stitches, and put on the coat, and went downstairs into the smell of bread and lamp oil, wearing it against her heart like the accusation it had become.

Fru Dahl looked up from the kitchen doorway. "Lieutenant Brandt telephoned," she said. "He'll come at seven. And he asked — imagine — whether we'd have the apple cake."

Beatrix smiled the smile she had practiced in a hundred border towns. "Tell him yes," she said, and wondered, as she said it, which of the five names was already in Copenhagen, and how close.
