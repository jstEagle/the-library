## 23. Sisters and Brothers

He came on the fourth day of December, in a motorcar with diplomatic plates and a driver who did not get out.

I was in the orchard when it came up the road — I had taken to being in the orchard whenever any car came up the road, spade in hand, looking like exactly what I appeared to be: a woman turning frozen ground around her aunt's apple trees. The spade was real. The work was real. Only the innocence of me was false, and that part no one could see.

The car stopped at the gate. The driver stayed behind the wheel, breath steaming, eyes forward like a man paid not to notice things. And from the far door stepped my brother.

Erik had always been beautiful the way a drawn blade is beautiful — long lines, quick hands, a face that made strangers trust him before he'd earned it. He was thinner now. The uniform under his greatcoat was not the one he'd worn leaving Riga; it was cut to occupation pattern, grey where ours had been green, and the sight of that grey on his shoulders did something to me that three years of war had failed to do. It made me cold in a way the weather couldn't touch.

"Beatrix." He said it carefully, as if the word might break.

"Erik." I set the spade against the fence post. My hands wanted to shake and I forbade them to. "You're a long way from your ministry."

"Not so far. Copenhagen is an hour by rail, and the roads are good this time of year." He smiled, and it was our mother's smile on a stranger's face. "May I come in?"

"It's Marta's house now. But she'll feed you if you're polite."

"I remember how to be polite."

*Do you,* I thought, and led him up the path through the dead rows.

Marta met us in the kitchen with the look she gave everyone — flat, measuring, a woman who had buried a son and therefore owed surprise to no one. She put coffee on the stove without being asked and then, in what I understood even then to be a deliberate act, took her mending basket and went out to the glasshouse, closing the door behind her with a click that said *whatever happens in here, I don't want to know.*

We were alone. That was the point of the visit, of course. Everything about it was arranged to leave us alone.

"You look well," Erik said.

"I look like a woman who digs in frozen ground for wages."

"That too." He sat down across the table from me, unhurried, and began unbuttoning his gloves finger by finger. "The house is smaller than I remembered."

"The house hasn't changed. You have."

Something moved behind his eyes — hurt, or the memory of hurt, filed away fast. "Three years," he said. "Three years, Trixie. You used to write every week."

"You know why the letters stopped."

"I know what I was told."

"And you believed it." I poured the coffee because my hands needed work. "That's the difference between us, maybe. I never believed anything anyone told me. Not after '13."

He laughed — short, real, startled out of him. "Vask's first lesson."

"Vask's only lesson. Everything else was decoration."

We drank. Outside, wind moved down the orchard rows and made the bare branches knock together like distant applause. I peeled apples while we talked — Marta's winter apples, small and hard, keeping well in the cellar — because peeling gave my eyes somewhere to be that wasn't his face. One long ribbon of skin, unbroken. Vask had taught me that too: keep your hands busy and your face will follow.

"How did you find me?" I asked.

"Aunt Lise's house was in the family papers. A boarding house for exiles, run by an old woman with Baltic sympathies. If I were looking for a fugitive courier, it's the second place I'd check."

"And the first?"

He looked at his coffee. "Wherever you'd be needed."

The ribbon of apple skin broke. I started another.

"They sent you," I said. Not a question.

"They asked for someone who spoke Danish and knew the region. I volunteered." He said it smoothly, but I heard the seam in it — the half-second pause of a man who has rehearsed a sentence until it stopped sounding rehearsed. "There are people in Riga who want to talk to you, Beatrix. Former colleagues. They're not angry. They're *worried.*"

"Worried."

"A courier disappears off the last boat out of a collapsing country. She surfaces eight months later in neutral Denmark, working in an orchard, asking no questions, wanting nothing. What would you think, if you were them?"

"I'd think she was doing exactly what she was trained to do. Waiting."

"For what?"

"For orders." I met his eyes then, and held them, and let him see nothing in mine at all. "But there's no one left to give them, is there, Erik? The government's gone. The army's gone. The men who signed the dispatches are dead or scattered or—" I let it hang. "*Or traitors.*"

The word landed between us on the table like a dropped knife. Neither of us picked it up.

"You shouldn't say such things lightly," he said quietly.

"I've never said anything lightly in my life. You know that better than anyone. You used to complain about it. *Trixie weighs every word twice before she spends it.*"

"You were seven. I was allowed to complain."

"You were allowed everything. That was the trouble." I pushed the bowl of peeled apples toward him and a paring knife, an offering and a test both. "Peel. Talk. Like when we were children."

He hesitated — and then he did it, took up the knife, drew an apple toward him across the wood. His hands were still quick. Of course they were. Quick hands, patient eyes. Our father used to say God gives every family one artist and one accountant, and in ours Erik got both gifts and I got neither, only a memory that wouldn't quit.

"Do you remember," he said, turning the apple, "the summer the Colonel taught us cards?"

"I remember you cheating."

"I remember *you* catching me cheating and saying nothing for two whole games, just watching how I did it." He glanced up. "I never decided whether that was loyalty or practice."

"Neither did I," I said honestly, and he laughed again, and for perhaps a minute we were in the old kitchen in Riga with the samovar ticking and the world intact, two children playing at adults.

Then the minute passed, as minutes do.

"There's a man watching this house," Erik said. "Danish officer. Brandt. He comes twice a week, asks questions, writes reports. Do you know what he reports?"

"He tells me he's a liaison for displaced persons. I tell him I'm a gardener. We're both very convincing."

"Trixie." He set down the knife. "Listen to me. Please. Just listen, and don't weigh the words, just hear them." His voice had changed — lower, faster, the rehearsal burned away. "There are men coming. Not Danes. Men from the east, from the occupation authority, and they don't have diplomatic passes and they don't peel apples. They believe there's a courier here, and more than a courier. They believe there's something *with* the courier. Papers. Codes. And they believe there may be a man alive whom the whole world thinks is dead, and if they find either of those things in this house—"

"Stop."

"—then nothing I've done, none of it, none of the—"

"*Stop.*" I hadn't raised my voice. I almost never raise my voice. But I said it the way Vask said *halt*, and Erik stopped, the way soldiers stop.

We looked at each other across the apples, across the steam, across twenty-five years of shared blood and three years of whatever had grown over it.

"What have you done, Erik?" I asked softly.

And I watched him decide. I watched it happen — watched the truth rise in him like water in a lock, watched him close the gate on it. His face did the thing I had seen him do since childhood: the small settling, the shutter coming down, the beautiful blade going back in its sheath.

"I've done what keeps you alive," he said. "That's all. Whatever you've heard, whatever you suspect — everything I've done since the fall has been to keep you and Mother and the girls alive. You can hate me for the methods. Don't hate me for the arithmetic."

"Arithmetic." I stood, and gathered the bowl of apples, and carried it to the window because if I didn't move I was going to do something unforgivable with the paring knife. "Our people died in ditches, Erik. There's no arithmetic that comes out of ditches."

"You don't know what I—"

"No," I said. "I don't. That's rather the point, isn't it? So tell me. Tell me plainly, brother to sister, and I'll weigh it. You know I'll weigh it fairly. It's the one thing I've ever been good at."

Silence. Long enough that the kettle ticked as it cooled. When I turned, he was standing too, buttoning his gloves again, and his face was composed and kind and utterly closed, and I understood that I had lost — that whatever battle this was, I had just lost it at my own kitchen table, over apples, with love sitting right there between us unable to speak.

"I'll come again before Christmas," he said. "There's a car that can take you south, Beatrix. Any time. No conditions. You'd be safe, and Mother would—"

"Give my love to Mother."

"Trixie—"

"Give her my love," I said, "and tell her I'm pruning the trees. Tell her the orchard needs me. She'll understand. She planted it."

He stood a moment longer. And then — this I did not expect — he crossed the room and kissed my forehead, the way he had when we were children and I'd had a nightmare, his hand cupping the back of my head, brief and fierce.

"Whatever happens," he murmured into my hair, "remember that I loved you first. Before all of it. Remember the order of things."

Then he was gone, down the path, into the car, and the engine noise faded down the road toward the coast, and I stood at the window holding a bowl of apples with my heart going like a trapped bird.

*Remember the order of things.*

As if love were a queue and he had claimed his place in it. As if the order of things could protect any of us from what was coming down that road behind him.

* * *

Marta came in from the glasshouse without pretending otherwise. She hung her mending on its hook, poured herself the last of the coffee, and sat across from me in Erik's abandoned chair.

"Well?" she said.

"He wants me to go with him. South. No conditions."

"And you said?"

"I said the orchard needs me."

Marta nodded slowly, turning her cup. She was seventy if she was a day, with forearms like tree roots and a face that had stopped performing decades ago. "My son Jānis," she said, to the window, "had a brother-friend from school. Guna's boy. When the Russians came through the first time, that boy made a choice — informed on a weapons cache, saved his own skin, thought he was saving his family too. And you know what happened? They shot the family anyway, later, for other reasons. And the boy lived to be old, and every year on the anniversary he came to Jānis's grave and stood there, and I watched him stand there, winter after winter." She looked at me. "Hate is simple, girl. It was the pity that wore me out."

"Marta—"

"I'm not telling you anything. Old women don't tell young women anything; we just say words near them and hope the frost takes." She rose, took the bowl of apples, and began putting them away in the cellar crock. At the door she paused. "Your brother's hands shook when he peeled. Did you see? First three turns of the skin, they shook. Then he steadied himself." She went down the stairs. "A man steady in war and shaking over apples — that's a man at war with himself. Those are the dangerous ones. And the sad ones. Often both."

* * *

I walked the orchard until dark, which in December means walking it until four o'clock. Frost was coming out of the ground in white breath along the fence line. At the back corner, where the hedge thinned and the slope ran down to the strait, I stopped and looked at the ice — grey-white, wind-scoured, stretching toward the far shore where the church tower stood up small against the sky.

Somewhere out there, if he lived, was a man I had promised to judge with my own eyes. Somewhere behind me, in a warm ministry office, was a brother I had promised myself I would never stop loving, and who had just sat at my table and lied to me with his whole body except his hands.

Two promises. Two directions. And between them, me, standing in a dead orchard, learning at last what Vask had tried to spare me: that the hardest battlefield isn't the one with artillery. It's a kitchen table. It's a peeled apple. It's the face of someone you'd die for, arranging itself into kindness while it hides the thing that might kill you.

I went inside and lit the lamp and took the forged letter from its hiding place in the flour tin, and spread it on the table under the light, and looked — really looked, for the hundredth time — at the capital letters. The little backward lean of the *V*. The flaw that wasn't Vask's.

It was Erik's. It had been Erik's since the moment I saw it, and I had known, and I had refused to know, and Marta was right: the pity wears you out long before the hate does.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would walk the ice road again. Tomorrow I would count the searchlight intervals and mark the thin places. Because whatever my brother had sold, and whoever was coming behind his diplomatic plates, they were coming soon — and the crossing would not wait for Christmas.

Outside, far down the fence line, something moved. A shape, there and gone, tall and stooped against the snow.

I blew out the lamp and stood in the dark, listening.
