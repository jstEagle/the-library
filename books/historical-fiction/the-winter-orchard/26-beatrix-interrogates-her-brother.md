## 26. Beatrix Interrogates Her Brother

She chose the glasshouse because it had one door and no curtains.

Marta had gone to the village with the sled, an errand that would take three hours if the road was bad and four if it was good, and Beatrix had watched the sled go down the white lane until it disappeared, counting the minutes the way Vask had taught her to count everything — not with a clock but with the body, the way a heartbeat fills a silence, the way a kettle takes two hundred heartbeats to sing. Then she went to the room Erik was using, the small one at the back with the view of the frozen garden, and knocked once, and did not wait for an answer.

He was sitting on the bed with his boots off, reading a ministry dispatch he had not bothered to hide, because he believed — this was the thing about Erik, the thing that had always been true and had finally become fatal — that his position protected him. That paper was armor. That a diplomatic pass was a wall. He looked up at her with the expression he had worn since he was six years old and caught: caught at the jam, caught at the cards, caught at the Colonel's letters — and behind the guilt, already assembling itself, the smile that had gotten him out of everything.

"Beatrix. You look terrible. Is it the cold, or—"

"Shut the door is behind you," she said. "Come with me."

He came. That was the first surprise. She had prepared for refusal, for the raised voice, for the invocation of his handlers and his immunity and his precious pass, and instead he set down the dispatch, pulled his boots back on with the unhurried air of a man humoring a child, and followed her across the yard with the snow squeaking under them both. Perhaps he thought she wanted a private reconciliation. Perhaps he wanted one. She would learn, in the next hour, that both things could be true in the same man at the same time, and that this was the whole horror of him.

The glasshouse stood at the end of the orchard wall, its panes furred with frost on the outside, its inside smelling of damp earth and dead tomato vines and the ghost of summer. Beatrix had cleaned it in November, before she understood what it would be for. Now she shut the door, put the iron bar across it — a length of firewood, wedged — and turned to face her brother in the grey-green light.

He looked around, and the humor drained out of his face by degrees, the way color drains out of a sky before snow.

"You've been talking to Brandt," he said.

"I've been talking to the letters."

* * *

She had brought them. She had carried them against her body for two days, inside the lining of the coat she had mended in her first week at the orchard house — the coat that had given up Vask's letter, and so had earned the keeping of the others. There were four now. The original summons, dated three weeks after the Colonel's reported death. A second, intercepted by Brandt before it reached the house, addressed to the Danish liaison's superior, describing "the courier Nystrom" in terms so precise they could only have been written by blood. A third, which she had taken from the pocket of one of Erik's watchers when the fool left it drying over the kitchen stove — a route map of the southern ice, annotated in a hand she had known since childhood. And the fourth she had written herself, two nights ago, copying Vask's capitals from memory, and it had taken her eleven attempts, and on the eleventh she had sat back with her hands shaking and understood, finally and completely, how good her brother had become at this, and how long he must have practiced.

She laid them out on the potting bench, one by one, in the order she wanted him to see them.

Erik did not touch them. He stood with his hands in the pockets of his beautiful ministry coat and looked at the paper the way a man looks at a mirror he has been avoiding.

"Where did you get the second one?" he asked. His voice was careful.

"From a man who was following me. Where did you lose it?"

"I didn't lose it. It was a copy. The originals go to—" He stopped himself. The word *handlers* sat in the air between them, unspoken, enormous.

"To Riga," Beatrix said. "Or whatever they're calling it now. The occupation government. Section Four." She had gotten the number from Brandt, who had gotten it from a clerk, and the clerk would pay for it eventually, and Beatrix had decided not to think about that. "You report to a Major Lindqvist. He reports upward. And the reports are about me, and about a colonel of a republic that no longer exists, and about a letter that was supposed to summon that colonel to a frozen orchard where men with rifles would be waiting."

"Beatrix—"

"You wrote it in his hand." Her voice did not rise. She had trained it never to rise; Vask had beaten that into her in a summer courtyard in 1913, *a courier who shouts is a courier who is heard, and a courier who is heard is a courier who is dead.* "You wrote it in his hand and you dated it after his death and you used his private salutation, the one about the bees, the one he only ever used with family — and do you know how I knew it was false, Erik? Do you know how I knew my own brother had written it?"

He said nothing. The frost on the glass ticked as the sun found it.

"Because of the capital letters." She picked up the forged summons and held it where the light came through. "He made a *V* like a bird in flight, always, and you make it like a soldier at attention. He told me once it was the only vanity he allowed himself. You were in the room when he said it. You laughed. And when you sat down to forge him, you made the *V* the way *you* make it, because you were never taught to forge him — you were taught to write, and you forgot that forgery is forgetting yourself." She set the letter down. Her hands were steady. She was almost grateful for that, almost proud, and then she saw that her steadiness was the thing that frightened him most, and she stopped being proud of anything at all. "You forgot yourself for every letter but one. And the one letter you couldn't forget yourself in was the letter where you pretended to be the man who raised you."

Erik sat down. Not gracefully. His knees simply stopped agreeing to hold him, and he sat on the upturned seed crate beside the bench, and for a moment he looked like what he was — twenty-six years old, grey-faced, a boy in a stolen coat.

"You don't know what it was like," he said. "After the surrender. After they took the archive."

"Tell me."

"You want the chain? Fine. I'll give you the chain." He laughed, a small broken sound with no humor anywhere in it. "You'll hate me either way, so I might as well be thorough."

* * *

He talked for a long time. Beatrix stood by the bench and let him, because this was the other thing Vask had taught her: when a man finally speaks, you do not interrupt, because every interruption costs you a sentence you will want later.

They had taken him in December of '18, he said — not arrested, nothing so clean. Invited. A car, a driver, a ministry building with the republic's eagle still bolted over the door because the occupiers found it useful, a kind of trophy, a kind of joke. Major Lindqvist had been polite. Lindqvist was always polite; Erik said the word *polite* the way other men said a disease. They had a list. The list had courier routes — the winter routes, the ice routes, the ones only three living people knew — and the routes had stopped working in November, which meant someone inside the network was still moving, and they wanted the someone.

"And they had names," Erik said. "They had the names of the men in the Riga cell. Tomas. Anders. The brothers Vītols. You remember them."

She remembered them. She had carried dispatches to them.

"They had them in custody. Not charged. Just — held. And Lindqvist said, very reasonably, over very good coffee, that the men would be released if the routes were confirmed. Confirmed, Beatrix. Not sold. Confirmed. He made it sound like a clerk's errand. Like correcting a misprint." Erik's hands had come out of his pockets and were turning over each other in his lap. "And I thought — it's paper. It's old paper. The routes are dead anyway if the network's blown. I'll confirm two of them, the two that are already burned, and the men walk out, and nobody loses anything except ink."

"And they shot them anyway."

"They shot them in February." His voice went flat, which was how she knew how deep it went. "All five. After I'd confirmed four routes. Because Lindqvist said the confirmation had been — incomplete. And then he said, still polite, still with the coffee, that my service had been noted, and that there would be other occasions to be useful. And there were. There always are, with men like that. It's not a transaction. It's a hook. You give them one thing and you're theirs, because now you're the man who did the first thing, and the first thing gets worse every day you don't confess it, and so you do the second thing to make the first thing smaller, and it never gets smaller. It only gets heavier." He looked up at her. "You'd have said no. I know that. You'd have let them shoot Tomas and walked out with your spine intact and I used to hate you for it, a little, in the good years, before I understood it was the only thing about you I should have copied."

Beatrix kept her face still. Inside, something was tearing along a seam that had been weakening for weeks.

"The letter," she said. "The summons. That was the second thing."

"The letter was the ninth thing." He said it without pride. "The letter was Lindqvist's idea, not mine. They knew Vask was alive — a deserter's report out of Königsberg, a description that matched. And they knew he'd never come to any address they offered, because he's Vask, because he trusts paper he can smell the lies in. But he'd come for you. Or he'd come to warn you. Either way he'd come to the orchard, and the orchard is Danish soil, which is the whole elegance of it — if he crosses onto neutral ground they can take him quietly, and if he doesn't come, then the letter tells them where *you* are, and you lead them to him eventually, because you're his, the way I was his." He paused. "The way I am theirs."

"And your rehabilitation. Your pass. Your beautiful coat."

"They've offered it in writing. Full amnesty, restored commission, my name cleared — my name, Beatrix, do you understand, they've had me listed as a collaborator since March, they've had *me* on their own lists, I'm a tool with a file — if the Colonel is delivered. Alive. To a specific pier in Copenhagen, on a specific night." He swallowed. "New Year's Eve."

The frost ticked. Somewhere out in the orchard a branch cracked under the weight of snow, a sound like a small shot, and both of them flinched.

"You knew about the pier," Beatrix said slowly. "You knew about the night. Which means you know the operation is moving early. Which means you know Brandt has been confined, and the seizure has been scheduled, and the watchers on the southern road are yours."

"I know what I've been told."

"You know everything you've been told. That was always your gift, Erik. You remember what people tell you." She came around the bench then, and stood over him, and for the first time since he had arrived under his diplomatic pass she let herself look at him — really look, the way Vask had taught her to look at a document, for the flaw in the capitals, for the truth under the hand. And what she saw was not a traitor. That was the unbearable discovery of the whole hour. She saw a boy who had tried to save five men with four routes and had buried them with his own signature; a boy who had spent a year doing monstrous arithmetic, one betrayal weighed against one life, and finding the sum never came out; a boy who had forged his father-figure's hand because it was the only coin he had left to spend, and had spent it hoping — she believed this, she saw it in the wreck of his face, she would spend years deciding whether believing it was mercy or weakness — hoping the Colonel would be smart enough not to come.

"You could still stop it," she said.

"No."

"You could walk into the Danish ministry with me tomorrow. Everything you've told me. Lindqvist, the pier, the night. Brandt would—"

"Brandt is in custody and you know it. And if I defect, they don't lose the operation — they accelerate it. And the first thing they accelerate is you." He stood up, and he was taller than her, he had been taller than her since he was fourteen, and it had never meant anything until now. "You think I haven't run this? Every night since I got here? You think I haven't sat in that little room doing the arithmetic?" His voice cracked and he let it. "There is no version where I confess and everyone lives, Beatrix. There's the version where I deliver Vask and live, and there's the version where I don't and we all — " He stopped. Started again, quieter. "There's a version where I burn it all down and I'm the hero of my own confession, and it lasts about a day, and then Lindqvist's colleagues in Copenhagen explain to the Danish government that a Baltic forger with a diplomatic pass has been running an operation on their soil, and the orchard house gets closed, and the refugees get sent back east, and Mama and Papa are on the first boat to Riga. You've seen what's happening to the boats."

The name of their parents landed in the glasshouse and sat there between them, and Beatrix understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that this was the true hostage. Not Vask. Not her. The two old people in a rented room in a Copenhagen boarding house, whose existence was the quiet guarantee that kept her brother polite and her own silence advisable. They had never needed to threaten her directly. They had only needed to love her family enough to use them.

"You've left me one road," she said.

"I've left you the road where nobody else gets hurt." Erik picked up his gloves from the bench, unhurried again, the armor going back on piece by piece. "Deliver him, Beatrix. He's old and he's tired and he's a symbol, and symbols are the only things wars can't kill — they just get reissued. Lindqvist won't even — " He stopped himself, and she watched him decide not to lie, and it was almost worse than the lie. "They'll try him. It'll be quiet. He might live. Old men survive tribunals; it's the young ones who get shot."

"He taught you to ride," Beatrix said. "Before the war. He taught you to ride on that lame mare at his brother's farm, and you cried when she was put down, and he told you that loving a thing doesn't save it, but it's still the only reason to be there."

Erik's hand stopped on the door bar.

"I'm asking you to be there," she said. "Not for him. For the boy with the mare. Because the man you're planning to hand over on New Year's Eve is the last person alive who remembers that boy, and if you do this, Erik, you won't have sold a colonel. You'll have sold the only witness to who you were before all this. And then there will be no version of you that anyone can rehabilitate, including you."

He stood with his back to her for a long moment. The frost was melting on the south-facing panes, and the light through the glass had gone from grey to the pale gold of a winter afternoon already dying.

"You sound like him," he said finally. "That's the cruelest thing about you. You've always sounded like him."

Then he lifted the bar, and opened the door, and the cold came in off the orchard like a held breath finally released, and he walked back across the snow toward the house without looking at the letters still laid out on the bench — all but one. At the door of the glasshouse he paused, and without turning, without seeing it, he said:

"The pier is the Nordre Toldbod. Eleven o'clock. And Beatrix — the watchers on the southern ice aren't Lindqvist's men. Two of them are mine. Whatever you're planning — and I know you're planning, you've been planning since the day you found the letter — plan around that."

He walked up the white lane toward the house, his ministry coat dark against the snow, and Beatrix stood alone among the dead tomato vines with her brother's handwriting spread out in front of her and the last of the light going off the glass.

Then she picked up the letters, and folded them back into the coat's lining, and went to find Marta's sled tracks, because the old woman had been gone four hours now, and in this cold, four hours was either nothing at all — or everything.
