## 2. A Stranger at the Mews

He came at the hour when the mews smelled of wet straw and cooling iron, and Ada knew before he spoke that he had been waiting a long time for her to come out.

She had learned to read men the way she read handwriting — by pressure, by slant, by what they chose to hide. This one leaned against the wall beside the Daimler as though the wall were holding him up rather than the reverse. His coat had once been good; now it hung on him like a borrowed opinion. The left side of his face was a ruin of old scarring, pulled taut from temple to jaw, so that his eye on that side sat slightly wrong, always watching even when he wasn't.

"Miss Winterbourne," he said.

Her hand stopped on the crank. Nobody in this household used her surname to her face. The mistress said *Ada* in the warm, careless way of women who own things, including people. The cook said *miss* without the name attached, the way one says *the weather*. Her surname existed on paper — wage books, references, the parish roll — and nowhere else.

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said, and kept her voice level because she had practiced level voices in mirrors until they came naturally.

"I should hope so." He pushed off the wall and straightened with an effort that cost him something; she saw it travel through him like a current. "I've been standing here since half four. You drive out at six to take Mrs. Petch to the laundry, then back through Russell Square at ten past. You're punctual. That's useful."

Every instinct she owned told her to walk back inside and lock the kitchen door. Instead she set down the crank and folded her hands, because a woman who looked frightened confirmed whatever a man already believed about her, and she refused to confirm anything.

"You've mistaken me for someone who matters."

"No," he said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

His name, when he finally gave it, was Corporal — no, former corporal — Daniel Reeve, and he gave it reluctantly, like a man handing over the last coin in his pocket. He would not come inside. He stood in the mews with his shoulders hunched against the drizzle, and he kept his body angled toward the street, the habit of a man who had learned that doors could become traps.

"I need you to understand something first," he said. "Before you decide anything. I need you to understand that I know what you do."

The rain had found the gap between her collar and her neck. She did not move to fix it. "And what is it I do?"

"You steam open letters." He said it plainly, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "In the kitchen, Tuesday evenings, when the house is at church. You use the kettle and a knife you keep wrapped in flannel in the drawer behind the flour. You copy certain passages into a notebook you hide inside the lining of your glove box, under the spare wheel jack. And you reseal them so well that Lady Verity has never once caught you at it, though I suspect she knows anyway and permits it, because a woman in her position wants one person in the house who reads everything."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. Ada heard the rain on the stable roof, and beneath it her own blood, and beneath that a single thought repeating: *he has watched me, he has watched me, how long has he watched me.*

"The kettle trick," she said at last, "is older than you think. Half of Whitehall uses it."

"That's how I know about it." Something moved across the ruined side of his face — she realized it was the closest he could come to a smile. "I was taught it too. By better hands than mine."

"And whose hands were those?"

He reached into his coat slowly, telegraphing every movement the way one does with horses and frightened women, and drew out an envelope. Not sealed. Opened and resealed, badly — the gum smeared, the flap warped where it had been steamed and dried and steamed again. But the handwriting on the front stopped her breath.

She knew that hand. She had copied it twice into her notebook, letter by letter, the tall ascenders that leaned like men walking into wind, the peculiar crossed *t* that hooked backward as if retreating from its own word. Captain Elias Thorn, Royal Army Medical Corps, killed in action at Nooitgedacht, December 1900. Dead seven years. Dead officially, dead in the regimental records, dead in the memorial pamphlet Lady Verity kept in the drawing room cabinet between a hymnal and a photograph of young men squinting into colonial sun.

Dead, and writing letters that arrived in Bloomsbury postmarked from towns that did not appear on railway timetables.

"This is the second one," Reeve said. "The first went to the movement. This one—" he turned it over, and she read the direction written on the back in that same leaning hand — "*to the driver who reads.*"

Her mouth had gone dry. "He doesn't know me."

"He knows of you. There's a difference, and in our trade the difference keeps people alive." Reeve held the letter out. "Read it. Then decide whether you're angry with me or with the world. Most people are angry with the world, once they've read it."

She took it. Her fingers were steady, which surprised her; she had imagined, in the small hours, that if this moment ever came her hands would shake. They didn't. They did what hands trained on other people's secrets do — they opened the letter carefully, along the old seam, preserving the fold.

*To the driver who reads —*

*I am told you are careful, and that careful is worth more than brave just now. Three things. First: the man who carried my last letter sold its contents within a day, and the man he sold them to wears a uniform you will be invited to trust. Second: there is a house at Marlowe Bend, above the chandler's, blue door, third floor, where a woman named Hester keeps papers that will clear my name or hang me honestly, whichever God prefers. Third: if the ice on the river breaks before those papers reach safe keeping, they go under the water with everything else, and I go with them.*

*I made a promise once, to a girl in a garden, that someone would stand witness for me. I no longer expect it. But I am told you keep accounts. So consider this one entered.*

*E.T.*

Ada read it three times. On the third reading her eyes snagged on the same phrase each time — *a girl in a garden* — and something cold moved through her that had nothing to do with the rain.

Because she knew about the garden. She had copied the account of it herself, two years ago, from a letter in Margaret Thorn's hand to Lady Verity: *my brother made me swear at the Henley meeting, August 1908, that if he ever came back someone would stand witness for him, and I swore it, and I have not been able to keep the oath, and I beg you to help me keep it now.* A borrowed pen. A promise sealed in front of thirty suffragists drinking lemonade on a lawn, witnessed by everyone and remembered by no one but the two women who mattered.

Margaret Thorn was dead now too — fever, 1911 — and her promise had passed like an unclaimed parcel to whoever happened to be standing nearest when the news came. Ada had been standing nearest. She had said, quietly, to a grieving woman she barely knew: *if he ever comes back, I'll stand for him.* She had thought it a kindness. Kindnesses, she understood now, were debts with delayed payment dates.

"You've gone white," Reeve said.

"It's the weather." She folded the letter along its original creases. "Who sold the last letter?"

"A man inside your circle. I don't know which one. That's rather the emergency." He glanced up the mews toward the street, and his whole body tightened like wire. "There's a colonel at the War Office — Ashe — who has been pulling threads since autumn. Every thread leads to this house. When he pulls the last one, he won't knock. He'll come at night, and everyone in this household will be guilty of something, including the driver who reads, especially the driver who reads, because she'll have evidence in her glove box that could hang a colonel."

"How do you know what's in my glove box?"

"Because Elias Thorn told me to find out, and I'm very good at finding out, and you are very good at hiding things, and between us we've established that neither of us sleeps much." He held out his hand for the letter; she did not give it back. He almost smiled again. "Keep it. It's addressed to you. Well — to whoever you are when nobody's watching."

* * *

They walked, because walking was harder to follow than driving, and because she wanted time. Down the mews, left at the coal yard, along the canal path where the water lay flat and brown and indifferent to history. Reeve limped on his right leg and told her about it without being asked — Mauser ball, Spion Kop, a Boer field hospital where a medical captain named Thorn had spent eleven hours digging the bullet out of him with instruments boiled over a spirit flame while shells came in.

"He was supposed to die there," Reeve said. "Officially he did. It was cleaner for everyone if he did. He'd seen something at a place called Marlowe Bend — no, that's not the town, that's the false name on the letters, the real place is—" he stopped himself. "It doesn't matter. He reported what he saw up the chain, and the chain buried it, and buried him with it, and he let them, because a dead man can move and a disgraced man cannot. Seven years moving. Seven years collecting paper. And now the paper's nearly enough, and the net's nearly closed, and the only person left to carry word is a chauffeuse in Bloomsbury who steals other people's mail."

They stopped at the bridge. Below them a barge slid past, loaded with coal, its wake folding the reflections of the gasworks into ribbons.

"Why me?" Ada asked. "You could carry it yourself."

"I've been carrying things for seven years and everything I carry gets found." He looked at her then with both eyes, the whole one and the ruined one, and she saw that underneath the army-broken gauntness was something stubborn and clean. "You're invisible. That's not an insult — it's the rarest thing in England. A woman nobody sees, who sees everything, who can drive any road in this country without anyone remembering her face. Ashe has files on forty people in this movement. He has nothing on you. You're not even a footnote. Be grateful. Footnotes get arrested."

"And if I refuse?"

He considered the question honestly, which she appreciated. "Then I'll find someone else, and they'll probably be caught, and Elias will hang for a war crime he reported instead of committed, and a colonel will get a knighthood. And you'll go back to steaming envelopes on Tuesdays, knowing all of it, for the rest of your life." He paused. "Which, I'm told, is the worse sentence."

A tram rattled past on the far road. Somewhere a factory whistle blew the hour — nine o'clock, and somewhere in a fine house a lady was waking to tea she expected to be hot and a world she expected to hold still.

Ada looked down at the letter in her hand. *Consider this one entered.* She had kept accounts her whole life — wages owed, favors logged, names memorized, the great ledger of small persons upon which the visible world unknowingly rested. Here, at last, was an entry that mattered.

"There's a house in Camden," she said. "A safehouse the movement uses. If I carry word there tonight, what exactly must I say?"

Reve told her. It took four minutes. By the end of it she had memorized it, because memorizing was the one thing she had never needed paper for.

"One more thing," he said, as they turned back toward the mews. "Ashe's clerk. The man who copies the intercepted letters. Watch his hand if you ever see it. Elias says the traitor's letters match—"

He never finished. A whistle shrilled from the coal yard behind them — two notes, sharp, deliberate — and Reeve went rigid, all the ease of the canal walk burning off him in an instant.

"They're early," he said. "God help us, they're early."

Down the lane, moving fast, came two men in civilian coats who walked like soldiers, and behind them a third figure Ada recognized with a jolt that dropped through her like a stone through ice: Colonel Ashe's clerk, the pale young man from the War Office outer room, the one whose handwriting she had seen once, briefly, on a routing slip — and whose *t*'s hooked backward, retreating from their own words.

Reeve shoved the letter's meaning into her hands with nothing left to hand her but words.

"The blue door, third floor, Hester — go now, and Miss Winterbourne — whatever they offer you, whatever they threaten — *lie well.*"
