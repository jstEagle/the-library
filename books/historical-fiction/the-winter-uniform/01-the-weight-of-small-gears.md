## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The watch on my bench belonged to a merchant named Herr Albrecht, who had owned it for eleven years and abused it for ten and a half. It was a fine piece — gold-cased, quarter-repeater, the kind of thing a man buys to tell the world he can afford to know the time — and it had spent most of its life in his waistcoat pocket collecting lint, cigar ash, and what I can only describe as a civilization of crumbs. When I opened the case that morning in November of 1911, I found all three, layered like geological strata.

"Ada," my master said from across the workshop, not looking up from his own bench, "you're talking to the watch again."

"I'm not talking to it," I said. "I'm explaining things to it. There's a difference."

"There is no difference."

There was, actually, but Master Ondrusek had been repairing clocks for forty years and had long since stopped believing anything happened inside a case that he couldn't do with his own hands. I respected him enormously. I also thought he was wrong about nearly everything except gears.

I picked the lint out with a pair of tweezers finer than any needle, blew the ash free — gently; you never blow hard into a movement, you'd be surprised how many people don't know that — and set about the real problem, which was the balance staff. Someone, presumably Herr Albrecht himself, had dropped the watch at some point in its pampered life, and the staff had bent by an amount so small that three other repairmen had pronounced the watch "temperamental." Watches are not temperamental. Watches are honest to a degree that would be considered rude in a person. This one was telling anyone who listened that its balance staff was bent by two hundredths of a millimeter, and nobody had bothered to listen.

That is more or less the whole of my philosophy, if you want it: listen to small things. Great events happen to other people, elsewhere, in capitals and boardrooms and the pages of newspapers printed on paper too good for wrapping fish. But a bent balance staff will lie to a man every single day of his life, and fixing it is worth something. Fixing it is, in fact, everything, if you are the sort of person who believes the world runs on small true things rather than large false ones.

I was twenty-three years old, and I believed exactly that.

* * *

The workshop sat above a chandler's shop on a street that smelled permanently of tallow and river damp, in a city whose name you would recognize and whose streets you would find unremarkable. We had four benches, one window that admitted light for precisely five hours a day in winter, a stove that heated either the head or the feet but never both, and a wall of drawers that Master Ondrusek organized according to a system known only to him and, through some process I never witnessed, to me.

I had been apprenticed to him for six years. Before that I had been apprenticed to nothing, which is the polite way of saying that my father died of the lung fever when I was sixteen and left me a mother, two younger brothers, and a talent for fiddling with mechanisms that until then had expressed itself mainly in taking apart the household clock and being beaten for it. A neighbor who knew Ondrusek's late wife made an introduction. Ondrusek looked at my hands, asked me to file a flat on a bit of brass, watched me do it without breathing, and said, "You'll do. Don't cry in the shop. Crying rusts things."

It was the kindest sentence anyone had ever spoken to me, and I have never entirely forgiven him for it.

Apprenticeship, for a girl, meant I would never hold a journeyman's papers, never open my own shop under my own name, never sign a repair ticket that a customer would trust on sight. It meant I did the work and Ondrusek's name went on the ticket, and I had decided, somewhere around my nineteenth year, that this was an acceptable arrangement. Deliberately acceptable. Chosen, the way you choose a coat that doesn't quite fit because the alternative is no coat.

Here is what I mean by deliberate smallness. Every morning I came up the stairs at seven. Every morning I lit the lamp over my bench and cleaned it — the lamp, not the bench, though the bench too. I worked on watches, mostly, and occasionally on the small domestic clocks that people brought in wrapped in shawls as though they were ill children. I ate lunch at half past twelve. I swept the floor at six. On Sundays I sent money home to my mother, who wrote back letters consisting chiefly of my brothers' appetites. In the evenings I read whatever the lending library had, which skewed heavily toward adventure novels featuring men doing enormous things in enormous places, and I read them the way you eat sweets: knowing they are not food.

Great events, I told myself, happen to other people. My job was to make sure that when those events required a working watch, the watch worked.

I was content. I want to be clear about that, because everything that came afterward has a way of making the before look like foreshadowing, and it wasn't. It was just a life, a good one, chosen carefully and kept deliberately small, the way you bank a fire so it will last the night.

* * *

Herr Albrecht arrived at four o'clock to collect his watch, which was early, which meant he wanted to talk.

Ondrusek dealt with customers the way a fortress deals with siege engines, so the talking fell to me, as it usually did. I brought the watch out wrapped in chamois, opened the case to show him the new staff, and let him hold it while I explained — in the tone one uses for a beloved but dim relative — that the watch had never been temperamental, merely wounded, and that it would now keep time to within a few seconds a day provided he did not drop it, submerge it, or feed it further crumbs.

"Crumbs," said Herr Albrecht.

"The pocket collects them. The case hinge acts as a funnel."

He peered into the movement with the expression of a man looking into a well. "And this little wheel here — this is the heart?"

"That's the balance. And yes, more or less."

"And you fixed it. You personally." He looked at me over the watch, then at the ticket, where Ondrusek's name sat in his careful upright hand. Something moved behind his face — not suspicion exactly, more the mild surprise of a man discovering the sausage he likes is made by someone specific. "Well," he said, and paid, and tipped me a coin I pretended not to notice so that he could pretend he hadn't given it, and left.

Ondrusek grunted from his bench. "He'll drop it within a month."

"He'll drop it within a fortnight."

We were both silent a moment, enjoying this, and then the bell over the door rang again and a boy came in with a telegram, and the afternoon changed shape.

Telegrams were not unknown to us. Ondrusek received perhaps four a year, mostly from suppliers, worded like ransom notes. But this one the boy handed directly to me, which was odd, and it was addressed to *Miss Ada Halloran, horological apprentice*, which was odder still, and I stood there holding it while Ondrusek put down his tweezers with the deliberation of a man setting down a tool he may need to throw.

"Well?" he said.

I opened it. I remember thinking, absurdly, that the paper was thinner than usual, or that I was reading it more slowly than usual, or both.

> OBSERVATORY MASTER CLOCK FAILING STOP REQUIRE COMPETENT REPAIRER IMMEDIATELY STOP REFERRED BY PROFESSOR HARTMANN WHO VOUCHES FOR MASTER ONDRUSEK STOP TRAVEL AT ONCE STOP ALL EXPENSES PAID STOP VOSS DIRECTOR

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two times my eyes kept snagging on the same impossible fact, which was that it was addressed to me.

"Hartmann," said Ondrusek, and took the slip from my hand. Professor Hartmann had been a customer once, years ago — a marine chronometer, a delicate job, done mostly by me while Ondrusek supervised and signed. Hartmann had watched my hands over the top of his spectacles for two hours and said nothing, and apparently remembered.

"The mountain observatory," Ondrusek said slowly. "The Lindqvist clock."

I knew of it the way everyone knew of it: the great refractor telescope, the master clock that regulated every observation on the mountain, the star catalogue that the academy published and the newspapers celebrated. Dr. Lindqvist's catalogue. It was the kind of achievement that existed in my life only as a fact, the way the moon existed — large, distant, responsible for tides I had no occasion to think about.

"They're asking for you," Ondrusek said. "Not me. You."

"It says referred by Hartmann. He vouches for you—"

"He vouches for your hands. They've written to you." He looked at me over his spectacles, and for a moment I saw him weighing something, the way he weighed a mainspring, testing for flaws. "You could refuse. Send them a letter, say the shop cannot spare you. They'd find someone else. Some journeyman with papers and a beard and a talent for breaking escapements."

"Yes," I said.

"You won't refuse."

"No," I said. "I won't refuse."

He nodded once, as if I'd passed a test I hadn't known I was sitting, and turned back to his bench, and that was the entire conversation, because we were not a shop that discussed feelings. But that evening, after I'd closed, he climbed the stairs behind me — he never climbed stairs if he could avoid it — and stood in the doorway of my lodgings holding a parcel wrapped in brown paper.

"Tools," he said. "Mine. The good ones. Bring them back."

"Master—"

"A clock that matters gets repaired by whoever can do it. That's the whole trade. Everything else is paperwork." He thrust the parcel at me, and before I could say anything human to him he was already descending the stairs, calling back, "Crying rusts things, Halloran!"

I stood in my narrow room with the parcel in my arms and laughed until I had to sit down on the bed.

* * *

I packed that night and the next morning both, because packing is a thing I do badly and repeatedly. Two shirts, the grey dress, the black dress, needles and thread, the tool roll Ondrusek had given me, a book I would not read. My mother's letter, answering the news, consisted of the observation that mountains were cold, that observatories were full of strange men, and that I should on no account marry any of them. My brother Tomas had added a postscript requesting a souvenir, specifying "something valuable."

At the station, waiting for the mountain train, I had time to think, and what I thought was this: nothing about my life had prepared me for this, and yet nothing about my life had failed to prepare me for this. Six years of listening to small things. Six years of finding the two-hundredth-of-a-millimeter lie inside a mechanism that everyone else had pronounced temperamental. Whatever was wrong with a famous clock on a famous mountain, it would be found the same way everything was found: by opening the case, cleaning away the lint and the ash, and paying attention to what the mechanism was trying to say.

Great events happen to other people, elsewhere. I held onto that sentence on the train like a rail in rough weather. I was going up the mountain to fix a clock, not to enter history. History was for Lindqvists and Vosses and professors with catalogues. I would do the work, take the pay, come down before the deep snow, and be at my bench by Advent.

The train climbed. The air thinned. The trees gave way to scrub and the scrub to rock, and the light changed to that high thin silver that makes everything look recently manufactured. Somewhere above the clouds there was a dome, and inside the dome a clock that had kept time for thirty years for men who measured the universe, and it was failing, and they had sent for me.

I remember looking at my reflection in the dark window — a small woman with ink-stained fingers and her master's tools in her lap — and thinking, with the confidence of someone who has never once been wrong in a way that mattered:

How much trouble can one clock be?

* * *

They met me at the terminus, or rather a carriage met me, driven by a silent man with a face like a closed drawer, and the road went up and up through country that had stopped pretending to be inhabited. By the second hour I had seen no house, no field, no smoke. Just rock, and wind combing the dead grass flat, and ahead of us the mountain shouldering into a sky the color of a bruise coming up.

I thought about Herr Albrecht's watch, snug now in his waistcoat pocket, gathering its first fresh crumbs. I thought about the balance staff, straight and true, keeping honest seconds for a man who would never know its history. Small work. True work. Mine.

The road turned. The clouds tore for a moment — a seam of hard blue — and through it I saw the observatory for the first time: the long low buildings clinging to the shoulder of the peak, and above them the dome, white and vast and blind, like an eye turned inward.

Something in my chest tightened, and I told myself it was the altitude.

The driver said nothing. The horses leaned into the grade. And the mountain, patient as any mechanism, began to take us up.
