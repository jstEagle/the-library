## 5. The Chaplain's Kindness

The rain had found a way through the canvas roof of Ward Two overnight, and by morning there was a shallow lake on the floorboards that reflected the ceiling beams like a second sky. Elodie stepped around it with the particular weariness of a woman who had stopped being surprised by anything the war could do to a building. Three days since Theo Marchand had been carried out of the mist. Three days of fever, of morphine measured in grains and hoarded like gold, of the satchel hidden beneath her cot where she could feel its corner against her heel when she slept — if she slept.

She slept perhaps two hours a night now. The rest of the dark she spent listening: for boots in the corridor, for the creak of the ward door, for the sound of her own name spoken in Captain Hale's voice, which she heard sometimes even when no one was speaking at all.

Father Aubert came at mid-morning, as he always did, moving down the rows of wounded men with his stole tucked into his belt and his hands smelling faintly of incense and carbolic. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with white hair cropped close to his skull and a face built for kindness — heavy-lidded eyes, a mouth that turned naturally upward at the corners, the whole architecture of him arranged to suggest that whatever grief you carried, he had room for it too. The men loved him. They asked for him before they asked for morphine, some of them, and Elodie had watched dying soldiers grip his hand the way children grip a rope over deep water.

"Mademoiselle Sterling." He said it quietly, standing beside her at the wash basin where she was rinsing bandages. "You have not eaten."

"I've eaten."

"You have eaten standing up, in the last hour, something from a tin." He took the bandage from her hands, wrung it out with surprising gentleness for such large fingers, and laid it across the line. "Sit with me a moment. There is coffee. Real coffee — the curé at Saint-Aubin sent it up with the wine shipment, God bless his cellar."

She should have refused. She had work; she always had work. But her legs ached in a way that felt less like muscle than like argument, and the smell of real coffee — not the acorn-and-chicory substitute that passed for it this far north — reached something in her that had nothing to do with thirst.

They sat on an overturned crate behind the ward, under the eave where the rain broke into threads. Aubert poured from a battered pot into two enamel cups and handed her one without ceremony.

"The officer," he said. "Marchand. He is worse?"

"He is better. The fever broke last night." She drank. It was absurd how good it was. "He'll live, if infection stays away from the wound."

"And his mind? Fevered men say strange things."

Elodie looked at him over the rim of the cup. It was an ordinary question, the kind any priest might ask, the kind she herself had answered a hundred times with a hundred fevers. But three days of hiding a satchel had made her hear questions differently. Every question now arrived wearing a uniform.

"He says strange things," she said carefully. "Mostly about a wood called Virey. Mostly about men whose names I don't know."

Aubert nodded slowly, as though this confirmed something sad and expected. He looked out at the rain, at the mud track beyond the tents where a supply wagon stood axle-deep in water, its driver cursing it in Flemish.

"I knew a man once," he said, "who came back from Verdun talking like that. Names, names, names. His own mother couldn't quiet him. You know what quieted him in the end?" He didn't wait for her answer. "Being believed. One person, one time, saying *I believe you* — and after that he could sleep. Not forget. Sleep." He turned those heavy-lidded eyes on her. "Has anyone believed him yet, do you think?"

The coffee was warm going down and cold sitting in her stomach. She thought of Theo in the grey hours, gripping her wrist with a strength fever should have burned away, saying *you have to swear it reaches Paris*, and her saying nothing, because she had learned long ago never to swear what she could not carry.

"I think," she said, "that believing a man is easy. Believing him is free. It's what comes after believing that costs."

Aubert smiled — that kind, unhurried smile — and for a moment she felt the truth of it, the simple animal comfort of being near someone whose whole vocation was mercy. Then he set down his cup and folded his hands, and she saw that beneath the comfort there was structure, the way there is structure beneath a sermon.

"That is why I have come," he said. "Not only for coffee. I want to help you, Elodie. Both of you."

*Both.* The word landed like a stone dropped into still water. She kept her face still. Three days ago she would have missed it. Now she counted everything.

"Help us with what, Father?"

"With what cannot be said in front of the orderlies." He spoke softly, though they were alone under the eave, the rain loud enough to drown a confession. "I am not a fool, my daughter. I have been a priest through two years of this and I was a hospital chaplain at Sedan before that. I know what an officer does when he will not let go of a leather bag even unconscious. I know what it means when a nurse hides papers under her cot instead of turning them over to the section commandant, as regulations require." He raised a hand before she could speak. "No — don't deny it. Denial is for tribunals. This is a church conversation, and in a church conversation we may speak plainly."

Her heart was beating hard and slow, like something walking downstairs in the dark. *Under her cot.* Had she been seen? By whom? Or was he guessing — guessing well, because guilt makes people predictable?

"What exactly," she said, "do you imagine you know?"

"I know that Sergeant Delacroix died on Tuesday and his name is not in the morning report. I know that Private Osei-Bonsu — no, forgive me, that was another station — I know that the reports here are thin, Elodie. Thin as communion wafers. And I know that a man who carries proof of something command wishes buried will be buried with it unless someone moves him." He leaned forward. "I have contacts. Church couriers. The diocese moves wine, vestments, letters between seminaries all the way to Paris, and no intelligence officer in France searches a reliquary case. I can take your officer south. I can take whatever he carries. Within the week, if God is willing and the roads hold."

For one full breath, Elodie wanted it so badly that her whole body leaned toward him like a plant toward light. A week. Ten days, maybe, before the bridge closed for the season — everyone said it, the ferryman's wife said it, the quartermaster said it — and here was a road that bypassed the bridge entirely, a road lined with cassocks and incense smoke, offering to carry the weight she had been carrying alone since the night a half-dead man mistook her for a chaplain and put his faith in the wrong cloth.

And then Theo's voice, from three days of fever, from yesterday morning when the sweat had finally cooled: *Kindness near command is a currency, Nurse. Someone is spending it. Find out who holds the purse.*

"Father," she said slowly, "why me? Why offer this to me? Why not go to Marchand directly?"

"Because he doesn't trust me." Aubert said it without rancor, almost with approval. "Fevered men are honest. He looked at my collar and saw a uniform. You, he looks at and sees — what? Someone who counts things others leave uncounted. Word travels in a hospital, my daughter. Even among the unconscious." He smiled again. "Besides. A priest asking questions of a wounded officer draws eyes. A priest bringing coffee to an exhausted nurse draws none."

It was all reasonable. That was the trouble. Every piece of it fit together the way a good lie fits together — snugly, without gaps, answering every objection before it could be raised. She thought of Hale's visit, of his careful voice and his offer of protection in exchange for paper, and understood suddenly that she was being courted twice, from two directions, by two men who both claimed to want to carry her burden.

"Let me speak with him," she said. "Theo. If he agrees—"

"Of course. Of course." Aubert rose, gathering the pot, and pressed his free hand briefly over hers — warm, dry, heavy. "Take your time. But not too much of it, Elodie. The bridge at Aurelie's crossing closes sooner than they're saying." He paused at the edge of the eave, rain silvering his shoulders. "Ten days. Perhaps fewer. The engineers came through yesterday measuring the ice load. When the military decides a river is closed, it closes like a book."

He went inside, and she sat a long while with the cooling cup, watching the rain stitch itself into the mud.

* * *

Theo was propped on pillows when she came to him that evening, and the change in him frightened her more than the fever had. Fever was fire, and fire she knew how to fight. This was the ash afterward: a man hollowed out, his cheekbones standing sharp under skin the color of the dressing-station linen, his eyes following her across the ward with the patience of someone who has decided not to hope.

"You've seen him," Theo said, before she'd finished checking his pulse. "The priest."

"How could you possibly—"

"Because you have the look of a woman who's been offered something she wants." He almost smiled. It cost him visibly. "What did he offer?"

She glanced down the row of beds. The nearest patient was asleep, breathing in the whistling rhythm of a collapsed lung. Still, she lowered her voice until it was barely above the sound of the wind in the canvas.

"A church courier. South to Paris. Reliquary cases the intelligence officers don't search. He says he can move you within the week."

Theo was silent for a long moment. Outside, the rain had stopped and the cold had come in behind it, sharp as glass.

"And what does he want in return?"

"Nothing. He wants to help."

"Nobody wants to help, Elodie." He said her name — her Christian name, which she had never given him — and she realized he must have taken it from somewhere: her chart, her kit, the tag on her coat. "People want things, and helping is the shape their wanting takes. Ask yourself what a chaplain gains by moving a disgraced officer and a bag of seditious paper past every checkpoint in the sector." His hand found her sleeve, gripped it with what strength remained to him. "Kindness near command is a currency. Someone is spending it. The question is who's holding the purse — and what they're buying."

"You said that before. In the fever."

"I say true things in fevers. It's the only time no one punishes me for it." He coughed, a dry tearing sound, and she held the cup to his lips and hated how little of it he managed. When he could speak again, his voice had dropped lower. "Did he tell you how he knows about the satchel? Did he tell you who told him? Because I promise you, a man doesn't guess his way to the exact contents of a hidden bag. Someone told him. And whoever told him either wants the bag moved — or wants to watch where it goes."

The ward lamp guttered in the draft. Somewhere down the corridor a stretcher party passed, boots squelching, a man moaning the low continuous moan that meant pain too large for words.

"He knew about Delacroix," she said. "Sergeant Delacroix, who died Tuesday and isn't in the report. He used it to show me he understands what I—" She stopped.

"What you keep." Theo studied her face. "Your ledger."

She hadn't told him. She was certain she hadn't told him. He read the question in her expression and shook his head slightly.

"You count them," he said. "At night, by candle, when you think everyone's asleep. I wake at odd hours. I see the light under the curtain of your alcove, and I hear the pen." A pause. "Four hundred and eleven, as of Tuesday night. Is that right?"

The number went through her like cold water. Four hundred and eleven names in her small disguised hand, each one a man the morning report had dissolved into euphemism — *died of wounds*, *missing*, *returned to duty* — or simply left out altogether, the arithmetic of embarrassment. She had thought of the ledger as invisible. She had thought of herself as invisible. And here was a man three days out of fever who had counted her counting.

"Yes," she said. "Four hundred and eleven."

"Then you understand what I'm carrying better than anyone alive. Which is why I'll say this plainly, and you may call it fever if it comforts you." He pulled her closer, and his eyes were clear and terrible. "Do not give him the satchel. Do not tell him where it is. If he offers again — and he will, kindness always offers twice — watch his hands, not his face. Faces are trained. Hands only know what they intend to do."

"And if he's honest? If he's exactly what he appears to be — a tired old priest trying to save one more man?"

"Then God will forgive us for doubting him." Theo sank back against the pillows, exhausted by the effort, but his grip on her sleeve lingered a moment longer. "But Elodie — if he's honest, why hasn't he already reported the missing names himself? A man of God who knows the reports are thin and says nothing for two years — what is his silence worth, and to whom?"

She had no answer. She straightened his blanket, checked the dressing on his wound, performed the small liturgies of nursing because her hands needed occupation. At the curtain she paused.

"Ten days," she said. "Before the bridge closes. Maybe fewer. Whatever we decide, we decide soon."

"We," he repeated, very quietly, as though tasting the word. "Go and sleep, Nurse. Count your names. I'll keep watch on the priest."

* * *

He did not sleep, of course. Neither did she. At midnight she sat in her alcove with the candle shielded behind a tin plate, the ledger open on her knees, and added the day's entries: two men dead of wounds that the report would call *light* that same morning; a boy of nineteen from Lyon who had drowned in the flooded trench behind Ward Four, unrecorded because drowning was not a battle death and therefore, apparently, not a death at all. Four hundred and fourteen.

The pen scratched. The candle breathed. And through the canvas wall, faint but unmistakable, she heard voices in the chapel — Aubert's low rumble, and beneath it another voice, clipped and official, a voice she had heard only once but would know anywhere, the way you know a bell that once rang for you.

Captain Hale.

She pinched out the candle and sat in the dark with the ledger against her chest, listening. The voices rose and fell. She caught fragments — *the chapel clock*, *the road to the mill*, *ten days* — and then boots on wet gravel, moving away toward the officers' quarters, and silence.

When she finally lay down, the satchel's corner pressed against her heel beneath the cot, familiar as a heartbeat. Ten days. Perhaps fewer. And somewhere between the chapel and the officers' quarters, in the space between a priest's kindness and a captain's questions, the trap — if it was a trap — had already begun to close.

She just could not yet see which door it was closing on.
