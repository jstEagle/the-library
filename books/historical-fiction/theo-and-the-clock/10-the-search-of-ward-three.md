## 10. The Search of Ward Three

The order came down from division in a courier's leather wallet, and Captain Edmund Hale read it twice before he allowed himself to feel anything at all.

*Document irregularities reported at Casualty Clearing Station No. 9. Conduct thorough search of premises and personnel. Report findings within forty-eight hours.*

Irregularities. He set the paper on the field desk and looked out through the canvas flap at the station below — the long ward tents sagging under new snow, the cookhouse smoke standing straight up in the windless cold, the chapel with its crooked spire like a finger raised in mild objection to the whole war. Somewhere down there was a woman who had once told him, on a pier at Boulogne with gulls screaming overhead, that she intended to count everything the war tried to hide.

He had thought it a charming eccentricity then. He had been twenty-six and in love, and he had believed love meant never having to choose between a person and a purpose.

He knew better now.

* * *

He rode down at mid-morning, alone, because an escort announced intention and he wanted, for as long as possible, to be only a man visiting a hospital. The road from the ridge was rutted ice, and his horse picked its way with the exaggerated care of an animal that had learned what artillery did to the confident. Below him the river ran grey between its banks, and he could see the bridge — the last one for forty kilometres, its timbers dark with old water, its approaches already stacked with crates marked for evacuation before the thaw closed it. Ten days, they were saying now. Perhaps fewer. The engineers had stopped pretending the season would be generous.

Elodie Sterling met him at the entrance to Ward Three, and he watched her face do something complicated and quick before it settled into professional courtesy. He had spent two years learning to read reports written by men trained to conceal, and so he read her easily: the flicker of alarm, the decision not to show it, the small tightening at the corners of her mouth that was not quite a smile and not quite its opposite.

"Captain Hale." She wiped her hands on her apron, though they were clean. "We weren't told to expect division."

"I'm not here officially," he said, which was the first lie, and he felt it sit in his chest like a swallowed stone. "I was passing through."

"No one passes through here," she said. "This is the end of the road unless you're wounded or dead."

"Then I'm neither, and glad of it."

She almost smiled. Almost. And God help him, he catalogued the almost the way he catalogued everything now — filed it, dated it, noted its condition — because somewhere in the last three years he had become a man who could not experience a thing without also archiving it, and he hated himself for it with a quiet, constant heat.

"Walk with me," he said.

They walked the length of the ward. It smelled of carbolic and wet wool and the particular sweetness beneath both that no amount of disinfectant ever reached. Men lay in rows, some sleeping, some staring at the tent roof as if reading something written there. A sergeant with bandaged eyes turned his head toward their footsteps. A boy of nineteen — Hale would have sworn he was younger — gripped a nurse's wrist and said, *tell my mother the letter wasn't finished*, over and over, in the flat voice of morphine.

"You're short-staffed," Hale observed.

"We're always short-staffed."

"The morning report lists eleven nurses. I count seven."

"Two are sick. One is at the laundry. One—" Elodie paused, and the pause was a hair too long, "—one resigned."

"Resigned."

"People do. It isn't a crime."

"Not yet," he said, and heard how it sounded, and wished it unsaid.

She stopped walking. They stood between two cots, and around them the ward breathed its slow, suffering breath, and Hale understood that whatever happened next would happen in front of witnesses whether he wanted it or not.

"Edmund." She used his Christian name deliberately, a blade laid flat on a table. "Why are you here?"

And there it was — the moment he had rehearsed on the ride down and abandoned somewhere in the last hundred metres. He could tell her. He could say: *there is a report, there is an investigation, there are men above me who believe papers are moving through this station that should not move, and I am the instrument they've chosen, and I am asking you — begging you — to let me find nothing.* He could say all of it and watch her decide whether he was still the man from the pier or merely a uniform wearing his voice.

Instead he said: "There's been talk of irregularities in the casualty documentation. Names unreported. I'm to look into it."

"And will you?" she asked. "Look into it?"

"With everything I have," he said, and this, at least, was true in every direction at once, which was the only kind of truth he had left.

* * *

Father Aubert found him at the cookhouse at noon.

Hale had seen the chaplain perhaps four times before — a broad, soft-spoken man with ink-stained fingers and the habit of standing slightly too close, as though proximity were a form of absolution. Today he carried two tin mugs of coffee that tasted, when Hale accepted one, of chicory and scorched metal.

"You'll be searching the station," Aubert said. Not a question.

Hale lowered the mug. "You're well informed."

"I'm well informed about everything in this place, Captain. It's the one advantage of being nobody." The priest sat down on the bench across from him, unhurried, and looked out at the snow. "I wanted to speak with you before you began. Man to man. Or as near to it as either of us gets."

"Speak, then."

"There is a satchel." Aubert said it quietly, without looking at him. "Belonging to an officer brought in nine days ago. Marchand. You may know the name."

Hale kept his face still, but behind it everything accelerated. Marchand. The name had crossed his desk twice — once in a missing-persons signal from a disbanded reconnaissance unit, once in a memorandum from division marked *recovered or deceased, confirm*. An officer who had walked out of Virey Wood with documents in his teeth, metaphorically speaking, and then vanished into the medical system like a coin into deep water.

"Go on," Hale said.

"He keeps it under his cot. Refuses to surrender it even sedated. The Sister — Miss Sterling — has charge of his care." Aubert finally turned and looked at him, and his eyes were kind and tired and entirely unreadable, which Hale recognized as the chaplain's native country. "I tell you this because I believe the officer is being used. He's feverish, half-mad with guilt, and someone has convinced him that his delusions constitute evidence. I'd hoped to reason with him myself. I've failed. So I bring it to you, because a search conducted properly finds what needs finding, and a search conducted badly finds other things — things that hurt innocent people."

"Innocent people," Hale repeated.

"Nurses mostly," said Aubert. "Young women doing their best in an impossible place. Whatever the officer carries, they didn't put it in his hands. But searches are blunt instruments, Captain. They don't distinguish between the guilty and the merely present."

It was beautifully done. Hale could see the seams if he looked — the slight over-preparation, the way the priest had steered the conversation away from any question of *how* he knew these details, the careful planting of pity like seed corn. But knowing a thing was constructed was not the same as knowing why, or by whom, or against whom. And the plain fact remained: a name, a location, a document. Division wanted results. Here was a result delivered gift-wrapped, requiring no tents overturned, no women humiliated, no ward torn apart while dying men watched.

Unless it was bait. Unless the satchel was already gone and this was theatre designed to close the file. Unless Father Aubert served the church, or France, or himself, or some fourth master Hale hadn't imagined.

"Thank you, Father," Hale said. "You've been helpful."

"One more thing." Aubert rose, brushing crumbs from his cassock. "Miss Sterling is the finest officer in this station, in every sense but rank. If your search touches her, touch gently. Some people carry more than their share so others can carry less. That isn't a crime. It's arithmetic."

He walked away across the snow, black against white, and Hale sat with his bad coffee and understood that he had just been managed — and that he was going to let it happen anyway, because the alternative was worse.

* * *

He gave Elodie warning. Not much — an hour, delivered in the flattest administrative language he possessed, standing at the ward entrance with a clipboard he didn't need. He watched her receive it and watched nothing move in her face at all, and he thought: *she's better at this than I am,* and thought also: *good.*

The search began at fourteen hundred hours.

He conducted it himself rather than delegate it, which drew a raised eyebrow from his corporal and cost him the pretense of distance. Two military policemen went through the records tent — the official ledgers, the morning reports, the supply manifests — while Hale walked the wards with Elodie a half-step behind him, opening footlockers, lifting mattresses, running his hand along the seams of tent walls where paper could be folded flat against canvas.

He searched Marchand's cot thoroughly. Under the frame, in the straw tick, inside the rolled greatcoat at its foot. Nothing. The officer himself watched from his pillow with fever-bright eyes and an expression of such perfect, insolent blankness that Hale nearly laughed. *You know exactly what I'm looking for,* the expression said, *and we both know I won't find it.*

In the chapel, Hale stood beneath the clock for a long time.

It was an old thing, provincial-made, its case dark oak carved with vines gone shallow from a century of dusting. It ticked with a sound too loud for the small stone room, patient and obstinate, like a heart that refused to be reasoned with. He looked at it. He looked at it for longer than a man conducting a routine search ought to look at a clock. Behind him, Elodie stood very still, and he could hear her breathing — controlled, deliberate, the breathing of someone counting seconds.

He could have asked for it opened. A clock case was a hiding place older than the war; any competent intelligence officer would have demanded the back panel off. His corporal was ten feet away with a crowbar in his kit. All he had to do was speak.

He thought about the pier at Boulogne. He thought about the memorandum from division, the general's signature at the bottom of it, the campaign history already being drafted in an office that smelled of wax and certainty. He thought about four hundred names that might or might not exist in a ledger somewhere, waiting for someone willing to count them.

"Chapel's clear," he said.

His voice sounded strange to him. Elodie exhaled — barely, a fraction of air — and if he hadn't been listening for it he'd have missed it entirely.

* * *

By dusk the search was formally concluded. Nothing found. No irregularities confirmed beyond the ordinary chaos of a forward station. He signed the report himself, in his own hand, each word a brick in a wall he might someday be shot against: *Premises inspected in full. No documents of the nature described were located. Station personnel cooperative.*

Cooperative. He wrote it and thought of her face at the ward entrance, giving him nothing, keeping everything.

But he was not a fool, and he did not intend to be played for one twice. Whatever had been in that satchel — and something had been, Aubert's performance notwithstanding — it was still moving. Papers like those did not stay still; they traveled, toward Paris, toward press offices, toward the one thing command feared more than defeat, which was correction. And travel from this station meant one road, south along the river, toward the bridge that would close in ten days or fewer.

So at eighteen hundred hours he posted his watchers. Four men, rotated in pairs, dressed as engineers assessing the bridge approach — a cover story with the advantage of being partially true, since the engineers genuinely were assessing it. Their orders were simple: observe all traffic leaving the station after dark. Note faces, note vehicles, note anything carried that a medical evacuation had no business carrying. Report to him directly, to no one else, in writing.

He gave the orders in his own quarters, door barred, and afterward sat for a long time looking at the candle.

He had just falsified an intelligence report. There was no gentler word for it. *Nothing found* was a finding, and it was false, and he had made it false with full knowledge, and the penalty for that in wartime was not theoretical. Against it stood — what? A woman he had loved at twenty-six and had never successfully stopped loving, though he'd had three years and a war to manage it. A conviction he couldn't prove that the truth about Virey Wood mattered more than the careers built on burying it. And underneath all of it, thin as wire: the memory of Elodie at the pier, saying *someone has to keep the count, Edmund, or they'll spend us like coins and call the spending victory.*

He had called it idealism then. He had kissed her hand and promised to write and believed, in the fat comfortable way of men who have never been tested, that idealism was a phase a sensible woman would grow out of.

She hadn't grown out of it. She had armed herself with it.

And he — he had just handed her, free of charge, the one piece of information she needed most: that the hunt had come to her door, had searched her wards, had found nothing, and had therefore not ended. Any intelligent person would understand what an empty search meant. It meant the hunters would return. It meant the road south would be watched — except that he had chosen the watchers himself, and he had written their orders himself, and buried in those orders, in language only a staff-trained eye would catch, was a gap. A blind spot. The eastern towpath, the stretch where the fog came off the water thickest, where his watchers' sightlines crossed a drainage culvert and died.

He had built them a door and disguised it as a wall.

Hale pinched out the candle and sat in the dark, listening to the wind worry the tent ropes, feeling the shape of what he was becoming settle over him like the snow outside — silent, accumulating, transforming the familiar landscape into something he no longer recognized.

Down the hill, in Ward Three, a fevered officer slept with empty hands. In the chapel, a clock ticked over whatever it held. And somewhere between them, a woman who counted the uncounted was making plans he could guess to the hour, and he found — this was the terrible part, the part he would examine for years afterward — that he was hoping she was fast enough.

Outside, the first watcher took his post on the bridge road, and the trap closed by one degree, gentle as frost.
