## 3. What the Workshop Kept

The letter sat on her table for three days before Rafael could bear to touch it again, and when she finally did, she did not read it. She read the hand. That was the trouble. Anyone could forge words; her father had taught her that before he taught her to write her own name properly. But no one forged *that* hand — the impatient downstrokes, the way the *R* in her name leaned forward like a man hurrying toward a door.

She put the letter in the drawer where she kept the lens cloths, which was a lie of a drawer, because nothing in it had ever been only a lens cloth, and went down the spiral stair to the workshop to wait out the remembering.

* * *

She had been nine years old the first time he set a pen in her hand and said, "Now we keep a ship honest."

The workshop smelled then as it smelled now — lamp oil, cedar shavings, the iron-and-vinegar bite of ink — and her father, Aldo Marchetti, sat her on his stool and showed her a bill of lading for the *Cormorant*, which had come into harbor carrying, according to the paper, forty barrels of salt herring.

"How many barrels did she carry?" he asked.

"Forty," said Rafael, who trusted paper the way children trust their mothers.

"How many did you count on the dock?"

She had counted. She counted everything; her father had taught her that too, before the pens, before the names. "Thirty-eight."

"So the paper is lying." He turned the page toward her with the tenderness other men showed to photographs of their children. "Two barrels of something that isn't herring, walking off this ship and into somebody's pocket, and every honest man in the harbor signing his name under a lie. Now." He dipped a pen and wrote a single word — *herring* — in a hand so like the clerk's that Rafael laughed out loud. "We are going to make the paper tell the truth. Or at least," and here he smiled, and the smile was the whole of her childhood, warm and crooked, "a more interesting truth."

He called it keeping ships honest for years. She believed it the way you believe in the lamp itself: absolutely, and without examining the machinery. He corrected manifests. He adjusted cargo lists. He added a barrel here, struck one there, and always — he was scrupulous about this, and made her be scrupulous too — always in the direction of what was actually aboard. A ship carrying forty barrels would sail with papers saying forty. Never thirty-nine. Never forty-one. The harbor, he said, ran on paper the way the light ran on oil, and dirty paper wrecked more ships than dirty weather ever had.

"You are not forging, Rafaela," he would say, catching her practicing a customs clerk's slanted hand at the kitchen table while her mother pretended not to see. "Forging is making a lie look true. We are making a truth look like itself. There is all the difference in the world, and someday you will understand it."

She was twenty-two before she understood that the difference was smaller than a barrel, and that barrels could be counted in both directions.

* * *

Tomas found out the way he found out everything: by being cheerful and unobservant about everything except ships.

They had been married two years. He would come up the harbor road after a run to the southern ports, salt in his beard, and stand in the lamp room with her while she trimmed the wicks, telling her about the water the way other men told jokes. He was a poor liar and a worse forger — she had once watched him attempt to sign a chit in the harbormaster's name and produced something that looked like a spider drowning — and he thought his wife's father was simply a meticulous old clerk with a hobby of old papers.

Then, in the autumn of her twenty-fifth year, the *Cormorant* came in again, and Tomas counted.

"Thirty-eight barrels," he said at dinner, pleased with himself, the way a man is pleased who has found a coin on the road. "And the manifest says forty. Old Aldo will want to know. He's always after the clerks about sloppy paper."

Rafael's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. Her mother, across the table, went very still in the way of a woman who has spent thirty years being still at the right moments.

"He'll know," Rafael said carefully.

"He'll *love* it. He'll write them a letter in that clerk's hand of his, and they'll never know how he—" Tomas stopped. Rafael watched it happen, watched the cheerful machinery of her husband's mind arrive at a place it had never been asked to go. He looked at her. He looked at his mother-in-law. He looked at his plate.

"He writes in other hands," Tomas said. It was not a question.

Her mother stood, gathered the plates, and left the room, which was her way of saying that some conversations belonged to the younger generation.

"He keeps ships honest," Rafael said. It sounded thin even to her, a child's prayer repeated past its usefulness.

Tomas was quiet for a long time. Then he reached across the table and took her hand, which was what he did instead of arguing, and said, "Show me."

* * *

She showed him, because she had never once in her life been able to refuse him anything, and because some part of her had been waiting years for permission to look at the workshop with adult eyes.

He went through her father's files the way he read charts — patiently, thoroughly, humming. And for three evenings he found nothing wrong. Papers in her father's hand correcting papers in other hands, all of it in the direction of truth, all of it small, all of it, Tomas agreed, the work of a fussy honest man with too much time and a genius for ink.

On the fourth evening he found the second drawer.

It was locked, and her father had gone to the mainland for the week, and Tomas looked at Rafael and Rafael looked at the drawer and neither of them said anything for a moment, and then Tomas said, "I can open it without marking it. Probably," and Rafael, who could have opened it in thirty seconds and who hated herself for knowing that, handed him the thin blade from her father's own kit.

Inside were ledgers. Not corrections — records. Years of them. Shipments that had come in carrying thirty-eight barrels and sailed out carrying papers for forty, the two extra barrels' worth of value flowing, entry by careful entry, into a name that meant nothing and a name that meant everything: Voss & Sons, the shipping concern of Harbormaster Voss, the man who signed Rafael's father's permits, the man who had attended her wedding and drunk her father's wine and called him *the most reliable pen in the harbor*.

"He's not keeping ships honest," Tomas said. His voice had gone flat, the way it went in weather. "He's keeping *Voss* honest. Honest to the arrangement."

Rafael sat down on the stool she had sat on at nine years old. The workshop smelled the same. Her father's stool was the same. Nothing was the same.

"Maybe there's a reason," she said, and heard how it sounded, and hated that too.

"Maybe there is." Tomas was fair to a fault; it was one of the ten thousand things she loved about him. "We ask him. When he's back. We ask him to his face, and he tells us, and if it's bad we go to the port authority in the city, not Voss, and we do it right." He closed the drawer. He looked at her, and his face did the thing it did before he said something kind and immovable. "Rafi. Whatever he's done — you didn't do it. You were a child with a pen. Whatever he is, you're the one who counts the barrels. That's who you are."

She believed him. That was the last thing she believed easily for a long time after.

* * *

Her father came home on a Thursday. They confronted him on the Friday, the three of them in the workshop, and Rafael would remember for the rest of her life how old he suddenly looked — not guilty, which she might have borne, but *tired*, the tiredness of a man who has been carrying something heavy up the same stairs for twenty years.

"Voss takes his share," Aldo said. "Two barrels in ten, sometimes three. And in exchange, no ship of mine is ever delayed, no paper of mine is ever questioned, and no inspector ever looks too closely at anything with Marchetti's hand on it." He spread his palms. "I have kept this harbor's paper clean for thirty years. Voss is the dirt I pay to keep the rest of it clean."

"That's not clean," Tomas said. "That's laundering."

"It's *surviving*." For the first time her father raised his voice, and Rafael flinched, because he had never once raised it at her. "You think I don't know what he is? You think I haven't spent twenty years writing down every barrel, every ship, every name —" and he pulled the second drawer open and pulled out not the ledgers Tomas had found but a thinner book beneath them, a book Rafael had never seen, with columns in her father's hand going back further than her own life. "Every crime he's made me a party to. Dates. Amounts. Signatures. One day this ledger ends him. That was always the plan. I just needed it *finished*, and I needed to be alive, and I needed Voss to never once suspect—"

He stopped. All three of them stopped, because through the floor came the sound of boots in the lamp room above, heavy and unhurried, and a voice calling down the spiral stair with terrible friendliness:

"Aldo? It's Voss. I saw your light. Thought I'd come up for a glass of that wine."

* * *

They never finished that conversation. Her father hid the thin ledger inside the frame of his stool — the stool she was sitting on, though she didn't know it then — and went up the stairs smiling, and poured the wine, and Voss stayed an hour and left, and three days later Aldo Marchetti took the mail boat to the mainland and never came back. The boat went down in weather no one called a storm. They gave him a burial at sea with an empty coffin, and Voss spoke at the service about *the most reliable pen in the harbor*, and Rafael stood in the rain and counted the mourners because counting was all she had left.

Tomas kept counting too. She saw it in him over the following weeks — the cheerful machinery of him grinding at the ledgers, at the thin book in the stool, at the arithmetic of two barrels in ten. He told her he'd found a name in the records, a courier, someone who moved Voss's papers up the coast. He told her he'd written to a contact in the city. He told her, the morning he went out, that the weather was turning and he'd be back by dark, and he kissed her on the mouth in the doorway of the lamp room, and she had said, "Mind the shoals," which is what she always said, and he had said, "Always am," which is what he always said.

The shoals were not what took him. She learned that later, or told herself she had — that a man who asks too many questions about barrels can drown in calm water, that the sea is a convenient instrument for men who keep ledgers of their own. There was no proof. There was never any proof. There was only the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said: *Tomas counted. Tomas wrote letters. Tomas drowned.*

For three years Rafael had carried it as guilt — her father's crime, her father's pen, the inheritance she'd refused to open. She had burned papers by the drawerful, thinking she was burning the past.

Now, standing in the workshop with a letter in a dead man's hand dated three years after his death, she understood at last what her father had been trying to tell her all along, in his warm crooked way, with his pens and his truths:

Guilt was not what she'd inherited.

The ledger was.

She pulled the stool from under the workbench and turned it over, and her fingers found the seam in the frame where a thin book had slept for three years — and found, where the book should have been, only a square of clean dust, and a note in her father's hand that read: *If you're reading this, ask Ivo where my courier keeps what matters.*
