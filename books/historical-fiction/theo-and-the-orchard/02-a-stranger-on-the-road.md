## 2. A Stranger on the Road

The road out of Paris had been mud for three days, and Theo Marchand had stopped pretending he would ever be clean again.

He had left in the hour before dawn, when the city still belonged to the carters and the night soil men, when the barricades at the gates were manned by soldiers too cold and too bored to look closely at a young man in a threadbare coat with a farmer's stoop learned overnight. He had walked south with nothing but the clothes he wore, forty-one sous, a hunk of black bread going stale in his pocket, and the memory of his brother's face pressed against the window of the tumbril's escort cart — not weeping, that was the worst of it, not weeping, only watching him back with an expression that said *go*, and *don't stop*, and *whatever they ask you, you never saw me*.

Lucien. Twenty-six years old and arrested for what? For a pamphlet, perhaps. For a name spoken in the wrong room. For being the brother of a man who had once dined with General Aubertin, back when Aubertin was a hero and dinner parties were still safe. The Republic did not need reasons anymore; it needed only suspicion, and suspicion was cheaper than bread.

By the third day the road had emptied into country. Hedgerows rose on either side, bare and black as ironwork, and beyond them the fields lay fallow under a sky the color of dishwater. Theo's boots — Lucien's boots, really, half a size too large — had filled with water crossing a ford and had not dried since. Each step made a small sucking sound, like the earth reluctant to let him go.

He passed a shrine to some saint whose face had been chiseled away, leaving a smooth blank oval. He passed a burned farmhouse, its roofbeams reaching up like ribs. He passed a milestone worn illegible and did not bother to read it, because it did not matter where he was, only that he was somewhere else.

Somewhere else. That was all Paris had left him.

Toward evening of the third day, hungry in the way that had stopped being pain and become a kind of weather, he smelled woodsmoke and apples — old apples, cider apples, the sweet-rot smell of fruit forgotten past its season — and crested a low rise to see the orchard below him.

It was larger than he expected, maybe two hundred trees laid out in long ranks down a gentle slope, their branches bare and silvered with lichen, pruned with a precision that seemed almost defiant in so neglected a landscape. A stone wall enclosed the near side. Beyond the trees stood a long low farmhouse with a slate roof, a barn, a press-house with its door open to the cold, and a thread of smoke rising straight up into the windless air.

Theo stood on the rise for a long moment, doing what he had taught himself to do since Lucien's arrest: counting exits. The lane ran north along the wall toward the road he'd come by. There was a gap in the hedge to the east, a cattle track by the look of it. The orchard itself would give cover if anyone came looking. He could not have said why he thought someone might. He only knew that since the thirteenth of Ventôse, when the men came for his brother, he had lived inside a continuous low hum of fear, like a note struck and never allowed to fade.

He went down the slope.

* * *

The first person he met was a boy, fourteen or fifteen, driving a barrow of pruned branches toward a burn pile. The boy stopped when he saw him and did not smile. He looked at Theo's face, then at his hands, then at his boots, in that order, and Theo understood that he was being assessed — weighed like livestock at market, priced and found uncertain.

"I'm looking for work," Theo said. His voice came out rougher than he intended, three days of cold air and silence in it. "And lodging, if there's any to be had. I can pay a little."

The boy said nothing. He set down the barrow handles slowly, the way one sets down something that might be needed quickly, and called over his shoulder, in a flat peasant drawl Theo could not place, "Maman. Someone's come."

A woman appeared from behind the press-house — broad, gray-streaked hair pinned carelessly, forearms red to the elbow as though she'd just been washing something. She looked at Theo the way the boy had, but longer, and with more arithmetic in it.

"You're a long way from anywhere," she said.

"I'm a long way from everywhere."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I have." He held her gaze because he had learned that much: the guilty look away, or so people believed, and so the innocent must learn not to. "My name is Marchand. Théodore Marchand. My family farmed outside Melun before —" He let the sentence die, which was also true, and let her fill the silence however she liked.

She filled it with a question he hadn't expected. "Who told you there was work here?"

"No one. I saw the smoke."

Something moved behind her eyes — not quite alarm, not quite relief. She glanced past him at the empty road, at the rise he'd descended, as if confirming he'd come alone, and whatever she found there seemed to settle something.

"Vasseur," she said. "I'm Vasseur. This is my orchard, such as it is. We lost our hired man to the levy in autumn and my hands aren't what they were." She wiped them on her apron as if to demonstrate. "Pruning season won't wait for the Republic's convenience. Can you use a knife?"

"I can learn."

"Everyone says that." She turned and called toward the orchard, "Étienne! Come and look at this one."

* * *

Étienne Vasseur came up the row between the trees with a pruning knife still in his hand and an expression Theo would remember afterward for a long time: not hostility, exactly, but a kind of narrowed attention, the look of a man reading a document he suspected had been forged.

He was perhaps fifty, lean as the apple trees themselves, with a face weathered into deep furrows and eyes a pale, watery blue that seemed to hold no warmth and no cruelty either — only measurement.

"So," Étienne said. "Marchand."

"Sir."

"Where from?"

"Melun originally. Paris lately."

"Lately." The word hung there. "How lately?"

"A week ago."

"And why does a man leave Paris in winter?"

"The same reason everyone leaves Paris in winter," Theo said, and heard how it sounded even as he said it, and cursed himself silently. Too quick. Too polished. A farmhand should stumble over his answers, should mumble, should be grateful and stupid. Instead he heard himself answering like a man in a deposition, and he saw Étienne hear it too.

The old man looked at him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled — a small, dry movement of the mouth that reached nowhere near his eyes.

"Well," he said. "You're honest about your lying, at least. That's rarer than honesty." He turned to his wife. "We'll try him."

"Maman?" the boy said, and Theo realized with a small shock that the woman was the boy's mother, and that the household was smaller than it had first appeared: the Vasseurs, the boy — Julien, he was told later — and one other, a figure he'd glimpsed distantly among the trees and who had not yet come to be inspected.

"We'll try him," Madame Vasseur repeated, in a tone that suggested the decision had not entirely been hers to make, nor entirely her husband's either. "There's a loft over the press-house. Straw and a blanket. Supper's at dark. You'll earn both."

"I'm grateful."

"Don't be grateful yet," she said. "Be useful. Gratitude keeps. Usefulness spoils."

* * *

The loft over the press-house was low-ceilinged and smelled of apples and mice and old wood, and Theo lay on the straw after supper — a thin soup with cabbage in it, and bread, real bread, wheaten, which nearly undid him after Paris's sawdust loaves — and listened to the sounds of the house settling around him.

Supper had been strange. He had eaten at the long table in the kitchen with the family and with the fifth member of the household, who had appeared only when the food was served: a young woman, introduced simply as "Elodie, she helps with the trees," who had sat across from him and said perhaps ten words all evening, none of them to him.

She was not pretty in the way Paris defined prettiness — too spare, too watchful, her brown hair cut short and practical as a soldier's, her hands nicked and stained with the work of the orchard. But there was something in the way she held herself, some economy of motion, that reminded him of — and here the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome — of the officers he'd seen around Aubertin's table in the years before everything changed. People who moved as if the space around them mattered. As if sightlines mattered.

She had asked him one question, passing him the bread: "Which road did you come by?"

"The north road. Through Saint-Martin."

"There was a patrol on it Tuesday," she said, to no one in particular, and returned to her soup.

Now, in the loft, in the dark, Theo turned the day over the way one turns a coin to check for clipping.

They had taken him in too easily. That was the first thing. A stranger off the road in the winter of '94, in a countryside crawling with agents and requisition officers and committees of surveillance, and the Vasseurs had fed him within the hour. Either they were fools — and no one survived these years by being a fool — or they had wanted something from the road, and he had happened to be it.

Or they were afraid of something, and wanted to know what he was before he knew what they were.

He remembered the way Julien had watched him eat. Not rudely — the boy had been raised better than that — but continuously, the way one watches weather. He remembered Madame Vasseur asking *who told you there was work here* with the particular emphasis of a woman checking whether a code phrase had been answered correctly. He remembered Étienne's dry smile, *you're honest about your lying*, and understood suddenly that the old man had not been making conversation. He had been telling Theo that he knew. Not what — only that there was something to know.

And Elodie. *There was a patrol on it Tuesday.* Said lightly, tossed off like a comment on the frost. But why would a farm girl track patrols? Why would she mention one to a stranger, unless —

Unless it was a warning. Or a test. Or a message meant for whoever might be listening.

Theo lay back on the straw and stared at the rafters, where a spider had abandoned its web sometime in autumn and left the silk hanging like a ghost of a plan.

He had come here to disappear. To be dull. To be a pair of hands and nothing else, a laborer with no history, no opinions, no name worth writing in any ledger. He had practiced his story on the road until it wore smooth. And within four hours of arrival he had already answered questions too well, watched too carefully, lain awake cataloguing what he'd seen — and every one of those things, he understood now, was precisely what a spy would do.

He laughed once, softly, without humor. If the Vasseurs were hiding something, then he had spent his first evening proving himself the most interesting object on the farm.

Below him, through the floorboards, he heard the kitchen voices, low and indistinct. Once, clearly, Madame Vasseur's voice rose: "—too clean for a walker, Étienne. Look at his hands." And Étienne's answer, quieter, lost.

His hands. Of course. Three days of road and his palms were still soft as a clerk's. He flexed them in the dark, feeling the blisters the pruning knife had raised that afternoon — Étienne had put him to work immediately, testing him on a row of old Pippins, watching his grip, his stance, the way he held his shoulders — and understood that the blisters were the first honest thing about him they'd seen.

Tomorrow they would be worse. Good. Pain was credible. Pain was the one credential no agent could forge.

He slept finally, badly, dreaming of Lucien's face at the cart window and waking twice to the sound of his own breath.

* * *

In the morning there was frost on the inside of the loft window, feathered white across the glass, and when Theo went down the world had been remade in silver. The orchard stood silent under it, every branch furred with ice crystals, and the light came up gold through the trees and set the whole slope burning coldly, as if the orchard were lit from within.

It was beautiful. It was the first beautiful thing he had seen since the thirteenth of Ventôse, and it caught at his throat unexpectedly, and he stood in the doorway of the press-house letting it hurt him, because he had decided that a man who could no longer be hurt by beauty was a man the Republic had already taken everything from.

Étienne was already in the rows, knife flashing. Theo took up a second knife from the bench by the wall — it had been left out for him, oiled and sharpened, which he chose to read as a small verdict — and went to work.

They pruned in silence for an hour. Then, without looking up, Étienne said:

"You cut like a man who's read books about trees."

Theo's knife paused for half a heartbeat. "I cut like a man learning."

"Mm." The old man moved down the branch, considering. "In my experience, a man learning watches the tree. A man pretending watches the teacher." He made his cut, clean as a signature. "You watch the tree, Marchand. I'll say that for you. Whatever else you are, you're not watching me."

"Should I be?"

The pale eyes came up then, and held him, and there was no smile in them at all.

"Not yet," Étienne said, and went back to his pruning.

Theo worked. The frost began to burn off, rising in a faint mist off the grass, and somewhere down the slope he could see the young woman — Elodie — moving along her own row, small and deliberate, pausing now and then to look not at her tree but outward, down the valley, toward the north road.

Watching the road. On a morning with no patrol due, no traffic, nothing coming but cold.

Theo bent to his branch and made his cut, and tried to look like a man who noticed nothing at all.
