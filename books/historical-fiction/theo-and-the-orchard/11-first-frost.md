## 11. First Frost

The frost came without mercy, the way winter always does in the Vaux valley — not as snow, not as warning, but as a silence that arrived between one breath and the next.

Elodie felt it before she saw it. She woke in the loft above the press house with her breath ghosting silver above the blanket and a stillness in the air that had teeth in it. Through the gap in the shutters the orchard lay white and luminous under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, every branch furred with crystal, every apple still clinging to the boughs sheathed in glass.

*First frost.* The words moved through the house faster than any bell.

She was down the ladder before her boots were laced. Madame Vasseur stood in the yard in her shawl, hands on her hips, looking up at the trees the way a woman looks at a debt she cannot pay.

"All of it," the old woman said. "Tonight. Everything that's left on the boughs comes in tonight, or it rots on the branch by morning. Frost-touch, and the flesh goes to brown water inside the week."

Elodie did the arithmetic without meaning to. Four hundred trees, at least. Perhaps five hundred. The household was Madame Vasseur, old Perrin whose hands shook, the boy Lucien who was more hindrance than help, herself — and Theo.

She found him already in the yard, pulling on his gloves, his breath steaming. He had heard the same bell she had heard — the one that rang in no steeple, that rang only in the bodies of people who had worked land before. He looked at the orchard the way she did: not with dread, exactly, but with the particular gravity of a man who understood that the night would be long and that the cold did not care whether they survived it.

"The ladders are rotten," he said. It was the first full sentence he'd offered her before dawn in all the weeks he'd been at the farm.

"Then we climb carefully," Elodie said. "Or we fall memorably."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live, if he ever allowed himself one.

* * *

They worked by lantern light and then, as the sky paled, by nothing at all — by the strange blue luminescence of a frost morning, in which every apple seemed to carry its own small lamp. The cold was total. It got into the joints of the fingers first, then the wrists, then somewhere behind the sternum where no amount of movement could reach it. Elodie's hands went through the familiar litany of numbness: the ache, the burn, the absence. She had picked in worse. She had picked in a snowstorm in the Jura with a patrol's torches moving along the valley road below her, and had crouched in a tree like a thief with dispatches sewn into her bodice, praying the cold would keep the soldiers by their fire.

She did not think of that now. She thought about apples.

Theo worked two trees over. She could track him by sound even when the fog of her own breath blurred the world — the particular rhythm of his picking, quicker than Perrin's, more careful than Lucien's, the soft double-thud of fruit into the pannier. He had learned the orchard fast, she'd give him that. Too fast, some wary corner of her mind still whispered. A Paris clerk who handles a ladder like a born orchardman. But the whisper had grown quieter over the weeks. It was growing quieter now, and she resented it.

"Baskets," Lucien shouted from somewhere, his voice cracked with cold and excitement, because for a boy of twelve even catastrophe was a kind of festival.

"Mind the Calvilles," Madame Vasseur called back. "The Calvilles bruise if you look at them wrong."

By mid-morning the yard had become a village of baskets. By noon the press house smelled of crushed fruit and iron and cold sweat. They worked through the gray noon without stopping, bread eaten one-handed among the rows, and when the light began to fail again — the short winter day folding up like a fan — they were not half finished, and the temperature was falling the way a stone falls.

"We'll lose the light," Perrin said, squinting at the western sky where the last color was draining out like blood from a face.

"Then we work in the dark," said Madame Vasseur, and that was the whole of the council of war.

* * *

The night came down hard and clear, the stars sharp as nailheads, the cold deepening from bite to blade. They hung lanterns in the trees — a thing Elodie had never seen done on any farm she'd worked, but Madame Vasseur swore by it, said the light kept the fruit from shocking, though Elodie suspected the old woman simply could not bear to work in the dark in an orchard her dead husband had planted.

Theo climbed the tall Renettes at the orchard's heart, the trees that needed a man's reach. Elodie worked the ladder below him, and for a long time they said nothing, and the silence between them had changed its character. It had been, in the early weeks, a silence of mutual surveillance — two people each pretending to be simpler than they were, each listening to the other for the false note. Now it was the silence of people working toward the same end with their hands, which is a different country entirely, and she did not know when they had crossed the border.

"Your fingers," Theo said suddenly, from above. "You can't feel them."

"I can feel them."

"You've been shaking for an hour."

"It's called working, city boy."

He came down the ladder without being asked, which was insubordination, and took her hands in his before she could object — took them right out of the air, chapped and filthy and blue at the nails, and held them between his own. His palms were warm from the work. The warmth went into her like water into cracked ground.

"This is not necessary," she said.

"No," he agreed. He did not let go. He chafed her fingers, briskly, professionally, his eyes fixed on some point over her shoulder as though the act were a medical procedure in which neither party was present. "Madame Vasseur will lose the harvest if you drop from the ladder. I'm protecting the harvest."

"Of course."

"The harvest," he said again, with the gravity of a man swearing an oath, and Elodie laughed — a short, surprised sound, torn out of her before she could prevent it, the first laugh she had given anyone in this country since spring. It frightened her more than the patrol had, that laugh. She pulled her hands back and climbed.

Above her, in the lantern light, she heard him exhale — long and slow, like a man setting down a weight — and then the rhythm of his picking resumed, and the night went on.

* * *

They finished at what the cock judged to be two hours before dawn, though no cock in its right mind was singing in that cold. The last baskets came in under a sky going gray at the edges, the frost on the grass now thick enough to crackle underfoot like breaking crockery. The orchard stood empty at last, every bough stripped, the trees black and bare against the paling east, and Elodie felt the strange grief she always felt at a finished harvest — the sense of a season dying, of a door closing that would not open again for a year.

Madame Vasseur counted baskets by lantern light, her lips moving, and then looked up at her ragged crew with an expression that on another face would have been called gratitude.

"There's cider in the cellar," she said. "The good cask, the one I was saving. Go down, all of you, before I come to my senses."

* * *

The cellar was a world unto itself — dug deep into the hillside below the press house, walled in fieldstone that wept cold moisture, hung with the year's drying apples in braided strings and the onions in their nets. It smelled of earth and fermentation and the sweet rot of fallen fruit composting in the corner, and it was warm — not truly warm, but warm by contrast, warm the way a grave is warm, and after the orchard it felt like mercy.

Perrin and Lucien did not last long. The boy was asleep before his cider was finished, curled against the wall like a cat, and Perrin hauled him up with the tenderness of a grandfather and steered him toward the farmhouse, leaving the two of them alone among the casks.

Elodie told herself she would go up too. She told herself this with the same voice that had told her, for months, to keep distance, keep the mask, keep moving, keep nothing. She sat down on an upturned barrel anyway. Theo sat against the wall across from her, his back to the cold stone, a mug of cider between his hands, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the cellar held them in its earthy dark like two things planted.

"Why did you come here?"

She hadn't meant to say it. The question had been living in her for weeks, fed and watered in secret, and it came out now in the quiet the way frost comes out of the ground — silently, and all at once, and irreversible. Theo looked up. In the lantern's low light his face was all planes and shadow, the exhaustion of the night carved into it.

"You know why," he said. "Work. Shelter. I told you."

"You told me what a man tells a farmer. I'm not asking what you told Madame Vasseur."

He was quiet for a long moment. She watched his thumb move on the mug's rim, around and around, a small orbit of thought.

"My brother was arrested in Paris in the summer," he said at last. "You know that much. I told you that much." He stopped. Started again. "What I didn't tell you is that they didn't want my brother. They wanted the people around my brother. He was a printer — you know this — and he printed things for people, and one of the people he printed for was—" He stopped a second time, and Elodie felt the air in the cellar change, the way it changes before a door opens somewhere in a house.

"Was who?" she said, though she knew. She had known since the letter. She had known since the handwriting, since the night she'd found him holding that page with a face like a man looking at a ghost. But she wanted to hear him say it, because if he said it, then the two of them were standing on the same side of the same door, and she had been alone on her side of it for so long that the loneliness had stopped feeling like loneliness and started feeling like skin.

"Theo," she said, when he didn't go on. "Was who?"

"General Aubertin," Theo said. The name dropped into the cellar like a stone into a well. "My brother printed pamphlets for him, years ago, before — before everything. Before the war, before the Terror, back when the general was only a man who came to dinner and talked too much about apple trees." A ghost of something crossed his face. "He loved orchards. Did you know that? He used to say a country could be judged by its orchards. He said a republic that let its orchards fail had already failed in its heart."

Elodie's hands had gone still in her lap. The cold was in the cellar now, the true cold, seeping up through the stone, but she barely felt it. *He loved orchards.* Of course he did. Of course the old man had chosen this farm, this valley, this failing little orchard at the edge of nowhere, as the relay point in a chain of couriers stretching back toward Paris and forward toward the sea. He had hidden his network in the thing he loved, the way a man hides a letter in a book he knows by heart.

"The letter," she said carefully. "The one you found."

"You knew about the letter." It was not quite an accusation. His eyes had come up to hers, and there was no surprise in them, only a tired, patient certainty, as though he had carried this knowledge for weeks the way she had carried hers, and tonight the two weights had finally touched.

"Yes," Elodie said. "I knew about the letter."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere above them, in the frozen yard, ice shifted on a barrel hoop with a sound like a small bone breaking.

"Then we should say the rest of it plainly," Theo said. "Because I am so tired of saying things sideways, Elodie. I have been saying everything sideways since I came down that road. I am tired down to the marrow." He set the mug down. "The letter is in the hand of a dead man. It names you. And I have been watching you for a month, trying to understand what a hired girl who memorizes roads by the rhythm of her own pruning wants with a letter from a man who was guillotined in the Place de la Révolution."

The old Elodie — the Elodie of the Jura snowstorm, of the sewn bodice, of the mask worn so long it had grown into the face — would have denied it. Would have laughed, deflected, given him a peasant's blank stare and let the silence do the killing. She felt that old reflex rise in her like a tide.

And then she looked at him. At the exhaustion and the stubbornness and the terrible honesty of him, sitting against a cellar wall at the end of a frozen night with cider he hadn't drunk, waiting for her with the patience of a man who had already decided to trust her and was only waiting for her to catch up.

"My name is not Elodie Crane," she said. "That much, at least, is true of what I told you."

"What is it true of, then?"

"It's the name on the papers I carry. The papers are good. I made them myself." She heard her own voice go flat and precise, the courier's voice, the voice she used for routes and timings, and she could not seem to make it warm again. "I am what the general's letter says I am. A courier. I have carried for the network since the spring. Dispatches, names, routes — the things the Republic says do not exist and will hang you for carrying. The orchard is a relay. Madame Vasseur does not know. She thinks I am a girl fleeing a bad marriage in Normandy, and I have let her think it because her ignorance keeps her safe."

"And the routes." His voice was very quiet. "The pruning."

"You saw that."

"I see everything you do. I have since the first week." He said it without embarrassment, the way a man reports the weather. "You prune in patterns. You change the pattern when the road changes. I thought at first it was superstition."

"It's memory," Elodie said. "A road is easy to forget. A rhythm isn't. The valley road is a long cut and two short ones. The river path is three short and a crosscut. I can walk this orchard blind and know every route out of this valley by the shape of the trees. The general taught it to me himself." Her throat tightened on the last words, and she was surprised — she had not expected grief to be in there, waiting. "He is the only person left alive who knows my face and my true name in the same room. And they cut off his head in October."

The silence that followed was long. The lantern flame leaned and righted itself. Theo did not look away from her, and she found she could not look away from him either, and between them, in the earthen dark, something was quietly dismantled — the last wall, or the last pretense of a wall, she could not have said which.

"You believe he's dead," Theo said at last. Carefully. The way a man steps onto ice.

"I saw the reports. I read the prison lists. I did the work of believing it." She paused. "Why do you say it like that?"

"Because of the letter." He leaned forward, and the lantern threw his shadow huge and wavering across the apple strings on the wall. "Elodie — the letter is dated the ninth of Brumaire. Nine days after the execution. I've turned it over a hundred times in my head. The hand is his — I would know it anywhere, he taught me my letters when I was a boy, he had that way of crossing his t's like a fence rail. And the date is nine days after they killed him."

"Then the date is wrong. Or the hand is forged. Forgers—"

"Or," Theo said, and his voice dropped so low it was barely above the sound of the lantern, "he is not dead. Or the man they killed was not him. I don't know which is worse, and I don't know which to hope for. But I know this: the letter names a rendezvous, and half the page is torn away, and I think — I have begun to think — that the man who has the other half is the same man who keeps coming to this valley counting heads."

Elodie went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the frost.

"Delacour," she said.

"Delacour."

Above them, far off, a dog began to bark on the far side of the valley — three sharp notes, then silence, then three more. Elodie knew that dog. She knew every dog in the valley, and which of them barked at carts, and which at strangers, and which, on two nights in the past month, had barked at a rider on the ridge road who did not come down but only stood, and watched, and went away.

The lantern guttered. Elodie rose, and Theo rose with her, and for a moment they stood close in the dark among the casks, two people who had just handed each other the last of their secrets and found the weight lighter for it.

"Tomorrow," she said, "you and I are going to talk about that torn page. All of it. Everything you've held back, everything I've held back. Tonight—" she glanced up at the cellar ceiling, toward the frozen yard, toward the ridge road and its patient rider "—tonight we sleep. And tomorrow, Theo, we find out what Delacour knows. Because I have the feeling he isn't counting heads anymore."

The dog on the far ridge barked three times more, and stopped.

And in the silence that followed, Elodie understood that the season of hiding — his and hers both — had just come to its first hard frost.
