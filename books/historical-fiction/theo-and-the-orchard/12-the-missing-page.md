## 12. The Missing Page

The letter had been bothering him for a week before he understood why.

It was not the handwriting, though he had gone over that a hundred times in his head — the way Aubertin's *a* leaned like a man walking into wind, the ink pressed hard at the downstrokes as though the general had never learned to write anything gently. It was not the words themselves, which Theo had all but memorized: the reference to documents proving the betrayal of the Republic's own generals, the rendezvous at the coast before the year turned. He had carried those sentences around with him like stones in his boots.

What bothered him was the paper.

He sat in the loft above the cider press with the letter open on his knee, the last grey light of a December afternoon coming through the slats in long pale bars. Below him, Elodie was banking the fire in the kitchen, and he could hear Vasseur grumbling about the price of salt, and somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, and all of it was ordinary, and none of it was what bothered him.

The paper was wrong. That was it. He had known it without knowing it, the way you know a face is familiar before you can place it. The letter ended mid-sentence — not mid-thought, but mid-*word*, the final line breaking off after *and the man who* — and the edge of the tear was clean. Too clean. A page torn in haste tears raggedly, especially when wet or old. This had been torn against a straight edge, deliberately, by someone who wanted two pieces that would fit back together.

And the piece that was missing — where was it?

Theo held the letter up to the light. The remaining half was the left side; the writing marched off the right-hand edge and stopped. So the missing piece held the end of every line. The end of the sentence about the coast. The end of whatever *the man who* became. And possibly — his stomach tightened at the thought — other things entirely. Names. Dates. Instructions meant for eyes alone.

He lowered the letter and sat very still, listening to the house below him breathe.

He thought about how the letter had come to him. Not directly — never directly. It had been passed to him in Paris by a woman whose name he never learned, a laundress with chapped hands who had pressed it into his coat pocket while pretending to brush ash from his sleeve, three days after his brother Luc had been taken. She had said only: *You'll know the hand.* And he had. God help him, he had known it instantly, the way you know your own mother's voice in a crowd.

But the letter had been folded twice, and folded again, and it had been damp along one edge, and there had been a smell to it — faint, sour, institutional. The smell of a prison.

Luc's cell.

Theo closed his eyes and let the memory come, because he had been refusing it for days and refusal was no longer possible. The night before Luc's arrest, Luc had been drunk — not happily drunk, but the desperate kind, the kind that talks. They had sat in the back room of the Café de la Paix while Luc ranted about his commanding officer, about papers, about *a dead man's hand*, about how some things were worth more than a man's life and less than his soul. Theo had thought it was drink talking. He had walked home alone through the snow and left his brother to stumble after him, and by morning the gendarmes had come, and Luc had been shouting something as they dragged him out — shouting about a letter, about *give it to Théo, he'll know what to do* —

They had taken the letter from Luc. Of course they had. And then someone in that machine of clerks and informers had looked at it, and looked at the handwriting, and made a decision. Half the letter was dangerous enough to kill for. But perhaps the half they kept was the half that mattered most — the half with names, with the rendezvous, with the ending of the sentence that began *and the man who* —

*And the man who carries this is trusted by me beyond my death.*

Theo's hands were shaking. He set the letter down on the floorboards and pressed his palms flat against his knees until the trembling slowed.

Delacour.

The agent's face rose in his mind unbidden: the soft, patient face, the eyes that never hurried, the voice that asked questions the way a physician asks where it hurts — gently, precisely, already knowing. Delacour had stood in the Vasseur farmyard in November with his requisition lists and his polite questions about unregistered persons, and he had looked at Theo a moment too long when Theo spoke, and remarked, pleasantly, that Paris accents traveled badly in the provinces. He had eaten their bread. He had drunk their cider. He had taken the orchard's stores with a receipt written in beautiful copperplate, and he had smiled at Elodie in a way that made Theo want to break his teeth.

And now Theo knew — knew with the cold certainty of a man reading his own death warrant — that somewhere in Delacour's possession, folded inside some ledger or locked in some strongbox in the district office at Mortagne, lay the right-hand half of General Aubertin's last letter.

Which meant Delacour knew the left half existed. Which meant Delacour knew the letter was whole once, and had been divided, and might be rejoined. Which meant Delacour was waiting — patiently, softly, the way he did everything — for whoever held the missing half to come looking for its twin.

*Come,* said the silence of the loft. *I have been so patient.*

* * *

He went down the ladder too fast and nearly took the last rungs in a single drop, catching himself on the wall. In the kitchen, Elodie was slicing black bread, and the knife made small rhythmic sounds against the board, and she glanced up when he came in and her eyes narrowed almost immediately.

"You've found something," she said. Not a question.

"I need to show you the letter."

"You showed me the letter."

"I need to show you what I missed."

She set down the knife. Wiped her hands on her apron with the deliberate calm he had come to recognize as her armor — everything done in order, everything controlled, nothing wasted. "Vasseur's gone to the far pasture. Talk."

He laid the letter on the table between them, smoothing it flat with both hands, and told her. About the clean tear. About the prison smell. About Luc, drunk and rambling, and the gendarmes, and the shouting in the doorway. About the laundress and the coat pocket and the words *you'll know the hand*. He watched her face while he talked, and it was like watching ice form over moving water — the surface stilling, the current beneath going faster and faster.

When he finished, she was quiet for a long moment. Then she picked up the letter, tilted it toward the candle, and read the broken final line aloud, softly, as if testing whether the words would burn her mouth.

"*And the man who—*"

"Names," Theo said. "Or instructions. Or both."

"And Delacour has them." She set the letter down. Her fingers lingered on the paper a heartbeat longer than necessary, and he saw — or thought he saw — something flicker across her face that was not calculation. Something almost like fear, quickly buried. "How certain are you?"

"As certain as I am of my own name."

"That could be arranged to be uncertain," she said dryly, but there was no humor in it, and then, more quietly: "If Delacour has the page, he has had it since autumn. Why hasn't he moved? Why hasn't he simply arrested everyone within ten miles of this orchard?"

"Because he doesn't have the whole," Theo said. "Half a letter proves nothing. Half a letter is an anomaly, a curiosity, a fragment in a file. He needs the other half. He needs the hand that holds it." He heard his own voice go flat and cold. "He's fishing. He's been fishing since November. Every question about unregistered persons, every requisition, every friendly visit — bait on the hook."

Elodie turned away from him and stood at the window, arms crossed, watching the dark come down over the orchard. The bare trees stood in rows like a congregation waiting for a verdict.

"There's a way this ends well," she said at last, without turning. "Tell me what it is, because I can't see it."

"I go to Mortagne."

She turned. "No."

"I get close to Delacour. I find where he keeps his papers. I take the page."

"With what skill? With what help? You're a clerk's son from the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, not a burglar. Delacour has gendarmes within a shout, and you—" She stopped herself. He watched her choose her next words with the same care she gave the grafting knife. "Théo. If he takes you, he takes you *with the letter's left half in your head*. You'd give him the whole thing in an afternoon. You'd give him me."

"I'd give him nothing."

"You don't know that." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and she covered it by turning back to the window. "No one knows what they'd give until the pliers are real. I've seen men swear otherwise. I've *been* the reason men swore otherwise."

The confession hung in the air between them, unintended, and neither of them reached for it. Outside, the first flakes of evening snow were starting down, fat and slow, catching in the black branches.

"He has my brother," Theo said finally.

Elodie's shoulders went still.

"Whatever else this is — your mission, the general, the network, all of it — Delacour holds Luc in a cell in Mortagne, and Luc is there because of a letter he tried to give *me*. I'm the reason my brother is sleeping on stone. And now I learn that the key to whatever trap is closing around us is sitting in the same man's desk drawer." He laughed, and it came out bitter. "Do you see the shape of it? Delacour doesn't even need to hunt anymore. He only needs to wait. My brother is the bait, the letter is the hook, and I am the fish that keeps circling, telling itself it's smarter than the line."

"Then don't take the bait."

"And leave Luc there? Leave the page there?" He crossed to her, close enough to see the candlelight doubled in her eyes. "You told me once that you memorize routes by the rhythm of pruning — that everything you do here is cover for something else. Well. Let me go to Mortagne as cover for something else. A laborer seeking winter work. A man petitioning to see his brother — that's legal, Elodie. That's even *encouraged*. The Republic loves a family reunion it can listen to."

"They'll listen, yes. And watch. And follow you back."

"Then I won't lead them back."

"You can't promise that."

"No," he admitted. "I can't."

She was silent for a long time. Snow ticked softly against the glass. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of argument, the voice of someone reading a map of ground she already knows is lost.

"If you do this, you do it properly. No improvisation. No heroics. You go as a petitioner, you weep if you must, you ask about your brother and nothing else. You do not go near his offices. You do not go near his lodgings. You look at nothing, you touch nothing, you learn one thing only: does Delacour carry the page on him, or does he keep it stored?" She held up a finger. "Because those are different problems. A man who carries it can be met on a road. A man who stores it must be robbed, and robbery is a different trade entirely, and one I may actually be able to help with."

Theo stared at her. "You'd help?"

"I'd rather help than bury you." She said it flatly, but her hand came up and gripped his wrist, hard, once, and let go. "Listen to me. Delacour is not a fool, which means he has already imagined you doing exactly what you're proposing. Which means the petition to see your brother may be the trap itself. Which means—" she exhaled, "—which means we do not decide tonight. We watch him first. There's a market day at Mortagne in four days. Vasseur will need to sell stock; he always does before the deep cold. We go as a household. We look like farmers. And while I sell apples that taste like wood shavings, you will stand in a public square and do nothing but *look*, and tell me later what you saw. Understood? Nothing else. You look."

"And if what I see tells me the page is in his coat pocket?"

"Then God help us both," Elodie said, "because I have an idea, and you're going to hate it."

* * *

Mortagne wore its market day like a borrowed coat that didn't fit. The square was full enough to seem prosperous — carts of turnips, bolts of rough cloth, a fiddler playing to three children and a dog — but the cold had gotten into everything, and the buyers haggled without heat, and soldiers in blue greatcoats stood at the corners with the bored patience of men who had learned that watching was cheaper than acting.

Theo stood by the fountain with a sack of pruned branches over his shoulder, looking exactly like what he appeared to be: a farmhand waiting for his master to finish selling. It was the easiest disguise he had ever worn, because it required no effort at all. He was cold, he was tired, he was nobody.

Delacour arrived at midmorning, and Theo felt him before he saw him — a change in the texture of the crowd, a slight stiffening near the cloth stalls, the way water knows a dropped stone before the eye finds the rings.

The agent crossed the square unhurried, greeting people by name, pausing to admire a cow, to joke with the fiddler. He wore a heavy coat with a high collar and carried a leather satchel slung across his body, the strap crossing his chest, the bag resting against his left hip under the coat's flap. He did not set it down. He did not loosen the strap. When he bent to examine a child's wooden horse at a stall, he turned with the satchel tucked against his body, protected, automatic, the way a man turns who has carried something precious for months.

Theo counted. Delacour touched the satchel eleven times in a quarter hour. Eleven times — adjusting it, patting it, shifting it — each touch unconscious, each one a confession.

A man does not guard an empty bag.

He forced himself to look away, to study the fountain, the church tower, the listless pigeons. His heart was going hard and strange. *It's in the satchel. He carries it. He carries it everywhere, which means he's waiting, which means he wants to be found, which means—*

Which meant the page was bait too. Which meant any road, any meeting, any moment of carelessness was designed. Delacour was not storing the letter; he was *wearing* it, dangling it, letting it be seen by anyone sent to see.

And yet.

And yet a man who carries a thing eleven months grows careless with it eventually. A man who carries it sleeps with it beside his bed, eats with it on his knee, drinks with it under the table. A thing carried becomes a thing available — not today, maybe not this month, but sometime, on some dark road, in some crowded room, in the space of one turned back.

Theo shouldered his branch-sack and moved slowly along the square's edge, keeping the fountain between himself and the agent, memorizing what he had come to memorize: the cut of the coat, the position of the strap, the rhythm of the touches. Eleven touches. He would remember eleven.

Near the cloth stalls he passed close — closer than he intended, a shift in the crowd — and Delacour turned, and for one full second their eyes met.

Recognition did not flash. Nothing flashed. Delacour's face remained pleasant, mild, forgettable, the face of a minor official enjoying a market day. But his hand came up and rested on the satchel flap — the twelfth touch — and he smiled, very slightly, the smile of a host seeing a guest arrive early.

*There you are,* the smile said. *I wondered when you'd come to look.*

Theo walked on, legs steady, breath smoking, and did not look back. Behind him the fiddler played, the children chased the dog, the pigeons shuffled on the frozen fountain rim, and the whole bright cold scene went on being ordinary, and he understood now that it would never be ordinary again. Somewhere under that mild grey coat, against that soft patient heart, rode the end of a dead man's sentence — *and the man who* — and Theo Marchand had just shown the man who guarded it exactly where to aim.

That evening, back at the orchard, he told Elodie what he had seen. The satchel. The strap. The twelve touches. The smile.

She listened without interrupting, standing at the window with her back to him, and when he finished she was quiet so long he thought she hadn't heard. Then she said, still facing the glass and the dark trees:

"The satchel never leaves him. Then we don't take the page from the satchel." She turned, and in the candlelight her face had gone calm in the way he had learned to fear and trust in equal measure — the calm of a woman stepping off a ledge and calling it a path. "We make him give it to us. Théo — I know what Delacour wants. I know what he'd trade that page for. And tomorrow, I'm going to offer it to him."

Theo felt the cold walk up his spine. "Offer him what?"

Elodie smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "Cider," she said. "The best barrel in the cellar. And a bargain he can't refuse."
