## 13. A Bargain in Cider

The summons had burned, but paper leaves ghosts.

Elodie found the evidence three days after the fire in the kitchen grate — a corner of heavy cream-laid stock that had not caught, curled like a dead leaf among the ash, and on it, in ink gone brown at the edges, half a printed line: *...the Committee of General Security hereby orders...* She knew the format. She had forged enough of them to know them by heart.

She stood over the hearth with the scrap pinched between finger and thumb while the wind worried the bare apple rows outside, and she let herself feel nothing for exactly ten breaths. Ten was her discipline. Ten breaths to be afraid, to be furious, to imagine every ruin that could grow from one concealed document. Then she closed her fist around the scrap and went to find Theo Marchand.

He was in the far field, breaking ice off the water trough with the flat of a spade, his breath smoking, his shoulders carrying the particular slump of a man who has been lying by omission and knows it weighs more each day. He'd been carrying it since the frost night, she realized now. Since the cellar, when he'd held her and said almost nothing true. She had mistaken his silence for tenderness. It had been arithmetic instead — a man counting what he could afford to tell.

"Marchand." Her voice carried flat across the frozen ground.

He turned. Saw her face. Set down the spade.

* * *

She did not take him to the house. Houses had ears — Vasseur's deaf mother-in-law who heard everything, the boy Piou who slept behind the cheese room and missed nothing. She took him to the cider shed at the orchard's edge, the low stone building where the press stood under its winter shroud and the last barrels breathed their slow vinegar sweetness into the dark. She shut the door, dropped the wooden bar, and put the burnt scrap of paper on the barrel head between them.

"Explain this."

Theo looked at it a long moment. In the grey light through the slatted vent his face was pale and very still, the stillness of a man deciding whether to keep swimming or let himself go under.

"You went through the grate."

"I went through the grate because you've been wrong for a week. You burn nothing cleanly, Marchand. You're a Parisian; I thought your whole generation learned to burn things properly before they could walk." The anger came out of her level and cold, which was how she knew it was real. Hot anger was performance. This was the other kind. "A Committee order. Addressed to whom? Concerning whom? Why does a hired hand from nowhere have a Committee summons in his pocket, and why did he feed it to the fire rather than show it to the woman whose orchard it might get requisitioned?"

"It doesn't concern the orchard."

"Everything concerns the orchard. Everything concerns me. That is the entire shape of my life." She stepped closer. The barrels breathed around them, sweet-rotten, patient. "So you will say it plainly, here, where no one can hear, or I will walk out of this shed and go to Delacour myself and tell him I've discovered an unregistered Parisian hiding among my fellow laborers, and we will see which of us the Republic believes."

It was a cruel thing to say and she meant it only as leverage, but she watched it land like a blade between ribs, and something in his face broke open — not fear. Relief, almost. The terrible relief of a man who has been holding a door shut against a flood and is finally permitted to let go.

"My brother," Theo said. "His name is Luc. He was arrested in Brumaire for printing pamphlets — nothing, foolishness, verse about the Committee that would have been laughed at two years ago. My mother sold everything to pay a lawyer who took the money and disappeared. And three days ago a letter reached me through the widow at the crossroads farm. From Delacour's office." His voice stayed steady, which cost him; she could see it costing. "They will release Luc. Fully release him, papers restored, no surveillance. All I have to do is inform. On the traffic through this valley. On the farms that shelter unregistered persons. On—" he stopped.

"On me," Elodie said.

"On whoever moves what moves through here. They didn't name you. They don't need to name you. They want the courier line between here and the coast, and everyone attached to it." He passed a hand across his eyes. "I burned it. I sat in the kitchen after everyone slept and I burned it, and then I got up the next morning and pruned trees beside you as if I were still the man who arrived here with blistered hands and nothing to hide, and I told myself that burning it was the same as refusing it. But it isn't, is it. Refusal has to be spoken. Otherwise it's just another secret, and secrets rot."

The shed was cold enough that their breath mingled above the barrel head. Elodie looked at the scrap of paper, at this man who had held her in a cellar full of apples and said nothing, and she made herself separate the two facts — the silence and the refusal — because her life depended on separating them correctly.

"Why didn't you tell me that night? In the cellar. You held me and you said nothing."

"Because telling you made it yours." He said it simply, without excuse. "If you knew, then you'd carry it too, and you'd either send me away — which you should have done, probably — or you'd stay and start planning around a knife at your throat, and I couldn't bear to watch you do that. I know what it is to plan around a knife. My family's been doing it since '92." He laughed, short and bitter. "And because some cowardly part of me thought if I didn't say it aloud, it wasn't quite real, and Luc wouldn't quite be in a cell, and I wouldn't quite have to choose."

"And now?"

"Now you've found the ash, so it's real." He met her eyes. "Ask me the question you actually came to ask."

She hadn't known until he said it what the question was, but there it was, waiting, and she asked it. "When they offered you your brother — for one moment, even one breath — did you consider it?"

The silence lasted four of her disciplined breaths. She gave him all four.

"Yes," Theo said. "For one breath I saw Luc walking out of the Conciergerie into the light, and I saw my mother's face, and I wanted it so badly I couldn't breathe. One breath. Then I thought of you memorizing roads by the rhythm of your pruning, and I thought: she trusts this valley with her life, and I will not be the reason it fails." He shook his head slowly. "One breath, Elodie. I won't insult you by pretending less."

She turned away from him and walked the length of the shed and back, her boots loud on the stone floor, because she needed the motion the way other people needed prayer. He had considered it. Of course he had considered it; any man who hadn't would be a fool or a liar, and she had worked with both and survived neither well. What mattered was what lived on the far side of the breath. What mattered was that he had burned the paper badly, like an amateur, but he had burned it.

What mattered — and this was the thought that stopped her pacing — was that Delacour believed he had a hook in Theo Marchand. A hook in a fish can be used by the fisherman. Or by anyone clever enough to hold the line.

* * *

"There's cider in that barrel," she said. "Third one from the wall. Vasseur hides his good stuff from the requisition men under a false mark. Pour two cups."

Theo raised his eyebrows. "Is this a celebration?"

"This is a negotiation. Negotiations go better when both parties are drinking." She pulled the crate under the vent light and sat, and after a moment he worked the tap and filled two rough earthenware cups, and the smell rose up sharp and appley, cutting through the shed's cold.

"To Luc," she said, and touched her cup to his. "May he be insufferable and free by spring."

Theo drank. Then he waited, because he was learning her rhythms, learning that she built her plans the way she built her grafts — cut first, join second, seal last.

"Here is what you haven't seen," she said, "because you've spent the week staring at your own guilt like a man staring into a well. Look up instead. Delacour made you an offer. Think about what the offer tells us."

"That he suspects the orchard."

"He already suspected the orchard; he requisitioned our stores in autumn to see how we'd squeal, and we squealed politely, and that bought us time but not safety. No. What the offer tells us is that Delacour is *fishing*. He doesn't have the line. If he had proof of the traffic, he wouldn't trade it for information — he'd use it, take the credit, climb. He offers a brother for a name because he has nothing, and he thinks we have something, and he wants us to hand it to him wrapped and tied." She turned her cup in her fingers. "Which means the danger is not that Delacour knows. The danger is that Delacour is greedy, impatient, and ambitious, and greedy impatient ambitious men can be led."

Theo set his cup down. "Led where?"

"Led to believe he's winning." She heard her own voice drop into the register she used for routes and signals, the flat practical voice, and she noticed that it steadied them both. "You will go to him."

The color left Theo's face entirely. "Elodie—"

"Not yet. Hear it. You'll go to him — carefully, through intermediaries, the way a frightened man would, the way a man would who'd wrestled with his conscience for weeks and lost. You'll tell him you have information. Small at first. True, even — we'll give him truths, cheap ones, things he can verify and feel clever about. A cart that moved in November. A name in a village that means nothing. Each piece you sell him buys your credibility, and each piece makes him trust the next one. And when he's grown comfortable, when he's started to think of you as his creature—" she paused, and let the picture finish itself in his mind, "—we sell him the thing he wants most. The courier route. A rendezvous. Time, place, cargo."

"You'd give him a real rendezvous?" Theo's voice was careful. "With real people?"

"With a rendezvous that exists on paper and nowhere else. A farm that's empty. A date when the road will be full of nothing but fog. He raids it, finds nothing, and concludes either that we moved the cargo — meaning the traffic is real and worth more patience — or that someone tipped the couriers. Either way, he learns to rely on you more, not less, because you'll be the one who says: *they must have been warned, give me another chance, I'm close.*" She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Delacour's ambition will do our lying for us. Men like him cannot admit a dry well. They just dig deeper."

"And meanwhile the actual dispatches move on the actual nights, on roads he isn't watching, because his watchers are standing in a fog bank waiting for couriers who were never born."

"Meanwhile," she agreed, "the work goes on."

Theo was quiet for a long time. Outside, the wind moved through the bare orchard with a sound like slow water, and somewhere a crow complained and fell silent. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — the guilt still in it, but underneath the guilt something harder taking shape, something she recognized because she wore it herself every day.

"There's a flaw," he said. "Luc. Every day I play this game, Luc sits in a cell, and if Delacour ever smells the trick, he doesn't arrest me — he writes to Paris and Luc dies for it. You're asking me to spend my brother's life as stakes."

"I'm asking you to spend it as *leverage*, and there's a difference, and I won't pretend the difference is comfortable." She leaned forward. "But listen to the rest, because this is the part I've been turning over since I found your ash. Delacour promised Luc's freedom for a name. Promises from agents of the Committee are worth the paper they're written on — you taught me that yourself when you showed me the handwriting of a dead man. But suppose the game works. Suppose Delacour comes to believe you're his informant, feeding him a rich vein. Then your brother isn't a hostage anymore. He's an *asset's family*. Agents protect assets. Delacour will want Luc released — quietly, gradually, so as not to frighten his new pigeon — because a released, grateful, watched brother is worth more to him than a cell. Your lie doesn't abandon Luc, Theo. Done right, it's the only rope that reaches him."

Theo stared at her. In the grey light his eyes were wet, though his face didn't move, and she understood that no one had ever handed this man a plan that included saving his brother — that he had been carrying the choice as a choice, two loves on a scale, and she had just quietly removed one side of it.

"You've been thinking about this since the grate," he said hoarsely.

"Since the grate. While you were busy drowning, I was counting the water." She reached across the crate and took his hand — his pruning-scarred, ink-stained, trembling hand — and held it flat against the cold wood between them. "But hear the price, because there is one, and I won't soften it. If you do this, you become a traitor in fact as well as appearance. To Delacour, yes, but also to anyone watching who can't see the seams. Aubertin's people, if any survive. My handlers, if they learn of it. There may come a day when the wrong friend finds you selling secrets to the enemy and acts on it before I can explain. You'd be walking a rope with knives on both sides, and I can't promise to catch you. I can only promise to be at the end of the rope."

"And you?" His fingers tightened on hers. "What do you become, planning treason against a Committee agent inside your own mission? If your handler learns you've improvised an operation he never ordered—"

"Then I'll have a great deal to explain," Elodie said, "and by then it will be done, and done things explain themselves better than intended ones." She lifted her cup. "My mother had a saying about grafting. You don't ask the rootstock's permission. You cut, you join, you seal, and in spring either it takes or it doesn't, and the tree never once consults you."

Theo laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, brief and cracked — and raised his cup, and the cider was sharp and cold and tasted of the autumn they'd survived.

"One condition," he said. "No more ash. Whatever comes next — whatever I carry, whatever I'm afraid of — it goes in the same fire as yours, together, and we stand there and watch it burn properly. Parisians burn things properly. I'll teach you."

"We'll teach each other," she said. "That's the whole arrangement."

They drank the barrel down to the dregs between them, sitting in the cold shed while the light failed, and laid the plan joint by joint the way she'd taught him to lay a graft — the contact surfaces clean, the cambium aligned, wax pressed into every gap. By full dark they had the shape of it: the first small truth Theo would sell, the widow at the crossroads as intermediary, the empty farm near Saint-Aubin for the phantom rendezvous, the dates chosen against the almanac for fog. Elodie committed it all to the vault behind her eyes and burned nothing, wrote nothing, trusted only the dark and the man beside her.

It was Theo who blew out the lamp and barred the door behind them, and Theo who stopped suddenly on the path, his hand closing hard on her arm.

Down the lane, at the edge of the lantern-light from the farmhouse, a carriage stood in the frost — sleek, black, unhurried, its horses steaming. And beside it, stamping his feet against the cold and smiling his thin smile at the lit windows of the house, waited Citizen Delacour, who had sent no word and announced no audit, and who had come, tonight of all nights, to call.
