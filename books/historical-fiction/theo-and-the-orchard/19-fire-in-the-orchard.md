## 19. Fire in the Orchard

**Theo**

They came for Aubertin at dusk, when the light went the color of old cider and the harbor town's lamps were being lit one by one, like a man counting his fears.

Theo watched from the upper window of the cooper's loft, his breath fogging the glass. Below, in the square, four soldiers walked the general out of the customs house with his hands bound before him and his coat torn at the shoulder where they had taken him two nights past. Aubertin did not stumble. He never stumbled. Even now, even ill, even gray as ash from the fever that had ridden him through three provinces, he walked as though the square were a parade ground and the soldiers an honor guard too stupid to know it.

*You will trade the page for nothing,* Elodie had said. *A page is paper. A man is not.*

But the page was not a page anymore. It was the half-letter, Delacour's recovered fragment, and with what Theo carried sewn into his own coat lining it made the whole — the whole truth of the generals sold to the enemy by their own committee, the names, the dates, the signatures. Enough to hang a dozen men in Paris. Enough, if it reached foreign presses, to crack the Republic's story like ice under a boot heel.

And enough, apparently, to buy a prisoner.

The adjutant — Roques, the man who had killed Delacour at the mill and vanished with the torn half like a ghost returning to haunt its own body — had sent word through a fishwife who did not ask questions: *The committee wants the documents. Bring them to the customs pier at moonrise. The general goes free. Or bring nothing, and watch him put on the cart for Paris tomorrow.*

A lie, probably. Theo knew it was probably a lie. Men who wanted documents did not release witnesses; they released them into rivers. But knowing a thing was a lie did not stop it from being the only door in the wall.

He had spent the afternoon doing the arithmetic no one should ever have to do. If he gave them everything, the truth died on a French pier, burned or buried, and the men who had betrayed an army kept their seats. If he gave them nothing, Aubertin died on a cart, slowly, publicly, in Paris, and the promise Theo had made in a peacetime garden — *deliver it whole, to no one else* — curdled into the vow that killed its maker.

So he would give them something true and something false, braided together like graftwood and rootstock, and pray the cut took.

In his coat, against his heart, lay the real dispatches' location written in Elodie's hand — no. Not the location. A decoy. She had written it herself, her letters small and fierce: *the millstone at the abandoned press above Gravelines.* There was nothing there but a stone and rats. The true papers were already gone, already moving, because while Theo stood in this window playing the martyr, Elodie was walking down to the neutral ships at anchor with a barrel of salt herring and a nation's worth of treason packed between the fish.

*Everything burns,* she had said last night, her forehead against his, in the dark of the loft where they had stopped pretending they were only comrades. *That is what fire is for. You burn the field so the root survives.*

Below, the soldiers reached the pier. One carried a lantern; its light swung and made shadows leap along the pilings like living things.

Theo buttoned his coat over the false letter and went down the stairs to give away a war.

* * *

**Elodie**

The herring stank magnificently, which was the point. No customs officer in the world wanted to open a barrel of salt herring after dark, and the one who did want to — a boy of perhaps seventeen with a bayonet he clearly did not know how to hold — had been paid three livres to look elsewhere, and looked elsewhere with the diligence of youth.

Elodie walked the gangplank with the barrel on her shoulder, and the ship — the *Vrouw Geertruid*, Dutch-flagged, fat-bellied, bound for Hamburg on the morning tide — received her without comment. The captain, a broad man named Verhoeven with a beard like winter brush, had been paid in English gold and asked exactly one question: *Is it heavy?*

*Heavier than you know,* she had said, and he had laughed, and that had been the whole contract.

Now she wedged the barrel into the false bottom of the fish hold, beneath three more barrels of genuinely appalling herring, and pressed her palms flat against the wood. Through the planks she could feel the sea working, the slow respiration of the tide. In a few hours this wood would be carrying the only copy in the world of what General Aubertin had spent six years and his own good name assembling: the proof that the Republic had fed its own armies to the enemy's guns, and signed the orders in triplicate.

By spring it would be in the London papers. By summer, in every coffeehouse from Amsterdam to Philadelphia. And the men whose names were in those pages would wake one morning to find the world had read their mail.

It should have felt like triumph. It felt like cold.

Because Theo was not on this ship. Theo was walking toward the customs pier right now with a lie in his coat and a dead man's handwriting in his heart, and she had let him go, because someone had to hold the soldiers' eyes while the ship slipped its mooring, and because he had kissed her once, hard, at the top of the loft stairs, and said, *If I don't come back, deliver it whole. To no one else.* Her own words given back to her, the courier's creed turned into a love letter.

She climbed out of the hold. Above deck, the crew worked in silence, casting off the smaller lines. Verhoeven stood at the rail and looked at her with the frank appraisal of a man who has smuggled many things and regrets none of them.

"The tide turns at moonrise," he said. "I wait for no one. Not even pretty couriers."

"I know."

"You have the face of a woman leaving something behind."

"Keep your observations, Captain. Sail your ship."

He grunted, almost a laugh, and turned back to the water. Elodie stayed at the rail, watching the lights of the harbor town, watching for fire.

Because that was the other half of it — the part that made her chest ache like a bruise. The orchard. Vasseur's orchard, the failing little orchard that had hidden her for a year, that had taught her the rhythm of pruning and the language of grafts, where Madame Vasseur pretended not to see what Elodie carried and fed her anyway. Roques's plan, relayed through the fishwife with bureaucratic precision: at moonrise, a patrol would ride out and burn the orchard. Unregistered persons had been found there, the report would say. Seditious traffic. The fire would explain everything — the missing stores, the strangers, the graves of questions no one would dig up once the evidence was ash.

Cover, they called it. As if a year of her life could be covered by smoke.

She watched the dark road that led inland, toward the trees, and waited for the sky to change color.

* * *

**Theo**

The pier smelled of tar and dead fish and the particular metallic fear-sweat of men who carry loaded muskets on cold nights. Moonrise was close; the moon came up fat and low behind the masts, turning the harbor water into hammered pewter.

Roques stood at the pier's end beside Aubertin. The adjutant was younger than Theo had imagined — thirty perhaps, with a neat scar through one eyebrow and the patient face of a man who had done terrible things efficiently and intended to continue. Aubertin swayed slightly on his feet. The fever had him still, then. Good. A sick man was a slower man, and slowness might save him.

"You brought it." Roques held out his hand.

"I brought part of it." Theo did not move. "The rest is hidden where only I can reach it. Your committee taught me that much — trust no one, especially not each other."

Something flickered in Roques's scarred eyebrow. Respect, maybe. Or the calculation of a man deciding whether to shoot.

"Show me the part you brought."

Theo drew out the folded pages — Elodie's forgery, three hours of her work by candlelight, a convincing half-truth stitched from genuine fragments. He held them just beyond Roques's fingers. "The general walks to the end of the pier first. Alone. Then you get your reading."

"And if I simply take both?"

"Then you get ash. The rest is with a friend who has instructions and a torch." He let the lie sit in the salt air. "You've seen what fire does to paper, Adjutant. You're planning to teach an orchard the lesson tonight."

Roques's face did not move, but his eyes went briefly inland, toward the dark line of trees, and Theo knew the plan was true, knew the riders were already saddling, and hated himself for the small relief of having guessed correctly.

"The end of the pier," Roques said at last. "Walk, General."

Aubertin walked. Ten paces, fifteen, his boots loud on the wet planks, and then he was beside Theo, close enough that Theo could smell the sickness on him, camphor and sweat, and hear the ruined whisper:

"Theo Marchand. Still keeping promises?"

"Trying to."

"Give them the pages," Aubertin breathed. "Whatever happens. Paper is—" He coughed, wet and deep. "Paper is replaceable. I am not, and neither are you."

*I know,* Theo thought. *That is why the pages are lies, and the truth is already at sea.* But he said nothing, because Roques was striding toward them with his hand out, and the moment had come to be either brave or dead.

He handed over the forged pages. Roques read them by lantern light, fast, his lips moving, and Theo watched him the way Elodie had taught him to watch — not at the face but at the hands, at the weight shifting from heel to heel.

"He'll check the millstone," Theo told himself. *Let him. Let him ride all night to find rats and a cold stone.*

Roques looked up. "Where is the remainder?"

"At the place described on the last page. Which you now possess." Theo nodded at the papers. "Read it again when your hands stop shaking."

For a heartbeat Theo believed it would work. He actually believed it — believed in the arithmetic of lies, believed the moon would rise and the ship would slide out and the orchard would burn for nothing worse than ash.

Then Roques smiled, thin as a knife's edge, and said, "There is no last page, Marchand. I've read this letter before. I carried it out of Delacour's files myself." He raised two fingers, and the soldiers on the pier raised their muskets. "You gave me three pages of very elegant fiction."

* * *

**Elodie**

She saw the fire before she understood it — a bloom of orange far inland, wrong-shaped, too wide for a lamp or a farmhouse window. Then the wind brought the smell, resin and smoke, and she knew.

The orchard.

Even at this distance she could see the shapes of it: the long rows of apple trees going up like a hundred small pyres, the pear trees by the wall, the great twisted elder that Vasseur's grandfather had planted. Flames stood up out of the darkness and leaned east with the wind, and sparks streamed off the burning crowns like a migration of orange birds.

Madame Vasseur. The farmhands. The cellar where she had slept through the winter of '94 listening to mice in the walls and memorizing routes by the rhythm of the pruning knife. All of it feeding the fire, all of it becoming weather.

Someone had gotten out — she could see small figures on the far slope, moving away from the blaze, and she chose, deliberately, ferociously, to believe one of them was Madame Vasseur, wrapped in a blanket, cursing the Republic with her magnificent Norman vocabulary. She would believe it until told otherwise. Some beliefs you carried like ballast.

Verhoeven appeared at her shoulder. "Your friends burn?" he asked, without judgment.

"My home," she said, and heard how strange the word sounded, and how true.

Behind them the anchor chain began to run. The tide was turning. Somewhere down the pier, in the pewter light, Theo was standing in front of loaded muskets holding three pages of nothing, and she could not go to him, because the ship was moving, because the papers beneath the herring were the whole reason any of them had bled, because duty was a current stronger than grief.

*Stronger*, she thought. *Not louder.*

She gripped the rail. The harbor town slid slowly astern, lamp by lamp, and the fire on the hill burned brighter than all of them together, and Elodie Reyes stood on a neutral deck with salt spray on her lips and did not look away from the burning until the headland took it from her.

* * *

**Theo**

The muskets did not fire.

Later, Theo would try to reconstruct why — Roques wanting him alive to reveal the hiding place, the soldiers uncertain about shooting in a crowded harbor, the moonlight making everyone theatrical — but in the moment there was only the sound of boots, many boots, and Roques saying, "Bind him. He'll tell us where the rest is by morning, one way or the other," and the rough rope biting Theo's wrists.

And then the pier exploded into noise.

It came from the water side — a boat, a longboat pulling hard from the shadow of the moored ships, and figures rising in it, and the flat crack of a pistol. One soldier folded. Another turned and fired high. Chaos, then — lanterns swinging, shouts in French and Dutch, a soldier grappling Theo and losing him as someone pulled Theo backward off his feet, and the pier planks coming up hard against his shoulder.

A voice at his ear, ragged with fever: "Hold still, boy, unless you want to lose an ear."

Aubertin. Free — his bonds cut, a captured knife in his shaking hand, kneeling on the planks in his torn coat like a beggar king. Behind him, rowing like demons, three men Theo had never seen, one of them bleeding from the scalp.

"The adjutant's network," Aubertin rasped, sawing at Theo's ropes. "I had friends he didn't know about. Every man needs a few." The ropes parted. "Up. Boat. Now."

Theo got up. His legs nearly refused. Down the pier, Roques was shouting orders, trying to reform his soldiers, and for one moment his eyes found Theo's across the lantern-light — and there was no rage in them, which was worse than rage. Only patience. *I know where the letter came from,* those eyes said. *I know about the brother. I will find you.*

Then hands hauled Theo over the gunwale, the boat leapt away from the pier, and the harbor opened around them, black water and silver moon-path, and behind them on the hill the orchard burned like a second sunset.

Theo knelt in the bilgewater and looked back. The trees he had learned to prune. The wall where Elodie had taught him grafting, her hands over his, the scion and the stock, *cut clean, join true*. Going up in orange ruin to cover a theft of paper, and the paper was safe, was at sea, was ten meters behind him in a Dutch hull full of herring.

"We lost," he said hoarsely. "The orchard. Everything—"

"We won," Aubertin said. His voice was a ruin, but his hand, when it closed on Theo's arm, was iron. "Look at the water, boy. What do you see?"

Theo looked. Moonlight, masts, the black headland.

"Nothing," he said.

"Exactly." The old man's cracked lips bent into something that had once been a smile. "Nothing is what victory looks like at sea. Everything we burned was only the tree. The seed is aboard that ship, and by spring it will be growing in every printing house in Europe." He coughed, and steadied himself against the thwart. "Now row, Marchand. We must reach the *Geertruid* before the tide takes her past the roads — and before that adjutant remembers he has a brother of yours in a cell in Paris."

The words went through Theo like cold iron. Lucien. The summons he had burned. The debt he had never told her about, surfacing now at the worst hour, riding on the enemy's patience.

The longboat bent to the oars. Ahead, the *Vrouw Geertruid* was making sail, her canvas filling pale and enormous against the moon, and Theo rowed toward her with his blistered hands and his burning lungs and one thought repeating like a pulse:

*Get aboard. Get to her. Tell her everything — the brother, the bargain, all of it — before the century turns and the truth arrives without you.*
