## 4. Dispatches and Deadlines

The shop announced itself by smell before it did by sign: a dark sweetness of pipe tobacco and cured leaf bleeding out into the frozen street like warmth from a wound. The sign itself was small and apologetic — *K. Havel, Tobacconist* — the paint gone gray at the edges, a wooden cigar painted on it so many times over that it had become less a cigar than a brown idea of one. Above the door, icicles hung from the lintel in a row as even as organ pipes.

Quentin stood across the street for the better part of an hour, mending nothing, selling nothing, being nobody. That was the discipline. Herr Voss had taught him it in the first winter of his other life, standing beside him at a tram stop while the snow came down like static. *You do not approach a door you have not already left a hundred times,* Voss had said, breath smoking. *Stand there until your feet believe they've done it before. The body is the only papers that can't be forged.* So Quentin had bought a roll from the baker two doors down, eaten it slowly, argued mildly with a fishmonger about the price of herring, spat into the gutter, stamped his feet against the cold — performed, in short, the whole dull liturgy of a journeyman bookbinder with an hour to kill and no reason in the world to be anywhere. Only when his feet had learned the cobblestones did he cross the street and push open the door of K. Havel's establishment.

A bell rang above him, tinny and tired.

Inside, the gloom was total and deliberate. The window had been painted over from within years ago; what light entered came through the transom and fell in a single dusty blade across a counter crowded with jars, tins, and clay pipes hung from pegs like small drowned men. The air was thick enough to chew. Behind the counter sat a man who might have been sixty or ninety, bald as an egg, with a mustache the color of old ash and eyes that had been folded shut by decades of smoke. He was reading a newspaper through a loupe held in one bloodless hand, and he did not look up.

"Good day," Quentin said, in the flat accent of the river provinces. "I'm told you stock the Vraney mixture. The coarse cut."

The loupe lowered half an inch. One eye appeared behind it, magnified and pale, like something raised from deep water.

"Vraney," the tobacconist repeated. His voice was gravel poured slow. "Haven't stocked Vraney since the autumn. Nobody smokes Vraney anymore. It's a soldier's tobacco." He set down the paper. "What regiment would that be?"

"The Ninth Sappers," Quentin said. "But I'm out of that trade now. Bookbinding." He set his satchel on the counter, turned it so the false spine faced outward, and opened it to show the blank folios stacked inside, the bone folder, the awl, the little brick of beeswax wrapped in linen. "I bind ledgers, mostly. Ledgers and prayer books. There's more call for prayer books lately, if you follow me."

"I don't follow anybody," said the tobacconist. But his hand had moved, without hurry, to a drawer beneath the counter, and when it came back up it held not tobacco but a brass key. "Back room's where I keep the strong stuff. Mind the step. It's been a wet month."

The back room smelled of leaf and lamp oil and, beneath both, faintly of gun grease. It was narrow as a coffin stood upright, shelved floor to ceiling with bales of tobacco in brown paper, and between two of the bales, where a shelf should have been, there was a door instead. The key turned. Beyond it: a room barely larger than a table, a table itself, one chair, one lamp, and a man.

The man was younger than Quentin expected, which meant the network was thinner than he'd been told. He had the soft hands of a clerk and the red-rimmed eyes of someone who had not slept properly in a week. He wore a shopkeeper's apron over civilian wool, and he was packing a leather wallet with the speed of a man rehearsing departure.

"You're the binder," he said. Not a question. "Sit down. We haven't long, and I have bad news, and bad news keeps worse than tobacco."

Quentin sat. He kept his satchel on his knees, the way a workman does, the way a man with nothing to hide does. Inside it, sewn into the false spine beneath three blank folios, lay the dispatches: eleven pages in a cipher Herr Voss himself had designed, containing figures that could shorten this war by a season or lose it outright, depending on whose hands they reached first. Eleven pages. Six weeks of travel. Two checkpoints, one frozen river, one bribe paid in bound books. They weighed nothing. They weighed everything.

"Talk," Quentin said.

"The talks are dead." The young man said it flatly, the way you report weather. "Or sleeping. Depends which delegate you ask. The eastern delegation walked out over the corridor question nine days ago. They're saying it's theater now, that neither side ever intended to sign before spring. Which means—" he spread his hands, "—the war goes on breathing. And everything moving through the cold keeps moving."

"And my cargo?"

"Is now worth twice what it was and worth nothing at all, simultaneously." The young man almost smiled; it didn't take. "If the armistice signs, those figures are yesterday's newspaper. If it doesn't, they're the most dangerous eleven pages in the province. Congratulations. You're carrying a lottery ticket in a burning house."

Quentin felt the satchel's weight shift on his knees, though of course it hadn't moved. "Then forward it. That's the point of me. Point of the whole chain. I carry, the next man carries—"

"The next man is taken."

The words dropped into the room and lay there.

"Taken," Quentin repeated.

"Four days ago. At the Millbridge crossing. Our man — you'd have known him as the clockmaker — was stopped for a routine search and they found what they found. Whether they were tipped or simply lucky, we don't know, and not knowing is the worst part." The young man rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. "He's in the garrison lockup. He hasn't talked yet — or he has, and they're sitting quiet on it, letting the line run so they can pull the whole net up at once. Either way, every hand this passes through from tonight onward is a hand inside a trap. Yours included."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere beyond the tobacco bales, the shop bell rang — a customer, ordinary, mortal, buying pipe tobacco on a gray afternoon — and Quentin heard the tobacconist's gravel voice murmur prices, and the ordinariness of it made the little room feel more secret and more fragile, like a match cupped in two palms outdoors.

"How compromised?" Quentin asked. "Names? Routes? Or just the man?"

"If they've broken him, all of it. If they haven't, just him. We won't know until they move, and when they move it'll be fast." The young man leaned forward. "Which brings us to your instruction, and you're going to hate it."

"Tell me anyway."

"Hold. Wait here. You don't go forward, because forward is Millbridge and Millbridge is where the net is. You don't go back, because back is the river checkpoint and the river checkpoint has started searching spines — actual book spines, if you can credit their imagination. You stay in this town, under your cover, being a bookbinder, until we send word. A week. Maybe two. Maybe—" he shrugged, and the shrug admitted everything, "—longer."

"A week." Quentin heard his own voice come out level, which surprised him, because underneath the levelness something was clawing. "Two. Possibly longer."

"It's the craft, binder. Sometimes the craft is standing still."

*Sometimes the craft is standing still.* Herr Voss had never said that. Herr Voss had said the opposite, had built the whole catechism of invisibility on motion: *Never sleep two nights in the same bed unless the bed is boring. Never be interesting. Never be early, never be late, be unremarkable in every particular, and above all keep moving, because a man in motion is a rumor and a man standing still is a fact, and facts can be arrested.* Six years Quentin had lived by it. Six years of towns entered at dusk and left at dawn, of names worn like coats and changed with the weather, of a self kept deliberately shallow so that no one could dig in it and find anything. Motion was not merely his method. It was his anatomy. To be told *hold, wait, stay* was to be told *stop your heart and see how you like it.*

And there was a second thing, quieter and worse, coiled in the lining of the satchel along with the dispatches. Because the false spine had two pockets. One held the cipher pages. The other held a letter — a private letter, sealed with wax, addressed in a dead man's careful hand to a family across the border — and Quentin had broken the first rule of his entire trade the night he'd put it there. *Nothing personal in the kit,* Voss had said. *Personal things are hooks, and hooks are how they land you.* But the letter had been in his keeping for six years, and it had crossed two rivers and three identity changes with him, migrating from coat to coat, bag to bag, like an organ the body refused to part with, and it was going nowhere except where it was going, orders or no orders, armistice or no armistice.

He looked at the young clerk-packer across the table and thought: *I am carrying two cargoes and you know about one.*

"Understood," Quentin said aloud. "I hold. Where do I lodge?"

"There's a widow on Tanner's Lane takes lodgers. Say you're recommended by the cooper. And binder—" the young man hesitated, and for a moment his clerk's face showed something younger and frightened underneath, "—don't be clever. I know your kind. The good ones are always the worst for it. You'll want to improvise some route around the trouble, invent a third way, because standing still feels like dying to men like us. Don't. The trouble isn't a detour you can route around. The trouble is the weather. You wait out the weather."

"The weather," Quentin said, "has killed more couriers than patrols ever did."

"Yes," the young man agreed cheerlessly. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

He took a room on Tanner's Lane that evening — a narrow bed, a washstand, a window overlooking a yard where someone had strung laundry lines that in summer would hold sheets and in winter held nothing but the memory of sheets, the ropes sagging under frost like old harp strings. The widow was deaf in one ear and incurious in the other ear, which was the ideal arrangement, and she charged fairly and asked nothing except whether he snored. He said he didn't know. She said that was the honest answer and gave him the room.

He unpacked with the economy of long practice: tools on the sill, folios in the chest, satchel under the mattress at the head of the bed where a sleeping man's weight would lie over it all night. Then he sat on the bed in the gathering dark and did the thing he hated most in the world, which was nothing.

It is a peculiar torture, idleness, for a man trained to vanish through movement. Every hour stationary was an hour in which the town's eyes adjusted to him, in which faces became familiar, in which the cooper's recommendation curdled from a shield into a thread that could be pulled. Stillness accumulated detail, and detail was evidence. By tomorrow he would be *the binder lodging at the widow's*. By the day after, someone would know his breakfast habits. A week, and he would have a shape, and shapes could be described to sergeants.

He tried to work. He had brought in a commission that afternoon — a merchant's account book with a cracked spine, honest work, honest glue — and he sat with it on his knees under the lamp, trimming and pasting, letting his hands do their dumb competent labor while his mind ran its circles. *Hold. Wait. The clockmaker taken. The net waiting to close.* He turned the figures over. If the courier chain was blown at Millbridge, then the dispatches were worth delivering precisely never, and destroying them precisely soon — except that destroying them meant the war went on a season longer, meant the winter conscriptions, meant the columns of boys marching east through the frost with cloth shoes, and he could not make his hands want that either. So the eleven pages stayed sewn in their spine, radioactive with usefulness, and he stayed still as a plugged bottle, and the frustration sat in his chest like swallowed stone.

Near midnight he heard the widow shuffling below, banking her stove, and then the house ticked and settled into its cold. Quentin lay on the narrow bed with his hands behind his head and watched the frost ferns grow on the window glass, branching and branching, patient as bureaucracy.

Two days, he told himself. Three. A man could endure anything numbered.

He was wrong about the numbering, though he wouldn't learn that for another day. What he learned sooner — the very next morning, in fact, when he went down to fetch water and found the widow's other lodger reading the gazette by the stove — was that the town had its own weather coming. The gazette announced a veterans' parade, Thursday next, in honor of the old regiments, bands and banners and a collection for the pension fund. The widow's lodger read the list of honored units aloud in a voice fat with nostalgia, and Quentin, standing in the doorway with his water jug, heard a number spoken that he had not heard spoken in six years — the number of a regiment that officially no longer existed, having been disbanded, renamed, and quietly erased after the first winter of the war, along with everyone who had served in it.

The water jug was heavy in his hand. The stove ticked. The lodger read on, warm and oblivious, and outside on Tanner's Lane the frost let go of the eaves in a long bright crash, like something beginning to fall.
