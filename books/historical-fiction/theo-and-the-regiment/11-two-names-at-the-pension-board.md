## 11. Two Names at the Pension Board

The Pension Board sat in what had once been the town's wedding hall.

Quentin noticed the irony the moment he stepped inside, because the room had not been redecorated so much as repurposed — the garlands of paper flowers still looped along the cornices, gone the color of old teeth, and the dance floor was now a waiting area of mismatched chairs where men with missing pieces of themselves sat in the particular silence of people who had learned that no one wanted to hear about it. A chalkboard by the door still bore, faintly, the ghost of some celebration — *Mirela & Anton* — half-scrubbed, the chalk dust ground into the slate so that the names would outlive the marriage, the hall's purpose, possibly the republic itself.

Above it, someone had hung a new sign on a nail: **BOARD OF RELIEF — VETERANS' CLAIMS — PRESENT YOURSELF AND YOUR PAPERS IN ORDER OF ARRIVAL.**

*Present yourself.* Quentin stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands and thought that in six years he had presented himself as a bookbinder, a journeyman, a cousin, a lodger, a man with a limp on one occasion and a stutter on another, and never once — not at a checkpoint, not at a census, not to Madame Ostrer with her terrible patient pencil — had he presented himself. The self was the one thing he had never shown anyone.

The hall smelled of wet wool, coal smoke, and the particular staleness of government offices everywhere: paper that had been handled by too many hands, ink that had dried in a hurry, the faint sweet rot of a vase of flowers nobody had changed since the armistice talks began. A stove stood in the corner radiating heat in a grudging, uneven way, so that the room had climates — warm near the stove, arctic by the windows, where frost still feathered the lower panes. The men nearest the stove had arranged themselves with the unconscious territorial precision of old soldiers. The men by the windows shivered and said nothing.

Dremmer was already there, third in the queue, sitting very straight on a chair too small for him. He had shaved. He had, Quentin saw with a strange lurch, put on a collar — an actual collar, buttoned, with a jacket brushed clean and his one eye aimed forward at the clerk's table with the fixed intensity of a man aiming a rifle at something that could not be killed by rifle fire. His badge tray was nowhere in sight. His cap was in his lap, held in both hands, and his knuckles were white around its brim.

He did not look at Quentin when Quentin sat down beside him. He looked at the clerk.

* * *

The clerk was a young man with a face like an unlined ledger, and he was bored in the profound, geological way of a person who has processed four hundred claims this winter and expects to process four hundred more. He had a stamp. Quentin could not take his eyes off the stamp. It sat on the desk beside an inkpad, a wooden handle worn smooth, and it fell and rose and fell and rose with the rhythm of a pump drawing water from a very deep well — *approved, deferred, denied, deferred, approved* — each fall ending a man's morning, perhaps a man's year, with a sound no louder than a knuckle rapped on a door.

"Name," said the clerk, to the man at the table.

"Kolar, sir. Josip Kolar. Seventh Rifles."

"Papers."

Rustling. A cough. The stamp. *Deferred.* The man gathered his papers with the care of someone gathering spilled grain, and the next man took his place, and the next, and the line shortened with the terrible efficiency of a machine that did not care what it fed on.

Dremmer's turn came on a Tuesday morning that had begun with sleet.

Quentin had not planned to speak. That was the thing he would turn over later, in the nights that followed, turning it like a stone in the pocket to feel all its edges: he had not planned it. He had told himself, walking over, that he would simply be present, a witness in the room, a body in a chair, and that Dremmer would name him and the clerk would ask his trade and he would say *bookbinder* and that would be that. He had rehearsed the smallness of it. He had believed, the way a man believes a rope bridge will hold because it held yesterday, in the smallness.

"Name," said the clerk.

"Dremmer, sir. Sergeant Ansel Dremmer. Fourth Regiment of the Line, Second Battalion." The voice came out steady, parade-ground steady, and then cracked on the next word like ice under a boot. "Pension claim. Wound to the — " he gestured at his ruined eye with a hand that was not quite steady — "to the left side. Sustained at the Kolvary bridge, second year of the war."

The clerk looked up for the first time. He had a way of looking at people that was not unkind and not kind — the look of a man checking a sum.

"Papers, Sergeant Dremmer."

Dremmer produced them. Quentin watched the clerk go through them — the discharge chit, the regimental attestation, the medical note with its laconic Latin — and watched the clerk's finger stop on the third page, tap twice, and lift.

"This attestation is countersigned by a witness. It says here — " the clerk turned the page toward the hall, though no one in the hall could read it at that distance, and perhaps that was the point, the theater of it — "it says the witness is present today and will vouch for the claimant's service."

The hall went quiet in the way rooms go quiet when everyone in them understands, at the same moment, that something is about to be asked of someone.

"Name of witness," said the clerk.

Dremmer turned in his chair. His single eye found Quentin's, and there was no triumph in it, no gloating, none of the sly arithmetic Quentin had braced himself for outside the bindery. There was only a kind of bleak, embarrassed apology — the look of a man who has led you into a field he was told was safe, and knows now that it is not, and cannot say so out loud.

"Quentin," Dremmer said. And then, because the clerk was waiting, because the word alone was a coin too small to buy anything: "Quentin Montrose."

* * *

The name went into the room and the room did not end.

That was the astonishing thing. Quentin had spent six years imagining this moment in a hundred forms — the checkpoint, the cell, the lamp in the face, the knock at midnight — and in every version the world had convulsed. In none of them had the world simply continued: the stove ticking, the frost on the windows, the clerk dipping his pen.

"Montrose," the clerk repeated, writing it. "First name."

"Quentin."

"Trade or profession."

"Bookbinder," Quentin said, and his voice held, and he hated how well it held.

"Relation to the claimant."

And here was the trap, laid flat and innocent on the desk between the inkpad and the stamp. *Relation.* The clerk wanted a word to put in the box, and every word was a lie, and every lie was a small stone added to the wall of the other name, the buried one, and the wall was high, and he was tired, and Dremmer was watching him with that bleak apology, and somewhere behind the clerk's bored face the machinery of the record was turning, turning, turning, ready to engrave whatever he said into the one place from which nothing could ever be scraped away.

"Comrade in arms," Quentin said. "Fourth Regiment of the Line. Second Battalion. I served under Sergeant Dremmer before — " before I buried it, before the maps went mad, before I learned that a man can be unwritten — "before the regiment was disbanded."

The clerk's pen scratched. *Comrade in arms.* Four words. Six years of invisibility, spent in four words, and the clerk did not even look up.

"You attest," the clerk said, in the tone of a man reading a form he has read four hundred times, "that the claimant, Sergeant Ansel Dremmer, served in the Fourth Regiment of the Line, Second Battalion, and sustained the described wound in the action at the Kolvary bridge, and that his character and service were — " the pen paused for the next printed phrase — "honorable."

"I attest it."

"Your name and mark."

Quentin signed. His hand did not shake, which he would remember too, later, with something close to grief — that his hand had betrayed him by its steadiness, had signed *Quentin Montrose, bookbinder, comrade in arms* in the same clean slanted script he used on the spines of other men's books, as if this were simply another repair, another mending of a broken record, when it was in fact the first entry the world had made against him since the war began.

The stamp fell. *Approved.*

Dremmer made a sound — not a word, not quite a breath, something between the two, the sound a man makes when a weight he has carried so long he has forgotten it is a weight is suddenly, officially, removed. His hand went to his collar and straightened it, though it did not need straightening. His eye was wet and he did not wipe it and did not look at Quentin, and Quentin understood that the not-looking was itself a kind of thanks, the only kind Dremmer knew how to give without making it worse.

"Next," said the clerk.

Quentin stood. His legs felt strange, lighter and heavier at once, the way a man feels when he has set down a load and only then registers how the ground has changed under him. He walked out of the wedding hall past the chalk ghost of *Mirela & Anton*, past the men by the frost-fretted windows who watched him with the flat appraisal of men who had heard a name spoken and filed it away the way soldiers file everything, in the deep drawers where nothing is ever really thrown out.

* * *

The corridor outside was long and dim, lit by windows at the far end that turned the sleet into a curtain of gray light. It smelled of floor wax and cold stone. His boots rang on the tiles, and each ring sounded, to his newly sensitized ear, like the stamp. *Approved.* The name had been approved. That was the joke he could not laugh at: the board had approved the name, had entered it into the record, had made it official, and in doing so had done precisely what the war had never managed to do in six years of searching.

He had done the right thing. He held the thought up and examined it from all sides, the way he examined a damaged binding, and it was the right thing, undeniably — a man had traded his eye for a bridge, had come with his papers in order, had shaved and collared himself and sat straight in a small chair, and all he had asked was for one living voice to say *yes, that happened, he was there, it was honorable.* Against that, what was a name? Names were paper. He had built his life on the knowledge that names were paper.

But paper cuts.

He was halfway down the corridor before he saw her, and then he stopped, because there was nowhere to go — the corridor had one door behind him and one light ahead, and between them, standing beside the window with her ledger held against her chest the way other women held prayer books, stood Madame Ostrer.

She was not hiding. That was the first thing he understood about the scene, and the most frightening. She was not pretending to read a notice board or study the frost. She stood in full view, in her gray coat and her good boots, with the ledger's marbled cover catching the gray light, and she looked at him with the expression of a woman who has confirmed a suspicion she did not want confirmed, and who has come anyway, on a sleet-gray Tuesday, to see it with her own eyes.

"Madame Ostrer." His voice was steady. His voice, he was beginning to understand, was the enemy's most reliable collaborator. "You have business with the board?"

"I have business with everyone, Mr. — " she paused, delicately, a pause the width of a name — "Montrose. The census is a business with everyone. That is its whole nature, though people find it rude when I say so." She came a step closer. The sleet ticked against the glass behind her. "I came to file the district's quarterly returns. I did not know I would find the pension board in session. I did not know I would hear — " she touched the ledger's edge with one finger, a gesture so small it might have been accidental — "a name."

"Many names were spoken in there."

"Yes." She looked at him, and her eyes were not unkind, which was the worst thing about them. "Many names. And yours was one of them, and it was spoken in answer to a question, and it was written down, and it was stamped." She recited the sequence like a lesson she had taught a hundred times to slower pupils. "You understand, Mr. Montrose, that I have spent the winter asking you questions. Trade, kin, previous service. And you have spent the winter answering them so smoothly that I began to doubt my own arithmetic. A woman in my position begins to wonder if she is going mad — if the small inconsistencies are real, or if grief has made her a suspicious old woman who sees soldiers' hands on every bookbinder in the district."

"Madame — "

"Let me finish. I have not finished, and I find, at my age, that if I stop I may not begin again." She adjusted her grip on the ledger. "I heard a man in that room say *Fourth Regiment of the Line, Second Battalion,* and I heard you say it after him, and I heard the clerk write it, and I want you to know what I did then, because I think you should know. I did not open this book." She lifted it slightly. "I stood in that doorway, and I held it shut, and I listened to you do a decent thing for a one-eyed man who had nothing left to trade but your name, and I thought: *there. That is what the inconsistencies were.* Not a spy. Spies do not pay other men's debts. Spies do not stand up in a cold hall and hand the government the one thing they have spent six years hiding, for nothing, for a stranger."

The corridor was very quiet. The sleet had thickened; the light at the window's end had gone the color of pewter.

"You are not going to report me," Quentin said. It was not quite a question.

Madame Ostrer looked at him for a long moment, and in that moment Quentin saw what he had not let himself see in all her visits: the two sons, the empty chairs, the whole geography of loss that she carried the way he carried a letter in a false spine — close, hidden, and heavier than anyone could guess from the outside.

"No," she said. "Not today. But I will tell you what I told myself in that doorway, Mr. Montrose, and you may make of it what you like. A name in a record is like a seed in winter ground. It does nothing. It lies there. And then the weather changes." She drew her coat tighter and turned toward the board's office door. "I have returns to file. Good morning."

She went in. The door closed behind her with the soft, definitive click of well-made government hardware, and Quentin stood alone in the gray corridor with the sleet ticking on the glass, and understood that the weather had already begun to change — that somewhere across the town, in a requisitioned tobacconist's shop that had been silent for two days, men who were not clerks were going through a courier's papers, and that every paper they found would lead to another, and that the name now stamped in the wedding hall's ledger was a seed in winter ground after all, and he had planted it himself, with his own steady hand, in his own clean script.

He put on his hat and walked out into the sleet, and behind him, faint through the closing door, he heard the stamp fall. *Approved.*
