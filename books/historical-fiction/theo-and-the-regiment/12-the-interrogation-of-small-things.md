## 12. The Interrogation of Small Things

The requisition order arrived on a Tuesday, folded twice and sealed with the grey wax of the District Security Office — not red, which would have meant urgency, but grey, which meant permanence. Madame Ostrer had handled enough government paper in her nineteen years of census work to know that grey wax was worse than red. Red meant a crisis, and crises passed. Grey meant a procedure, and procedures ate you slowly.

*By authority of the Armistice Commission for Internal Verification, all household registers for the Millward district are to be surrendered for cross-examination against the deserter rolls. Transcription to be performed under supervision. Refusal constitutes obstruction.*

She read it three times at her kitchen table while her tea went cold, then read it once more aloud, because there were certain sentences that changed their weight when spoken. They did not change this one. *Surrendered.* As though the books were contraband. As though nineteen years of walking doorsteps in bad weather, of asking mothers how many children still lived at home and watching them count on fingers that sometimes stopped too early, had been the preparation of evidence rather than the keeping of a record.

She put on her good coat, the one with the mended cuff she always wore for official business because it made clerks underestimate her, and she carried her registers to the Security Office in a canvas satchel, one book per armful, like a woman bringing eggs to market who suspected the market would smash them.

* * *

The room they gave her was on the second floor, above the guardhouse, and it smelled of cold iron and lamp oil. A single window looked out onto the drill yard, where four soldiers were practicing the stacking of rifles in weather too cold for anything sensible. The table was long, scarred, and lit by two lamps placed so that whoever sat across from her would be shadowed and she would be lit. She noted this without offense. She had spent two decades noting things; it was the whole of her trade.

The officer arrived at half past nine. He was younger than his rank suggested, perhaps thirty, with the careful neatness of a man whose tailoring did the work his face declined to do. His name, stenciled on a card he laid before her like a playing card turned face up, was Lieutenant Havel. He set down beside the card a leather folio, a bottle of ink, a pen, and — this was the detail she fixed upon immediately, the way a jeweler's eye goes first to the flaw — a pencil.

"Madame Ostrer." He pronounced it correctly, which surprised her; most people said *Oz-ter*, flattening it, and she had long ago stopped correcting them. "You'll understand the necessity. The armistice requires verification of population on both sides of the provisional line. Deserters move through districts like water through cracks. Your registers are the most complete record of who belongs here and who does not."

"Who was here," she corrected mildly. "A census is a photograph, Lieutenant, not a film. It tells you who stood still on a given day. It cannot tell you what they intended."

"Intentions," Havel said, "are what we hope your entries will suggest."

He opened the folio: typed lists, columns of names, the deserter rolls from three regiments disbanded or dissolved or simply vanished into the winter. He arranged them to his left. To her right he placed the first register — Millward proper, households one through ninety-four — and between them, in the exact center of the table, the pencil.

"You will transcribe each entry onto these summary sheets," he said. "Name, age, trade, kin, prior service. In ink. Where an entry is ambiguous, damaged, or illegible, you will mark it thus—" he drew a small circle in the air with one finger, "—and I will examine the original myself."

"And the pencil?"

His eyes came up. For the first time he seemed to actually see her, and she understood that until now she had been furniture — a functionary requisitioned along with her books.

"The pencil is for your convenience, madame. Drafting. Corrections."

"Of course," said Madame Ostrer, and picked up the pen.

* * *

They worked through the morning. The rhythm of it settled over the room like snowfall: Havel reading a name from the deserter roll, Ostrer finding the corresponding household, transcribing, Havel comparing, nodding or not nodding. The lamps hissed. Outside, the rifle-stacking drill ended and another began. Her hand ached in its old familiar way, the ache of twenty years of entries, and she let the ache steady her.

Household forty-one: the Kessler widow, three daughters, one son listed as *miller's apprentice*. The son was on no roll. She wrote him out in full, in ink, in her small even hand.

Household fifty-eight: the Ferenc brothers, both marked *laborer*, though she remembered the elder had limped in a way she had never asked about and never reported. Not on any roll either. Ink.

Household sixty-three.

She had known this line was coming since the moment Havel spread the rolls on the table. She had known it the way one knows a storm is coming — not by seeing it but by feeling the pressure drop, the animals go quiet, the light go strange. Household sixty-three: *Quentin Marlow, journeyman bookbinder, lodger, age unrecorded, kin none stated, previous regimental service — none.*

None. She had written it herself, six weeks ago, in this same small even hand, after an interview conducted in the parlor of the Widow Salm's boarding house while rain ticked at the windows. She remembered the interview with terrible clarity now, the way one remembers a hand of cards lost. She remembered his answers — smooth, courteous, each one shaped like a key fitted to her questions' locks. She remembered asking about his hands. *Bookbinding is hard on the hands,* she had said, and he had agreed, and shown her the calluses, and the calluses had been true calluses, real ones, earned — but earned at a grip wider than a bookbinder's press. She had known it the way she knew weather. And she had written something down anyway, in the margin, in pencil, because that was her habit and her discipline: *hands wrong for trade.*

Now Lieutenant Havel's finger came down the roll and stopped.

"Marlow," he said. "Quentin Marlow. Bookbinder." He checked the folio. Checked it again. "Not listed. Good. A clean entry." He glanced up. "You have a note in the margin."

"I make notes," she said. "It is my practice. A census that records only answers is half a census. It should also record doubts."

"What doubt did you record?"

She could have circled it. That was the procedure he himself had named: ambiguous, damaged, illegible — mark it, and the original would pass to his own examination. One small circle drawn around five words in pencil, and the line would become his, and whatever he found in those five words would be his finding, not hers. She had watched enough men like Havel work to know what he would find. A bookbinder with a soldier's hands, lodging alone, kinless, arrived in autumn when the roads were still open and the war still nominally running. A name on no roll — which was itself suspicious, because the rolls were incomplete and every man knew it, and innocence kept better company with paperwork than guilt did.

Or she could smudge it. Her thumb was resting on the margin already, she realized. She had not told it to. Nineteen years of handling paper had given her thumbs an intelligence of their own.

She thought about her sons.

She thought about them the way she thought about everything now, which was sideways, obliquely, in the manner of a woman who had learned that grief approached head-on only when invited. Tomas, dead at Kolvary in the second year, a name on a casualty list she had received by post with a postage due stamp. Anselm, dead the following spring, not in battle but of the fever that moved through the encampments like a landlord collecting rent. Two sons, entered in her own early registers as *children resident*, later amended in her own hand — the amendment the hardest writing she had ever done, harder than any testimony, harder than the letter to the pension board she had written nine times and burned nine times. She knew precisely what a pencil line weighed. She had drawn the heaviest one of her life herself, drawing a rule through *resident* and writing *deceased* above it, and her hand had not shaken, because the state required steadiness, and the state got it.

And she thought — sideways, obliquely — about the man in the boarding house parlor, showing her his hands and lying to her kindly. Lying the way people lie who have nothing left to protect except the lie itself. She had interviewed eleven hundred souls in this district and she could sort a lie from a silence the way a cook sorts grain from chaff, by weight, by sound, by the way it moved in the pan. Quentin Marlow's lie had been heavy. Heavy lies belonged to men carrying something. And in nineteen years she had learned that the state's instruments — the rolls, the summaries, the grey wax, the pencil — could not tell a heavy lie that protected a family from a heavy lie that threatened one. The instruments saw only weight.

"Lieutenant," she said, "may I ask you a question? You may decline."

He inclined his head, wary but curious. Curiosity was the crack in every young officer; she had built a career on it.

"When you were a boy, did anyone ever write your name down wrongly? On a school list, a muster, anything of that kind?"

The question disoriented him visibly. "I — yes. Once. A clerk spelled it *Havvel*. My father made him correct it."

"And did it matter to you? That it be right?"

"It mattered to him," Havel said, and then heard what he'd said, and something moved behind his face — brief, quickly governed, but real. "It mattered to us."

"Yes," said Madame Ostrer. "That is the whole of the census, Lieutenant. Every name in these books is someone's father insisting it be written right. You are not cross-checking names against deserters. You are cross-checking fathers against the war." She turned the register toward him, open to household sixty-three, her thumb — deliberately now, with the full consent of her mind — covering the marginal note. "This man answered every question I put to him. Whether he answered truly is beyond my office. My office is the record. The record says what it says."

Havel looked at the page. Looked at her thumb. She held his gaze with the flat patience of twenty years of being underestimated by men in uniform, and after a moment he nodded, and drew the register back, and moved to household sixty-four.

Her thumb lifted from the margin. The pencil words remained: *hands wrong for trade.* Unsmudged. Uncircled. Simply — unremarked. She had discovered, in the space of one held breath, a third option that neither procedure nor conscience had offered her: not truth, not mercy, but delay. A fact left sitting in plain sight, unwitnessed. There were, she reflected, worse moral positions. Most of history was conducted from inside one of them.

* * *

They broke for bread at noon, brought by a guard who did not look at her, and worked again until the lamplight was needed. By late afternoon they had crossed into the outlying households — the mill road, the tannery lane, the farms strung along the river toward the provisional line — and it was there, copying her own entry for the Vess holding, that the day turned.

Household eighty-nine: *Vess, Marta, widow; Vess, Lena, daughter; Vess, Ilya, son — absent, military.* She had recorded that family years ago, back when the border was a rumor and Ilya Vess was just another farm boy gone to garrison, and she had amended the entry the following spring in her own hand: *son — absent, enemy service,* because the maps had gone mad and the garrison he served now flew the other flag. She remembered the mother's face when she'd come to re-verify the household — not grief, exactly, but the particular stony patience of women who lived within sight of a line that might move over them like weather.

Now Havel laid a new map on the table. Fresh printing, ink still sharp. The provisional line, redrawn.

"Effective the first of the month," he said, mostly to himself, squaring the sheet. "The commission adjusted the eastern districts. Millward stays with us. But the river parishes—" his finger traced a boundary that had jumped, casually, catastrophically, eastward, "—go to the other delegation. Their census becomes their problem."

Ostrer looked at the new line. It ran south of the Vess farm. She knew the farm's position on the parish map the way she knew her own kitchen — she had walked that road twice a year for a decade, counting chickens and children and woodpiles. The house that had been in her district yesterday was in a foreign country today. Marta Vess, who had answered her questions honestly for ten years, was now, on paper, an enemy national. And her son — her son was already an enemy officer, which meant the family was now doubly marked, doubly suspect, doubly *theirs*.

And a man in a boarding house in Millward — a bookbinder with a soldier's hands and a heavy lie — had asked her, six weeks ago, one question more than courtesy required. She had almost forgotten it until this moment, and remembering it now was like hearing a floorboard settle in an empty house. He had asked, idly, folding his gloves, whether the census tracked where people's *kin* lived, or only where the counted person slept. She had answered: only where they sleep. And he had nodded, and thanked her, and something in his face had been doing arithmetic.

She copied the Vess entry onto the summary sheet in ink, exactly as it stood, and added the amendment regarding the son, and said nothing about the map. Havel was already rolling it up. He had redrawn a family out of their country with the flick of a printed line and had not once looked at the names beneath it.

"Tomorrow we do the mill road registers," he said, gathering his folio. "Same hour."

"Lieutenant." She stood, stiff from the long sitting, and buttoned her coat. "A procedural question. When the transcription is complete — the originals. Are they returned to the district archive?"

"They're retained for the duration of verification."

"Which is?"

"Until the armistice is signed." He paused at the door, and for a moment the young man behind the rank showed through, tired, cold, wanting the war to be paperwork and finding it was not. "Or until it isn't."

He left. The lamps hissed. Outside, the drill yard was empty and dark, and the first snow of the week was beginning to come down through the lamplight, soft as erasure.

Madame Ostrer stood alone in the requisitioned room with her satchel of surrendered books and understood, with the clarity of a woman who has spent her life making marks, that she had just made the most important mark of her career: a blank. She had left a line unwitnessed, a name unexamined, a mercy delivered in the grammar of bureaucracy, deniable, invisible, perfect. A pencil, she thought, gathering her coat around her, can be a weapon or a mercy, and tonight it had been neither — tonight it had been a promise, unsigned, to a stranger.

She locked the room behind her, as instructed, and walked home through the falling snow past the darkened bindery on Cooper Lane, where a single candle still burned in an upstairs window, and where — she did not let herself look directly, she was too good a census taker for that — a light was moving, patient and deliberate, the light of a man sewing something into the lining of a coat.
