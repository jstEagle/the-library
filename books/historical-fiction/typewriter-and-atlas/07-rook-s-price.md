## 7. Rook's Price

The cellar smelled of glue and tallow and the particular sourness of a man who had slept in his coat for years. Rook sat across from her on an upturned crate, turning one of the stolen plates over in his hands as though it were a coin he was deciding whether to bite.

"So," he said. "We settle what you owe me."

The baby slept against Oscar's chest, wrapped in the bookbinder's ruined apron, her breath a small warm metronome. Oscar had learned in three days of flight to count that breath the way she once counted em-quads: *in, out, in, out* — the only measure left in her life that had not been seized or burned.

"You pulled me past the Salt Gate," she said. "One lie, well told. I'll pay for the lie."

"One lie." Rook laughed without much sound in it. He was a narrow man, all knuckle and tendon, with a face like type that had been set crooked and never corrected. "That guard would have opened your bundle if I hadn't told him the hymnals were bound for the garrison chapel. That's not a lie, girl. That's a whole psalm."

"Then name your price for the psalm."

He set the plate down between them on the packing crate, face up. Even in the guttering lamplight she could read it — her father's hand in every counter and serif, the letter *T* of *TRUTH* cut with that slight forward lean he used when he wanted a word to seem to be walking toward you. Twenty years of sorting type by feel in the dark, and still her fingers itched to touch it.

"These plates," Rook said, "are worth more than this cellar, more than this street, maybe more than this city by the time the ice goes. The censors are offering a house on the canal for information leading to them. A *house*, Dravenmoor. I've slept in doorways that had better manners than me, but I know what a house is worth."

"I don't have a house."

"No. You've got a father's secrets and a dead woman's child and a face the Salt Gate guard will remember till his dying day because I lied so beautifully for you." He leaned forward. "So we trade in something else. What do you have?"

Oscar looked at him for a long moment. In the print shop she had perfected the useful silence — the silence that made customers lower their voices, that made her father explain himself without being asked, that let her stand unseen at the edge of every conversation like a piece of furniture no one thought to move. She reached for it now.

And found, to her astonishment, that it did not work. Rook simply waited, unbothered, picking at a scab on his knuckle, as comfortable with quiet as she was. Silence, she realized, was only a tool against people who feared it. This man had lived inside it too long to be afraid.

So she did the thing that twenty years of training forbade. She spoke first.

"You can't read."

His hand stopped mid-pick. For the first time since the Salt Gate, Rook looked genuinely off balance. "What did you say?"

"The crates you smuggle. I watched you load them at the gate. You hold each book by its spine and look at the cover for exactly as long as a man looks when he's memorizing shapes, not reading words. You knew my hymnals were hymnals because of the cross stamped on the boards, not the title." She kept her voice level, gentle even, though gentleness felt like a blade turned wrong way round. "You move the most dangerous literature in Vessengard, and you can't read a word of it."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, the bookbinder's cat walked along a shelf, dislodging dust that fell through the light like slow snow.

"That's a cruel thing," Rook said quietly, "to say to the man keeping you alive."

"It's also the only thing I have to sell." She shifted the baby higher against her shoulder. "You want the value of the plates. I can't give you that. But I can give you something the censors can't put a price on, because they'd burn it if they understood it. I can teach you to read the very type you carry. Every crate you smuggle blind, you could open with your eyes. Every broadsheet, every pamphlet, every forbidden book — you'd know which ones are worth dying for and which ones are kindling. No one in this city can offer you that. No one else in this city *is* that."

"And why would you teach me? Out of kindness?" His mouth twisted. "You don't strike me as a kind woman, Dravenmoor. You strike me as a careful one."

"Because a man who can read won't need to sell me," she said. "A man who can read will understand what these plates cost to make. And because—" she hesitated, and heard her father's voice in the hesitation, *never tell them what you want twice*— "because someday someone will hand you a page and ask you what it says, and I want you to be able to say it aloud. That's all printing is, in the end. Someone saying it aloud so it can't be unsaid."

Rook stared at her. Then, slowly, he began to laugh — a real laugh this time, low and rusted, like a press being worked after years idle.

"A house on the canal," he said, shaking his head. "And here you are offering me my letters like it's gold." He spat into the straw. "Done. Lessons start tonight. First word I learn is going to be your name, so I know what to shout when the censors come through that door."

"They're coming?"

"They're coming everywhere, girl. They came asking at the bookbinder's front room yesterday while you were sleeping. Old Hendrik told them he rents his cellar to a wine merchant." Rook's grin faded. "Wine merchants don't usually smell of ink. We should assume we're living on borrowed hours."

Oscar looked down at the plate between them, at her father's leaning, walking *T*. Guilt rose in her like floodwater. She had just traded away the thing her father had guarded longest — not the plates themselves but the craft, the secret grammar of how a Dravenmoor taught, how a Dravenmoor read the world. She had spent twenty years learning which questions never to ask, and in one night she had answered every question a stranger put to her, and worse, had given him the means to ask more.

*I'm sorry,* she said silently, to the man who might already be in chains. *I'm spending your silences to keep her breathing.*

The baby stirred, sighed, and gripped Oscar's finger with a hand no bigger than a piece of nonpareil type. Whatever she had traded, she would trade again. That was the arithmetic of survival, and it never balanced.

"Tonight, then," she said aloud. "Bring me any book you like. We'll start with the alphabet."

Rook stood, stretched until his joints cracked like green wood, and paused at the foot of the stairs.

"For what it's worth," he said, not looking at her, "the mother of that child — the pamphleteer they hanged on Wren's Steps — she taught letters too. Down by the tanneries, free, to anyone who came. Half the dockhands can read because of her." He glanced back, and his crooked face was almost soft. "She never charged either. Maybe that's where you get it."

Then he was gone up the stairs, and Oscar sat alone with the lamp and the sleeping child and the terrible, tender weight of being compared to a dead woman she had never met.

Ilse Varn found the foundry mark at half past nine in the evening, by candlelight, with her sleeves rolled and her fingers black to the second knuckle.

It was a small thing — they always were. A tiny monogram pressed into the margin of the banned broadsheets: a *D* entwined with a stylus, struck slightly off-center, the way a die wears after ten thousand impressions. She had catalogued forty-one examples of it over six years, pulled from confiscated sheets, and every catalogue entry ended the same way: *source unknown; suspected Dravenmoor foundry, unconfirmed.*

Unconfirmed no longer. Because tonight, spread across her desk in the Censorate's back offices, were the broadsheets from the abandoned infant's wrappings — and beside them, taken from the raid on a smuggler's cache last spring, a printer's proof bearing the same monogram, same wear, same off-center strike. And on the proof's verso, in pencil so faint it was nearly a confession rather than a note: *O.D., corrected 3rd pass.*

Old Dravenmoor cut his own corrections. Everyone in the trade knew that. But Ilse had spent six years reading other people's marginalia, and she knew a second hand when she saw one. The *3* was written differently from the rest — rounder, surer, the hand of someone young who had learned numbers before letters, the way apprentices learned weights before words.

There were two Dravenmoors, then. Or there had been.

"He's lying," said Captain Brenner behind her, meaning the old man, who sat even now in a cell two floors below, silent as a closed book. "Twenty years in that shop and he claims he never printed a seditious line. Claims the plates were sold south years ago."

"People lie best about the things they love," Ilse said. She held the pencil notation close to the flame. *O.D.* Not the father's initials. The daughter's — if the daughter existed, if the daughter had spent her life standing in that shop like a piece of furniture no one thought to move.

"Varn." Brenner's voice changed, went flat and official. "The order's signed. At dawn we take the shop apart plank by plank. If the girl exists, we find her. If the plates exist, we find those. And if the child exists—" He paused, delicately. "Well. The Crown prefers no loose threads."

Ilse nodded and said nothing, which was its own kind of skill. But when Brenner had gone, she sat a long while looking at the infant's wrapping, at the broadsheet lining it, at the small desperate tenderness of a hanged woman choosing, as her last act, to swaddle her child in the truth.

At dawn, she thought. The ice would begin to sing before dawn. She had grown up on this river; she knew the sound it made the night before it broke, a long groaning exhalation across the whole city, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

She hoped, in a place she did not examine too closely, that whatever was hidden in the Dravenmoor cellar would be gone by then.

She hoped it, and she picked up her lantern anyway, and went down toward the cells to ask the old man one last question — the one about the second hand, the younger hand, the hand that wrote *corrected* as if correction were a form of love.
