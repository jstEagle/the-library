## 10. A Face Worth Remembering

The news came on a morning that smelled of thaw and woodsmoke, carried up the stairs of the print shop by a boy who had once run from censors and now ran errands for no one in particular because there was no one left to forbid him.

"Dravenmoor," he said, standing in the doorway with his cap wrung in his fists. "They opened the cells this morning. The old man—" He stopped, swallowed. "He didn't last to see it."

Oscar was at the case, sorting type by feel, her fingers moving through the letters like a hand moving through water. She did not stop. She finished the line she was setting — *the truth requires no license* — and then she set down the composing stick, very carefully, as if it were something sleeping.

"How," she said.

"The fever. They say he wouldn't give them a name. Not one. Three weeks of questions and he gave them nothing but the alphabet." The boy's voice cracked on the last word, and Oscar understood that he had loved her father too, in the way everyone who had ever watched him cut a letter had loved him: from a distance, reverently, without ever being permitted closer.

She thanked the boy. She heard her own voice do it, steady and low, and marveled at it. Twenty years she had spent perfecting the art of not speaking, and now words came out of her like water from a cracked dam, and the world did not end.

Her father had died in a cell that smelled of straw and rust, and his last act on earth had been silence — but it was not *her* silence, not the silence of a woman swallowing her own name. It was the silence of a locked door held shut while someone ran through it. She could live inside that difference. She would have to.

* * *

They buried him on the fourth day, in the printers' yard behind the ruined chapel where three generations of Vessengard's type-cutters lay under stones worn smooth by rain. The revolution had swept through the city two weeks before, and the streets still bore its signatures: burned barricades, whitewashed walls suddenly crowded with broadsheets, soldiers' buttons glinting in the gutters where they'd been torn loose. The river ran open now, brown and loud, carrying ice south in broken plates that knocked against the pilings like slow applause.

Oscar stood at the graveside with the child on her hip. The baby — no longer quite an infant, rounder now, heavier, her eyes gone from river-gray to a deep determined brown — wore a bonnet Ilse Varn had sent, of all people, wrapped in plain paper with no note. Oscar had stared at the parcel for an hour before opening it. Some debts, she understood, were paid in small soft things.

Rook came to the burial smelling of salt and pipe smoke, stood at the back with his cap off, which she had never seen, and left before the last prayer was done. That was Rook's way of weeping, if he wept at all. He had come twice since the crossing, both times to collect on their arrangement — reading lessons, traded for the quiet movement of certain crates — and both times he had sat across from her at the workbench with his enormous smuggler's hands hovering over a primer page as though the letters might bite. He learned slowly. He learned furiously. Last week he had read aloud, haltingly, the first paragraph of the first free pamphlet printed in Vessengard, and when he finished he had looked up at her with an expression she recognized from her own mirror: the look of a person discovering a room inside themselves they had never been allowed to enter.

"He'd have hated the sermon," Rook had said afterward, jerking his chin toward the chapel. "Too many words. Not enough of 'em true."

At the graveside now, Oscar laid her palm flat on the cold stone and felt beneath it, imagined beneath it, the texture of her father's hands: the burn scar on the left thumb from the casting pot, the ink that lived so deep in the creases no lye had ever lifted it. *You taught me every letter except my own,* she thought. *So I have cut it myself.*

The baby reached down and patted the stone, once, twice, the way she patted everything — approving, claiming. Oscar laughed, or made a sound near enough to laughing that the mourners turned, and she let them see her face while she did it.

* * *

The shop reopened on a Tuesday.

There was no ceremony. There was a broom, and a bucket, and the long labor of washing the smell of soldiers out of the front room, and the careful inventory of what the raid had taken and what, miraculously, remained. The old press stood where it had always stood, squat and iron-ribbed, missing one screw the censors had pocketed out of spite. Rook found her a replacement within a day, from a source he declined to name, and refused payment beyond a second reading lesson.

Word spread the way word does in a city of printers: quietly, then all at once. By the second week there were men at her door wanting pamphlets set. By the third, a delegation from the new civic council, asking — asking, not ordering — whether Dravenmoor's would take the commission for the city's first free gazette.

Oscar stood in the doorway with sawdust on her apron and the baby asleep against her shoulder, and she said yes. Then she said something else, something she had rehearsed in the dark of the bookbinder's cellar while cutting her first letter by candlelight, and saying it out loud felt like setting a bone that had healed wrong.

"One condition. My name goes on it. Not his. Mine."

The councilman blinked. "Dravenmoor is your name."

"It was his shop. His craft. His silences." She shifted the child gently. "I'm Oscar Dravenmoor, and I set the type myself, and I sign it myself. That's the price."

He agreed, of course. Everyone agreed, these days; agreement was cheap and everywhere, like spring mud. But the saying of it mattered. The saying of it was the whole inheritance, finally collected.

* * *

She worked through the nights, because the days belonged to the child and the child did not believe in night.

She set the gazette's first edition the way her father had taught her — stick in the left hand, case before her, the letters coming to her fingers by touch and memory — and then she broke from his teaching in the only place that mattered. At the foot of the front page, below the last column, she set a line no censor would ever have permitted and no apprentice would ever have dared:

*Set and signed: Oscar Dravenmoor, Vessengard.*

Two words more than any printer in the city's history had put beneath his work. When the first proof came off the press, damp and gray and trembling slightly in her hands, she held it up to the lamp for a long time. The letters were clean. The letters were hers. Somewhere under the floorboards, in the tin box where she kept the plate she had saved from the fire — the one bearing the hanged pamphleteer's furious, beautiful hand — the past kept its own counsel.

She pulled five hundred copies that day. The press sang. Ink got into everything: the cracks of her knuckles, the grain of the floor, the hem of the baby's blanket where she slept in a basket beside the stone. By evening the whole shop smelled the way it had smelled in her childhood, and Oscar stood in the middle of it and breathed until her chest hurt.

* * *

But a signature, she had decided, was not enough. A signature borrowed the alphabet; it did not make one.

On the last night of the month, after the child slept and the lamps were trimmed low, Oscar took a blank piece of type-metal to the bench and began to cut.

She had designed the face in her head for weeks without admitting it: a letterform built for broadsheets and proclamations, sturdy in the stem, generous in the counter, with serifs that flared just slightly outward — like a door opening, she thought, like a mouth about to speak. Her father's faces had been beautiful the way fortresses are beautiful: defensible, closed, every curve calculated to survive a censor's calipers. Hers would be beautiful the way a shout is beautiful. Legible at a distance. Impossible to pretend you hadn't read.

She worked the graver the way she sorted type, by feel, by dark, her eyes half-closed, trusting the twenty years of apprenticeship that lived in her wrist. Metal curled away in bright threads. The letter emerged — a capital, the first letter of a name.

Not hers.

When it was done she held it to the lamp, turning it, and the little face of metal threw light around the room like a coin. Then she inked it, pressed it to scrap paper, and stamped it once, twice, three times along the bottom margin of a blank sheet, and looked at what she had made.

*Wren. Wren. Wren.*

She named the typeface for the child whose mother had wrapped her in sedition and asked the world to forget her face. The world would not forget. Oscar would see to it personally, one printed page at a time, for as long as her hands held out. Every proclamation, every ballad sheet, every dangerous truth that came off the Dravenmoor press would walk out into the city wearing those letters — wearing her granddaughter's name — and no magistrate, no minister, no army would ever be able to unname her again.

The baby woke at dawn, as she always did, and Oscar lifted her from the basket and carried her to the window, where the river showed between the rooftops, running free of ice at last.

"There," Oscar told her, and pointed at the sheet drying on the press, at the three small stamped names shining in the lamplight. "That's you. That's going to be on everything."

Wren grabbed her ink-stained finger and held it with the full serious strength of a person who has recently discovered the world can be gripped, and Oscar laughed out loud — loudly, unguardedly, the way her father never had — and the sound went up through the rafters of the old shop and out the open door into the waking street, where the presses of Vessengard were beginning, one by one, to speak.
