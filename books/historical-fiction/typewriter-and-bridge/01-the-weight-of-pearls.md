## 1. The Weight of Pearls

The scales at the Meridian Trading Company's weighing station had been calibrated in the spring, before the rains, and Noor Galloway trusted them the way he trusted few things in Kessarin — which is to say, not at all, but more than he trusted anything else.

He stood at the long cedar table with the brass weights fanned out before him like a fortune-teller's cards, and one by one the divers' hauls came across the dock to him in their oiled canvas bags. Each bag had a name chalked on it in the diver's own hand, or in a brother's hand, or in no hand at all when the man who'd carried it up from the beds was the sort who'd never held a pencil. Noor wrote the weights in his own book first — a cheap bound thing with a cracked spine that he kept in his shirt, against his ribs — and then he entered them again in the company ledger with its ruled columns and its watermarked pages, and the two numbers were always the same, because the scale did not lie and Noor did not either.

It was the third column that troubled him. The third column was for the divers themselves.

The morning had come in gray and breathing, the way monsoon mornings did, the harbor fog lying on the water like a second sea. Beyond the breakwater the pearl beds waited under nine fathoms, and beyond the beds the seasonal bridge stretched off toward the mainland, its timbers dark with old rain, its chains already hung with the first of the season's warning flags. The bridge was the town's one artery. When the monsoon closed it — and it would close soon, the gulls were flying inland already, which was as reliable a forecast as any clerk's almanac — Kessarin would become an island of mud and company property, sealed until the ice came and the road froze hard enough to bear a sledge.

Noor had lived through eleven closures. He knew the arithmetic of them by heart. Four months, give or take, of everyone in the compound breathing the same wet air: the clerks, the divers, the divers' widows and children, the resident director in his raised house that never flooded, and Noor himself, nineteen years old, keeper of the ledgers, son of a dead man, debtor to a company that owned the air he'd been breathing since he was nine.

"Bag," said the diver in front of him, setting down his canvas sack with the tenderness of a man setting down a sleeping child.

Noor weighed it. Fourteen ounces of shell and nacre, small pearls, the kind the beds gave up in a lean year's first haul. He wrote the weight twice, chalked the diver's tally on the board behind him, and looked up to mark the face.

That was the habit. He'd had it since he was small, since before he could say why. Every diver who went down, he memorized. Every diver who came up. He kept them in his head the way other men kept songs — faces, names, the way a man's shoulders sat after a season's diving, the cough a man developed in his third year, the particular gray that crept into the lips of a man who'd gone down too often too fast. His father had taught him to count the boats out and count the boats in, standing on this same dock with his hand heavy on Noor's neck, saying: *A number is only honest if somebody remembers what it stands for, boy. Paper forgets. Paper forgets anything you tell it to.*

His father had been wrong about that, in the end. Paper didn't forget. Paper remembered exactly what it was told, and nothing else, and that was the whole trouble.

Noor counted the boats now, out of habit, out of grief, out of whatever the thing was that lived in his chest and had lived there since the day the company's boat came back without his father. Eleven boats had gone out to the beds at first light. Eleven boats rode at their moorings now, riding the small gray swell, their dive flags dripping.

Forty-one divers had gone down this morning. He had stood at the rail and counted them into the water the way he counted everything — by what could be carried, by what could be held in two hands, by what could be kept. Forty-one men over the sides in their canvas suits and their stone weights, forty-one breaths held, forty-one lives let down into the cold green dark on ropes.

Thirty-eight had come up.

He had watched for the three. He always watched. The first two had surfaced late, hauled up half-drowned with the bends already whistling in their joints, and they were down in the divers' quarters now, packed in wet blankets, alive — he'd checked, he'd walked past the quarters on his way to the station and heard them coughing, which was a sound he catalogued with the rest. The third —

The third was the trouble.

"Bag," said the next man, and Noor weighed, and wrote, and looked up, and marked the face, and the ledger's third column waited for him like an open mouth.

The manifest for the day's dive sat at his elbow, delivered from the office an hour ago in Mr. Voss's tidy hand. Voss wrote a beautiful hand, upright and even, the letters of a man who had never once been in a hurry to be honest. The manifest listed the day's divers: forty-one names. And beside the names, in the column headed *Returned*, the number thirty-nine.

Thirty-nine.

Noor set down the brass weight he was holding and looked at the number for a long moment, the fog pressing against the station windows, the gulls crying somewhere inland where they shouldn't be.

Thirty-eight had come up. He had counted them himself, one face at a time, the way his father had taught him. He had counted them the way he counted everything, because his father had taught him that too: *measure by what can be carried.* A man could carry a bag of pearls. A man could carry a name. A man could carry, if he was careful, if he was disciplined, the memory of forty-one men going down and thirty-eight coming up, and the difference between those numbers was a thing that weighed more than all the pearls in the harbor.

The manifest said thirty-nine.

It was possible he was wrong. He made himself say that, made himself give the thought its fair hearing, because a clerk who couldn't doubt his own count was no better than a clerk who lied. It was possible he'd miscounted at the rail in the gray light, possible he'd counted a man twice or missed one in the crowd of dripping canvas and rope. Possible. But he had counted the faces at the rail and he had counted the faces at the mooring, and between those two counts he had walked the dock twice, and the number that lived in him was thirty-eight, and the number that lived in him had never once been wrong. Not in eleven years. Not since the day he'd stood on this dock at nine years old and counted the boats out and counted the boats in and understood, with the terrible clarity of children, that the boat that came back without his father was still counted as *returned*.

"Bag," said the next diver, and Noor started, and weighed, and wrote.

The morning went on. The bags came across the table, fourteen ounces, nineteen, eleven, a good haul of twenty-six from the boy Rassim on his first season, and Noor entered each weight in two books and chalked each tally on the board and memorized each face, and all the while the manifest lay at his elbow with its thirty-nine, patient as a stone.

By noon the weighing was done. The pearls went into the company's strongbox under the escort of two men with sticks, and the divers went off to their quarters and their rice, and the fog began to lift off the harbor in rags, and Noor sat alone at the cedar table with his two books and Voss's manifest, and did the thing he always did when a number would not sit still in him: he carried it.

He carried it out of the station, along the dock, past the boat sheds where the widows' mending was hung out to dry in the brief pale sun — Sefora's husband's old coat among them, still on its peg after two years, because Sefora did not believe in throwing away what a man had touched — and up the company road to the counting house, where the ledgers lived.

The archive was at the back of the counting house, behind the clerks' room, down a corridor that smelled of paper and lamp oil and the particular dust of things nobody was meant to open. Noor knew the smell well. He had spent half his life in that corridor. But he had never gone past the door at its end, the one marked with the faded stencil *ARCHIVE*, because the archive belonged to Betsabeh, and Betsabeh was the company's oldest servant and its least questioned, and because a junior clerk did not open the archive without cause.

He had cause now. He told himself it was a small cause, a clerk's cause — a discrepancy of one, a miscount to be checked against the office copies — and he knocked, and no one answered, and the door was not locked, and he went in.

The archive was dim and enormous. Racks of ledgers rose to the ceiling, forty years of the company's seasons bound in oilcloth, and at the center of it all, at a desk lit by one green lamp, sat a small old woman with clouded eyes, mending a book's broken spine with thread and beeswax, her hands moving with the certainty of someone who did not need light to know her work.

She did not look up.

"You're the Galloway boy," she said. "The one who counts."

Noor's mouth went dry. "I — the manifest, ma'am. There's a discrepancy in the day's dive. I wanted to check it against the office copies."

"Discrepancy." Betsabeh said the word the way a woman might say *rain*, or *rust* — the name of an old familiar weather. She set down her needle and turned her clouded eyes toward him, and though he was not at all sure they could see him, he felt seen more thoroughly than he had ever felt in his life. "How many years have you kept the ledgers, boy?"

"Six, ma'am. Since I was thirteen."

"And in six years, how many discrepancies?"

"None, ma'am."

"None," she repeated softly. "And now one." She rose, small and straight-backed, and crossed to a rack of ledgers with the sureness of a woman who could have found any book in that room blindfolded, because in every way that mattered, she was. She drew down a volume, blew the dust from its cover, and set it on the desk between them. "The office copies live in the clerks' room. The originals live here. If you want to check a count, boy, you check it against the original. That is the whole of the archive's purpose, and the reason so few people are invited to remember it."

Noor looked at the ledger. His hands, which had weighed forty-one bags that morning without a tremor, were not steady now.

"Which dive?" Betsabeh asked.

"Today's. The — the first of the season. But it wouldn't be — the original wouldn't be bound yet, ma'am, the season's barely —"

"Today's," she said again, and there was something in her voice that made him stop. She turned to the rack behind her and drew down a slim folder — not a bound ledger at all but the season's working file, the carbons and manifests of the first weeks, and she laid it open on the desk under the green lamp and turned her face toward him. "Read me the third column."

Noor's throat worked. "Returned. It says — the manifest says thirty-nine."

"Read me the carbon," she said. "The office carbon, the one Voss's clerk made this morning when the boats came in."

He found it. The carbon copy, thin as onion skin, the figures faint. He held it to the lamp and read, and the word came out of him smaller than he meant it to.

"Thirty-eight."

The archive was very quiet. Somewhere above them, rain began — the first of the season, soft on the counting house roof, the sound Kessarin would live under for four months.

"Thirty-eight," Betsabeh said. "And the manifest says thirty-nine. And which one goes to the office, boy? Which one goes into the record?"

"The manifest," Noor said. "The carbon stays in the file."

"Yes." She closed the folder gently, the way one closes a hand over something living. "Forty years I have kept this room. Forty years I have watched the carbons and the manifests disagree, and every year the disagreement is one man, or two, or three, and every year the manifest is the one that travels over the ice to the mainland, and the carbon is the one that stays in the dark with me." She sat down again, and her clouded eyes found him, or found the place where he stood. "You counted the boats out and the boats in today. I could see it in your face when you came through my door. Your father counted the same way. He used to stand at my desk, when he was your age, and say that a number is only honest if somebody remembers what it stands for."

The rain thickened. Noor stood in the green lamplight with the discrepancy of one man sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone, and he thought of his father's name in the company's books — *lost at sea, account closed* — and of the third column, and of the forty years of dark this small blind woman had kept watch over, alone, with a needle and thread and a lamp.

"Who was it today?" Betsabeh asked quietly. "Who didn't come up?"

"Old Mahir," Noor said. "The one with the bad shoulder. He went down at the far bed and his line went slack and they — they grappled for him, but the current —" He stopped. Old Mahir, who had taught half the dock to hold their breath. Old Mahir, whose face he had memorized that morning at the rail, gray-bearded, squinting into the fog.

"Old Mahir," Betsabeh repeated, and reached for a fresh sheet of carbon paper, and set it before Noor with the pen beside it. "Then write it down, boy. While the ink is wet and your count is true. Write it in your own book, and write it again in mine, and one day —" she paused, and the rain filled the pause, and when she spoke again her voice was as even as a ruled line — "one day someone will ask what happened to Old Mahir, and there will be two answers in this town. The manifest's answer, and ours."

Noor took up the pen. His hand was steady now, steadier than it had been all day, and he understood, in the way one understands a thing all at once and forever, that he had just crossed from one side of the ledgers to the other — that there had been, all along, two kinds of clerks in Kessarin, and that the space between them was exactly the width of one man's name.

He wrote: *First dive of the season. Forty-one down. Thirty-eight up. Mahir, son of Darius, did not return. Witnessed and counted by Noor Galloway, keeper of the ledgers.*

Outside, the rain came down harder, and far off toward the bridge he heard the harbor bell begin to ring — the long, slow tolling that meant the officials were gathering, that meant the flags were going up, that meant the season was closing early and the compound was sealing its doors.

The bridge was closing. And Noor Galloway had just written his first honest line in a room full of forty years of lies.
