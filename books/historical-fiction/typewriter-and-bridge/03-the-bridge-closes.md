## 3. The Bridge Closes

The wind changed on a Tuesday, though Noor Galloway did not know it was Tuesday until Voss mentioned it at the morning tally, and by then the wind had already made its argument better than any calendar could. It came down off the highlands smelling of iron and drowned vegetation, the monsoon's advance guard, and it bent the palms along the compound wall until they lay like penitents. The harbor boats came in early. The divers came in wetter.

Noor stood at the weighing table under the lean-to, the canvas flapping above him like a thing trying to escape, and weighed the last baskets of the day while the sky went the color of a bruise. He had learned long ago to keep his count in his head the way other men kept a knife in their boot — not for show, but because a day might come when he would need it and there would be no time to go looking.

Fourteen went down this morning. Eleven came up.

He did not write that anywhere. He wrote what the manifest required: fourteen down, fourteen up, harvest by basket, weight by weight. But in the ledger behind his eyes, the one no clerk could seize or burn, he kept the other column, the true one, and it grew longer every week the way a debt grows, silently and with interest.

The wind gusted and took a tally sheet off the table. He caught it two steps away, pressed it flat, and looked up to find the last diver of the day watching him from the edge of the lean-to — a young man named Oren, one of the season's new hands, his lips blue from the cold water, his hands shaking around a cup of tea he hadn't drunk.

"They're saying the bridge closes tomorrow," Oren said. "Early. The ferryman told my cousin the chains are already loaded on the barge."

"The bridge closes when the officials say it closes," Noor said, which was the company answer, and he hated himself a little for the smoothness of it.

"And after that?"

After that, Noor thought, we are all of us sealed inside a bottle with the men who write our deaths down as desertions. But he said, "After that, it's the season. Same as every season. You dive, you get paid, the bridge opens in spring."

Oren looked at him the way a man looks at a wall he suspects is load-bearing — as if one good push would bring the whole structure down and reveal what it was holding up. Then he drank his tea and went out into the wind, and Noor watched him go and thought: eleven of fourteen. Eleven of fourteen. Eleven of fourteen.

He had begun, without deciding to, to think in threes.

* * *

The chains went on at dawn.

Noor heard them before he saw them — the groan of the winch barge, the clank of iron on iron carrying flat across the gray water — and he climbed to the compound's upper walkway, where the drying racks for the pearl nets stood empty in the rain, and watched the officials do their work. There were six of them in oilskins, moving with the unhurried competence of men performing a ritual they had performed many times. The bridge was the only link between Kessarin and the mainland road: a span of black timber and cable, two hundred years old, that swayed in the storms like a man walking home drunk. Every monsoon it closed. Every spring it opened. Between those two facts, an entire town's life was contained, the way a ledger's two covers contained everything a company wished to be true.

The closure notice went up on the customs post at nine. Noor was sent to copy it into the company's daybook, which meant he stood in the rain reading it three times over.

BY ORDER OF THE HARBOUR COMMISSION, the notice read, THE KESSARIN SPAN IS CLOSED TO ALL TRAFFIC UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE. NO VESSEL SHALL CROSS. NO PERSON SHALL PASS. THE SEASON IS DECLARED.

*The season is declared.* Four words that meant, in the language of the port: you are where you are, and you will be there for six months. The compound would hold — Noor had walked its perimeter enough times to know it — forty-one clerks and staff in the main house and offices, and beyond the stockade wall, in the divers' quarter, some two hundred souls: divers, wives, children, widows, and the old men who had dove until the water took their hearing and left them nothing but their stories.

Two hundred people. One gate. And on the company's side of that gate, a set of books in which Noor had already found one number that could not be made honest.

He copied the notice in his best hand, and his best hand, he noticed, was not as steady as it had been in September.

* * *

Voss called the staff together that afternoon in the counting room, and Noor stood at the back with the other junior clerks while the head clerk laid out the season like a man laying out a corpse for viewing.

"Rain season protocols," Voss said. He was a dry man, Voss, dry as a dock receipt — a narrow face, narrow hands, a voice that never rose because it never needed to. "The bridge is closed. Nothing comes in, nothing goes out. That includes letters, and I will hear no appeals on the subject. The mail barge made its last run this morning. Anything you failed to send is your own affair."

Noor thought of the letter he had not written to his mother's sister on the mainland — the one he had drafted four times and sent none of, because every version began *I have found something in the ledgers* and every version ended with him imagining Voss reading it over some customs man's shoulder.

"Rations are calculated for the full complement," Voss continued. "The divers' quarter draws from the company store on the schedule posted at the gate. Wages are suspended during closure — standard practice — and will be settled at season's end." He paused, and his eyes, pale as weak tea, moved across the room and rested on Noor for exactly one second longer than they rested on anyone else. "The manifests for the final week are to be reconciled before Friday. Galloway. That is your desk."

"Yes, Mr. Voss."

"Reconciled," Voss repeated, as though the word had not landed the first time, "means agreeing. It means the count in the book matches the count in the world. I trust you understand the difference between a book that agrees and a clerk who does not."

Someone laughed — a junior man named Pell, nervous, eager to be on the right side of any joke in the room. Voss did not smile. Voss never smiled; Noor had once spent an entire evening trying to remember whether he had ever seen it, and concluded that the man's face had been built without the mechanism.

"Dismissed," Voss said, and the room emptied, and Noor walked back to his desk in the rain-dark afternoon and sat before the final week's manifests with his pen uncapped and did not write for a long time.

Reconciled means agreeing. He looked at the manifest for the day his father's crew had gone out — the day that lived now in the back room of his memory like a tenant who paid no rent — and he read the count again: fourteen down, fourteen up. And he thought of the harbor that morning, the boats coming back light, the wives standing at the seawall counting heads with their lips moving, and coming up one short, and the company clerk — Voss's predecessor, but the same company, the same pen — writing *up: fourteen* in a hand so neat it might have been engraved.

The count in the book did not match the count in the world. It had not matched for years. And now the bridge was chained, the gate was shut, the rain was coming down like the sky meant to drown the land on principle — and Noor was sealed inside with the books, and the books were sealed inside with him.

He picked up his pen. He wrote *fourteen* where the manifest said fourteen, because he was not yet ready to die for a number, and then, beneath the desk's lip, where no one had ever looked and no one would ever think to look, he cut a small mark with his thumbnail into the wood. One stroke. The first entry in a ledger made of nothing but will.

* * *

Sefora came to the pay window on the third day of the rain, and she did not come alone.

Noor saw the crowd gathering from the counting room window — a knot of perhaps thirty from the divers' quarter, standing in the downpour without umbrellas because umbrellas were a thing that happened to other people — and he saw Sefora at the front of it, and his stomach went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

She was the widow of Daneh Maral, and Daneh Maral had gone down on the last day of October and had not come up, and the manifest said he had.

She reached the pay window — a barred opening in the compound's outer office wall, built for transactions, not conversations — and she put both hands flat on the sill, and the rain ran off her elbows in ropes.

"My husband's final wages," she said. Not loudly. That was the thing Noor would remember afterward, the thing that made his hands unsteady at his own desk ten feet away: she was not shouting. The crowd behind her was quiet too, quiet as a held breath. "The season's wages, owed to his account. The account is in his name. I am his widow. I have the marriage paper."

The pay clerk — a soft, frightened man named Hobb — looked past her to Noor, as if Noor were the flood and he the man without a boat. "The season isn't ended, mistress. Wages are settled at season's end. It's the rule."

"The rule," Sefora said. "The bridge is closed. The season cannot end until spring. My children have eaten rice for nine days. Rice and rainwater. And you are telling me that the money my husband bled for is sitting in a box on your side of that wall, and I cannot have it because of a rule."

"Company policy," Hobb said, and Noor heard the words die in the man's mouth even as he said them, heard him understand that he was a wall being asked to hold back the sea.

"Then I will speak to whoever makes the policy."

"The resident director doesn't receive—"

"Then the head clerk. Voss. I know his name. Everyone in the quarter knows his name; he is the name on every paper we sign and none of the papers we're given copies of."

Hobb looked at Noor again. Noor looked down at his ledger. He was a junior clerk; the window was not his; the words he wanted to say — *take it, take the whole box, it was his, he earned it with his lungs* — were the words of a man with no standing and no protection and a secret in his coat pocket, and he hated each of them individually.

Voss came. Of course Voss came; Noor heard the measured footsteps in the corridor before he saw the man, and when Voss appeared at the window, the rain seemed almost to quiet, the way a room quiets.

"Mistress Maral." Voss's voice through the bars was mild, almost kind, which was the most frightening register he owned. "The company is not without feeling. Your loss is noted in the record."

"Noted," Sefora said.

"An entry was made."

"An entry was made." She said it slowly, tasting it, and Noor's pen stopped moving entirely. "My husband is an *entry*. Fourteen men went down that day. Thirteen boats came back. I stood on that seawall and counted, Mr. Voss, the way I have counted every morning for eleven years, because a wife who doesn't count is a wife who is lied to. And your entry says fourteen."

The silence that followed was the loudest Noor had ever heard. The rain filled it, drumming on the office roof, on the crowd's bare heads, on the barred sill where Sefora's hands had not moved.

"The manifests are reconciled by sworn clerks," Voss said. "If you have evidence of a discrepancy, you are welcome to submit it in writing, and it will be reviewed at season's end."

"Spring," Sefora said. "You will review it in spring. After the bridge opens. After the historians come and the record is—" She stopped herself. Noor saw her stop herself, saw her decide, with a visible effort, not to say whatever she had been about to say in front of thirty witnesses and a barred window. "My husband's wages," she said instead. "Now. Or I will stand at this window every day until the wood wears through."

"You may stand where you like, mistress," Voss said. "This is company ground. Good day."

He turned and walked back down the corridor, and his footsteps were as measured as they had been coming, and Noor understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water filling his chest, that Voss was not afraid of her. That Voss had heard *fourteen* and *thirteen* and had filed the discrepancy away the way he filed everything — under *manageable*.

The crowd dispersed slowly, sullenly, back into the rain. Sefora was the last to go. Before she turned, she looked through the bars, past Hobb, directly at Noor — at the junior clerk with his pen frozen above a manifest — and her eyes were not angry. That was the worst of it. They were measuring. The way he measured pearls. The way he measured men against what they could carry.

She had counted thirteen. She had said it aloud, in front of the company's wall, with the company's head clerk standing on the other side of it.

And Voss had written nothing down.

* * *

That night Noor could not sleep. The rain had settled into its long monsoon rhythm, a sound like the whole world whispering through its teeth, and he lay in his narrow room above the counting room and listened to it and thought about the word *reconciled*.

The compound was sealed. He walked it in his mind the way a prisoner walks his cell: the stockade wall, the gate chained on the company side, the bridge chained at both ends, the harbor empty of everything but rain. Forty-one staff. Two hundred in the quarter. One pay window with bars. And somewhere in the main house, in the locked cabinets of the archive, the older ledgers — the ones from before his time, the ones with his father's season in them, the ones no one had asked to see in years because no one in Kessarin could read them anymore except—

He sat up in the dark.

Except Betsabeh.

The old archivist, half-blind, half-forgotten, who kept the company's dead paper in the back rooms of the main house the way a lighthouse keeper keeps a lamp no ships believe in anymore. Who had been here forty years. Who had been here, Noor realized, lying very still in the dark, for the season his father died.

The rain whispered. The compound breathed around him, two hundred and forty-one souls sealed in a bottle, and at the heart of it, behind three locked doors and forty years of dust, lay the only thing in Kessarin that might tell him whether his father's name was written in ink or in a lie.

Noor got up, dressed in the dark, and took the archive key from the hook in Voss's office where the spare hung — the spare Voss believed no one knew about, because Noor had dusted that hook every morning for three years and had never once let his eyes rest on it.

The corridor to the back rooms was black as the bottom of the sea. Noor had spent his life learning to measure what could be carried, and he carried nothing now but a lamp, a key, and a question.

The archive door was heavier than he remembered. It opened without a sound, which somehow was worse.

And from the darkness at the back of the room, where the oldest ledgers slept, a voice said: "You took the wrong key, boy. That one opens the cabinets. Mine opens the truth. Sit down, and mind the lamp."
