## 12. What Can Be Carried

Betsabeh had learned to read in the dark, which was fortunate, because the dark was where she now lived most of the time.

It was not a true darkness. Her left eye still caught shapes, still found the edge of a page if she held it at the angle her brother Farhad had once called *the reading tilt* — chin down, paper raised, lamplight slanting across the grain from the north window. But the right eye had gone out two winters ago like a lamp starved of oil, and the left was following, dimming by degrees so gentle and so merciless that some mornings she could not say whether the world had grown fainter or whether she had simply grown tired of looking at it.

She did not tell Noor this. He carried enough.

What she told him instead, on the evening she began the great copying, was a lie of the ordinary kind: that her eyes were no worse than last season, that the blurring was only the damp working into her old lenses, that Kessarin's monsoon fogged everything eventually, glass and grief alike. He nodded the way young people nod when they have decided an old woman is entitled to her small dignities, and he brought her the ledgers anyway, because they had both understood by then what the nights were for.

The archive room smelled as it always did — coal smoke and camphor, dust and the sweet rot of paper that has been loved too long by too few hands. Betsabeh had tended this room for thirty-one years. She knew its contents the way a midwife knows the children she has delivered: the 1849 customs registers with their water-stained corners; the payroll books bound in shark leather that Voss believed were valuable and were not; the shelf of damaged volumes marked for pulping, behind which, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the carbons that had already cost one man his name twice over — Noor's father, struck through, struck through again, erased from the world's arithmetic while his son weighed pearls ten feet above the water that took him.

Eleven names. She had watched Noor find them one by one in the ghost impressions, watched his finger tremble on the page the night he read his father's name aloud in a voice he tried to keep level and could not. She had put her hand over his then, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the ledger itself had not already said in its cold carbon ghost-script: *he was here, he went down, he did not come up, and someone decided that was inconvenient.*

Now, forty years after the same ledgers had swallowed her own brother, she sat down at the long table with a fresh quire of rice paper, a pot of iron-gall ink, and the intention of committing treason in her best hand.

* * *

The copying had to be perfect. That was the first law, and she taught it to herself again before she wrote a single character. A false record survives by being tidy; a true record survives by being exact. If her copy showed a smudged figure, a doubtful date, an abbreviation where the original spelled a thing out, then some clever lawyer in some clean office across the ice would find that flaw and use it to unravel everything — every name, every widow, every drowned man whose insurance share had been routed into a shell account under Voss's signature. The truth, Betsabeh had learned across four decades of watching men falsify paper, does not win arguments. It wins only if it is more difficult to destroy than the lie.

So she worked slowly. Slower than she wanted. Slower than she could afford.

The manifest for the season's third dive ran to six pages in the original. In the margin of the fourth page, in the clerk's neat commercial hand, the number of divers who descended read *twenty-two*. The number who ascended read *twenty-two*. But the equipment-issue column listed twenty-three sets of weights, and the air-line log recorded twenty-three lines run wet, and there it was — the twenty-third man, present everywhere except in the count, the way the dead are present everywhere except in the conversations of the living.

Betsabeh copied all three columns onto her rice paper, side by side, and drew a single line beneath them, and beneath the line she wrote, in her careful archivist's script: *Discrepancy noted by the undersigned, keeper of records, Meridian compound, Kessarin, this day of the monsoon.* Then her name. Then the date. A record deserved a witness attached to it, or it was only paper.

Her eyes ached by the second hour. By the third, the characters swam, and she had to close her eye and press the heel of her hand against it until the swimming stopped, and resume. She thought, not for the first time, about how much of her life had been spent doing precisely this: sitting alone with other people's numbers, making sure the numbers matched the bodies. It was not work anyone thanked you for. Archivists are remembered, when they are remembered at all, as furniture. But furniture holds up the house.

Farhad would have laughed at her phrasing. Farhad, who had gone into the water in 1838 with a debt of eleven rupees and come out of it as a line item labeled *deserted*, would have said something wicked and funny about furniture, about houses, about the company that owned both. He had been the funny one. She had been the careful one. And the careful one had lived to be old and half-blind in a room full of paper, while the funny one lived only in the ghost impressions of a carbon book — unless she could help it.

She dipped the pen. She kept writing.

* * *

Noor came to her on the fifth night, past midnight, when the compound had gone quiet except for rain and the groan of the chained bridge swinging somewhere out in the black. She heard him before she saw him — he had learned to move quietly, but not quietly enough for a woman who had spent thirty-one years listening for footsteps in her archive — and she did not look up from her copying, because looking up meant lifting the page into focus, and lifting the page into focus cost her a small coin of pain each time, and she was hoarding her coins.

"You've done nine days' work in five," he said, standing over the table.

"Insomnia is a gift," she said. "Most people waste it."

He picked up the finished sheets — she let him, though her hands twitched; no one touched the copies but her — and turned them toward the lamp. She watched him read without reading, the way he did, his eyes moving in that quick ledger-keeper's scan, checking her columns against whatever version of the originals lived behind his own eyes. He had a good memory. Better than good. It was the memory of a boy who had learned early that everything could be taken except what you carried in your head.

"The figures are exact," he said finally. There was wonder in it, carefully hidden, the way he hid everything.

"They'd better be," she said. "I've only got the one eye left to check them with."

The silence that followed had a particular quality. She recognized it. It was the silence of a person deciding whether to pretend he hadn't heard.

"Betsabeh." He set the pages down. "Your eyes."

"Are my eyes," she said, "and my business, and older than your father, rest him. Sit down, boy, you make the room feel inspected."

He sat. He was quiet a long moment, and she copied two more entries while he gathered whatever it was he'd come to say, because her time was short in ways he didn't yet understand and she refused to waste even the awkward silences.

"When we're done," he said at last, "when the bridge opens and the commission comes — I want you to be the one who reads the names. At the hearing. Not me. You knew the archive before any of us. You knew—" He stopped.

"My brother," she finished for him. "You may say his name, Noor. That's rather the point of all this."

"Farhad," he said, carefully, the way one handles a borrowed instrument. "You knew Farhad's story from inside. The commission should hear it from you."

And there it was — the tenderness she had been refusing to look at directly for days, arriving now in the shape of a future she would not be alive to see. She set down her pen. She looked at him — or at the shape of him, the young shape, the shoulders already bent from years of stooping over other men's accounts — and she made a decision she had perhaps been making all along, since the night she caught him comparing manifests and chose, deliberately, catastrophically, to become his family instead of his judge.

"No," she said.

His face fell, and she almost smiled.

"No," she said again, "because I won't be reading anything at that hearing, child. My eyes will not last the season. They may not last the month. I can just make out the columns tonight by holding the page at the reading tilt, and tomorrow the tilt won't be enough, and the day after that I'll be copying by touch and faith, and faith makes sloppy records." She folded her hands on the table, over the nine days of treason she had written in her best hand. "Which is why the copying must finish now, while I can still swear to it. And why you must learn to do it after me. And why—"

She rose, joints announcing themselves one by one, and went to the cabinet at the back of the room — the deep drawer she had opened perhaps six times in thirty-one years, the one that held not company property but her own small estate. From it she took a flat wooden case, scarred at the corners, smelling of cedar and old lacquer, and carried it back to the table.

"This was Farhad's," she said. "Not the company's. His. Bought with his first wages, before the debt, before the water. He was going to be a writer — did I ever tell you? Poems, mostly. Terrible ones. All moonlight and pearl-divers, and every diver lived forever." Her thumb found the brass clasp by touch. "When he signed on with the company, they made him sign with their pen, their ink, their paper, and they kept the contract, and we never saw it again. So he bought this, after, and he said — I was twelve, I remember exactly — he said, *'They'll write me however they want, Betsa. But I'll write myself too, and mine will be the better hand.'*"

She opened the case. Inside, on faded velvet, lay a fountain pen, black lacquer worn silver at the grip where a hand had held it thousands of times, the nib gold and fine as a reed. She lifted it the way one lifts a sleeping bird.

"He never got to write himself," she said. "The water wrote him instead, and then the clerks wrote over the water. Two erasures. I've spent forty years learning to read erasures, Noor, and I'll tell you the only thing I know for certain: inheritance isn't property. Property gets seized, taxed, pulped, sold. What my brother left me wasn't this pen. It was the obligation to witness. The pen is just — the instrument. A record deserves a good instrument." She held it out to him across the table, across the lamplight, across the whole widening gulf between her failing eyes and his young ones. "Take it. Write yourself. Write the others. Make yours the better hand."

Noor did not take it at once. He looked at the pen the way he looked at the manifest numbers — measuring, weighing, testing whether it could bear what would be asked of it. Then he reached out and closed his fingers around it, and she saw his hand shake, just slightly, and pretended she hadn't, because he was entitled to that dignity too.

"I don't know if I'm any good at it," he said. "Writing things that matter."

"Nobody is," Betsabeh said. "That's why it matters that we do it anyway. Now sit. Lesson one: how to hold a nib so the ink doesn't blot. Your father's generation wrote with sticks and patience. We'll make an archivist of you yet."

* * *

They worked together after that, every night, and she taught him everything in the order she had planned it out in her head during the sleepless afternoons: first the copying hand, fast but legible, suited to rice paper; then the abbreviations that saved space without losing meaning; then — most precious, most secret — the craft of reading erasures. How to tilt a page against raking light and catch the valley a stylus leaves in the fibers even after the ink is scraped away. How to breathe steam over certain inks and watch the ghost rise. How to know, from the pressure of a strike-through, whether the hand that crossed out a name was angry or merely obedient — anger presses harder; obedience is uniform, bureaucratic, chilling in its evenness.

Voss's hand, when they found it in the margins of the dive manifests, was obedient. Uniform. Even. Betsabeh hated it more than she could say, and said so once, and Noor laughed — really laughed, the first time in weeks — and the sound of it in the archive room was worth more to her than the entire pearl harvest.

Her eyes worsened exactly on the schedule she had privately predicted. By the twelfth night she was holding pages at arm's length. By the fifteenth she was copying entries she could barely see, dictating figures from memory while Noor verified them against the original, their roles reversing by degrees, the student becoming the eyes. She found she did not mind. This, too, was inheritance: the moment when the teacher becomes the thing taught, becomes the lesson itself, becomes the reason the student works carefully.

On the seventeenth night, near dawn, Noor finished transcribing the last routing page — the shell account, Voss's signature, the director's seal, the whole elegant machinery of theft laid out in columns — and sat back, and looked at the stack of completed copies, and said, quietly:

"It's done. All of it. Every discrepancy, every erased name, every rupee routed. It fits in a coat lining."

Betsabeh touched the stack with two fingers, the way she touched the oldest books. Eleven men. Forty years of her brother's silence. One boy's father. All of it reduced to papers light enough to sew into cloth, small enough to carry through a search, durable enough — if the fates were kind, and they rarely were — to outlive everyone in this room.

"Then listen to me," she said, "because this is the last lesson, and it isn't about paper." She waited until he looked up. "Whatever happens — and something will happen, Noor, men like Voss always notice eventually, and when they do they will not be gentle — whatever happens, the record leaves this compound. Not you. Not me. The record. Promise me you understand the difference."

He frowned. "I don't—"

"A body can be locked in a storeroom," she said. "A body can be transported, fined, drowned, forgotten. Paper that has left the building cannot. Get it out, get it into other hands, get it into the commission's transcript, get it spoken aloud in front of witnesses — and then it stops belonging to any of us. It belongs to everyone. That's the only immortality on offer in Kessarin, child, and I've paid forty years' rent on it, and I am telling you it is worth the price."

She heard him swallow in the dark. "And you?" he asked. "Where do you fit?"

Betsabeh smiled at the lamplight she could barely see, and began, with great care, to bank the flame.

"I'm the furniture," she said. "I hold up the house."

Three days later, Tam came running through the rain to the archive door, white-faced, dripping, and would not come inside, only stood on the threshold gasping the words that ended the tender season of their lives: *Voss knows. Voss has known for days. He's ordered a fire inspection of the whole compound — tomorrow morning — and he's posted himself outside your quarters, Noor. He's coming for the archive.*

Betsabeh stood very still among her shelves, her ruined eyes turned toward the sound of the boy's voice, and felt — not fear, strangely, but a kind of terrible clarity, the clarity of a woman who has finally finished her sums.

"Well then," she said calmly, reaching for the oilcloth. "We'd best decide what can be carried."
