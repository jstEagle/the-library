## 17. Sefora's Silence

The first morning, there were nine of them.

Sefora had chosen the hour carefully — first light, when the compound's clerks came stumbling out to the latrines with their collars half-buttoned and their eyes still soft with sleep, when the guards at the stockade gate were trading yawns and did not yet have their tempers about them. She stood on the packed mud outside the palisade, in the gray drizzle that never quite became rain and never quite stopped, and she unfolded the scrap of paper she had copied from memory, because no one would give her paper anymore.

"Rashid al-Mirren," she read aloud. Her voice cracked on the second syllable and she let it crack. "Diver, third class. Entered the water on the fourteenth of the harvest month. Did not come up."

Behind her, nine people stood in silence. Three widows. A mother whose son had been nineteen. Two old divers who had survived by luck or cowardice and could not look at her. The rest were children, brought because there was nowhere else to leave them.

She read eleven names that first dawn. Eleven men the ledgers said had deserted — walked away from their debts, abandoned their families, vanished into the monsoon country as if the sea had never held them. Eleven men whose insurance shares had been paid out to a signature Sefora had never heard of until Noor Galloway told her about it through the mending basket.

The clerks crossing the yard slowed. Some stared. One, a boy with ink-stained fingers, stopped entirely and stood listening until an older man took his elbow and steered him away.

Sefora folded her paper. "Tomorrow," she said to the nine behind her, "we come again."

* * *

By the fourth day there were thirty-one.

Word moved through Kessarin the way water moves through limestone — slowly, invisibly, finding every crack. The fishing families on the east side had known for years that something was wrong with the manifests; you cannot lose that many husbands and brothers to "desertion" without someone counting. But knowing was not saying, and saying was not standing in front of the company's walls while its clerks watched from behind glass.

Now they were saying it. Now they were standing.

Sefora organized them the way she had once organized a household on a diver's wages — which is to say ruthlessly, with nothing wasted. The vigil began before dawn and ended when the compound's work bell rang, so that no one could accuse them of idling away the working hours. They stood in silence except for the reading. They did not chant. They did not carry signs; signs could be confiscated, but names lived in the mouth. When the guards ordered them to disperse, they dispersed, and returned the next morning, and the next, patient as tide.

"They want us to be a mob," she told the widow Amara, who had begun standing beside her every day with her dead brother-in-law's name written on her palm in henna. "A mob can be broken. A crowd is harder. And a congregation—" she looked down the line of wet, silent faces, "—a congregation cannot be broken at all."

On the seventh morning, a guard threw a bucket of gray wash-water over the front rank. It was cold enough to make the children cry out. Sefora did not move. She finished reading the eleventh name — Behram Oduya, struck through twice in the carbon copies, Noor had told her, twice, as if once had not been certain enough — and then she turned to the guard, water running off her chin, and said only:

"You are Behram Oduya's cousin. I knew your mother. Write to her and tell her what you did this morning."

The man's face went the color of the wash-water. He set the bucket down and did not touch it again for the rest of the season.

* * *

What Sefora had not expected was what the silence did to the compound itself.

Noor had explained it to her once, in one of the messages sewn into the mending basket — that strange, gentle boy who kept the ledgers and measured everything by what could be carried. *A ledger is only powerful if everyone agrees to believe it,* he had written. *Take away the agreement and it is just paper.*

Each dawn, the names went up over the wall like smoke. Each dawn, the clerks walking to their desks had to pass under them. Sefora watched through the palisade gaps as the compound changed. Clerks who had once crossed the yard in pairs now crossed alone, heads down, walking fast. The mess hall grew quiet. A clerk named Tam — young, upright, Voss's shadow — had taken to standing at the gate during the readings, and though he never spoke, he never left either, and his face each morning was a little more like a man watching his own house burn.

And the families inside — the divers' wives and mothers quartered in the compound's lower rows — began to come to the fence. At first just to watch. Then, on the ninth morning, old Mother Ishvari pressed her palm flat against the palisade boards, and Sefora, on the other side, pressed hers against the same place, and after that there was no question of the vigil belonging only to those outside.

Two hundred people. That was the count on the twelfth dawn, when the rain finally broke and the sun came up the color of brass over the bridge towers. Two hundred, standing in the mud from the fish market to the customs house, and among them now merchants who bought pearls, boatmen who ferried the company's cargo, even two off-duty constables who had removed their hats and stood at the back as if hoping not to be counted and unable to stay away.

Sefora read the eleven names. Then, for the first time, she added a twelfth line, one she had been saving:

"And we ask the Meridian Trading Company: where are the bodies?"

Silence answered her. Two hundred people's worth of it, heavier than any shout.

* * *

He came to her at dusk, after the vigil had dissolved and the lamps of Kessarin were being lit one by one along the harbor road. She was mending nets in the doorway of her sister's house — her husband's boat was long gone, sold piece by piece to feed the erased men's families, but her hands still needed work or they started shaking — and she saw the young clerk coming up the street with his collar turned up and his face gray as ash.

Tam. She knew him from the gate. She put down the netting needle.

"I can't stay," he said, before she could say anything. His voice was low and cracked. "If anyone sees me—"

"Then say it quickly."

He glanced up and down the empty street, then pressed something into her hands: a fold of papers wrapped in oilcloth, small as a prayer book. "It's the shell account. Voss's routing pages — the ones Noor couldn't copy before they locked the archive. Every 'deserted' diver's insurance share goes through it. There's a second signature besides Voss's. You'll see whose." He swallowed. "I took it from the director's correspondence tray tonight. He'll know by morning. Maybe sooner."

Sefora unwrapped the corner and looked. Columns of figures, neat as hymn verses, and beside each figure a name she had spoken at dawn. Beside each name, a deduction. *Equipment wear. Unrecovered gear. Administrative fee.* Her husband's share, reduced by a third before it ever reached anyone, and then routed onward to an account under a name she did not know — but the seal stamped beside it she recognized from the closure notice nailed to the bridge. The resident director's own.

"The director," she said quietly. "It goes to the director."

"It always went to the director." Tam's hands were trembling. "Voss keeps the books but the director takes the money. That's why the record has to be tidy before the historians come. Not for Voss. For *him*." He looked at her then, and his eyes were wet and furious. "My brother is on your list. Daryush Tam. Fourth name. I was fourteen when they told us he'd run off. My mother believed it until the day she died. She died ashamed of him, do you understand? She went into the ground believing her son abandoned us." His voice broke clean through. "I stood at that gate every morning because I didn't know why I was standing. Now I know."

Sefora reached out and closed the young man's fingers back around the oilcloth packet. "Then don't give it to me," she said. "I'm a widow outside the wall. Anything found on me is found and burned by nightfall. Keep it. Hide it where only you know. When the time comes — and it will come, the ice always opens — you hand it to someone whose word carries weight. Do you understand me? You are a clerk of the Meridian Trading Company. Your testimony will weigh more than all my grief."

"But if they search my quarters—"

"Then hide it better than grief hides," she said. "You've had practice. We all have."

He stood a moment longer, breathing hard, a young man holding his brother's death in his hands. Then he nodded once, turned up his collar, and went back down the dark street toward the compound, walking like a man carrying something breakable through a storm.

* * *

That night Sefora sat with the copy she had allowed herself — one page only, memorized and rewritten in her own hand, the rest committed to memory the way her husband had taught her to memorize the tide tables, because the sea takes paper but it does not take what lives in the skull.

She thought about the arithmetic of it. That was the thing no one warned you about widowhood: how much of it was arithmetic. How many mouths, how many days, how much rice. For three years she had done sums of survival while the company did sums of profit, and both sets of numbers had her husband in them, and only one set told the truth.

Now here was the truth in figures. Eleven shares. Deducted, routed, sealed. Her husband sold twice — once to the sea, once to a signature.

She got up before dawn, as she had every dawn for twelve days, and folded her paper of names, and woke the children of the vigil gently, the way you wake people for a funeral.

But when she stood before the two hundred that thirteenth morning, with the brass sun rising over the bridge towers and the compound's clerks gathering at the fence to listen whether they meant to or not, Sefora did not begin with the names.

She held up the single page she had copied, high enough for the guards to see, high enough for whoever watched from the director's windows to see.

"Yesterday," she called out, and her voice carried clear across the mud, "someone inside the walls gave the truth a body. Eleven men. Eleven shares. One seal." She let the silence stretch until it hurt. "So today we add a name to the reading. Not a dead man's name. A living one."

She unfolded the page and read the shell account's title aloud, letter by letter, and then the director's name, and the sound that went through two hundred people was not a shout and not a cheer but something older than either — the low, rolling murmur of a crowd becoming a congregation becoming, perhaps, a verdict.

At the fence, she saw the young clerk Tam standing very straight, his hands buried deep in his pockets, and she saw that around him other clerks had gathered too, five of them, then eight, facing outward toward the vigil instead of inward toward the compound.

And above the director's quarters, a window slammed shut.

They would come for the boy, Sefora thought, folding her page. They would come for him before the day was out — and through him, they would come for Noor, and for Betsabeh, and for every name on her list still waiting to be spoken.

She raised her voice and began the first name. Better to fill the morning with the dead than waste a moment of the light.
