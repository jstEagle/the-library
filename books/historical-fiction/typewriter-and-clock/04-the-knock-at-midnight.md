## 4. The Knock at Midnight

The knock did not come as a knock.

It came first as a sound Edmund Amberdale had trained himself to hear the way a sailor trains himself to hear weather: three soft taps against the shutter of his window on the alley side, spaced like a heartbeat that had learned caution. Then a voice, low and frayed, saying nothing so useful as his name—only, "Courier. Courier, please."

He had been awake anyway. Sleep had become in the capital a thing done in shifts, like sentry duty, and Edmund's shift was ending. He lay in the narrow bed at Madame Kessler's boardinghouse with his boots on and his coat folded over the chair within reach of his hand, the way he had slept for eleven years, in forty rooms, in six countries whose borders had since been redrawn by men who never once carried their own luggage. The candle was out. The dispatches were under the floorboard beneath the washstand, wrapped in oilcloth, and he knew without looking that they were there, the way he knew the weight of his own hands.

The voice came again. "Please. I know you're awake. You people are always awake."

*You people.* He almost smiled at that. There was no *you people* anymore; there was barely an *us*. The government he served had moved its offices twice in a week and now operated out of a schoolhouse with the maps taken down from the walls, as if geography itself had become indiscreet. The city around Madame Kessler's house emptied by daylight and filled by rumor at night. That afternoon he had watched a woman carry a birdcage down the Boulevard Aurel with the bird still inside it, walking with the careful dignity of someone who has decided that civilization consists of not abandoning one's canary. By evening the shelling to the east had a new rhythm, closer, arrhythmic, like a heart with something wrong in it.

Edmund rose without sound. This was the first discipline they taught you—not running, not shooting, not cipher. Silence. A courier who could be heard was a courier who could be followed; a courier who could be followed was a courier who could be read; and a courier who could be read was, functionally speaking, already dead, merely awaiting the paperwork.

He unlatched the shutter an inch. Cold poured through, smelling of coal smoke and wet stone and, underneath, faintly, the iron tang that had hung over the city for days now and which everyone pretended was the river.

The alley was dark except for the spill of one window across the way, where a curtain glowed the color of weak tea. A woman stood against the wall below him, hooded, holding something bundled against her chest the way one holds either contraband or a child, and for a moment Edmund could not tell which, and the not-knowing opened a cold seam in him that he would spend the rest of his life trying to close.

"Open the door," she said. "I can't climb. I've been walking since—" She stopped. Recalculated. He heard her do it, the small audible economy of a person deciding how much truth she could afford. "Since this morning."

"Who sent you?"

"The Ministry. Fourth section." She gave him a phrase—a recognition phrase, old, from a rotation two years dead—and Edmund's hand went still on the latch, because the phrase was correct and therefore everything else was suspect. Correct phrases were the cheapest commodity in a collapsing state. They changed hands in cafés. They were sold by men with receipts.

"Wait," he said, and closed the shutter, and stood in the dark listening to his own pulse, which was behaving disgracefully.

Eleven years of doctrine said: *do not open the door at night. Do not accept unscheduled deliveries. Anything that arrives unannounced is either a trap or a burden, and both will slow you.* The creed was carved into him more deeply than any loyalty, because loyalties changed hands too, but the creed had never once betrayed him. Carry only what can be carried. Feel nothing. Be no one. He had written those sentences himself, once, in a notebook he later burned, and he had lived by them the way other men lived by prayer, and they had kept him whole through purges and annexations and the slow moral erosion that comes of being trusted by institutions that trust no one.

And yet his feet were already on the stairs.

* * *

The boardinghouse slept around him in layers. On the ground floor, Father Ivo snored behind his door with the steady, unembarrassed rhythm of a man who had made his peace with God and found God a heavy sleeper. Above him, the soldier—Corporal Vey, though he'd given another name at the door—tossed and muttered in whatever country his dreams had retreated to. And somewhere above that, past curfew, past midnight, past all decency, Anselm the pianist played.

He always played after curfew. Softly, with the felt damper half-lowered so the notes came out muffled and round, like music remembered rather than performed. Nocturnes, mostly, or pieces that sounded like nocturnes, and Edmund had decided in his first week not to wonder about it. Everyone in Madame Kessler's house had one behavior that made no sense and harmed no one, and the unwritten law of the place was that these behaviors were none of each other's business. The priest drank tea like a man drinking to forget. The soldier polished boots that would never be inspected. The pianist played to no audience at midnight. And Edmund counted things—the stairs, the exits, the seconds between distant shellfire and its echo—which was its own kind of music, if you were built the wrong way.

Madame Kessler herself sat in the ground-floor parlor with a lamp turned low, doing needlework that never seemed to progress, and when Edmund came down the stairs she looked up at him over her spectacles with an expression he had catalogued but never decoded: not curiosity exactly, but a kind of patient readiness, as if she had spent thirty years keeping lodgers and had long ago concluded that whatever walked down her stairs at odd hours was between them and their respective gods.

She said nothing. She turned back to her needlework. But as he passed into the kitchen toward the alley door, he heard her set down her hoop, and heard the lamp turned up one notch—not enough to see by, just enough, should anything happen, for the house to pretend it had been awake all along.

He unbolted the alley door and the cold came in like a creditor.

* * *

She was older than her voice. That was his first thought, and it was a coward's thought, a clerk's thought, a way of looking at a human being and seeing paperwork. She was perhaps thirty, hollow-cheeked, with chapped lips and the gray pallor of someone who had been afraid for so long that fear had become a metabolism. Her coat was a man's coat. Her shoes were wrong for the season. And the bundle in her arms—

"Fourth section," she repeated. "You're Amberdale. They said the tall one, the quiet one, the one who doesn't drink. Don't worry, I'm not staying. I couldn't stay anywhere." She laughed then, a single dry note with no humor in it, the laugh of a person who has discovered that terror and comedy share a border. "I've been carrying this since noon. My arms have stopped working properly. Please take it before I drop it, because I swear to you I'm going to drop it, my hands don't belong to me anymore."

"Take what?" Edmund asked, though his arms were already opening, already reaching, some reflex older than the creed asserting itself in him like water finding a crack.

She transferred the bundle quickly, the way you pass something hot, and stepped back immediately as if to prevent herself from taking it again. And the weight settled into Edmund's arms—heavier than documents, lighter than a body, warm, moving slightly, breathing—and the world tilted on some axis he had not known it possessed.

Inside the blanket, an infant. Weeks old, perhaps two months. A scrap of a face, red-chapped from cold, a mouth working at nothing, fists curled like ferns before they unfurl. It did not cry. It looked up at him with the grave, unfocused patience of the very young, and then its eyes caught the light spilling from the kitchen doorway, and Edmund Amberdale, who had spent eleven years making himself invisible, ceased entirely to exist.

Because he knew those eyes.

They were gray-green, the color of the sea off the northern coast in April, the color of a particular afternoon four years ago on the steps of the Ministry archive when Lise Marchand had come out into unexpected sunlight with a stack of translation folios held against her chest and squinted at him and said, *You're the one who never talks. I've decided that means you listen.* Gray-green, flecked, lit from inside by some private amusement at the world. Eyes he had memorized deliberately, the way he memorized routes, because memory was the only cargo he permitted himself, and then had spent four years trying to forget because forgetting was safer and he was, above all things, safe.

Lise Marchand, translator, second desk of the linguistic section, who read five languages and laughed at the sixth. Who had shared a table with him in the ministry canteen for one autumn and part of a winter, trading observations about the idiocy of diplomatic French while around them the republic quietly rotted. Who had once, in the archive stairwell, with the smell of dust and binding glue all around, put her hand flat against his chest over his heart and said, *There. Something in there after all,* and he had not denied it, which for Edmund Amberdale was a declaration of love of the most extravagant kind. Who had been arrested in the purge of the Foreign Ministry on a Tuesday in March, along with thirty-one others, and whose name had appeared afterward on no list, in no record, attached to no charge—as if the state had reached into the world and removed her the way one removes a misprinted page.

He had asked. Once, carefully, through channels. The answer came back in the negative space where an answer should have been, and Edmund had understood, and had done what the creed required, which was nothing, and had carried the nothing with him ever since, and the nothing had weighed more than every pouch he'd ever slung across his shoulder.

Now the nothing breathed in his arms and had her eyes.

"She isn't mine," the woman in the alley said. She had pulled her hood back; her hair was cropped short and unevenly, self-cut, the haircut of flight. "That is—I mean, I'm not the mother. I was told you'd understand. Were you told? No. Of course not. Nobody tells anyone anything anymore, that's rather the whole problem with our beloved republic." She was talking too fast, the way people talk when silence might kill them. "A man brought her to me yesterday. Or the day before. The days have stopped working. He said, take her to the boardinghouse on Rue Vasseur, ask for the tall lodger, say fourth section. He paid me in silver. Actual silver. Do you know what silver is worth now? More than the child, apparently. More than anything."

"Who was the man?"

"I don't know. A tired man. They're all tired men." She rubbed her face with both hands. "There was a note. Tucked in the blanket. I wasn't supposed to read it, but I can't help reading things, it's a disease. I'll tell you what it said and then I'm going, and you won't remember me, right? That's the arrangement? I walk away and I was never here?"

"You were never here," Edmund said, and the words came out of him in the flat courier cadence, automatic, while every other part of him was elsewhere—in a blanket, in a pair of gray-green eyes, in a stairwell four years gone.

She fumbled inside her coat and produced a slip of paper, small, folded twice, soft at the creases as if it had been handled many times—or written many times, drafted and redrafted in some room where a man sat with a pen and a sleeping infant and could not get the sentence right. She pressed it into his free hand. Her fingers were ice.

"Forget the face," she quoted softly, before letting go. "That's what it says. Isn't that the strangest thing you've ever heard? Forget the face. Like a command. Like a military order." She stepped backward into the dark. "Good luck, courier. Good luck, little—" and she didn't finish, and then she was gone, footsteps quick and light on the frozen cobbles, fading, and then the alley belonged to the snow and the distant guns and Edmund Amberdale standing in a doorway holding the one thing in the world he had no instructions for.

* * *

He did not unfold the note at once. That was discipline too, though it cost him now like a torn muscle: stand in the cold, close the door, bolt it, count to sixty, let the ordinary sounds of the house reassert themselves—the settling timbers, the pianist's muffled nocturne overhead, Madame Kessler's lamp clicking off in the parlor with a small decisive snap, as if she had seen everything worth seeing. Only then, in the kitchen, by the light of a match cupped in his palm, did he open the paper.

Four words, in ink, in handwriting he recognized before his mind finished processing the shapes of the letters.

*Forget the face.*

He knew that hand. He had watched it move across canteen napkins, sketching little caricatures of the deputy undersecretary with a balloon for a head. He had received one letter in it, once, unsigned, delivered by hand during that autumn, containing nothing but a time and a place and a small drawn star, and he had kept it until the purge made keeping it dangerous, and burning it had been the hardest act of tradecraft he ever performed, harder than any crossing, because it was the only document he had ever carried that was addressed to his heart rather than his office.

Lise's handwriting. Unmistakable. Which meant one of two things: she was alive, or someone wanted him to believe she was alive, and both possibilities were unbearable in opposite directions.

The baby stirred against his chest and made a sound—not a cry yet, a pre-cry, a rehearsal—and Edmund found himself doing something his body knew and his mind had never authorized: he rocked. A small motion, hip to shoulder, instinctive, ancient. The sound subsided. The gray-green eyes drifted shut. And Edmund stood in a stranger's kitchen at one o'clock in the morning, holding the daughter of a woman the state had erased, and felt his invisibility—the armor he had worn so long he had forgotten it was armor—crack straight down the middle like river ice in spring.

Because here was the truth he had spent four years not thinking: he had never stopped watching doors. In every café, on every platform, in every crowd, some peripheral, deniable, unprofessional fraction of Edmund Amberdale had been scanning faces for hers. He had filed it under vigilance. He had called it habit. He had told himself that a courier noticed everyone. But a courier did not learn the names of birds from a woman who pointed them out on windowsills. A courier did not keep, in the vault of his perfect professional memory, the exact weight of a hand laid flat against his chest.

*Forget the face.*

He looked down at the sleeping child, at the small furrowed brow, at the mouth that had her mouth's stubborn set even in miniature, and understood that the order had arrived four years too late. The face was already kept. It had been kept in the one place no purge could reach, no furnace could burn, no checkpoint could search. He had smuggled it across every border he'd ever crossed, undeclared, and now it had come home to roost in his arms wearing a blanket and weighing less than his dispatches.

The match burned down to his fingers. He did not notice until it stung.

In the blanket's hem, as he adjusted his hold, his thumb found something that did not belong to wool—stiffness, a ridge, the suggestion of stitching where no stitching should be. Sewn in. Concealed with care, by hands that had known how such things were done, which meant Lise had known, which meant the story of the last four years was larger and darker than any he had imagined, and it was sewn into a few inches of hem and handed to him in an alley by a stranger with ice-cold fingers.

Overhead, faintly, the piano stopped mid-phrase, as if the player too had heard something change in the house below.

Edmund stood very still in the dark kitchen, the note in one hand, the child rising and falling on his chest with each small breath, and outside the guns kept their broken time, and somewhere east of the city a government he served was negotiating away the last of itself, and none of it—not the dispatches under the floorboard, not the route mapped in his head, not the creed worn smooth as a rosary bead—none of it meant anything at all beside the fact that she had known, in whatever final hours were hers, to bring this child to his door.

He unfolded the note again, though he knew every word. Beneath the four words, in smaller script, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper, there was one line more that the stranger had not read aloud—or had missed, or had chosen to spare him:

*If you are reading this, you are the only one I trust. Forgive me.*

Edmund closed his fist around the paper, and the baby sighed in her sleep, and upstairs the piano began again—softly, after curfew, for no one—playing something slow that sounded, for the first time in all the nights Edmund had listened, like a lullaby.
