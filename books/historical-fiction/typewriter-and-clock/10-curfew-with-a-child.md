## 10. Curfew, With a Child

The knock came at nine minutes past eight, which meant it was not a knock at all but an announcement.

Edmund knew the difference. A knock that wanted entry came soft, apologetic, twice. This one came three times, hard, with the flat authority of a fist that had a rifle slung beside it, and it arrived at the hour when Madame Kessler's hallway clock had already been stopped for the winter—the whole house running on private time, on the time of people who had nowhere to be and everything to hide. He was sitting on the edge of his bed with Lise against his shoulder, feeding her from the bottle Madame Kessler had produced that morning without comment, and the sound went through him like a nail through a board.

Below, in the hall, the clock that did not run ticked anyway, in Edmund's imagination. Below, Madame Kessler's slippers crossed the floor at an unhurried pace, and he heard her pause—two seconds, no more—before she opened the door.

"Good evening, Sergeant."

"Curfew inspection, madam. Papers for everyone in the house."

Edmund stood very still. The bottle in his hand had gone lukewarm. Lise, mercifully, had finished, and lay against his chest with her eyes open, watching the ceiling with the grave, unblinking attention of the very young, as though the plaster above her held a document she was reading line by line.

He had rehearsed this, in the way he rehearsed everything: the cover story, the commercial clerk, the papers in his coat lining, the route memorized in three redundant versions. He had rehearsed checkpoints and searches and the specific tone of voice that made officials lose interest. He had not rehearsed an infant in his arms during a curfew sweep in a boardinghouse that was not, on any paper anywhere, supposed to contain one.

He set the bottle on the sill. He looked around the room with the courier's eye—the eye that inventoried a space in half a second and knew what would be found and what would be missed. The blanket, Lise's blanket, lay folded on the chair. The note was sewn where it had always been sewn, into the hem, invisible. The bottle. The bottle was the problem. Nobody fed an empty room.

He wrapped the bottle in his spare shirt and pushed it under the mattress, and even as he did it he understood the absurdity: a man hiding a baby's bottle as though it were a coded dispatch, as though the milk itself were evidence. In a way it was. Milk was testimony. Milk said: someone here is loved enough to be kept alive.

The footsteps on the stairs were heavy and plural. Two men, maybe three. Lise chose that moment to make a sound—not a cry, not yet, but the small throat-clearing prelude to one, the overture of an infant preparing to announce herself to the world—and Edmund did the only thing his body knew how to do, the thing he had learned in ministries and border towns and the backs of trucks: he moved.

There was a wardrobe against the wall, and beside the wardrobe, a gap, and behind the gap, the cold brick of the chimney breast—a space perhaps fourteen inches wide that he had noticed on his first night and dismissed as irrelevant, because couriers did not hide, couriers were invisible in plain sight. He folded himself into it with Lise pressed against his chest, her face turned inward, her ear—so small, so impossibly small—resting over his heart, and he pulled the coat of the wardrobe's neighbor, an old fur that smelled of camphor and the previous century, across the gap until only a blade of lamplight reached him.

The door opened without a knock.

"Madame Kessler. This the last room?"

"The last, Sergeant. As I said."

The voice that answered was young, and tired, and trying to sound older. "Papers."

Edmund heard the shuffling of documents. He heard the soldier's breathing, the wet click of his throat. He heard, distantly, Father Ivo in the room across the hall saying something in his mild, ruined voice—*yes, of course, of course, here you are*—and the pianist Anselm's door standing silent, Anselm himself no doubt lying feverish and still, playing the one role left to a deserter with a wound: the man too sick to be interesting.

And he heard Lise's breath against his chest, quickening, the small bellows of her working up to the full announcement.

*Not now,* he thought, with a desperation that had no dignity in it at all. *Not now, not now, little one, I am asking you—*

She had never been asked to do anything in her life. She was nine days old. She had been carried through an alley at midnight by a woman who was dead or worse, and she had been fed by a stranger, and she had slept in a drawer padded with a sweater, and now a man made of camphor and terror was asking her for silence, and she opened her mouth—

— and Madame Kessler's voice came up through the floor like a bell.

"Sergeant. Before you go. You'll want to note the christening."

A pause. The soldier's boots stopped on the landing.

"The what, madam?"

"The christening, Sergeant. Downstairs. The priest baptized a child this evening—Father Ivo, he's staying with me, defrocked or not he remembers the words, and the mother wanted it done before the shelling started in earnest. You'll want it in your report. Births are to be registered, aren't they? Even now. *Especially* now."

Another silence. Edmund, pressed into fourteen inches of cold brick with his heart hammering against an infant's ear, understood that he was listening to a performance, and that it was the finest he had ever heard, and that he could not see the stage.

"A child," the sergeant said. "Whose child?"

"Mine," said Madame Kessler.

The word fell into the house like a stone into a well. Edmund waited for the splash and heard none. The sergeant said, slowly, "Yours, madam. You're—forgive me—"

"Sixty-one years old in March, Sergeant, and a widow since the last war, and if you are about to express surprise I will remind you that women have been surprising men for some considerable time. My daughter's girl. Mine to raise now. The mother is—indisposed. The father is at the front, or was, until the front came to us. The priest christened her at seven o'clock. Her name is Anna. You may write it down."

"You'll have papers for—"

"I will have papers when there is a government left to issue them, Sergeant, and so will you. Until then you have my word and the priest's, and the priest is standing in the hall if you'd like to ask him whether he performed the sacrament or whether a woman of my age invented a granddaughter for her own amusement at eight o'clock on a winter's evening."

A long silence. Then, unbelievably, a sound Edmund had to strain to identify: laughter. Short, reluctant, wrung out of the young man like water from a cloth.

"No, madam. No, I—fine. The christening. Anna. I'll note it." The boots moved. Stopped. "The lodgers. The one across the hall—the priest—he's registered?"

"Defrocked and documented, Sergeant. He shows me his papers every Tuesday. It's the highlight of his week."

Another huff of almost-laughter. The boots descended. The front door opened, admitted a gust of snow-smelling air, and closed, and the bolt went across, and Madame Kessler's slippers crossed the hall at the same unhurried pace, and stopped at the foot of the stairs.

"You can come out now, Herr Amberdale," she said, not loudly. "The fur coat is not as thick as it looks, and neither are you."

* * *

He came out stiffly, Lise in his arms, his left leg asleep from the hip down, and found her at the bottom of the stairs with a candle in one hand, looking up at him with an expression he would spend the rest of his life failing to fully describe: not triumph, not kindness exactly, but the flat, practical tenderness of a woman who had done what the evening required and would now require tea.

"Anna," he said.

"You needed a name. Anna was my mother's. It was lying around." She turned and led the way down. "Sit. I'll heat milk. And you will tell me, Herr Amberdale—and think carefully, because I have just committed a crime for you, and I am sixty-one years old and too old to commit crimes for strangers—whose child this is, and how she came to a boardinghouse in a blanket at midnight, and why you sleep in your boots."

He sat. The kitchen was warm from the stove, and yellow, and small. Father Ivo appeared in the doorway with his collarless shirt and his ruined hands folded, drawn by some instinct of the defrocked, and Anselm leaned on the doorframe behind him, gray-faced, his wound dressed and his eyes fever-bright, and nobody asked to be included and nobody was excluded. Madame Kessler set the kettle on and did not look at any of them.

"She was left at the door," Edmund said. "Nine days ago. With a note."

"Whose child?"

He was quiet long enough that the kettle had time to start its low complaint. "A woman I knew," he said finally. "At the Ministry. Before."

Madame Kessler nodded, once, as though this explained everything, which in a sense it did. "And the note said?"

"To forget her face."

Father Ivo made a small sound—not quite a word—and crossed himself with the hand that no longer had the right to do it, and did it anyway.

"Well," said Madame Kessler. She took the kettle off before it whistled, because whistling was a luxury, and poured. "Then she is Anna until further notice, and the christening stands, and Father Ivo will perform it properly tomorrow so that the sergeant's report is not a lie, only slightly ahead of itself. And you—" she set the cup in front of Edmund with a click of finality, "—will stop sleeping in your boots, because a man who sleeps in his boots is a man expecting to run, and a man expecting to run drops things, and you cannot drop *that* one."

She said *that one* the way other people said *the dispatches*, or *the sacrament*, and Edmund found, to his considerable surprise, that his eyes were hot, and that he had to look into his tea.

"Madame Kessler," Anselm said from the doorframe, his voice rough with fever, "the sergeant's report. It will say a child was born in this house."

"It will say a child was christened in this house, which is true as of tomorrow, which makes it true as of tonight. In my experience, Sergeant, documents are like soup. It is all in the reheating." She gathered the cups. "Bed. All of you. Curfew ends at six and the milk order comes at seven, and if we are going to be a family of liars we will at least be an orderly one."

* * *

Later—past midnight, in the dark of his room, with Lise asleep in the drawer and the snow ticking against the glass—Edmund lay awake and took inventory, because inventory was the only prayer he knew.

He thought about the sergeant's young voice, and how it had wanted, so badly, to be done with the whole evening. He thought about Father Ivo crossing himself without permission. He thought about Anselm in the doorframe, feverish, who had said nothing and whose saying-nothing had been a kind of vow. He thought about a woman of sixty-one inventing a granddaughter out of thin air and her own dead mother's name, and how she had not hesitated for even the length of a footstep.

The courier's creed said: carry only what can be carried, feel nothing, be no one. He recited it now, the way he had recited it for eleven years, and heard, for the first time, how it sounded in a house where four strangers had lied to an armed man in order to keep a nine-day-old infant out of a report.

It sounded like something written by a man who had never been offered tea.

Lise stirred in the drawer and made her small sound, the prelude, the overture, and Edmund was out of bed and lifting her before he was fully awake, and she settled against his chest with a sigh that seemed, in the dark, almost satisfied, and he stood at the window holding her and watching the snow come down over the ruined city, over the checkpoints and the furnaces and the burning ministries, over everything that could not be saved and the one small thing that could.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would map the three checkpoints—the river bridge, the rail yard, the pass—and rehearse the clerk's cover until it fit him like skin. Tomorrow he would be the courier again, disciplined and invisible, carrying his sealed cargo toward the border.

But tonight, standing in the dark with his daughter's breath on his collar, Edmund Amberdale admitted what he had been refusing to admit for nine days: that he was no longer carrying her.

He was keeping her. And there was a difference, and the difference had a name, and the name was Anna until further notice—and somewhere beyond the mountains, in a town called Halst where a neutral journalist was said to take her coffee at the same café every morning at ten, there were people who could make the truth of the dispatches in his boot louder than any government.

He did not know yet how he would reach them. But as Lise slept against his heart, Edmund understood that the route had already changed—that somewhere between the wardrobe and the kitchen, between the lie of the christening and the truth of the tea, he had stopped planning an escape and begun planning a delivery.

The snow fell. The house breathed around him, four strangers and a child, a family of liars, keeping its own time.
