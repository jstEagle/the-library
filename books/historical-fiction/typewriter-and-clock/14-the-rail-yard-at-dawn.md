## 14. The Rail Yard at Dawn

They left before the lamps went out.

That was Anselm's phrase for it, delivered in the grey hour when the pianist stood at the window of Madame Kessler's boardinghouse with his coat already buttoned to the throat, watching the last municipal lamplighter make his rounds with a pole that trembled more than it used to. *Before the lamps go out*—as if the whole war were a matter of timing, of slipping beneath the wick-trimmer's arm while the flame still held. Edmund had come to understand that Anselm thought in such terms. A man who played only after curfew learned to measure the world in permitted intervals, in the narrow corridors of the night when no one official was listening.

The city gave them up grudgingly. Frost had come in the night, the first hard frost of the winter, and it lay on the cobbles like a skin drawn over the face of the town, so that every footfall seemed to Edmund a small profanity. He carried the dispatch case under his coat, against his ribs, where the leather had warmed to the temperature of his body until he could no longer tell where the documents ended and he began. The baby—Lise, though he did not yet say the name aloud, not even in the privacy of his own skull—rode against his chest in a sling Madame Kessler had fashioned from a pillowcase and a length of sash cord, her weight distributed with a midwife's cunning so that from any angle greater than a few paces he appeared merely thick-waisted, a clerk who had been eating well in a season when eating well was itself a kind of alibi.

Anselm walked half a step ahead, his limp more pronounced in the cold, his hands jammed in his pockets. He had refused to look at the child. In three days of preparation he had not once looked directly at her, and Edmund understood this too, or understood it the way one understands a wound in another man that resembles one's own: the pianist had done things in the war that he could not carry unconfessed, and infants, with their terrible candor, were the one audience he could not yet face.

"The yard is east of the gasworks," Anselm said, for the fourth time. "There's a man called Brozek who works the points at the far end. He was a friend of my brother's. He'll wave us through the freight shed, and from there it's a milk train to Vodna, and from Vodna the mountain road." He paused at a corner, listened to the wind moving refuse along the gutter, and went on. "He owes me nothing. Remember that. He owes my brother, and my brother is dead, and dead men's debts are the only currency that never devalues."

"You've said."

"I've said." Anselm's breath hung in the air. "I'll keep saying it until you stop looking at me as though I were the plan. I'm not the plan. I'm a man who knows a man. The plan is that nobody looks at you at all."

They moved through streets that had been emptied by decree and fear in equal measure. Shopfronts were shuttered, and on some the shutters had been chalked with the names of the families behind them, a superstition or a defiance—Edmund could not decide which—that the shells had not yet reached far enough to test. A dog followed them for three streets, trotting at the precise distance of a debt collector, and gave them up at last at the canal bridge with the air of an animal concluding they were not worth the trouble.

The gasworks announced itself by smell before sight: the sweet-rot stink of coal and tar, thick enough to taste. Beyond it, the rail yard opened in the dark like a black lake, its tracks running in converging lines toward a horizon where the sky had not yet decided to lighten. Freight cars stood in ranks, their couplings furred with frost, and somewhere deep in the yard a shunting engine breathed and clanked to itself, the restless animal of the place, unable to sleep.

"There." Anselm touched Edmund's sleeve. At the far end of the yard, where the tracks fanned out toward the points, a lantern moved in a slow, deliberate arc—three swings left, a pause, three swings right. "That's him. That's the all-clear."

They crossed the yard in the shadow of a standing freight train, Edmund's heart conducting its own private percussion against the dispatch case. The child slept. He could feel her breath through the wool of his coat, a small tidal warmth, and he had learned by now to read her sleep the way a sailor reads water—by the pressure of it, the rhythm, the tiny shifts that meant dream or hunger or the beginning of a cry. She had been fed at four. She would hold until seven, Madame Kessler had said, with the confidence of a woman who had raised sons in a harder winter than this one. *If she holds,* Edmund had said, and Kessler had looked at him with her flat grey eyes and said, *She'll hold. Babies keep their promises better than governments.*

The freight shed was a long barn of corrugated iron, its interior stacked with crates stenciled in three alphabets, two of them obsolete. Brozek was waiting inside, a squat man in a railway greatcoat with a lantern in one hand and a broom in the other, sweeping frost-melt from a floor that did not need it. He looked at Anselm for a long moment without greeting, and then at Edmund, and then—he could not help himself, no man could—at the slight, impossible curve of Edmund's coat front, and his face did something complicated and final.

"You're the brother of Tomas," he said. Not a question.

"I am."

"Tomas is dead."

"Two years."

Brozek set down the broom. From inside his coat he took a flask, drank, offered it to Anselm, who declined, and to Edmund, who declined, and then screwed the cap back on with the care of a man closing a conversation. "The milk train leaves at first light from platform three. It goes to Vodna and nowhere else. The guard is a cousin of my wife and asks nothing, but the *Feldgendarmen* walk the platform when they walk it, and they walk it when they please." He looked at Edmund again, longer this time. "Your papers?"

Edmund produced the wallet. Brozek examined the commercial clerk's pass under the lantern, tilting it so the light caught the seal, and handed it back with a shrug that contained, in the grammar of shrugs, an entire philosophy about papers, seals, and the difference between them.

"You'll stand in the shed until the whistle," Brozek said. "Then you walk, you don't run, and you don't look at the platform, and if anyone asks, you're a grain factor going to Vodna to inspect a shipment that no longer exists. Say it like you're bored. Boredom is the one thing they never suspect."

* * *

They waited in the shed among the crates while the sky outside performed its slow, reluctant confession of grey. Anselm sat on a crate with his bad leg stretched out and his eyes half-closed, and Edmund stood at the shed's open mouth, watching the yard come up out of the dark in increments: first the rails shining, then the frost smoking off the wagon roofs, then the signal gantries standing against the lightening east like gallows in a field. The child slept on. He counted her breaths without meaning to, a habit now, a rosary of small warm numbers.

The whistle came at 6:40, a thin, far sound, and with it the milk train, four wagons and a brake van, easing along platform three with the unhurried contempt of rolling stock that knows it is beneath everyone's attention. A handful of figures waited on the platform—two women with market baskets, a soldier with his arm in a sling, an old man reading a newspaper dated four days earlier. Edmund walked as he had been told to walk, bored, unhurried, a grain factor with nothing to inspect, and felt Brozek's eyes on his back the whole way, and did not turn around, because turning around was the first thing a guilty man did and the last thing an innocent one thought of.

The platform was longer than it needed to be, and the walk along it gave Edmund time to notice what he did not want to notice: that the yard had too many men in it for the hour, that two of them stood by the water tower in the posture of men who were not waiting for anything, and that a staff car had drawn up beyond the stationmaster's office, its engine ticking, its paint the grey of a gun barrel.

Anselm saw it a half-second after Edmund did. He said nothing. He did not need to. The whole plan contracted, in the space of a breath, to a single instruction Edmund gave himself: *walk, don't run; bored; grain factor; nothing to inspect.*

The man who stepped out from beside the stationmaster's office was not *Feldgendarmerie*. Edmund knew the breed at a glance now, having passed through the hands of three different services in as many weeks. This one wore the field-grey of the invading army but with the collar tabs of a service that did not advertise itself, and he carried his cap under his arm as though the morning were a social occasion, and his gloves were leather so fine they made no sound when he flexed his hands. He was perhaps forty, narrow through the shoulders, with a face that would have been unremarkable in any ministry corridor in any capital in Europe—the face of a man whose profession was to be forgotten—and his eyes were the only memorable thing about him: pale, patient, and quite without hurry, the eyes of a fisherman who has already cast and is willing to wait out the whole day.

"Papers," he said. Not loudly. The word had the quality of something set down on a table between them.

Edmund produced the wallet. The officer took it without touching Edmund's hand, opened it, and read. He read the way Edmund imagined a man reads a will—slowly, twice, with attention to the clauses.

"Amberdale," the officer said. "Edmund Amberdale. Commercial clerk." He looked up, and the pale eyes did their patient work. "Grain?"

"Grain and seed."

"For Vodna."

"There's a shipment."

"There's a war," the officer said mildly. "Shipments have been irregular." He turned a page of the wallet with one gloved fingertip. "You are traveling with a companion."

"A fellow clerk."

"A fellow clerk who is limping." The officer did not look at Anselm, which was worse than looking. "Your name, sir?"

Anselm gave a name. It was not his own; Edmund had never learned what name Anselm traveled under, and understood now that he never would. The officer wrote nothing down. He had no notebook. That, too, was information.

The train stood breathing beside them. The women with the baskets had boarded. The soldier with the sling had boarded. The old man with the old newspaper had boarded. The platform emptied around the three of them the way water empties around a stone, and the guard at the rear of the brake van looked down the platform, saw the collar tabs, and developed a sudden fascination with his own coupling gear.

"You will forgive the delay," the officer said. "It is a habit of my service to be inconvenient at hours when inconvenience is least forgivable." He held the wallet up to the light, angling the seal as Brozek had done, but where Brozek's shrug had dismissed the seal, this man's tilt of the head was interrogating it. "Your pass was issued in the capital."

"Yes."

"Ten days ago."

"Yes."

"A great deal has changed in ten days."

"So I'm told," Edmund said, and heard his own voice do the thing it had been trained to do: flatten, dull, take on the texture of a man discussing freight rates. "I'm told a great deal changes everywhere."

Something moved at the back of the officer's eyes—not amusement, exactly; recognition, perhaps, of a familiar instrument played competently. He closed the wallet. He did not hand it back.

"Amberdale." He said the name as though weighing it on a small internal scale. "Do you know what I collect?"

Edmund said nothing. Silence was a coin he had learned not to spend too early.

"Names," the officer said. "Not the way the police collect them—in files, in cabinets, in triplicate. I collect them the way a jeweler collects stones. I hold them to the light. Most of them, you know what they turn out to be? Glass. Everyone in this country is carrying a name that isn't theirs, or a name that is theirs and wishes it weren't. Glass, glass, glass." He turned the wallet over in his gloved hands. "But every so often—" He stopped. The pale eyes came up, and for the first time they were not patient at all; they were quick, and bright, and entirely awake. "Every so often, a stone."

The whistle blew again, longer, the guard's arm out, the brake van door rattling.

"You're holding my papers," Edmund said.

"I am holding your papers." The officer smiled, and it was a courteous smile, and it was the most frightening thing Edmund had seen in a winter of frightening things, because it was the smile of a man who had already decided something and was now merely arranging the furniture around the decision. "And I will return them, of course. This is a free passage. The general staff has decreed it. Any clerk may travel to Vodna to inspect his grain." He stepped closer—close enough that Edmund could smell the wool of his coat and, beneath it, something like carbolic—and his voice dropped to a register meant for two people and no witnesses. "But I will tell you what I think, Amberdale, since we are being honest with one another, as honest as two men can be on a railway platform in wartime. I think you are not a grain factor. I think your companion is not a clerk. And I think—" the pale eyes flicked, once, with the speed of a tongue testing a wound, to the curve of Edmund's coat front, and back up, and the smile deepened by a single degree— "that you are carrying something more than papers. Something that breathes."

The platform tilted. Edmund felt the world perform its slow revolution, the frost-smoking wagons, the gallows of the signal gantry, the grey sky, all of it wheeling around the fixed point of the officer's finger. His hand, of its own accord, rose an inch toward his chest—and stopped, because stopping it was the only thing left, the last wall between the man and the stone.

And in that held breath—in the exact center of it, in the pause between the officer's sentence and whatever sentence Edmund's face was about to write in answer—the child woke.

* * *

She did not cry at first. That was the mercy and the horror of it: she stirred, and made the small pre-cry sound, the questioning sound, the sound of a person asking the world whether the world was still there—and Edmund's hand completed its journey, and he gathered the sling's edge aside and lifted her against him, out of the warmth and into the cold morning, and her eyes opened.

They were Lise's eyes. They were always Lise's eyes. In the grey light of the platform, in front of the pale-eyed man with the silent gloves, the infant looked up at Edmund with the gaze of a dead woman, and Edmund felt his face do what no training could prevent: it *changed*. He felt it change. He felt the grain factor fall away like a coat dropped from the shoulders, and something else stand in its place—something that could be read by any man whose profession was reading faces, something that said *mine*, said it in letters a child could spell.

The cry came. It was small and furious and utterly without diplomacy, and Edmund did the only thing left in his inventory: he turned her into his chest, her mouth against the wool over his heart, his hand cupping the back of her skull, and he held her there, and the cry became a vibration, a muffled thrumming against his ribs, a second heartbeat arguing with the first.

The officer watched. He had all the time in the world; his train was not going anywhere, and neither, for the moment, was Edmund's. The milk train's guard had begun, very quietly, to close the brake van door. Anselm had not moved at all, which was its own kind of eloquence—a man who had learned in the war that stillness was the only camouflage that never failed.

"A child," the officer said. He said it without surprise, which was somehow the worst thing. He said it the way a man says *ah, there it is* upon finding the object of a long search—not triumph, not yet; something quieter, something that had the shape of satisfaction still in its wrapper. "A child, on a milk platform, at dawn, in a war." He flexed his silent gloves. "You understand my difficulty, Amberdale. My instructions are to let the traffic flow. Clerks, grain, widows, deserters—the general staff is not interested in the small fish; the river is full of them and the nets are for sturgeon. But my instructions also say that I am to know what flows. And a man carrying an infant toward the mountains, with papers that smell of the Foreign Ministry—" he tapped the wallet once, twice, against his gloved palm, "—is not a small fish. A man like that is a *current*. And currents, Amberdale, are what I am paid to chart."

He held out the wallet. Edmund took it, one-handed, the other cradling the child, and the transfer of the small leather weight between them had the formality of a rite, of something being given and something being kept, both at once.

"The train," the officer said, "is departing."

It was. The guard was waving. The women with the baskets were already settled, and the old man's newspaper had lowered to reveal a face that had seen everything and intended to report nothing.

"Go and inspect your grain," the officer said. And then, as Edmund turned, as Anselm limped forward, as the three of them moved toward the brake van with the child's muffled crying still thrumming against Edmund's heart like a signal tapped out in a code only two people in the world could read: "Amberdale."

Edmund stopped. He did not turn all the way. He gave the man his profile, and the pale morning, and the child.

"We will meet again," the officer said. "This country is becoming very small. Soon it will be a single room." He put on his cap, and the brim of it came down, and the eyes went into shadow, and the voice came out from under it, mild as milk. "And when we do, I will not ask you for papers. I will ask you for the price of my silence. I am a reasonable man. I charge reasonable rates. But I always—" the smile again, heard rather than seen, in the pause before the last words— "*always* collect."

The brake van swallowed them. The train gathered itself with a shudder and a complaint of couplings, and the platform slid away—the water tower, the staff car, the grey coat and the silent gloves, the man beneath the cap brim who did not watch them go, because a man who had already collected did not need to watch.

Edmund stood in the doorway of the brake van with his daughter against his chest and the dispatch case against his ribs, and the yard fell away behind them, and the frost smoked off the fields, and Anselm sat down slowly on the bench and put his head back against the slats and closed his eyes, and said, to the ceiling or to God or to no one:

"He knew."

"He knew."

"He let us go." Anselm opened his eyes and looked at Edmund, and there was something in the pianist's face Edmund had not seen before—not fear; arithmetic. The cold arithmetic of a man totaling a bill he already understood would come due. "Men like that don't let things go, Amberdale. They *lend*. And a man who lends—" Anselm's hand rose, briefly, toward the child, and stopped in the air, and fell again to his knee, and closed on it, knuckle-white. "A man who lends always names his interest. We have until he names it. No longer."

The train ran on toward Vodna through the smoking frost, and Edmund held his daughter and did not answer, because there was no answer yet, because the price was still in its wrapper, still unnamed, and because somewhere behind them, in a grey staff car already turning back toward the capital, a man with silent gloves was writing nothing down—writing nothing down, Edmund understood at last, because the ledger he kept was the kind that is never written, the kind that is carried entire in a pair of pale, patient eyes, and it had just grown longer by three names, and one small stone that breathed.
