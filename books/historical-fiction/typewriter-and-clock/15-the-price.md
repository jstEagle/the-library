## 15. The Price

The town was called Brenn, though by the time Edmund reached it he could not have said how many hours the road had taken, or whether the gray light that greeted him at its edge was dawn or merely the sky giving up on the day altogether. Brenn had once been a market town—there were iron hooks above the doorways where geese and hams had hung in peacetime, and a fountain in the square with a stone boy pouring water from an urn into a basin that no longer held water, only leaves and ash—but now it was a throat through which refugees passed, and like any throat it had grown raw.

He came in on foot, as a commercial clerk would, with a sample case and a coat too good for his station but not good enough to be worth robbing. The baby rode against his chest inside the coat, wrapped in the blanket whose hem he had not yet dared to open fully in daylight. She slept. The laudanum from Madame Kessler's bottle was nearly gone; he had rationed it like a miser rationing his last coin, and each time he measured out a drop he felt the shame of it settle another layer deeper into him, like silt in a riverbed. *You sedate her so she will not be heard,* he told himself. *You sedate her because you cannot bear to hear her.* Both things were true, and neither made the spoon lighter.

Brenn had one inn still taking guests—the Adler, or what had been the Adler; someone had pried the iron eagle off the signpost, leaving only four holes in the wood—and Edmund paid for a room with the last of the currency that would still be worth anything by spring. The innkeeper, a woman with forearms like a stevedore's and eyes that had stopped registering faces years ago, took the money without counting it and told him dinner was bread and something that had been soup earlier in the week.

He ate standing at the window of the common room, watching the square. That was habit, not fear—or rather it was fear wearing habit's clothing, which was the only way he permitted himself to feel anything anymore. The square was full of the usual traffic of collapse: carts piled with furniture that would be abandoned within twenty miles, a man selling cigarettes individually from a suitcase lined with velvet, two soldiers of the advancing army leaning against the fountain with the boneless ease of men who knew they would not be shot here. They were not looting. They were not even watching. That was the worst of it. They did not need to watch. The country was coming to them.

The baby stirred against his chest, and he shifted her weight without looking down, the way a man shifts a bag of grain he has carried so long it has become part of his body's map of itself.

"Amberdale."

His name, spoken quietly, in a voice that had rehearsed saying it.

Edmund did not turn at once. He finished the bread, set the crust on the windowsill—for the birds, or for whoever needed it more than he did—and then turned, arranging his face into the mild blankness of a clerk interrupted.

Captain Voss stood three feet away, hands clasped behind his back, in a uniform so clean it looked newly issued, as if the war laundered itself for this man personally. He was perhaps forty-five, with a long, patient face and spectacles that caught the lamplight and gave nothing back. He looked less like an officer than like a schoolmaster who had wandered out of some other century and found this one adequate.

"You have the advantage of me," Edmund said.

"I don't think I do." Voss smiled—not broadly, but with genuine warmth, which was somehow worse. "Edmund Amberdale, courier second class, Foreign Ministry of the Republic of Verlaine. You crossed the rail yard at dawn yesterday with papers that took me eleven minutes to pretend to examine. You have a talent for being unremarkable, Herr Amberdale. It is a rarer gift than people think. May I buy you whatever passes for wine here?"

"There is no wine."

"Then whatever passes for the absence of wine." Voss gestured toward a corner table with a courtesy so complete it seemed almost tender. "I promise you are not under arrest. Arrest is paperwork, and I am on holiday from paperwork. Consider this a conversation between professionals."

*Professionals.* The word landed precisely where Voss wanted it to land, Edmund understood that even as he sat down. Not enemies. Professionals. Men who understood that documents moved, that information had weight and value, that borders were not walls but tollgates. Voss was offering him a shared language before he offered anything else, and the offer was already working, because Edmund felt the pull of it—the old comfort of the trade, the fellowship of men who carried things.

The baby slept on beneath his coat. He kept his breathing slow and shallow, an old trick, making himself a closed room.

Voss sat opposite him and removed his spectacles, polishing them on a handkerchief that appeared, like everything about him, immaculate. Without the glasses his eyes were smaller and sharper, two points of attention in a soft face.

"You're wondering how I found you," he said. "It isn't mysterious. A courier travels light, alone, and toward the border. In a country emptying itself, anyone moving against the current of the roads is either a fool or a functionary, and fools don't walk as straight as you do." He replaced the glasses. "Also, your boots are wrong. A commercial clerk wears his heels down evenly. You wear the outside edge, from standing at doors, waiting to be admitted. Little things, Herr Amberdale. The war is made of little things."

"And what does the war want with me?"

"The war." Voss considered the word as though tasting it. "The war wants nothing. Wars are appetite without a mouth. No—it isn't the war that wants anything. It's men. It's always men." He leaned forward slightly, and lowered his voice into a register of confidence, of conspiracy shared, of two sensible men in a stupid world. "Here is what I know, and please correct me where I'm wrong. You left the capital after the ministries began burning their ledgers. You carry sealed dispatches—official pouches, diplomatic seals, the kind of paper that will be worthless in six weeks when the government that sealed them ceases to exist, but very valuable in the meantime to certain parties who intend to exist quite comfortably afterward. And you are traveling with—" his gaze flicked, briefly, gently, to Edmund's chest, and back up to his eyes, and Edmund felt the floor tilt a degree beneath the table— "considerable discretion. Which suggests the dispatches are not all you carry."

Edmund said nothing. He had learned silence young, in the courier service: silence was not emptiness, it was a wall, and walls could be maintained indefinitely by men who had nothing left to lose except the thing behind them.

Voss waited. He was good at waiting. Then he sighed, and the sigh had real weariness in it, and that was the most dangerous thing yet, because Edmund recognized the weariness. It was his own.

"You think I am going to threaten you," Voss said. "Everyone thinks that. Threats are the first tool everyone reaches for and the last tool that works. A threatened man becomes a stone, and stones can't be reasoned with, only broken, and broken stones tell you nothing except where the pieces fell." He spread his hands on the table—clean hands, unscarred, a clerk's hands, Edmund noted, mirror to his own. "So. No threats. Instead, an accounting. Listen to the arithmetic, and then decide."

He counted on his fingers, unhurried.

"One. Your government is finished. Not defeated—finished. There is a difference, and you of all people know it, because you've been carrying the proof in your pouches since before the armies crossed the last river. Two. Whatever those dispatches contain, delivering them to the border legation accomplishes nothing. The legation staff began burning their files Tuesday. I have the smoke reports. You would be carrying truth to a chimney. Three." He paused, and his voice softened further, became almost kind. "Three. You are not alone anymore. A man alone can afford principles. A man responsible for a child cannot. Principles are a luxury item, Herr Amberdale, and winter is coming."

The room went very quiet around Edmund, or perhaps it was simply that his own blood went quiet. *A child.* Voss had not said it as a threat. He had said it as a fact, laid on the table like a coin, and the gentleness of it was the blade. Every fantasy Edmund had constructed over two days and eighty miles—that the officer at the rail yard had seen nothing, that the muffled cry had been swallowed by the noise of freight cars, that his invisibility had held—collapsed at once, and beneath it he found not panic but a strange, cold clarity. Voss suspected what he carried. But he did not know *where*. If he knew the child was here, under this coat, in this room, there would be soldiers at the door and no conversation at all. Voss was fishing. Courteously. Precisely. With a hook baited with Edmund's own terror.

"He is mistaken," Edmund said, and was pleased at how level his voice came out. "There is no child."

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "Of course not. There is no child, and I saw nothing at the rail yard, and we are two travelers discussing the weather." He let the irony sit between them for exactly as long as it needed to, then withdrew it, folding it away like the handkerchief. "What I am offering is this. The documents. All of them, seals intact—I don't need them opened, opened papers are evidence and evidence requires me to do my job, and I am enjoying my holiday. In exchange: passage. My authority extends through this corridor and the pass beyond it. You would travel under my protection, unquestioned, unsearched, unremarked. And silence. Nothing in any report, ever, about a courier named Amberdale or a clerk who wore his heels down wrong. You would arrive at whatever life you choose to build on the other side with clean paper and no ghosts." He smiled again, the schoolmaster's smile. "That is not a small thing I am offering. Men have committed crimes for less than clean paper."

"And if I refuse?"

"If you refuse, nothing happens today. I am not a bandit." Voss rose, smoothing his coat, and for a moment he stood in the lamplight looking genuinely sorrowful, and Edmund hated him for it, hated how much of it seemed sincere. "If you refuse, I will wish you a safe journey, and I will continue to do my job, which consists of noticing things. And the road ahead is long, and full of checkpoints, and checkpoints are staffed by young men with rifles and no imagination, and imagination is precisely what a man in your situation requires in the officers he meets." He put a card on the table—heavy stock, engraved, absurd in this town of ash and cabbage soup. "I am at the commandant's house until tomorrow evening. After that, the front moves, and I move with it, and my protection moves with me, and the young men with rifles remain."

At the doorway he paused, half-turned, and said—without turning fully, without ceremony, almost to himself:

"They will tell you I am corrupt. Perhaps I am. But consider, Herr Amberdale: corruption is only loyalty to something smaller than a nation. A family. A self. A child." Now he did turn, and behind the spectacles his eyes were tired and human and bottomless. "Every man serves an appetite. Mine happens to be honest about itself. Good evening."

And he was gone, his footsteps precise on the stair, and the door of the inn closed behind him with the softness of a man who never needed to slam anything.

* * *

Edmund did not sleep. He sat on the narrow bed in his rented room with the lamp turned low and the baby awake now against his shoulder, feeding hungrily from the bottle Kessler had pressed into his hands along with the laudanum, her small fists working, her eyes—Lise's eyes, always Lise's eyes—fixed on his face with the absolute, undivided trust of a creature who did not yet know that trust was a currency and that every man at the table was counting.

He turned Voss's arithmetic over and over, the way he'd once turned cipher keys, looking for the flaw.

The terrible thing was that the arithmetic was sound. That was the horror of it, the true horror, deeper than the threat: Voss had not lied to him once. The legation was burning—the smoke reports were consistent with everything Edmund already feared. The dispatches were addressed to a government negotiating its own funeral. Delivering them faithfully would be an act of perfect obedience and perfect uselessness, a courier's virtue performed for an audience of chimneys. And Voss's price was not even cruelty. It was theft, plain theft, the oldest transaction in wartime: papers for passage. Corrupt, yes. But honest in its corruption, transparent as glass, requiring nothing from Edmund but that he become, openly, what the whole machinery of his profession had secretly been all along—a man who carried things for whoever paid.

*He is not an army,* Edmund thought, watching the baby's eyelids sink and rise, sink and rise. *Armies at least believe in something, even if it is only their own momentum. This man believes in nothing except the transaction, and the transaction always wins, because the transaction is the only god that accepts every offering.*

And yet. And yet there had been that moment at the table, when Voss had spoken of a child, and his voice had not hardened but softened, and Edmund had seen—had been meant to see?—something flicker behind the spectacles that was not appetite at all. Something that might have been memory. Everyone carried something. Even Captain Voss. Perhaps especially Captain Voss, who had made a career of collecting what other men carried, and who knew better than anyone the weight of it.

It didn't matter. It must not matter. Sympathy for the wolf was how the wolf got inside.

The baby finished the bottle and sighed, a tiny, astonishingly adult sound of satisfaction, and Edmund held her upright against his shoulder and patted her back as Kessler had shown him, feeling her heartbeat quick and birdlike against his own. Outside, the square had gone dark. Somewhere across the mountains the legation burned, and somewhere behind him the boardinghouse stood with its cradle and its milk and its silence, and somewhere ahead the pass waited with its young men and rifles and no imagination.

Tomorrow evening, Voss had said. After that, the front moves.

Which meant he had one day. One day to decide whether to surrender the truth to a courteous thief or carry it onward through a narrowing corridor of guns, with a daughter asleep against his heart and a blanket hem full of dead woman's secrets sewn against his ribs.

He looked down at her, at the small face slack with milk-drunk sleep, and found himself speaking aloud, barely a breath, the way couriers spoke to sealed pouches they could not open:

"Not him. Whatever else—we are not his cargo."

She slept. And in the morning, when the bombardment came rolling up the valley like weather, driving the town's few remaining souls into cellars and church crypts, Edmund would gather his case and his coat and his secret and go down into the stone dark with the rest of them—carrying, as he now understood he always would, one thing more than he could bear, and bearing it anyway.
