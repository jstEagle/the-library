## 16. A Name for the Child

The church of Saint Aldric had been dead for a century before the war found it. Its congregation had dwindled to nothing by the time of Edmund's grandfather, its roof had gone to moss and jackdaws, its bell had been sold to pay a debt no one remembered—and so when the shells came walking across the valley that night, the town's living churches were full of the living, and this one was left to the dead, and to Edmund.

He came down into the crypt because the stone above it was thickest. That was all. He told himself it was procedure: seek cover, protect the cargo, wait out the barrage. The word *cargo* sat strangely in his mouth now, the way a key sits strangely in a lock it no longer fits. He had been saying it for three days—*the cargo is fed, the cargo sleeps, the cargo needs changing*—and each time he said it he heard, underneath his own voice, another voice entirely, one that did not belong to a courier at all.

The crypt smelled of cold earth and candle grease and the sweet rot of old flowers left on tombs by some faithful hand that still came, once a year perhaps, to remember people who could not be forgotten by anyone else. Slabs of marble lay along the walls like beds in a dormitory, each carved with a name and two dates and, here and there, a line of scripture worn nearly smooth by the thumbs of mourners long dead. In the corner farthest from the stair, someone had stacked wicker chairs against the damp, and Edmund dragged two of them down, set them facing each other, and sat with the bundle of blankets against his chest while the world outside tore itself into pieces.

The bombardment arrived in waves. First the far-off cough of the guns—a sound like a giant clearing its throat, courteous almost, apologetic. Then a silence of four or five seconds in which the whole valley seemed to hold its breath, and in which Edmund held his too, counting without meaning to, the way he counted everything now: seconds between shellfire, ounces of milk, hours of sleep, the small arithmetic of keeping an infant alive in a country being unmade. Then the arrival itself—not one sound but several braided together: the shriek descending, the detonation that you felt in your teeth and your sternum before your ears consented to hear it, the groan of masonry somewhere, the tinkle of glass falling like sleet, and then the dust, which came down through the crypt ceiling in fine gray veils, settling on the marble names, settling on the blanket, settling on the child's eyelashes until she looked, in the candlelight, as though she had been sleeping here for a hundred years among her ancestors.

She slept through it. Most of it. The first night of shelling she had cried until Edmund thought his heart would crack along some seam he hadn't known it had; but somewhere between then and now she had made her accommodation with the war, as children do, as dogs do, as the old do—the way none of the rest of us ever quite manage. Now when the shells walked past she only frowned in her sleep, a small crease between the brows, an editor's frown, Lise's frown, the one she used to give a badly translated clause. Edmund watched that frown come and go and felt the strange vertigo of recognition that never dulled: *I know you. I have known you since before I knew you existed.*

He had not meant to come to this town at all. Father Ivo had rerouted them after the rail yard—after Voss, after the officer's eyes that saw too much and let it go, after the baby woke and cried and Edmund had pressed her mouth, gently, terribly, against his own chest to muffle the sound, feeling her heartbeat hammering against his heart like a second pulse trying to escape. After that they had walked through the hills on smugglers' paths, and the plan had been to skirt the town entirely, but the shelling began at dusk and the roads died and the church stood open, and open doors in wartime are arguments no reasonable man refuses.

So: the crypt. The candles. The dust falling on the names.

Edmund took out his watch—an heirloom habit, though the watch had stopped mattering days ago; time now was measured in feedings and barrages—and held it up to the candle, more for something to do than for any information it gave him. Half past eleven. Somewhere beyond the walls, men he would never meet were aiming guns at streets he would never see, on behalf of governments that had already decided how this would end. The dispatches rode against his ribs in their oilcloth, sewn where no search would think to look, heavier than lead and lighter than air, depending on the hour. And the child slept on his chest, rising and falling with his breath, having no idea—mercifully, permanently, blessedly no idea—that she was the most dangerous thing in the valley.

"Your mother," he said to her, before he knew he was going to say anything at all.

His voice sounded enormous in the crypt. It bounced off the marble and came back to him sounding like someone else's voice, someone who talked to babies in graveyards, someone who had already crossed some line he'd spent thirty years guarding.

"You should know about her," he went on, because once begun, the sentence refused to stop, the way water refuses to stop once the ice has broken. "Not tonight. Tonight you're asleep, and it's a long story, and half of it I only know from letters. But someday. When you can hold a story in your hands without dropping it."

The shells walked closer. One landed near enough that the candle flames lay flat and the dust sifted down in a sudden gray sheet, and Edmund bent over the child instinctively, curving his back around her the way a wall curves around a courtyard, and stayed that way until the echo finished dying in the hills. When he straightened, his neck ached and his heart was going hard, and the baby had not stirred.

*Carry only what can be carried,* he thought. *Feel nothing. Be no one.* The creed, recited nightly for eleven years like a rosary. He turned it over in his mind now and heard, for the first time, what it actually was: not a philosophy. A fear wearing a philosophy's coat. A man who feels nothing cannot be hurt; a man who is no one cannot be found; a man who carries only what can be carried will never have to choose what to drop. It had kept him alive through the purges, through the years of safehouses and assumed names, through the morning he learned what had happened to Lise Marchand and discovered that grief, deferred, does not die—it waits, patient as a creditor, and charges interest.

It had kept him alive. It had not kept him *living*. There was a difference, and the difference was asleep on his chest, breathing in the rhythm of the bombardment as though the guns were merely weather.

* * *

Somewhere above them, part of the town burned. He could smell it seeping down the stair—woodsmoke and something sharper, chemical, modern, the smell of a century ending badly. The crypt's little windows, slits at ground level, glowed orange on one side, and the shadows of the tombs swung slowly with the flicker, so that the dead seemed to shift in their sleep, turning over, murmuring.

Edmund got up, carefully, the way he had learned to get up in the dark without waking her—one hand cradling the head, weight transferred in increments, the whole operation conducted with more tenderness and more skill than he had ever applied to anything—and carried her to the far wall, away from the glow, and sat down again on the cold stone with his back against a slab whose name he could not read in the dimness.

He should sleep. Sleep was procedure. Sleep was fuel. But his mind would not close shop, and so instead he did what he had done every night since the blanket's hem had given up its secrets: he took out the letters.

They lived now inside his coat, in an oilskin packet, next to the dispatches—two cargoes riding side by side over his heart, the republic's crime and a woman's love, and he had stopped pretending to himself that he knew which weighed more. He did not read them by candlelight in a crypt; he knew them by heart, every line, the way couriers know their routes. But he took them out anyway, and held them, and let his thumb find the edge of the third letter, the one that began *My dear E., if you are reading this then I did not manage to be clever, which will surprise no one who knows me—*

Lise Marchand had been clever. She had been clever the way rivers are strong: quietly, constantly, and with a direction that could not be argued with. He had loved her in the two years before the purge, in the way of people who work in the same building and share a language—she translated, he carried, and between the translating and the carrying they had built, without either of them admitting it, a country of two. He remembered her hands on the ministry's dictionaries, ink on the side of her right palm. He remembered the evening she had laughed at his French accent and then, mortified at her own laughter, covered her mouth, and he had thought: *there, that, whatever that is, I want to keep it.* He remembered that he had never told her. Eleven years later he could recite the exact reasons—he was nobody, she was watched, the season was wrong, the war was coming—and every reason was true and every reason was ash. Some things burned refuse to stay ash. They come back as smoke, and smoke gets into everything.

And she had known. That was the wound inside the wound, the pearl inside the oyster. The letters proved it. She had named him—not in the register, not anywhere findable, but in the last document she signed, in the cipher of the note pinned to a blanket in a midnight alley: *Forget the face.* Written in a hand he recognized the way you recognize a voice through a wall. She had chosen him, at the end, knowing the cost of choosing, and had sent him not a plea but an instruction, because even dying she understood what he was: a courier. She had addressed the delivery.

Except that the delivery had eyes. Gray-green eyes, Lise's eyes exactly, with that faint amber ring around the pupil that he had once, across a desk strewn with treaties, mistaken for candlelight.

"Forget the face," he said aloud to the crypt, to the dust, to the dead whose names he couldn't read. "I want you to know I tried. Four days. I gave it four days of professional effort." The baby sighed in her sleep, a small wet sound, entirely indifferent to his confession. "It didn't take."

* * *

The barrage slackened toward midnight, the way storms and bombardments do, the guns tiring or reloading or moving their appetite elsewhere. What replaced it was worse in a different register: the individual sounds of a damaged town—someone calling a name in a voice that expected no answer, a dog barking with the flat persistence of a mechanism, fire doing its patient arithmetic on somebody's house. Edmund sat in the flickering dark and listened to a world rearranging itself and understood, with the calm of complete exhaustion, that he was at a border of his own.

On one side of it stood the man he had built: Edmund Amberdale, courier, a name on papers he could burn in eight seconds, a man designed to pass through history without leaving prints. That man had rules. Rule one: carry only what can be carried. Rule two: feel nothing. Rule three—the rule under all the rules, the foundation stone—*leave no traces.* No letters kept, no favors owed, no names spoken in sleep, no faces remembered past the completion of the job. A trace is a thread, and a thread can be pulled, and a man who is pulled is not a man anymore but luggage.

On the other side of the border stood a father. And a father, he was beginning to understand, was a man made entirely of traces. A father was a trail of bread crumbs leading backward through the years, deliberately dropped, so that someone small could always find her way home. A father was a name given, not assumed. A father was the opposite of everything the creed had ever taught him, and the creed had kept him alive, and the creed had also, he saw now with terrible clarity, been slowly killing the part of him that Lise had loved—the part that covered his mouth when he laughed, that wanted to keep things, that had looked at a translator across a desk and thought *mine*, and said nothing, and regretted it in the grammar of every day since.

The child woke.

Not crying—waking. Her eyes opened in the candlelight, unfocused, finding first the ceiling vaulted with shadow, then his face, and she studied him with the gravity infants bring to the study of faces, as though confirming an appointment. Her fist worked free of the blanket and rose, wavering, and closed around his thumb with a grip that had no strength in it and all the strength in the world.

"Well," Edmund said. His voice cracked on it. He cleared his throat and tried again, and the crypt listened. "Well. Here we are."

Outside, a shell fell far off, a soft thud like a door closing in another wing of the house. Inside, the candle flame stood perfectly straight, as if attention itself had paused.

"I've been calling you 'the cargo,'" he told her. "In my head. It's a professional habit. I'd like to tell you it's a bad habit, but honestly it's the only reason either of us is alive—I don't feel things, I move things, and for eleven years that's been the entire architecture of my soul." The baby blinked at him. Amber ring, gray-green iris. Lise's eyes looking out of a face that was still deciding what face to be. "But you're not cargo. You have never been cargo. Cargo doesn't grab. Cargo doesn't frown at me in your mother's—"

He stopped. The word had arrived ahead of his permission, the way truth arrives, the way shells arrive: suddenly, and then it has always been there.

"—in your mother's way," he finished, barely above a whisper.

He looked down at the marble slab against his back. In the candlelight he could read it now: *ANSELM VAY, 1841–1889, AND MARGIT HIS WIFE.* Below the dates, worn nearly smooth: *Rest in the Lord.* A hundred and fifty years dead, this Anselm, and still carrying his name through the dark, still holding a place, still—by the accident of Edmund leaning against him—keeping watch over a child not his own. Names outlast everything, Edmund thought. Names are the only cargo that survives all borders.

Lise had trusted him with everything at the end. Her evidence, sewn into a hem. Her letters, folded into a lining. Her daughter, pressed into his arms by a stranger's voice in an alley, with an instruction she must have known he would disobey—because she had loved him, or known him, or both, and both were the same thing in the end. *Forget the face,* she had written, and what she meant—the letters made this plain, plain as a bell—was *keep her alive, and the fastest way to keep her alive is to make her no one.* It had been good tradecraft. It had been exactly what he would have advised. And it had asked him to erase the one thing he could not survive erasing.

"There's a rule," he said to the baby. Her grip on his thumb tightened and loosened, tightened and loosened, a tiny tide. "Rule three. Leave no traces. It's a good rule. It's kept me alive through things I'll tell you about when you're older, or possibly never." He swallowed. "But a name isn't a trace. A name is an anchor. And I have been a drifting thing for eleven years, and I am tired, and you—" his voice went again, and this time he let it, "—you deserve an anchor. You deserve better than a man who won't even admit you exist. Your mother deserved better than that too, and I owe her a debt I can only pay in one currency."

The candle guttered, steadied. Dust hung in the light like a slow snowfall falling upward.

"So," Edmund Amberdale said, in a crypt full of strangers' names, in a town that was burning, in a country that had perhaps forty-eight hours left on its clock. "Lise."

He said it once, testing the weight of it, the way a courier tests a seal. Then again, properly, formally, the way names are given.

"Lise. After your mother. Who was braver than anyone I have ever known, including everyone in the histories, and I have carried a great many histories." The baby made a sound—not a word, not yet, but a syllable-shaped acknowledgment, a notarization. "You will grow up with a name that some very dangerous people might someday connect to some very dangerous paper. That's the risk. I've run the numbers, and I find I no longer care. Do you understand? I no longer care. That's what you've done to me in nine days. Eleven years of discipline, undone by a person who weighs less than my dispatch case."

He laughed then—quietly, so as not to wake her further, though she was awake, watching him with her mother's eyes—and the laugh echoed off the tombs and came back sounding like something released rather than something lost.

"Lise Amberdale," he said. And then, because the creed deserved a funeral and funerals require witnesses: "There. It's done. I've left a trace. Let them pull the thread. Let them follow it all the way back to a boardinghouse in the capital and a woman who out-thought an empire. I hope they choke on it."

The child yawned, enormously, unimpressed by ceremony, and burrowed into his shoulder, and was asleep again within the minute—asleep with a name now, though she didn't know it, the way she didn't know the war, the way she didn't know that the man holding her had just, in the space of a candle's burning, ceased to be one kind of man and become another. Edmund sat against the tomb of Anselm Vay and Margit his wife and felt the change settle over him like the dust: fine, total, irreversible. He was still a courier. He would always be a courier; the skills were in the bone now. But the cargo had changed forever, and the destination had changed, and the address on the final delivery was no longer a legation or a ministry or a border post.

It was a girl. It was a lifetime. It was *home*, wherever he could build one.

He banked the candles, tucked the oilskin packet back over his heart, and settled in to sleep sitting up, the practiced sleep of soldiers and fathers, one ear open for the guns.

Near dawn the shelling stopped altogether, and in the ringing quiet he heard footsteps on the crypt stair—slow, uneven, deliberate. A shape filled the doorway, gray against gray, and a voice he knew said softly, "Amberdale. We need to talk about the road east. The passes are closing faster than I hoped—and there are men on them asking questions about a courier with a child."

Father Ivo stood in the candlelight, collarless, coat torn, smelling of smoke and the road, and behind his eyes was news that could not wait for morning.
