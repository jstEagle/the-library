## 17. The Priest's Road

Father Ivo had not worn his collar in four years, but his feet still remembered the roads.

They left Madame Kessler's boardinghouse an hour before dawn, in the blue-black hour when even the shelling paused, as though both armies were ashamed of themselves. Ivo led them down the kitchen stairs and out through the coal cellar, past a hatch he had asked Kessler to install in the spring of '35, when she had complained that her cellar smelled of damp and he had replied that cellars should smell of something. She had understood him then. She had never once said so aloud.

The hatch opened onto a drainage channel that ran behind the tanneries, and the channel ran toward the river, and the river ran away from everything that was falling.

"Mind the third plank," Ivo said, without turning around. Behind him he heard Edmund shift the child higher against his chest, heard the small wet sound of her breathing, heard the courier's boots find the safe plank as if the man had been born to it. They always did. That was the thing about the ones who came through the houses — the frightened ones, the hunted ones, the ones with false names folded into their hatbands — they learned the ground quickly, because the ground was the last thing they trusted.

There had been forty-one of them, over the years. Ivo kept the count the way other men kept rosaries: one bead at a time, moved through the fingers in the dark. A printer from the east quarter whose presses had printed the wrong pamphlet. Two sisters who had worked in the telegraph office and heard too much of what crossed the wires. A boy of sixteen who had struck an officer and would have been shot for less than striking. The widow Brandt and her three children, carried out in a laundry cart on a night when the snow lay deep enough to muffle the wheels.

Forty-one souls, and not one of them had ever been told about any of the others. That was the discipline of it. Not silence imposed from above — Ivo had been relieved of his obedience along with his collar — but silence chosen freely, each keeper of a safehouse knowing only the next door in the chain, the way a relay runner knows only his own stretch of road.

Kessler's boardinghouse had been the fourth house. There had been eleven once. He no longer knew how many remained.

He had never called it resistance. The word was too grand, too martial; it belonged to men with armbands and printing presses and theories. What they had done was smaller and stranger than resistance. It was mercy conducted as infrastructure. A bed that existed for someone. A bowl of soup that appeared without explanation. A name on a register that had been written in a different hand than the one that signed it. The occupying authorities — first the old regime's police, then the new men, then the army now shelling its way toward the capital — had never quite believed such a network could exist, because it had no center, no treasury, no oath. It had only a hundred small decisions made by a hundred ordinary people, each one deniable, each one alone worth nothing, together worth forty-one lives.

Ivo had lost his collar for refusing to read a decree from the pulpit. That was the official version, and it was true as far as it went. But the deeper truth, the one he confessed to no one, was that he had refused because by then the roads had already begun. You could not stand in a pulpit and announce the names of fugitives on Monday after spending Sunday night walking them through the reed beds. Something in a man breaks, or something in a man chooses, and he had chosen, and the Church had shaken the dust of him from its shoes, and he had gone on choosing every night since, collarless, unfrocked, unrepentant.

He glanced back once. Edmund walked with the infant tucked inside his coat, her head just visible below his chin, and the man's face wore the expression Ivo had seen on thirty-nine of the forty-one: the look of someone carrying more than he admits, pretending the weight is ordinary, praying no one asks him to put it down.

*You are not the first,* Ivo thought at him, kindly. *And you were never walking alone, whatever you believe.*

* * *

They rested at midmorning in the ruin of a lime kiln above the river road, a beehive of cracked brick half-swallowed by ivy. Ivo had used it before; there was a loose stone near the base where he had cached bread and a flask, months ago, in the optimistic season when he had still believed the war would pass them by like weather. The bread was gone — mice, or some other traveler on the priest's road — but the flask held, and he passed it to Edmund, who drank sparingly and then, after a pause, tipped a few drops onto his little finger and touched them to the baby's lips.

"She won't take it," Edmund said, when the child turned her face away. "She wants milk."

"There's a farm two valleys over," Ivo said. "A woman named Halina. Her husband is at the front, or was; her cows don't know the difference." He watched the courier absorb this — the geometry of it, another stranger folded into the route, another door that would open. "You'll need to trust me for the next day and a half. Can you?"

Edmund looked at him for a long moment. In the grey light filtering through the kiln's broken crown, his eyes were the color of the river in November, and behind them Ivo saw the calculation running, fast and thorough, the arithmetic of a man who had survived by trusting no one.

"You've done this before," Edmund said finally. It was not a question.

"I've walked people places, yes."

"How many?"

Ivo smiled and took the flask back. "Enough to know that the ones who ask how many are the ones who think they're the only ones." He stoppered it. "You're not the only one, Mr. Amberdale. You never were. The whole country is full of people carrying things they can't set down. That's what a country is, in the end. A road everyone walks together, pretending to walk alone."

Edmund said nothing, but his hand rose unconsciously to cover the small head against his chest, the way a man shields a candle flame, and Ivo thought: *there it is.* The confession without words. The courier had stopped being a courier somewhere between the boardinghouse and the kiln, and didn't know it yet.

* * *

The priest's road, as Ivo thought of it — no map had ever named it — followed the drainage channels and drovers' paths that stitched the hillsides together below the real roads, the ways that shepherds and smugglers and, in the bad years, priests had used to move what needed moving: untaxed brandy, unstamped Bibles, unbaptized infants, unregistered men. It was a road made of other people's neglect. The state had surveyed the highways and ignored these paths for centuries, and so the paths had become the country's honest veins, carrying its actual life beneath the official skin.

He had learned them as a young curate, serving parishes too poor to keep their own horses, walking between villages with communion wine in one saddlebag and letters in the other. Letters! Half the diocese's correspondence had traveled that way, hand to hand, because the postal service was slow and the postmasters were curious. He had carried death notices, marriage dispensations, a bishop's private doubts, a widow's savings sewn into a coat hem. Even then, even in the innocent years, he had been a courier of other people's sealed and secret things.

Everyone was, he reflected, watching Edmund navigate a scree slope with the child balanced against his shoulder. Everyone carried. The soldier carried his orders; the officer carried his ambitions; the minister carried his signatures; the traitor carried his price. The only question worth asking of a man was not *what do you carry* but *for whom*. Ivo had carried for God, once, and then for the fugitives, which he had come to understand was the same assignment issued by a different office.

They walked through the morning along a shelf of path above the river, the water running high and brown with snowmelt, and Ivo talked — not because Edmund encouraged it, but because the road demanded voice, the way long roads do, and because he had decided, somewhere in the dark of the coal cellar, that this man needed to hear certain things.

"The house you stayed in," Ivo said. "Madame Kessler's. How long have you known her?"

"I met her four days ago."

"And you trusted her with the child within two days of that." He let it sit. "Why?"

Edmund was quiet for twenty paces. "She didn't ask questions."

"No," Ivo agreed. "She didn't. Do you know why she doesn't ask questions?" He turned to look at the younger man directly. "Because she spent nine years after the last war not asking questions. Her son came home from that war in a box with a flag on it, and before the box, for three years, she ran a rooming house near the rail station where soldiers on leave could sleep, and drink, and weep, and telephone home, and none of it ever reached their officers. She has been keeping secrets for strangers since before you were shaving, Mr. Amberdale. The not-asking is not indifference. It is the oldest skill she owns."

"I didn't know that."

"Of course you didn't. That's rather the point." Ivo stepped over a fallen birch, its white wood already greying. "You thought you were passing through a place where people happened to be. A boardinghouse. A room. Strangers thrown together by circumstance." He shook his head. "That house is a node. Has been for years. The pianist who plays after curfew — you thought he played for himself? Anselm plays to cover sounds. To give the house a reason for noise. The defrocked priest sleeping in the attic — you thought I was hiding from the Church?" He laughed, short and dry. "I am the Church, Mr. Amberdale, or what's left of it that's worth keeping. We were all placed, or we placed ourselves, and the network runs from the capital to the mountains through kitchens and attics and rooms let by the week, and it has moved forty-one people that I know of, and I only know my piece of it."

Edmund had stopped walking. Below them the river shouldered past between alder banks, and the wind came up the valley smelling of thaw and distant smoke.

"Then I was never—" He stopped again, and Ivo watched him reach for the creed he'd built his life on and find it missing a wall. "I believed I was neutral. Invisible. That was the discipline. Carry only what can be carried. Feel nothing. Be no one. If you're no one, no one uses you."

"Yes," Ivo said gently. "And while you were being no one, a woman in a boardinghouse kept a cradle in her cellar for ten years in case it was needed. And a pianist memorized the guard rotations at the rail yard by listening to drunk sentries talk. And a priest mapped every goat track in three valleys. You were never alone, son. You were surrounded. You simply couldn't see the shape of it, because the shape of mercy looks exactly like ordinariness. That's its camouflage. That's why it survives."

The courier stood very still. Against his chest, the baby stirred and made a small sound, half complaint, half song, and Edmund's hand moved over her automatically, soothing, practiced already, and Ivo saw the exact moment the man stopped arguing with himself.

"Forty-one," Edmund said quietly. "You said forty-one."

"People," Ivo said. "Not packages. I count them as people. It's the only accounting I've ever done that I'm not ashamed of."

* * *

They reached Halina's farm at dusk, when the valley below was blue with woodsmoke and the first stars stood over the ridge like nails holding the sky up. The farmhouse sat low and solid, roof weighted with stones against the mountain wind, and Halina herself came out before they knocked — a broad, weathered woman in a man's coat, who looked at Ivo, then at Edmund, then at the bundle in Edmund's coat, and said only:

"How many days has she had?"

"Since the twelfth," Edmund said. "Mostly sugar water and what Kessler could spare."

Halina clucked like a hen and held out her arms with the authority of a woman who had raised six and buried two, and Edmund — Ivo watched this closely, watched the surrender of it — hesitated only a heartbeat before handing his daughter across the space between them. The baby went into the stranger's arms, blinked at the strange face, and began, with astonishing suddenness, to cry with her whole body, hungry and furious and alive, and Halina laughed and turned for the house, saying something about a cow named Beauty who owed them all a debt.

Edmund stood in the yard with his empty arms hanging at his sides, looking at his own hands as though they had been amputated.

"She'll be fed," Ivo said. "Come inside. There's soup, and there are things you need to see before tomorrow."

Inside, by lamplight, over barley soup thick enough to stand a spoon in, Ivo laid out the route on the scrubbed tabletop, drawing in spilled salt with one blunt finger. The drover's path over the shoulder of Mount Serel. The shepherd's hut at the tree line where they could shelter if the weather turned. The descent to the pass road — and here his finger stopped.

"Here is your third checkpoint. The mountain pass. And here—" he tapped a point below it, "—is the town of Vellmar, where the road narrows. As of yesterday, Vellmar is on the line of advance."

Edmund looked up. "The army's turning south?"

"The army is coming through the pass country," Ivo said. "Which means the shelling will follow the roads, and the roads will follow the valleys, and we will be walking beside all of it for two days." He wiped the salt map away with his palm, erasing the future he'd drawn. "There's a church in Vellmar with a crypt. Old stone, thick walls. If the guns catch us in the open, we make for the crypt. Understand? Whatever happens, the crypt."

Edmund nodded slowly, and Ivo saw him file it away with all the other procedures, dread converted once more into method. But then the courier did something Ivo had not seen him do. He looked toward the back room, where Halina was feeding the child by the stove, humming some tuneless lullaby, and his whole face changed — softened, opened, became briefly the face of a man instead of a function.

"Father," he said. "When this is over. When she's safe. Will you christen her? I know you're not—" he gestured vaguely, "—they took your—"

"They took the collar," Ivo said. "They didn't take the hands." He found, to his own surprise, that his throat was tight. Forty-one souls carried, and not one of them had ever asked him for a sacrament; they had all been in too much of a hurry to be saved in any way but the literal one. "What name?"

"Lise," Edmund said. "After her mother."

Ivo looked at him for a long moment, at the grief and the tenderness and the stubbornness all standing in the man's face at once like three figures in a window, and he thought: *the network holds. It always holds. It is made of exactly this.*

"Then Lise she shall be," he said. "God willing, in a church with its roof still on."

Outside, far to the north, beyond the black ridgeline, the sky flickered — once, twice, a soft orange pulse like heat lightning, too regular for weather. Halina came to the doorway with the baby, fed and drowsy now, her small fist curled against Edmund's coat as he took her back, and all of them stood in the farmyard watching the horizon pulse.

"That's Vellmar," Halina said flatly. "Or the road to it."

Ivo counted the flashes, timed them against the faint delayed thunder, and did his arithmetic, and did not like the answer. The guns were closer tonight than they had been this morning. The road ahead was shrinking, and the time on it with them.

"We leave an hour before dawn," he said. "Earlier than planned. We'll walk in the dark and sleep in the crypt if we must." He put his hand briefly on Edmund's shoulder — the first time he had touched him — and felt through the wool the tension of a man braced for what was coming and resolved to walk into it anyway.

"Sleep now," he said. "All of you. Tomorrow the road gets narrow."

Above the valley, the northern sky pulsed again, patient as a clock, counting down toward the town where the pass began.
