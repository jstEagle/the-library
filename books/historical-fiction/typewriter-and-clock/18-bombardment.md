## 18. Bombardment

The first shell came at dawn, and it did not sound like thunder.

Edmund had heard thunder all his life—thunder over the capital in August, thunder rolling down the river valleys on his courier routes, thunder that announced itself and then passed. This was different. This arrived without warning and without departure. It was a sound with a fist in it, a sound that reached through the floorboards of the presbytery where they had sheltered for three nights and took hold of the bones of the house itself.

He was awake already. He was always awake now, in the hour before light, when Lise's breathing went shallow and quick against his chest and he would lift her to his shoulder and walk the narrow room until she settled again. She was nearly five months old and growing heavier by the week, her fists stronger, her eyes—Lise's eyes, gray-green, watchful even in sleep—opening longer each day onto a world that seemed determined to give her nothing worth watching.

So he felt the second shell before he heard it, a pressure through the stone like a great heart beating under the town.

"Lise," he said softly, though she needed no waking. The whole town was awake. He could hear it through the window: doors, voices, the particular pitch of fear that is not screaming but something worse—the sound of people doing arithmetic with their lives. *Do we run? Do we stay? Which cellar? How long?*

Father Ivo was already in the kitchen when Edmund came down with the child wrapped in the blanket whose hem still held its sewn secret against his own coat lining. The old priest stood at the cold stove as if he might coax fire from it by will alone, his collarless shirt open at the throat, his white hair uncombed. He had not worn the collar since they left Madame Kessler's; he carried it folded in his breast pocket like a letter he was afraid to open.

"You should go today," Ivo said without turning. "Before the road closes. The smugglers' path over the ridge will hold for another day, maybe two. After that—" He gestured vaguely at the ceiling, at the sky beyond it, at the war arriving overhead.

"And you?"

"I am a priest." He said it simply, the way a man says *I am a carpenter* or *I am from the coast*. "This is my parish. It has been my parish since before they took the collar away, and it will be my parish after."

"They took the collar," Edmund said. "The Church released you. You owe them nothing."

Ivo turned then, and in the gray light his face looked carved out of something older than the church around them. "Owed," he said, tasting the word. "You are a courier, yes? You carry things for pay, for orders, for a government." He did not wait for confirmation; he had never asked, which was its own form of knowing. "Tell me—who do you owe?"

Edmund had no answer. He had spent eleven years perfecting the art of owing nothing: no debts, no attachments, no name spoken twice in the same town. And yet here he stood at dawn holding a child who was owed everything, in a defrocked priest's kitchen, waiting out a bombardment because the smugglers' path was watched and the child cried too easily and he could not—could not—run the ridgeline with her wailing into the wind.

The third shell fell closer. The jars on the shelf rattled their small glass teeth.

* * *

They spent the morning in the crypt.

It had been Ivo's idea, delivered with the offhand authority of a man who had hidden fugitives through two wars and one purge: *When the shelling comes close, take the stairs behind the sacristy. There is room below for thirty, though God grant there are never thirty.* The crypt smelled of candle wax and cold stone and centuries of incense soaked so deep into the walls that the air itself seemed consecrated. A dozen townspeople were already there when Edmund descended with Lise—a baker and his wife, an old schoolteacher, three children who regarded the darkness with the solemnity of the very young confronted with an adventure their parents' faces contradicted.

Anselm was not among them. The pianist had taken a fever in the night, his wound—the one he never spoke of, the one Edmund had dressed twice with hands that remembered field dressings from a training he'd been ordered to forget—hot and angry along his side. He lay in the presbytery's back room, sweating through his shirt, murmuring fragments of Chopin to a ceiling he could not see. Ivo had wanted to move him to the crypt too, but the stairs were steep and Anselm's fever made him dead weight, and so they had propped him on pillows and left water within reach and prayed, in whatever private languages each of them kept, that the shells would choose elsewhere.

Lise slept against Edmund's chest in the dark, and he counted her breaths the way other men counted rosaries.

Above them, the world came apart by degrees.

It was strange, he thought, how a bombardment has a grammar. The far shells were punctuation—full stops that ended conversations, ended streets, ended lives he would never learn the names of. The near shells were questions, shrieking down, demanding answers no one had. And between them ran the long commas of the guns themselves, the rolling percussion of batteries somewhere east, patient as typists, working through some list of their own.

The schoolteacher's children played a game with pebbles in the corner, assigning each pebble a family and marching them across a flagstone kingdom. The baker's wife wept silently, without drama, the way women weep in cellars—economically, as if grief were rationed too.

Near noon, a shell struck close enough that dust sifted down through the vaulting like gray snow, and one of the children laughed, delighted, catching it on her tongue. Her mother pulled her close and did not correct her. Some mercies are lies, Edmund thought. He had built a life out of them.

It was in that dust-fall, in that ringing half-silence after the strike, that Father Ivo climbed back down into the crypt. He had gone up an hour before to check on Anselm and to stand at his door and look at his town, and what he brought back with him was not news but a change in his face—a settling, like snow deciding to slide.

"The tenement on Kral Street," he said quietly, to the room, to no one. "It took a hit. The end nearest the tannery. There are people trapped on the upper floors." He looked at the men in the crypt, and his eyes moved past them the way a shepherd counts. "The rescue crews are at the hospital quarter. They will not come for hours."

No one moved. That was the truth of cellars, Edmund thought later—they keep you alive by keeping you still. The baker looked at his wife. The schoolteacher looked at her students. Everyone performed the same arithmetic and arrived at the same answer, and the answer was *someone else*, and no one was ashamed of it, or everyone was, and shame weighed less than shells.

Father Ivo nodded, as if they had answered him aloud. Then he turned and climbed the stairs, unhurried, a man going to fetch something he'd forgotten upstairs.

"Father." Edmund heard his own voice as if it belonged to someone else. "Father Ivo."

But the priest was already through the door, and above them the guns resumed their patient typing, and Edmund stood in the dark with a sleeping child in his arms and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly what was about to happen and exactly how little his understanding would weigh against it.

* * *

He told himself he stayed in the crypt because of Lise.

He told himself this while the afternoon wore on and the shelling slackened to that intermittent, contemptuous pattern armies use when a town has stopped being a target and become a chore. He told himself this while the baker's wife pressed bread into his hand and the schoolteacher offered to hold the baby, both of them kind, both of them complicit in the fiction that kindness and staying were the same thing.

He told himself this until four o'clock, when a boy came stumbling down the crypt stairs—fourteen, maybe, sent by someone, or perhaps simply running from the noise—and said, in the flat voice children use for catastrophes they have not yet been given permission to feel: "The priest. The old priest. They're saying he got people out of the Kral Street tenement, four of them, and then it came down again, and he went back in for the little Ferenc girl, and—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. The grammar of bombardments, Edmund thought, always ends the same way.

He handed Lise to the schoolteacher before he knew he was doing it. Later he would try to reconstruct the decision and find only this: the weight leaving his arms, the sudden terrible lightness, and his feet already on the stairs. Perhaps it was debt. Perhaps it was the thing Ivo had asked him at dawn—*who do you owe?*—arriving, as truths do, late and in disguise. Or perhaps it was simpler and worse: that he had spent eleven years being no one, carrying everything and belonging nowhere, and he had watched an old man belong so completely to a broken street that he walked into its collapse the way other men walk home.

Kral Street was a lesson in what shells actually do. They do not destroy buildings; they undress them. The tenement's façade stood intact at one end, windows staring, absurdly domestic—a curtain still hanging, a geranium pot balanced on a sill over sixty feet of nothing. The other end had simply ceased, folding down into itself like a cake cut away, exposing rooms to the sky: a bedroom with a bed still made, a kitchen with plates stacked to dry, the whole intimate inventory of lives opened to the air like a confession.

Men were digging in the rubble with hands and lengths of pipe. No one shouted anymore; shouting was for the first hour, and the first hour was gone. They dug the way the guns fired—patiently, methodically, working through a list.

They showed him where. Under a slab of flooring that had come down whole, angled like a roof, like a deliberate shelter, like something merciful that only looked like destruction. Four living had come out from under it before the second collapse. The fifth had gone in after a child's voice and had not come out.

Edmund knelt. He did not remember kneeling. His hands found the edge of the slab and the rubble beneath it, and he dug, and the work was wordless and total, and for the first time since the night in the alley behind Madame Kessler's he was not thinking about checkpoints or dispatches or the cipher in the blanket's hem. He was thinking about nothing. Nothing was a mercy. Nothing was almost rest.

They uncovered Father Ivo at dusk.

He was curled around a space no larger than a bread oven, and in the space, unscratched, asleep or insensible, was the Ferenc girl, perhaps three years old, her small fist closed around the button of his shirt. He had covered her with his body and his body had done what bodies do for those they belong to. The beam that killed him had missed her by the width of a prayer.

Someone lifted the girl out and carried her away, and her small voice rose once in protest and faded. Edmund stayed kneeling. He put his hand on the priest's shoulder, on the ruined shirt, and felt beneath the cloth the folded shape at his breast pocket—the collar, carried all these miles, worn by no one, kept like a letter he'd been afraid to open.

* * *

They buried him the next morning, in the hour of gray light before the guns woke, because the living had learned the town's new schedule and mourned inside it.

There was no coffin to spare—carpenters were making coffins all day and could not keep pace—and so they laid him on a door taken from the Kral Street tenement itself, which seemed right to everyone without anyone saying why. Eight men carried him to the small cemetery behind the church, uphill, past gardens stripped bare by looters and past a wall with a prewar advertisement for a brand of soap that promised purity. The words had been there so long they had become part of the town, and now they presided over the procession with an irony no one had the strength to notice.

Edmund carried the collar.

He had taken it from the pocket the night before, sitting alone in the presbytery kitchen where Ivo had stood at dawn and asked his question. The cloth was stiff with dust and something darker. He had meant to wash it and found he could not; some things must be carried as they are, or not at all.

Now, at the graveside, while a neighboring priest—Ivo's old seminary classmate, summoned from a village twelve kilometers away, arriving dusty and weeping—spoke the offices in a voice that cracked on every third word, Edmund stepped forward and unfolded the collar. It was a small thing, a ring of white cloth, absurdly slight for what it had cost. He had expected his hands to shake. They did not. Eleven years of carrying things had taught them steadiness, and it seemed fitting, somehow, that they should spend it here.

He placed the collar in Ivo's folded hands.

"There," he said, not loudly, not for the prayers or the mourners, but for the man himself, wherever the man had gotten to. "You're wearing it again."

The neighboring priest glanced at him—at the collarless stranger with the soldier's bandage roll in his pocket and the courier's stillness—and said nothing, and Edmund understood that the Church, like every institution, knew how to leave certain mercies unexamined.

They filled the grave by hand. Edmund shoveled with the others. The earth came up in clods studded with pale roots, and each stroke of the spade rang against the stones below, and Lise—awake now, held against his chest inside his coat, her head just visible at his collarbone—watched the sky with her mother's eyes as the winter light strengthened over the ridge they would have to cross.

*Neutrality*, he thought, driving the spade a final time. He had believed in it the way he had believed in the creed: carry only what can be carried, feel nothing, be no one. Move through wars untouched because you belong to neither side. But Father Ivo had belonged to a street, and the street had killed him, and the girl lived, and somewhere in that equation Edmund's careful arithmetic had finally failed. Neutrality was not a position. It was a luxury, like the geranium on the sill, like the curtain in the standing window—something that exists only until the day the building comes down, and it always comes down, and what remains afterward is only what you chose to be while you still had the choosing.

He was not neutral. He had not been neutral since midnight in an alley. He was a man carrying a dead woman's daughter, a deserter's confession, and a nation's treason sewn against his skin, and every one of those cargoes had chosen him, and he had said yes.

The thought did not frighten him. That was the strangest part. Standing over the fresh grave with the wind coming off the mountains, he felt lighter than he had in eleven years—as if the priest's death had paid some toll on Edmund's behalf, clearing a road he hadn't known was blocked.

* * *

Anselm was worse.

The fever had climbed through the night, and by the time Edmund returned from the burial the pianist's lips were cracked and gray and his eyes had gone bright with a light that had nothing to do with health. He was trying to sit up.

"The letter," he said, before Edmund was fully through the door. His hand clawed at the blanket, at the pocket of the coat hanging on the chair. "The sealed letter. If I die here, Edmund—if I die here it goes in the ground with me and Marta never knows, and I cannot—I cannot be buried with it, do you understand? It has to arrive. It has to *arrive*."

"It will arrive," Edmund said. He sat on the edge of the bed and eased the man back against the pillows. The letter was safe, sewn where the other secrets were sewn; he did not say so. "You'll deliver it yourself. We leave tonight, when the shelling stops. The path over the ridge."

Anselm laughed, and the laugh became coughing, and the coughing bent him double, and when it passed he lay gasping and grinning like a skull. "Tonight. Yes. Listen to me, planning timetables. I used to plan concert programs this way." His hand found Edmund's wrist and gripped it with surprising strength. "There's something else. Something you need to know before we go, because if I say it after the ridge it will be too late to matter."

"Then say it now."

Outside, the guns had begun again—but farther east, moving on, the war rolling forward like weather, leaving this town to count its dead. Somewhere down the valley a church bell was ringing, though whether for an alarm or an armistice or merely because some other stubborn priest had decided the hour required it, Edmund could not tell.

Anselm pulled him closer. His breath was hot and smelled of iron.

"Voss," he whispered. "The officer at the rail yard. He wasn't there by chance, Edmund. He was asking after a courier. A courier traveling with a child."
