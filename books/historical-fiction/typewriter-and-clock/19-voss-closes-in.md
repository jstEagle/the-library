## 19. Voss Closes In

The column moved the way water moves through a broken pipe—slowly, unevenly, filling every gap in the road that the shelling had left open. Edmund walked at its edge with the child against his chest, wrapped in the blanket whose hem had stopped being a hem and become an archive. Around him the refugees of Verlaine carried what they had decided, in the last hour they had to decide, was worth carrying: a birdcage with no bird, a framed portrait of a grandfather no one living had met, a sewing machine bolted to a handcart, a bundle of winter coats that would be useless by spring and were everything now.

He had learned to read a column the way he once read a timetable. The ones who walked in the middle were the ones with something to lose. The ones at the edges had already lost it and were simply waiting for the road to admit it. Father Ivo was three days in the ground behind them, and Edmund still caught himself turning to speak and finding no collar, no dry humor, no hand on his shoulder steering him away from the worst version of a plan. Grief, he was discovering, was not a weight you set down. It was a weight you learned to distribute.

Lise slept against his collarbone, her breath a small warm metronome against his throat. He had stopped counting her breaths at night and started counting them without deciding to, the way a man counts the bells of a church he is not listening to.

The road bent north along a ridge of grey hills, and from the ridge they could see the valley town of Sarnov, its chimneys still standing, its church spire leaning like a man listening to bad news. Smoke rose from it in three places, thin and orderly, the smoke of occupation rather than destruction. Someone was cooking there. Someone was keeping records. The thought made his hand drift, without his permission, to the inside of his coat, where the dispatches lay sewn against his ribs.

They had left the smugglers' paths two days ago because Father Ivo was dead and the paths died with him, and because a column of four hundred moving people is a river, and rivers do not take side channels. Edmund had argued for splitting off with Anselm's coffin and going alone. Anselm, feverish and lucid by turns, had refused from his stretcher with the only authority left to him, which was the authority of a dying man's wishes.

"They'll be looking for a man alone," Anselm had said, his voice fraying at the edges like old rope. "A man alone with a baby is a story. A man in a column is a statistic. Be a statistic, Edmund. Statistics get waved through. Stories get opened."

It was good advice. It was also, Edmund understood now, standing on the ridge and looking down at the checkpoint that had not been there yesterday, advice with an expiration date.

* * *

The checkpoint sat where the road narrowed between a stone wall and a frozen ditch, and it had not been built so much as assembled: two trucks, a trestle of planks, a stove whose pipe threw woodsmoke across the road in a grey banner, and perhaps thirty soldiers in field grey with the new armbands of the occupation authority. But it was not the soldiers that made the column's murmur die in its throat. It was the staff car parked behind the trucks, mud-splashed, engine ticking as it cooled, and the man standing beside it with his hands clasped behind his back, studying the refugees the way an accountant studies a ledger he already suspects is cooked.

Edmund knew the silhouette before he knew the face. Captain Voss. The counterintelligence officer from the rail yard at dawn, the one whose eyes had rested on a squalling infant for four seconds too long and then, with the courtesy of a man setting a trap rather than springing one, let it go. *For a price to be named later.* Edmund had spent the intervening days telling himself the price would never be named, that Voss had too much territory to police and too little time, that the encounter had been one of ten thousand and had sunk without a trace into the officer's memory.

The staff car told him otherwise. Voss had not stumbled on this column. Voss had come to it.

The line compressed. Edmund let it compress him, let himself become one body among hundreds, and did what the courier's discipline demanded: he made himself smaller than his fear. He adjusted the blanket over Lise's face so that only a tuft of dark hair showed. He slouched. He put a farmer's dullness into his shoulders and a farmer's patience into his feet.

Then he did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since the ridge. The dispatches were sewn into his coat, against his skin, in a lining pocket that had survived two searches already. But two searches had been searches of a man walking alone. This would be a search of a column, and columns were searched differently—swept, sifted, sampled. A good officer did not search four hundred people. A good officer searched the ten people who were trying hardest not to be searched.

And Edmund knew, with the cold clarity that had served him through every checkpoint of his working life, that he was about to be one of the ten. Voss had seen the baby once. Voss was looking for a courier with a child. The child was the signature on the letter, and Edmund was carrying it in plain sight.

There was only one place on the road where suspicion could not follow.

* * *

Anselm was dying. There was no kind way to hold the thought, and Edmund had stopped trying to hold it kindly. The fever had climbed through the morning, and by the time the column halted before the checkpoint the pianist's hands, resting on the blanket over his chest, were performing a tremolo no composer had written. His sister's letter—the sealed confession Edmund had carried since the boardinghouse, the second cargo sewn against his skin—was still there, undelivered, a debt Edmund had promised to discharge in Halst.

The coffin had been built two nights ago by a carpenter in the column who had asked no questions, because carpenters in collapsing countries stop asking questions the way other men stop smoking. It was a plain box of unplaned pine, and it rode on a cart between the sewing machine and the birdcage, and the column had organized itself around it without being asked to, the way water organizes itself around a stone.

Edmund reached the cart as the soldiers began their work at the head of the line, and he stood for a moment with his hands on the coffin's edge, and what he felt was not strategy. That was the thing he could not have explained to Voss or to any officer with a ledger: that the plan arrived after the feeling, the way lightning arrives after the charge. Anselm had carried his confession across half a country to give it to a sister who did not know he had fired on civilians, and the confession was going to travel the last miles in the same box as the man, and no one—not Voss, not the whole apparatus of the new order—was going to open that box. Some cargoes you protected not because of what they were but because of what it would mean to lose them.

He worked quickly, with his back to the cart's side, the blanket-wrapped child transferred to the crook of one arm, his free hand sliding into the lining seam he had opened with a borrowed knife the night before. The dispatches came out in two bundles, oilcloth-wrapped, thin as prayer books. The coffin's false bottom was a plank Anselm himself had loosened in a lucid hour, saying, *I read your face, courier. I know where you'll want it.* Edmund lifted the plank, laid the bundles flat beneath the straw padding that lined the box, and set the plank back so that the seam closed over them like water closing over a stone.

Then, because the plan had arrived and the feeling was still there, he did one thing more. He took Anselm's confession—Anselm's own sealed letter, the one addressed in a dying man's careful hand to *Fräulein Marta Anselm, Halst*—and he placed it on the pianist's chest, beneath his folded hands, where a letter to the dead's family belongs. If the box was opened, let it be opened on a letter that would break a searcher's heart before it broke a case. Let the first thing an officer saw be grief, not evidence.

Lise stirred against his arm and made a sound, half sigh, half complaint, and Edmund pressed his lips to the crown of her head and whispered the only prayer he owned, which was not a prayer at all but a procedure: *Be heavy. Be boring. Be a statistic.*

* * *

The search reached them in the middle of the afternoon, in a fall of light snow that did not decide whether it meant to be weather or not.

Voss ran it the way he did everything, Edmund observed from twenty places back in the line: courteously, thoroughly, and with an economy of motion that was more frightening than shouting would have been. Soldiers moved down the column in pairs, opening bags, lifting false bottoms in carts, running their hands along coat linings with the flat professional touch of men who had done this a thousand times and found things a thousand times. A woman's bundle of letters was confiscated and read on the spot. A man who argued was made to kneel in the snow for an hour, which taught the rest of the column everything it needed to know.

Edmund watched the pairs approach and performed the last discipline he had: he stopped watching. He looked instead at the road, at the ditch, at the leaning spire of Sarnov, and he let his face settle into the particular blankness of a man whose feet hurt and whose opinions extend no further than the next meal. It was a face he had worn through three governments. It fit him like an old coat.

The soldiers reached the cart. One of them, young, chapped, lifted the birdcage and looked into it with the expression of a man who has been surprised by emptiness before. The other walked around the coffin and stopped.

"You. The coffin. Who's in it?"

Edmund turned with the slowness of a tired man. "My brother," he said. It was not even a lie he had prepared; it was simply the nearest true-shaped thing. "A musician. He took a fever on the road."

The soldier looked at the coffin, at Edmund, at the child on his arm. "Open it."

"He's dead," Edmund said, and heard his own voice do something he had not instructed it to do—crack, slightly, on the word. He let the crack stand. There was no need to manufacture what was already there.

"Open it," the soldier said again, but without force now, the way a man repeats an order he hopes will be overruled.

It was overruled. The voice came from behind them, unhurried, pitched to carry no further than it needed to.

"That won't be necessary."

Captain Voss stood at the cart's tail, snow gathering on the shoulders of his greatcoat, his gloved hands still clasped behind his back. He looked at Edmund for a long, level moment—the same look from the rail yard, the one that saw too much—and then he looked at the coffin, and then, last and longest, at the child.

"Dead of what?" Voss asked.

"A fever. It's been going through the column."

"Name?"

"Anselm. He was a pianist." Edmund heard himself add, before the courier in him could stop the father in him: "He was a good one. Nobody will ever know how good."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes—not softness, nothing so usable as softness, but a flicker of the thing that happens when a man who has been reading a ledger all day is shown, against his will, a face. He looked at the coffin again, and Edmund watched him perform the calculation: a dead deserter, a sealed letter, a weeping brother, a pine box in the snow. And beneath the calculation, Edmund could feel the other thing, the thing Voss could not put into his report: the genuine article. The mourning in the column around the cart was not performed. Four hundred people had organized themselves around this box without being asked, and suspicion, which is a fine instrument for detecting lies, has no calibration for the truth when it appears in a shape the truth has no business taking.

Grief was the one cargo no officer ever confiscated, because grief looked the same in everyone's hands, including the guilty.

"Burial detail," Voss said to the soldier. "Behind the wall, past the church. Before dark." And then, to Edmund, in a voice pitched with that terrible courtesy: "You'll want to say the words yourself, I imagine. We'll leave you to it. No one searches a funeral." A pause, thin as a knife's edge. "It's one of the few courtesies left."

He turned to go, then stopped, and Edmund felt the whole afternoon balance on the pivot of that stopped boot.

"The child," Voss said, without turning. "It was smaller, the last time. They do that." And he walked away toward his staff car, and the snow took him the way snow takes everything, gradually and then completely.

* * *

They buried Anselm at dusk, past the leaning church, in ground so cold the gravediggers—two old men from the column and a soldier who volunteered, to everyone's surprise including his own—could only cut the earth to half depth and heap the rest above in a long pale mound.

There was no priest. Father Ivo was three days dead and the church behind them was occupied, its bell rope cut, its door watched. So Edmund said the words himself, the ones he knew and then some he invented, standing at the head of the grave with Lise against his chest and the column's survivors gathered in a rough crescent behind him, their breath rising in the freezing air like the souls of the departed making their own arrangements.

"He played after curfew," Edmund said, because it was the truest thing he had, and the truest things were the only ones he trusted himself to say aloud. "In a house where nobody was supposed to be listening. He played so the rest of us could remember there was a world where the curfew didn't hold. I don't know what he did in the war. I know what he did in that house. He carried music the way the rest of us carried whatever we carried, and he never once set it down, even when it burned him."

The pianist among the lodgers, the deserter, the man who had fired when he should not have fired and could not live unconfessed—Edmund gave him back to the dark as what he had been at midnight in Madame Kessler's parlor, and the column stood with their heads bowed, and the soldier who had volunteered for the digging stood with them, and if any of them wondered what else the coffin might contain, the wondering died there, in the honest cold, among people who had buried too much that winter to audit the dead.

They filled the grave as best the frozen ground allowed. Edmund placed the marker himself: a flat stone from the wall, with ANSELM scratched into it using the point of the carpenter's chisel, and beneath it, because the dead man would have wanted the joke, a single word in the old musical notation—a fermata, the sign for *hold*, for *let it last longer than it should*.

The column moved on in the last light. Edmund walked at its edge with the child asleep against him and his coat, for the first time in three hundred kilometers, empty against his ribs. It should have felt like relief. It felt like an amputation—the phantom weight of the dispatches still pressing where they had lain, the habit of their presence outlasting the fact.

He knew where they were. He knew they were safe, safer than they had ever been, in the one place in Verlaine that Voss's courtesy had declared inviolable. And he knew, with a certainty that settled into him colder than the snow, that the courtesy had not been kindness. *No one searches a funeral. It's one of the few courtesies left.* Voss had let the coffin pass because Voss had not needed to open it. Voss had looked at a courier with a child and a dead brother and a column of mourners, and he had done the arithmetic officers do, and he had decided that the cargo would keep—that a man carrying what Edmund was carrying would eventually have to deliver it, and that a man who delivers can be followed.

The staff car's engine started somewhere behind them in the dark, and Edmund did not turn around. He walked north with the column, toward the border, toward the legation, with a dead man's confession in his pocket and a dead man's cargo in the ground behind him, and he understood that the race had changed shape. It was no longer a matter of getting past checkpoints.

It was a matter of getting somewhere—getting anywhere—before Captain Voss decided the courtesy had run its course.

The road climbed toward the pass, and the snow came down harder, and far behind them, patient as winter, the headlights of the staff car came on.
