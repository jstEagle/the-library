## 33. The Typewriter

The typewriter arrived on a Tuesday in March, carried up the three flights of the Halst house in a canvas satchel that had once, Ruth Aldervik told him, held a wireless set through two wars and one revolution.

"It weighs less than the wireless," she said, setting it on the kitchen table with the care of a woman handling something that might still detonate. "Though some mornings I wondered."

It was an Underwood, black as a beetle's back, its round keys worn to the metal at E and T and A—the letters, Edmund thought, that any language leans on hardest. The ribbon was dry. The carriage stuck a little at the right margin, as if reluctant to go too far from home. He ran his thumb along the typebars and felt forty years of other people's sentences sleeping in the mechanism.

"I can't accept this," he said, because courtesy required it.

"You can and you will." Ruth unwound her scarf but did not sit; she never sat in his kitchen, as if she considered it a place she was only passing through, a border post of someone else's life. "I've bought myself a new one. Noiseless. The old girl clatters like a telegraph office, and my editor complains he can hear me thinking from the next room."

"That's not a reason."

"No," she agreed. "The reason is that you've been circling this house for four months like a man pacing a cell, and I'm tired of watching you carry everything in your hands and put none of it down." She looked at him the way she looked at sources across a table—levelly, without mercy, without cruelty either. "You were there, Edmund. The couriers. The boardinghouses. Madame Kessler's milk. Father Ivo's roads. Anselm's letter. The things no commission of inquiry will ever ask about because they were too small to matter and too true to survive. You know what happens to those things if no one writes them down."

"They become ash," he said. "Like everything else the Ministry fed into the furnace."

"Or they become rumor. Which is worse than ash—ash at least is honest." She pulled her scarf back on, having apparently decided the visit would not outlast the typewriter's unpacking. "Write what you know. Not what you think historians want, not what the exiles in the cafés argue about over their coffee. What you know. What you carried." At the door she paused, hand on the latch, and for a moment the correspondent's armor slipped and he saw the woman underneath, tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "I kept a diary through the capitulation, you know. Eleven notebooks. And do you know what I wrote most often? *Today nothing happened.* Because the things that happened were all small—a woman leaving milk outside a door, a priest walking a stranger along a goat path—and I had been trained to believe small things weren't news." She opened the door onto the gray light of the street. "I was wrong. Prove me wrong more thoroughly."

Then she was gone, and the typewriter sat on the table between the bread tin and the clock, gleaming dully, patient as a confessor.

* * *

He did not touch it for six days.

This surprised him. He had expected, if anything, the opposite—that the machine would pull at him the way an unopened pouch once had, all those years of sealed flaps and wax stamps, the itch of cargo waiting to move. But the Underwood sat in the corner of the sitting room where he'd placed it, beneath the window where the light came down thin and silver in the afternoons, and he found himself avoiding the room altogether, finding reasons to take the long way to the kitchen, to check the meter, to stand in the hall listening to Lise in the yard below, counting her jumps against the cobbles in French, then German, then the English she was learning from the neighbor children with the ruthless speed of the young.

On the sixth day she came upstairs while he was pretending to read, climbed onto the chair beside him, and studied the machine with the grave attention she gave to all machinery.

"What is it?"

"A typewriter. It writes words."

"Why does it have so many keys?"

"So it can say everything."

She considered this. Then: "Can it say my name?"

"Yes," he said. "It can say your name."

"Say it, then."

And because he could refuse that child nothing that mattered, he rolled a blank sheet into the platen—he had bought paper a week ago without admitting to himself why, a ream of it, wrapped in brown paper, hidden in the wardrobe like contraband—and typed, slowly, hunting each letter the way a man hunts for a word in a language he half remembers:

*Lise.*

She leaned close and sounded it out, her finger under each letter, and when she reached the end she looked up at him with an expression he could not name and has never since been able to forget—the expression of a person meeting herself for the first time in a new medium, ink instead of breath.

"That's me," she said. "That's me in the machine."

"That's you," he said.

"Put it somewhere safe," she instructed, and went back downstairs to her rope and her cobbles, and Edmund sat alone with the sheet of paper, five letters long, and understood that Ruth had known exactly what she was doing, and that the machine had already done its work before he ever touched a key.

The page did not go into the fire. It went into the drawer of the sideboard, flat, face up, where he could see it every time he opened the drawer for the good spoons. A first delivery. Addressed to no one. Signed with a name that was true.

* * *

He began at night, after Lise was asleep, in the hour when the house made its small sounds—the settling of beams, the tick of the unsynchronized clock he kept deliberately wrong, running eleven minutes fast so that it belonged to no timetable but its own. He learned the machine's temper first, as he had once learned the tempers of border guards: which key struck heavy, how the carriage hesitated at the margin, the particular way the ribbon advanced or refused to. A courier learns his routes. A writer, he discovered, learns his instrument, and it is the same discipline wearing different clothes—attention, patience, the willingness to go back and correct.

The first night he produced three lines and threw them away.

The second night, seven lines, thrown away.

The third night he stopped trying to begin at the beginning—with the capital, the winter, the orders—and began instead where the truth had started, which was a knock at midnight and a bundle in his arms and a note in handwriting he had kissed once, years before, in a stairwell that smelled of rain and carbon paper.

*The note said: Forget the face.*

His fingers stopped above the keys. In the silence he could hear the clock insisting on its own private hour, and the wind coming off the harbor, and above it all, faintly, the memory of a woman's voice saying his name the way she said it—*Edmund*, two syllables, the second one always softer, as if she were setting it down carefully rather than dropping it.

He typed until two in the morning. When he finished, the page held neither literature nor history, only testimony, plain as a customs declaration: what he had seen, what he had carried, whom he had failed. He read it standing at the window with the harbor lights blurred behind the glass, and he did not throw it away.

In the morning it went into the drawer, on top of *Lise*.

* * *

The pages accumulated the way snow accumulates in a country used to snow—steadily, without drama, until one morning the world is simply different. He wrote of Madame Kessler and her son who had not come back from the last war, and of the cradle she left outside his door without a word. He wrote of Father Ivo's collar folded in a drawer like a flag retired from service, and of the smugglers' paths the defrocked priest had walked for thirty years, carrying fugitives the way other men carried grudges. He wrote of Anselm at the piano after curfew, playing Chopin at a volume calibrated to pass beneath the ears of patrolling gendarmes, and of the confession sewn into its envelope, and of the sister in Halst who wept and said, *he was the gentlest of the four.*

Some nights the words came easily, and he distrusted them, remembering how easily documents had flowed through his hands toward destinations that turned out to be crimes. Some nights every sentence had to be pried loose like a stone from frozen ground, and those were the nights, he came to understand, when he was telling the truth.

Ruth read nothing as he went. That was their agreement—she had proposed it and he had accepted with relief. "A witness needs a locked room," she said. "Every reader changes the sentence being written. Even a good reader. Especially me." So the manuscript grew in the drawer, in a folder he had bought from the stationer on the corner, and he carried it nowhere, showed it to no one, and found that this, too, was a strange new discipline: cargo that stayed put. Cargo whose whole purpose was to wait.

Lise knew, of course. Children know everything except the things adults arrange for them not to know, and even those they know by their shape. She would come into the sitting room in the evenings, climb onto the arm of his chair, and watch the typebars flicker like a flock of small metallic birds.

"Is it a story?" she asked one night.

"It's a true story."

"About the war?"

"About the people in it. About a boardinghouse full of strangers who became—" He paused, hunting the word, and she waited with the terrible patience of the very young. "Who became a family," he finished. "For a while. On the road."

"Am I in it?"

"You're in the middle of it," he said. "Where the knocking is."

She thought about this, chin on her fists. "Will I be able to read it someday?"

"Someday."

"Good." She slid off the arm of the chair, kissed the top of his head with the offhand gravity of a queen bestowing a favor, and paused at the door. "Don't lose it," she said. "Important papers shouldn't get lost. You told me."

He had told her that, in the way parents tell children things that are really instructions to themselves. He sat a long time after she was gone, the carriage of the Underwood resting at the margin, refusing to go further, and he thought: *she is right, and she does not know how right she is, and one day she will read these pages and find that the lost thing was never the papers—it was the man who carried them, and this book is the receipt.*

* * *

In April the plum tree in the yard flowered, and Lise brought him a fallen branch and demanded it be put in water, and he wrote the chapter about the river bridge with the blossoms browning in a jam jar beside the typewriter, shedding petals onto his drafts like slow confetti. In May the harbor filled with herring boats and the smell of tar, and he wrote about the rail yard at dawn, and Captain Voss's eyes that saw too much and let it go—for a price to be named later—and found, to his surprise, that he could write Voss fairly, could give the man his courtesy and his appetite and even, at the end, his crack of doubt. It cost him something to do it. He suspected that was the point.

In June, Ruth came to dinner for the first time—the first time she had crossed the threshold of his kitchen as a guest rather than a courier of machines and opinions—and afterward, while Lise slept and the clock insisted on its wrong hour, she asked, quietly, "How far have you got?"

"To the mountain," he said. "To the burning of the papers."

"And after that?"

"After that it gets harder. Everything after the border is happiness, and I don't know how to write happiness. I was trained to write reports. Reports end at the destination."

Ruth turned her wineglass by its stem, watching the lamplight swim in it. "My editor says every correspondent secretly believes the story ends when the dispatch is filed," she said. "It doesn't, of course. The story ends when somebody reads it and is changed. Sometimes that takes twenty years. Sometimes it takes a child asking a question." She looked up. "Finish it, Edmund. Not for the commissions or the archives. Finish it because somewhere in Verlaine—or whatever they call that country now—there's another boardinghouse, another woman leaving milk outside a door, another courier deciding whether to feel nothing. They'll never hear of Kessler or Ivo from any official history. Official histories are written by the men who signed the ledgers. Someone has to write the other ledger."

After she left he sat at the machine until dawn, and when the sky over the harbor went the color of pewter, he typed the last line of the chapter about the ridge line, where a nameless man had stood in the storm and fed his identity to the wind one page at a time, and had felt, for the first time in his adult life, weightless.

Then he pulled the sheet from the platen, read it once, and placed it in the folder with the others.

The folder was nearly full now. Nearly—but not quite. Because there remained one document he had not yet dared to set down, the letter that had arrived from the capital the previous autumn, the clerk's careful account of a translator's final hours, and the sentence in it that he had read perhaps a hundred times and still could not type without his hands going still on the keys: *she named you, Herr Amberdale. On the last document she signed, she named you.*

He looked at the folder. He looked at the machine, patient in the lamplight, its worn keys waiting for the letters that hurt the most.

Outside, the clock struck four—eleven minutes early, faithful only to itself—and Edmund Amberdale rolled in a fresh sheet of paper, and began.
