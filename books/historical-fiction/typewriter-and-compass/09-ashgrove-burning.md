## 9. Ashgrove Burning

The knock came at half past three in the morning, and it was not a knock so much as an argument the door was losing.

I had been cleaning the last of the true ledger's type — forty pages of names, each letter cut by my own hand over eleven sleepless nights, my thumbs split and wrapped in rag — when the first boot hit the wood. Then another. Then the flat, patient voice of a man who had never once been kept waiting by anything he could kick.

"Ashgrove. Open in the name of the Provisional Commission."

Ilse was across the room with her coat already on, which told me she'd heard them before I had. She always did. Twenty years of listening for boots, I supposed, made a person quick.

"The ledger?" she asked.

"Sewn." I touched the lump at her breast where it rode against her heart like a second heartbeat. "You?"

"Orders in my pocket. Forged ones. They'll hold a door for ten minutes if they're stupid, five if they're not."

"And if they're neither?"

"Then we go out the way paper does," she said. "Up."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the thing you have spent your whole life protecting: when the moment comes to lose it, you will be surprised how much of you is already packed.

My father built this shop before I could walk. He cut his first type at fourteen, apprenticed to a man who beat him for crooked serifs, and he swore — I heard him swear it, sitting on this very floor with me asleep against his knee — that no letter leaving Ashgrove hands would ever be anything but true. Every press in this room he tuned himself. Every tray of type, every brass rule, every worn wooden case with its compartments like little beds where the alphabet slept: his. Ours. Mine, after, though the city believed otherwise. The city believed a son ran this shop, a quiet young master who wore gloves indoors and never drank with the harbor men and signed his name in a hand that trembled only slightly more than his father's had.

The city was wrong about a great many things. It was about to be corrected.

They broke the door on the fourth blow. Six men came through it with lanterns and rifles and the particular confidence of people who have been told they are the good guys tonight. Behind them, unhurried, came Colonel Brandt — Ilse's old commanding officer, the one whose amnesty offer she had refused three days ago in a room that smelled of ink and lies. He looked around my shop the way a tax assessor looks at a rich widow.

"You've been busy," he said.

"I'm a printer," I said. "Busy is the trade."

He nodded toward the front room, where the requisitioned presses still stood loaded with the government's proclamations — the false census abstracts, the tidy numbers, the erasures dressed up as arithmetic. "Burn those first," he told his men. "Then the cases. The commission wants pre-revolution records gone by morning, and I intend to be thorough."

One of the soldiers tipped a lantern over a stack of the false ledgers almost gently, the way you'd lay down a drunk friend. The flame took the paper with a sound like a long inhale.

And here is the thing I did not expect, standing there in my father's shop while it began to die: relief. Sharp and shameful and clean. Because those burning pages were lies — beautiful, well-set lies, printed on my presses in my father's tradition — and fire is the one editor even tyrants fear. Let them burn their own falsehoods. Let the smoke carry the arithmetic of the erased out over Vellamare and choke the gulls with it.

But then they went for the type cases.

"No—" The word left me before I could weigh it, and Brandt turned, interested, the way a cat turns.

"The cases too," he repeated, watching me. "Lead melts, Master Ashgrove. All that careful cutting. Gone to slag by breakfast."

Ilse moved then — not toward him, but toward the door, unfolding her forged orders with the bored authority of a woman who has carried real ones. "Commission business," she said loudly. "This building is scheduled for archive seizure, not destruction. You're burning inventory the historians' manifest already claims. Do you want your name on that discrepancy, Colonel? Because I promise you it will be legible. We do print things clearly in this city."

It bought us the confusion she'd promised. Five minutes if they were stupid; these were maybe threes. But threes is enough when you've spent eleven nights memorizing the distance from the composing stone to the loft ladder.

"Up," I breathed, and we went.

* * *

The print loft was my father's sanctuary and mine: low-beamed, smelling of linseed and dust, hung with drying proofs like laundry of ghosts. Below us the soldiers fed the fire, and orange light climbed through the floorboards in stripes, painting the loft in the colors of a furnace door.

We crossed it fast. Ilse went first along the beam-walk toward the loading window — the tall one above the alley where bundles of finished work used to be lowered to carts. She got the sash up two feet before it stuck, groaned, and put her shoulder into it with a curse in survey-corps border dialect that would have gotten her shot twice over back home.

"It won't—"

"It will." She braced both boots against the frame. "Everything sticks. Everything jams. That's the whole history of printing, Felix, and the whole history of me. Things stick until someone puts their whole weight on them."

The window gave all at once and she nearly went through it into the alley, catching herself on the sill with the ledger's corner digging into her ribs. I scrambled after her along the beam, and below me, through the gaps in the boards, I saw it.

A soldier had reached the type cases.

He didn't know what he was holding. Of course he didn't. To him it was a heavy box of gray metal, and he tipped it over the flames the way boys tip beehives, and my father's alphabet — every lowercase g he'd ever cut with that little curled tail because his own handwriting curled, every capital A sharpened over thirty years until it looked like a mountain with ambitions — went into the fire with a hiss and a soft silver run of melting lead that glowed like something alive being born instead of killed.

I stopped. I don't remember deciding to stop. My body simply refused the window the way a tree refuses the axe for one more second.

"Felix." Ilse's hand closed on my wrist. Not pulling. Just holding. "Look at me."

"I'm looking."

"Not at it. At me." Her face was smoke-striped and terrible and steady. "That's lead. What he's melting is lead. Your father isn't in the lead. He never was. He's in forty pages sewn against my chest, and in your hands, and in whatever you cut next, wherever you cut it. Shops burn. That's why we print more than one copy of everything worth keeping. That's the entire point of the trade, love. Fire is why the press exists."

Below us, the false ledgers burned on, and the soldiers cheered quietly, professionally, men pleased with a night's work.

And I understood — standing in the heat with my father's craft literally dripping off the rafters — that I could grieve later, or I could grieve now, but either way I was going to grieve walking. So I did the thing he would have done. I took one last look around the loft — the proofs hanging, the beam-walk, the little window where I'd sat as a girl watching ships and pretending to be their future owner — and I said goodbye to it silently, in the language of people who keep accounts:

*Paid in full.*

Then I went through the window after Ilse, and I did not look back.

I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the family trade now: I did not look back partly out of courage and partly because looking back is how people fall off buildings. Both things can be true. Most true things travel in pairs; ask any census.

* * *

The alley took us north between walls that radiated heat like the flanks of animals. Behind us the windows of the shop lit orange one by one, floor by floor, my father's life going up in the order he'd built it — ground first, then the workroom, then, with a soft whump of escaping years, the loft itself. Glass went somewhere above us. I heard the big press fall through weakened joists with a boom I felt in my teeth, and my eyes stung, and I told myself it was the smoke, which was even true.

Ilse walked fast, coat buttoned to the throat, one hand pressed flat over the ledger as though holding a wound closed. Neither of us spoke until the harbor quarter opened ahead of us — masts black against a graying sky, the historians' ship already rigged and restless at her moorings, lamps moving on her deck like fireflies with paperwork.

"They'll report the printer dead in the fire," Ilse said at last. "Brandt will make sure of it. 'Master Ashgrove, tragically lost.' Very tidy. Very printable."

"Good," I said.

She glanced at me. "Good?"

"He wanted a son who inherited the shop. Instead he gets a daughter who inherited the truth and burned the shop herself, near enough. I'd call that an improvement in the estate." My voice cracked on the last word, and I let it. There was no one in that alley to perform steadiness for except the woman who had already seen me sign my own name in wet ink at three in the morning, crying, and had said nothing except *it's a good hand*.

She stopped walking. In the light of the burning shop behind us, reflected red off the harbor water, she looked at me the way you look at a page you have just realized is the original, not the copy.

"When this ship sails," she said, "there's no Vantor in the survey corps to go back to. No Ashgrove shop to come back to. There's just—" she touched the ledger through her coat "—this, and us, and whatever port takes us."

"I know."

"Say it anyway."

I watched the historians' ship strain at her lines, impatient as a held breath. Somewhere behind us my father's presses were collapsing into slag, and somewhere ahead of us a gangplank waited with a passenger list on a clipboard, and a line for signatures, and a blank space exactly the width of a full name.

"Ilse," I said. "I'm not running from the fire. I'm delivering the mail."

She laughed — a short, wrecked, wonderful sound — and took my hand, and we came down out of the alleys onto the quay as the first true light of morning found the water, and the tide began to turn.
