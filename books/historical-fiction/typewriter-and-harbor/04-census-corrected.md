## 4. Census, Corrected

I have been lied to by better men than the census taker, and I have lied to better men myself, so when Mr. Pell returned to Clifton on a grey Thursday with his leather satchel and his air of mild, bureaucratic menace, I received him in the parlour with tea and the particular warmth one extends to a wasp that has already stung once.

"Mrs. Marsh." He settled into the chair across from me as though he owned it, or intended to within the quarter. "Thank you for receiving me again."

"The pleasure," I said, "is entirely yours."

He smiled at that — a small, dry smile, the smile of a man who has been insulted politely in a hundred parlours and has learned to collect insults like stamps. He opened his ledger. I watched his hands. A man's hands tell you what his mouth will not: these were careful hands, ink-stained at the second finger, and they turned the pages of that ledger with the tenderness of a priest handling scripture.

"You'll recall," he said, "that when I called in March, certain entries required clarification."

"I recall you were tedious."

"And now the schedule requires me to finalize the household returns for the night of April the second." He wetted his pencil with his tongue, an unlovely habit. "So let us be thorough. Who slept under this roof on census night?"

"You did not sleep under this roof, Mr. Pell, and yet here you are, asking questions. The census is evidently a flexible instrument."

"The census," he said, "records who *usually* resided at the address. Persons temporarily absent are noted. Persons present are noted. It is not flexible, Mrs. Marsh. It is merely honest, which some find difficult to distinguish."

There it was. The first blade, slipped between the ribs of civility where no one looks for it. I poured him more tea because a man drinking cannot write.

* * *

Let me say something about lying, since I am about to do a great deal of it and I believe a woman should know her own tools.

Lying is not the opposite of truth. Lying is architecture. You take the true materials available to you — real names, real ages, real rooms — and you build something adjacent to fact, close enough that a tired inspector's eye slides off it like rain off slate. The amateur liar invents; the professional *omits*, *rearranges*, *redirects*. Amateurs are caught by their imaginations. Professionals are caught only by other professionals, and there were, in Bristol in 1912, perhaps four people capable of catching me, and two of them were dead.

Mr. Pell was not one of them. But he was persistent, and persistence is the raw ore of competence, and I had learned never to underestimate a man merely because he was dull.

"Very well," I said. "The household. Myself, Adeline Marsh, widow, forty-six years of age. My occupation — " I let him write *independent means*, which was true in the way that a cracked bell is still a bell — "and my daughter's former governess, Miss Vesper Iversen, who serves as my driver and secretary. She was present on census night. She is twenty-four."

"She was born where?"

"Born where?" I raised my eyebrows as though the question were faintly indecent. "Goodness. Does the Crown require the womb as well as the name?"

"It requires place of birth, Mrs. Marsh."

"Bath," I said, without hesitation, because Vesper had told me Bath and I had committed it to memory along with her other fictions, the way one memorizes the combination of a safe one intends to use. "Her family were in service. Respectable people. She has references if the Crown wishes to interview them."

Something flickered behind his spectacles. Not suspicion exactly — appetite. The look of a man who has found a thread and is deciding whether it is worth pulling, knowing that threads sometimes unravel entire garments, and garments sometimes belong to important people.

"And your son," said Mr. Pell.

The clock in the hall ticked. Outside, a cart went by, and somewhere below in the kitchen Vesper was washing china, and I could hear the small precise clink of cup against saucer, and I thought: *she is listening*. Of course she was listening. She read letters before she delivered them; she heard conversations through doors; she was a collector of other people's truths the way some women collected spoons. I had known this about her for months and permitted it, because a servant who knows too much is dangerous but a servant who knows too much and stays anyway is *invested*, and investment is the strongest glue in the world.

But this — this she must not hear whole.

"My son," I repeated pleasantly, "died. In infancy. 1889. There is a stone in Arnos Vale; you may take a rubbing if the Crown requires poetry."

Mr. Pell consulted his papers. "The register of the parish of Saint — "

"Is mistaken, or I am, and I assure you I am not. Mothers are rarely confused on the subject." I smiled. "Would you like more tea?"

He wanted more tea. Men almost always want more tea. And while he drank it I performed the small theatre of grief — the pause, the glance toward the window, the hand briefly at my throat — and I want to be honest here, in the privacy of my own head where honesty costs nothing: the grief was real, but its deployment was strategy, and I no longer knew, after all these years, which part of me was the widow and which part was the performance of one. That is the price of a life built on omissions. Eventually you omit things from yourself.

* * *

My son's name was Edmund. He is thirty-one years old this spring, though he has the mind of a boy in many respects and the gentleness of a saint in all of them, and he lives at Hillcrest Asylum outside Bath, where I have paid for his keep every month for three decades with money that appears in no ledger anywhere, least of all the one in my typewriter case.

I visited him in February. I go four times a year, always alone, always telling the committee I am visiting my late husband's cousins in Somerset, and every time I sit in that dayroom with its smell of boiled vegetables and carbolic soap, Edmund takes my hands and says, "Hullo, Mother. Have you come to take me home?" and every time I say, "Not today, my darling," and every time he nods and forgives me instantly, because forgiveness is the one thing they never managed to institutionalize out of him.

A suffragist leader with a lunatic son. Do you see? One whisper — one *lunatic mother, hereditary taint* — and thirty years of work becomes a cautionary tale. They would not print my speeches; they would print my son's diagnosis. The movement would survive me, but the argument I embody — that women are fit for citizenship, fit for judgement, fit for power — would be handed a wound to pour itself into. So Edmund stayed hidden, and the hiding became a wall, and the wall became a house, and I lived in it so long I mistook it for a self.

This is what I understood, sitting across from Mr. Pell while he wrote *son, deceased, 1889* in his careful hand: the census was never about Vesper Iversen's invented sister. That had been the hook. This was the line. Someone had given him my household to pull apart, name by name, and there was only one name in it worth the pulling.

Which meant someone knew. Or suspected. Or had simply cast a net into Bristol waters and caught my shape in it without knowing what they'd caught.

I intended to find out which.

* * *

"Now," said Mr. Pell, licking his pencil again — I began to understand the tongue as an instrument of state — "the young woman. Miss Iversen. She gave her own return in March, I believe, when I called on the servants' matters."

"She did."

"There was a sister listed. On an earlier register. An Eleanor Iversen." He looked up. "She does not appear now."

"Eleanor died," I said smoothly. "Influenza. Terrible winter. You may verify the burial."

"I intend to."

"Of course you do." I refilled his cup to the brim, an act that was either hospitality or drowning. "Mr. Pell, may I speak frankly?"

"I'd be honoured."

"You are being used." I set down the pot and folded my hands. "You are a diligent man doing a diligent job, and someone has fed you a list of questions dressed up as routine inquiries, and you are too intelligent not to wonder why a driver's dead sister merits a second visit to a private house in Clifton. Ask yourself who benefits. Because it is not the Crown, and it is not the census, and it is certainly not Miss Iversen, whose only crime is that she drives a motorcar for a woman the newspapers enjoy hating."

He sat very still. I watched the thought move through him like weather.

"And who," he said finally, "would benefit, Mrs. Marsh?"

"If I knew that, sir, I should be the one holding the satchel." I stood, which ended interviews; standing is the most eloquent verb in the English language. "You will find my household in order, my answers consistent, and my patience exhausted. Good afternoon."

At the door he paused. "For what it's worth, Mrs. Marsh — I don't care for being used either."

"No," I agreed. "Nobody does. It's practically the one thing we have in common, you and I."

He left. I listened to his boots on the gravel until the gate clicked, and then I stood in the parlour with the teacups and the cold hearth and did what I always did after performances: I counted the exits.

* * *

Vesper came in to clear the tray. She moved quietly, that girl — quiet the way water is quiet, all that motion underneath. She gathered cups with her eyes down, and I watched her, and I thought about how much she knew and how little I knew about how much she knew, which is the arithmetic that keeps employers awake.

"He asked about Edmund," she said.

Not a question. Her back was to me, her hands steady on the tray.

"He asked about a son. I informed him my son died in infancy."

"Yes." She turned. Her face was composed, but composition is not the same as blankness; hers was composed the way a letter is composed, everything chosen. "And the sister. Eleanor. You told him she died."

"Influenza. The winter of 'ninety-one. There will need to be a grave, eventually, if he checks. I'll attend to it."

She nodded slowly. Then, in a voice carefully stripped of anything at all: "You lie well, ma'am."

"Fluently," I corrected. "Well implies effort. Fluency implies practice. Would you like to know the difference?"

"I expect I'm about to."

I crossed to the window. Below, the drive lay empty, the elms dripping, Bristol grey and patient at the bottom of the hill. "Someone sent that man here, Vesper. Twice. With questions shaped like yours and then shaped like mine. That is not bureaucracy. Bureaucracy does not investigate dead sisters in Bath. Someone is building a case, brick by brick, and they are using the census as their trowel."

"Mrs. Calloway," Vesper said.

The name dropped into the room like a coin into a well. I turned. "You've met her?"

"At the committee. She asked me questions. About the accounts, about your routines. Politely." A pause. "Everyone is polite to drivers. We're furniture that talks."

"And what did you tell her?"

"Nothing. Furniture doesn't remember." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one's possibility. "Ma'am. If someone is building a case against you — against this house — what is it they're building it *from*?"

An excellent question. A dangerous question. The kind of question that comes from a girl who reads letters before delivering them and has, I would wager my typewriter, seen considerably more of my correspondence than I have ever admitted aloud.

I considered three answers. The truth: that there was a ledger, and money that had wandered, and a son alive when the stone said otherwise, and a daughter pregnant and estranged in a harbourside tea room, and a plan forming at the edges of my nights involving an Atlantic and a new name. All of it. Hand her the whole rotten magnificent edifice and see whether she flinched.

Or the second answer: a partial confession, calibrated, the kind that buys loyalty with the currency of trust.

Or the third answer, the one I chose, because I am who I am:

"The truth," I said, "is that powerful women are not permitted mistakes, Vesper. Men make errors and call them experiments. We make one error and it becomes our biography. So yes — someone is building a case. And when they finish it, they will discover that every brick they laid was mortared with gossip, and gossip, unlike concrete, does not bear weight in daylight." I smiled at her, and I confess the smile was entirely strategic and entirely sincere, which is the trick of my life in a single sentence. "You drove me through worse than this in the WSPU marches. Keep your head and your hands steady, and we shall arrive."

She held my gaze a moment longer than a servant ought. Whatever she saw there — the strategist, the mother, the frightened animal in good tailoring — she filed it away behind those watchful eyes and said only, "Yes, ma'am," and carried out the tray.

* * *

That evening I sat at my desk and took out the second ledger — the true one, the one that lived inside the typewriter case like a heart inside a chest — and I added the month's payment to Hillcrest, and beneath it the sum marked *R.*, which was Rosalind's rent, paid secretly to her landlady for two years now, because a mother may disown a daughter publicly and still cannot bear to think of her hungry. Hypocrisy, yes. But hypocrisy is just love wearing its work clothes.

Then I wrote to my solicitor regarding a berth. Four berths. I will not yet say for whom; even on paper, even to myself, some decisions prefer to ripen in the dark.

Before I sealed the letter, I heard the front door, and Vesper's footsteps, and a second set of footsteps — light, quick, smelling of ink even from the hall — and Tobias Finch's voice saying something that made her laugh, actually laugh, a sound I had not heard from her in eight months of employment.

I put down my pen and listened to that laugh travel up the stairs, and I felt the thing I feel whenever the people around me begin to attach themselves to one another instead of to me: a cold, precise alarm, like ice forming on a harbour.

Because a household is a machine, and machines run on loyalty, and loyalty runs on need — and if they began needing each other more than they needed me, the machine would shake itself apart, and I would be left alone with my ledgers and my lies and a son behind a wall in Bath, and no one, in the end, would choose me.

I sealed the letter. Nine days, the sailing notice had said. Morning tide.

There was time. There was always time, right up until there wasn't.
