## 7. The Meeting of the Committee

The committee met on the second Thursday of every month in the back room of a temperance hall on Queen's Road, a room that smelled of cold tea, coal smoke, and the particular despair of folding chairs. I had driven Adeline to eleven of these meetings before the one in question, and my duties had always been the same: park the motorcar where it could be seen from the window—Adeline believed the sight of an automobile was worth three arguments—and wait.

I want to be honest about what waiting meant, because waiting was where all my sins lived. Waiting meant I read the minutes. Waiting meant I learned which women took their tea with lemon and which took it with grievance. Waiting meant that by the spring of 1912 I knew the Bristol movement the way a housemaid knows a house: through its dust, its drafts, and the things people said when they believed no one of consequence was listening.

I was nobody of consequence. That was the entire architecture of my life, load-bearing and load-bearing and load-bearing.

* * *

That Thursday it rained, the fine grey sideways rain that Bristol produces the way other cities produce fog or charm. Adeline sat beside me in the motorcar with her hands folded on her programme and did not speak for the length of Hotwell Road, which was itself a kind of weather.

"Mrs. Calloway has requested an item be added to the agenda," she said at last, to the windscreen rather than to me.

"Has she."

"She has." A pause with weight in it. "You will wait outside the room tonight, Vesper. Not in the hall. Outside the door."

In eleven meetings I had never been invited inside, but I had also never been explicitly exiled to the corridor. The corridor was worse than the motorcar. The corridor was where you were sent so that you could hear everything and see nothing.

"Yes, ma'am," I said, because I had learned that with Adeline Marsh, obedience bought you proximity, and proximity was the only currency I had ever managed to save.

She went in ahead of me, upright as a lamppost, her hat at the angle that meant war conducted politely. I took up my post against the wall outside the door, close enough that the draught under it carried voices the way a river carries leaves.

* * *

The meeting opened with minutes, treasurer's report, correspondence—the liturgy of committees everywhere, designed to lull everyone into believing that nothing is ever decided in rooms like this, only recorded. Mrs. Pym read the treasurer's report in a voice like wet paper. Income: subscriptions, a concert in Clifton, a legacy of nine pounds from a deceased sympathiser in Portishead. Expenditure: printing, halls, travel, "miscellaneous."

Miscellaneous. I had fetched enough of Adeline's post to know that miscellaneous was doing more work in that report than any other word in the English language.

Then Mrs. Calloway asked, in a voice like a clean knife laid on a tablecloth, whether the committee might hear the figures for the printing account separately from the rest.

The silence that followed had texture. I heard it through a door.

"Separately?" said Mrs. Pym.

"The pamphlet runs have doubled since autumn," Calloway said. "Our output has not doubled. Our distribution has not doubled. And yet the printing expenditure has risen by some forty percent. I merely wonder where the difference goes. Paper does not eat money, Mrs. Pym. Only people do."

Now, I will say this for the room: nobody gasped. These were suffragists; they had all been shouted down at public meetings, pelted with rotting fruit, arrested in ones and twos. A direct accusation over arithmetic was, for them, almost restful.

"It is not forty percent," said Adeline, and her voice came through the door the way light comes through frosted glass—all the heat removed, only the shape remaining. "It is thirty-one, and the reason is that we now print in two colours, because colour sells papers to people who would never buy a plain one, and because Mr. Finch at the press charges separately for the second plate. If Mrs. Calloway doubts the figure, she is welcome to accompany me to the press and count the plates herself. I would enjoy the outing."

A ripple of laughter, dutiful and relieved. I leaned my head back against the wall and thought: *she didn't even blink.* She had the number ready—not the true number, I suspected, but a number, polished and plausible, delivered with an invitation attached, because the surest way to end an inquiry is to make it sound like a day trip.

"I should be delighted," said Calloway, who did not laugh. "Though I confess my question was less about plates than about signatures. The cheques are countersigned, are they not? By whom?"

"By myself and Mrs. Pym, as constitution requires."

"And the counterfoils? Are they kept?"

"They are kept."

"Might a subcommittee review them?"

And here Adeline did something I can still see when I close my eyes, though I saw none of it, only heard it: she turned the whole thing into a compliment. "What a splendid idea," she said warmly. "We are forever being told we cannot govern, cannot count, cannot be trusted with a franchise because we are creatures of sentiment. Let us show Bristol a committee that audits itself voluntarily. Mrs. Calloway, you shall chair it. Choose your own members. I insist."

Another ripple of laughter, warmer this time. Applause, even. She had taken the knife out of Calloway's hand, engraved it with the movement's motto, and presented it back to her as a trophy.

But I stood in that corridor and felt the floor of my understanding tilt, because I had seen the ledger in the typewriter case, and I knew—I knew with the sick certainty of a woman holding stolen goods—that the counterfoils would not balance, and that Adeline had just handed her rival a shovel and pointed her at the exact spot where the bodies were buried, confident that by the time anyone dug, the ground would have been moved.

Unless the ground hadn't been moved yet. Unless Adeline intended to move it tonight, tomorrow, before the subcommittee ever convened. Unless the offer was a stalling manoeuvre dressed as magnanimity.

Or unless—this thought arrived uninvited and stayed like a bad lodger—unless Adeline wanted the audit. Wanted it narrow, wanted it controlled, wanted to sacrifice some small explainable discrepancy to satisfy the hounds while the larger quarry went out the back.

I had known this woman for a year and a half. I had driven her through rain and heckling and one memorable incident involving a turnip. I had believed her the most honest person I knew, honest the way weather is honest. And now I stood in a corridor smelling of floor polish and realised I could no longer tell whether I admired her or feared her, and that the two feelings had, without my noticing, become the same feeling.

* * *

The meeting moved on—or appeared to. They discussed the Hyde Park demonstration, the Prisoners' Bill, whether to send a delegate to Manchester. But something had changed in the acoustics. Before, the disagreements had been open, operatic, almost affectionate—Mrs. Pym against Miss Hartree over tactics, everyone against everyone over whether to support the Labour candidates. Now the disagreements were quiet, and quietness in a committee is a fever reading.

When they broke for tea I stepped away from the door, arranged my face into the blank pleasantness of a servant with nowhere better to be, and carried the tray in.

This was permitted. Servants are furniture that pours.

I poured for eleven women and I watched. Mrs. Calloway sat with her notebook closed but her hand resting on it, the way a farmer rests a hand on a gate. She was perhaps fifty-five, built like a chapel, with spectacles that caught the gaslight so you could never quite see her eyes. She thanked me for the tea—a thing almost no one did—and then said, conversationally, to the room at large:

"My late husband was in shipping insurance. He taught me one lesson I have never forgotten: the loss is never in the storm. The storm is honest. The loss is always in the paperwork afterwards."

"How dreary," said Miss Hartree.

"How useful," said Mrs. Calloway, and smiled at me, of all people, as she said it.

I smiled back the way furniture smiles, and poured, and my hands did not shake, because I had spent twenty-two years teaching them not to. But something behind my sternum had begun, quietly, to shake—the tremor of a woman realising she is standing on a bridge and hearing, faintly, rivets popping.

Because here is what I had not told anyone, what I had replaced exactly and said nothing about: I knew where the ledger lived. I knew the numbers in it. And Mrs. Calloway, who thanked servants and quoted dead husbands, had just announced to a room of eleven women that she intended to find the paperwork.

There were, I calculated, two possible versions of the future. In one, she found the ledger and Adeline fell, and the movement tore itself apart on the dockside for the newspapers to photograph. In the other, she found the ledger and Adeline destroyed it first, and then the accusation became unprovable, and unprovable accusations have a way of attaching themselves to whoever made them.

Either way, someone was going to need a driver who could keep her mouth shut.

I was beginning to suspect that keeping my mouth shut was the one talent I possessed that everyone in my life had quietly agreed to exploit.

* * *

After tea, business resumed, and I withdrew to the corridor, and the meeting entered its second act, which I heard in fragments through the door like a play performed behind a curtain.

"—the WSPU will not thank us for timidity—" that was Adeline.

"—timidity is not the word I would choose—" that was Calloway, mild as milk.

"—then choose another, Mrs. Calloway, you seem a woman of vocabulary—"

Laughter again, but thinner. Someone coughed. Somewhere in the room a chair creaked under the weight of a decision nobody was making aloud.

And then, near the end, a voice I did not know—young, nervous, new: "Might I ask after the Rosalind Fund? The subscription list mentioned it in January, and there's been nothing since."

Silence. The kind of silence that has a temperature.

"The Rosalind Fund," said Adeline, and her voice did something extraordinary: it softened. It softened audibly, deliberately, the way a hand softens when it closes. "The Rosalind Fund was wound up in February. The monies were transferred to the general account with the donors' consent. My daughter—" a pause, measured to the half-second, grief performed so well it might have been grief— "my daughter is estranged from me, ladies, as some of you know, and I will not have her name bandied about a committee room. The fund bore her name at her own request, before the estrangement. It is finished. Next item."

Next item. As if a name were an item. As if a daughter were a line in a ledger that could be transferred to the general account with the donors' consent.

I stood in the corridor with my back against the wall and my heart going like a typewriter, because I had read that subscription list in January—I read everything—and the Rosalind Fund had raised eleven pounds four shillings, earmarked, the notice said, "for the relief of a sister worker in distress." Eleven pounds four shillings, transferred to a general account that fed a private ledger that fed a bank account whose existence I alone in that building suspected.

Rosalind. The fund had borne her name. Adeline had let a room full of strangers subscribe money in her daughter's name and then folded it into her own escape fund like a napkin into a drawer.

Or—no. Stop. I was doing it too now, wasn't I? Leaping. Concluding. The ledger proved diversion, but did it prove malice? Perhaps the money went somewhere kind. Perhaps the son in the institution cost money—though I did not know about the son yet, not then; that knowledge was still six weeks downstream of me, waiting like a weir. At that moment I knew only the shape of the hole, not what lay in it.

That is the thing about suspicion they don't put in novels: once it starts, it doesn't distinguish between the guilty and the innocent. It eats both. I sat in the motorcar afterward and watched the committee file out into the rain—Pym fluttering, Hartree arm-in-arm with a girl I didn't know, Calloway last, pausing on the step to look directly at our motorcar for three full seconds, the way you look at a horse you intend to buy—and I understood that I had stopped believing in Adeline Marsh, and that I had no evidence, and that the absence of evidence would protect her and condemn me, because I was the one who knew things I shouldn't, the one with forged papers and a habit of reading other people's letters, the one whose entire existence was already a small crime wearing a chauffeur's cap.

* * *

Adeline came down the steps beneath an umbrella held by Miss Hartree, dismissed Hartree with a kiss and a murmured kindness, and settled into the passenger seat shaking rain from her shoulders like a spaniel.

"Well," she said. "You heard, of course."

It was not a question. With Adeline it was never a question.

"Through a door," I said. "Badly."

"You heard enough." She looked straight ahead at the rain coming down on Queen's Road, at the lamps coming on one by one along the promenade, each with its little trembling halo. "Calloway will convene her subcommittee within the fortnight. She will request the counterfoils. Mrs. Pym will fetch them in a biscuit tin, because Mrs. Pym keeps everything in biscuit tins, God love her." A pause. "Vesper, on Thursday next I shall ask you to drive me to the press on Colston Street. There are accounts to discuss with young Finch's master."

"Accounts," I said.

"Printing accounts," she said, and turned her head and gave me the smile she used on platforms, the one that had recruited a thousand women and raised eleven pounds four shillings in a dead daughter's name. "You disapprove of something. Don't bother denying it; you have the face of a church warden at a christening. Whatever you think you know, Vesper—and I promise you, you know a fraction—remember this: everything I have done, I have done so that women like Mrs. Pym can stand up in public and be laughed at, and come back the next month and stand up again. That costs. Freedom is not free, whatever the Americans print on their teacups. Someone pays the bill, and it may as well be paid by people who can afford it."

"Which people can afford it?" I asked.

The smile stayed exactly where it was. Nothing moved except the eyes, and even the eyes only cooled, the way a lake cools a foot below the surface where the swimmers never go.

"That," she said, "is precisely the question Calloway is about to spend a fortnight asking. I suggest you decide whose side of it you'd rather stand on before she asks you too."

She faced forward. I started the engine. The wipers—hand-cranked, temperamental, mine to operate with a lever by my knee—swept their small useless arcs across the glass, clearing the rain and revealing only more rain.

I drove us home through the wet dark, past the docks where the cranes stood against the sky like gallows that had changed professions, and I thought about the fourth berth I didn't yet know existed, and the ship I didn't yet know was booked, and how a woman can sit in a warm motorcar beside the most powerful person in her life and feel, for the first time, not protected but *kept*—the way you keep a receipt, or a weapon, or a witness.

Behind us, in the lit window of the temperance hall, a single lamp still burned. Mrs. Calloway had gone back in alone. Through the rain-blurred glass I could see her at the committee table, notebook open at last, writing.

She wrote for a long time.

I put my foot down, and the harbour lights slid away behind us, and I did not look back again—but I thought about that lamp all night, and several nights after, the way you think about a tooth that hasn't started hurting yet but has already, you're quite sure, begun to loosen.
