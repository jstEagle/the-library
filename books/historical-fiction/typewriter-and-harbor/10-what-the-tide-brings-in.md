## 10. What the Tide Brings In

The storm arrived the way bad news always does in Bristol — first as a rumour, then all at once, and then it was inside the house with its boots on.

I had been warned. Mrs. Pellow, who owned the tea room and trusted me with the till because she trusted no one else, had squinted at the sky that morning and said, "We'll get water over the quay tonight, mark me. The river's got a grudge on." I marked her. I marked her by stacking the ledgers on the highest shelf, wedging the sugar tins under the counter, and putting my own small box of possessions — three dresses, a photograph I could no longer look at, and a bundle of unanswered letters I could not bring myself to burn — up on top of the flour sacks in the back room.

What I did not mark, what no one could have marked, was that the river's grudge would be the least of what came through my door that night.

* * *

By nine o'clock the wind had found its voice. The tea room had emptied hours before; nobody wants tea when the windows are shivering in their frames and the gas lamps along the quay are bending like drunkards. I was alone with the chairs turned up on the tables, doing the arithmetic of a woman who has learned that her whole future fits inside a cash box: eleven shillings and sixpence, of which four belonged to Mrs. Pellow, and a tip jar with a ha'penny in it that I was pretending not to count.

The water came at half past nine. Not gradually — that was the shocking part. I had imagined a tide as a polite thing, rising inch by inch like a bill. Instead the harbour simply stood up. There was a sound like a door being kicked open somewhere below, and then the Avon came through the cellar grate and across the floor of the tea room in a single black sheet, cold as a verdict, and it took my shoes with it before I had finished deciding to scream.

I got up on a table. That was the whole of my heroism: I stood on a table in my stockings while the sea rearranged the furniture of my life. The chairs went first, spinning out into the dark like startled gulls. Then a crate of crockery, then the tip jar — my ha'penny, gone to join the general fund of the drowned — and then the water found the back room, and I heard it, I *heard* it, lapping up toward the flour sacks and the box on top of them, and I did the only reasonable thing, which was to wade in after it up to my knees and haul the box above my head like a madwoman holding an offering out to the storm.

The photograph was soaked. The letters were soaked. My three dresses were floating around my ankles like pale, disappointed jellyfish.

"Well," I said to the room, because there was no one else to say it to. "That's that, then."

I have since learned that this is what I do in a crisis. I make a small, dry remark to an empty room, and then I get on with it. My mother, I am told, does the same thing, except that her empty rooms are full of admirers.

* * *

She came at half past ten.

I want to be precise about this, because for a long time afterward I told myself I had imagined the whole evening — the storm had soaked through my memory as thoroughly as through my letters — and I want the record to be exact. Half past ten. The door of the tea room opened against the wind with a sound like a shot, and a woman stood in it with a lantern, and the lantern lit her face from below so that for one moment I thought: *a stranger, a ghost, a bailiff.*

Then she said, "Is anyone in here? Mrs. Pellow? Anyone?" — and I knew the voice. I had been hearing it for nine years, from behind doors, through floors, in the memory I kept in the place where other people keep their mothers' voices.

"I'm here," I said. "Up here. On the table. It's a long story involving the tide."

The lantern swung up and found me. Vesper Iversen. My mother's driver. The woman whose face I had seen perhaps four times and whose name I had said to myself a hundred, in the dark, the way you say the name of a town you mean to visit someday and never do.

"Miss Marsh," she said. Not Rosalind. Miss Marsh. She had been sent, then. My mother knew.

"No one's called me that in two years," I said. "It's Mrs. — well. It's nothing, actually. It's Miss Finch on the ledger because Mrs. Pellow doesn't ask questions and I don't volunteer them. You can be Rosalind or you can be nothing. Everyone else here is."

She stood in the doorway with the water at her boots and the wind trying to take the lantern out of her hand, and I watched her decide something. I had watched my mother decide things my whole life — you could see it happen behind her eyes, a small committee convening and voting — and I saw the same motion in this woman's face, and I thought, with a bitterness I could taste: *she has been in that house so long she has learned its grammar.*

"Mrs. Pellow sent me," Vesper said. "She couldn't get down from Clifton. She said the ledgers were on the high shelf and the girl would be a fool to stay."

"The girl stayed."

"I can see that."

"The ledgers are safe, if that's what she's worried about. The crockery is in the harbour. My shoes are in the harbour. I'm told the harbour keeps excellent records and returns nothing."

Something happened to her mouth then — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if a person could afford one. "I've come to help," she said. "Whatever there is to help with."

"There's nothing," I said. "There's a box of wet letters and a wet photograph and three wet dresses, and in the morning there will be a wet tea room and I will owe Mrs. Pellow an explanation I haven't invented yet. Go home. Tell her — tell her the girl is a fool but a dry one would have been no better."

But she didn't go. She set the lantern on the bar, out of the wind's reach, and she took her coat off, and she waded into my ruined shop as though it were a job she had been hired to do, and I stood on my table and hated her a little for it, and hated myself more for how much I wanted her to stay.

* * *

Here is what salvage looks like, in case you have never done it: it looks like two women in a flooded room at midnight, passing wet things hand to hand and saying nothing. The letters went into the lantern's dry circle one at a time, and Vesper — I did not ask her to, she simply did it — held each one open near the light so the air could get at it, her hands careful, her face carefully blank.

She read them. Of course she read them. She was my mother's driver; reading other people's letters was practically her trade; I learned that later, and by then it was too late to be shocked. But that night I only saw a stranger holding my private ruin up to a lamp, and I said, "Those are mine."

"I know," she said. "I'm not reading them. I'm drying them. There's a difference, though I'll grant it's a narrow one."

"Whose side are you on?"

The question came out of me with more force than I'd intended, the way things do when they've been waiting. The wind took it and rattled it around the room. Vesper set the letter she was holding down on the bar — gently, the way you set down something that might go off — and looked at me, and I saw that she was not going to pretend she hadn't heard it.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the truth. I drive the motorcar. I deliver the letters. I've been telling myself for two years that I'm just the hands, and the hands don't get to have a side." She paused. "But I read the addresses. And for two years there's been no letter going out to a daughter in — wherever you were. And there's been no letter coming in. And I told myself that meant the daughter didn't write."

"I wrote." My voice did something humiliating. I made it stop. "Eleven letters in two years. The first one took me three weeks to write and cost me a week's wages in stamps before I could make myself post it. I told her everything. Where I was. What I'd — what had happened. That I was going to have a child. That I was frightened." The water moved against the table leg, patient, like a creditor. "I addressed them to the house. I knew they'd reach her. She reads everything. She *keeps* everything. She has a box — I used to find it when I was a child, letters from thirty years back, tied in ribbon, every one of them. She doesn't throw away a word. So I knew."

"You knew she'd get them."

"I knew she'd get them. I didn't know she'd read them. There's a difference." I heard my own voice do the thing hers does — the small dry remark, the door closing over the feeling. We were more alike than either of us could bear, my mother and I, and now apparently this stranger was catching it like a fever. "There was no answer. Eleven letters. Not a line. Not a returned envelope, not a servant sent with a message, not even a lawyer's letter, which would have been something, because a lawyer's letter means you've been *considered*. Nothing. I used to go to the window at my lodging house every morning before the post came, and then I stopped going, and then I stopped writing, and that was the silence finished. You can't keep knocking on a door that's been painted shut."

Vesper was quiet a long moment. The lantern hissed. Somewhere out on the quay a man was shouting about a boat, his voice torn sideways by the wind.

"May I ask you something," she said at last, "that isn't my business?"

"It's the middle of the night and my shop is under the sea. Nothing is anyone's business. Ask."

"The letters you wrote. Did you ever say anything in them that she'd — that would have made silence easier than answering?"

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You mean did I accuse her? Did I tell her what I think of the great Adeline Marsh, who stands on platforms and says *no more silence, no more shame, the truth for every woman* — did I tell her that her daughter sat in a rented room in Bristol writing *Mother, I am afraid* in eleven different ways?" I put my hand flat on the wet bar to steady myself, though the floor had stopped moving; it was only the rest of me that hadn't. "No. That's the worst of it. I was kind. I gave her every door out. I said, *I don't blame you, I know what it would cost you, I only want to know if you're alive to the cost.* I asked her — the last letter — I asked her to send anything. A word. A blank page. I said, *send me nothing at all, but write* nothing* on it, so I know it reached you and you chose.*"

"And?"

"And nothing. So now I know. She chose." I looked at the lantern, because looking at Vesper was suddenly more than I could do. "You want to know the funny part? I could have borne cruelty. I was raised on it — the kind that wears a smile and calls itself principle. I could have borne *I cannot help you, Rosalind, you've made your bed.* What I can't carry is the arithmetic of it. That somewhere in Clifton there is a woman who has read *I am frightened* from her only daughter eleven times and decided the postage wasn't worth it. That's not a silence. That's a decision, made eleven times, on eleven mornings, with her tea."

The words were out before I could weigh them, and once they were out they were true, and being true they were heavy, and I sat down on the table — actually sat, in my wet stockings, in my drowned shop — and put my face in my hands like a woman in a melodrama, because there was no one to see and no dignity left to spend.

* * *

Vesper did not touch me. I want that recorded too, because it would have been easy — a hand on the shoulder, the ordinary currency of comfort — and she didn't do it, and I understood later that this was its own kind of kindness. She understood, in the way of people who have been alone in rooms themselves, that some griefs close like anemones at the first touch.

Instead she said, "I'll hold the letters up to the lamp a while longer. The paper takes the water out better if you're patient."

And so we stayed like that, the two of us, for I don't know how long. Me sitting on a table in the ruins of my employ. Her standing in a foot of harbour, holding my eleven letters up to the light one after another, patient as a woman wringing out the whole Atlantic. The storm worked itself hoarse outside. The water in the room stopped rising and began, very slowly, to think about going home.

It was somewhere in that silence — letter six, or seven, held open to the lamp, my own handwriting gone soft and grey at the edges — that I looked at her properly for the first time. Not my mother's driver. Not the hands. A woman perhaps five years older than me, with ink on her fingers and a face that had learned to be a closed door, holding a stranger's most humiliating correspondence with more care than anyone had ever held anything of mine.

"You read them," I said. Not accusing. Just establishing the facts, as she had.

"Yes."

"All of them?"

"All of them. Twice, some of them. I'm sorry."

"Don't be." I took a breath that hurt. "Did she read them? Do you know? You'd know, wouldn't you. You'd have seen."

And here she did something I have thought about every day since. She could have said no. She could have said *I couldn't say, miss.* She could have done what everyone in that house did, what I had been doing myself for two years, which is to stand in the doorway of the truth and hold it shut. Instead she set the letter down, and looked at the lantern, and said:

"She read them. I watched her read the first one. She sat in the study with it for the better part of an hour. And then she put it in the drawer of her desk, under the blotter, and locked the desk, and rang for tea, and asked me about the tyres on the motorcar." A pause. "I don't know what she did with the others. But I know she read the first one. I know what her face does when she's decided something and doesn't want it known. I've had two years of practice at not knowing things. That one I couldn't not know."

The relief was obscene. That's the word for it — obscene — because what I felt, hearing that my mother had read my terror and filed it under the blotter, was not fresh grief. It was *vindication*. Eleven mornings of doubt, of *perhaps the letters never came, perhaps the address was wrong, perhaps she is ill, perhaps she is dead* — all of it burned away in one sentence, and what was left underneath was the terrible warm thing: *I was right. She chose. I was right.* I could have wept at being right. I nearly did.

"Thank you," I said, and meant it, and hated that I meant it.

"She's not what you think," Vesper said. And then, quickly, as if she'd said too much: "She isn't. She isn't what anyone thinks. That's not a defence. I want to be clear it isn't a defence."

"Then what is it?"

She was quiet for a while. The lantern flame stood very straight, the way it does when the wind has found a gap and gone through it. Outside, the shouting man had stopped shouting; either the boat was saved or it wasn't, and the harbour was not telling.

"I don't know yet," she said finally. "I came here tonight to fetch a woman's ledgers. I'm leaving with a great deal more than that, and I haven't finished counting it." She folded the last letter — the one that asked for *nothing, but write nothing on it* — and set it with the others, and put her hand flat on top of the little stack, the way you'd quiet a dog. "These should go somewhere warm tonight. I've a room over in Redcliffe with a fire that works half the time. You can't stay here. The floor's under the sea and Mrs. Pellow's explanation can wait for morning."

"I can't impose —"

"You're standing in your stockings in a flooded tea room holding a box of letters. The time for imposing was hours ago." She picked up the lantern. "Come on. The water's going out, and so should we."

* * *

We walked up through Redcliffe with the storm at our backs, shoving us along like a pair of drunkards being escorted out of a pub. She carried the box. I carried nothing, because I owned nothing that was dry, and there is a strange freedom in that which I recommend to no one.

At the door of her lodging house she paused, key in the lock, and said, without turning around: "There's a girl at the press — well, a boy, an apprentice — who'll help us dry these properly tomorrow. He's good with paper. He sleeps at the press and he quotes Shelley at you whether you've asked or not." A pause. "I'm telling you because you've been alone with this a long time, and alone isn't a thing you have to be. I'm not — I don't know what I am in all this yet. But I know what it is to be outside that house, looking in."

"And what is it?" I asked.

"Like standing on a table in a flooded room," she said, "watching the water decide what it's going to keep."

She opened the door. The stair smelled of coal and old toast, and somewhere above us a fire was working, or half working, and I followed her up into the small warm ordinary dark of a stranger's life, carrying nothing, owning nothing, and for the first time in two years not entirely alone.

I did not know then that the storm had brought more than the tide in. I did not know that the woman holding my letters had papers that would not bear looking at, or that a Mrs. Calloway was already counting spoons, so to speak, in the house on Clifton Down. I knew only that my mother had read my letters and chosen silence, and that a stranger with ink on her fingers had chosen otherwise.

It was, I would learn soon enough, the first small choice in a much larger one — and none of us, that night, had any idea what the morning tide was going to ask us to give back.
