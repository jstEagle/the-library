## 20. Morning Tide

The tide came in at 6:14 that morning, and I know this because Tobias told me twice, and because I had not slept since ten o'clock the night before, and because a woman who has spent all night walking a harbor will fixate on anything that arrives on schedule. The tide. The gulls. The ship's horn, which sounded at half past five like something clearing its throat before an unpleasant announcement.

I had walked from the tea room to the lock gates and back eleven times. I had counted the mooring ropes on the *Aurelia*—forty-one—and read the manifest pasted to the customs house door twice, as though the names might rearrange themselves overnight into a decision I could live with more cheaply. They did not. Adeline Marsh. Vesper Iversen. Tobias Finch. One berth reserved, name withheld pending confirmation. The clerk had underlined it in red, which struck me as officious even for a shipping line.

My feet hurt. My eyes felt scoured. And somewhere in the eleventh circuit of the quay I stopped being afraid and started being clear, which is either grace or exhaustion, and I have never learned to tell them apart.

Here is what I understood in the dark, reading Edmund Marsh's unsent letters by lamplight in a doorway while the rain came sideways off the water: you cannot outrun a debt by putting it on a ship. I had spent six years making myself small enough to slip through borders—forged papers, borrowed names, a sister who existed only in ink. And every mile of it had been a payment plan, interest compounding quietly, until Mrs. Calloway came collecting with a warrant in one hand and my true history in the other. Running would save my skin. It would not save me. There is a difference, and it is the width of a harbor.

But there were three people who could be saved, and one berth, and I am not so noble as to pretend the arithmetic didn't ache.

* * *

The dock at dawn was all gulls and coal smoke and men shouting numbers at each other as though numbers had ever once listened. Stevedores moved crates up the gangway of the *Aurelia* with the bored efficiency of men who had done this a thousand times and would do it a thousand more, and I thought: there it is. History, loading itself onto ships, indifferent as weather. I had spent years believing I was watching it through glass. This morning I intended to put my hand through.

Adeline arrived first, of course. She arrived the way weather arrives—suddenly, and with everyone already braced for her. She wore the grey traveling suit, the one with the collar that made her look like a monument to herself, and she carried a single valise, because Adeline Marsh did not believe in excess baggage unless it was people.

"You look terrible," she said, by way of good morning.

"I've been awake since Tuesday."

"Good. You'll want your wits." She looked out at the water, and for one unguarded moment her face did something complicated—the face of a woman about to leave the country she had spent twenty years trying to remake, carrying nothing but a valise and a scandal. Then the moment passed, and she was granite again. "Where is the boy?"

"Coming."

"And Rosalind?"

"Also coming. Though perhaps not in the way you've planned."

Adeline's jaw tightened. "Vesper. Whatever sentiment you've been cultivating—"

"It isn't sentiment," I said. "It's testimony. You taught me the difference, actually. You just didn't expect me to learn it."

She turned to look at me fully then, and I saw her doing the thing she did in committee meetings when a vote went wrong—recalculating, reallocating, deciding whether I was an asset or an obstacle. I watched her decide, and I watched her fail to decide, and I confess there was a small vicious pleasure in it. Let her sit with uncertainty for once. She'd earned it.

Tobias came down the gangway road at a run, coat flying, ink on his fingers as always, and skidded to a stop beside us looking like a boy who had slept three hours and would remember this morning for sixty years. He was carrying a wooden crate against his chest the way you carry a sleeping child.

"The letters," he said, breathless. "All forty-one. Retyped. I did the last ones between two and four—I hope the alignment holds, the ribbon's nearly dead, but—" He caught himself, glanced at Adeline, lowered his voice. "You're sure?"

"I'm sure."

"Sure about what?" said Adeline.

Rosalind arrived before I could answer. She came along the quay from the direction of the tea room, still wearing her apron, her coat buttoned wrong, one hand pressed flat against the swell of her belly as though holding a door shut. She stopped ten feet away and looked at her mother across the gap the way you look at a fire you both started.

"You booked me a berth," Rosalind said. "Without asking me."

"I offered you a future," Adeline said. "The asking seemed redundant."

"It usually does, with you."

"Rosalind—"

"No." Her voice cracked but held. "No, you don't get to manage this one. I have spent nine years being managed. I wrote to you. Do you understand that? I sat down in a rented room with no money and a child coming and I wrote to my mother, and you burned the letter unread. Vesper told me. She reads everything, Mother—it's her one flaw and her one virtue, and she told me the truth when you wouldn't."

Adeline's face went very still. "That letter was a threat to everything—"

"I was never a threat. I was an inconvenience. There's a difference and you've built a career on not knowing it." Rosalind's hand moved on her belly, an unconscious gesture, and I saw Adeline track it and flinch. "I'm not getting on that ship."

"You'd rather stay and be ruined?"

"I'd rather stay and be *here*. Where my child is born. Where Tobias can teach him to drive badly. Where the tide comes in on schedule and nobody has to forge anything." She took a breath. "And where you might, someday, come back and face me honestly instead of buying me passage like a debt you're settling."

The gulls screamed. A crane swung a crate overhead. Somewhere a whistle blew, long and flat, and the crowd on the quay began to press toward the gangway, and none of us moved.

* * *

Here is the part I had rehearsed all night, standing in doorways, mouthing the words to myself like a madwoman, and here is how it actually came out—which is to say, badly.

"Tobias," I said. "Come here."

He came. He always came; that was the whole trouble with him.

"Give me your hand."

He gave it, frowning, and I pressed into his palm the envelope I had carried since midnight—the berth ticket, third class, *S.S. Aurelia*, sailing on the morning tide, passenger name amended in the shipping office at five o'clock that morning by a clerk who took my bribe without comment and my forged signature without scrutiny. Some skills you keep.

"That's yours," I said.

He looked at the ticket. Then at me. Then at the ticket again, as though it might change its mind. "Vesper. No. No—you're the one who—Calloway will—"

"Calloway will do exactly what Calloway was always going to do, and she'll do it whether I'm in Bristol or New York. But if I go, I go having run, and I find I've run out of appetite for that." I laughed, a bit wildly. "Pun intended. Someone should write that down."

"This isn't funny."

"No," I agreed. "It's terrible. It's the worst morning of my life and I've had some competition. But listen to me." I took the crate from his arms and set it on a bollard between us, and lifted out the topmost letter—Edmund Marsh's handwriting, patient and looping, dated March 1904. *Dear Mama. The nurse says spring is early this year. I have counted the boats from the window and there are nine.* "Forty-one letters, fourteen years, and not one of them delivered. His mother left him on a hill and forgot the address. That's what running looks like, Tobias. That's what it costs. Somebody has to be the address."

"He's not my—"

"He's family. So are you. So is she." I nodded at Rosalind, who was watching us with her hand over her mouth. "I spent six years believing history belonged to bolder people, and then I met a boy who sleeps at a printing press and quotes Shelley at typewriters, and I understood that history belongs to whoever keeps the record. You keep records better than anyone I've ever known. Better than me. Take them. Take the letters—they're yours now, all of them, an inheritance from a man you never met who writes better than most MPs. Start a life where nobody checks papers at the door. Learn to drive in a country where they drive on the wrong side, God help everyone."

Tobias was crying, which he would deny to his grave, and I was pretending not to notice, which made us even.

"I can't take your berth," he said.

"You can and you will, because I've already amended the ticket and the clerk thinks I'm a Mrs. Havelock of Clifton and I'd rather die than explain that to customs." I put the ticket back in his hand and closed his fingers over it. "Tobias. Listen. The night you asked me to teach you to drive, you said you wanted to go somewhere faster than walking. Well. Here's faster than walking. Go."

He hugged me then, hard enough to crack a rib, ink and coal smoke and printer's oil, sixteen years old and shaking, and I held on and let the harbor blur a little and did not apologize for it.

"When I'm settled," he said into my shoulder, "I'll send for you. All of you. I'll buy four berths. Five. However many—"

"One," I said. "Buy one, and make it a big one, and we'll argue about who sits where. That's what families do."

* * *

Adeline watched all of this from three feet away with the expression of a chess player discovering the board has been replaced mid-game.

"You're staying," she said. "To testify."

"To testify, yes. Not to destroy you. There's a difference, and I've built a career on knowing it." I handed her the crate of original letters—Edmund's own hand, the only copies that mattered. "These are yours. Or his. Whichever you decide to be honest about first."

She took the crate and did not thank me, because Adeline Marsh did not thank people; she filed them. But she looked down at the topmost letter for a long time, and her thumb moved over the date, and I saw fourteen years land on her all at once, right there on the public quay, in front of stevedores and gulls and her estranged daughter.

"He asked about boats," she said quietly. "In every letter, he asks about the boats."

"Nine this spring, apparently."

Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "I used to count them with him. Before. When he was small enough that the institution was still called a school." She closed the crate lid. "You could have blackmailed me with these."

"I considered it. The rates were steep."

"Vesper."

"Madam."

For a moment neither of us spoke, and the ship's horn sounded again, impatient, and the gangway began to fill. Adeline straightened her collar—that grey monument of a collar—and became, visibly, deliberately, the woman who would board a ship alone and call it strategy.

"You'll hang me out to dry before the committee," she said. "And Calloway will parade you like a trophy, and the census office will ask you questions you can't answer comfortably, and in five years no one will remember any of our names except as a footnote in someone else's pamphlet." She picked up her valise. "Was it worth it?"

I thought about the ledger in the typewriter case. About Rosalind's hand on her belly. About a boy counting boats from a window on a hill. About a different boy, asleep at a press, waking to a ticket across the sea.

"I'll let you know in five years," I said.

She almost smiled. Then she kissed the air near Rosalind's cheek—Rosalind did not turn her head, but she did not step back either, which between those two constituted a treaty—and she walked up the gangway with her spine straight and her valise light and her son's letters in a crate she had to carry herself, because I refused to carry them for her, and because some things a mother should haul up a gangway with her own arms.

Rosalind and I stood at the rail's end and watched her go. Adeline did not look back until she reached the top of the gangway, and then she did—one glance, quick, taking inventory of us the way she took inventory of everything—and then she was swallowed by the deck, and the whistle blew, and the ropes came off.

"She'll write," Rosalind said.

"Of course she'll write. She'll write magnificent letters. She'll enclose money orders and instructions."

"And we won't answer."

"We'll keep them," I said. "Every one. Filed and dated. That's not forgiveness, but it isn't nothing."

The *Aurelia* began to move—a shudder, then a glide, the great hull swinging its nose toward the river mouth. On the lower deck, a small figure appeared at the rail, waving both arms, and Tobias shouted something we couldn't hear over the engines, and Rosalind waved back with her whole body, laughing and crying at once, and I stood very still with my hands in my pockets and let myself feel the full weight of what I had just done, which was: given away my escape, kept my name, kept my debts, kept my people.

It cost exactly as much as I'd feared. I paid it anyway. The tide took the ship out on schedule, because tides are honest that way, and I stood on the quay until the *Aurelia* was a smudge, and then a rumor, and then gone.

"Well," said Rosalind, after a while. "We're ruined, aren't we."

"Thoroughly."

"My mother will disown me from another continent."

"She'll disown you in impeccable prose."

Rosalind laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and then pressed both hands to her belly and winced, and went pale, and grabbed my arm with a grip that suggested the day had decided it wasn't finished with us.

"Vesper," she said, in a voice I would come to know very well over the next several hours, "I think you should fetch the car."

* * *

So that is how I came to spend the afternoon of the morning tide driving a suffragist's daughter through Bristol at illegal speeds, one hand on the wheel and one hand being crushed, while a warrant with my true name on it sat in a drawer at the census office and a committee waited to hear my testimony and a boy sailed east with forty-one letters in a wooden crate, learning to be somebody's inheritance.

Mrs. Calloway thought she had me cornered. The census office thought they had my number.

They were both about to learn what I had learned on the dock, watching a ship pull out with my future aboard and my conscience ashore:

Memory keeps. Papers burn. And I had spent all night choosing which one I was.
