## 21. Testimony

I had rehearsed the speech on the walk over, in the tram, and once more on the pavement outside the hall while Tobias pretended to adjust a shoelace that did not need adjusting. It was a good speech. It had structure. It began with an apology and ended with a principle, and somewhere in the middle it forgave Adeline Marsh without ever saying her name.

Then Mrs. Calloway opened the door and I understood that no one in that room wanted a speech. They wanted blood, or they wanted absolution, and neither of those things can be typed out in advance.

"Miss Iversen." Calloway stood at the head of the long table where the committee usually met to argue about printing costs. She had arranged the chairs like a courtroom, which told me everything about how she'd spent the three days since the warrants were drafted. "You may sit."

"I'll stand," I said. "I've been sitting behind a steering wheel for two years. My opinions have all been formed standing up."

Someone at the far end — Miss Hartley, who ran the Bristol branch's correspondence and had never once laughed at anything — made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Calloway's mouth thinned. Good. If I could make them uncomfortable before I said anything true, the truth would land softer.

There were eleven women in the room. I counted because counting is what you do when your hands want to shake. Adeline was not among them. She had boarded the *Corinthia* that morning with one trunk, one typewriter case, and no son, no daughter, and no driver. I had watched the ship pull out from the dock with Tobias's hat in my hands, waving it like a fool, and I had felt — this is the part I will not pretend was noble — relief. Enormous, shameful relief. The door had closed on her and I was still standing on English soil with my forged papers in my coat pocket and my true name folded up inside me like a letter I'd been carrying for years.

Calloway had expected me to be on that ship. That was the flaw in her architecture. Her whole case against me assumed I would run, because running was what she would have done, and people always build their traps out of their own timber.

"You've come to confess," she said. Not a question. She wanted it on the record as her victory before I'd said a word.

"I've come to return something," I said. "It isn't mine."

I put the second ledger on the table. The real one, the one I had found inside Adeline's typewriter case, with its neat columns of movement funds routed into a private account under a name that wasn't quite a lie and wasn't quite hers. I had copied every figure by hand, twice, and given the copy to Tobias for safekeeping, because I had learned from watching Adeline that a document only exists if it exists in more than one place.

"This covers fourteen months of subscriptions, bazaar proceeds, and the legacy from Mrs. Penhale," I said. "The money didn't vanish. It moved. It moved with great care and beautiful penmanship, and it moved into an account that funded, among other things, two berths on a ship that sailed this morning."

The room went very quiet. Eleven women and the sound of the harbor gulls through the window, screaming their usual opinions about fish.

"How do you know this?" Miss Hartley asked. "You're the driver."

"I'm the driver," I agreed. "And drivers are furniture. Nobody checks whether the furniture can read." I let that sit. "I read the letters. All of them, for two years. I know things about this committee that none of you know about each other, and I am not going to share any of them, because that isn't why I'm here."

"Why *are* you here?" Calloway's voice had the bright edge of a woman who has waited months for a moment and finds it slightly the wrong shape. "You're an illegal. Your papers are forged. You have no standing in this room whatsoever. You came to my office begging—"

"I came to your office because you threatened me," I said, "and I listened, and then I went home and thought about what I actually owed, and to whom. You offered me a trade: testify against Adeline Marsh or be deported. I'm declining the trade. I'm testifying anyway, about exactly one thing, and then I'm going to walk down to the census office and give them my true name, and you won't get to hold either of those facts over me again, because I'll have handed them over myself. There's a difference between confession and surrender, Mrs. Calloway. I'd explain it, but I suspect you'd find the distinction inconvenient."

She sat back. For a moment — just a moment — something crossed her face that wasn't triumph. It looked almost like disappointment, the way a child is disappointed when the jack-in-the-box springs and it turns out to be someone she knows.

I gave my testimony. I kept it narrow, the way a surgeon keeps an incision narrow: the ledger, the account, the dates, the sums. I did not mention Rosalind. I did not mention the son in the institution on the hill, whose letters I had retyped through two nights while Tobias fed me tea and quoted Shelley until I threatened to feed him the complete works. I did not mention that Adeline had offered me her own escape and I had refused it, because that was between her and me and the morning tide.

When I finished, Miss Hartley said, quietly, "Why now? Why not when you found it?"

"Because I loved her," I said. "That's not an excuse. It's just the weather I was standing in. And because I kept telling myself that history belongs to bolder people than me, and it took me two years and a ship sailing without me to understand that history doesn't belong to anyone. It just collects whatever we leave lying around. I'd rather leave the truth."

* * *

The census office on Quay Street smelled of ink and damp coats, and the clerk who received me had the face of a man who has spent thirty years being lied to and has stopped taking it personally.

"Name?" he said.

"Vesper Iversen."

"That's the name on the 1911 schedule," he said, tapping a card. "Born in Copenhagen. Father a merchant seaman. Sister named Lark, aged nine at time of enumeration." He looked up. "We've had some difficulty verifying the Copenhagen records."

"There's no difficulty to verify," I said. "I invented her. Lark, Copenhagen, the father — all of it. I was seventeen, I'd walked off a boat at Avonmouth with nothing but a change of clothes and an accent I'd practiced on the crossing, and the census form was on the table of the lodging house, and the landlady said everyone had to be entered, so I entered myself as someone with a family. A sister seemed safer than a husband. Husbands get written to."

The clerk's pen hovered. He was, I think, genuinely uncertain whether to write this down.

"And your true name?"

I gave it. All of it — the town, the year, the mother who died when I was six, the aunt who sold my place in her household to a cousin when I was fifteen, the boat, the crossing, the two years of driving through Bristol being nobody on purpose. Saying it aloud in that room was strange in a way I hadn't anticipated. It was like wearing shoes after a winter barefoot. My name fit worse than the fiction and better at the same time.

He wrote it all down. He asked his questions in the flat tone of a man reading from a form, and I answered in the flat tone of a woman who had finally run out of performances, and somewhere around the question about current residence I realized my hands had stopped shaking entirely.

"You understand," the clerk said, capping his pen, "that falsification of a census schedule carries penalties."

"I understand that I committed it," I said. "I understand that Mrs. Calloway reported me, and that she timed her report to coincide with the sailing of a ship she hoped I'd be on, so that I'd have no choice but to do what she wanted. You might note that too. Not because it excuses me. Because it's true, and your office seems to be in the business of what's true."

"It really isn't," he said, with the first flicker of humanity he'd shown. "But I'll note it."

* * *

They let me go that evening — released pending inquiry, my true name now a matter of record, my future a matter of committees. Tobias was waiting outside on the step, sitting very straight the way he did when he was frightened and determined not to show it, and beside him, holding a sleeping baby wrapped in a shawl that had been fished out of a flooded tea room eight months ago, stood Rosalind.

"Well?" Rosalind said.

"They have my name," I said. "The right one. And the ledger's with the committee, and Calloway got her testimony, just not the way she ordered it."

Tobias exhaled like a boiler venting. "And?"

"And I'm probably fined, possibly removed, and definitely unemployed, since my employer is currently somewhere off the coast of Ireland eating food she bought with subscription money." I sat down on the step next to them. The baby stirred and did not wake. Above us, in the flat above the press, a lamp was already lit — our lamp, the one we'd carried up together, the one that cost us what it cost. "Come on. It's cold, and I've told the truth twice today, which is my limit. Someone make me tea before I start lying again out of habit."

Rosalind laughed, and Tobias hauled me up by the arm, and we went in out of the dark — all four of us, the whole improbable household, assembled from wreckage.

It was only later, sorting the post Tobias had collected from the press that afternoon, that I found the envelope. American stamps. Boston postmark. My name on the front in handwriting I knew better than my own, because I had typed ten thousand letters in it without once signing one.

Adeline had written to me.

I held the envelope a long time on the doorstep, the cold coming up through my boots, the baby crying somewhere upstairs, the tide turning in the harbor below with its terrible punctuality. Some letters you burn unread. I had watched a woman do that once, and I had built a life on deciding I would never be her.

I broke the seal.
