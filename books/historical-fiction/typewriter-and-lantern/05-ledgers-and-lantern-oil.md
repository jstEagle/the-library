## 5. Ledgers and Lantern Oil

Gideon trimmed the wick at half past four, as he had every afternoon for eleven years, and found Ruth Calloway already waiting in the log room when he came down.

She had made herself small in the corner chair, the oilskin packet open on her knees, Marsh's letters fanned out like a hand of cards she was afraid to play. The stove ticked. Outside, the light of the afternoon was the color of dishwater, and the sea moved beneath it with the slow, muscular patience of a thing that had all winter to wait.

"You'll want a table," Gideon said, and dragged one from against the wall without waiting for her answer. It was the table where he ate, where Miriam had once set out bread and salt herring, where he now did his arithmetic in pencil on the backs of supply invoices. He wiped it down with a rag that smelled of paraffin and laid two lamps upon it — his own brass storm lantern and the cheap tin one he kept for the boat shed.

Ruth looked at the table as if it were an altar.

"I don't know how a man cross-examines paper," she said. "Mr. Marsh always did the ledgers. I only carried them."

"Then we'll learn together." He sat down across from her and drew his logbooks toward him — eleven years of them, cloth-bound, their spines cracked white at the hinges. "Every keeper keeps a log. Weather, wind, visibility, ships sighted, signals answered. Every entry signed. It goes to Trinity House, or it used to, before they decided this station wasn't worth the postage." He said it flatly, without bitterness; the bitterness had been spent years ago. "It's not testimony. It's chores."

"That's what Mr. Marsh said about his letters," Ruth said quietly. "He wrote them like a man doing chores. Dates, times, tonnage, names. He thought no one would read them that way. He thought that was what would save them."

Gideon opened the logbook for 1891 and felt something turn over in his chest, slow as the lamp mechanism above them.

* * *

The night of the Ferndale had been the ninth of November, and Gideon had written it down because writing things down was the whole of his employment.

He remembered the night itself in fragments — everyone remembered it in fragments, the whole coast did — but the page remembered it whole. *9 Nov. Wind SSE freshening to gale after midnight. Sea heavy. 11:40 p.m.: blue pyrotechnic observed bearing E by S, approx. 4 miles. Distress signal. Acknowledged by hoisting. Reported by telephone to Kirkcaldy station — line dead. Attempted telegraph — line dead. 12:15 a.m.: second signal observed, same bearing. Rocket apparatus manned but sea state renders launch impossible from east landing. 1:05 a.m.: signals cease.*

*Signals cease.* Two words. Thirty-one lives.

"They cut the lines first," Ruth said. Her finger rested on the entry without touching it, the way one touches nothing one is afraid to wake. "That's what Mr. Marsh wrote. The agent — Halloran, his name was Halloran — ordered the Ferndale loaded past her marks, and when the weather turned he knew what would come of it. So before she went down, the wires went down. Not the storm. Men with pliers and a cart. There was a lineman on the mainland who swore afterward he'd seen them, and then he stopped swearing."

"And the ship went out overloaded anyway."

"She went out because the insurance was worth more than the cargo and crew together. That's the arithmetic of it. Mr. Marsh kept the loading figures. He was the clerk; he entered them himself, in his own hand, because Halloran liked everything in Marsh's hand — neat, unimpeachable. Then when the inquiry began to smell wrong, Marsh became a drowned man. Officially. They buried a name, not a body." She looked up. "You were at that funeral. You told me."

"I was." Gideon turned the pages back, to the days before. *8 Nov. Barometer falling steadily. Advised by passing smack that Ferndale taking on cargo at Burntisland, departure delayed.* His own handwriting, younger then, more careful. A man recording the approach of a catastrophe one chore at a time, knowing nothing, suspecting nothing, and thereby preserving everything.

"The barometer readings," he said slowly. "They'll want to say the storm was unprecedented. An act of God. But here—" he ran his finger down the column— "here, and here, and here. Falling for thirty-six hours before she sailed. Anyone reading this knew a gale was coming. They sailed anyway."

Ruth pulled one of Marsh's letters across the table and laid it beside the logbook, aligning the dates. And there it was: two hands, two rooms, two lives that had never touched, saying the same thing in the same hours. Marsh's neat clerk's figures — *loaded to 14 ft 2 in., marks submerged, protested, overruled* — and Gideon's flat keeper's prose — *barometer falling steadily* — corroborating each other across forty miles of black water like two witnesses who had never met.

Neither of them spoke for a while. The stove ticked. Above them, faint through the floor, the great lens turned in its bath of mercury, keeping its patient time.

"It matches," Ruth said at last, and her voice cracked on the plainness of it. "Every date. Every hour. I've been carrying these letters for eight months, Mr. Okafor, sleeping in hedgerows, and I never once put them next to anything true. I didn't know there was anything true left to put them next to."

"There's always the log," Gideon said. Then he heard himself, and almost laughed — a short, rusty sound. "Eleven years I've written that book. Wind, weather, wicks trimmed, oil consumed. I used to think if I died tomorrow my whole life would fit in those columns and weigh nothing." He pressed his palm flat against the page, over the words *signals cease*. "And all the while it was this. A record no one reads is still a record. It was waiting."

* * *

Ruth worked the way, Gideon suspected, she had learned to work under Elias Marsh: precisely, silently, with a clerk's suspicion of her own memory. She copied nothing at first. She only matched — letter to logbook, date to date, laying the papers side by side on the scarred table until the lamplight fell evenly across both hands' evidence.

By the second night she had begun to trust him with the letters themselves, handing them across the table one at a time, watching his face as he read.

"He numbered them," she said. "See, in the corner. One through nineteen. He knew they'd have to survive without him. He said a testimony should be built like a lighthouse — so that any single piece failing doesn't bring the rest down."

"Did he." Gideon held letter seven to the flame-light. Marsh's hand was exactly as he remembered it from the funeral — no. From before the funeral. From the one time they had spoken, at the harbormaster's office, when Marsh had come to file some shipping paper and Gideon had waited behind him with a requisition for lamp oil. *Neat, unimpeachable.* A hand built to be believed.

"He's ill," Ruth said suddenly, into the quiet. "I haven't said it. He didn't want it in the letters in case they were seized. But he's ill, and getting worse, and that's why he sent me instead of coming himself. That's why the last ones are shorter." She smoothed the edge of letter seven with her bandaged thumb — the burn from the fire had scabbed dark along the side of her hand, and Gideon had watched her refuse, twice now, to let him dress it properly. "So whatever we build has to be finished. Not almost. Finished."

"Then we finish it." Gideon pulled the logbook for November 1891 fully open and weighted its pages with the tin lamp. "Start with the wires. If the lines were cut before midnight, someone on this coast may have logged the outage too. The Kirkcaldy station kept records. The ferry office kept records. Everyone on this coast writes things down, Mrs. Calloway — it's the national disease. We just have to find the pages before Vane's people do."

At the name, Ruth's shoulders drew in, and Gideon realized how often, these past days, they had done that — how the woman flinched at doors, at footsteps, at the sound of the supply boat's engine, though the fire had already taught her what Vane's politeness cost.

"He'll be back," she said. It was not a question.

"He'll be back." Gideon fed the stove another knot of peat. "A man like that doesn't stop asking questions. He just changes whose they are." He looked at the table — the letters, the logs, the two lamps burning side by side, his brass and her tin, throwing one shared circle of light. "Which means we work between shifts, and we hide nothing aboard this rock, and we assume every page can be taken from us tomorrow. Can you copy fast?"

"I can copy beautifully," Ruth said, and for the first time since she had washed ashore, something in her face that was not fear showed itself — dry, tired, nearly a smile. "Mr. Marsh used to say I wrote his hand better than he did."

"Then you'll write his hand," Gideon said, "and I'll write mine, and we'll make three of everything. One to send, one to keep, one to lose gracefully if we must." He uncapped the ink. "The historians will come with the thaw, you said. Then the thaw is our deadline, and winter is our workshop."

Outside, the wind had begun again, working up out of the southwest with that low, gathering note he had learned to hear the way other men heard church bells. He noted it automatically — *wind rising* — and then paused, pen above the page, struck anew by the old, astonishing truth of it: that even this was testimony. That the storm itself was being sworn in.

* * *

They fell into a rhythm over the following days, as natural as tide-tables. Gideon kept the light; Ruth kept the copies. Between shifts they sat at the eaten-on, worked-on table and built the account piece by piece — Marsh's numbers, Gideon's weather, the dead telegraph lines, the signals that ceased at 1:05 a.m. — until the packet of loose letters began to grow a spine, a shape, a voice.

On the fourth evening, matching Marsh's letter twelve against the logbook, Gideon found the entry he had forgotten making.

*10 Nov., 6:20 a.m. Wreckage washing ashore below east cliff — boats, spars, one lifebuoy marked FERNDALE. Three bodies recovered by crofters. Sea moderating.*

Beneath it, in his own younger hand, a line he had added almost as an apology, because the form had a space for remarks:

*No rescue attempted or possible. Lines down since prior evening. Light burned full throughout.*

*Light burned full throughout.* Six words. He had written them defensively, he remembered — afraid someone would ask why the tower hadn't saved them, wanting it known that the fault was not his lamp's. And now those six words sat on the page like a verdict, not on the light but on the men who had cut the wires and loaded the hold and let thirty-one souls steam out to meet a gale they had charted in advance.

"Read that," he said, and pushed the book across to Ruth.

She read it. She read it twice. When she looked up, her eyes were wet, but her voice was steady as a sounding-line.

"'Light burned full throughout,'" she repeated. "Mr. Okafor — do you understand what you've been doing all these years? You weren't keeping house. You were keeping witness."

Before Gideon could answer — before he could admit that he was only beginning to understand, that the awe of it sat in his chest like swallowed coal, warm and terrible — the sound came up through the floorboards and the soles of his feet both: the engine of the supply boat, off schedule, running hard against the rising wind, and a whistle blowing three short blasts that were not any signal he had ever taught the water to expect.

Vane was coming back. And he had not come alone.
