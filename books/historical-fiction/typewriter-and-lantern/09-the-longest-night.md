## 9. The Longest Night

The barometer began to fall at noon on the third of February, and by four o'clock it had fallen so far that the needle lay against the stop like a bird pressed flat against a window. Gideon had kept instruments for nineteen years. He had watched the glass drop before gales, before hurricanes, before the night in '87 when three fishing boats went to pieces on the Skerries and he had counted forty-one flashes of distress from his gallery and logged every one. He had never seen it fall like this.

"Ruth," he said quietly. "Come and look at this."

She came up from the log room with Marsh's letters bundled against her chest, as she carried them now everywhere, even to fetch water, even to sleep. She read the barometer the way she read everything — quickly, completely — and then she looked at him.

"How long?"

"The wind will back to the southeast tonight. By midnight we'll have the full weight of it." He touched the glass case gently, the way one touches a sick man's forehead. "This is not a storm, Miss Calloway. This is a verdict."

They worked through the afternoon in the particular silence of people preparing for siege. Gideon trimmed the wicks and filled the oil reservoirs until the lamp room smelled of paraffin and hot brass. He tested the rotation mechanism twice, then a third time, though he knew the clockwork was sound; his hands wanted occupation, and he let them have it. Below, Ruth copied nothing more. The copying was finished. What remained of Marsh's testimony — thirty-one names, the orders given aboard the *Ferndale*, the payments made, the death certificate signed over a living man's signature — sat now in a single fair copy, typed on Gideon's own machine, with the original letters folded inside it.

And beneath it all, the logbook. His own logbook, nineteen years of weather and light and ships seen and ships lost, open to the page from the eleventh of November, 1891: *2:14 a.m., blue rockets observed bearing ENE, Ferndale showing distress. No vessel put out. Rockets ceased 2:51.* Thirty-seven minutes. He had written those lines without knowing what they were. Now they were scripture.

At dusk the first squall came out of the dark like a thrown fist, and the tower shuddered on its foundations.

* * *

The supply boat had come two days ago, and Vane had come with it, and gone again, smiling, believing the packet slept in the oil store where Gideon's careful stammering had placed it. But Gideon had watched the boat stand off the landing stage longer than it needed to, and he had seen the way the boatman — Tam Breck, who owed money to half the coast — would not meet his eye. A man who has lied learns to recognize the shape of other men's lies, and Gideon did not believe Vane had left the island at all.

So when the knock came at half past eleven — three blows, deliberate, patient, above the screaming of the wind — he was not surprised. He was only afraid, in the way a man is afraid when the thing he has dreaded arrives precisely on schedule.

Ruth was already moving. She took the fair copy and the letters and the logbook and wrapped them in oilskin, and she looked at him across the lamplit table and said, "The wireless key is in the signal room. Go."

"There are five of them," Gideon said. "I counted shadows on the path."

"Then I'll be slow about opening doors."

He caught her wrist. Her burned hands were bandaged still, the right one stiff as a claw, and she did not flinch when he gripped her. "Ruth."

"Gideon." She said his name as if it were an argument won. "You watched a ship die and wrote down every minute of it because it was your duty to write things down. I forged a man's death and have spent two years carrying his pages through storms. Neither of us came here to lose them to a lawyer's hired fists. Go and send your message. I will hold this door until the sea itself comes through it."

He went.

* * *

The signal room was a stone cell at the top of the dwelling, built in the days when the Board had imagined telegraph wires reaching the rock, and abandoned when the cable failed. The instrument was old, its contacts green with salt, but Gideon had cleaned it monthly for years — not because anyone might hear him, but because a keeper keeps things; it was the whole of his religion. He connected the battery, set the fair copy before him, and began to send.

*TO NAVAL STATION INVERNESS STOP TESTIMONY FERNDALE WRECK NOV 11 1891 FOLLOWING STOP*

His fist was steady. Nineteen years of logging had given him a clerk's patience and a surgeon's economy, and the key spoke under his fingers in its small dry voice, that tiny clicking utterly swallowed by the roar of the storm, going out into the black howl toward a mainland he could not see, toward ears that might or might not be listening, on a frequency some junior operator might or might not be watching on the worst night of the winter.

It did not matter. That was the revelation that arrived somewhere between the second paragraph and the third, between the name of the owner's agent and the sum paid to the harbormaster: it did not matter whether anyone heard. What mattered was that the sending was true. Miriam had taught him that, in her quiet way, in the last year of her life, when she had stopped pretending the medicines were working and started putting her affairs in order with such calm that he had understood, finally, that courage was not the absence of fear but the refusal to let fear do your writing for you.

Below him, something heavy struck the outer door.

* * *

He heard it through the floor and the walls and the wind: a boom like a ram, then another. Then voices, thin and torn apart by the gale, and the splintering complaint of timber. They had brought a battering — an oar, perhaps, or a spar from Breck's boat. The outer door was oak, but oak is oak, and men with a grudge and a lever are men.

Gideon sent faster. His fingers flew. *AGENT HOLLIS ORDERED DOUBLE CARGO AGAINST CHIEF MATE'S PROTEST STOP LIFEBOATS INSUFFICIENT STOP MARSH PAID TO SIGN FALSE MANIFEST AND LATER DECLARED DROWNED STOP*

A crash below, nearer — the inner passage door. Then Ruth's voice, high and clear and furious, cutting through the storm like the light itself cut through it: "This is a lighthouse of the Northern Lights Board, you fools! Every minute you spend breaking my door is a minute your master's name goes further out into the world!"

Something slammed. Something else — a body, he thought, sickened, hitting stone. Then her voice again, ragged but standing: "Still here!"

He sent. His eyes blurred and he wiped them on his sleeve without stopping, because a sender cannot stop, a sender must keep the rhythm or the receiving operator loses the thread and the whole testament dissolves into noise. *WITNESS ELIAS MARSH ALIVE IN EXILE STOP ORIGINAL DOCUMENTS SECURED LIGHTHOUSE ROCK ISLET STOP REQUEST IMMEDIATE INQUIRY BEFORE RECORDS DESTROYED STOP*

The lamp turned above him. He could feel it through the ceiling, that great slow heartbeat, sweeping its beam out over a sea gone entirely white with fury, and there was a terrible beauty in it — the light burning steady while everything human beneath it shook and cracked and held. He thought: this is what Miriam saw in me. Not the keeping. The refusing to go dark.

The door to the signal room stair boomed once, twice. Then a new sound: Ruth's voice, no longer shouting, speaking low and rapid, and another voice answering — Vane's, smooth even through a foot of oak, even through a hurricane. Gideon could not make out the words. He did not need to. He knew an offer when he heard one being refused.

*REPEAT ALL PRECEDING STOP* And he sent it all again, every name, every date, every figure, while the door shook and Ruth Calloway stood against the end of the world with her burned hands and her unburned conscience.

* * *

Near three in the morning, the stair door gave.

He heard the wood fail — a crack like ice on a loch — and then boots on stone, several pairs, and he did the last thing left to do. He locked the signal room door behind him, pocketed the key, swept the fair copy and the letters and the logbook into the oilskin satchel, and tied it around his own body, under his coat, against his heart, where Miriam had once rested her head on cold nights when the fire was low.

Then he climbed.

The gallery door opened onto annihilation. The wind took his breath and gave him back nothing; the rain came sideways, hard as shot; the sea below was a churning cathedral of white. He made his way by the rail to the lamp room, sealed himself inside with the light, and stood among the warm brass and the turning glass with the satchel strapped to him and the storm trying to peel the roof off heaven.

Through the gallery glass, distorted by streaming water, he saw them come out onto the rock below — lanterns bobbing, figures bent double, Vane's tall silhouette gesturing up at the tower like a man cursing God from a pulpit. They could not climb in this. They could not even stand. They could only wait, as he waited, as everyone on that drowned coast waited, for the storm to finish its sentence.

And the light turned. Around and around, patient as forgiveness, flinging itself out to the limit of its reach and gathering itself and flinging again. Gideon Okafor put his hand flat against the warm glass of the lens and felt the clockwork tick beneath his palm like a second heart.

Somewhere out in that black chaos, he chose to believe, a young operator at Inverness was writing down every word. Somewhere, a name was being entered into a record that could no longer be bought or burned. Elias Marsh, alive. Hollis, guilty. Thirty-one souls, counted.

The door below would not hold forever. Neither would the storm. But the message was already loose in the world, and messages, unlike men, do not drown.

Far out in the white roaring dark, through a gap in the cloud the size of a coffin lid, Gideon saw a light that was not his own — steady, low on the southern horizon, answering.

A ship. Running for the rock through the worst night of the year.

He reached for his glasses, and his blood went cold, and cold again.
