## 3. Enemy Property

The river spoke to her before she saw it.

Greta had been walking since the grey hour before dawn, when the fields outside Ashford were still the color of pewter and her breath came out in small white ghosts that vanished as though ashamed to exist. Now, near midday, the sleet had turned to a fine needle-rain that found every seam in her coat, and through it she heard the Thames working at its edges — a low, grinding mutter, like a great animal turning in its sleep. The ice was forming. She had perhaps an hour, perhaps less, before the last crossing closed and England became an island again in earnest.

Her valise weighed almost nothing. That was the terrible arithmetic of flight: everything essential fit into one hand. A change of linen. Her mother's cameo. And beneath the false bottom of the case, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, forty-one letters tied with the ribbon from her wedding bouquet — every word Thomas had ever sent her, from the first shy note he'd left at a Heidelberg bookshop to the last, written from a training camp in Kent with a fountain pen that bled through the paper because his hand shook. *My dearest Greta. If anything should happen—* But nothing had happened, not to him; he had simply stopped, one wet November morning on a road in Flanders, and the stopping of him had happened to everyone else instead.

She crossed the meadow with the river's voice growing louder, and there it was: the bridge, a long stone spine arched over water gone the color of gunmetal. Ice laced the shallows at either bank in white ferns. On the far side, past the bare elms, she could see the chimneys of Ashcombe House standing up against a sky the color of dirty wool, smoke rising straight and thin from two of them.

Two chimneys smoking meant someone was home. Someone being home meant Beatrix Eastwick had kept her word.

Greta stood at the foot of the bridge and let herself feel, for exactly ten seconds, the full weight of what she was about to do. Then she counted — *eins, zwei, drei* — the way Thomas had taught her to steady herself before examinations, before train journeys, before the day she'd walked down the aisle of St. Mary's with half the village whispering *German girl* behind their hymnals — and stepped onto the stones.

Halfway across, the wind came down the river valley and hit her like a door flung open. It carried sleet sideways, carried the smell of coming frost, carried — faintly, impossibly — the sound of a typewriter. No. Imagination. Grief did that; grief filled empty air with the voices of the dead and the sounds of places you needed to be. She gripped the parapet, cold biting through her gloves, and looked down at the black water sliding under the ice shelves, and thought: *this is the last time I will cross this bridge as a free woman.* Whether that freedom ended in shelter or in a cell, she could not yet say. She walked on.

* * *

Beatrix Eastwick opened the kitchen door before Greta could knock.

Later, Greta would learn that Beatrix had watched her crossing from the stable loft, field glasses borrowed from the gamekeeper's cupboard pressed to her eyes, and had run the length of the garden with her coat unbuttoned and flying. But in the moment there was only the door swinging inward, the sudden bloom of warmth smelling of bread and coal smoke, and a young woman with ink-stained fingers seizing her by both arms and hauling her over the threshold as though the river itself were at her heels.

"You came." Beatrix's voice cracked on the second word. "You actually — the ice is nearly—"

"I know." Greta's teeth were chattering too hard for eloquence. "I ran the last mile."

"You're frozen through. Here—" Beatrix pushed her down into the chair nearest the stove, knelt, and began pulling off Greta's boots with brisk, impersonal efficiency, as though she were a nurse and this were triage. Her hands were shaking. So were Greta's. Their fingers collided over a bootlace and neither of them laughed, though something almost like laughter passed between them — the recognition of shared fear wearing the mask of practicality.

"Mrs. Ashcombe doesn't know," Beatrix said. It wasn't a question.

"No."

"She burned the solicitor's letter. I saw her do it. Which means she knows what it says, which means—" Beatrix stopped herself, jaw tightening. "It doesn't matter. I promised. A promise made in peacetime holds in wartime or it isn't worth the breath it cost."

Greta looked at her properly then. In 1912 she had known Beatrix only as a presence at the edge of garden parties — the quiet driver, the young woman with the notebook who watched everything and wrote down some of it. She had seemed then like a girl playing at being invisible. The woman kneeling at her feet now was not playing at anything. There was a line between her brows that hadn't existed two years ago, and her eyes held the particular steadiness of someone who has already decided what she will do and is merely waiting for the world to catch up.

"Thomas trusted you," Greta said.

Something moved across Beatrix's face — grief, quickly folded away, like a letter tucked into a pocket. "He trusted everyone. It was his best quality and the thing that killed him, trusting that the war would make sense if he just behaved honorably enough." She stood, brisk again. "Bath. Clothes. Food. In that order. Then we'll get you upstairs before the household wakes properly."

"The household?"

"Cook, two maids, and Mrs. Danvers who comes in for laundry. None of whom can know." Beatrix met her eyes. "I'm sorry. I know how it sounds — hiding you like contraband. But the village is already counting cigarette packets at the post office and asking after foreign voices. War makes people into—"

"Informants," Greta finished quietly. "I know the word. We have it in German too. *Denunzianten.*" She said it the way one touches a scar.

Beatrix went still. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be careful." Greta reached out and caught the younger woman's wrist — ink-stained, pulse jumping fast beneath the skin. "Listen to me, Fräulein Eastwick. You are risking prison for a woman you have spoken to perhaps four times in your life. I need you to understand what you are doing. Not the romance of it. The reality. If they find you, they will not care that your heart is good. They will care only that your ledgers are false and your guest is illegal."

For a moment Beatrix said nothing. Then she turned her wrist gently free, crossed to the dresser, and took down a plate, laying it with bread, butter, a rind of cheese, and a spoonful of plum jam saved from summer.

"Eat," she said. "Then I'll show you where you'll live."

* * *

The attic above the stables smelled of apples and dust and old hay — a sweetness like the inside of a violin.

Beatrix had prepared it, Greta realized slowly, taking in the details one by one. Prepared it days ago, perhaps weeks. An iron bedstead with a real mattress, blankets aired by a fire's warmth still lingering in the bricks. A washstand. A candle lantern with a stock of candles lined up like soldiers. On the crate that served as a table: a teapot, two cups, a tin of biscuits, and — Greta's throat closed — a small stack of English novels, their spines cracked with love, and a dictionary. German-English. Secondhand, its cover mended with tape.

"When did you—" Greta began.

"August," Beatrix said, not looking at her. "After the war started. I told myself I was being foolish. Sentimental. Preparing a refuge for someone who would never need it." She busied herself trimming the candle wick with her thumbnail. "But I kept thinking about what you said to me at the Captain's memorial service. Do you remember? Everyone else was saying *he died a hero* and *he died for England*, and you said — very quietly, only to me — you said, *he died because men make wars and women bury the results.*"

"I don't remember saying it."

"I remember hearing it." Beatrix set the lantern on the table and lit it. The flame steadied, throwing warm light up the sloped rafters.
