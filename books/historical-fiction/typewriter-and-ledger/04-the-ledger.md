## 4. The Ledger

The Ashcombe household accounts lived in a green ledger with a cracked spine, and for three years I had kept it the way a nun keeps her prayers — faithfully, precisely, without imagination. Flour by the sack. Coal by the hundredweight. Candles by the dozen, though Lady Vivian worked by lamplight on principle, saying electricity was a luxury the country could not afford while it argued about whether she might be permitted a vote. Every figure in my neat hand. Every column balanced to the farthing. I had believed, in the way one believes the floor will hold, that the numbers were simply the numbers.

Then there were three mouths in the house where the books recorded two, and the floor began to creak.

It started with bread. Mrs. Tilly baked on Tuesdays and Fridays, and Greta, hidden above the stables, ate what I carried up in a covered basket disguised as feed for the carriage horses. But a woman who has walked half-frozen through a winter night burns through bread like a furnace through coal, and by the second Friday the loaf I'd smuggled up was gone before supper, and Greta's thanks had gone thin at the edges, the way her voice did when she was trying not to be a burden. So I began buying double. Two sacks of flour from Hobbes the miller, one written in the ledger. A second loaf from the village baker, paid for in coins I counted out with my heart hammering, entered as *bread, household — 4d* when it had cost eight.

It was, I told myself, a small arithmetic of mercy. Two loaves bought, one written. What was the difference, really, between a lie of omission and a lie of addition? Both were still lies. Both still made my ears hot when I sat across from Lady Vivian at the long table in the morning room, the ledger open between us, her finger running down the columns while I held my breath like a diver.

"Flour is up," she said one grey Tuesday, not looking at me.

"War prices, my lady. Hobbes says the railways are prioritizing munitions."

"Mm." Her finger tapped once on the figure and moved on. She said nothing else. But I had driven that woman through eleven years of heckling crowds and thrown stones and one memorable afternoon in Carlisle when a milk churn had come through the windscreen of the motor, and I knew the difference between her silences. There was the silence of a lady who hadn't noticed, which was rare. And there was the silence of a woman who had noticed everything and was deciding what to do about it, which was not rare at all.

This was the second kind. I could feel it sitting in the room with us like a third person, patient as winter.

* * *

The typewriter was my own — a Remington I'd bought secondhand with my first wages, because a driver who read every letter her employer dictated might as well be useful with the keys as well as the wheel. It lived in my narrow room above the coach house, and at night, when the house slept, I sat before it in my dressing gown with a candle guttering and forged.

That is the word for it. I had tried out softer ones in the first week — *adjusted, amended, tidied* — but the candlelight and the small clicking keys did not permit softness. I was forging. I was inventing receipts for goods never purchased so that the household's cash would stretch to cover goods purchased twice. I was creating, out of thin air and ink ribbon, a fictional Mrs. Tilly who required an extravagant quantity of dripping, a chimney that needed sweeping twice in a fortnight, a horse liniment bought in quantities no sane stable kept. Each page I rolled out was a small act of treason against the truth, and I found — this was the part that frightened me — that I was good at it.

I had spent my whole life believing I was a person things happened *to*. Letters passed through my hands; I read them aloud; history happened to other people, louder people, people with titles and causes and names in the newspapers. But the keys under my fingers at midnight made a different argument. Every forged line was a sentence I had authored. Every balanced column was a plot I had constructed and was now living inside. I had become, without ever intending it, a novelist of the household accounts, and the story I was writing had a hidden woman in the attic and a lie at the center of it that grew one farthing wider every day.

Some nights Greta came down the outside stair to the coach house, wrapped in one of the Captain's old coats, and sat on the end of my bed to watch. She never touched the machine. She only watched my hands and listened to the clatter, and once she said, "In my father's office in Hamburg, there was a woman who did this. Fraulein Brandt. She had ink on her fingers always, and she hummed." A pause. "She would have liked you."

"She'd have arrested me," I said. "This is fraud."

"It is breakfast," Greta said. "It is bread for a person. The war has made everything into a crime except the actual crimes."

I did not answer, because the keys were clicking and the candle was burning and somewhere above us, in the big dark house, Lady Vivian Ashcombe was awake too. I knew it the way you know weather. The lamp in her study window burned past one o'clock, and I had learned that when the lamp burned late, she was reading the newspapers she'd hoarded since the bridge closed, all of them weeks old, all of them full of lists. Casualty lists. Shipments lost at sea. And — though I did not let myself think it directly, I thought it sidelong, the way you look at the sun — lists of another kind. Enemy aliens. Registration orders. The names of people who were, as of November, no longer neighbors but categories.

The lamp burned. The keys clicked. The house held its breath.

* * *

It was Mrs. Tilly who nearly undid us, and it was bread that did it — the same bread, the doubled loaves, the arithmetic of mercy. She came to me in the kitchen passage on a Thursday, wiping her hands on her apron, her face wearing the particular expression of a woman about to say something she has rehearsed.

"Miss Eastwick. I don't meddle. You know I don't."

"I know it, Mrs. Tilly."

"But there's flour going out of my pantry that isn't going into my ovens. Two sacks this fortnight, and my loaves are no bigger. And the dripping." She lowered her voice as though the walls had ears, which, given the attic above us, they very nearly did. "And there's a smell of coffee in the coach house of a morning, and I don't keep coffee, and you don't drink it."

The passage was very quiet. Somewhere above, a floorboard settled with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"Mrs. Tilly," I said, and heard my own voice come out steady, which surprised me, "if I told you that everything is as it should be, would you believe me?"

She looked at me for a long moment. She was sixty if she was a day, and she had cooked for Lady Vivian through the window-smashings and the hunger strikes and the year the Captain died, and she had never once, in all that time, asked a question she didn't already know the answer to.

"I'd believe," she said slowly, "that you're doing what you think is right, and that her ladyship knows more than she says, same as she always does. And I'd believe that if I'm to bake bigger loaves and ask nothing, then I'm to bake bigger loaves and ask nothing." She turned back toward the kitchen, then stopped. "The coffee, though. You'll want to be more careful with the coffee. Smell travels in the cold."

She went in, and the door closed, and I stood in the passage understanding that the conspiracy was now four, and that I had not recruited her — she had recruited herself, silently, with a sack of flour as her oath of allegiance. The house was changing shape around its secret, the way a river changes shape around a stone.

* * *

Lady Vivian said nothing for eleven days. I counted. I had begun counting everything — loaves, receipts, candles, silences — as though the winter could be managed by arithmetic, as though if the columns balanced, the world would.

On the twelfth day she summoned me to the morning room after supper. The lamp was turned low. The green ledger lay on the table, closed, and her hand rested on its cover the way one rests a hand on the neck of a horse that might bolt.

"Sit down, Miss Eastwick."

I sat. The chair was cold.

"Twenty-two years I have fought the Treasury, the Home Office, the Liberal Party, and the Archbishop of Canterbury, and I will tell you what I have learned about ledgers." She opened the book and turned it toward me, and her finger came down on the flour column, and stayed there. "No one is caught by the size of a lie. They are caught by its tidiness. A man who steals wildly is forgiven as a fool. A man who steals *neatly* is hanged as a planner." Her eyes came up to mine, grey and level and terribly awake. "Your chimney has been swept twice in a month. Your horse does not consume that much liniment unless it is dying, and I have seen the horse. And you have developed, in six weeks, the handwriting of a woman who is thinking about every letter she forms."

The fire ticked. I did not breathe.

"I am not asking you what it is," she said. "I am a suffragist, Miss Eastwick. I have spent two decades being lied about by people who never once had the courage to lie to my face. I know what a concealed woman smells like — coffee, in this case." She closed the ledger, softly, and pushed it across the table to me, and her voice, when it came again, had dropped into something quieter and far more dangerous. "I am asking you only this. Is she safe? Is she warm? And does she know that the letters her husband wrote to her are in the drawer of my desk, that I have not been able to open them in fourteen months, and that I should like — very much, and on my own terms, and in my own time — to be introduced to his widow?"

The candle flame leaned. Outside, far off across the fields, the river ice boomed once, a long groan like a door opening in the floor of the world, and in the silence that followed it, I heard my own voice say: "She's safe, my lady. She's warm. And she's been reading them to herself every night — but I think she'd read them aloud, if you asked."

Lady Vivian Ashcombe looked at me for a long moment, and then she rose, and took the key to her desk drawer from the chain at her waist, and held it out to me across the green ledger with all its tidy, criminal, merciful lies.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "you will drive me nowhere at all — because the bridge is closed, and we are none of us going anywhere — and instead you will carry a message to the attic. Tell her that her mother-in-law is a stubborn old woman with a fire lit in the small drawing room, and that there is a chair by it which has been empty for fourteen months."
