## 5. What the Village Saw

The postmistress of Lower Marleigh had counted things all her life, and she had grown very good at it.

Edith Crake had kept the village post office for nineteen years — first her father's ledgers, then her own — and in that time she had learned that a community tells on itself through its arithmetic. She knew that Farmer Dill bought stamps in fives and never more, because he wrote only to his sister at Christmas and to the corn merchant monthly. She knew that the vicar's housekeeper posted parcels every Thursday without fail, and that Mrs. Pell's letters went out in bursts, thick with paper, whenever Parliament did something foolish. She knew the weight of every envelope by the sound it made when it dropped into her brass tray, and she could tell you, without looking up from her counter, whether the Ashcombe post had come heavy or light.

Lately, the Ashcombe post had come wrong.

It was not the volume. Lady Vivian Ashcombe had always received a formidable quantity of mail — pamphlets, petitions, correspondence from women in Manchester who signed themselves *Yours in the Cause* — and the war had thinned it but not silenced it. No; what was wrong was subtler than volume, and Edith Crake prided herself on subtlety. The outgoing mail had changed hands. For years the Ashcombe letters had arrived at her window in Lady Vivian's own hand, that fierce upright script like fence palings. Now they came mostly typed, page after page of even black type, and they came in Miss Eastwick's hand when they came by hand at all — a small, quick, slanted hand, the hand of someone writing fast and hoping not to be noticed.

And there were other sums that would not balance.

The butcher's boy delivered to Ashcombe House twice weekly, as he always had, but the order slips had grown fat while the household had grown thin. Two joints where one would do. A double measure of tea. Eggs by the dozen and a half when the cook's requisitions had never once exceeded ten. Edith Crake heard this from her sister-in-law, who heard it from the butcher's wife, who copied the figures into her own little book the way other women copied recipes. *Feeding an army,* the butcher's wife said, and laughed, and then did not laugh, because there was no army at Ashcombe. There was an old woman, a driver, a cook, a gardener gone grey, and a boy who chopped wood.

Edith Crake stood behind her little window with its brass grille and thought about arithmetic. Sugar was rationed now, or nearly. Flour climbed by the week. In the villages along the coast they said German shops had been smashed in London, and here in Lower Marleigh there was no German shop, no German family, no German anything — except, whispered a voice she did not quite let herself hear, whatever it was that ate like two people and wrote like one.

She began a new column in her private ledger. She did not write anything in it yet. She only ruled the lines, neat and straight, and waited for the numbers to come to her.

* * *

Constable Tredwell had been a policeman in Lower Marleigh for eleven years, and in eleven years his most serious case had been a poached deer and a dispute over a drystone wall that had ended with both farmers bleeding and neither apologizing. He liked it that way. He liked knowing every dog by name and every drunkard by his route home. War, he had assumed, would happen elsewhere, in France, in the newspapers, in the mouths of the recruiting sergeant who came through in September and left again with three boys and a promise.

But war, it turned out, sent letters.

They came from the county office now, buff-coloured envelopes with instructions in language Tredwell had to read twice: *aliens of German origin residing within your district are to be identified, registered, and reported.* He sat at his kitchen table with the list before him and the fire ticking, and he read the names. There were none. Not one German-born soul registered in Lower Marleigh. He had checked the parish records himself, walking to the vestry in the rain, and the vicar had confirmed it over sherry: no Germans here, Constable, none at all, though of course one hears things.

One hears things. That was the trouble. Since November, everyone heard things.

Tredwell made his rounds anyway, because the county office expected rounds, and because a man likes to feel useful. He called at the forge, where young Willet was shoeing a cart horse and talking too loudly about Kitchener. He called at the Green Man, where the fire was lit and the talk was of mud in Flanders and the price of oats, and where conversation dipped, just slightly, when he came in — the way water dips around a stone.

"Evening, Constable."

"Tredwell."

He took his half-pint and stood with his back to the room, which was how he preferred to listen. Behind him the talk resumed, and he let it wash over him.

"—Ashcombe, you mean? Well, she'd say that, wouldn't she, she thinks the Kaiser and the King ought to settle it over tea—"

"—no, I'm telling you, the orders have doubled, my missus sees the slips—"

"—and who's to say what's up in that great pile anyway? Half the rooms shut since the Captain died. You could hide a regiment up there."

"You could hide anything," someone said, and then, quieter, in the tone of a man trying a key in a lock: "You could hide anyone."

Tredwell drank his beer slowly. On his next round he stopped at the schoolhouse gate, where Mrs. Pell was pinning up a notice about a knitting circle for the Red Cross, her fingers red with cold, her hat pinned at the defiant angle he associated with photographs in the *Mercury* — the ones where women were being dragged by their coats from Whitehall pavements.

"Afternoon, Mrs. Pell."

"Constable." She did not stop pinning. "Come to arrest me for wool?"

"Just asking after voices." He tried to make it sound like weather-talk. "Foreign voices. Anyone passing through, wintering over, that sort of thing. County wants it noted."

Mrs. Pell straightened and looked at him, and her look was long enough that he found himself studying the churchyard wall behind her.

"I'll tell you what I've heard," she said. "I've heard a good many men who couldn't find France on a map last spring calling for internment camps now. I've heard the baker ask Mrs. Hobbes if she's sure her mother's people were from Jersey and not Jever. And I've heard," she said, folding her cold hands, "that the county office sends out lists, and that lists want filling, and that a constable with an empty list is a constable somebody's going to start asking questions of. Is that the sort of thing you wanted noted?"

"It's the sort of thing," Tredwell said stiffly, "that keeps a village safe."

"Does it." She gathered her remaining notices under her arm. "My husband's family came from the Palatinate in 1709, Constable. Three hundred years of English soil, and by Christmas I expect someone will be counting our candle-ends. You note that, if you like. Good afternoon."

He watched her go up the lane, small and straight-backed against the grey sky, and he thought — not for the first time, and with growing unease — that the empty list in his pocket felt less like a clean sheet every day, and more like a debt.

* * *

Beatrix felt the change in the village the way one feels weather coming — not in any single sign, but in the pressure behind ordinary things.

The greengrocer's wife, who had once pressed a spare apple into her hand for the drive home, now weighed everything twice and looked past Beatrix's shoulder while doing it. The woman at the draper's asked, sweetly, whether the House was keeping well, and whether *everyone* at the House was keeping well, and the emphasis fell so lightly it might have been nothing at all. At the post office window, Edith Crake's eyes went to the typed pages in Beatrix's hand with an attention that had stopped being professional somewhere around the new year.

"They're counting," Mrs. Pell said.

It was Thursday, and they were walking back from the knitting circle through the churchyard, where the yews stood black against a sky the colour of dishwater. Beatrix carried the basket of wool. Her heart, which had been beating steadily all afternoon, now performed a single enormous knock against her ribs and resumed, too loud.

"Counting what?"

"Eggs. Cigarettes. Candle-ends." Mrs. Pell walked with her chin up, addressing the graves as much as Beatrix. "Edith Crake has started a book. Don't ask me how I know; I know. She counts what goes up the hill and what comes down, and she has noticed, as the whole village has noticed, that Ashcombe eats like a widow with a regiment. And Tredwell goes about asking after foreign voices, and every time he asks, someone remembers something they'd forgotten. That's how it works, Miss Eastwick. Fear doesn't need evidence. It needs arithmetic."

Beatrix kept her eyes on the path. Gravel crunched. Somewhere beyond the wall, the river ran under its lid of ice, patient as a held breath.

"People are frightened," she said carefully. "Frightened people talk. It doesn't mean—"

"It means exactly what it means." Mrs. Pell stopped by the lych-gate and turned to face her, and for a moment Beatrix saw her not as the knitting-circle woman with the defiant hat but as she must have looked on the pavement at Whitehall, jaw set, waiting for the mounted police. "I marched for six years. I know what a community looks like when it decides someone doesn't belong in it. It looks like this. Kind faces going careful. Questions wearing aprons. And the terrible thing—" her voice dropped, and something old and tired moved through it, "—the terrible thing is that I understand them. I spent my life believing ordinary people would rise up once they saw clearly. But I've learned that ordinary people rise fastest when they're frightened, and not always in the direction you'd hope. War makes neighbours into informants, Miss Eastwick. It doesn't turn them. It only gives them permission."

The wind came off the river and set the yews whispering. Beatrix thought of the attic above the stables, of the small stove, of Greta's hands wrapped round a mug, of the ledger open on her desk at midnight with its two columns that refused to agree. She thought of Edith Crake ruling her neat lines, waiting for numbers.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "You don't know that there's anything—"

"Child." Mrs. Pell's voice was almost gentle. "I've been organizing women since before you could write. Do you think I can't see a conspiracy of two? Three?" She adjusted her gloves, finger by finger, looking down the empty lane. "I'm telling you because I promised your Lady Vivian in 1908 that I would stand beside her, and I intend to keep that promise, and the way I keep promises is by making sure the people I love know exactly what's coming. What's coming is a search. Sooner or later — a list, a rumour, a slip — Tredwell will ride up that hill with the county's authority in his pocket, and he will be sorry, and he will do it anyway. Because that's what fear does. It makes good men deliver bad papers."

"And what do we do?"

"Now?" Mrs. Pell smiled, and it was not a comforting smile; it was the smile of a woman who had been arrested four times and had enjoyed precisely none of them. "Now you make sure that whatever they find, they find nothing that damns her. Nothing that damns the girl. Papers burn, Miss Eastwick. Ledgers can be made honest in an afternoon, if one is quick. And loyalties—" she patted Beatrix's arm once, hard, through the coat, "—loyalties are the only property the war hasn't figured out how to confiscate. Yet."

She went through the lych-gate and up the lane toward her cottage, small and straight against the darkening sky, and Beatrix stood alone among the graves with the basket of wool cutting into her arm.

History, she had always believed, happened in London, in Parliament, in Flanders — places with maps and correspondents. It had never occurred to her that it might happen here, in a post office, in a ledger's ruled columns, in the pause before a shopkeeper answered a question. It had never occurred to her that history was not an event but a weather, settling over everything, and that she had been living inside it all winter, breathing it, feeding it at her own table.

That night she burned three weeks of order slips in the laundry boiler and rewrote the accounts until they balanced to the halfpenny, and the typewriter clicked past midnight like a small determined heart.

Two days later, Constable Tredwell received a letter from the county office. It was not a request for information.

It was a warrant list — eleven names, enemy aliens unregistered within the district — and beneath the printed names, in a clerk's careful hand, a line that had not been there before:

*Re: Ashcombe House — discrepancies reported. Investigate.*
