## 2. Marlowe House

The Daimler smelled of leather and petrol and, faintly, of Mrs. Marlowe's violet pastilles, which she consumed the way other women consumed prayer—steadily, and in times of crisis without pause. Cyrus Crane had driven her for eleven months now, long enough to know that the pastilles came out before every speech, and that their appearance was a better barometer of trouble than anything printed in the newspapers.

It was the last Saturday of April, 1914, and the pastilles were out.

"Market Square," she said from the back seat, though he was already turning onto the Ashford road. She never gave directions twice. She considered repetition an insult to both parties.

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you'll wait at the corner by the ironmonger's. Not in front of the platform. I don't want the motor visible."

"No, ma'am."

She said nothing else for three miles, and Cyrus occupied the silence the way he occupied all silences—as a tenant occupies a room, carefully, touching nothing. He had a philosophy about carrying things, and it applied to silence as well as to trunks and letters: everything could be carried if you knew its weight. A suitcase you lifted with your knees. A coffin with six men and a slow pace. A secret you carried by never once looking at it directly, the way you never looked directly at the sun over open water.

He had been carrying secrets since he was nine years old, when the workhouse taught him that a boy who noticed things was useful and a boy who mentioned them was beaten. He had refined the craft in service since—noticing which footman drank, which housekeeper wept on Thursdays, which gentleman's letters arrived brown-edged from moneylenders—and he had perfected it behind the wheel of a motorcar, where a driver is furniture that happens to have hands. Nobody told a chair anything worth overhearing. Everybody told a chauffeur everything.

So he knew that Mrs. Edith Marlowe took sugar in her coffee but refused it in company because she thought sweetness read as weakness. He knew she wrote her speeches standing up, at the tallboy in the morning room, and burned the drafts in the grate afterward, and that the burning took longer than the writing. He knew that once a month a letter came from a woman in Leeds whose name made Mrs. Marlowe go very still, and that those letters were answered the same day, in full, by hand.

And he knew—he had known since the second week—that she did not know his Christian name. To her he was Crane, or "the car," or occasionally, when the light caught him at the right angle through the windscreen, simply a nod in the mirror. He had decided early that this was not cruelty but economy, and he had decided it so thoroughly that he almost believed it.

The road ran west out of the town, between hedgerows just breaking into white May blossom, and Cyrus drove the way he did everything: smoothly, invisibly, at exactly the speed that drew no attention. The Daimler was Mrs. Marlowe's own—a legacy from her father, along with the house and the small stubborn income that let her be disowned in comfort—and she drove it herself often enough that the village had learned not to remark on a woman at the wheel. But today there would be fists thrown, possibly, and she preferred him between her and the weather.

They came down past the water meadows, and there—the road bending east around the hill's shoulder—the orchard opened below them like something out of a country that no longer existed.

Hollis's orchard. Everyone called it that, though the Hollises had worked it for two generations and owned it outright, and though old Hollis himself had been dead these four years and his son Konrad ran it now with his English wife and his two children. Forty acres of apple and pear and cherry, laid out in ranks so straight they looked ruled, the blossom coming on so heavy this spring that the whole hillside seemed to have been snowed on in June by mistake. The bees were working it already; you could hear them from the road if you slowed, a low continuous hum like the earth itself thinking.

Cyrus slowed. Not much. A fraction. The kind of slowing a man does to check a stone in the tyre, except there was no stone and he knew it.

He had delivered to Hollis Farm perhaps a dozen times in his year of driving—parcels of grafting wax, a crate from a nursery in Maidstone, once a letter with a Berlin postmark that had taken six weeks to arrive and that young Greta Hollis had run down the lane to meet barefoot, waving at the car as though the postmark were a returning sailor. He remembered the wave. He remembered thinking it was wasteful, all that joy spent on paper, and then remembering that he himself had once walked two miles in the rain to collect a letter that turned out to be a bill, and how he had read the bill three times anyway, just to hold something with his name spelled correctly on it.

"Crane." Mrs. Marlowe's voice, from behind his shoulder. "You're drifting toward the hedge."

"Not drifting, ma'am. Stone in the road."

There was no stone in the road. She knew there was no stone in the road. In the mirror her eyes met his—grey eyes, level, missing nothing—and held for one second longer than courtesy required, and then she looked away at the blossom and said, quietly, "Konrad Hollis grafted half those trees himself. My father bought stock from him for thirty years and swore there wasn't a better nurseryman in Kent."

"Yes, ma'am."

"They're good people," she said, to the window, to the orchard, to nobody. And then, in a different voice entirely, the voice he'd heard through rally doors and once through a locked study door and never to his face: "Which is why what's coming will be ugly. Drive on."

* * *

Market Square had filled since noon. By the time Cyrus eased the Daimler into the shadow of the ironmonger's awning, there must have been four hundred people packed between the war memorial and the corn exchange steps, and the mood of them was not the mood of a meeting but of a storm front. He could feel it through the car's bodywork, the way you feel thunder before you hear it.

Mrs. Marlowe got out without waiting for the door he hadn't yet opened, which was typical, and stood a moment on the pavement in her dark green coat with her hat pinned like a flag, taking the measure of the crowd the way a general takes the measure of a field. Then she went forward alone.

Cyrus stayed with the car. That was the arrangement, unspoken and absolute: he was the wall at her back, the engine running, the route home already mapped in his head with two alternatives. He leaned against the wing and watched her climb the steps to the platform, and he watched the crowd watch her, and he thought, as he always thought, that she was smaller than her reputation and larger than her body, both at once, which was a trick he couldn't begin to account for.

A man near the front began to heckle before she'd finished the first sentence.

"—and who's going to mind the cradles while you're playing at Parliament, eh? Who's going to mind the *cradles*, missus?"

The crowd laughed—not most of them, but enough, the laugh of people testing whether it's safe. Cyrus's hands went still on the bonnet.

Mrs. Marlowe didn't flinch. She let the laugh die on its own, which took courage, because letting a laugh die means standing inside it. Then she said, in a voice pitched not loud but absolutely clear, the voice of a woman who has decided the acoustics are her problem to solve:

"I'll tell you who minds the cradles, sir. Women do. Women have minded the cradles since before there were parliaments to keep us out of them, and we shall go on minding them after you've been forgotten—which, at the rate you're going, will be soon. The question is not who minds the cradles. The question is why the man who asks it thinks a cradle is an argument."

The square went quiet in the particular way crowds go quiet when they've been struck rather than persuaded. Somewhere a baby cried, absurdly, on cue, and Mrs. Marlowe waited it out with the patience of a stone.

"You call it playing at Parliament," she went on. "I call it the franchise. Half the people in this square cannot vote on a single law that governs their lives—their wages, their children's schools, whether their husbands may beat them, whether their property is their own. Half the people in this square are governed without consent, and are told, when they object, that they are *playing*." She stepped forward to the edge of the platform. "I have been arrested twice, sir. Fed by tube once. I do not play."

Cyrus found that he had stopped leaning against the car. He was standing upright, arms at his sides, watching her the way he had once watched a man walk a tightrope over the Medway at a fair—except that this was higher, and there was no net, and the rope was made of words.

The heckler tried again—"Suffragettes smashed windows at—" and got no further, because a woman beside him, a farm wife with flour still on her sleeves, said loudly, "Oh, sit down, Albert," and the crowd laughed again, differently this time, warmly, and the shape of the afternoon changed. Cyrus felt the change in his chest like weather. It was not that Mrs. Marlowe had won them. It was that she had made winning look possible, and possibility, he knew from long experience, was the rarest cargo of all. Most loads he carried were griefs and bills and small deceits. This woman hauled possibility around the county in a green coat and a Daimler, and expected it to be unloaded by nightfall.

He should have been indifferent. He had trained himself to be indifferent; indifference was load-bearing in the architecture of his life, take it out and everything fell. But standing in the ironmonger's shadow with the crowd roaring its first real roar, Cyrus Crane discovered that something in him had shifted its weight, some beam he hadn't known was holding anything up, and the discovery frightened him more than any mob could have.

Because a man who is moved can be seen to be moved. And visibility, in his experience, was how you got hurt.

* * *

It went wrong at half past four, as he had known in his bones it would.

The trouble came not from the hecklers but from a knot of young men at the back—young men are always the knot, Cyrus thought; old men shout and young men shove—who had come, by the look of them, from the public houses on the Ashford road with the specific intention of converting opinion into bruises. They came around the side of the corn exchange while Mrs. Marlowe was still speaking, moving with the loose coordinated swagger of a pack that has found its purpose, and the first egg burst against the platform boards two feet from her boot.

The crowd screamed. Some of it screamed in fear and some in fury and none of it mattered, because the pack was through the outer ring of bodies now and a constable was wading toward the middle of it with the doomed unhurried air of a man who knows he is outnumbered and has decided to be outnumbered officially.

Cyrus moved.

He did not decide to move. Later, examining the moment the way he examined everything—by weight—he would find no decision in it at all, only motion, the way there is no decision in a hand snatched back from a flame. He crossed the square in eight strides, put himself on the bottom step of the platform with his back to Mrs. Marlowe, and became, for the first time in his adult life, deliberately, expensively visible.

"Missus!" the nearest one shouted, over her shoulder, at her. "Missus, come down here and say it to our faces!"

"She says it to everyone's faces," Cyrus heard himself say. His voice came out flat and even, the voice he used for delivering bad news. "That's rather the point of her."

Three of them turned. He registered them the way he registered everything: ages nineteen to twenty-three, farm labourers' builds, one with a cosh-shaped bulge in his coat pocket, all of them drunk enough to be brave and sober enough to aim. The middle one swung first, wide, telegraphed by a full second of shoulder. Cyrus was not a large man and had never been a fighting man, but he had been a workhouse boy in a city, and the first law of the workhouse was that you never took the second blow. He stepped inside the swing, took the man's wrist, and used the fellow's own momentum to fold him gently but unmistakably into the corn exchange's brick pillar. There was a sound like a sack of laundry and the man sat down in the gutter, blinking at the theology of it.

The other two hesitated. Hesitation was all Cyrus needed, because at that moment the constable arrived with two specials and the arithmetic reversed itself, and the pack broke apart into individuals, and individuals, discovering themselves suddenly accountable, evaporated down the alley by the butcher's.

Silence, of the ringing kind. Four hundred people looking at the chauffeur.

Cyrus looked at none of them. He turned, offered Mrs. Marlowe his arm down the last two steps as though this were a garden party, and said, "The car's just here, ma'am."

For a moment she didn't take it. She was looking at him—really looking, the grey eyes doing their inventory—and what he saw in her face was not gratitude exactly, nor surprise, but recognition, the expression of a woman revising a figure in a ledger she'd thought balanced. Then she took his arm, lightly, and let him walk her through the parting crowd, and neither of them spoke until the doors of the Daimler closed behind her.

He got them out of the square and onto the Ashford road before she said anything.

"That was well done," she said at last. "The throw. Where did you learn to throw?"

"City streets, ma'am."

"Which city?"

"London, ma'am."

A pause. Through the mirror he saw her hands folded in her lap, the knuckles still white from the platform, relaxing one finger at a time.

"You've been with me a year, Crane. You've driven me to forty-one meetings. You've never once said a word in my hearing beyond 'yes, ma'am' and 'no, ma'am.'"

"The occasion seemed to require more, ma'am."

"It did." Another pause, longer. The hedgerows ran white past the windows. "Why did you step in? You're paid to drive, not to fight."

He considered several answers and discarded them all as either false or too true. What remained was the smallest truth he could carry without dropping it.

"It seemed badly matched," he said. "Four hundred to one. I don't care for those odds even when I'm only watching."

She was silent for a full mile. When she spoke again her voice had changed—lower, stripped of the platform, addressed not to a servant but across one, the way you speak to someone through a wall you've only just noticed is thin.

"My father used to say a man shows you who he is in the ten seconds after he decides it isn't his business anymore." She looked out at the fields. "You showed me yours today, Crane. I shan't forget it."

"Yes, ma'am," said Cyrus, and rebuilt the wall, brick by invisible brick, all the way home.

* * *

But the wall had a crack in it now, and cracks let things in.

They took the long way back—her instruction, "by the ridge road, Crane, I want the air"—which meant passing above Hollis's orchard with the whole forty acres spread out below like a green-and-white map of some better country. The evening sun lay along the rows so that each tree threw its shadow eastward, rank upon rank upon rank, and the bees had gone home and left the humming to the heat, and somewhere down among the blossom a dog was barking at nothing, contentedly, the way dogs do when the world is exactly the size they want it.

A figure was moving along the far row with a ladder on her shoulder. Even at this distance Cyrus knew the set of her—Greta Hollis, nineteen this spring, all elbows and dark hair, doing a man's work in a man's way because there was no one to tell her not to. She propped the ladder, climbed, and began picking blossom-thick branches into order with quick sure hands, and the sight of it did something to Cyrus that he could not file anywhere. It looked like peace. It looked like the life people meant, he supposed, when they said *a life*—work that fed you, ground that was yours, an evening coming on that asked nothing of you but that you be in it.

He had never wanted anything that concretely before. Wanting, he had learned early, was a leak in the hull; you kept it patched or you drowned. But the orchard lay below him in the gold light, and Greta Hollis moved along the row like a note being written into a song, and the crack in his armor let the whole scene in at once, unexamined, unweighed, uncarried.

"Pretty, isn't it," Mrs. Marlowe said. It was not quite a question.

"Very, ma'am."

"Mm." She was quiet a moment. "Enjoy it while it lasts, Crane. I give England till August."

He glanced at the mirror. Her face in the small glass was turned toward the orchard too, and it wore an expression he had never seen on it—not the ferocity, not the platform steel, but something older and sadder, the look of a woman reading the last page of a book she loves and dreading the ending she can already see.

"Till August of what, ma'am?"

"Of whatever comes next." She settled back against the cushions and closed her eyes. "Drive on."

He drove on. But at the bend below the farm he allowed the car one more fraction of slowness, one breath's worth, and in that breath he saw Greta Hollis pause atop her ladder and look up—not at him, at the sky, at the rooks going over toward the wood—and lift her face into the last of the light like someone drinking.

Two days later, the tyre of the Daimler would tear itself open on a flint within a quarter mile of her gate, and Cyrus would spend forty minutes on his knees in the dust learning that some weights cannot be measured until they are already in your arms.

But that evening he knew only this: that the wall had cracked, that Mrs. Marlowe had seen it crack, and that somewhere down in the blossom a girl was climbing ladders in a world that had no idea what was coming for it in August—or, for that matter, what was already coming for it, quietly, in sealed envelopes, along roads like this one, in cars like his.
