## 4. What the Letters Say

There was a particular hour in the sorting room at Ashford that Cyrus Crane had come to think of as his own, though it belonged to no one. Half past six, before the morning delivery was bundled, when the oil lamps still burned against the grey coming through the windows and the other men had gone out to smoke by the loading door. The room smelled of gum, twine, and cold iron. He would stand at the long table with his hands flat on the wood and let the silence settle over the trays like a cloth laid over bread dough, and in that silence he would read.

He had a system. He was not proud of it, but he was honest enough with himself to admit it was a system, and not merely weakness. Letters that came sealed he read by holding them to the lamp and tilting the paper until the light passed through the envelope and the ink beneath showed itself in shadows—slow work, unreliable, and therefore forgivable, because a man could not be blamed for what he could not fully see. Letters that came open—circulars, printed matter, the county council's endless memoranda—were fair game by any law he recognized. And letters that were handed to him loose, as village letters sometimes were, passed from a kitchen table to a boy who was told to run them down to the post, those were the ones that weighed nothing and everything, and those he read entire.

Everything can be carried if you know its weight. That was the creed he had built for himself out of workhouse years and service years, out of being handed from one set of indifferent hands to another until he learned to make himself light. A sack of flour weighs a hundredweight and you carry it with your knees. A coffin takes six men and a cart. A letter, he had discovered at fourteen, holding his first stolen correspondence up to a dormitory window, weighs nothing at all—and yet he had seen strong men weep over them, seen women carry one folded page in a bodice for thirty years like a stone against the heart. The weight was not in the paper. The weight was in the knowing.

And so he knew things.

He knew that Mrs. Pettifer at the mill house wrote to her sister in Canada every fortnight and always began *Dearest Amy, I shall not complain, but*—and then complained for four pages, and always ended with a pressed flower or, in winter, a thread of wool from her knitting so the sister might hold something true of Kent. He knew that the vicar, Mr. Aldous, received a monthly letter from a bookseller in Tunbridge Wells and that the vicar's replies were written in a hand that shook with something other than age. He knew that Farmer Denne owed money to a cousin in Maidstone and had begun, that spring, to write shorter letters, and that the shortening of a letter is the surest symptom of a man drowning. He knew that the schoolmistress, Miss Verrell, was engaged to a railway clerk in Tonbridge, and that she told no one in the village, and that her letters to him were the only ones Cyrus had ever read that made him feel he had intruded on something holy rather than merely private.

He knew all this the way a riverbed knows the water—not by holding it, but by lying under it, letting it pass, keeping its shape.

He told himself it was harmless. He told himself it was almost a kind of service, being the one who knew, the one who carried it all, so that no one else had to. He was the village's memory without its face. He was the tray, the bag, the strap across the shoulder. When Mrs. Pettifer asked him at the gate, in her bright carrying voice, whether the post had brought anything from Canada, he could tell her truthfully that it had, and watch her hands go to her apron, and hand her the letter with the Canadian stamp, and see her whole face change like weather. That was the good part of the work. That was the part he could hold up in front of the other part, the lamp part, the tilting part, and say: see, I give as much as I take.

It was a lie of a very ordinary size. He had carried it so long it had worn smooth, like a pebble in the pocket.

* * *

The batch that came through on the last Thursday of June was heavier than usual, and heavier in the wrong way. The war in Europe was still a rumor then, still a set of names in the papers—Sarajevo was not yet a word that meant anything to the men of Kent, though it would, God knew, and soon. But the papers had been full of trouble all that spring, strikes and suffragists and the German fleet, and the letters that June reflected the weather of the nation the way a pond reflects a cloud: distorted, darkened, but unmistakably the same cloud.

There was a letter from the Territorial association to Farmer Denne about requisition of land for manoeuvres. There were two letters from the National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies, thick with printed enclosure, addressed to Miss Edith Marlowe at Marlowe House, and Cyrus read those with a professional interest that had nothing to do with the tilting trick, because Miss Marlowe's correspondence came open more often than not, her secretary being a careless young woman who sealed nothing. There was a letter to the vicar from the bishop's office, which he did not read, the bishop's people sealing properly and the vicar's hand being too small to make the lamp-trick worth the effort. There was the usual sediment of bills and catalogues and a circular about insurance against cattle sickness.

And there were three letters in Greta Hollis's hand.

He recognized the hand before he recognized anything else about them—the tall, leaning letters, the German-looking *g* with its deep descender, the way she crossed her *t*s with a single impatient stroke as if time were being wasted. He had seen the hand once before, on the parcel label she'd written at the farm gate the day of the flat tire, *Hollis, Orchard End, per carrier*, and he had thought then that even her handwriting looked like her: upright, quick, a little foreign in a way that made you look twice and then look again.

The letters were addressed to Herr Anton Hollis, Berlin, and they were not sealed.

That was the thing that stopped him. He stood at the long table with the three letters in his hand and the oil lamp hissing above him, and he looked at the unsealed flaps, and he understood, with a small cold drop in the stomach, that she had not sealed them because she had no seal and could not afford the wax, and that she had left them open because there was no one in the house she was hiding them from—and because she did not know that the world between Orchard End and Berlin contained a man like him.

He could have put them in the bag. That was the whole point of the creed, wasn't it—carry the weight, know the weight, but let it pass. He had let ten thousand letters pass. He had let Mrs. Pettifer's complaints and Miss Verrell's holy secrets and Farmer Denne's drowning pass through him and out the other side, and he had slept, if not well, then adequately, every night of his adult life.

He read them.

The first was short. It began *Mein lieber Anton*—and then, oddly, shifted into English, the way she had spoken at the gate, the two languages living in her like tenants who had agreed to share. She wrote that the plum trees had set well. She wrote that their father had been arguing with the agent about the cider press and had won, which she supposed was not news. She wrote that she had dreamed of him twice and that in both dreams he was cold, and she asked him—Cyrus could see the question had been rewritten, there was a crossed line and a fresh start—whether he had seen the specialist yet, and whether the cough was better, and she said, in a sentence that seemed to have cost her something, *You must not write to me that you are well if you are not well. I would rather have the truth in your hand than a lie in mine.*

The second letter was longer, and mostly about the orchard. She described the grafting—Cyrus had not yet been taught, that pleasure was still ahead of him in the summer—and she described it with such particular love, naming the rootstock and the scion and the angle of the cut, that he understood she was writing to her brother about the orchard the way other people wrote about a person. The orchard was a person. The orchard was the family, laid out in rows, pruned and tended, and Anton was in Berlin coughing in a city of stone while his sister told him, branch by branch, what he was going to come home to.

The third letter was the one that did the damage.

It was written in a smaller hand, pressed harder, the pen driven into the paper as if she could drive the words through it and out the other side into Germany by force. It was mostly in English and it said, in its second paragraph: *Papa says the English papers are making trouble for German names again and that it will pass as it always passes. He says we have been here thirty years and the orchard is older than anyone's grudge. I want to believe him. I want you to tell me he is right, and I want you to mean it, and I know you cannot do either.* And then, further down, in the middle of a paragraph about nothing—about the price of sugar, about a neighbor's dog—this, set down as if it had escaped from her: *If anything should happen, if it should ever come to the worst, I have asked no one for anything in my life, but I think if I asked God to keep one thing safe it would be your letters, all of them, the bundle in the cedar box under my bed. Everything else could burn and I would grieve and recover. Not those. You understand. You are the only one who understands, and you are four hundred miles away and getting further every time the newspapers open their mouths.*

Cyrus put the letter down on the table.

The lamp hissed. Out by the loading door, someone laughed, the ordinary laugh of men on a smoke break, and the sound came through the window and seemed to belong to a different country.

He had a rule about this. He had built the rule carefully over the years, mortared it with philosophy, and the rule was: the reading is the sin, and the carrying is the penance, and as long as you never, never let what you know change what you do, the account balances. He had watched Miss Verrell's fiancé step off the Tonbridge train one market day—he had known the man's face from a photograph that had slipped from an envelope—and he had said nothing and done nothing and walked on, and the account had balanced. He had known about Farmer Denne's debts when Denne had stood in the road boasting about a new cart, and he had said nothing, and the account had balanced. The rule was not that he was innocent. The rule was that he was a riverbed, and riverbeds do not interfere with the water.

But Greta Hollis had fed him plums on a hot afternoon and talked to him as though he were a person, had asked his opinion about the tire as though his opinion were a thing worth asking, had laughed at something he said in a way that had stayed with him for weeks like a burr in a sock. And she had written, in her tall leaning hand, that there was a cedar box under her bed containing every letter her brother had ever sent her, and that it was the one thing in the world she could not lose.

And now he knew it. And the knowing was not going to pass through him like water. He could feel it snagging, catching on the bed of the river, building up against him like a drift of leaves.

* * *

He carried the letters to the bag. He sorted the rest of the round with hands that moved by habit while his mind stood still at the gate at Orchard End, in the plum shade, watching Greta Hollis wipe her hands on her apron and say, *You'll take some of these, they'll only rot on the grass otherwise,* as though generosity were the most ordinary transaction in the world and he a person to whom it was naturally extended.

He thought about the word *foreign*. It had been used that week in the Fox and Hounds, not about her, not yet, but about a German waiter in Ashford, and the tone of it had been light, joking, the tone men use when they are trying on a coat to see how it fits. He thought about the newspapers in the vicar's reading room, the ones with the maps and the arrows. He thought about the sentence she had written—*it will pass as it always passes*—and the fact that she had written it in June, in peacetime, in an England where the worst thing that had happened that year to anyone she knew was a bad price for sugar, and how even so, even in June, some part of her had been writing letters to her brother as though packing for a journey.

He finished the round. He did it well; he always did it well; his feet knew the lanes the way his hands knew the twine. He took Mrs. Pettifer her Canadian letter and watched the weather change in her face, and he felt the old clean pleasure of it, the giving part, and then he felt the other part rise up under it like water under a boat. At the Marlowe House gate he handed the two thick suffrage letters to the careless secretary and asked, in the flat voice he used for official questions, whether Miss Marlowe would be wanting the trap on Saturday, and was told yes, half past two, the Maidstone meeting, and he touched his cap and went.

But at the top of Hollis Lane, where the road bent and the orchard showed itself—those long ranks of trees going down toward the Weald, green and gold in the late light, the most orderly and the most innocent thing in the county—he stopped the trap and sat for a moment with the reins loose in his hands.

Here was what he understood, sitting there, and it came to him not as a thought but as a weight, a real weight, the kind you take with the knees: that the letters were not the secret. The letters were only paper. The secret was the cedar box under her bed, and the brother four hundred miles away getting further every day, and the father arguing with agents about cider presses as though arguments were what decided things. The secret was that a family could plant thirty years into English soil and wake up one morning to find the soil reclassified. He did not know how he knew this. He only knew that the newspapers had a weather about them, and that he had spent his whole life learning to read weather in other people's faces, and the weather was turning.

And here was the other thing he understood, and it frightened him more, because it was about himself: that for the first time since he had built his rule, since he had become the riverbed, the tray, the invisible strap across the shoulder of Kent—he did not want to be innocent. He wanted, with a hunger that had no philosophy to cover it, to be of use. To the Hollises. To her. To the girl with the impatient *t*s who had asked God for one thing and did not know that a man in a sorting room in Ashford now held the address of that thing in his memory, filed under *H*, between a hymnal order for the vicar and a hat catalogue for the rector's wife.

The account was not going to balance. He saw that now. Some letters you read and carry, and some letters read you, and after that you are not the same man walking the same round.

He clicked his tongue and the horse moved on, down the hill, past the orchard wall where the first windfalls were already lying in the grass, sweetening the evening air, rotting gently into the soil that had held this family for thirty years and might, if the papers were believed, be asked to give them back.

Two days later, on the Saturday, he drove Miss Marlowe to Maidstone and stood by the trap in the crowd while she spoke about citizenship and the franchise and the rights of women, and her voice was like a bell rung in a high wind, and the crowd cheered, and he watched her and thought: she knows what it is to be refused a place in the world because of an accident of category. She knows it better than anyone.

He decided he would tell her. Not everything—never everything—but enough. That the Hollises were good people. That the girl had fed him plums. That the weather was turning, and someone ought to be standing near the orchard when it broke.

He did not know, then, that within a year he would be standing in that same sorting room holding a sealed official envelope addressed to Orchard End, and that the tilting trick would be useless against it, and that the weight of it would be the heaviest thing he had ever been asked to carry.

He did not know. He only felt the first cold drop of it, somewhere ahead in the summer, like thunder past the hills.
