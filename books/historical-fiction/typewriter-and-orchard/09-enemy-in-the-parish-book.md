## 9. Enemy in the Parish Book

Greta Hollis had always believed the parish register was the truest book in Kent.

Her mother had said so once, kneeling beside her in the cool of St. Aldhelm's while the verger trimmed the lamps. *Everything else can be argued with,* her mother had said, tapping the great leather-bound volume on its brass stand near the font. *The rent book, the almanac, even the Bible, if you're the arguing sort. But this—* and she had turned a page with one careful finger, as though the paper might bruise— *this is where we are written down. Births, marriages, deaths. What God did and what the vicar witnessed. Nobody crosses out what's written here.*

Greta was eleven when her mother said it, and she had believed it the way children believe in the floor beneath their feet: absolutely, without ever thinking to look down.

She was twenty-two on the last Sunday of August 1914, standing in the same churchyard with her father's hat in her hands because he had forgotten it in his agitation, when she learned that floors could be taken away too.

* * *

The morning had begun badly and grown worse by degrees, the way weather does.

It began with the milk. For eleven years the Hollises had sold their surplus to Mrs. Tice at the dairy, and for eleven years Mrs. Tice had left the money in a tin on the gatepost every Friday. On Friday there had been no tin. On Saturday, when Papa walked down with the cans himself, Mrs. Tice had come to the door wiping her hands on her apron and looking everywhere but at his face.

"I'm sorry, Mr. Hollis," she had said, loud enough for her neighbor across the lane to hear. "I've had to make other arrangements."

"Other arrangements," Papa repeated, as if the words were in a language he'd studied long ago and half forgotten.

"There's talk," Mrs. Tice said. "You understand. There's *talk*, and my husband says—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, and shut the door with a gentleness that was somehow worse than slamming it would have been. Through the window Greta saw her stand a moment with her hand still on the latch, shoulders bowed, before she turned away into the dim of the dairy.

Papa carried the full cans back up the hill without a word. He set them in the cool of the scullery, washed his hands, and combed his hair, and put on his good coat, and said, "We will go to church. We have always gone to church." As though attendance were an argument. As though thirty years of Sunday mornings were a ledger someone might consult.

Mama stayed home. She had been staying home more and more since the war began—not from illness, though she claimed a headache each week, but because she could not bear the way the congregation's eyes moved now, sliding off her like rain off waxed canvas. She was the German-born one; Papa had come over as a boy, so long ago that his English had worn away all trace of Saxony, but Mama still rolled certain vowels, still said *ach* under her breath when she burned her thumb, still made the plum cake at harvest with a recipe her own mother had brought across the water. In peacetime these things had been charming. Villagers had asked her for the recipe. Now they were evidence.

So it was only Greta and Papa who walked down the hill that Sunday, through orchards heavy with apples nobody seemed to want anymore, past the pub where two men stood outside smoking and let their conversation die as the Hollises passed, resuming it only when they were safely beyond earshot—or letting them believe they were beyond earshot. Greta caught the word *spies*. She kept walking. Her father's arm was rigid under her hand where she held it, though whether he was steadying himself or her, she could not say.

The church was fuller than usual. War filled pews the way revival meetings never had; fear, it turned out, was better than fire-and-brimstone at getting people up on a Sunday. Greta felt the attention settle on them as they came up the aisle—the small pause in murmured conversations, the heads that turned and turned back, the woman in the third pew who drew her child closer with a motion so automatic it must have been instinct rather than thought. Greta sat straight and opened the prayer book and looked at nothing.

Vicar Ellery took the text from Jeremiah: *"Cursed be the man that trusteth in man, and maketh flesh his arm."* It was not, Greta thought, a sermon about Germany. It was about trust, and how it fails, and how nations fail, and how the world had slid into the sea all at once while everyone slept. He spoke of duty and of sorrow and of the young men already drilling in the towns, and for a little while the church was simply a church again, and Greta let herself breathe.

Then, at the end, after the blessing, after the organist had risen and sat back down again uncertainly, Vicar Ellery stood at the lectern and cleared his throat in the particular way that meant an announcement.

"The parish council has asked me to convey some information," he said. His eyes went to a paper on the lectern and stayed there, refusing the congregation. "Under the new regulations concerning alien residents, all persons of German origin resident in this parish are required to present themselves for registration. Forms are available—" a hesitation, a swallow— "from me, or from Constable Prewitt. Registration is not a punishment. I want that understood. It is simply the law, and we are a law-abiding people, and I know—" and here his voice did something complicated, a crack smoothed over so quickly most of the congregation missed it, but Greta did not miss it, because she had known Nathaniel Ellery since he was a curate who came to the farm for apples and stayed for Mama's plum cake— "I know that the families concerned have been neighbors to us for many years, and that their hearts are—"

He stopped. Whatever he had meant to say, he looked at the paper again and decided against it.

"—are their own affair," he finished. "Registration forms are available. That is all."

The silence afterward had texture. It was not the comfortable hush before the organ voluntary. It was the silence of a room full of people carefully not looking at one another, each privately rehearsing what they would say later, in the lane, in the pub, over the fence: *Well, I always thought.* And: *One can't be too careful.* And: *They seemed such nice people, but then, wouldn't they.*

Greta rose when Papa rose. They walked up the aisle between bowed heads and averted eyes, and she held her chin at the angle her mother used for dealing with insolent tradesmen, and she did not cry until they were through the lychgate and out of sight of the tower.

* * *

The register itself she saw three days later, and it was the seeing of it that broke something in her that the sermon had only cracked.

She had come to the vicarage on the Tuesday morning to collect the registration forms—her father's pride being what it was, he had sent her rather than go himself, or perhaps he had sent her because he feared what he might say, or perhaps, though she did not let herself think this until much later, he sent her because he was already afraid of what might happen to him, and wanted his daughter seen doing the correct thing, publicly, in daylight, in company with the vicar's own household.

Vicar Ellery received her in the study, which smelled of pipe smoke and lamp oil and the particular dustiness of old books. The register lay open on the desk between them, and she saw it before she could stop herself from seeing it.

There, in the B column, in faded iron-gall ink, in the handwriting of a vicar forty years dead: *Hollis, Wilhelm Friedrich, son of Friedrich and Anna Hollis, born at Wexbourne Farm, this parish, 14th March 1861.* Her father. Baptized in this very font. Written down by a man who had shaken her grandfather's hand and called him neighbour.

And through the name, a single line. Ruled in fresh black ink, still sharp-edged, the pen having bitten slightly through the old paper. A line drawn through *Wilhelm Friedrich Hollis* the way a schoolmaster strikes out a sum done wrong. Beside it, in Vicar Ellery's smaller, neater hand, a note in the margin: *See alien registration, Aug. 1914.*

Below it, another entry. *Hollis, Edith Mary* — no relation to Mrs. Marlowe, though the coincidence had made them laugh once, years ago, at a fete — Greta's mother, born in this parish too, married in this church, and struck through in the same black line.

Greta stared at it. The study was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a clock ticked with the patience of furniture.

"You've crossed them out," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, flat and far away, as if someone else were speaking through her mouth.

Vicar Ellery closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them they were wet, and he did not trouble to hide it, which she was grateful for even then, even in the middle of everything.

"The diocesan secretary wrote to me," he said. "A circular. It seems there was—a question raised. About whether persons of enemy origin ought to remain on the rolls of baptism." He picked up the register's edge and let it fall again, gently, as if it had become something hot. "I argued against it. I wrote back. I told them your father has knelt at that rail for fifty years. I told them your mother taught half the village children their letters before the school board appointed a mistress. I told them—" He stopped. He came around the desk and stood looking down at the open page, at the neat black lines, and Greta saw that his hands were trembling, an old man's tremble, or a shamed man's. "I told them everything I could think of, Miss Hollis. And the reply came back that the register is a record of the parish, and the parish cannot be seen to—to extend communion to enemies of the Crown. The lines were to be ruled. By my hand. Or by my successor's, if I declined."

"And you didn't decline," Greta said. Not accusingly. She found she hadn't the strength for accusation. It was more like reading aloud. Confirming the fact of it, the way one confirms a death.

"I did not decline." He turned away to the window, where the churchyard yew threw its permanent shadow across the grass. "I am sixty-eight years old, Miss Hollis. I have buried most of this village twice over—once in the ground and once in my heart. If I resign in protest, they will send a younger man, a sterner man, a man who will rule those lines gladly and go further besides. And there are others whose names will be questioned next, if this fever spreads. People who sheltered you. People who traded with you. People who spoke kindly." He said the last words very quietly, and Greta thought—though she could not have explained why—of a motorcar and a quiet driver, of letters carried up and down the lanes, of a face she had fed plums to at the kitchen door in June when the world was still whole.

"I'm sorry," the vicar said to the window. "I want you to hear it from me plainly: I am sorry, and I believe it is wicked, and I did it anyway. That is what this war is teaching all of us, I think. How much wickedness a reasonable man will do, provided the reason is supplied ready-made."

Greta took the forms from the desk. Four of them, cream-colored, official crests at the head. *Alien Registration.* Fields for name, origin, date of arrival, occupation, place of residence, particulars of family, particulars of property. Property. She noticed that. Someone in a government office had thought to ask about property, and had given it a whole column.

"Thank you, Vicar," she heard herself say, in her mother's voice, the polite voice, the one that survived anything.

"Miss Hollis." He turned from the window. "When this is over—and it will end, these things always end—I will rule those lines back out. Ink can be unmade. I want you to know that I intend to try."

But she was already thinking about the column marked *property*, and about the deed to Wexbourne Farm, which lived in the tin box under her parents' bed, and about how a piece of paper crossed out in a church book was grief, but a piece of paper seized by the Crown was something else entirely.

* * *

Her father signed the form at the kitchen table that evening, the lamp turned up high, the whole family gathered as though for a signing of consequence—which, Greta understood, it was, though not in any way the government intended.

He wrote slowly, his big orchardman's hand cramped into the small ruled spaces. *Wilhelm F. Hollis. Origin: German (naturalized British subject, 1889).* He had to hunt for the naturalization papers to get the year right, and finding them meant opening the tin box, and opening the tin box meant laying the deed to the farm on the table under the lamp, and for a moment all four of them—Papa, Mama, Greta, and young Fritz home on leave from his apprenticeship in Canterbury—looked at it lying there among the certificates, the thick parchment with its wax seal, the map of the orchards drawn in someone's careful Victorian hand.

"They'll ask about this next," Mama said. She said it without drama, the way she announced frost coming, or blight. "Property is always next. You take a person's name first, then their goods. It was so in my grandmother's time, when the borders moved. The borders move, and the papers burn, and the ones who planned ahead—" she touched the deed, just barely, with one fingertip— "the ones who planned ahead kept something back."

"Keep it safe," Papa said. He said it to Greta, not to Mama, and something in his tone made her look up. He was not looking at the deed. He was looking at her, and behind his tired eyes was a calculation she had never seen him make before, a weighing of chances, a farmer's arithmetic applied to something no farmer should have to count. "If anything happens—if they come, if there are orders—you take the box, Greta. Not the silver, not the photographs. The box. Do you understand me?"

"Papa, nothing is going to—"

"Do you understand me?"

The lamp hissed softly. Outside, down in the dark valley, a dog barked at nothing, and somewhere further off a train went through the night carrying soldiers and shells toward the coast, toward France, toward the enormous machinery of the war that had made her family into a question.

"Yes," Greta said. "I understand."

She put the deed back in the box. She did not yet know why her hands were shaking. She told herself it was only the lateness of the hour, only the strangeness of the day, only the black lines in the parish book rising behind her eyes whenever she blinked.

Three days later, a grey government envelope arrived at Wexbourne Farm, addressed in typed characters to *W. F. Hollis, Alien, Wexbourne Farm, Kent*—the word *alien* printed right on the face of it, above the stamp, as though it were part of the address, as though it were his name now. And it was not the only letter that morning. There was a second envelope, thicker, sealed with wax, marked *Official — To be delivered by hand only*, and it was Cyrus Crane who brought both up the lane in the Marlowe motorcar, and it was the look on his face—white, stricken, a man carrying something heavier than paper—that told Greta, before anyone opened anything, that the world had finished asking questions and begun giving answers.
