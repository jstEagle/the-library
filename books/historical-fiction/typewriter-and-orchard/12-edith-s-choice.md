## 12. Edith's Choice

Edith Marlowe had learned long ago that the hours before dawn belonged to her alone, and she guarded them the way misers guard gold. The house was quiet then. The fire in her study had burned down to a red eye of coals, and the only sound was the scratch of her pen and, beneath it, the great slow breathing of Marlowe House itself—the settling of beams, the tick of the case clock in the hall, the wind worrying at the ivy on the south wall. In these hours she was not Mrs. Marlowe of the platform and the newspaper column. She was simply a woman at a desk, doing arithmetic.

The arithmetic this morning was grim. The relief fund stood at four hundred and eleven pounds, most of it pledged but not yet collected. The autumn march was six weeks out, and the committee had finally—*finally*, after two years of her badgering—agreed to join it with the war-relief banner carried alongside the suffrage one, a marriage of causes she had argued for with every ounce of persuasion she possessed. It had cost her three letters to London, two visits from Mrs. Frobisher herself, and one long evening of being lectured about priorities by women who had never stood on a platform in their lives. But it was done. The movement in Kent was, for the first time since the war began, moving again.

She set down her pen and pressed her fingers against her eyes. Through the window, the sky over the orchard country was going from black to a bruised grey, and she thought—not for the first time—that the land here had a way of looking wounded even in peacetime, all its hills like shoulders turned away.

That was when she heard the kitchen door.

Not the front door; Cyrus Crane never used the front door before seven, considering it, she supposed, a door for visitors and other imaginary persons. He came through the kitchen passage as he always did, his boots making that particular soft sound she had catalogued without meaning to: heel, then toe, careful even on his own ground. She heard him stop in the hall. She heard him stand there for what she judged to be a full minute, which was unlike him. Cyrus Crane did not loiter. Cyrus Crane moved through houses like weather through a valley—present, necessary, leaving no footprints.

"Miss Edith." His voice came through the study door, low. "You're awake."

"I am always awake at this hour, Cyrus. You know that better than anyone."

A pause. Then: "I need to speak with you. Before the others come down."

Something in his tone made her set aside the fund ledger. Something in it made her hands go still on the blotter. In eleven years of service—first as her father's man, then hers—she could count on one hand the times Cyrus Crane had asked to speak with her *before* the day began, and every one of those times had involved something broken: a horse, a carriage axle, once, memorably, a runaway cart of prize marrows belonging to Canon Whitstable.

He came in when she bade him, and she saw at once that this was worse than marrows. He stood just inside the door with his cap in his hands, turning it, and his face had the look of a man who has been carrying something heavy for miles and has finally, reluctantly, decided to put it down where someone else can see it.

"The Hollises," he said.

Edith felt the word land in her chest like a stone dropped into still water. She had been expecting it, in the way one expects rain—knowing it must come, hoping it would find someone else's roof first.

"What about them?"

"They've had notice." He said it flatly, the way he delivered everything, but she had spent eleven years learning to read the flatness. "Internment orders. Forty-eight hours. The whole family—Mr. Hollis, Mrs. Hollis, Greta too, though she was born in Maidstone and hasn't set foot in Germany since she was four years old." His jaw worked. "There's a list, miss. A parish list. Their names were crossed out of the register Sunday last, and now this."

"How do you know this?" she asked, though she suspected she already knew the answer, and the knowing sat coldly in her stomach. "Cyrus. How do you know?"

He met her eyes. It was one of the few things about him that had never changed, war or no war: when he chose to look at you, he looked at you completely, as if you were the only fixed point in the room. "I read the letter," he said. "It came through my hands. I'm the one who carries it out to them."

"You opened official correspondence?"

"I opened everything," he said, and there was no apology in it, only a kind of exhausted honesty that she found, absurdly, harder to be angry at than any excuse would have been. "I've told you before. Everything that passes through my hands, I carry twice—once in the bag and once in my head. I didn't open this one for curiosity. I opened it because I promised Anton Hollis I'd look after his sister, and I can't look after her if I don't know what's coming for her."

Edith rose from behind the desk. She went to the window because she could not look at him while she thought, and the grey light was strengthening now, laying the garden out in bands of shadow and pale gold. The roses were going over. The dahlias would follow. Another summer ending in ruin.

"And what is it you want from me, Cyrus?"

"I want you to speak for them." He said it plainly, no dressing on it at all. "Publicly. At the tribunal, in the papers if it comes to that. You have a name now, Miss Edith. People listen to you. Half the county reads your column, and the other half reads it so they can be angry at it, which is nearly as good. If you say the Hollises are decent people—if you say it loud enough, in print, with your name under it—they might be spared. Or Greta, at least. She's English-born. There's provision for it. There has to be."

"There is provision for it," Edith said quietly, "and it is exercised roughly never."

"Then exercise it."

"Cyrus—"

"*Exercise it.*" He almost never raised his voice. When he did, it was worse than shouting, because it came out of that flat calm like a crack in a wall, showing you how much weight the wall had been holding. "You stood on a platform in Ashford in March and told four hundred people that silence in the face of injustice is complicity. Your words, miss. I was there. I held the umbrella. You said a woman who sees wrong and holds her tongue to keep her position is no better than the man who does the wrong. And now here's a family that sheltered you—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended.

"—that sheltered you," he repeated, relentless, "in 1909, when your own name was mud and the constables were waiting at Ashford station and nobody else in this whole county would have you under their roof for a night. Old Hollis stood in his doorway with a shotgun across his arm and told the men from the National League they'd have to take you over his body. And now his daughter is to be locked up behind wire with a number instead of a name, and you're going to tell me about *positions*."

The silence after that was very long. Somewhere in the house a door closed; Mrs. Fairweather was stirring, and the smell of woodsmoke began to seep under the study door. Edith kept her eyes on the garden. Her heart was beating hard, and she was furious to discover that it was not entirely anger.

"You forget yourself," she said at last.

"No, miss," said Cyrus Crane. "For the first time in my life, I don't think I do."

* * *

She dismissed him—there was no other word for it—and he went, without another syllable, back through the kitchen passage, and she heard the front door close softly behind him as he went to bring the motorcar round. Then she stood in her study with the ledger open and the pen uncapped and did nothing at all for the better part of an hour.

Because the worst of it—the part she would not write in any journal, would not say to any living soul—was that he was right.

She remembered 1909 with a clarity that felt less like memory than like a scar. She remembered the wet November night, the mob outside the Ashford hall, the stone that had come through the window and caught her above the ear. She remembered fleeing across dark fields with her hat gone and her coat torn, guided by a boy—a farm lad, German accent thick as clotted cream—who had appeared out of a hedge and said, *Come, missus, quick, my father says.* And she remembered Wilhelm Hollis standing in his kitchen doorway in his shirtsleeves, arms crossed, facing down six men with lanterns, saying in that slow deliberate English of his: *This woman is a guest in my house. In Germany we have a word for what you are doing tonight. We also have a word for what I am doing. Go home and think which one you want to be.*

They had gone home. And she had slept in a bed that smelled of apples, under a roof built by a foreigner's grandfather, and in the morning Frau Hollis had packed her bread and cheese and said, *You fight for the voiceless, Fräulein. So fight for us someday, if it ever comes to that.* And Edith had laughed and said, *It will never come to that. England is not Germany.*

England was not Germany. England was England, which meant it did its cruelties quietly, with forms in triplicate and a vicar's pen and a sealed letter carried by a quiet man in a quiet cap.

She sat down at her desk and made herself think it through as she thought through everything: as strategy, as arithmetic. If she spoke publicly for the Hollises, what followed? First, the *Kentish Observer* would run it under some headline about suffragists and enemy sympathies—those two words had already begun to travel together in certain circles, like dogs that had scented each other across a field. Second, Mrs. Frobisher and the London committee, who had fought her for two years over every shilling of the relief fund, would have the weapon they'd been praying for. Third, the fund itself—four hundred and eleven pounds, earmarked for soldiers' wives and mothers in this very parish—would likely collapse within the month. She could see it all laid out with terrible clarity, the whole chain of consequence, link by link, and at the end of the chain stood not the Hollises but a row of widows in the village who had done nothing to anyone and whose husbands were already in France.

And yet.

And yet old Hollis in the doorway. Bread and cheese wrapped in a cloth. *Fight for us someday.*

She spent the morning in a state she despised in others: indecision. She wrote three letters and burned two. She drafted a statement—*I have known the Hollis family of Orchard End for many years...*—and got as far as the second sentence before the image of Mrs. Frobisher's face rose before her, patient and pitying, and the pen stopped.

At noon she walked down to the village to post what remained postable, and she heard her own name spoken in the shop in the lowered voice people used now for dangerous subjects, and she heard the word *Hollis* attached to it like a burr. Mrs. Pettifer, the ironmonger's wife, looked at her with something that was almost kindness and said, "You'll want to be careful, ma'am, who you're seen standing with these days. Times being what they are."

Times being what they are. The phrase of the age. It could justify anything, because it justified nothing; it was a shrug dressed as a principle.

She came home in the late afternoon and found Cyrus in the stable yard, checking the motorcar's tyres with a hand-pump, methodical as ever, as if the world had not cracked open between them at dawn. He did not look up. That, more than anything, told her how deeply the morning's quarrel had gone: Cyrus Crane always looked up. It was his single act of rebellion, that steady level gaze, the insistence on meeting the eyes of his betters as an equal. To withhold it was his version of slamming a door.

"Cyrus."

"Yes, miss."

"I've been thinking about what you said."

"Very good, miss."

"That tone will not help your cause."

"No cause of mine, miss." He straightened, coiled the pump hose. "I just drive the car."

She wanted to strike him. She wanted to laugh. She wanted, obscurely and unbearably, to cry, and she did none of these things, because she was Edith Marlowe and she had spent forty-one years learning that feeling a thing and being ruled by it were different countries, and she had sworn never to emigrate.

"Come inside," she said. "Mrs. Fairweather has made seed cake, and you will sit down at the kitchen table like a human being, and we will discuss this like two people who have known each other eleven years, because I am tired of talking to the backs of your head."

* * *

They sat at either end of the kitchen table with the seed cake between them like a disputed territory, and Mrs. Fairweather, sensing weather of her own, withdrew to the scullery with a great show of scrubbing pots.

"I cannot do it," Edith said. "I want you to understand that it is not cowardice. Or rather—I want you to understand that I know exactly how much cowardice is in it, and I have weighed it, and I have chosen, and the choice stands. If I speak for the Hollises, I lose the fund. Not risk it—lose it. Frobisher has been looking for a reason since August. And the fund isn't mine, Cyrus. It belongs to the widows. To Mrs. Tice, whose boy went down with the *Aboukir*. To the Pargeters, whose daughter writes to them from a hospital in Rouen. Four hundred and eleven pounds. I have their names in a book upstairs. I will not spend their bread buying a gesture for my conscience."

"It wouldn't be a gesture," Cyrus said. "It would be a family. Five of them. Six counting the dog."

"Greta may be spared regardless. She's British-born; the tribunal has discretion—"

"You know what discretion means in wartime, miss? It means they spell your name right on the order."

"Cyrus—"

"And I'll tell you what else discretion means." He leaned forward, and for the first time she saw that his hands, resting on the table, were not quite still. "It means Greta Hollis standing in a crowd on Saturday watching her father marched to a camp like a criminal, while the woman her family saved stands at her window and says nothing. Because I'll tell you what she'll remember. Not the wire. Not the soldiers. She'll remember who spoke and who didn't. Silence carries further than you think, Miss Edith. I know, because I've been silent my whole life, and I know exactly how far it reaches."

The clock ticked. The fire shifted. In the scullery, Mrs. Fairweather scrubbed a pot that had surely been clean for some minutes.

"You think I don't lie awake over this?" Edith said, and her voice, to her horror, frayed at the edges. "You think I haven't written the statement twenty times in my head? I knew their names before you ever drove me down that lane, Cyrus. I owe them more than you know. More than I can say. And I am telling you that if I spend myself on this, I will have nothing left to spend on anything, and there are a hundred fights after this one, and I am asking you—*asking* you—to trust that I know the difference between a battle and a skirmish."

"And I'm telling you," he said, "that for the Hollises this isn't a skirmish. It's the whole war, arriving on one cart, in forty-eight hours." He stood. He picked up his cap. And then, at the door, he paused, and what he said next he said quietly, without heat, which was somehow the worst of all. "In 1909, a man stood in a doorway for you. Nobody's asking you to stand in a doorway. Just to write your name under a piece of paper. I wonder which of you will sleep better."

He went out. The kitchen door clicked shut with the small precise sound of a thing closing properly, and Edith Marlowe sat alone at her table with the seed cake going stale between them and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that something had broken in the house that could not be mended by mending.

It was not the quarrel. Quarrels could be patched; she and her father had quarrelled magnificently for thirty years. It was that he had been right about the doorway, and she had known he was right, and she had chosen the ledger anyway—and worse, some traitorous chamber of her heart had noticed, in the middle of all that fury, how his hands had trembled on the table, and had thought not *what is he asking* but *what does it cost him to ask*.

She rose and went to the window. Down the darkening lane, past the yew hedge, the lights of Orchard End were coming on one by one—kitchen, parlour, the little lamp in Greta's window that she could just make out, a pinprick of gold against the black mass of the trees. Forty-eight hours. Thirty-six now, perhaps less.

Edith took out a fresh sheet of paper. She wrote at the top of it, in her best committee hand: *Statement concerning the Hollis family of Orchard End.* And beneath it she wrote nothing at all, because the pen would not move, because four hundred and eleven pounds hung from it like a stone from a rope, because somewhere in London Mrs. Frobisher was waiting with scissors.

She sat until the lamp burned low. And it was only much later—when the house was asleep, when she had blown out the candle and stood a moment in the dark of her room listening to the wind come up from the orchards—that she admitted what she had really been afraid of all day. Not Frobisher. Not the fund. Not even the newspapers.

She had been afraid of how much Cyrus Crane's opinion of her mattered—and of what she might become, in her own eyes, if she lost it.

Down the lane, unseen, a light moved in Greta's window, and went out.
