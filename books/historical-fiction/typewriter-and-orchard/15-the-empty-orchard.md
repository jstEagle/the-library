## 15. The Empty Orchard

The trees did not know they were abandoned.

That was the first thing Greta understood, standing in the wet grass of the third week in September with her father's coat buttoned to her throat and the house behind her empty of everything but echoes. The trees went on doing what trees did. They ripened. They dropped. They asked nothing of anyone.

A Cox's Orange Pippin near the south wall had let its fruit fall into a drift against the fence, and the windfalls lay there in the long grass like something spilled, going soft, going brown, feeding wasps by the hundred. Nobody had come to gather them. In fourteen years her family had never once let an apple lie where it fell. Her mother had walked these rows every morning of the season with a basket on her hip, turning each fruit over with two fingers, reading it like a letter—*this one today, this one waits, this one's for the cider press.* Her mother had been dead five years now, and still Greta could not walk a row without hearing that voice, patient and exact: *the tree tells you when, Greta. You only have to listen.*

But listening required staying, and staying had become a kind of trespass.

She bent and picked up one of the windfalls. It was warm from the sun despite the morning chill, heavy for its size, the skin freckled gold over red. A perfect apple. The sort her father would have wrapped in straw and sent up to Marlowe House with the first of the season, because Edith Marlowe liked them baked with a spoonful of honey and said so every year as though conferring an honour.

Her father was in a camp on the Isle of Man now. Eleven days. She counted them the way she'd once counted days until Anton came home from Berlin for harvest—except there was no *until* attached to this counting. There was no number at the end of it. Just the number growing.

She set the apple in her basket. Then she picked up another. And another. Not because anyone had asked her to. Not because the fruit was hers anymore, perhaps—the solicitor's letter had been vague in the way official letters are vague, all *pending consideration* and *matters in hand*, which meant the Crown's fingers were already reaching toward the deed in some drawer in London. But her hands needed work, and the trees needed hands, and between those two needs there was enough to fill a day, and a day was what she had. Days were all anybody had now.

By noon she had filled six baskets and lined them along the packing shed wall, and by then the first of the villagers had begun to arrive.

They came singly at first, the way crows do—one man with a sack, testing. Mr. Pettifer from the far cottage, who had bought Hollis apples every autumn at market and always said the Hollis pippins kept better than any in Kent. He didn't look at her. He walked down the row nearest the lane and began filling his sack from the ground, taking only windfalls, which was within his rights or nearly so—windfall law was old law, and half the parish had gleaned windfalls from half the orchards in the county since before anyone remembered otherwise. Greta stood among her baskets and watched him, and he watched the ground, and neither of them spoke, and the silence between them had a shape she recognised: it was the shape of a man doing something he knew to be shabby and deciding to do it anyway because everyone else would, and shame shared is shame halved.

By three o'clock there were nine of them. By four, children with pockets. One boy climbed into a tree—not a windfall-gleaner, a thief—and Greta called out to him, *those aren't ripe yet, you'll spoil the branch*, and the boy looked at her with a face full of the particular cruelty of children who have learned it from their parents, and said, "It's not your orchard no more, is it? My dad says."

And then he shook the branch hard, and green apples rained down around him, and he laughed.

Greta stood very still. Something in her chest went tight and small, like a fist closing around a coal. She thought about her father's face the morning the soldiers came—how he had straightened his collar before opening the door, as though dignity were a garment one could put on for the occasion. She thought about Anton's letters in Cyrus Crane's keeping, wherever they were kept. She thought: *I am twenty-two years old and I am being robbed by children, and I cannot even be angry properly, because if I am angry they will say see, see, the German temper, we told you.*

So instead of shouting she walked to the shed, brought out the scales her father used at market, and set them on a trestle by the gate. And when Mrs. Alderton came up the lane with a basket on her arm, hesitating at the gatepost with the expression of a woman about to commit a sin she has already rehearsed, Greta said, "Take what you like from the ground. If you want fruit off the trees, I'll pick it for you, and you can leave a penny in the tin if you're able."

Mrs. Alderton blinked. "I—that's very—"

"It's how we've always sold them," Greta said. "Nothing's changed except the name on the gate."

Something moved across the woman's face—shame, probably, or its cousin—and she left twopence in the tin and took six apples and went away quickly. Two others followed her example. Most did not. By dusk the tin held one shilling and threepence, and the rows nearest the lane looked as though a storm had passed through them, branches torn, unripe fruit trampled into the mud, and Greta locked the shed, and sat down inside among the baskets, and allowed herself, at last and briefly, to cry—not loudly, not for long, just enough to keep the rest of it from spoiling, the way you bleed a radiator so the pipes don't burst.

Then she wiped her face, and took the lantern down from its nail, and went out to look at the fence.

* * *

Cyrus Crane had learned that the hours after midnight belonged to him in a way no other hours did.

He had known this since the workhouse, where sleep was rationed like everything else and the night matron's footsteps were the only clock that mattered; he had known it in service, lying in attic rooms under roofs that leaked the stars in through the slates. Night was when the world stopped making demands and a man could take inventory of himself. What he carried. What it weighed. Whether the load had shifted.

Lately the load included a bundle of letters in oilcloth under the floorboard of his room above the Marlowe House garage—Greta's letters to Anton, unsent, undeliverable, addressed to a city that might as well have been on the moon. He carried them nowhere. He simply kept them, and the keeping had become a daily act, almost devotional, the way other men said their prayers. *Still here,* he told them each night. *Still safe.*

Tonight he told himself he was driving out to the Hollis farm to check the shed locks, which was even true, in the way that a man tells himself he is going for a walk when he is going to look at a lit window. The Austin's headlights he left off until the last mile, running dark on the moonlit road between the hedgerows, and when he pulled up short of the farm gate and got out, the smell of the orchard reached him before anything else—rotting apples and cut grass and woodsmoke, the whole melancholy perfume of September going to waste.

The fence along the lane had been broken in two places. He'd seen it on his last visit: rails splintered where a cart had backed into them carelessly—or not so carelessly—and the second break newer, fresher, pale wood showing where someone had leaned a ladder against the top rail to get over without troubling the gate. Tonight there was a third gap, wide as a doorway, and the gate itself hung by one hinge.

He stood in the dark and felt anger arrive in him slowly, the way cold water rises in a boot. He was not an angry man by trade. Anger was expensive and he had never been able to afford it; invisibility required equanimity the way sailing requires ballast. But looking at that broken fence—at the pale wounds in the wood, at the trampled grass, at the whole neglected sprawl of the finest orchard in Kent being picked apart by the very neighbours who had eaten its fruit for a generation—something in Cyrus Crane's carefully ballasted soul tipped over.

He was halfway back to the motorcar to fetch tools when he saw the lantern light moving between the trees.

His first thought was soldiers. His second was looters. His third—he saw the figure bend, lift something, straighten—was simply *her*, and the word arrived in him with a warmth entirely disproportionate to its information, and he stood in the shadow of the hedge and made himself stay still and watch, because watching Greta Hollis work by lantern light was not a thing a prudent man did, and Cyrus had never once in thirty-one years been a prudent man about the things that mattered.

She was gathering windfalls into baskets. Working alone, methodically, her hair pinned up under a scarf, her father's coat enormous on her. She worked the way her mother must have worked, he thought—no wasted motion, each fruit turned and judged and placed. He had delivered to this farm for years. He had watched this girl grow from a child racing paper boats down the ditch to a young woman who discussed grafting stock with him as though he were a colleague, and somewhere in those years, without his noticing the exact day of it, watching her had changed category—from habit to need.

He stepped out of the hedge and said, "You'll ruin your back, lifting like that."

The lantern swung round. For one heartbeat the light found nothing but his boots and trouser legs, and then it rose, and he watched her recognise him, and he watched the fear go out of her face and something else come in—relief, quick and unguarded, gone again almost before it showed, but he had seen it. He had seen it, and he would keep it, the way he kept everything.

"Mr. Crane." Her voice was steady. "You shouldn't be here. If someone sees your car—"

"No one sees my car. That's rather the point of me." He came forward into the lantern's circle. "Three gaps in your fence. Gate's hanging by a nail. Another week and there won't be an orchard, just a public park with delusions."

"I know." She looked away, toward the ruined rows by the lane. "I've been meaning to mend it. There hasn't been—" She stopped. Pride and exhaustion were fighting openly on her face, and pride was losing. "There hasn't been time. Or nails. Or—" a small, terrible laugh, "—a reason anyone would respect a fence of mine anyway."

"There's a reason," Cyrus said. "It's standing right here holding a lantern."

She looked at him then, fully, and the lantern light made her eyes very bright, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the orchard breathed around them—all that ripening and rotting, all that patience going to waste—and Cyrus understood that he had just crossed some line he hadn't known was drawn, and that he did not intend to step back over it.

"I've a toolbox in the car," he said. "And I've nothing to do until six o'clock tomorrow except drive Mrs. Fennimore to her sister's, and Mrs. Fennimore talks the entire journey whether one is present or not, so one may as well be tired for a good reason."

"You can't fix a fence alone in the dark," she said.

"No," he agreed. "Fortunately I'm not proposing to."

* * *

They worked until nearly three.

It went easier than either of them expected, and harder than either admitted. The gate they took down entirely and re-hung on new hinges from the toolbox, Cyrus holding the rail level while Greta drove screws, her mouth set in the fierce concentration he remembered from the afternoon she'd taught him grafting—*not there, lower, you want the cambium kissing, wood to wood, or it never takes.* She had strong hands. He noticed everything about her hands and filed the noticing away in the place where he kept things too large to carry openly.

The third gap was the worst. The post had rotted through at the base and the whole thing leaned like a drunkard, and they ended up digging it out together in the moonlight, the spade biting, the earth smelling of rain and worms, Greta hauling on the post while Cyrus levered it, and when it came free they both staggered and nearly went down in the hole together and caught each other instead—her hand gripping his forearm, his steadying her shoulder—and for a moment they stayed like that, longer than balance required, the lantern throwing their two shadows into one shape against the hedge.

Neither mentioned it. Both remembered it.

"New post," Cyrus said, voice slightly rougher than he intended. "There's seasoned oak in your shed—I saw it stacked."

"That's for the packing benches."

"The packing benches will forgive you. This fence won't."

She laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, brief and low, and it did something to the night, warmed it, made the ruined orchard for one moment sound like an orchard again. He dug the new hole while she trimmed the post, and they set it together, tamping the earth down in layers the way her father had taught her, and when it stood true she put her palm flat against the wood as though checking a child's forehead for fever.

"They'll break it again," she said quietly. "You know that. Pettifer's cart, or worse. It's not really about windfalls. Windfall law is just—" she searched, "—permission. Permission is what they're taking. First the register, then the names, then the fence. You take a person apart by taking what holds her up."

Cyrus drove a screw home and considered his answer, because with her he had fallen out of the habit of answers that were merely smooth.

"My mother," he said, "worked in service in a house in Ashford. Cook-general, one woman doing everything. The mistress of the house used to rearrange the kitchen every few months. Move the pans. Change the shelves. Never any sense to it—just change, to remind my mother the kitchen wasn't hers." He tested the rail. It held. "I was eight before I understood that's what she was doing. I thought she was simply untidy."

Greta was quiet a moment. "What happened to your mother?"

"Died when I was eleven. Workhouse after." He said it the way he reported weather—flat, factual, the weight already distributed evenly along the whole length of the memory so no single part of it sagged. "Which is why I know about fences. Everything can be carried if you know its weight. But it helps enormously to have someone else lifting at the same end."

She looked at him across the mended gap, and in the lantern light her expression was one he had never seen on her—not gratitude, which is a debt, but recognition, which is a gift. As though he had said something true about her own life that she hadn't yet found words for.

"Cyrus," she said—his Christian name, the first time, landing on him like a stone dropped into deep water. "Why are you doing this? Truly. You risk—Edith Marlowe's house, your position, everything—for a family the whole parish has decided to hate. Why?"

He could have given the easy answer. The promise to Anton. Duty. Any of the respectable scaffolds a man builds around a truth too large to show plainly. He stood with the screwdriver in his hand and the rotten old post lying between them like a felled soldier, and he heard himself say:

"Because eleven days ago I stood in a crowd and watched your father shake hands with the constable like a gentleman, and not one person in that crowd saw him. They saw a German. I've spent my whole life being unseen, Miss Hollis, and I always believed it was the worst thing that could happen to a person. Watching that crowd taught me it isn't." He put the screwdriver back in the box. "The worst thing is seeing clearly and saying nothing. I've done it my whole life. I find I've lost the talent for it."

The words hung in the cold air between them. Somewhere down the valley a dog barked twice and gave up. Greta Hollis looked at Cyrus Crane for a long time, and whatever she was weighing, she weighed it thoroughly, because she was her father's daughter.

"Well," she said at last, and her voice was not quite steady. "Then you'd better help me carry the baskets in. The trees don't know they're abandoned, and I refuse to let the parish decide they are."

* * *

They loaded the baskets onto the handcart and hauled them to the shed, and inside, by lantern light, she graded the fruit the way her mother had taught her—cooking apples in the left bins, eaters in the right, bruised ones to the cider press pile—and Cyrus stacked and handed and learned, and the work had a rhythm to it that felt older than the war, older than the registers and the sealed letters and the camps, as though the two of them had fallen by accident into some ancient order of things: orchard-keepers and carriers, growers and bearers, each useless without the other.

"These," she said, holding up a russeted apple, "go to the internees' families. When I can find out where they are. There's a Mrs. Brandt in Maidstone whose husband was taken the same day as Papa. Someone should send her apples. Apples say—" she turned the fruit in her hand, "—that somebody remembers you exist."

Cyrus thought of the Quaker clerk in Maidstone, of the postmistress who owed him, of the slow secret postal system he was building out of favours and silences, one corruption at a time. He thought: *I could route apples the way I route letters.*

"I'll find out where she is," he said.

Greta looked up sharply. "How?"

"Same way I find out everything. I read other people's mail." He said it lightly, and immediately wished he hadn't, because her face changed—went careful, went distant, the way it had gone careful once before when he'd let slip how much passed through his hands. The guilt arrived on schedule, punctual as the Royal Mail. "Greta. The letters of yours I carry. Your brother's. I've never—"

"I know you haven't," she said. And then, softer, looking down at the apple in her palm: "I know exactly what you are, Cyrus Crane. You're the man who memorises lives so he can carry them properly. I worked that out the first month I knew you." She set the apple in the bin with the others bound for strangers. "Just—carry mine gently. That's all. They weigh more than they look."

He drove back through the sleeping lanes as the sky went grey at the edges, the Austin's engine ticking, the oilcloth-wrapped bundle of unsent letters riding in the box beside him like a passenger. At the top of Marlowe Hill he stopped and looked back. The Hollis orchard lay below in the last of the dark, and he could not see the fence he'd mended, but he knew it was there, standing, holding—new oak in an old line, invisible in the darkness, doing its work regardless.

In the garage he lifted the floorboard and added nothing and removed nothing and simply knelt there a moment with his hand flat on the boards, the way Greta had laid her palm on the new post.

It was Mrs. Fennimore, three hours later, delivered chattering to her sister's door, who gave him the news that turned his blood to well-water: her sister's boy was home on leave, stationed with the detachment at Maidstone, and he'd let drop over supper that there was talk—official talk—of a survey. Alien-owned land. Agricultural holdings. *A list*, Mrs. Fennimore said comfortably, buttering her toast, "of all the property belonging to enemy persons, so the Government knows what's what. Nothing to worry about for good English folk, of course."

Cyrus thanked her, and held the door, and smiled the smile that had opened ten thousand doors for him.

Then he sat in the motorcar in the lane and understood, with perfect clarity, that the fence he had mended in the dark was already too late, and that the next weight coming down the road toward the Hollis orchard would not be carried by cart at all—it would arrive on paper, stamped and sealed, and it would be addressed, this time, to the land itself.
