## 17. Suspicion Falls

Edith Marlowe had learned long ago that trouble never announced itself with trumpets. It arrived the way damp arrived in an old house—quietly, through some unnoticed crack, spreading along the skirting boards until one morning the whole wall was soft and the whole house was compromised. So when Mrs. Pettifer from the post office lingered at the gate on Tuesday morning, twisting her gloves in her hands and saying, "Miss Marlowe, I wonder—might I have a word? A private word?" Edith felt the damp come in before a single word was spoken.

They stood in the hall because Mrs. Pettifer would not sit. She was a small woman built like a wren, all quick movements and bright anxious eyes, and she had been delivering Edith's correspondence since before Edith had learned to write letters worth delivering. That history made what came next worse, not better.

"It's Mr. Crane," she said. "I wouldn't say anything—I wouldn't dream of saying anything—but it's not me you want to worry about, is it. It's Major Hallowes."

Edith kept her face still. "What about Cyrus?"

"Seen. At night." Mrs. Pettifer's voice dropped as though the walls themselves might inform. "At the Hollis orchard. Three times now, by different accounts. Young Tom Verrall saw the motorcar turn down the lane past the church after eleven o'clock last Thursday. And Mrs. Dench's boy swears he saw a lantern moving among the trees near midnight on Sunday. They're saying—" she swallowed— "they're saying he's carrying letters for them. Enemy correspondence. That he's mixed up in something."

"Are they saying that," Edith said, "or are they saying it loudly enough that they hope someone will repeat it?"

Mrs. Pettifer flushed. "I only came to warn you, miss. Because of all you've done for the relief fund. Because people talk, and when they've run out of Hollises to talk about they'll want someone else, and a woman like yourself—a woman who's already made herself conspicuous—" She stopped, wringing her gloves into a rope. "You understand what I'm telling you."

"I understand perfectly," Edith said. "Thank you, Mrs. Pettifer. You were kind to come."

"Kindness has nothing to do with it," the little postmistress said fiercely, and then, as if embarrassed by her own honesty, bobbed a curtsy and hurried back down the drive.

Edith stood in the hall a long while. The morning light came through the fanlight above the door and lay on the tiled floor in a pale wedge, and dust moved through it, slow and indifferent. Somewhere upstairs a window rattled in the wind off the orchard country—the wind that came up from the Weald carrying, in summer, the smell of apple blossom and cut grass, and now, in this grey October, only the smell of coming rain.

She should call Cyrus in and ask him plainly. That was the sensible thing, the thing she would have advised any of her committee women to do: confront, establish facts, control the narrative before the narrative controlled you. She had spent fifteen years teaching women that silence was the enemy's greatest ally, that shame thrived in dark corners, that the first act of resistance was always speech.

And yet she found she did not want to ask him. She found that the thought of his face when she asked—that careful, closed face of his, the face of a man who had learned in some workhouse childhood to give away nothing—made her chest tighten in a way she refused to examine.

Because she knew. Of course she knew. She had known since the night she'd heard the motorcar leave and return, since she'd found the shed locked with a new padlock and Cyrus standing beside it with a lantern and an expression of a man caught between two duties. She had said nothing then either. She was accumulating silences the way other women accumulated debts, and she understood, with cold clarity, that every one of them bore interest.

* * *

The constable came on Wednesday.

His name was Constable Pike, and he was a decent enough man in a weak sort of way—young, earnest, ambitious in the small manner of village policemen, wanting chiefly to be thought well of by whoever happened to be watching. He arrived at Marlowe House at half past ten, helmet under his arm, notebook already open, and asked if Miss Marlowe might spare him a few minutes on official business.

"Official," Edith repeated, showing him into the morning room. "That sounds grave."

"It's just inquiries, miss. Routine." He sat on the edge of the chair she indicated, perched like a bird on a wire, and consulted his notebook though Edith suspected he knew its contents by heart. "We've had a report. Regarding movements at the Hollis farm. After hours, as you might say."

"Have you."

"Yes, miss." He cleared his throat. "A motorcar was observed turning into the Hollis lane on the night of Thursday last, at approximately eleven o'clock. And again on Sunday. The description given matches your vehicle, miss. Dark green Wolseley, nearside lamp with a crack in the glass."

"You know my car's lamp has a crack in it?"

"I've seen it about, miss." He colored slightly. "It's a distinctive vehicle. Not many motorcars in the parish."

"No," Edith agreed. "Not many. Which makes any motorcar memorable, and any description of one reliable. Whose is the report?"

"I'm not at liberty—"

"Tom Verrall," Edith said. "Or the Dench boy. Or both. It hardly matters; there are only so many people in this village who are out of their beds after eleven o'clock, and most of them are poachers." She let the silence sit for a moment. "Constable, may I ask what precisely Mr. Crane is alleged to have done?"

Pike looked at his notebook again, as though hoping the answer might be written there in someone else's hand. "Being seen at the premises of registered enemy aliens during prohibited hours, miss. Possibly conveying correspondence. There's—" he hesitated— "there's a feeling, you understand, that letters might be going out of the country. To Germany. Through channels." He said *channels* the way one says *smugglers' coves*, with a certain relish borrowed from novels.

"To Germany," Edith said. "Through channels. From an interned orchard-keeper's daughter whose brother is dying in a Berlin hospital. Constable, do you know what a Red Cross letter is?"

"I—no, miss. Not precisely."

"It is a form. A single card, both sides, twenty-five words maximum, censored twice, taking six weeks to travel a distance a pigeon could fly in an afternoon. It is the only means by which Greta Hollis may tell her brother that she is alive, or learn whether he still is. If Mr. Crane were carrying such a letter, he would be committing no crime whatsoever. He would be performing an act of ordinary decency that this war has somehow made extraordinary."

Pike shifted. "Be that as it may, miss, I'm instructed to put questions to Mr. Crane. Formally. There's those above me now, you understand. Military liaison. They don't—" he gestured vaguely with his pencil— "they don't do things the old ways."

"And where is Mr. Crane at present?"

"He's wanted at the station, miss. Eleven o'clock. He's been sent for."

The clock on the mantel showed twenty minutes to eleven. Edith looked at it, and at the young man perched on her chair with his notebook full of other people's suspicions, and she felt the familiar fatigue settle over her—not the fatigue of the body but of the spirit, the particular exhaustion of watching reasonable people perform unreasonable things because the unreason had been stamped with an official seal.

"Constable Pike," she said, "I am going to accompany Mr. Crane to your station. Not because he requires protection—he is quite capable of answering questions—but because I find I have a few questions of my own, and I prefer to ask them where answers are given under oath rather than over garden fences."

* * *

Cyrus was waiting in the yard behind the police station when they arrived, standing beside the motorcar with his cap in his hands. He had shaved, Edith noticed. He had put on his good collar. He had dressed for the occasion the way a man dresses for a funeral—his own—and the sight of it did something painful to her chest.

"They've sent for me," he said quietly. No question in it. He looked at her, and there was in his eyes neither fear nor appeal, only a kind of weary readiness, the look of a man who has carried heavy things all his life and has simply been handed another.

"Yes," Edith said. "Walk in with me. Say what's true. Nothing more than what's true."

"That's generally been my practice, miss."

"Don't *miss* me in there," she said, low. "In there I am Miss Marlowe of the relief committee, and you are my driver, and we are employer and employee and nothing else. Do you understand why?"

He considered her for a moment. "Because if they think there's nothing between us but wages, they've got no lever."

Something in her flinched at the word *nothing*, though she knew he meant it tactically, and she hated—hated—that he had learned to speak of human connection in the language of levers and loads. "Yes," she said. "Precisely that."

Inside, the station smelled of tea gone cold and boot polish. Sergeant Hobbes presided behind a desk scarred with fifty years of initials, and beside him, to Edith's dismay, sat a man she did not know: a civilian in a good coat with a military armband, thin-faced and precise, with the look of a man who enjoyed his work the way cats enjoy hunting—professionally, without malice, and without mercy.

"Miss Marlowe." He rose politely. "Lieutenant Ashworth. Liaison office. I don't believe we've met, but I know your name, of course. Your work with the National Union is well regarded." He said it smoothly, and Edith heard in the smoothness the sound of a file being drawn slowly across a lock.

"Lieutenant."

"We're conducting a routine inquiry regarding communications with the enemy. This man"—a nod toward Cyrus, who stood with his cap in his hands and his face arranged into perfect blankness—"is employed by you, I understand."

"He is."

"Drives for you."

"He does."

"Has access to your correspondence. To the post. To the house."

"All of that is true," Edith said, "and none of it is a crime. Shall we proceed to whatever it is you actually wish to ask?"

Ashworth smiled faintly, as if she had confirmed something pleasant. He turned to Cyrus. "Name?"

"Cyrus Crane."

"Where were you on the night of Thursday last, between the hours of ten and midnight?"

Cyrus did not hesitate. "Driving."

"Driving where?"

"On parish roads."

Ashworth's smile thinned. "Let us be plainer, Mr. Crane. Were you at Hollis farm on Thursday night?"

"I was on the Hollis lane," Cyrus said. "The motorcar developed a fault with the nearside rear tyre. I stopped to attend to it."

"At eleven o'clock at night. On a lane that leads nowhere but to an interned family's property."

"The fault did not consult the clock, sir."

There was a silence. Hobbes wrote something. Ashworth folded his hands. "And the previous occasions? Witnesses place a vehicle matching yours at the farm three times in two weeks. And a lantern among the trees. What does a man attend to with a lantern among apple trees at midnight, Mr. Crane?"

"Fences," Cyrus said. "The Hollises' fence was broken. Broken fencing is a trespass matter, and trespass against that property falls to nobody now that the family's taken. Somebody must see to it or the stock will wander and the trees will be stripped. I see to it."

"Somebody," Ashworth repeated softly. "A public-spirited individual. On his own time. In the dark. Carrying—" he lifted a paper from the desk— "because we also have a report of parcels being removed from the premises. Crates. Taken by cart to a shed behind this address. Would you know anything about crates, Mr. Crane?"

Edith watched Cyrus's hands. They did not move. His face did not move. But she saw the stillness change quality—from calm to held breath—and she understood that they had reached the true question, the one underneath all the others, and that everything now depended on the next thirty seconds.

"The shed is mine," she said.

Every head turned. Even Cyrus's composure flickered.

"The shed stands on my property," Edith continued, in the clear carrying voice she used in halls of four hundred people, "and it contains my property. Lifting gear, tarpaulins, spare parts for the motorcar, and the winter stores for the relief depot, which I keep there because the vicar's cellar floods. If crates have been moved, they are my crates, moved at my instruction, containing my goods. Is there some particular law against a woman storing jam and engine oil in her own outbuilding, Lieutenant? If so, I shall require the number of the statute, in writing, so that my solicitor may admire it."

Ashworth regarded her for a long moment. The room was very quiet. Outside, rain had begun against the window, fine and persistent, the kind of rain that could go on for days.

"Your goods," he said at last.

"My goods."

"And you take personal responsibility for the contents of that shed."

"Entirely," said Edith Marlowe, and heard, beneath her own steady voice, the door of some prison closing behind her—though whether hers or his she could not yet have said.

Ashworth closed his notebook. "Then there's nothing further today. Mr. Crane, you will make yourself available should we wish to speak again. Miss Marlowe." A small bow, precise as a paper cut. "This country is at war. Everyone is watching everyone. One forgets it at one's peril." He gathered his papers and left, and the rain filled the silence behind him.

Hobbes cleared his throat, embarrassed. "Nothing personal, Miss Marlowe. Orders is orders."

"Of course, Sergeant. Give my regards to Mrs. Hobbes."

Outside, the rain had thickened. Cyrus held the umbrella over her without being asked, walking half a step behind as he always did, and it was only when they reached the motorcar that she spoke.

"You lied to them."

"I told them what was true," Cyrus said, cranking the engine. "The fence was broken. I mended it."

"You told them the crates were mine."

He straightened, rain running off the brim of his cap. "You said it first."

"Because otherwise they'd have turned that shed inside out by noon, and everything in it—the deed, Anton's letters, forty years of that family's life—would be in an evidence room in Maidstone by Friday." She climbed into the passenger seat and shut the door hard. "Do you imagine I don't know what I've done? I have just sworn, before a police officer and a military liaison man, that I am personally responsible for contraband. If that shed is searched and found to hold what it holds, it is not you they will charge, Cyrus. It is me. I have made myself the wall between them and Greta Hollis, and walls get broken, and I have spent my whole life making certain I was never on the side that gets broken."

He sat with his hands on the wheel, not starting the engine, looking out through the streaming windscreen at the grey street, the shuttered shopfronts, the notice board outside the church where the names of enlisted men multiplied week by week like a slow contagion.

"Why did you do it?" he asked at last. Not accusingly. Wonderingly, almost. As though she were a phenomenon he had encountered and could not classify.

And Edith, who had answers for journalists and hecklers and cabinet ministers, who could construct a case in her sleep and defend it standing, found she had no answer ready that would survive daylight. *Because it is right* was true and insufficient. *Because they are innocent* was true and irrelevant. *Because you carried those letters in your coat for weeks and I watched you grow into a man I cannot—*

"Drive," she said. "Before I think better of it."

He drove. The wipers ticked. The lanes ran silver with water, and the hedges bent under the weight of it, and somewhere behind them the village went on talking, and ahead of them, at the end of the wet road, the lights of Marlowe House burned yellow through the rain—and in the shed behind it, under tarpaulin, under padlock, under her sworn word, the papers waited.

It was three days later that the summons came: a cream envelope, hand-delivered, bearing the crest of the Kent Federation of Women's Suffrage Societies. Mrs. Bexhill-Smythe requested Miss Marlowe's attendance at a meeting of the executive committee, Friday next, at her earliest convenience.

*Matter urgent,* the note concluded, in a hand Edith knew well. *Regarding the relief fund.*
