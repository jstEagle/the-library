## 19. What Invisibility Costs

The rain came in off the Weald that October morning the way it always did—sideways, patient, indifferent to whose roof it found. Cyrus Crane stood in the yard behind Marlowe House with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, watching water pool in the wheel ruts of the drive, and he thought about the first time anyone had ever said his name aloud as though it belonged to someone.

He had been seven years old. It was at the workhouse at Faversham, and a new clerk with ink-stained fingers was going down the intake ledger, reading names back to the children to confirm them, and when he came to *Crane, Cyrus* he looked up—not at the ledger, not at the matron standing behind him, but at Cyrus himself—and said, "Cyrus. That's a king's name, that is. Persian." And then he went on to the next child, because that was all there was to say, but for one moment Cyrus had existed in the eyes of another person, had been seen whole, and the feeling had been so violent and so strange that he had hidden in the wash-house afterward and cried into a sack until the sack smelled of him and he was ashamed of that too.

That was the trouble with being seen. You couldn't unsee yourself after.

He was thirty-one now, and he had spent twenty-four of those years perfecting the art of not being looked at. It was not cowardice, whatever Greta might have teased him about if she'd known his thoughts—it was craft. A man who worked in service learned early that invisibility was a kind of currency. The footman who was seen was the footman who was blamed; the driver who was noticed was the driver who was dismissed; the boy in the workhouse who caught the matron's eye got the extra chores, or worse, got her attention in ways no boy wanted. He had learned to stand against walls. To keep his hands still. To arrange his face into something so unremarkable that eyes slid off it like rain off glass.

And then, somewhere along the way, the craft had become the man.

* * *

He thought about it now, driving the long road toward Ashford with Edith's correspondence in the leather case beside him—the ordinary letters, the committee letters, the ones that were only what they appeared to be—and he thought: *I have carried other people's lives for so long I have forgotten whether I have one of my own.*

It was true of the work. He read the village mail the way other men read newspapers, and he knew things no one would ever credit him knowing. He knew that Mrs. Pettifer at the mill owed money to her sister in Maidstone and pretended the parcels were gifts. He knew that young Tom Aldridge wrote to two girls in different towns and kept his spelling worse for one of them so she'd think him simple and not mind his wandering eye. He knew which marriages were held together by habit and which by fear, which households had stopped writing altogether, and what that silence meant. He was the keeper of the parish's inner life, its paper bloodstream, and not one soul in Kent suspected it. They saw a driver. A quiet fellow. Crane. What was his first name again?

Even Edith, who trusted him with her cause and increasingly, he thought, with something less nameable than trust—even Edith saw mostly what she needed to see: a reliable man, a discreet man, a man who asked nothing. She did not see that the asking-nothing was itself an appetite, a hunger fed on being overlooked until being overlooked felt like safety and safety felt like a room with the door bricked up.

His mother had died when he was four. He had one memory of her—a hand on his forehead, the smell of coal smoke and lavender—and he had carried that memory the way he carried everything: carefully, silently, weighing it each night before sleep to make sure it hadn't lost anything in the keeping. That was his philosophy, the one he'd built his whole life on: everything can be carried if you know its weight. Letters. Secrets. Other people's griefs. His own.

But lately he had begun to suspect the philosophy had a flaw in it, the way an apple can look sound from the outside and be brown all through at the core.

* * *

The flaw had a name, and the name was Greta Hollis.

Not in any way the village gossips would have understood, thank God, because the village gossips had teeth these days and the constable had already come once to ask his questions about why a chauffeur was mending fences at an enemy alien's orchard after dark. Edith had covered for him—had lied for him, smooth as poured cream, and he still didn't know what to do with the knowledge that she'd done it—but that wasn't what he meant.

What he meant was this: three nights ago, standing in the wet grass of the Hollis orchard with a fence rail in his hands and Greta holding the other end, she had looked at him across the rail and said, "You have a scar. Here—" and touched her own eyebrow, "—just there. I've wondered for months how you got it."

And he had stood there with the rain coming down and realized that in thirty-one years, no living person had ever noticed his face closely enough to wonder about a scar.

"It was a dog," he said. "At the workhouse. I climbed the wall to steal apples from the vicar's garden and the verger's dog found me first."

Greta laughed—that low, startled laugh of hers, the laugh that seemed to surprise even her—and said, "You stole apples? *You?*" as though he were a man capable of appetites, of mischief, of history. As though he were somebody.

"I was seven," he said. "It was the last time I did anything interesting."

"That isn't true," she said, and went on holding the fence rail, and looking at him, and the strange thing—the thing that kept him awake that night and every night since—was that he believed her. She thought he was interesting *now*. She thought the man who memorized other people's letters and carried other people's secrets and stood against walls was worth looking at, worth wondering about, worth asking questions of that no one had ever asked.

She had made him visible to himself. That was the truth of it, and it terrified him more than the soldiers at the orchard gate, more than the internment orders, more than the whole rotten machinery of suspicion grinding through the county. Because a man who is invisible can survive anything—he has nothing to lose, no self to lose, no face to lose. But a man who has been *seen*, once, properly seen—a man who knows what it feels like to exist in another person's eyes—that man has something to protect. That man can be hurt.

* * *

He stopped the car at the top of Gallows Hill, where the road bent and you could see the whole valley spread out below: the village smoking quietly under the rain, the church spire, the river, and beyond the hop fields the dark line of the Hollis orchard, bare-limbed now, its rows running straight as ruled lines toward the grey horizon.

From here, in peacetime, it had looked like a painting. From here, today, it looked like a wound.

Forty-eight hours. That was what the sealed letter had given them, and the forty-eight hours had come and gone, and the Hollises were gone too—taken away in a police van on a Tuesday morning while half the village stood at their gates to watch, and the other half stayed indoors and told themselves it was nothing to do with them. Mr. Hollis had refused to run, insisting on his innocence the way a man insists on dry ground while the flood takes him, dignified and doomed. Mrs. Hollis had packed her own trunk. And Greta—British-born, they conceded, grudgingly, as though her birth were a technicality she'd slipped past them—Greta had been permitted to stay, alone, under watch, in a house stripped of its men and most of its meaning.

Cyrus had stood in the crowd that morning. Invisible. Hating it. Hating every skill that had made him invisible, every year of practice at being nobody, because this—*this*—was what the craft was finally for: standing in a crowd doing nothing while a family that had sheltered Edith Marlowe when she needed sheltering was carted off like cattle, and no one could point at him afterward and say he'd lifted a finger.

Except he had lifted fingers. He had moved trunks at midnight. He had the deed and Anton's letters locked in the shed behind Marlowe House, wrapped in oilcloth, and every day he expected the knock on the door, and every day he went out to the shed anyway and checked the lock, because the promise was the only solid thing left in him.

*You'll look after her,* Anton had said, home on leave, shaking his hand in the orchard with the summer light coming through the leaves. *Whatever happens. Her and the papers. Whatever happens.*

He'd promised too lightly. That was the sin he couldn't stop turning over, like a stone with something underneath it. He'd promised the way you promise a dying man anything, to ease him, meaning it and not meaning it, meaning it the way you mean *yes, of course, next summer*. He hadn't known the promise would come due. Nobody knows. That's the whole trick of oaths—they're written in peacetime and called in during war, and the interest is always paid in a currency you didn't agree to.

* * *

In the workhouse they had taught him one lesson thoroughly, though they hadn't meant it as a kindness: that a boy with no name and no people could be assigned anywhere, set to anything, forgotten indefinitely. He had been farmed out at fourteen to a livery stable in Canterbury, then to a household in Tunbridge Wells where the mistress never once learned that he disliked boiled mutton, because learning such a thing would have required seeing him as a person with preferences, and servants were furniture that walked. Then Edith Marlowe had hired him, and for six years he had driven her to rallies and meetings and prisons-gates, carrying her banners and her leaflets and her fury, and he had believed—dimly, without examining it—that serving a great cause was a substitute for having a life of your own. That reflected glory was a kind of light.

But causes, he had learned this autumn, are also rooms with bricked-up doors. Edith's movement, which had taught ten thousand women to stand up and be counted, to be *seen* at any cost—to be imprisoned and force-fed rather than unseen—could not find its voice for one German-born orchard family, because the times had made sympathy a contagion and the movement could not afford to be ill. He understood it. That was the worst part. He understood the arithmetic perfectly: one family weighed against the vote for millions. He simply could no longer persuade his heart to sign the sum.

Because Greta had shown him the flaw in his philosophy. Everything can be carried if you know its weight—yes. But some things change weight in the carrying. Some things grow heavier the longer you hold them, and some things grow so heavy they press a shape into you, and one morning you look down and realize you are no longer the shape you chose. He had chosen the shape of the invisible man, and it had kept him safe for thirty-one years, and now it was killing him by inches, because the invisible man could stand in crowds and move trunks at midnight and lie to constables, but the invisible man could not take Greta Hollis's hand in daylight. Could not tell Edith what he was becoming. Could not be, in the open air, the person Greta's eyes insisted he already was.

Visibility cost. That was the lesson of the autumn, written in parish registers with names struck through, in sealed letters, in police vans on Tuesday mornings. To be seen was to be counted, and to be counted was to be taken. The Hollises had been visible—German-born, land-owning, distinct—and visibility had destroyed them. His whole life he had thought invisibility was the price. Now he knew it was also the cost, paid out daily, in a coin he was running out of.

* * *

He drove back through the rain as the light failed, and at the bend above the orchard he stopped the car again, though he had nowhere to stop and no reason, and he sat with the engine ticking and watched the last grey light go off the trees.

Then he got out and walked down the lane to the orchard gate, because he had told Greta he would come, and because keeping small promises was the only training he had for keeping large ones.

She was waiting under the big Bramley at the end of the row, lantern lit, a sacking bundle over her arm. Against orders, against sense, against everything the season demanded, she had been grafting—cutting scions from the old trees, wrapping them in damp cloth, preparing stock that might live in Wales or nowhere, in spring or never. Preparing, in the only language she had left, for a future she could not yet name.

"You're late," she said, but she was smiling, and the lantern threw her shadow long across the wet grass, and Cyrus Crane—who had spent a lifetime arranging his face into nothing—felt his face do something it had never done in front of anyone: it simply told the truth.

"Greta," he began, and stopped, because down the lane behind him, where the road met the sky, a motorcar engine sounded—wrong, too fast for the lane, stopping—and a door slammed, and a voice he knew called his name into the dark.

It was Edith. And she did not sound like a woman who had come to bring good news.
