## 37. Orchard Restored

The letter came on a Tuesday, in the second week of October, when the light had gone thin and gold and the orchard smelled of bruised fruit and coming frost.

Greta saw the postman's bicycle from the top of the long ladder, where she was picking the last of the Ribston pippins into a canvas bag that rode against her hip. She did not climb down at once. She had learned, these two years, to let good news and bad news arrive at the same pace—neither rushing toward either. The envelope was brown and official-looking, and her heart performed its old trick of stopping, then starting again too fast.

By the time she reached the gate, Cyrus was already there. He had a way of appearing when letters came that she had stopped questioning months ago; he simply materialized at the edges of things, coat collar up, hands in pockets, as though he had been standing there all along and it would be rude of her to notice. He held out the envelope without a word, but his eyes were doing something they almost never did. They were shining.

"Read it," he said. His voice was rough. "Out loud. I've waited long enough."

Her fingers were not steady. She turned the envelope over twice before she found the flap. Inside: a single sheet, typed, with a stamp she recognized from the land agent's office and a heading she had come to dread—*In the Matter of the Hollis Orchard, Weald Parish*—and then words that refused, at first, to arrange themselves into sense.

*...the Court finds for the Defendant. Title to the premises is confirmed to Mrs. Greta Hollis, née record held under the name of Edith Mary Hollis (born Whitcombe), of English birth and domicile... The seizure order is hereby discharged...*

She read it three times. On the third reading, the paper began to shake so badly in her hand that Cyrus took it from her gently, the way one takes something breakable from a child, and read it aloud himself, slowly, in his flat driver's voice that could make even a court order sound like a weather report—and somehow that made it realer than anything shouted ever could have.

"It's yours," he said. "It was always yours. Her name—the whole time. Your mother's name was on the deed, English as rain." He looked up at her, and the shine in his eyes spilled over into something like a laugh. "All those men in that courtroom, all their rules about enemy property, and the answer was sitting in a church crypt in a coffin the entire time."

Greta sat down on the low stone wall by the gate because her legs informed her they were no longer interested in holding her up. The orchard spread out below her in its autumn dress—rows and rows of trees going amber and rust, heavy with the crop no one had been permitted to sell at full price, the crop that had hung there all season like a question. Two years. Two years since the soldiers at the gate, since her father's straight back and her mother's white knuckles as the cart took them away down the lane, since the parish register with its careful inked lines through German names. Two years of tending trees under watch, of neighbors who crossed the road rather than pass her, of windfalls stripped in the night by people who would have spat at her father a year before and shook his hand over the fence a year before that.

And now a piece of paper said the ground was hers.

"They'll appeal," she said. It was not a question; it was simply the shape her caution had grown into, the callus over everything.

"The solicitor says they'd be fools to try. Mr. Quill—" Cyrus still could not say the Quaker clerk's name without a small private smile, as though the man were a conjuring trick he'd personally arranged "—he found the original conveyance. 1889. Signed, witnessed, filed. There's nothing to appeal *to*, Greta. It's done."

*It's done.* She made herself say the words back, silently, testing them the way you test ice. *It's done.*

Then, quite without warning, she began to cry—not the quiet, rationed crying she had practiced for two years, doled out in the dark where no one could count it against her, but great ugly sobs that bent her double over the stone wall, that frightened a robin off the gatepost, that made Cyrus hover beside her with his hands half-raised, unsure whether she was a person to be held or a fire to be smothered. She solved the question for him by grabbing his lapels and pulling him down until his forehead rested against hers, and they stayed like that, breathing the same cold apple-scented air, while the letter lay between them on the wall like a small sleeping animal.

"I keep thinking," she managed at last, "of Papa signing papers at that table by the window. He was so *careful*. He never trusted lawyers, he said, so he did everything himself, everything in duplicate, everything in Mother's name because—" her breath caught "—because he said England would never take what an Englishwoman owned. He *knew*. Even then. He knew what might come, and he built us a shelter out of paper, and I thought he was just being fussy."

"He wasn't fussy," Cyrus said. "He was a man who understood weight." He said it the way he said such things—as though quoting some private scripture of his own. "Everything can be carried if you know its weight. He knew exactly what this place weighed."

They stayed a long while. At some point she noticed he had picked up the letter and folded it along its original creases with the precision of a man who had spent his life handling other people's most important moments, and had tucked it inside his coat, against his chest, where he carried things that mattered—which meant, she understood now, that he intended to carry it home to Marlowe House and put it in the strongbox with the deed, and that this was his way of saying *I will guard it* without having to find the words, because Cyrus Crane would rather carry a piano than say a feeling aloud.

* * *

The marker was her mother's idea, though her mother was forty miles away in an internment camp on the Isle of Man and would never know she'd had it.

That was how Greta thought of it afterward—that the idea had arrived the way ideas do in families, traveling some private wire, surfacing in the daughter's mind wearing the mother's voice. *If you get it back, Liebling, put something in the ground. Not a flag. A tree. Flags come down. Trees stay.*

She chose the spot on a Sunday morning in late October, walking the rows alone while mist stood in the lanes and the grass soaked her boots silver. She wanted somewhere Anton would have loved, and that narrowed it immediately: the southeast corner, where the ground rose slightly and caught the first sun, where the oldest Bramley stood leaning like a tired grandfather over the hedge. Anton used to sit under that Bramley with his books when he came home from Berlin, reading aloud passages of philosophy in his terrible stage-German and laughing at his own accent until her father told him to hush or the apples would drop early. He had been seventeen the last full summer. He had talked about becoming a doctor, about the orchard being his anchor—*my place to return to*, he'd called it, tapping the ground with his heel, *no matter where medicine takes me.*

Medicine had taken him to a hospital bed in Berlin instead, and the war had taken the letters, and the distance had taken everything else, and in March a Red Cross card had come with six lines of type telling her what she had already known in her body for weeks—the way you know weather is changing before the sky admits it.

He was buried somewhere outside Berlin. She had never learned where. There was no grave to visit, no stone with his name, no patch of earth in England or Germany where grief could be set down like a suitcase. For two years her mourning had been a thing with no address, wandering.

So she would give it one.

Cyrus helped her make the marker, though making was perhaps too grand a word for what they did. He brought a length of seasoned oak from the woodpile behind Marlowe House—oak he had been keeping, he admitted, since spring, "in case"—and she shaped it with the drawknife while he held it in the vice of the workbench her father had left behind, the vice still bearing the ghost-marks of her father's grip. They worked mostly in silence. That was another thing the war had taught them both: that silence shared is a different substance entirely from silence endured.

"What shall it say?" Cyrus asked, when the plank was smooth and pale as bone.

She had thought about this for days. Her first instinct was the full formal thing—*Anton Hollis, 1896–1917, beloved son and brother*—but the chisel felt wrong in her hand for formality. Formality belonged to the parish register, to the tribunal, to the typed Red Cross card. This was oak from Kentish timber, cut by a Kentish hand, for a boy who had eaten plums off this very bench and gotten the juice on Kant.

"Just his name," she said finally. "And 'of this orchard.' That's all. He doesn't need dates. Everyone who needs to know, knows."

Cyrus nodded as if she had settled some theological dispute. He cut the letters himself when her hands tired—his chisel-work slow and exact, each stroke of the V-groove clean as typesetting. *ANTON HOLLIS — OF THIS ORCHARD.* When it was finished she ran her thumb along the grooves and found she could read the name with her eyes shut, which seemed right somehow. Names should be legible to grief in the dark.

They planted it on the last mild afternoon of the month, and they planted it properly, because her father would have risen from whatever peace he had found to haunt them otherwise. Cyrus dug the hole with the long spade from the shed, cutting a neat square through the root-mat of the old Bramley's drip line, and Greta lined it with broken crocks for drainage the way her mother had taught her, and they set the post and tamped the soil in layers, firming each one with the flat of the spade, and Cyrus fetched water from the trough even though the ground was wet enough, because planting is a ceremony and ceremonies want water.

Before they set the post, Greta knelt and put her hands flat on the bare earth of the hole.

There was a moment when she almost spoke to him—to Anton, to the air, to whatever thin frequency might carry between a Kentish orchard and a Berlin grave. She had never been one for talking to the dead; her family were Lutherans of the quiet sort who believed the dead were busy elsewhere. But kneeling there with cold mud soaking through her skirt, she found the words arriving anyway, uninvited, in German, in the dialect of her childhood kitchen:

*Du bist zu Hause. Egal wo sie dich gelegt haben—hier bist du zu Hause.*

*You are home. Wherever they laid you—here, you are home.*

Then she lifted the post, and Cyrus guided it true, and they filled the hole around it, and it was done.

She stepped back. The marker stood at the corner of the row, modest as a fencepost, catching the low western light. Anyone passing on the lane would see only a piece of oak with a name on it, and might wonder, and might ask, and she would tell them: my brother. He grew up here. He died over there. This is where he lives now.

Mourning given soil. She hadn't known until that moment how much she had needed the phrase, or that she had been composing it for two years without knowing. Grief needs somewhere to put its feet. Hers had been pacing a locked room all this time, and now the door stood open onto a row of apple trees, and the wind came through carrying the smell of late pippins, and somewhere down the valley a church bell rang four o'clock as though the world were an ordinary place.

Cyrus stood beside her, hat in his hands—he had removed it, she realized, without her noticing, the way he did at gravesides and doorways, his whole body quietly insisting on respect.

"He'd have liked the corner," Cyrus said. "Best sun on the farm."

"He'd have complained the Bramley dripped on his book."

"He'd have complained beautifully." And then, because Cyrus could not leave a tender moment unblessed with dryness: "We'll graft from the Bramley in spring. Put a dozen of its children down this row. In twenty years it'll be a shade corridor. Some doctor will sit under it complaining about the dripping."

Greta laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, bright and cracked at once—and then pressed her sleeve to her eyes, and then laughed again, and Cyrus watched her do it with the expression of a man watching something he had decided, privately and permanently, to protect.

* * *

Edith came the following Saturday, motoring down from London with the car smelling of petrol and cold air and, faintly, of the committee rooms she had lately been burning her bridges in. She walked the orchard rows in her good boots without complaint, inspecting the trees with the frank appraising eye of a woman who had learned to evaluate everything—people, arguments, fruit—for its usefulness and its truth.

"So it's finished," she said, when they reached the marker. She stood before it a long moment. The wind moved her hair off her forehead, showing the grey coming in at the temples that she had stopped bothering to disguise. "Anton Hollis. Of this orchard." She read it aloud softly. "Yes. That's exactly right. Dates are for officials."

"That's what I told him," Greta said, nodding at Cyrus.

"You would have," Edith said, and there was warmth in it, and something underneath the warmth that Greta had learned to recognize: the particular tenderness Edith wore like armor worn inside-out, visible only from certain angles.

They walked on. At the far corner, where the Coxes grew, Edith stopped and faced them both, and Greta saw her draw the breath she drew before platform speeches—the same breath, the same straightening of the spine—but what came out was not a speech.

"I want to say something, and I want neither of you to interrupt me, because if you do I shall lose my nerve and start talking about the harvest instead." Her hands were clasped in front of her, the old committee-room clasp. "Two years ago I refused to speak for your family. I told Cyrus the cause couldn't afford it. I wrote a denunciation—I drafted it, in my own hand, at my own desk—and I let it sit because I was afraid, and then I burned it, and I have told myself for two years that burning it was enough. It was not enough. Speaking at the tribunal was closer. But what I did this month—going to the magistrate, spending what was left of my reputation, letting those women expel me from the movement I built with my own hands—" her voice caught, steadied; she was too well-trained to falter for long "—that was the first thing I have done in years that I am entirely unashamed of. And I find I mind the expulsion less than I minds losing either of you, which tells me something about myself that I perhaps should have learned thirty years ago."

The wind moved through the orchard. Somewhere a wood pigeon went through its five-note sentence, over and over, like a man rehearsing.

"Cyrus," Edith said, "you are the most infuriating man I have ever employed, and I have employed you for eleven years partly because you never once told me what you thought unless I asked, and partly because whenever I did ask, you told me the truth in fewer words than any man alive. I am no longer employing you. I am asking you—as a free woman asking a free man—if you will stay anyway. Not as my driver. As—" and here the great Edith Marlowe, who had addressed crowds of ten thousand without a tremor, actually hesitated "—as mine. If you'll have me. I'm told I'm difficult."

Cyrus looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something Greta had never seen him do: he smiled fully, openly, with his whole face, and it transformed him so completely that she understood suddenly why Edith had kept him eleven years, and why she had fought the understanding for eleven years more.

"Madam," he said, "I have carried your correspondence for a decade. I intend to carry considerably less interesting things for the rest of it. Yes."

Edith laughed—one short, surprised bark of a laugh, quickly mastered—and then, glancing sideways at Greta as though checking whether the witness would permit it, she reached out and took his hand. Just took it, plainly, in front of God and the Bramley and the wooden marker with a dead boy's name on it, and held it.

Greta felt tears start again and did not fight them. The orchard was restored; the family, it appeared, was too—reassembled in a new configuration, patched with oak and honesty, planted in ground that no tribunal could touch.

"Well," she said, when she could trust her voice. "Someone ought to mark the occasion properly. Come inside. I'll open the last jar of last year's plum preserve, and we'll drink tea out of the good cups, and we can plan the grafting."

They walked back up the row together, three abreast, the woman who had lost her movement, the man who had found his visibility, and the girl who had won back her ground—all of them, Greta thought, refugees of one kind or another, gathered now under trees that had outlasted every order signed against them.

Behind them the marker stood in the long light, patient as oak, holding its small cargo of name and earth.

And it was there, halfway up the row, that Cyrus stopped suddenly, patting his coat pocket with the particular urgency Greta had come to know—the urgency of a man who has remembered a letter.

"There was one more thing," he said, drawing out an envelope. "It came to Marlowe House yesterday. Forwarded twice—it's been chasing you since September." He turned it over in his hands, and Greta saw his eyebrows rise, and saw, above his shoulder, the handwriting on the front—and felt the ground tilt gently beneath her, the way it does when the past sends word that it is not finished with you after all.

The address was in her mother's hand.
