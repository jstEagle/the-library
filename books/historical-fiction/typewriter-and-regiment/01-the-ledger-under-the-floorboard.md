## 1. The Ledger Under the Floorboard

The trouble with triage is that it teaches you arithmetic before it teaches you mercy. You learn to count in the space between one breath and the next: this man will live, this man might, this man has already left us and simply hasn't told his body yet. By the spring of 1917 I could do the sums faster than most officers could sign their names, which was convenient, because signing their names was very nearly all they did.

Chateau Verrier had been somebody's idea of paradise once. There were still faded cherubs on the ceiling of what we called Ward Two, gazing down with painted serenity at rows of men who had been introduced to artillery. The ballroom floor had been scoured so many times the parquet had gone the colour of old bone. Matron Odile Ferrand said the house had been built by a sugar merchant with more money than taste, and that now it belonged to the war, like everything else worth having.

I was twenty-six years old and I had stopped believing in almost everything except iodine, morphine, and ink.

The ink came later that night.

* * *

It began, as most of my days did, with a stretcher. Private Thomas Wren, Second Battalion, carried in at half past four with his leg opened from knee to ankle and his eyes fixed on a point somewhere above the ceiling cherubs, as though he'd been promised they would explain everything if he waited long enough.

"Name?" I asked, though I already knew it. We always ask anyway. It's not for the record. It's for them — proof that someone wants to know who they are while there's still a who to know.

"Wren, sir. Thomas." He corrected himself immediately, mortified. "Nurse. Sorry, Nurse."

"Thomas Wren," I said, writing it. "You're allowed to be confused about rank. Half the generals are."

That earned me something that was almost a laugh, which in my professional opinion does more good than the carbolic. Then the morphine took him and the laughing part of Thomas Wren went wherever it goes, and I set his leg with Dr. Anselm muttering measurements beside me like a bookkeeper who disapproves of the accounts.

It was afterward, when the ward had settled into its evening rhythm of moans and snoring and the occasional hymn sung badly through a bandaged jaw, that the orderly brought me the day's admission list to copy into the hospital register. And there it was, the small quiet violence I had learned to look for: Wren's name entered, but no note of the shell shock. No mention that he'd been found three kilometres behind his own lines, sitting in a flooded ditch, singing to himself, unable to tell anyone which year it was. The regimental report, which arrived folded inside the same folder, described him as "wounded, cause: enemy action."

Enemy action. As if the enemy had reached across no-man's-land and personally rearranged the furniture of his mind.

I understood why they did it. Shell shock made the brass uncomfortable. It suggested that courage had limits, and men whose careers depended on courage being infinite preferred not to have the suggestion filed where inspectors might read it. So the word vanished, and with it went any chance that Wren's treatment would be understood rather than punished, any chance that when he was sent back — and he would be sent back; they were always sent back — someone would know what they were returning.

I copied the official entry faithfully. I always do. The official record must be perfect; that's what makes mine necessary.

Then I went to my room, knelt on the cold boards beside the bed, pried up the loose floorboard under the iron frame, and took out my ledger.

* * *

It was an ordinary account book once, bought in Amiens for keeping track of linen. Now it held other inventory. I wrote by candlelight, low, the way you learn to write anything that matters in wartime — hunched, quick, as if the flame itself might be reporting to someone.

*Private T. Wren, admitted 14 April. Shell shock. Found singing in a ditch. Sings well, according to the corporal who carried him. Afraid of the dark, which he told me ashamed, as if fear were theft.*

I paused over that last line. It wasn't clinical. Ledgers aren't supposed to notice whether a man sings well. But I had decided long ago that a name alone was not enough. A name can be denied; a name can be claimed to belong to someone else, some other Thomas Wren. What power cannot easily argue with is the particular: the ditch, the song, the shame about the dark. Erasure works on summaries. It chokes on details.

So I collected details the way other people collected shrapnel souvenirs.

There were eleven names in the ledger by then. Eleven men whom the paperwork had begun to swallow. Some of the omissions were small — a wound misrecorded, a decoration quietly never processed. One was not small at all.

Corporal Émile Duras. I turned back two pages to his entry, because I always turn back to his entry, the way you press a bruise to check it's still there.

Duras had deserted. That was the word used at the time, whispered by the stretcher-bearers: ran, actually ran, during the push at the Chemin des Dames, and was caught within the hour, and was shot at dawn three days later behind the orchard wall, with a chaplain who arrived too late and a firing party drawn from his own company. He was twenty-two. He had a mother in Marseille who received a telegram informing her that her son was "missing, presumed dead," which was a lie with a stamp on it.

No trial. Or rather, a trial of the kind conducted at dawn by men who had already decided, with a defence officer who met Duras that morning for the first time. The official record listed him as missing. Missing — as though he had wandered off into the fog of war, as though the fog itself had eaten him, when in fact it had been eight men with rifles and a wall with morning light coming over it.

I had held his hand the night before. Not knowing, then, what the dawn held. He'd asked me whether God kept records, and I — raised on enough catechism to be dangerous — said yes, all of them, every hair on your head numbered. He seemed comforted. I have thought about that answer nearly every day since, because I am not at all certain it was true, and because if heaven keeps no ledger then someone on earth bloody well ought to.

*Duras, É., corporal,* I had written. *Executed without trial, 2 March. Last words: asked whether his mother would be told he was brave. He was brave. He was also afraid, and tired, and nineteen months from home, and none of those things should be capital crimes.*

The candle guttered. Outside, somewhere past the orchard, the guns were doing their slow patient work, the way rain works on a roof — not trying to get in all at once, just forever.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about keeping a secret ledger in a field hospital: it is the loneliest act of obedience I know. You would think defiance would feel like fire. Mostly it feels like kneeling on cold floorboards at midnight, blowing out your candle whenever footsteps pass, and wondering — not for the first time — whether you are a witness or merely a madwoman with good penmanship.

Because what was the ledger *for*? I asked myself that more often than I liked. If I died tomorrow — and nurses die; the shells don't check our uniforms — the board would go back under the floor and rot there with the rest of the house, and eleven names would be twelve names would be nothing. Ink holds only if someone reads it. Ink is a promise made to a future I couldn't guarantee, addressed to people I couldn't name, in a hand that might be mistaken for fiction.

And yet.

And yet every night I lifted the board and wrote, and every night the act felt less like madness and more like the only sane transaction available. Power had decided these men were inconvenient — Wren's fear, Duras's despair — and had reached for the one weapon that outlasts bullets, which is the tidy sentence, the amended form, the record that says what powerful men wish had happened. Very well. Two could keep books. Mine simply happened to be true.

I finished Wren's entry and sat for a moment with the pen idle, listening to the hospital breathe. Somewhere below, Matron was making her last round; I knew her step on the stairs, unhurried, the step of a woman who had been walking toward suffering for so long it had become a kind of home. I thought about telling her. I had thought about it perhaps forty times. Odile Ferrand missed nothing — she had once deduced from the state of my boots that I'd been into the town without leave, and from the state of my face that I'd regretted it — and yet she had never once remarked on how often I disappeared at night with a candle stub, or why my linen accounts balanced so beautifully despite my handwriting looking, in that book, like the handwriting of a woman writing something else entirely.

Perhaps she knew. Perhaps she had her own floorboard. Everyone in this war had a floorboard, I suspected — some private plank beneath which they pressed the things the century refused to hold officially.

I wrapped the ledger in its oilcloth, lowered it into the dark between the joists, and set the board back over it. From above, the room looked exactly as it should: narrow bed, washstand, crucifix left by some previous occupant that I'd never had the heart to remove. A nurse's cell. Nothing buried here but dust.

I blew out the candle and lay down, and sleep, when it finally came, was thin and full of ditches.

* * *

Dawn at Verrier arrives grey and smelling of carbolic and woodsmoke, and on the morning of the fifteenth of April it arrived with something else besides.

I was in the entrance hall early — the transports came at six, and I liked to be there before them, counting stretchers before anyone could count them for me — when I heard it. Not a vehicle. Smaller. A sound at the great front doors, the ones with the sugar merchant's monogram in the glass, a sound like a very small fist, or perhaps just a body leaning where a body shouldn't lean.

I crossed the hall and pulled the door open on the cold.

A boy stood on the steps. Seven years old, perhaps eight, in a coat cut for a much richer child and ruined by a much longer road. Mud to the knees. His eyes had the particular wide blankness I had learned to read in men coming off the trains — the look of someone who has seen the thing and has not yet agreed to have seen it. In one fist, crushed soft as a flower pressed too long in a book, he held a note.

He didn't speak. Neither did I, for a moment, which was unlike me; I am told I can talk to anyone, including the unconscious.

Then he held the note up toward me, the way you'd offer a pass to a sentry, and I took it, and the paper was good stock — the kind of paper that doesn't come from villages, that comes from desks in rooms with carpets.

The handwriting was confident, unhurried, the hand of a man accustomed to being obeyed:

*This boy is nobody's concern. See that he is moved on quietly. Forget his face.*

Forget his face.

I looked at his face. I looked at it deliberately, thoroughly, the way I looked at wounds — cataloguing, refusing to glance away. A thin child. Dark hair cut recently by someone unpractised. Eyes the brown of strong tea, red-rimmed from crying that had clearly gone on for days and had recently given up. And around his neck, on a bootlace, a small identity disc, which children of officers sometimes wore as play, engraved with a name.

I tilted the disc to catch the grey light.

The surname on it was one I knew. Not from life — from ink. From two pages back, under the loose floorboard, in my own handwriting: a colonel reported *missing, believed killed* the previous autumn, with a clerk's neat annotation in the margin of the casualty list that I had copied out word for word because it had struck me even then as odd, as a door closed slightly too carefully —

*No dependents.*

Behind me, in the hall, the first transport rattled into the courtyard, and above us the sugar merchant's painted cherubs went on smiling at nothing, and the boy on the step shivered, and I made — standing there in the doorway with the note going soft in my grip — the second promise of my life to a stranger named after all: that whatever forget his face meant, and whoever had written it, his face was now remembered, permanently, by me.
