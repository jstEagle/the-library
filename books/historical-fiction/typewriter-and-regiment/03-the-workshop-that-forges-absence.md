## 3. The Workshop That Forges Absence

There is a particular smell to a room where men are unmade on paper. It is not ink, exactly, though there is ink enough. It is the smell of warm lamp oil and cold tea and the faint vinegar of blotting paper, and underneath it all something sweeter and worse — the smell of a job being done *well*. I have worked in that smell for eleven months now, and some mornings I wake with it in my throat and have to sit on the edge of my cot until the nausea decides whether it will be a sermon or merely a headache.

My name is Elias Quill. Yes. My mother had a sense of humour, or a premonition; the two are difficult to distinguish in retrospect. I was a clerk of the Army Records Office for nine years before the war made my trade fashionable, and I was good at it — punctual, neat, possessed of a hand so regular that officers used to ask if I employed a machine. Then came the Halloway affair, which I will not describe except to say that I refused an instruction once, only once, and was broken for it like a biscuit in a fist. Demoted, disgraced, and posted here: Chateau Verrier, Records Workshop, Annex C. The place where absence is manufactured.

I want to be precise about what we do, because people imagine forgery when they hear the word *workshop*, and forgery is the least interesting thing in the building. Anyone can add a lie. What we do is subtract. A man dies at Verrier; his death certificate goes into the tray marked INCOMING; and by the time it leaves the tray marked OUTGOING he may have died somewhere else entirely, or of something more respectable than what killed him, or — this is the elegant case — he may never have been admitted at all. A shell-shocked private becomes a case of "gastric fever." A deserter shot without trial becomes "missing, presumed." A colonel's dependents column, which ought to read *one son*, becomes a clean white nothing, because a clean white nothing cannot inherit, cannot ask questions, cannot grow up with a face.

You will want to know who orders these things. So would I, some days. The instructions arrive typed, unsigned, on paper watermarked by no office that officially exists. Major Renwick's name surfaces in them the way a pike surfaces in a pond — briefly, and always just before something smaller disappears. But I am not paid to wonder. I am paid to strike lines through names in a hand so regular it might be a machine's.

* * *

The workshop occupies the old orangery, glass-roofed and draughty, forty desks arranged in ranks like beds in a ward — and I mean that comparison seriously, because the two rooms perform the same operation, one on bodies and one on paper, and both are called necessary. We work under a civilian supervisor named Herr Fassbinder-Byng, a small damp man who says "efficiency" the way other men say prayers. There are twelve of us clerks. Three are boys too young to shave. Two are women past retirement who type with terrifying serenity. The rest are men like me: damaged goods, reassigned, grateful to be anywhere with a roof, and careful not to look at what our hands are doing.

I looked. That is my crime, if you want one. I looked.

It began in February, when a file crossed my desk with a name I knew — a gunner from my own county, a boy whose mother had written to me twice in my Records Office years asking after her son's letters. The instruction said: *amend cause of death. Executed for cowardice → died of wounds.* And I amended it. Of course I amended it. I am still employed. But I copied the original first, folded it into my boot, and carried it around for three weeks like a stone, until I understood that the stone was not going to dissolve and that I had become the sort of man who keeps stones.

After that I began keeping copies. Not many. One in ten, perhaps, chosen by no system I could defend — the ones whose names sounded like home, the ones with children listed, the ones where the erasure was so thorough it looked like craftsmanship. I hid them in the false bottom of a tea chest in the storeroom, beneath a stratum of expired forms, because no one in the history of armies has ever wanted to steal expired forms. I told myself I did not know why I kept them. This was a lie, but a survivable one. A clerk can live on lies the way other men live on tobacco.

* * *

On the fourth of March I destroyed a page myself. I remember the date because it was the day the plum trees along the south wall blossomed absurdly early, as though the chateau had not heard about the war. The file concerned a Colonel — I will not write the surname even now, even here, even in my head it comes out muffled — a colonel killed in the third week of January in circumstances the official record was later pleased to call *regrettable confusion*. The instruction was comprehensive. Death location amended. Circumstances struck. Next of kin: none. Dependents: none. And appended, in that unsigned typing: *the boy also. See that no record connects them.*

*The boy also.* Four words. I sat with them for a long time. Somewhere in France, presumably, there was a boy — seven years old, the file suggested, though files suggest ages the way weather forecasts suggest sunshine — and four typed words were reaching out across the country to find him and make him into nobody. I did as instructed. I am telling you plainly: I did as instructed. I rewrote the colonel out of January, I whited the son from the dependents column, and then, because the original page was flagged for destruction rather than filing, I fed it into the stove in the corner myself, and watched it curl, and felt the stone in my chest gain a companion.

That was six weeks ago. I tell you all this so you will understand what happened last Tuesday, and why I have not slept properly since.

* * *

Last Tuesday I was sent across the main courtyard to the hospital wing with a requisition for lint — the workshop borrows medical supplies the way a parasite borrows blood, and I was the lowest organism available. It was evening, the hour when the lamps come on in the wards and the whole chateau glows amber against the dark like something that has forgotten what it is. I cut through the back corridor by the linen store because it is shorter, and because I am a coward about passing the amputation ward, and that is how I came to see her.

A nurse. Alone, at a small table dragged under the corridor's one window, with a ledger open before her and a candle burning low. She was copying. Not charting — nurses chart in a quick slanting scrawl that says *I have thirty more of you to get through*. This was different. She wrote the way I write, or the way I used to write before my hand learned to flinch: slowly, evenly, each letter set down like a brick in a wall someone meant to stand for a hundred years.

And I saw the page she was copying from.

I would know that page anywhere. I burned it. I held its corners while it went. The colonel's service record, the January entry, the struck circumstances — and there it was, resurrected, in her careful hand, transcribed line for line including the amendments, *including the amendments*, so that her ledger recorded not only the man but the lie, the erasure itself preserved like a fly in amber. She was not undoing the record. She was keeping the record of the record. She was doing, in candlelight, with defiance instead of authority, precisely the opposite of everything Annex C exists to do.

I stood in the shadow of the doorway like a burglar in my own hospital and felt something turn over in me that I could not immediately name. It was not fear, though fear arrived shortly after, punctual as ever. It was shame — but shame of a new vintage. I had thought of the workshop as a machine. Machines are comfortable. Machines have no faces. But here was the truth of it, standing in a corridor with a candle: every page I burned had a twin somewhere, a person who remembered, a mother, a comrade, a nurse with steady hands — and the machine was not a machine at all. It was twelve clerks and a supervisor and a major with clean gloves, human beings agreeing, one signature at a time, to forget on command. The engine of erasure was *us*. And this woman had seen through the machinery to the men inside it, and she was writing us down.

She looked up. I want to be honest about that too, because a braver man would have walked away and a wiser man would never have stopped. She looked up, and her eyes went from my face to the requisition chit in my hand to whatever my face was doing, which I suspect was a great deal, and she did not startle. She simply looked at me the way one looks at weather — assessing, unhurried — and then, deliberately, she turned the ledger so that I could see the heading of the page.

Not the name. The heading. The little annotation she kept above each entry, in her even hand: *omitted from official record — restored below.*

Restored. As if names were church windows and she had taken up glazing.

Then she turned it back, and blew out the candle, and the corridor was dark, and I was left holding a requisition for lint like a man holding a ticket to a train he no longer recognises.

* * *

I delivered the chit. I walked back across the courtyard under the ridiculous early blossom. I sat at my desk in the orangery and struck lines through names for two more hours, and every stroke felt like what it was.

Here is what I have concluded, in the sleepless arithmetic since Tuesday. She knows. She must know — not everything, perhaps, but enough. She knows pages leave the workshop lighter than they entered, and she knows the shape of the hand that lightens them, because she has been reading our subtractions the way a doctor reads a wound. And she has said nothing. No report, no accusation, no appeal to Matron or the military police. She is doing something quieter and far more dangerous: she is waiting, and counting, and building her wall of bricks one letter at a time.

Which means the question is no longer whether I keep my stones in the tea chest. The question is whether I remain part of the machine or admit, at last, that the machine has a pulse, and that mine has been beating on the wrong side of the desk for eleven months.

This morning there was a memorandum on my desk, unsigned, typed, familiar watermark. Routine audit, it said. An inspector arriving within the fortnight. All workshop records to be reviewed, reconciled, and — the word sat there with terrible calm — *disposed of* prior to decommissioning. Decommissioning. They are going to burn the orangery, and everything in it, and call it tidiness.

I looked at the memorandum for a long time. Then I looked across the courtyard, at the hospital wing, at one particular window behind which a nurse was almost certainly writing names that powerful men had paid good money to lose.

I picked up my pen. And for the first time in eleven months, I did not strike anything out.
