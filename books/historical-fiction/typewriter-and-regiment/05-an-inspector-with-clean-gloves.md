## 5. An Inspector With Clean Gloves

He arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were our worst day—convoy days, the days the ambulances came down the hill in a groaning line and we learned what the front had been thinking about all week. Nobody with any decency schedules an inspection on a Tuesday.

Major Aldous Renwick stepped out of a staff car so clean it looked as though it had never heard of mud, let alone been driven through it, and I understood at once that this was a man who arranged the world so that it would not dirty him. His gloves were white. His boots were polished to a shine that threw back the grey October light. He shook Matron Ferrand's hand with a warmth that was entirely manufactured and entirely effective, and then he turned that warmth on the rest of us, distributed evenly, like rations.

"Nurses, orderlies, the whole brave apparatus," he said, beaming at the courtyard as if we were a view. "I've read such good things about Chateau Verrier."

Nobody had written anything good about Chateau Verrier. We were a converted country house with a leaking roof and a mortality rate we did not discuss in front of the men. I decided in that first minute that I disliked him, and then I decided, more carefully, to be careful, because there was a difference between a fool and a man who pretended to be one, and Major Renwick was very much the second sort.

"I'll want to see everything," he told Matron, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring a garden. "Wards, stores, kitchens, records. Everything. I do my best work when I'm thorough."

"Inspectors usually do," said Matron, in the tone of a woman agreeing with a doctor's diagnosis of a patient she had already privately given up on.

I was triaging a sergeant with a thigh wound when he first spoke to me directly. He appeared at my elbow without sound, which is a skill I associate with cats and with men who have been taught to move quietly around other people's lives.

"Efficient ward," he said. "You've a system."

"We have a war," I said. "The system came after."

He laughed—genuinely, I think, which unsettled me more than a lie would have. "Jude Marchetti," he read, from the roster pinned by the door. "Unusual name."

"Italian, originally."

"And you keep the ward book?"

"I keep everything that needs keeping."

Something moved behind his eyes at that. Not suspicion—suspicion I could have named and armed myself against. It was more like a man checking a pocket to confirm a key is still there. "I admire that," he said. "In an institution, the person who keeps everything is worth more than the person who runs it. Commanders come and go. Records endure."

"Records endure," I agreed, and heard my own voice say it lightly, lightly, while somewhere under my ribs a floorboard lifted and my ledger sat in its dark and did not breathe.

* * *

He was thorough. That was the worst of it. A lazy inspector I could have charmed, misled, or outlasted. Renwick spent two full days being excellent at his job.

He counted the stores and found our discrepancies and accepted Matron's explanations with a smile that said he was accepting them the way a chess player accepts a move. He toured the surgical ward and asked Private Hollis—shell-shocked, struck from the regimental roll as a "clerical transfer"—how long he'd been with us, and Hollis, who could not reliably remember his own mother, said "since the spring," and Renwick wrote something in a small black notebook, and I watched the pen move and thought: *there it is, there is the cost of a sentence.*

He was polite to everyone. He thanked the orderlies. He complimented the kitchen. He asked Sister Byrne about her family in Cork and remembered the details an hour later, which is either charm or tradecraft, and by the second day I had stopped believing there was a difference.

And through all of it, he asked about the workshop.

The records workshop occupied the old orangery at the east side of the grounds—a long glass-roofed building where clerks sat at trestle tables turning the war's chaos into tidy paper. I had never been inside. I had never needed to; the paper came to us, and some of the paper, I had learned, was lies with good handwriting. Now Renwick walked its length with Elias Quill at his side, and I saw through the grimy panes the two of them bent over a table, the major's white-gloved finger resting on a page while Quill—grey-faced, careful, a man who had the look of someone carrying a full cup across a moving ship—answered questions I could not hear.

I had seen Quill only in glimpses since the boy came. Once in the corridor, once at the well. Each time he had looked at me with an expression I had not been able to read, and each time I had looked away first, because I could not afford for anyone to wonder what a records clerk and a ward nurse had to say to each other. The boy—Thomas, though we did not use the name aloud—lived in the linen store behind the folded sheets, fed on bread and condensed milk and the conspiracy of six women, and every day he was there was a day I expected to hear boots on the gravel.

Renwick did not ask about a boy. That was the thing. He asked about everything else with such completeness that the absence of one question rang like a dropped instrument.

* * *

It was on the second evening that he found me alone, and I understood that it had not been an accident, because nothing about Major Renwick was an accident.

The ward was quiet. The lamps were turned low. I was at the desk updating the admission book, and he came in without knocking, the way men of his rank enter rooms, and stood looking at the shelves of files with his hands behind his back.

"Colonel Ashworth's ward was in this building, I'm told," he said. "Before he went missing."

The name went through me like cold water through cloth. I kept my pen moving. "Before the spring offensive, that would have been."

"May of last year." He turned a file on the shelf a quarter-inch, straightening it, though it had been straight. "You'd have been here then."

"I was."

"Tell me about his last week." He said it the way he'd said *I admire that*—pleasantly, as though asking after my health. "The officers' account is thin. Confusion of battle, you know how it is. But nurses notice things. Nurses always notice things."

"We notice wounds," I said. "We're busy."

"Of course." He smiled. He pulled a chair over and sat in it backward, arms folded on the rest, a posture of a man settling in for a comfortable chat, and I thought: *he has rehearsed this. He has sat in a mirror and found the angle where he looks harmless.* "Indulge me. I'm told he was in this ward for three days before he went back up the line. Wounded—graze, wasn't it? Superficial. And then he insisted on returning early. Unusual. Colonels usually enjoy a chateau."

"He was that kind of colonel," I said, and hated myself for how much truth I gave away in the tense.

"That kind," Renwick agreed softly. "The kind who writes letters. The kind who asks questions." He watched me. "The kind, I'm told, who had begun asking questions about the workshop. About how the paperwork moved. About a private executed without trial whose file had grown remarkably clean."

The ward was very quiet. Somewhere down the corridor a man moaned in his sleep, and the sound rose and fell like a tide against a seawall, and I made myself breathe with it.

"I wouldn't know about files," I said.

"No," he said. "I don't imagine you would." He rose, unhurried, and set the chair back exactly where he had found it. At the door he paused, and the lamplight caught his gloves, immaculate, unwrinkled, gloves that had touched nothing all day but paper and other people's trust. "You have a good memory, I think, Nurse Marchetti. That's a rare quality. It can be a burden, though. Remembering things the war would rather put down. Men who carry too much—" he tapped his temple, gently, almost kindly, "—sometimes find it affects their fitness for service. Even nurses. Especially nurses, actually. The boards are terribly unsentimental about women."

"Good night, Major," I said.

"Good night." And then, at the threshold, without turning, in the same pleasant voice a man uses to remark on the weather: "Lovely linen store you have. Very deep. One could stand in there for hours and never be found, if one didn't want to be."

The door closed behind him.

I sat at the desk with my pen still in my hand and did not move for a long time. The admission book lay open, its columns neat, its omissions invisible. Down the corridor, the sleeping man moaned again. In the linen store, behind the folded sheets, a seven-year-old boy was sleeping with his back against the wall the way children sleep when they have learned that mornings can take things away.

He wasn't forgotten. That was what I finally let myself understand, sitting in the dark with my heart going like a triage drum. Forgotten was a passive thing, an accident of paper. This was active. This was a man with clean gloves and a small black notebook, asking about a colonel's last week with the patience of a fisherman mending a net. Ashworth had asked questions, and Ashworth was dead and struck from the record, and his son had been delivered to our door with a note ordering us to forget his face—and now the man who had written that note, or the man who paid the man who had written it, was standing in my ward telling me, in the language of courtesy, exactly how far he was willing to go.

He had found the linen store. Whether he had looked in it, I didn't know. Whether he knew about the ledger, I didn't know. But he had told me he could end my career with a sentence, and he had told me he knew where the boy slept, and he had done it smiling, and he would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, until I handed him what he wanted or until he stopped needing to ask.

I put the pen down. I went to the window. Across the dark lawn the orangery glowed under the moon, all that glass, all that paper, the workshop where absence was manufactured by lamplight—and I remembered what Renwick had said on his first morning, cheerfully, to Matron, in my hearing: that the workshop would be decommissioned at the end of the month, and the building cleared.

*Cleared*, he'd said. And Matron had asked, mildly, what cleared meant, and he'd smiled and said, *burned, of course. Standard procedure. Paper is a fire hazard.*

I stood at the window a long time, and then I went down the corridor, past the sleeping men, to the linen store, and I sat on the floor among the folded sheets with Thomas's head heavy against my shoulder, and I did the arithmetic of what we had and what we had left.

The ledger was not enough. A ledger in one nurse's handwriting, hidden under a floorboard, was a diary, a delusion, a woman's hysteria—Renwick would bury it in a sentence. It needed to be more than mine. It needed witnesses, and originals, and names in other hands, and I knew exactly one man who could tell me which originals still existed and where they slept, a grey-faced clerk who had looked at me across a courtyard twice as though he were carrying something he could not set down.

Tomorrow, I would find a reason to speak to Elias Quill.

Behind me, in the dark, the boy murmured in his sleep—a word, maybe a name, maybe his father's—and my arms tightened around him without my permission.

Three weeks, Renwick had said, before the fire. I had a feeling, watching the moon move behind the orangery glass, that we would not be given three.
