## 3. Requisition

Marta Quenn had been awake since four o'clock, which was not unusual, but she had been awake *usefully* since four o'clock, and that was a distinction she intended to defend.

The trouble was the stove. The trouble was always the stove. It had opinions about damp weather the way old men had opinions about football, and it expressed them in smoke. She had fed it three sticks of kindling and one page of a 1911 water-rate dispute that no reasonable person would ever need again — she kept a small basket of such pages by the grate for exactly this purpose, and had privately decided that if history remembered nothing else about her tenure, it would at least be true that the 1911 water-rate dispute had kept an archivist's hands warm through two hard winters — and still the chimney sulked.

So she was standing on the back step of the town hall in her cardigan, waving a copy of the municipal code at the smoke like a woman shooing a chicken off a table, when she heard the lorries.

Not the sound itself. Vellm heard lorries all the time; the quarry road ran above the town and the drivers used their horns generously. It was the *rhythm*. Quarry lorries came in ones and twos, grumbling, spaced out like bad teeth. This was a column. This was many engines keeping time together, and keeping time together is something only armies bother to learn.

Marta stopped fanning the smoke. She stood very still on her own back step, in her own slippers, holding the municipal code of a republic that might already be a past tense, and listened to the river town below her wake up into its last ordinary morning.

The baker's boy was first down the lane, as he always was, his bicycle rattling over the cobbles with more bread than sense. He got as far as the fountain before the first soldiers reached it, and then there was a moment Marta would think about for years afterward: the boy simply stopped pedaling, put one foot down, and watched them come, chewing, as though they were weather. A boy learns early what can and cannot be argued with. He turned his bicycle around with great dignity and pedaled home, and Marta noted — because noting things was the whole of her profession — that not one soldier looked at him twice. That was the first mercy of the morning, and also the first lesson: they were not here for children. They were here for paper.

*They are here for paper,* Marta thought, and felt her stomach drop through the step, through the foundations, through the cold clay under Vellm, all the way down to the river gravel.

She went inside. She did not run. Running tells everyone everything, and besides, she was sixty-one years old and had a hip with strong views on the subject. She walked, at the pace of a woman going to fetch more kindling, and she locked the archive door behind her with the little brass key she wore on a ribbon around her neck, next to her heart, where it clinked against her spectacles case and made her feel like a very small, very well-organized church bell.

* * *

By seven the square was full of grey-green men doing grey-green things with great competence and no curiosity. Marta watched from the town hall's tall window, the one with the crack shaped like a heron, and took inventory, because inventory was how she prayed.

Two sentries at the doors. One officer on the steps consulting a map as though Vellm were a sum he'd been asked to check. A sergeant directing four men up the stairs toward the council chamber, and — her breath went thin — another pair already on the archive landing, arguing quietly with the lock.

The lock won, briefly. Marta allowed herself one small, uncharitable satisfaction: the locksmith who'd installed it in 1929 had been a drunkard and a fraud in every other respect, but he had built a door like a grudge. Then the sergeant produced a crowbar, and Marta understood that the argument was going to be settled by the older technology.

She could have gone down. She had rehearsed this, in the way one rehearses one's own funeral — idly, at night, with a certain grim pleasure. She had a speech prepared. *I am the municipal archivist; these records are held in trust; I am obliged to inform you that removal without proper transfer documentation constitutes—* She had even worked out where the officer would interrupt her, because they always interrupted at the same place, right at the phrase "constitutes," as though the word itself were a barricade.

Instead she stayed at the window, and she will never fully explain to herself why, except that some part of her understood, even then, that the ledger did not need her speech. The ledger needed her elsewhere, later, doing something other than standing nobly in a doorway while a gunnysack filled. A door is just a door. A doorway is where people get arrested. And Marta Quenn had work to do that required her to remain, provisionally, uninteresting.

So she watched her life carried out in a sack.

That is nearly the whole of it, and she knew it was nearly the whole of it even as it happened, which made it worse. Two soldiers, methodical, lifting the ledger — her ledger, eleven years of entries in her own fair hand, dissidents and debtors and foreign-born residents and the merely unlucky, cross-referenced, indexed, corrected in three colors of ink — and dropping it into a canvas gunnysack with the tenderness of men loading turnips. The corner of the binding caught on the shelf edge and tore, and Marta heard herself make a small sound, and pressed her hand flat against the cold glass until the sound went back where it belonged.

Eleven years. She had begun it the winter after the border revisions, when the town clerk — a decent man, a dull man, a man whose idea of danger was a misfiled invoice — had said, *Marta, someone ought to keep a proper list of who lives here and why, before someone else decides what the record is.* And she had said yes the way she said yes to everything, which is to say immediately and with a fountain pen already uncapped. She had interviewed half the town. She had drunk forty cups of terrible coffee with frightened families. She had written down names that people begged her not to write down, and names that people begged her to write down, and she had learned the difference between the two requests, and she had recorded both kinds faithfully, because an archive that flatters its subjects is not an archive, it is a guest book.

And now it was in a gunnysack, riding on a private's shoulder between a coil of wire and somebody's breakfast tin, and the private was whistling.

* * *

At half past eight they sealed the building. Not just the archive — the whole hall, wax seals on the doors, a printed notice pasted to the glass that misspelled *municipal.* Marta read it twice from across the square, where she had stationed herself at the fountain with a marketing basket and the expression of a woman buying nothing in particular, and she found the misspelling so comforting she nearly laughed. Armies could take your ledger, your hall, your republic, apparently your entire form of government, but they could not take the time to check their spelling, and there was a lesson in that too, though she suspected it was not an encouraging one.

"Morning, madam."

It was one of them — young, freckled, rifle slung with the casualness of a boy carrying a school satchel. He nodded at her basket. "Market's shut."

"So I see." She smiled at him, and was surprised by how easily it came, and slightly appalled at herself. "A pity. I had my heart set on cabbage."

"Yes, madam." He looked relieved, the way boys look relieved when an old lady turns out to be an old lady and not a problem. Then, because he was young and not yet entirely cured of conscience, he added, quietly, "You'll want to be indoors by curfew. Eight o'clock. Some of the lads get twitchy after dark."

"Thank you," said Marta, meaning it, hating that she meant it, filing it away: *some of the lads get twitchy after dark* — a fact, an asset, a line in a future index. "You have a mother somewhere worried about you, I expect."

He blinked. "Yes, madam. In Ostrel."

"Then write to her," said Marta, "and tell her you met a foolish old woman who wanted cabbage," and she walked home before he could decide whether he'd been threatened or comforted.

Her house smelled of yesterday's coffee and the lavender she stuffed into drawers against moths, and for the first time in thirty years of living above her work, it smelled like nowhere. The archive was sealed. Her key hung useless against her breastbone. She set the basket down, took off her cardigan, folded it, unfolded it, refolded it, and then caught herself doing it and sat down hard in her chair with her hands in her lap like a scolded child.

Here is what violation feels like, she thought, staring at the ceiling. It does not feel like fire. Fire announces itself. It feels like this: the ordinary room, the ordinary chair, and underneath everything the knowledge that somewhere across the square, in a requisitioned office that smelled of boot polish, a man she had never met was opening her ledger and reading her neighbors' names aloud to another man, and neither of them could pronounce half of them properly.

She had spent eleven years making sure those names were legible. She had never once imagined the audience.

* * *

She gave herself until noon to grieve, and she kept to the schedule strictly, because grief, like everything else in her custody, responded well to firm cataloguing. From nine to ten: anger, during which she scrubbed a floor that did not need it. Ten to eleven: despair, during which she sat by the dead stove and permitted herself exactly one image of the gunnysack, corner torn, whistling private, and then closed the drawer on it. Eleven to twelve: bargaining, during which she inventoried what remained.

What remained. She made a list, because lists were her liturgy.

One: the ledger itself — gone, sealed, sack.

Two: her working notes — gone, same building, same seal, presumably same eventual sack.

Three: her memory — intact, extensive, and, she reflected grimly, about to be tested in ways her schoolmistresses had never promised.

Four—

Four was the thing she had not let herself think about all morning, the thing sitting in the corner of her mind wearing a dust sheet, and at five minutes to twelve she stood up, crossed the room, and pulled the dust sheet off it herself.

The typewriter.

Not the machine — the machine was only a machine, a good sturdy Remington with a temperamental capital T, and they were welcome to it if it came to that. The *case.* The leather case with the false bottom her late husband had built for smuggling brandy across the lake in the year of the last scandal, God rest his sentimental soul, and which she had repurposed within a week of his funeral for the transport of documents that ought not to travel openly. And in the lining — she knelt, and her hip objected, and she told it to wait its turn — in the lining, flat as autumn leaves, lay the carbons.

Every entry. Every name. Eleven years of second impressions, typed in that faint purple ink that carbon paper produces, the ghost-copy of every living soul the old government had thought worth counting and controlling. She had made them without being asked, over six years, one page per evening, the way other women did needlework. Her husband had called it her hobby. She had let him.

She laid the carbons on the kitchen table and counted them, because counting was how she breathed. Forty-one sheets. Forty-one sheets of names that now existed in exactly two places in the world: a gunnysack on a private's shoulder, and a widow's kitchen table.

The army had the original. Fine. Let them. An original is only ever the first draft of the truth, and Marta Quenn had spent her whole life among drafts, and she knew something that the men with the crowbar and the misspelled notice did not yet know:

You cannot requisition a copy you do not know exists. You cannot burn a memory that has already left the building. And you cannot, whatever your notice says, however many lorries you bring, seal a town's record when the record has learned to walk.

She put the carbons back in the lining, weighted the false bottom with the typewriter, and set the case by the door — not hidden, not displayed, simply *present*, the way one keeps a loaded word in a conversation.

Then she put the kettle on, because the stove had finally decided to cooperate, and because whatever came next would go better hot.

She was pouring the second cup she did not intend to drink when she remembered the courier — the young man from the stranded train, the one with the official satchel and the eyes of a man who measured rooms by their exits, the one she'd lent the good lamp to last night while he waited out the bridge repairs. The one who had watched her type for an hour without asking a single question, which in Marta's long experience meant either stupidity or kinship, and he was plainly not stupid.

He would have seen the lorries come in. He would have seen the seals go on. And a courier, she thought, setting down the pot, a courier whose road runs through a town that has just been sealed — a courier is going to want to know what the army thinks it has taken.

And Marta Quenn, keeper of the second draft, found she very much wanted to know what he would do about it.

She took the good lamp off the windowsill, tucked it under her arm, and went to find him.
