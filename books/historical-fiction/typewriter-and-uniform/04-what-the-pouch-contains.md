## 4. What the Pouch Contains

The summons came at half past seven in the morning, delivered by a boy of perhaps fourteen who wore a soldier's cap two sizes too large and carried a rifle he clearly did not know how to hold. He knocked on the door of the Riverine Hotel's third-floor room with the flat, official rhythm of someone who had been told to knock that way, and when Ivo opened it, the boy thrust out a folded paper without meeting his eyes.

"Commandant wants you. Town hall. Nine o'clock." A pause. "Don't be late. He counts."

"Does he," said Ivo.

"He counted me late once." The boy touched the brim of the enormous cap, which slid down over one eyebrow, and departed down the stairs with the clattering urgency of someone who had somewhere else to be and no desire to explain himself further.

Ivo closed the door and stood for a moment in the grey morning light, listening to the river sounds below — the slap of water against pilings, a barge horn, the ordinary noises of a town that had woken up occupied and was pretending, with admirable discipline, that nothing had changed. Vellm was good at pretending. So, as of this morning, apparently, was Ivo.

He laid the summons on the bed beside the diplomatic pouch and considered them both.

The pouch was leather, government-issue, stamped with the seal of the Ministry of External Affairs on one face and the courier's warrant number on the other. It weighed, empty, about as much as a loaf of bread. Full — and it had not left his person since the capital — it weighed about as much as a loaf of bread plus whatever a republic's final word to the world weighed. He had never opened it. That was the whole architecture of his profession: the courier carries, the courier does not know. A man who knows what he carries can be made to talk. A man who carries only a sealed weight is carrying nothing but a shape.

He had believed this for eleven years. He had built a life on it, the way a man builds a house on a foundation he has never personally inspected.

Now an army commandant wanted to "verify" it.

Ivo dressed slowly, choosing the least memorable clothes he had — the brown coat rather than the grey, the collar that had lost its starch sometime around the second week of the collapse. In the hotel's narrow mirror he looked like what he had spent years learning to look like: nobody in particular, a man between places, forgettable by design. Except that today the design had failed. Today there was a paper with his name on it in a soldier's pocket, and a seal on a pouch downstairs in the hotel safe, and a boy who counted.

Downstairs, the landlady — Frau Ostrow, widowed, formidable, with forearms like a stevedore and a manner suggesting she had personally survived several worse invasions than this one — pressed a roll into his hand at the door.

"You're going up to the town hall," she said. It was not a question. Nothing in Vellm stayed secret longer than it took to walk past the bakery.

"I've been asked to report."

"Mm." She looked at him the way she might look at a delivery of slightly bruised fruit — assessing, but not without sympathy. "Eat the roll before you get there. Commandants are less frightening to men who have eaten." She turned back to her counter, then added, over her shoulder: "And if they ask you about the archivist woman, you've never met her. Everyone in this town has decided they've never met her. It's going very well so far."

"I have met her," said Ivo. "She fixed my typewriter ribbon."

Frau Ostrow sighed the sigh of a woman watching a novice handle a live grenade. "Then you met a stranger who was kind to you once. That's all anyone needs to remember." She flapped a dish towel at him, gently, in the universal gesture of women shooing men toward fates they could not prevent. "Go. And chew."

* * *

The town hall of Vellm had been built by people who believed in columns, and the occupation had moved into it the way damp moves into a cellar — thoroughly, without malice, and with every intention of staying. Soldiers stood at the doors. More soldiers stood in the corridor, where the notice board still displayed, absurdly, a poster advertising the autumn harvest festival, its cheerful lettering now flanked by a typed order prohibiting assemblies of more than three persons. Ivo noted that the festival poster had not been taken down. Perhaps the army intended to hold the festival. Perhaps it simply hadn't occurred to anyone that joy required paperwork too.

Captain Elsen was waiting outside the commandant's office. Ivo recognized him from the previous day — the officer who had supervised the sealing of the archive, tall, precise, with the kind of face that suggested he had been handsome once and had then read too many regulations. He had been schooled in Vellm; Marta had mentioned it, in the offhand way she mentioned everything, filing the fact away as though facts were a currency she expected to need later.

"Courier Amberdale." Elsen consulted a clipboard. "You're punctual. The commandant will appreciate that."

"The landlady fed me first. It seemed prudent."

Something flickered at the corner of Elsen's mouth — not quite a smile, but the memory of one, filed away under some regulation or other. "This way."

The commandant's office had been the mayor's office until roughly forty-eight hours ago. The mayor's portrait had been taken down, leaving a paler rectangle on the wallpaper, and in its place hung a map of the river valley with pins in it. Behind the desk sat Colonel Brandt, who was eating breakfast — bread, cheese, a small pickle — with the unhurried thoroughness of a man who believed that meals taken standing up were meals wasted.

"Courier." Brandt did not look up. "Sit. Have a pickle."

"I'm fine, thank you."

"Suit yourself." Brandt finished chewing, wiped his hands on a napkin with genuine care, folded the napkin into quarters, and only then raised his eyes. They were pale, unhurried eyes, the eyes of a man who counted things. "You carry a diplomatic pouch. Sealed. Warranted through to Greyharbor consulate. Yes?"

"Yes, sir."

"And you haven't opened it."

"No, sir."

"Good man. Honest errand." Brandt leaned back, and the chair creaked with the particular complaint of furniture that had served three governments and resented all of them. "Here is my difficulty, courier. The situation has changed. The government that issued your warrant may or may not exist by Thursday. The consulate you're bound for is neutral, which means it will take anything you hand it and file it under 'interesting.' And I am sitting in a town full of paper that my superiors want accounted for." He gestured vaguely at the walls, at the town beyond them, at the entire collapsing century. "So. Before that pouch goes anywhere, it gets verified. Contents checked against manifest. Standard procedure in wartime."

Ivo felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts when a ship changes course so gradually that only the stomach notices.

"With respect, sir," he said, "the pouch is sealed under ministry protocol. Verification would require breaking the seal, which voids the warranty of—"

"Warranty." Brandt smiled. It was a friendly smile, which made it worse. "That's a good word. Warranty. You sound like my wife arguing about a stove." He picked up the manifest copy from his desk — Ivo's own manifest, the one he'd surrendered at the checkpoint yesterday, and seeing it lying there in another man's office gave him a sensation like finding his diary in a stranger's hands. "Manifest says: documents, diplomatic, sealed, quantity one bundle, weight approximately two kilograms. Now. Here's what interests me, courier. Two kilograms is a lot of treaty."

The room was quiet. Somewhere below, a soldier laughed at something. The harvest festival poster stared in through Ivo's memory, relentlessly cheerful.

"Two kilograms," Brandt repeated, "is a lot of treaty. Treaties are thin things. I've signed several. Four pages, five, with the fancy copies maybe ten. Nobody sends two kilograms of treaty downriver in the last week of a war, because nobody downstream is going to sign anything ever again." He laced his fingers together on the desktop. "So either your ministry is sentimental, courier, or that pouch contains something else. And I would very much like to know which."

There it was. Not a request. An arithmetic problem, laid out on a desk between a pickle plate and a stolen manifest. The army wanted the contents. The word "verified" was a curtain, and behind it was simply theft wearing a uniform — or worse, something more patient than theft. Ivo understood, with the clarity that arrives precisely when it is least welcome, that the pouch was never going to reach Greyharbor in the custody of the republic's army. Whatever happened in this room, the question was only whether it happened politely.

And here was the other thing, the thing that arrived underneath the first thing, quiet as sediment settling: he did not want them to have it. Whatever it was. He didn't know what it was, and he did not want them to have it. Eleven years of honest errands, and the honest errand had ended here, in a requisitioned office, being offered a pickle by a man who intended to open his satchel.

So he lied.

It was astonishingly easy. That was the vertigo of it — not the fear, which was real enough, humming along under his ribs like a second pulse, but the smoothness. The words came out already ironed.

"It's correspondence, sir," Ivo said. "Personal files. The ministry cleared out its confidential registry before the evacuation — personnel records, cipher keys, that sort of housekeeping. There was no time to burn it properly and no time to distribute it, so it all went in one pouch with orders to deliver it to the consulate for destruction. Neutral ground, neutral incinerator." He shrugged, a small shrug, the shrug of a man mildly embarrassed by bureaucratic nonsense. "Nobody downstream is going to sign anything, as you say. But cipher keys are cipher keys. The ministry would rather they burned in a neutral furnace than sit in a captured registry."

Brandt looked at him for a long moment. Elsen, by the door, had gone very still, in the way of a man doing rapid mental arithmetic of his own.

"Cipher keys," said Brandt.

"And personnel files. Half of it's probably obsolete already." Ivo let a note of weary professionalism creep in, the tone of a courier who has carried many stupid packages for many panicking ministries. "If you want to verify the weight against the manifest, I have no objection. Breaking the seal is above my authority, but if you order it, I'll witness it and note it in my log, and the ministry can argue with you about it later, assuming there's a ministry later." He paused, and then — because the best lies are mostly true — added: "Frankly, sir, I'd just as soon hand the whole thing to your incinerator and travel light. It's been riding on my shoulder since the capital and it smells like the registry basement."

Another silence. Brandt glanced at Elsen. Elsen glanced at the clipboard. Outside, a barge horn sounded on the river, long and low, like the town clearing its throat.

"The bridge at Ostrel is mined," Brandt said at last, conversationally, as though changing the subject entirely. "The mill lock at Karn has a garrison. The pontoon at Greyharbor is ours as of Tuesday. Three checkpoints between here and your neutral furnace, courier, and at each one, soldiers with orders to inspect papers." He stood, buttoned his tunic, and came around the desk. Up close he smelled of cheese and gun oil. "Your warrant covers one courier and one pouch. It does not cover detours, delays, or complications. Do you understand me?"

"I believe so, sir."

"Good." Brandt picked up the last of his bread, considered it, and set it down again. "The pouch stays sealed. For now. Consider it... traveling under observation. If it reaches Greyharbor intact, fine — the ministry's ashes go to the neutral furnace, everyone is happy, the war ends, we all learn French. But if I hear that pouch has been opened anywhere on this river — if so much as a thread of that seal looks at me sideways—" He smiled again, the friendly one. "Well. Then it wasn't cipher keys after all, was it? And then we'll have a much longer conversation. Dismissed."

* * *

Ivo walked back down through the town hall, past the soldiers, past the harvest festival poster, out into the morning, and discovered that his legs were performing their duties adequately while the rest of him remained upstairs in that office, still lying, still lying smoothly, fluently, with details and shrugs and even a joke about the smell of basements.

He had lied to a colonel. To a colonel of an occupying army, on behalf of a government that might already be dead, about the contents of a bag he himself had never opened. He stopped at the corner by the bakery — where a boy was handing loaves across a counter with the brisk economy of someone whose father's name might or might not be on a list in a gunnysack — and bought nothing, and stood there feeling the ground rearrange itself beneath him.

Because here was the thing he could not stop noticing: it had felt good. Not the fear — the fear was a cold coin in his pocket and would stay there. But the lying itself, the pivot, the moment the words came out bent instead of straight — some part of him that had spent eleven years being the republic's hands had felt those hands close around something of its own for the first time. He had switched sides in a colonel's office between one sentence and the next, and no one had declared it, least of all him. There had been no ceremony. No oath. Just a man describing cipher keys that did not exist, in a voice that did not shake, while a pickle sweated on a plate.

A courier carries; a courier does not know. But a courier who lies has chosen, and a courier who has chosen is carrying something heavier than any pouch.

He walked back toward the Riverine Hotel, and it was only halfway there, passing the shuttered windows of the town hall annex, that the thought arrived — the thought that had been waiting for him all morning, patient as Marta Quenn's filing system:

She had known. The archivist, typing her copies through the nights, copying every record in town *before someone else decides what the record is.* She had watched soldiers carry her life's work out in a gunnysack, and she had not wept, and she had not argued, and Frau Ostrow's entire street had conspired to have never met her. People who had already lost everything once did not waste time on grief. They made copies.

Ivo stopped walking.

A ledger of names, confiscated at dawn. An archivist who typed everything twice. A town full of strangers practicing the art of having never met her.

He turned, slowly, and looked back up the hill toward the municipal archive, its windows dark, its door sealed with army tape — and began, without quite deciding to, to climb.
