## 14. The Broadcast

The inn at Dravny had a name once, painted in cheerful blue letters above the door, but the army had requisitioned the *n*, and so the sign now read THE SALAMANDER IN, which Marta Quenn found, on the whole, an improvement. A salamander, she told Ivo, was a creature that lived comfortably in fire. An inn that admitted to being merely *in* something — in trouble, in the way, in the path of history — was at least honest.

"You make jokes," Ivo said, setting down the typewriter case with the care of a man handling a sleeping infant that might wake up armed. "We are carrying the only copy of four hundred names through a war zone, and you make jokes."

"Jokes are load-bearing, dear. Take them out and the whole structure sags." Marta patted the case. "Besides, the names are not *in* the case anymore, strictly speaking. They are in the lining, in the ribbon spool, in the spare carbons sewn into my petticoat, and in a hollowed-out bar of soap I am rather proud of. The case is just the furniture we arranged around them."

The smuggler — a broad, silent man called Ferenc who smelled of tar and charged in secrets rather than coin, and who had therefore been paid in Ivo's courier warrant, a transaction Marta still considered the strangest she had ever witnessed — had put them ashore a mile short of the pontoon at Greyharbor. "Walk the rest," he'd said, in the tone of a man who had said his last word on the matter and several of everyone else's. "The water past the mill is watched, and I am not watched, and I intend to keep the arrangement."

So they walked, and now they sat in the Salamander In, at a table by the window, with a plate of gray bread and a pot of tea that tasted faintly of the pot, and Marta was content. Content was perhaps too strong. Marta was *functional*. She had slept three hours on the barge, curled against the typewriter case like a hen against an egg, and had dreamed, absurdly, of the municipal filing system at Vellm, arranged in perfect order, and had woken homesick for a cabinet of drawers.

Ivo was not content. Ivo was doing the thing Ivo did, which was to sit very straight and look at the middle distance as though the middle distance had submitted paperwork requiring review.

"Eat," Marta said, pushing the bread across. "You cannot out-argue a checkpoint on an empty stomach. Believe me, I have tried. In '09 I talked my way past a customs officer over the question of a consignment of school atlases, and I could not have done it if I hadn't had breakfast. He kept wanting to confiscate them because the maps showed the old borders. I said, *Sir, if you confiscate every atlas with an outdated border, you will need a second warehouse by spring.* He laughed. He gave me tea. Borders change, but breakfast is constant."

"That is not a moral, that's a grocery list."

"Everything is a grocery list if you live long enough." She tore the bread and gave him the larger half, because he was thinner than he pretended to be. "Now. Tell me the plan again. Slowly. As though I were a filing clerk with a head cold."

Ivo told it. The pontoon at Greyharbor was a bridge of boats across the river mouth, the last crossing before the neutral consulate's quarter. There would be a checkpoint. There were always checkpoints now, but this one had been, until recently, a formality: show papers, state a purpose, cross. Ivo's courier warrant — no, Marta corrected herself, Ivo had *no* warrant, Ivo had traded the warrant to a man who smelled of tar — Ivo's former warrant would have covered one traveler. The pouch's diplomatic seal would have covered itself. Marta would have been the typewriter case's chaperone.

"And now?" Marta asked.

"Now we are two travelers with no papers and a typewriter," Ivo said. "Which is a much better story than it sounds. People carry typewriters. Clerks go where clerks are sent. A woman of your age with a typewriter case is the most invisible object on any road in this country. Soldiers look through you. They look *for* me, a little — a courier's face gets remembered — but they look *through* you."

"Flattery," said Marta, "from a person who once described my filing system as 'aggressively alphabetical.'"

"It was a compliment. Most filing systems are cowards about it."

She was still smiling when the radio in the corner cleared its throat.

The inn's radio was a large cabinet model with a lace doily on top, because even the war had not managed to separate the innkeeper from her doilies. It had been muttering weather all evening — fog on the lower river, fog on the coast, fog everywhere, the sky apparently as uncertain as everything beneath it — and now, at the top of the hour, it stopped muttering and began to speak in the voice the whole country had learned to dread: the announcer from the capital, the one with the beautiful diction and the pause before bad news, as though bad news were a guest who required announcing.

Marta knew the pause. She had heard it in the spring, when the first ministries fell. She had heard it in the summer, when the army stopped pretending to be the republic's army and simply became *the* army. She heard it now.

She did not hear the words at first. That was the strange thing. The voice said them plainly enough — *the capital has fallen, resistance has ended, the government of the republic has ceased to exist* — but her mind, trained on forty years of records, snagged on the phrasing. *Ceased to exist.* Not *fallen*, not *surrendered*, not *fled*. *Ceased to exist.* As though a country were a subscription. As though forty years of marriage registrations and land deeds and school prizes and the municipal water rates of Vellm had been a filing error, now corrected.

Around the inn, the reaction was quiet, which was the loudest thing Marta had ever heard. A card game stopped mid-trick. The innkeeper turned the doily's radio down, then up again, then down, as though volume were a dial for belief. A man in a bargeman's cap said, "Well," to nobody. A woman began to cry without noise, the way people cry when they have practiced.

Ivo had gone the color of the tea.

"The commission," Ivo said. It came out flat, the way a floor sounds when something heavy lands on it. "The pouch. The diplomatic seal. All of it — the moment that voice finished talking, it stopped meaning anything. There is no republic to be diplomatic *for*. There is no government whose seal obligates anyone. The soldiers at Greyharbor—" Ivo stopped, and Marta watched the arithmetic finish itself behind her companion's eyes, the cold arithmetic Ivo was so good at and so tired of. "The soldiers at Greyharbor are not obliged to honor any papers. Because there are no papers anymore. There is no *anymore*."

"Drink your tea," Marta said.

"Marta—"

"Drink it. It's terrible, but it's ours." She poured, and her hand was steadier than she had any right to expect. "Now. Let us do what we do. Let us read the record."

"The record?"

"The broadcast. Word for word, as far as we heard it. That is what I do, dear. Someone says a thing, and I write it down, and then it is a fact that can be checked instead of a wind that blows through you." She took a pencil from her hair — she kept one there the way other women kept pins — and pulled the paper napkin toward her. "*At the top of the hour, the announcer stated:*" she wrote, "*the capital has fallen. Resistance has ended. The government of the republic has ceased to exist.*" She underlined the last phrase twice. "There. Now it's a document. Now it can't pretend it didn't happen."

"You are writing the death of your country on a napkin."

"I am writing the death of a *government* on a napkin. Countries are made of records, Ivo. Rivers, borders, armies — those come and go. I have outlasted two governments already and I barely outlasted the influenza. The country is the part where people know each other's names." She tapped the case at her feet, gently, the way you'd tap a piano. "I am carrying a country in this case. It weighs eleven kilos."

Ivo almost laughed, and then didn't, and the not-laughing was worse.

"Say the rest of it," Marta said. "Say the thing you're not saying."

Ivo looked at the window, where the fog was coming off the river in gray handfuls. "The pouch," Ivo said. "The official pouch. It still has the seal. In a soldier's hands, that seal meant: *do not open, do not delay, this belongs to a government you must not antagonize.* An hour ago it was armor. Now it's—" Ivo's jaw worked. "Now it's a locked box with no authority behind it. Which means it's not armor anymore. It's a *reason*. It's the most interesting object at any checkpoint. It's a box that says *open me, I'm from a government that no longer exists to complain about it.*"

"Then we don't take it to the checkpoint."

"It has to cross. It's the mission."

"The mission, Ivo." Marta set down the pencil. She chose the next words with the care she chose all words, but these she chose the way she chose words for an epitaph. "The mission was issued by a government that has ceased to exist. I heard it say so myself, on the radio, on a doily. You are not a courier anymore. You are a person carrying a box."

For a moment Ivo said nothing, and Marta understood — not fully, not yet, but the shape of it — that this was the sentence Ivo had been walking toward for the whole length of the river. A courier who was not a courier. A pair of hands with nothing assigned to them. Marta knew that vertigo from the inside: she had felt it the day the army carried her ledger out of Vellm in a gunnysack. You spend your life being the instrument of a thing, and then the thing is gone, and you are left holding your own hands, wondering what they are for.

"You are for the names," Marta said, because it was true and because true things are the only ones worth saying to a person mid-freefall. "That was always the mission. The republic was just the delivery service."

Ivo looked at her then, really looked, and something in the straight back eased by one degree. One degree. Marta would take it.

* * *

They took a room because walking at night toward a checkpoint was suicide and sitting in the common room was theater — everyone in the inn was performing calm, and Marta did not trust her own performance past midnight. The room had one bed, one chair, one candle, and a window that looked at the river, or would have if the fog had permitted.

Marta sat on the bed with the typewriter case open across her knees and did not take the carbons out. There was no need. She knew what was in the lining the way she knew the alphabet: four hundred and eleven names, in the order the old government had entered them, each with the reason given — *dissident, debtor, foreign-born, agitator, relative-of, associate-of, none given* — and she had retyped every one of them in a single night by stove-light while patrols passed the shutters, and she could recite the first twenty in order and the last ten, and the ones in the middle she could find the way a pianist finds middle C: without looking.

Ivo stood at the window, not seeing the fog.

"Greyharbor will have heard the broadcast," Ivo said. "By morning, certainly. The garrison there — do you know what they'll be? Yesterday they were garrison troops of a republic, bored, underpaid, stamping papers. Tomorrow they're the army of the thing that won. Every stamp they made last week is retroactively a favor to the enemy. Every traveler they let through last week is a question they'll want to answer with a bayonet. Fear does that. Fear makes yesterday's clerks into tonight's inquisitors."

"And yet."

"And yet?"

"And yet soldiers are still people, and people can be read, and I have spent forty years reading people the way they never learned to read records." Marta closed the case. "The sergeant at Ostrel hesitated when he saw your seal. You told me. He *recognized* it, and he hesitated, and a man who hesitates at a courier's seal is a man who was taught to respect paper. Respect for paper doesn't die with a government, Ivo. It's deeper than governments. It's the one thing they put in soldiers before they put the uniform on, if they put anything decent in at all."

"You want to gamble four hundred lives on a sergeant's school days."

"I want to gamble on the fact that somebody, somewhere in that man's education, made him copy out a document by hand and *feel* it." She said it lightly, but she meant it with her whole chest. "It happens. It happened to a boy I taught, once, in this very river country. He used to bring me the attendance registers from the garrison school because the teacher was ill, and one day he read the old admission rolls — names of children, sixty years dead — and he stood there in my archive with his cap in his hands and said, *Madam, these were people.* That boy joined the army. That boy is possibly a captain by now. And if he is standing at a pontoon tomorrow, some part of him still knows that names were people."

Ivo turned from the window. "You think the soldier at Greyharbor might be your boy."

"I think the country is smaller than it pretends to be. I think rivers repeat themselves. I think we will find out tomorrow, and either way the names cross, because the names are not in the case, dear, the names are in the lining and the petticoat and the soap, and I have decided that the soap is non-negotiable."

That got the almost-laugh out of Ivo again, and this time she let it survive.

* * *

The candle was down to a thumb's width when Ivo spoke from the chair, where Ivo had been sitting with the decoy pouch on both knees like a cat that had outstayed its welcome.

"I keep thinking I should open it."

"The pouch."

"The seal's dead. There's no crime in opening it anymore — no crime *against*. Whatever's in it belongs to a government that has ceased to exist, which means it belongs to whoever's holding it, which is me." Ivo's thumb moved along the wax seal, the republic's arms pressed into it, the eagle or the wheat sheaf or whatever it was — Marta had never been able to see anything in it but a smudge with ambitions. "It's the last thing I have that says who I am. And it's a lie. It's been a lie since the capital."

Marta sat up against the headboard. "Open it in the morning, if you open it. Decisions made by candlelight after a broadcast are like letters written after midnight — accurate only about your own heart, and dangerous to act on." She blew on her tea, which had long since gone cold, and drank it anyway, because breakfast is constant and tea is its loyal deputy. "But I'll tell you what I think is in it, and you may quote me in your memoirs, if either of us lives to be quoted. Governments in their last days do not send couriers to deliver treaties. Treaties require two governments, and theirs was in a hurry to stop being one. Governments in their last days send couriers to be *looked at*. A pouch on a road is a lantern. Everyone watches the lantern."

"You're saying I was the diversion."

"I'm saying you were the *lantern*, and lanterns are not blamed for the light. Someone else carried the real thing, Ivo, and carried it some other way, and we will perhaps never know who, and that is fine. The record doesn't need to be complete tonight. It needs to be *ours*."

The candle guttered. Out on the river, through the fog, came the sound Marta had been listening for all evening without admitting it: the slow, patient knocking of water against the hulls of the pontoon boats, three miles down. Tomorrow they would walk into that sound, past soldiers who no longer owed anyone courtesy, carrying a country in a typewriter case and a lie in a leather pouch.

"Sleep," Marta said, and lay down with one hand resting on the case, as she had on the barge, as she had every night since the gunnysack.

She was nearly asleep when Ivo said, very quietly, from the dark: "Marta. If they stop us. If they open the case."

"Then I will do what archivists do," Marta said, into her pillow, with her eyes closed. "I will begin reading aloud, and I will not stop until someone makes me, and by the time someone makes me, every name in that room will have heard its own name said back to it. You cannot un-ring four hundred and eleven bells, dear. Not even with a bayonet."

The candle went out on its own. In the dark, the river knocked patiently at the pontoon boats, and somewhere down that gray mile, a garrison that had ceased, an hour ago, to owe anybody anything, was lighting its lamps for morning.

Marta slept, and dreamed of drawers, all of them open.
