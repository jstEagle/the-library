## 11. Three Days

### Marta

Marta had forged documents before, though she had never called it that. She had aged paper for restoration work — tea baths, candle smoke, a hot iron pressed over damp cloth to give a sheet the tired look of forty years. She had matched inks for repairs to Wren's damaged plates. She had copied hands so faithfully that Aldous himself had once held her imitation of his own signature up to the light and said, half laughing, *Marta, if you ever turn to crime, at least have the decency to rob someone who deserves it.*

She was seventy-one years old, and it appeared the answer was yes.

The shipping manifest lay before her on the colorist's bench, weighted at the corners with four grinding stones. A genuine manifest of the *Meridian*, lifted from the harbormaster's office by Sefa's clever fingers, and now Marta's task was to make one more line exist on it than had ever been written there.

*One roll of carpets, bonded silk, consigned to Marchetti & Co., agents, Port Sable.*

The trouble was not the words. The trouble was the clerk's hand — a cramped, joyless script that leaned left like a man walking into wind. Marta had spent two evenings copying it from the genuine entries, filling a scrap of waste paper with the letter *r* until her wrist ached. The *r* was the whole battle. This clerk made his *r* like a small angry flag. Most people made it like a little roof.

She dipped the pen. Her hand shook — not with fear, or not only fear, but with the tremor that had been creeping into her fingers these past two years, the one that made fine vermilion washes a gamble. She steadied her wrist against the bench edge the way Wren had taught her fifty years ago, when she was an apprentice herself and he was already old, already certain, already Aldous.

*You don't fight the shake,* he'd said. *You build around it. A wall, a brace, a habit. The line doesn't care how you feel. The line only cares where it lands.*

The line landed true. *Carpets.* One word, in a dead clerk's living hand.

Marta sat back and looked at what she'd done and felt nothing so simple as guilt. What she felt was older and stranger — the memory of being twenty-three in this same room, watching Aldous Wren seal a letter addressed across the border and ask her, casually, terribly, whether she'd carry it to the post. She had carried it. She had carried dozens of them, for years, and told herself it was friendship, and it was, and it was also cowardice, because she had never once asked him plainly what the letters were for, because asking would have made her responsible, and carrying them unasked let her feel only useful.

Well. She was responsible now. There was no version of the next three days in which she was merely useful.

Footsteps on the stair — Casper, by the weight of them, still moving like someone apologizing for taking up space even now, even with everything at stake. Marta slid the manifest under a stack of blank folios and turned to the window as if studying the light.

"We should talk about the seals," Casper said from the doorway. They were holding two sticks of sealing wax, red and black, and their face had the particular grayness of someone who has not slept and does not intend to.

"Sit down before you fall down," Marta said.

"There's no time to—"

"There is exactly enough time, and no more, which is why you'll sit." She pulled out the second stool, the low one, the apprentice's seat, and something crossed Casper's face at the sight of it — grief and comfort tangled together — before they sat.

"The seals," Marta said. "Show me."

Casper laid the wax sticks on the bench between them like evidence. "The Admiralty's warrant covers papers. If the manifest is inspected at the quay, it needs a customs stamp. Sefa says the night inspector barely looks, but *barely* isn't *never*. And if they open the portrait backing—" They stopped. Couldn't finish it. The portrait stood against the far wall where it had stood for three nights, a fat merchant and his plain wife painted by a journeyman Casper had paid too much, and behind the canvas, sewn into the lining, the finished plates of the atlas, flat as secrets.

"Then we're all transported," Marta said mildly, "and I'm too old for the colonies, so I'd die on the ship out of spite and ruin everyone's voyage." She took the red wax. "Give me the stamp impression."

"You can't copy a customs stamp."

"I copied the Admiralty's great seal in 1761 for a commemorative plate, and the Lord Admiral himself praised the likeness. He was standing next to me at the time. I smiled and nodded and thought, *you fool, you've just complimented your own forgery.*" She held out her palm. "The impression, child. And then go sleep for two hours, because a shaking hand inks a shaking line, and tomorrow night you draw the last plate, and it has to be perfect."

"It won't be perfect," Casper said quietly. "It can't be. That's rather the point."

Marta looked at them — this strange, stubborn, half-grown person Wren had collected off the docks six years ago the way he collected everything broken and interesting — and understood, finally, what the boy had decided. Not the plan itself; she'd known the shape of that since the summons came. But the cost. Casper had already paid it in their head. Whatever happened at the Admiralty in three days, whatever proof they handed over, Casper had chosen which lie to tell and which truth to save, and there was a stillness in them now that hadn't been there a week ago.

Aldous would have recognized it. It was the stillness he'd had, the last year, packing his trunk for the coast that killed him.

"Two hours," Marta repeated. "Then you come back and we melt wax together, and I'll teach you the one thing about forgery that matters."

"Which is?"

"Commit." She turned the red stick in her fingers. "A hesitant forgery convicts you. A confident one just becomes a document. Same as maps. Same as lives. Now go."

Casper went. Marta waited until the stair stopped creaking, then drew the manifest back out, and by the light of one lamp, in a dead clerk's angry left-leaning hand, she committed.

### Sefa

The *Meridian* smelled of pepper and bilgewater, and her first mate smelled of neither, which meant he bathed, which meant money, which meant the captain could afford to be picky, which was bad news.

Sefa stood on the quarterdeck with her cap pulled low and her story rehearsed down to its bones. She was nineteen, she'd worked the Vellamare quays since she was twelve, and she knew every officer of every vessel in the harbor the way other girls knew suitors — by reputation, by gait, by what they'd do when squeezed.

Captain Orla Voss did not look like a smuggler. That was the first thing. She looked like a schoolmistress gone to seed: gray braid, spectacles, hands folded on the rail. Sefa had heard the stories anyway — silk in the ballast, brandy in the water casks, a hold that had never once been searched honestly in eleven years. And she'd heard the other story, the one Marta had told her quietly over mending: *Wren bought passage from her once, years ago, for a crate he wouldn't name. She never asked. He never forgot. If you mention my name, she'll listen. Whether she says yes is another matter.*

"You're the girl who wants to ship carpets," Captain Voss said. It wasn't a question. "At midnight. On a tide I haven't announced. Consigned to an agent in Port Sable who, I'm told, is a widow with no warehouse."

"That's right."

"And the carpets are sewn into a portrait."

Sefa kept her face still. Below them, a stevedore dropped a crate and swore in three languages. "I couldn't say. I'm told the owner will deliver it themselves, wrapped and sealed. I'm only arranging the berth."

"I see." Voss adjusted her spectacles, which had the effect of making her eyes disappear entirely. "And who is the owner?"

"A client of the workshop."

"Which workshop?"

"You know which one." Sefa heard her own voice come out flatter than intended, and made herself add, politely, "Ma'am."

Voss was quiet long enough that the harbor filled the silence — halyards knocking, gulls arguing over fish guts, the far-off bell of the customs launch. Then she said, "Ravensworth. The apprentice. Wren's last one."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Wren is dead."

"Three months, ma'am."

Voss turned from the rail and looked at her properly, and Sefa understood she was being measured — not for honesty, exactly, but for weight. Could she carry what she was carrying. "I owed that man a favor," Voss said. "He drew me a chart once that kept my hull off a reef that isn't on any chart, because the reef didn't want to be on any chart. Eleven crew alive because of an old man's ink. So the favor exists, girl, and it's real, and here's my difficulty." She held up one finger. "If I take your carpet, and it's found, I hang. That's the price of my trade now, since the war fever started — everything's treason if the Admiralty squints." A second finger. "If I refuse, I insult a dead friend, and I have to keep sailing this harbor looking at his empty window every morning. Also a price." A third finger. "And if I take it and ask nothing, I'm a fool, and fools sink ships."

"What do you want, ma'am?" Sefa asked. "Straight out. I'll tell you straight if I can give it."

That earned her something — not quite a smile, but a loosening around the eyes. "Straight, then. Three things. First: the fee doubles, cash, before the tide. Second: nobody boards my ship that night but the owner and one porter. No entourage, no goodbyes on the quay, nothing a watchman could describe later." She paused on the third finger and didn't raise it yet.

"And third?"

"Third." Voss looked out at the water, at the gray line of the horizon where the enemy coast lived, the coast that had taken Aldous Wren's ship and life both. "Third, whoever owns this carpet tells me themselves, tonight, before I agree. Not through you. Not through the old woman. I want to look them in the eye and hear them say what they're asking me to risk. If they can do that without flinching, I'll take the roll to Port Sable and put it in Ilse Marchetti's own hands, and I'll consider the debt paid in full." She lowered her hand. "Can they do that?"

Sefa thought of Casper — Casper who lied badly to census takers, whose face was a public notice board, who had once gone white as gesso when Mr. Quill asked a direct question. Casper, telling a stranger captain *I am smuggling the coastline of a nation at war, sewn into a painting, please*.

"They can," Sefa said. And discovered, saying it, that she believed it. Something had changed in Casper Ravensworth this week. The flinch was gone. In its place was something quieter and much harder to argue with.

"Tonight, then. After dark, the tavern on Rope Walk, the one with the blue door. Tell them to come alone and bring the deposit." Voss picked up her ledger, conversation over. At the companionway she paused. "Girl. Your name?"

"Sefa."

"If anyone asks, you were never aboard today."

"No, ma'am," Sefa said. "Nobody was ever aboard."

### Casper

The workshop at midnight was a different country. Casper had learned its daytime geography long ago — where the light fell at each hour, which floorboard announced visitors, how the smell of the ink shifted with the weather. But at night, with the shutters latched and one candle burning inside a tin shield, the room contracted to the size of the plate on the bench, and everything outside it — the sealed map room, the Admiralty, the whole machinery of the state grinding slowly toward dawn in three days — became weather happening somewhere else.

The final plate. Plate forty-one. The northern harbor.

Casper had drawn it eleven times in the past two days. Twelve, counting the one on the bench now, half-inked, the coastlines true, the soundings faithful to Wren's field notes, every mark exactly where the dead man's measurements said it belonged. That was the torment of it. To finish the atlas honestly was one night's work. To finish it *twice* — once true, once false — was to hold the same coastline in both hands and decide, stroke by stroke, which version got to live.

Because that was the bargain Casper had bought with three days. *One final plate remains,* they'd told the Admiralty clerk, voice steady by some miracle of borrowed calm. *Master Wren's notes for it are incomplete. Give me three days and the atlas is yours entire.* And the clerk had written it down, and the clock had started, and now the navy expected a finished northern harbor in seventy-two hours, and Casper intended to give them one.

Just not this one.

This one was the true plate — the gift, the inheritance, the thing that would sail sewn behind a merchant's smug painted face. Casper inked it now with the care of a priest handling relics: the breakwater's curve, the deep channel threading past the sandbar that no naval chart recorded, the little anchorages tucked under the headland where a ship could hide from anything smaller than God. Wren's notes were precise to the fathom. Somewhere across that water, Ilse Marchetti had walked these shores with a chain and a theodolite while her nation and Wren's sharpened knives against each other, and the two of them had sent the measurements home in letters disguised as gossip, and the atlas was what their friendship looked like when it dried.

*Yours,* Wren had written, in the letter Casper had read so many times the creases were going soft. *Whatever crowns claim it, whatever navies want it, the atlas goes to Ilse. Tell my heir. Make them understand.*

Casper understood.

The candle guttered; they trimmed the wick without looking up. Their hand was steady. Marta had been right about the sleep — two hours had turned into four, and the four had done more than a week of fretting ever had. There was a strange peace in the work now, in the narrowed world of line and wash. Fear was still there, camped at the edge of the light like wolves around a fire. But the line didn't care how you felt. The line only cared where it landed.

The door opened below — no knock, just the familiar scrape of the latch, and then footsteps Casper knew by heart climbing the stairs. Marta first, then a lighter tread behind her, quick and careful, learning the floorboards already. Sefa.

They came in carrying bread, cheese, and a bottle of the cheap red wine Marta pretended not to like, and without a word being spoken the workshop rearranged itself around them: Marta at the second bench with her manifest and her wax and her furious little flags of *r*, Sefa cross-legged on the floor with the harbor charts spread around her, memorizing the approaches to the *Meridian*'s berth, marking which watchmen walked which beats at which hours, drawing the whole quay in her head the way she drew everything, in routes and timings and escape lines.

Three people, three benches' worth of distance apart, building one thing.

It was Sefa who broke the silence, around two in the morning, not looking up from her charts. "The captain wants to meet you. Tonight, after dark, blue door on Rope Walk. Alone." A beat. "She wants you to say it yourself. What you're asking her to risk."

Casper's pen paused over the breakwater.

"She'll say yes," Sefa added quickly. "I think she'll say yes. But you have to walk in there and say the words out loud to a stranger, and—" she glanced up, and her expression was the closest thing to gentleness her sharp face allowed, "—I told her you could do it. So don't make a liar of me."

Casper looked down at the plate. The true harbor, nearly finished, beautiful, damning. Tomorrow night: the false one, the flawed proof for the Admiralty, a cable's length of deliberate error hidden in Wren's own technique where only someone who knew his hand would ever find it. Two harbors. Two truths. One of them would sail east behind a painting, and one would sit in a government vault wearing a lie, and Casper would be the only person alive who knew which was which.

"I can do it," Casper said. Their voice came out level, and they were almost surprised.

"Mm." Marta didn't look up from her forging. "Of course you can. You're Wren's." She sanded the manifest, blew the excess away, and read the finished line one last time — *one roll of carpets, bonded silk* — in a dead clerk's hand, perfect, committed. Then she set down the pen, flexed her aching fingers, and said the thing all three of them had been not-saying for two days:

"Now we only have to survive the handing-over."

Outside, the tide was turning. Casper could hear it, faint under the floorboards, the harbor breathing in its sleep — and somewhere across that sleeping water, the coast that had killed their master waited for its atlas, three days and one dangerous tavern meeting away.

Casper dipped the pen, and the line landed true.
