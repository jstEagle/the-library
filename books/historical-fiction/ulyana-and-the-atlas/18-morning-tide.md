## 18. Morning Tide

The tide turned at half past four, and I was on the quay to watch it.

I had not meant to be there so early. I had told myself I would come at the last moment — that standing on the stones for hours would only give the night more time to find me, to press its weight down through my boots and into my bones. But sleep had been a country I could not reach, and by three o'clock I had given up pretending, and by four I was walking the long way through the sleeping city with the sailcloth bundle's absence strange against my back. Strange how quickly a weight becomes part of you. Three nights carrying it up and down stairs, into cupboards, under floorboards, across rooftops — and now my shoulders kept reaching for it, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

The harbor at that hour was all grey water and greyer stone, lamps still burning in their brackets because no one had come to snuff them, gulls arguing over something invisible in the shallows. The *Meridian* rode at her berth with her cargo hatches closed and her crew moving along the spars like slow black letters being written onto the sky. Somewhere aboard her, wrapped in oilcloth inside a crate marked as dye-stuffs, was everything Master Wren had spent his last years making. And somewhere in the captain's strongbox, sealed with Quill's forged stamps, was the letter.

*To Ilse Marchetti, cartographer, from Aldous Wren.* Eleven words in a dead man's hand that had crossed a border more dangerous than any coastline he ever drew.

I stood where Sefa had taught me to stand — out of the way of the winch-teams, near the bollard with the iron ring worn smooth as an old coin — and I watched the tide come in.

It came the way it always came, indifferent and exact, lifting the hulls, straightening the mooring lines, sliding green and cold between the pilings. The same tide that would carry the atlas out. The same tide, three years ago, that had carried Wren out toward the coast that killed him. I had never thought much about tides before this winter. Now they seemed to run through everything — through the census rolls and the Admiralty ledgers, through Marta's shaking hands and Sefa's silence in the cell, through Quill standing at the quay's edge with one end of the atlas in his arms, saying nothing, giving me the letter like a man laying down a card he had held too long.

*Go,* he had said. That was all. *Go, before I am seen remembering what I owe him.*

And then he had walked back up toward the customs house, and I had not looked after him, because looking would have made it real, and I needed it not to be real until the ship was gone.

* * *

The captain found me an hour later. He came down the gangway wiping tar from his hands, a broad man with a beard going to rust and eyes that had done their arithmetic about me already.

"You're the carpets," he said.

"I'm the carpets."

"They're aboard. Stowed dry, per the arrangement." He looked out at the water rather than at me, which I understood to be a courtesy. "Your friend with the seals did good work. Harbor inspection passed us without opening a single crate. First time in nine years." A short laugh, not entirely kind. "There's a lesson in that about paper, if you're the sort to learn lessons."

"What sort of lesson?"

"That the empire runs on ink, girl. Same as your workshop does." He spat over the edge of the quay, thoughtful. "Same as mine."

"Casper," I said. "My name is Casper."

He considered that. "Casper, then. You'll want to know she sails with the morning bell. Weather's fair, wind's northwest and steady. Once we clear the point, there's no calling her back." He finally looked at me directly, and whatever he saw in my face made his voice drop into something almost gentle. "You're sure you won't come? There's a bunk behind the chart room. I don't ask passengers their business past the three-mile mark."

For one whole breath I let myself want it. The open water, the border crossing behind me, the road from whatever foreign port to wherever Ilse Marchetti lived — delivering the atlas with my own hands, watching her face when she understood what had crossed the sea to reach her. It would have been the clean ending. The ending where the promise is kept loudly, witnessed, complete.

But the workshop had a roof that leaked over the drying racks. Marta's hands were worse than she admitted; I had watched her misjudge a wash of Prussian blue twice this week and say nothing either time. Sefa was sitting in a cell on suspicion of aiding the theft of government property, and someone had to be standing on the quay, respectable and visible and stubbornly present, when the questions started. And Wren had left me more than the atlas. He had left me the chair, and the smell of the ink, and the particular silence of a room where someone has just stopped working and you can hear the lamp burn.

"No," I said. "She goes without me. That was always the shape of it."

He nodded as if I'd confirmed something he'd guessed. "Then this is goodbye, Casper the carpets." He held out his hand, and I shook it, and his grip was careful, as though I were something breakable or valuable or both. "Your master did me a kindness once that I won't speak of on an open quay. Tell him so, wherever he is."

"He's not anywhere," I said. "That's rather the problem."

"Everything's somewhere," the captain said. "That's the first thing the sea teaches you." And he went back up the gangway, and I stood alone again with the gulls and the turning tide.

* * *

They brought Sefa at first light.

I heard her before I saw her — the particular rhythm of her walk, quick and flat-footed, a dockworker's walk that wastes nothing — coming along the quay from the direction of the cells, and I turned, and there she was, with a bruise yellowing along her jaw and her coat missing two buttons and her hands jammed in her pockets like she didn't trust them not to do something.

"They let you go," I said. Stupid thing to say. She was obviously let go; she was obviously standing there. But grief and relief had scrambled something in me, and stupid things were all that came out.

"Lack of evidence," she said. Her voice was rough, like she'd been using it for shouting or for not-shouting, which amounts to the same damage. "Two days in a cell and the clerk couldn't find a single document with my name on it. Turns out Marta's forgeries are better than the government's originals." She stopped beside me at the bollard, close enough that our shoulders nearly touched, and looked out at the *Meridian*. "Is it true? Did it go aboard?"

"It's aboard. Everything. The plates, the letter. All of it."

Sefa was quiet for a while. Down the quay, the crew were casting off the smaller lines, coiling them with the easy economy of people who have done ten thousand mornings exactly like this one. "They asked me who I worked for," she said at last. "Over and over. Different men, same question, different voices, like they thought I'd get confused." She flexed her fingers inside her pockets. "I gave them Marta's name once — said I colored maps for an old woman, piecework, nothing official. They wrote it down. Then I shut my mouth and stayed shut."

"You could have given them mine," I said. "One name and you'd have slept in your own bed Tuesday night."

"And then what?" She turned and looked at me fully, and her eyes were furious and tired and absolutely certain. "Then they'd have had you, and the workshop, and Marta, and every map in that room, and I'd have traded all of it for two nights' sleep. I'm not clever like you and Marta, Casper. I can't forge a manifest or argue with an inquisitor. The one thing I know how to do is hold a line. So I held it." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her something; I saw it land in her shoulders. "It wasn't noble. It was just the only job I'm qualified for."

"It was the most important job," I said. "Marta bought the time. You bought the silence. I only carried the thing." I swallowed. "Quill carried half of it, in the end. I keep trying to make the arithmetic come out fair and it won't. Somebody always pays more than their share."

"That's not arithmetic," Sefa said. "That's just people." And then, because she was Sefa, and tenderness embarrassed her: "Is that the ship?"

"That's the ship."

"Then look at it properly. You'll want to remember it."

So I looked.

* * *

The morning bell rang at seven, thin and bronze over the water, and the *Meridian* began to move.

It happened slowly, the way important things happen — so slowly you could almost believe nothing was happening at all. The great lines dropped away into the water. The sails took the wind in stages, canvas filling with a sound like a held breath released. The hull came off the quay by the width of a hand, then an arm, then a body's length, and the gap between ship and shore filled with grey-green water that had been waiting all night for exactly this.

I watched the deck shrink. I watched the crate of dye-stuffs that was not dye-stuffs riding in the shadow of the mainmast. I watched the flag at her stern — our flag, the enemy's flag, depending on which side of the border you stood on, which was the whole sickness of the world in a single piece of cloth — dip and lift in the wind.

And then the harbor opened, and the coast came into view.

Wren's coast. The one he had drawn plate by plate in the last years of his life, the harbors and headlands and soundings that the navy didn't know and Ilse Marchetti did, the shoreline he had sailed toward when he was old and should have known better, the rocks off it that had taken his ship and given back nothing but a box of effects and a debt I had spent a winter learning to pay. From the quay, in the early light, it was only a blue-grey suggestion beyond the harbor mouth — beautiful, the way dangerous things are always beautiful at a distance, all soft edges and promise.

The *Meridian* sailed toward it, and I stood very still and let myself feel both things at once, because there was no room in me anymore for feeling them separately: the release, huge and light as the tide itself, *it is done, it is done, she will have it* — and underneath it the grief, which had not gone anywhere and was not going to. The atlas was leaving and Wren was still dead. The letter was crossing the sea and he would never see her read it. We had won, if this could be called winning, and the prize was that everything went on: the workshop, the tide, the census next year, the empty chair.

"The coast keeps its secrets," I said aloud. I hadn't meant to say anything.

"What?" Sefa said.

"Nothing. Something Wren used to say. That a coast is a liar — it shows you exactly what it wants you to see, and the truth is all in the soundings." I wiped my face with the heel of my hand, roughly, before anyone could call it crying. "He drew the truth anyway. Every rock. Even the ones that killed him. He said a mapmaker's job isn't to love the coast, it's to tell it honestly."

Sefa said nothing, but she moved closer, shoulder against shoulder now, and stayed there, which from Sefa was a speech.

The ship cleared the point. For a moment her sails hung against the pale sky like a page being turned, and then she was small, and then she was a smudge, and then the sea had her, and the harbor settled back into its ordinary noises — hammers, gulls, a mate swearing at a boy about a fouled line — as though nothing irreplaceable had just left it.

"She's gone," Sefa said.

"No," I said. "She's begun."

* * *

We walked home the long way, past the fish market setting up, past the customs house where a light was already burning in a third-floor window — Quill's window, I thought, or the window where Quill would spend the day writing the report that would save us or bury us, and I found I could not make myself afraid of it. Whatever he decided, he had already decided it, there on the quay, with the atlas in his arms and the letter in mine. Men do their truest work in the dark before dawn. The paperwork is only where they explain it afterward.

Marta was waiting when we came through the workshop door, and I knew before she spoke that she knew. She was standing at the big table with her hands flat on the wood, and her eyes went from my face to Sefa's bruised jaw and back, and then she sat down, suddenly, all at once, the way old women sit when the thing they've been holding up has finally been set down.

"Well," she said. "Well."

"It's aboard," I said. "Both of it. The atlas and the letter."

"And the girl?"

"Released. No evidence."

"And Quill?"

I hesitated. "He carried one end," I said. "That's all I know how to tell you yet. Maybe it's all there is."

Marta nodded slowly, and pressed both hands over her mouth for a moment, and when she took them away her eyes were wet but her voice was level. "Then we open the workshop. Today. Shutters back, stove lit, sign out. If we hide, we confess." She looked around the room — at the drying racks, the pigment drawers, Wren's chair pushed in neatly at the head of the table, the place on the wall where the map room door now bore the government's lead seal, dull and official as a scar. "Aldous kept this place alive through two wars and one bad marriage. We'll manage a census."

I should have gone to the stove. I should have lit it, put the kettle on, done the hundred small acts that make a morning ordinary again. Instead I walked to the head of the table and stood beside Wren's chair, and my hand went to its back without asking my permission, the way it had a hundred times, reaching for the man who wasn't in it.

"We kept the promise," I said. To him, or to Marta, or to the room. "Whatever else happens — whatever they find or don't find, whatever Quill writes in his report — we kept it. The atlas is sailing to her. He'd have hated how we did it and he'd have done it exactly the same way."

"Amen," Marta said quietly.

And that was when we heard the knock.

Three knocks, unhurried, official — the knock I had learned to dread all winter, the knock that meant the government had arrived at the door of the only home I had left. Sefa was on her feet. Marta's hands went flat on the table again. And through the frosted pane of the workshop door, in the grey-gold light of the morning the atlas had just sailed into, I could see the shape of a man waiting on our step, patient as tide.

I went to answer it.
