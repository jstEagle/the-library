## 9. The Arrest

They took her at the gate.

Edith had expected it since dawn — had dressed for it, in fact, choosing the grey wool with the high collar and the buttons that did not tear away easily, because a woman about to be handled by strangers ought to be dressed like a fact rather than a rumor. She had pinned her hair herself, both hands steady, and had left the workshop door standing open behind her so that the smell of ink and scorched paper could follow her out into the frost like a confession she no longer wished to hide.

There were four of them: two constables from the district office she did not know, a thin man with a leather case who introduced himself as a clerk of the magistrate's court, and Hale. Hale stood slightly apart, his cap in his hands, looking anywhere but at her face, and Edith understood that he had argued against this and lost, or had not argued at all and was ashamed. Either way, she was gentle with him. "Good morning, Constable," she said, as though he had come to collect a subscription. He flinched as if she'd struck him.

"Mrs. Marlowe." The clerk consulted a folded paper without much interest. "You are to accompany us to the magistrate's residence at Ashcombe. There will be questions regarding certain publications. Certain correspondence."

"Certainly," Edith said. "Shall I bring my own pen? I write better with my own pen."

The younger constable made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. The clerk did not look up. Behind her, the valley lay white and silent under the last hard week of winter, and beyond the hedgerows the river ran dark between its banks, still locked in ice, still holding. Still holding, she thought. Everything holds until it doesn't.

She did not look toward the lane, toward the bend where the carriage house stood, toward any place Isandro might be. She had rehearsed this too. Not looking was a discipline like any other.

* * *

The magistrate's parlor at Ashcombe smelled of beeswax and cold tea and the particular staleness of rooms where men make decisions they believe are history. It was a good room, honestly — she gave it that. Tall windows onto the water road, a fire built up too hot, portraits of three generations of men whose opinions on the fitness of women to hold property were presumably painted into their jowls. Magistrate Pellowe received her standing, which she noted was a courtesy, and then sat, which she noted was the truth.

"Mrs. Marlowe." He gestured to the straight-backed chair placed opposite him, deliberately lower than his own. She sat in it without comment; she had spent thirty years being offered lesser chairs. "I will be candid with you, because I am told candor is your preferred currency."

"I prefer accuracy," said Edith. "But candor will do."

"You have been printing seditious material in this valley for the better part of two years. Pamphlets. A periodical. Instructions on — " he turned a page — "the organization of public assemblies, the evasion of lawful authority, the funding of persons engaged in unlawful agitation. We have seized examples. We have testimony." He looked up then, and his eyes were not unkind, which made them worse. Kindness in an official is a tool like any other; she had watched men use gentleness the way locksmiths use picks. "The press itself, I understand, has been destroyed. By fire. Conveniently."

"Conveniently for whom?"

"For everyone, Mrs. Marlowe. That is rather my point." He set the papers down and laced his fingers over them, a man settling in for the long work of dismantling something. "Here is what I can offer. You are a woman of means, of reputation, of — family connections that reach into rooms I do not pretend to enter. If you cooperate fully — names, correspondents, the distribution network, the source of funds — I have reason to believe this may be treated as a disorder rather than a conspiracy. A fine. A binding over. Perhaps a short confinement in comfortable circumstances." A pause, delicately weighted. "Whereas if you do not cooperate, the matter must be pursued as what it appears to be: an organized enterprise, conducted with deliberation, involving many persons whose names we should very much like to have."

Through the window behind him, past his shoulder, Edith could see the water road running down toward the bridge, and the river beneath it. The ice had gone grey-green at the edges overnight. She had been watching that ice for weeks the way other people watch clocks. Somewhere downstream, past the bend, past the ford where Ruth had come half-drowned out of the floodwater, past all the miles of frozen silence, spring was working at the underside of the world with patient hands.

"The fire," she said, "was an accident of carelessness. Ink rags, a stove, an old building. Tragic. I had complained about the stove for months."

"Did you."

"To anyone who would listen."

"And the press?"

"Destroyed with the building, I imagine. That is generally how fires work."

Pellowe exhaled slowly through his nose, the sigh of a man who has done this before and knows exactly how long it takes. The clerk opened his leather case and laid out papers one by one on the low table between them: a pamphlet, charred at one corner; a page of figures; a letter. Edith's eyes went to the letter despite herself, and she caught the flicker in the clerk's expression — they had put it there for precisely that purpose, a fisherman's fly.

It was a copy, she saw. Not one of hers. But it bore the cipher — the small innocuous marks, the prices and quantities that were nothing, that meant everything. Ruth's cipher, taught to Isandro in the loft above the workshop while snow sealed the windows. *Three dozen of the blue at ninepence* meant twelve women safe and moving north. *Send the grey wool* meant a name already taken, strike it from every list, protect her people. Edith had written in that cipher for two years. She knew its shape the way she knew her own signature.

"This was found," the clerk said mildly, "in the possession of a courier operating out of Millbrook. He has given us a description of the person from whom he received it. A man. Young. A driver."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a door closed, unhurried, domestic. Edith folded her hands in her lap and made her heartbeat obey her, the way one calms a horse on a bridge.

"I employ a driver," she said.

"Name?"

"Isandro Whitlock. He drives me. He has driven me for three years. He carries my letters to the post, my parcels to the carrier, my hatboxes to the milliner when I cannot be bothered. He is nineteen years old and he is paid eleven shillings a week plus his keep, and if you imagine that a boy of nineteen is the organizing intelligence of a movement that spans four counties, then your intelligence services are in a worse state than even I had hoped."

The clerk wrote it down. She let him. The name would be found anyway — found in the wage book, found in the mouths of neighbors, found in the memory of every soul in the valley who had seen the young man with the serious face loading crates at midnight. Better it come from her, framed as employment, as service, as nothing. A driver. Eleven shillings a week. Let them go looking for a servant and find only a servant.

But underneath the words, in the place where she kept the things she could never say aloud, she was speaking to him directly, across the frozen miles: *This is your protection. This is your wall. When they come to you, you are only a boy who drove a difficult woman. Know nothing. Remember nothing. Be dull, Isandro. Be so dull their eyes slide off you like rain off oilcloth.*

"Mr. Whitlock," Pellowe said, tasting it. "And where is Mr. Whitlock now?"

"At my house, presumably, mucking out the stable and resenting every minute of it. He resents most minutes of most days. It is one of his chief qualities. Youth believes it is entitled to resentment; I have never had the heart to disabuse him." She heard her own voice going on, light and dry and relentless, and marveled distantly at how well the machinery worked, how many years of county dinners and parish meetings had fitted her for exactly this: the filling of air until dangerous questions suffocate in it. "You may question him, of course. He will tell you I am a tyrant about punctuality and that I under-tip at Christmas. Both charges are true."

Pellowe studied her for a long moment. Then he leaned back and said, almost gently, "You understand, Mrs. Marlowe, that if you take this entirely upon yourself, we must act accordingly. The consequences will not be theoretical."

"I have read the relevant statutes," Edith said. "I have also read Milton, Burke, and Mary Wollstonecraft, and I promise you the statutes are the least interesting of the four. Yes. I understand."

"And you persist."

"I have persisted since 1906," she said. "You are merely the latest in a long line of gentlemen asking me to stop."

* * *

They kept her in the parlor while the clerk went to fetch the formal statement — a courtesy, they called it, though she suspected it was theater, letting her sit alone in the handsome room so that the weight of the house might do what the questions had not. The constable at the door pretended to study the ceiling.

Edith rose, stiff after the hours in the chair, and went to the window.

The water road lay below, rutted and freezing, running down to the bridge — the old stone bridge, the only way out of the valley, drowned all winter under the flood and then locked under the ice, and open now, or nearly. She could see the meltwater standing in silver sheets along the fields, see the ditches running loud and bright, see — yes — see the dark seam opening along the river's spine, the first true crack, black water showing through white like a sentence surfacing through silence.

Tomorrow, perhaps. Tomorrow or the day after, the ice would go all at once, the way it always did, with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and the river would run free to the sea, and anything carried to the far bank after that would arrive. Would arrive. The ledger was gone from the workshop — she had seen to that, she had seen to all of it — and wherever it was tonight, it was moving, or waiting to move, or already in the hands that would carry it. She allowed herself, for exactly the length of one breath, to picture Isandro walking that bridge with the satchel against his chest, and then she put the picture away, carefully, the way one puts away a letter one intends to keep forever.

He would hate what she had done. He would call it sacrifice and she would call it arithmetic: one woman, fifty years old, whose name was already in every file that mattered, weighed against a boy of nineteen with his whole unbegun life ahead of him. No honest ledger could balance any other way. He would hate it anyway. He had a habit of answering her letters when she hadn't asked him to; she had known for a year, had said nothing, had found his careful masculine hand in the margins of her own correspondence and loved him for it with a love she had filed under *gratitude* because there was no drawer in her life large enough for what it actually was.

Farewell, then, without goodbye. Goodbye was a word for people who believed they would meet again, and she was not certain she believed it, and she refused on principle to say anything she did not mean.

Behind her, the door opened, and the clerk returned with the statement and a pen, and the constable by the door straightened, and downstairs a carriage was being brought around, wheels crunching on gravel going soft with thaw.

Edith turned from the window. Down along the river, faint but unmistakable, came a deep report — the ice shifting, testing, beginning at last to give.

She sat down, took the pen they offered her, and began to sign.
