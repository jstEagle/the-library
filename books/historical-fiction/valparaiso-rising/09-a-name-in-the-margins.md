## 9. A Name in the Margins

The lock on her father's study had been changed in March.

Camila knew this because she had picked the old one herself, twice, when she was seventeen and convinced he was hiding letters from a woman in Concepción. He hadn't been. He'd been hiding shipping manifests from the tax assessor, which in hindsight should have told her everything she needed to know about what her father considered worth locking away. The new lock was brass, English-made, and smug about it.

She stood in the corridor with a candle in one hand and a hairpin in the other and felt, absurdly, like she was visiting a version of herself she'd outgrown. The girl who had picked locks looking for a mistress had been almost hoping to be scandalized. Scandal was survivable. Scandal made for good stories at dinner parties.

What she was looking for now was not survivable, and she knew it, and she was going to look anyway.

The hairpin found the tumblers the way an old song finds a forgotten verse. The lock gave with a click so soft it might have been an apology.

* * *

The study smelled of ink and cigar smoke and, underneath both, the particular salt-rot of paper stored too long near the sea. Camila set her candle on the desk and stood for a moment in the small circle of its light, taking inventory the way she'd been taught to take inventory at fourteen, when her father had decided his daughter should understand the business even if she would never be permitted to run it.

*Understand it, Camila. Never trust it to a man who doesn't love the numbers.*

She had loved the numbers. That was the humiliating part. She had sat cross-legged on this very rug with columns of freight costs spread around her like a card game, and she had been happy.

The desk drawers first. She worked through them with the efficiency of long practice: correspondence, sorted by region; a tin of licorice drops he pretended he didn't eat; a pistol, unloaded, kept for a danger that had never come; letters from the Cortázar office in Santiago that were, on their surface, perfectly boring. Prices. Schedules. *The usual terms for the southern lots.*

The southern lots.

She had read that phrase in the manifest copies she'd been stealing out of the counting house for three weeks now, and every time she read it, something in her chest had gone colder. Because she had grown up on the docks, and she knew what the southern lots were. The southern lots were the deep beds off Isla Verde where the water went dark at ten fathoms and the divers came up bleeding from the ears. The southern lots were where her father's divers went and did not always come back from, and here was the phrase in her father's own hand, in his own tidy merchant's script, priced per kilo like so much wool.

*The usual terms.* As if there were terms. As if the dead had agreed to them.

She kept searching. Not because she wanted to find more — God, she did not want to find more — but because half-knowing was worse than knowing, and she had learned that from watching Xanthe Nakamura all these years. The boy in the counting house who saw everything and said nothing, who had a way of looking at her father's back that she had once mistaken for the natural reserve of an indentured clerk and now understood to be the look of a man memorizing the location of a knife.

She found the false bottom of the filing cabinet at half past one in the morning.

It wasn't even clever. Her father had bought the cabinet secondhand from a German shipping firm and had never noticed — or had never cared — that the base panel lifted out if you pressed the left corner. Beneath it: a packet of contracts tied with green ribbon, and beneath the contracts, a photograph.

Camila untied the ribbon. The contracts were what she'd expected, and reading them was like swallowing broken glass in a smooth broth — each clause individually bearable, the whole of it lethal. Meridian Trading Company, party of the first part. Cortázar y Hermanos, party of the second. *Exclusive salvage rights to all recoveries in the designated beds, in perpetuity, consideration as noted in Schedule B.* In perpetuity. Her father had sold the sea itself, or rather had sold the right to force men down into it until the sea kept them, and he had signed it with the same flourishing *M* he used on her birthday letters.

She set the contracts down. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected to shake.

Then she picked up the photograph.

It was old — older than her, older than Xanthe. A dozen men on the shingle beach below the counting house, squinting into a sun that had set twenty years ago. Divers. She recognized the trade by their gear: the weighted belts, the masks with their hand-cut glass, the coiled lines at their feet like sleeping snakes. Most of them were laughing at something off-camera. One had his arm around another's shoulders. They looked, she thought, like men who had just come up from somewhere beautiful and were still carrying the light of it in their faces.

Her father's handwriting circled one face.

She had to bring the candle closer to read it, and even then the ink had faded to the brown of old blood. A loop of the pen, careless, quick — the way he circled figures he wanted checked, or names he wanted struck from a shipping list. The circle enclosed a young man in the second row, slim, with high cheekbones and an expression that was not laughing, that had not been laughing, that looked directly out of the photograph across twenty years of salt and silence and straight into her.

*Expendable.*

One word. Her father's whole hand, his whole confident merchant's hand, and one word.

Camila sat down on the floor. She didn't decide to; her legs simply ended the argument. She sat with her back against the filing cabinet and the photograph in her lap and the candle guttering, and she looked at the circled face and did the arithmetic she had spent three weeks refusing to do.

The face was Xanthe's face. Not exactly — the photograph was of a man grown, and Xanthe had been a boy when she'd first known him, all elbows and watchful eyes — but the face underneath was the same. The same cheekbones. The same unlaughing mouth that was not sad, exactly, but was holding something in reserve. The diver in the photograph had his hand resting on the shoulder of the laughing man beside him, and there was a wedding band on his finger, and Camila thought: *his mother. The laundress from the cliff road. He has her grave up there, I've seen him walking to it, I've seen him from the window a hundred times and never once wondered—*

She had wondered. That was the lie she would not be permitted to keep. She had wondered, the way you wonder about a stain on the ceiling, idly, without ever climbing the ladder to touch it.

*Expendable.* Written about a man with a wife and a son and a wedding band and a face full of light from somewhere beautiful.

And here was the thing that broke her, the thing she would carry for the rest of her life: her first instinct, her honest, reflexive, animal first instinct, was to protect her father. Even sitting on the floor of his locked study at two in the morning with the evidence in her lap, some loyal daughter deep in her chest was already composing the defenses. *He didn't mean it the way it looks. It was a business term. Everyone used that word then. He was under pressure from Cortázar. He never would have—*

But the word was in his handwriting. And the handwriting did not hesitate.

She had watched her father write. She knew his hesitations the way a musician knows a rest in a familiar score. He hesitated over sums that wouldn't balance. He hesitated over letters to the bank. He had hesitated, she remembered now with a clarity that felt like a physical blow, over the *e* in her own name when he wrote to her at school, because his hand had never fully trusted the Spanish spelling against the English one. He was a man who hesitated over everything.

He had circled a man's face and written *expendable* without a single correction.

* * *

The candle was dying. Camila became aware of this slowly, the way you become aware of cold water rising, and she understood that she had been sitting on the floor of her father's study for the better part of an hour, which was the kind of thing a girl did in novels and a woman did not do in counting houses.

She stood. Her knees cracked. Somewhere below her, the harbor made its small night noises — rope against mast, water against stone, the far-off bark of a dog on the Calle Serrano that she had heard every night of her life and had never once, until this moment, thought of as the sound of a town where people were being killed for the contents of the sea floor.

She should burn it. The thought arrived dressed as wisdom. Burn the contracts, burn the photograph, go back to bed, be the daughter of a wealthy man until the day she was the wife of one. That was the sensible play. That was the play her father would make. Evidence destroyed was evidence that never testified, and the Cortázar cartel had already demonstrated — three weeks ago, when the diver Tomás Reyes came up with his throat opened by a line that everyone agreed had simply *snapped* — what they did to loose ends.

She held the photograph over the candle flame.

The corner began to curl. The heat breathed up against her fingers. And in the last second before the fire could take the face, she saw it again — the diver's expression, that unlaughing look, that reserve — and she thought of Xanthe standing in the counting house corridor that morning, saying in his quiet way, *Señorita, your father's correspondence tray was disturbed when I dusted. You may want to speak with him about the lock.* Which had been either a warning, or an accusation, or an offer, and she had not known which, and she had been a coward about not knowing, and she had said only, *Thank you, Xanthe,* as if he were a servant reporting a spilled inkwell.

She snuffed the flame between her fingers. The pain was bright and immediate and it felt, obscenely, like the first honest thing she'd done in years.

"Burn nothing," she said aloud, to the empty study, to her father's ghost, to herself. "Keep everything."

Because that was the difference, she understood suddenly, between her father and her. He burned things. He had been burning things his whole life — names from ledgers, debts from books, whole men from the record of the world — and calling it business. Well. She was a Meridian too, and she had the family talent for keeping accounts, and she intended to keep this one to the final figure.

She worked fast now, the candle stub cupped in her scorched hand. The contracts went inside her bodice, flat against her ribs where they rose and fell with her breathing like a second, criminal heartbeat. The photograph — she hesitated over it, one last time, the daughter in her making one final appeal — and then she folded it along the crease her father's cabinet had worn into it, and put it with the others.

The false bottom went back into place. The drawers closed. The lock, when she clicked it shut behind her, made its soft smug English sound, and she nearly laughed at it, at all of it, at the absurdity of a family that locked its sins in cabinets and thought brass was security.

* * *

The corridor was dark. She had left her own candle at the top of the stairs, and now she stood in her father's study doorway with her father's secrets pressed against her heart and no light at all, and she found that she was shaking after all — not from fear, or not only from fear, but from the sheer delayed enormity of what she had done. She had committed theft and conspiracy before breakfast. Her father would call it betrayal.

Her father had written *expendable* in his own hand, and had not hesitated, and she was done calling anything of his betrayal.

She felt her way along the wall to the stairs. At the landing, the harbor window showed her the water, black and silver under a thin moon, and beyond it the dark humps of the moored ships, and — she stopped — a light moving along the naval pier where no light had any business being at this hour. A lantern, low and steady, moving with purpose. A patrol, or a smuggler, or a soldier, or a man.

It didn't matter. What mattered was that the town was waking up around her, checkpoint by checkpoint, soldier by soldier, and the clock her father had started twenty years ago with a flourish of his confident *M* was finally running down.

She blew out her candle at the top of the stairs and stood in the dark with the contracts against her ribs, and thought: *Tomorrow I find the boy in the ledger's margins. Tomorrow I tell him what his father's face was worth to mine.*

Down in the harbor, the light on the pier went out.
