## 17. Descent

The water took him the way it always had — not gently, but completely.

One moment he was a man crouched on the barnacled ladder of the naval pier, the ledger sealed in oilcloth against his chest, Marisol's rope harness biting his shoulders; the next he was nothing the harbor could name. Not an indentured clerk. Not a son of Meridian. Not even, for the length of one held breath, a man with a plan. Just a body in the dark green cold, sinking past the light like a stone that had finally been permitted to fall.

He had expected terror. He had rehearsed terror on the walk down from the tunnels, while Elena strapped the packet to him and recited the route a final time — *under the pier pilings, along the silt line, up inside the shadow where the magistrate's boat waits, and Xanthe, if you surface early I will never forgive you, and I mean that with enormous affection* — and all through her instructions his heart had hammered like a clerk stamping papers too fast. But now, beneath the surface, the hammering stopped. The water pressed it quiet. The cold slid through his shirt and found the old scars on his shoulders, the ones from the sorting floor, and said: *I know you.*

He had been in this water before. Of course he had. Every boy in Valparaíso had been in this water. But there was a difference between swimming and diving, between splashing in the shallows off Playa Ancha and going down — truly down — into the places where the pearl divers worked until their ears bled and their lungs forgot how to want air. His father had gone down every day of his working life. His father had gone down one last time and not come up, and the ledger said so in Xanthe's own careful hand, entered by a boy of eleven who did not yet understand he was writing his own inheritance.

*Nakamura Kenji. Debt outstanding at death: 340 pesos. Salvage rights: transferred.*

Xanthe was carrying those words against his chest now, wrapped in three layers of oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, and the harbor seemed to know it. The current tugged at him like a curious hand. Somewhere above, muffled to a heartbeat, the garrison drums were changing watch. He did not look up. Looking up was how divers died — Elena had told him that, or maybe it was one of the old men in the tunnels, Tomás with his ruined ear, who talked about the water the way other men talked about God. *The surface is a promise the sea makes to keep you coming back down. Don't trust it. Trust your hands. Trust the dark. Trust the count.*

So Xanthe counted.

He kicked downward along the pier's outer face, following the pilings as Elena had drawn them for him in charcoal on a scrap of sailcloth — she drew everything, that woman, as if the world only became real once she'd put lines around it. Down past the first fathom, where the light still meant something, gold-green and trembling. Past the second, where the gold gave out and the world went the color of bottle glass. At the third fathom the harbor bottom rose to meet him, soft and gray, a country of silt and dropped things: a boot sole, a broken jug, the ribbed shell of a clam big as his palm. He touched the bottom because Tomás had told him to. *Touch the bottom once,* the old man had said, gripping Xanthe's wrist with fingers like knotted rope, *so the water knows you've paid your respects. Then it will let you go where you're going.*

He touched it. The silt was cold silk. And in the half-second his hand rested there, something loosened in his chest that had been tight for twenty years.

Because this was the place. Not this exact patch of mud, perhaps — the harbor was wide and its dead were scattered — but this depth, this dark, this pressure that pressed on a man like a debt. This was where they sent the divers whose names the ledgers carried. This was where the company's arithmetic ended, where 340 pesos outstanding became zero pesos owed, where a man stopped being an asset and started being a margin note. Xanthe had spent his whole life on the dry side of that arithmetic, copying the numbers, keeping the columns straight, telling himself that ink was harmless, that paper was paper, that a boy who erased a dead man's name was only doing what he was told.

His father had died down here in the arithmetic. And here was his son, swimming through it, carrying the proof.

*I'm sorry,* he thought, and didn't know which of them he was apologizing to. Maybe both. Maybe the whole column of names.

His lungs began their argument. It started politely — a tightening, a suggestion — and then grew less polite, the way creditors do. He turned along the silt line, kicking toward the pier's shadowed underside, counting strokes the way Elena had drilled him. *Forty strokes along the bottom, then angle up under the third piling cluster, and whatever you hear above you, whatever light comes down, you stay in the shadow until my signal.* She had tapped it out on the table: two knocks, pause, one knock. *Two-and-one means come up. Anything else means stay down and be a rock. Can you be a rock, Xanthe?*

*I've been furniture my whole life,* he'd said, and she had almost smiled, which from Elena was practically applause.

Thirty strokes. Thirty-five. The pilings came down out of the gloom like the legs of some drowned cathedral, crusted white with barnacles, streaming with weed that waved slowly, slowly, in no hurry at all. The weed did not know about checkpoints. The weed did not know about Captain Ibarra's patrol boats or Cortázar's enforcers or the burn order that would turn twenty years of testimony to ash in two days' time. The weed simply grew where the water let it, and Xanthe felt a sudden, absurd tenderness toward it, and understood that he was losing his mind a little, down here in the cold, and decided that was acceptable. Divers probably always lost their minds a little. It was probably part of the trade, like the ruptured ears and the salt sores. You went down into a place that did not care whether you lived, and you came back up knowing something you hadn't known before.

What he knew now, at forty strokes, angling upward into the pier's black underside, was this: he was not afraid of the water. He had thought he was. He had spent his whole life thinking he was afraid of the water, the way he'd thought he was afraid of heights, of soldiers, of his supervisor's voice saying *erase that name, boy.* But it wasn't fear. It was grief wearing fear's coat. The water was just where his father had stopped existing, and a boy doesn't visit the place his father stopped existing unless someone makes him, and nobody had ever made him, and so the water had become a locked room in his own chest that he walked around for twenty years.

He was walking through it now. Swimming through it. And it was cold, yes, and dark, yes, and his lungs were burning like a lamp running low — but it was also, unmistakably, home. The same salt. The same slow push of current. The same muffled thunder of the town above, the sound his mother must have heard every morning she watched her husband row out, the sound Xanthe himself had heard from the counting house window without ever letting himself hear it.

*Papá,* he thought, into the dark, using the word for the first time since he was small enough to say it without shame. *I have your book. I'm bringing it up.*

His lungs screamed. He ignored them the way he'd learned to ignore everything — patiently, thoroughly, with excellent posture — and rose through the last fathom into the pier's shadow.

* * *

Surfacing is its own kind of drowning, if you do it right.

Elena had explained it on the sailcloth map, tapping the black wedge she'd drawn under the pier. *You'll come up into air pocket territory — the pier deck above, maybe a foot of headroom at low tide. You must breathe before you break the skin of the water. Roll your face up, take one breath through the gap, silent, before your body makes you gasp. A gasp echoes under a pier like a shot. Can you do that?*

*I can hold my breath longer than any man you've ever met,* Xanthe had said. *It's the one talent indenture leaves you.*

Now he proved it. Rising the last few feet with his lungs collapsed to raisins, he rolled onto his back, tilted his chin, and found the seam of air between water and pier decking — a black velvet inch of space smelling of tar and salt and old wood — and drank it in through pursed lips, slow, slow, while his body shook with the effort of not gulping. One breath. Two. Then he floated, flat on his back, the ledger riding his chest like a second heart, and let the darkness close over him.

It was almost peaceful. That was the obscene thing about it. Above him, six inches of planking separated him from the naval pier's night watch, from boots and rifles and lanterns and the whole machinery of men looking for exactly what he was. Below him, ten fathoms of cold held every dead diver the company had ever spent. And here in between — in the thin wet nowhere, breathing borrowed air — Xanthe Nakamura floated and felt, for the first time in his life, entirely free.

Not safe. Never safe. Free. There was a difference, and he was only now learning it. Safety was the counting house: warm, dry, and fatal. Freedom was this — cold, dark, chosen.

He listened. Under the pier, sound traveled strangely; he could hear the harbor talking to itself. Water lapping the pilings in long slow syllables. The groan of moored hulls somewhere out in the anchorage. Farther off, muffled by stone and distance, the customs house clock striking the quarter hour — nine-fifteen. The magistrate's boat was due at half past. Elena would be on it, in her cartographer's uniform with her forged orders and her face arranged into official boredom, and Marisol would be somewhere near the pier gate with her market basket and her widow's talent for being underestimated, and Camila —

Camila was supposed to be safely in the tunnels, and both of them knew she wouldn't be. Xanthe had argued with her about it for an hour and gotten nowhere. *You burned your bridges in front of the whole garrison,* he'd told her. *Your father will lock you in the house.* And Camila had looked at him with her chin up, her father's stubborn jaw set at an angle that would have made old Meridian proud under any other circumstances, and said, *My father locks doors. I pick locks. Where do you imagine I learned that? Not from him. From watching what his locks were for.* There was no arguing with that. There was no arguing with any of them, really. He had assembled a family of people who could not be argued with, and some traitorous corner of his heart was proud of it.

A sound cut across his listening: oars. Not the magistrate's boat — wrong rhythm, too hurried, four rowers instead of two. Then a lantern's glow bleeding down through the gaps in the pier decking, turning the black water around him a bruised amber, and the scrape of a hull coming alongside the pier's seaward face, close enough that he could hear the coxswain's low voice: *—sweep under the pilings, he can't have gone far, Ibarra wants the water checked before the boat comes in—*

The patrol boat. Here. Now.

Xanthe went still. Not calm — still. Calm was a luxury; stillness was a discipline. He flattened himself against the nearest piling, arms spread, cheek to the barnacled wood, the way he'd seen limpets cling, and pulled the oilcloth bundle tighter against his chest with crossed forearms so that from below — if anyone looked below, and the lantern light suggested they might — he would read as driftwood, as shadow, as one more piece of the pier's own body. His legs hung down into the dark. He curled them up slowly, slowly, heels to his backside, making himself small. Making himself invisible. Twenty years of practice, and here at last was the examination.

The lantern swept.

Light came down through the decking gaps in long blades, swinging, and where it crossed the water it lit the surface from above — a trembling ceiling of gold just inches over his face. He watched a blade of it slide toward him across the swell and thought, with strange detachment: *this is the light they see by when they pull bodies out.* The blade reached his piling, climbed the barnacles, touched the edge of his shoulder—

—and moved on, because the eye that held the lantern was looking for a swimmer, a fleeing man, a splash and a struggle. It was not looking for a limpet. Nobody had ever looked for a limpet. Invisibility, Xanthe thought, tasting salt, is a craft like any other. You spend your apprenticeship resenting it. Then one night it saves your life, and you understand it was never a curse. It was training.

Above him, voices. Ibarra's voice — he knew it now, that unhurried baritone, the voice of a man who offered freedom papers the way other men offered cigarettes. *Check the ladder. Check the mooring rings. And look me in the eye when you tell me the magistrate's berth is clear.* A pause. Boots on planking directly overhead, close enough that grit sifted down and ticked against the water beside Xanthe's ear. Then Ibarra again, quieter, almost to himself, and Xanthe would carry these words for the rest of his life: *He'll come by water. Divers always come home by water.*

*Divers always come home by water.* Was it a threat? A warning? For one vertiginous moment Xanthe wondered if Ibarra knew — truly knew — what was strapped to his chest, if the whole patrol was theater staged for his benefit. But no. Ibarra had made his offer and been refused, or rather postponed, and a man like Ibarra did not chase; he waited at exits. He was waiting at the pier because the pier was where any sensible runner would run. He simply could not imagine that the runner would choose the water — the water where his father died, the water the whole town knew the Nakamura boy avoided, the water that had taken so many of Meridian's divers that it should have been the last place on earth one of them would choose.

That was the flaw in Ibarra's arithmetic, Xanthe thought, and felt something that was almost laughter move through his chest without daring to become sound. The captain had studied the ledgers. He had never studied the divers.

The patrol boat pushed off. The lantern's glow shrank, swung away, dissolved into the general dimness of the anchorage. Oars faded. The harbor resumed its murmuring, and Xanthe hung there in the black under the pier, alone with his heartbeat and his cargo, and discovered that his hands were shaking — not from cold, though the cold was real, but from the delayed arrival of everything he had refused to feel while the light was on him. Fear, mostly. And underneath the fear, rising through it like bubbles through dark water, something else. Something that had no name in any ledger he'd ever kept.

Hope, maybe. Or its twin, the one that looks identical from underneath: readiness.

* * *

The church bells struck the half hour, and out beyond the mole a boat's riding light appeared — two lamps, steady, unhurried, the rhythm of rowers who had nowhere to hide. The magistrate's boat, keeping its appointment. Elena would be aboard, sitting very straight, her forged orders squared on her knee, watching the pier with eyes that missed nothing. Two-and-one, she'd promised. Two knocks and one, tapped on the hull, and then he could rise.

Xanthe breathed his thin ribbon of air and waited, floating in the cathedral dark, and let himself think about his father one more time — not the ledger entry, not the number, but the man. The man the tunnel divers had described to him three nights ago, their voices low in the lamplight: Kenji, who sang to the new divers on their first descent so they wouldn't panic. Kenji, who kept a hidden list of the dead scratched on slate, because someone had to remember them properly, with their true names, not their debts. Kenji, who had gone down one last time in bad water because the company's foreman said the quota needed filling, and who had known, everyone believed, that the water was bad, and had gone anyway — because if he didn't, the fine would fall on the younger diver scheduled after him, a boy of sixteen.

A man who chose the descent so someone else could rise.

Xanthe pressed his palm flat against the oilcloth bundle over his heart. Inside it were forty pages of names. His father's list had been slate and memory; his was ink and paper, but they were the same document, really, written thirty years apart by two men of the same blood: *these people existed, they mattered, and you will not erase us.* His father had begun the testimony. His son would finish it. All that remained was the last few feet — the hardest few feet, the ones every diver knows, where the water holds you and the air calls you and you must decide, one final time, which one you belong to.

The taps came, faint through the hull of the distant boat, carried somehow across the water the way sound carries at night when the harbor is holding its breath. Two. A pause. One.

Xanthe filled his lungs with the last of the pier's dark air, tipped his head back, and rose — slowly, deliberately, breaking the surface into the open night like a man coming up for the first time in twenty years, which was exactly what he was. Cold wind touched his wet face. The stars were out over the anchorage, reckless and countless, and the magistrate's boat was sliding toward him through the black water, and on its stern stood a straight-backed figure in army blue who lifted one hand — not a wave, Elena never waved, just a single raised hand, a surveyor marking a point fixed and true.

Xanthe swam toward the light.

And that was when he saw the second boat — unlit, low in the water, sliding silent along the pier's far side with men in it who did not row like sailors, and at its bow a cigarette ember flared once, briefly, illuminating a face he knew from the counting house, a face from Cortázar's payroll, before the ember died and the boat slipped into the pier's shadow behind him, closing the water off, cutting the road home.

The magistrate's boat was thirty yards ahead. The cartel's boat was ten yards behind.

Xanthe swam harder.
