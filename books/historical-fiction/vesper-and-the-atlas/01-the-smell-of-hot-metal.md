## 1. The Smell of Hot Metal

The type spoke to Ines Galloway before the city did.

She had learned its language at nine years old, standing on a crate her father had built for exactly that purpose, her small fingers learning the compartments of the case the way other children learned the streets of their quarter. Lowercase here, capitals above, the rare letters—k, z, the long s—tucked into their corners like shy relations. By twenty-three she could sort type in the dark, and often did, because the dark was when the work was quietest, and quiet was what Ines prized above nearly everything else in the world.

It was the third of February, 1848, though nothing about the day announced itself as remarkable, and Ines would have been offended by the suggestion that it should be. She sat at the stone with the government almanac spread before her in galley proofs, setting the month of March: feast days, market schedules, tide tables for a river that had flooded twice in living memory, the birthdays of ministers whose faces appeared on posters in every arrondissement. The type was eleven-point Caslon, worn soft at the edges from years of service, and it fit her fingers like a second skin.

*March the ninth — His Excellency the Minister of the Interior's birthday,* read the copy, written in an official hand so bloodless it might have been composed by a machine. *Let all citizens mark the occasion with appropriate gratitude.*

Ines set it without comment. That was the whole art of it, she thought—setting words you did not believe, cleanly, accurately, without letting your hands tremble or your opinions bleed into the spacing. A compositor who smuggled her feelings into the metal would produce crooked lines, uneven word-spacing, a page that looked subtly wrong to every reader even if no one could say why. The state paid for straight lines. Ines gave them straight lines, and kept whatever else she had for herself.

Outside, the Rue des Fondeurs went about its morning. The knife-grinder called his wares at the corner. Two women argued cheerfully over the price of cabbage. A boy swept snow-slush from before the bakery with the weary rhythm of someone who would be sweeping it again by afternoon. And beneath all of it—the thing Ines heard the way one hears weather building behind mountains—a restlessness. Men standing too long outside the Café des Arcades, not reading the newspapers posted in the window but watching each other read them. The cobbler's shutter half-lowered by ten o'clock, though he had no reason to close. A silence around certain names that had grown so habitual no one noticed it anymore, the way one stops noticing a limp.

History, Ines thought, gathering like thunderheads over the rooftops. Let it rain somewhere else.

* * *

Her father came down the stairs at half past eight, feeling his way along the wall with one hand, and Ines performed the small ritual she had perfected over three years: she did not look up, did not offer help, did not let her face do anything at all while Aldous Galloway navigated the last four steps by memory and the creak of the boards.

"Morning," he said, in the tone of a man reporting good news.

"Morning." She slid the March galleys toward him. "Proofs. Mind the fourth line of the ninth—I think the minister's name wants a wider space after the particle."

He bent over the proof sheet, holding it at arm's length, then closer, then at arm's length again, his lips moving faintly. He was sixty-one years old and had set type for forty-six years, and now his eyes were going the way of all things, clouding from the edges inward like windows fogging in winter. The physician in the Rue Saint-Clair had used the word *cataract* and then the word *inevitable*, and Aldous had thanked him gravely and never spoken of it since.

"The fourth line," he said at last. "Yes. Wider space. Good eye."

"You have the same eye," Ines said. "You just keep it further from the paper these days."

He laughed, and the laugh was genuine, and Ines felt the familiar warmth of it and the familiar ache underneath, both at once, the way one feels the heat of a fire and knows the wood is burning down. He could still read proofs if she held them at the right distance under the right lamp. He could still press a palm flat against a forme and tell by touch alone whether the type stood true—he claimed the metal spoke through his skin, and Ines believed him, because she had watched him find a rogue letter upside-down by fingertip alone. But the fine work, the careful work, the work that required eyes, had passed to her without ceremony, the way a house passes, or a debt.

They worked through the morning in the comfortable rhythm of two people who had shared a trade and a grief and a kitchen for many years. Her mother had died of the fever in '41, and neither of them spoke of it, and the silence had hardened into something almost architectural, a room they both lived around. The shop itself was narrow and deep, wedged between a chandler's and a shop that sold religious medals, its front window displaying almanacs, ledgers, wedding invitations, and a single framed specimen of ornamental type that Aldous had cut himself in his prime and which customers occasionally asked to buy, to his quiet horror. The press was a Stanhope, iron-framed, older than Ines, and it bore on its crossbar a dent shaped like a star where, according to family legend, her grandfather had struck it with a hammer during some dispute whose cause had been forgotten but whose anger remained, permanently impressed in iron.

That was the thing about printing, Ines thought, inking the rollers. Everything left a record. Metal remembered pressure. Paper remembered ink. She liked that about it—that the world could be made to hold still, letter by letter, in rows that ran true.

At noon she went out for bread and found the city performing its usual theater of calm. The baker gave her the loaf with yesterday's end thrown in, as always. The newsboy was shouting prices for the *Gazette*, and the headline concerned a new tariff on wool, and men stood around it nodding as though tariffs were what mattered, as though the tariff were not a kind of lid placed carefully over a pot everyone could hear beginning to sing.

"Bad winter for it," the baker said, meaning the wool tariff, or possibly the mood; with bakers one could never tell.

"Every winter is bad for something," Ines said, and carried her bread home, and locked the door behind her, and felt the small satisfaction of a woman returning to a place where everything was exactly where she had left it.

* * *

The afternoon brought the almanac's final pages, and with them the tedium that Ines had come to regard as a kind of luxury. Tide tables. Weights and measures. The official list of public holidays, including the Minister's birthday, which she set twice because the copy repeated it, once in the calendar proper and once among the notices, as though gratitude this significant could not be expressed a single time.

By four o'clock the light had gone thin and gray, and she lit the lamps, and the shop filled with the amber glow that made the type cases look like a wall of small drawers in some enormous cabinet of the world—every word ever said, sorted into compartments, waiting. Her father had gone up to nap, and the press stood silent, and Ines distributed the morning's type back into its cases, letter by letter, the most meditative of all the printer's tasks, the undoing of speech into its elements.

This was the hour she loved best, though she would have denied loving anything about the work, which she described to acquaintances as tolerable. The city murmured outside. The lamp hissed. Her fingers moved through the case with the blind certainty of water finding a channel, and the day's words dissolved back into raw material, ready to become anything tomorrow.

Anyone can be made to say anything, she thought, sliding an *e* home into its compartment. That was the whole secret of the trade, and the whole danger of it, and she had made her peace with the danger years ago by deciding it was not hers. Printers were instruments. What was printed was chosen by men who signed their names, men with opinions and reputations and something to lose. Ines Galloway set type. Whatever the type said belonged to somebody else.

She was so absorbed in this philosophy, and in the satisfying click of the letters finding their beds, that she did not at first register the sound at the door—or rather, she registered it as nothing, as the settling of an old building, until it came again: not a knock, precisely, but a weight against the wood, a body leaning where bodies did not lean.

The lamp flame leaned too, as though the draft from under the door had changed its mind about direction.

"Ines," her father called down through the floor, having woken, having heard it too. "Was that the bell?"

"No," she said, which was true, and moved toward the door anyway, which was perhaps not wise, and put her hand on the latch, which was the last ordinary act of the last ordinary day of her life.

Through the frosted glass of the shop's transom, the gaslight of the street showed her a shape—bent, unsteady, wrong—and a darkness spreading across the lower panes that she told herself, even as she opened the lock, was only shadow.
