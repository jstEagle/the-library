## 5. Apprentices of the Night

The first pages were printed at three in the morning, and Ines would remember afterward that the city had been quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

She had spent two days deciding who to trust, and both days had taught her the same lesson: trust was not a thing you found but a thing you built, plank by plank, like a press. Her father had given her one name before retreating into his silence — Sabine Corvel, the bookbinder on Rue des Tanneurs, who bound ledgers for half the merchants in the quarter and whose cart came and went through the customs gates more often than any cart had a right to.

Ines went to her under a pretext, an order for rebacking a set of parish registers, and stood in the binding room while Sabine worked, her hands moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once been watched. The room smelled of hide glue and leather and the faint sweetness of old paper. Sabine was perhaps forty, broad-shouldered, with grey shot through her dark hair and a scar along her jaw she did not explain and Ines did not ask about.

"Registers," Sabine said, turning the sample over. "Fine work. Your father's shop?"

"Yes."

"Your father used to bind pamphlets for people who couldn't afford to be seen buying them." She said it without looking up, the way one remarks on weather. "Before your time. Before his time too, maybe."

Ines's mouth went dry. "I don't know what you mean."

"No," Sabine agreed pleasantly. She set down the register and finally looked at her, and her eyes were the flat brown of river water — nothing stirring on the surface, everything moving underneath. "But I hear you've been buying lamp oil like a woman printing scripture all night, and I hear the almanac order was finished a week early, and I hear there's a child sleeping above your type cases who arrived the same night as a stranger nobody saw." She wiped her hands slowly on her apron. "I hear a great many things. It's the trade I'm in. Paper travels; so does talk."

Every instinct Ines owned told her to deny it, to smile, to be unremarkable. That had been her religion for twenty-three years: be the ink, never the story. But the plates were hidden under the floorboards upstairs, and somewhere in the city men were being held in prisons that appeared on no map, and their names existed now only because she had printed them, and if she denied it here, in this room smelling of glue, then they would go back to being no one.

"I need routes," Ines said. "Ways to move paper out of this quarter without it passing through hands that count it."

Sabine studied her for a long moment. Then she reached beneath the bench and drew out a leather scrap, testing its give between her fingers as though the conversation were about bookbinding after all.

"Bibles," she said. "Nobody searches a Bible twice. And wallpaper rolls — the paste masks the smell of fresh ink, and customs men hate getting paste on their gloves." A pause. "And ledgers. Every merchant in this city ships false accounts somewhere. One more lie in a ledger is invisible."

"You'd do this? You don't even know what's in it."

"I know what's in it." Sabine set the leather down. "My brother was taken from a café on the Boulevard Saint-Michel four years ago. The police told my mother he'd gone south for work. My mother believed them because believing was easier than the other thing." She flexed her scarred jaw. "If your atlas has his name in it, then he was somewhere. Somewhere is a kind of mercy. I'll carry your paper to every corner of this city for that."

* * *

Tomas came to them rather than the other way around, which Ines would later decide was the only reason she trusted him as fast as she did.

He appeared on a Tuesday, a boy of seventeen with ink permanently shadowed into the creases of his knuckles, standing in the shop doorway with the particular stiffness of someone who has rehearsed a speech and lost it. Rain had soaked him through. He looked at the cases, at the composing stone, at Ines's father dozing in his chair by the stove, and something in his face cracked open.

"They dismissed me," he said. "From Delacroix et Fils. I set a line wrong. One line. Except the line wasn't wrong — I set exactly what the copy said, and the copy said *the minister is blameless*, and I set it, I swear I set it letter-perfect, and they beat me anyway because—" His voice failed and restarted. "Because the foreman says I have a look. A look, madame. Like I'm reading what I set instead of just setting it."

"And are you?" Ines asked quietly.

He looked at her then, and she saw it — the hunger, the terrible incurable hunger of a compositor who cannot touch type without tasting meaning. She recognized it because she had spent her whole life starving herself of it.

"I need hands," she said. "Hands that can set small type fast and clean, in a shop where the work doesn't exist officially. You'll be paid in food and in not being asked questions."

"The work that doesn't exist," Tomas repeated. His eyes went again to the cases, the stone, the iron handpress under its dust sheet, and Ines watched him understand — really understand — what she was offering, the way only another printer could. Not employment. Initiation.

"I set eight hundred ems an hour," he said. "Small text, close-spaced. Nobody in this city sets faster."

"Then you'd better start," Ines said, and pulled the dust sheet off the press.

* * *

They became, without ever agreeing to it, a family of the night.

The rhythm established itself within a week. By day the shop performed its respectable life — almanacs, playbills, a wedding announcement for the mayor's cousin, set by Tomas with flawless dullness while his fingers itched. By day Ines's father sat by the stove and said little, though some evenings Ines would find him standing before the covered press with one hand resting on its frame, the way a man touches the shoulder of an old horse he once rode hard.

By night, the true work.

Sabine brought materials in her cart: paper bought in three cities so no single purchase would raise a flag, ink mixed slightly off-standard so it could never be traced to a supplier, and — her masterstroke — a contract for five hundred Bibles from a pious bookseller, which gave them perfect cover for buying Bible paper in quantity. The atlas pages would ride inside legitimate bindings or beneath them, sewn into the spines of devotional works, rolled into wallpaper, slipped between the double covers of merchant ledgers.

At nine each evening, when the shutters closed, Ines unlocked the floorboards and lifted out the plates. They still frightened her — those hand-drawn maps in the stranger's fierce, slanted hand, the fortress at Valmont with its subterranean levels marked in red, the depot on the Île with its list of holding cells, and beside each site, in columns of careful script, the names. Names of the disappeared. Four hundred and eleven of them, counted so far, the number growing as new plates arrived wrapped in oilcloth through channels Sabine would not describe.

Tomas set the explanatory text — the stranger had supplied it, terse and furious — and Ines locked up the forms herself, because locking up was the compositor's sacrament and she would not delegate it. The press ran from midnight until nearly four. There was a music to it, she discovered: the pull of the lever, the kiss of platen on paper, Tomas's voice counting impressions in a whisper, Sabine folding and sewing by candlelight with her scarred jaw set in concentration. Four hundred copies of the first signature done in six nights. No one spoke much. Words seemed profane in that room, as if they might be overheard by the very men the pages accused.

On the seventh night, Ines pulled the first completed sheet of the second signature and held it to the lamp — the map of the Valmont prison, every corridor and cell rendered exact, and along the margin, printed in type Tomas had cut down to near-invisibility, the names of thirty-one men held there.

She had expected to feel afraid. Fear was there, yes, a low constant hum like the stove. But underneath it, rising through it, was something she had no honest name for. It felt like the moment the form locks into the chase. It felt like the moment wet paper peels from type and the impression is perfect and permanent and cannot be unsaid.

Power. That was the word, and it shamed her a little, and she let it stand anyway.

"Look at it," Tomas whispered, coming to stand beside her, his young face lit gold. "Thirty-one names. Last month they were dead men walking around alive. Now they're—" He searched for it.

"Evidence," Ines said.

"Soldiers," Sabine corrected from her chair, not looking up from her needle. "That's what paper does. It makes soldiers out of the disappeared." She bit her thread. "My brother's name goes in the next signature. I checked the plate twice."

Ines went upstairs past midnight to check on Vesper — the child slept in a drawer-bed lined with soft paper offcuts, and had begun, in recent weeks, to sleep through the press's rhythmic thudding as though it were a heartbeat she'd been born knowing. Ines stood over her in the dark and felt the strangeness of her own life settle onto her like a coat that fit better than the old one. She had wanted to be unremarkable. She had thought invisibility meant safety.

But watching Sabine below, sewing names into Bibles; watching Tomas wash the ink from his hands with the reverence of a priest at ablution; feeling the plates' weight through the floorboards beneath her feet — she understood that she had been invisible the way furniture is invisible, the way the poor and the obedient are invisible. This was different. This was invisibility chosen, sharpened, worn like a mask in a play where the whole audience was the enemy. A ghost with a press.

"We could print anything," Tomas said when she came back down. He said it the way other boys said *we could steal anything*, with the drunk brightness of transgression. "Anything at all. No one knows we exist."

"No one knows we exist yet," Ines said. "That's a season, not a climate. Paper leaves trails — Sabine's routes, the ink, the boy who set one line wrong and got beaten for it. Sooner or later someone counts what shouldn't exist and starts asking who made it real."

Below the window, out in the dark street, a lantern swung past — slow, deliberate, the pace of a man walking a beat who has nowhere particular to be at three in the morning. It paused, briefly, across from the shop. Moved on.

Sabine had stopped sewing. None of them spoke. The press ticked as it cooled.

"Back to work," Ines said softly, and pulled the next clean sheet from the stack, and the night took them all again.
