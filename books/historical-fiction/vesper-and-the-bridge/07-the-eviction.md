## 7. The Eviction

The bailiffs came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for deliveries, for the quiet arithmetic of the workshop's slow dying—ink ordered, ink paid for, ink not paid for. I had imagined them arriving at night, or at dawn, with lanterns and shouting. Instead it was a gray midmorning, and the knock was polite, and politeness, I have since learned, is the sound power makes when it is certain of itself.

There were four of them. Three wore the council's gray-and-gold and stood in the street with the studied boredom of men who had done this many times and would do it many more. The fourth held the writ, and read it aloud to the empty street as though the street were the guilty party.

"By order of the Surveyor-General's office, the premises known as Nakamura and Voss, Mapmakers, are seized for debt and forfeiture. All instruments, records, and papers within are to be inventoried and held."

*All papers.*

I was upstairs with the atlas on my knees when I heard it. I had finished the last of the lettering two nights before—the colophon, *surveyed and drawn by Ivo Voss, completed by his apprentice*, and I had sat for an hour afterward just looking at it, the way you look at a finished thing because you are afraid of what you'll do once you admit it's finished. Now the words on the writ downstairs rearranged themselves in my head: *all papers*. Not the atlas alone. The library. Forty years of Ivo's correspondence, his field notebooks, the marginalia in his reference folios where he thought out loud in pencil. Everything that could tell an inquirer—or an inquest, or a historian—*how* he had known what he knew.

They weren't just evicting me. They were erasing the evidence of the evidence.

I want to say I moved with calm and cunning. The truth is I knocked over the lamp, swore, caught it, swore again, and then moved with calm and cunning.

* * *

The atlas went into the oilskin first. I had wrapped it days ago, half from superstition and half from the same instinct that makes a sailor check the weather twice. It was heavier than a book should be—every plate, every corrected proof, Ivo's hidden notes sewn into the binding at the back where a spine repair would hide them—and it pressed against my chest like a second heartbeat when I slung the strap across my body.

Then the plate. I had known for a week which one I would take if it came to choosing, and I had hated myself for the choosing, because it felt like the first small betrayal in what would certainly be a long career of them. The stress-figure plate. The one where Ivo's careful hand had annotated the council's own numbers with the arithmetic that unmade them—*declared load 40,000 stone; measured pier displacement, winter prior: nil*. One sheet of paper, and the whole official story of the bridge's unsafety came apart like wet pulp.

The rest of the hidden plates—the district surveys, the land titles traced in Ivo's fine red ink up to names with council seats—went into the false bottom of the coal chute.

I should explain the coal chute. Every workshop in the ferry district had one, a cast-iron door in the alley wall where the coal carts once delivered, back before the district got poor enough that the carts stopped coming. Ours was rusted shut and had been for years; Ivo had oiled the hinge every autumn anyway, saying a mapmaker should know every way out of her own territory. I had thought it one of his small eccentricities, like his hatred of the color violet or his habit of naming his compasses. It was not an eccentricity. He had known, at the end, exactly what was coming, and he had prepared an exit for a girl he would never see use it.

I pried the plate up—loose boards, a cavity between the joists, the plates wrapped in waxed cloth and tallow-paper—and my hands were shaking so badly I smeared charcoal across the first one before I remembered to wipe them. Then the atlas-plate went in with its siblings, and I closed the boards over them, and I stood in the middle of the workshop that had been my whole life since I was eleven years old and I let myself feel, for exactly ten seconds, what I was about to lose.

Ten seconds. I counted them off the way Ivo had taught me to count seconds for timing ink sets. *One*—the drying racks with their half-finished commissions for merchants who would never pay now. *Two*—the big south window with its warped pane that turned the afternoon light the color of weak tea. *Three*—his chair. *Four*—the burn scar on the table where he'd set down a hot stylus and laughed at himself. *Five*—the smell of linseed and lampblack that I would spend the rest of my life encountering in other rooms and having to sit down. *Six, seven, eight*—the library, the library, the library. *Nine.* The door, with their fists beginning on it. *Ten.*

Then I was the coal chute, and the coal chute was me, and I have never been so grateful for a small body and a childhood spent climbing things I was told not to climb.

* * *

The chute let out into the alley behind the tannery, which is a sentence I offer without elaboration except to say that the smell covered my tracks in every sense. I came out the far end gray to the elbows and shaking, and I stood in the mouth of the alley with the atlas against my ribs and watched, from forty feet and one bad angle, the bailiffs carry Ivo's library out into the street.

They did it wrong, of course. They always do it wrong. Books went into crates spine-up, spines crushed, a folio of his Venetian harbor charts used to wedge a crate level. One bailiff flipped through a notebook with his thumb, reading a line or two, and I felt my whole body go rigid—until I saw which notebook it was. His tide tables. Harmless. The dangerous things had never been on the shelves. Ivo had kept the true record of his last two years in the one place no bailiff would inventory: interleaved into a dummy folio labeled *Speculations on the Tides of the Inner Sea, Vol. III*, a book so boring that its own author had called it "an instrument of mercy for insomniacs." That folio was under my coat, against my back, and I had lifted it from the shelf an hour before the knock, on a hunch so cold and clear it hadn't even felt like a hunch. It had felt like hearing him say it: *Dominic. The tides, girl. Take the tides.*

So I had the atlas, the plate, the folio. I had the clothes I stood in, four silver bits, a stub of pencil, and nothing else in the world.

I watched them nail the council's seal across the workshop door—wax the size of a saucer, gray and gold, and under it a printed line I could read even at that distance, because I had lettered a hundred such notices for other people's doors: *PREMISES CONDEMNED. ENTRY FORBIDDEN BY ORDER OF THE COUNCIL OF VESPER.*

*Condemned.* The same word they'd used for the bridge. I stood there and understood, with the slow cold way understanding arrives when it has been waiting for you, that I had just watched them do to a building what they had done to a district, and what they had very nearly done to a man's life's work. You don't need to burn a thing down. You only need the word, and the wax, and enough people willing to look away.

I was still standing there—too long, far too long, grief making me stupid—when a hand closed on my arm.

I nearly broke the hand. I'm not proud of the sound I made, somewhere between a scream and the noise a kettle makes, and I had the pencil—God help me, the *pencil*—raised like a dagger before I saw the face under the hood. Sela. Ferry-roughened, wind-burned, furious.

"You're standing in the open," she said, "in the district where every third person owes the council money and half of those would sell their grandmother for a remission, holding the most valuable satchel in Vesper. Walk. Now. And if anyone asks, you're my cousin from upriver and you're simple."

"I'm what?"

"It's working already. Walk."

* * *

We went the way the ferrymen go, which is to say the way that doesn't exist. Down the tanners' stair, along the flood-wall where the ice groaned and shifted like something sleeping and dreaming badly, through the gate in the pilings that I had walked past a hundred times and never once seen, because Sela's people had built it to be unseen. The city above us went on with its afternoon. Somewhere behind us the seal was cooling on my old life.

The hiding place was a keeper's cell in the heart of the bridge itself—a room built into the stone of the second pier, dry, dark, smelling of tar and rope, with a slit window that looked out on the gray water sliding past below. There was a cot, a stove, a shelf of chipped mugs. Generations of keepers had waited out storms in that room, listening to the river try the bridge and fail.

"It's not comfortable," Sela said, "and it's not safe, and my father will say I've lost my mind, and he'll be right. But the council's men don't come onto the span since the closure—they're afraid of it, which is the one sensible thing they've done. You can stay until we decide what's next."

I set the atlas down on the cot and my hands, having held steady through everything, chose that moment to shake. Sela looked at the oilskin, then at me, and did the one thing no one had done since Ivo died: she didn't ask what was in it. She put a mug of something hot in my hands instead and sat down across from me on an upturned crate, and we listened together to the river.

"They took the library," I said finally. It came out smaller than I meant it to.

"I saw. From the water." She turned her mug. "My mother's ferry license was in the council archive. They 'misplaced' it in the spring. Whole district's licenses, gone, and with them the right to work the crossing." She looked up. "They're not after you, Dominic. They're after the idea that anything of ours can be written down and kept. You're just the one holding the pen."

I looked around the cell—at the rope-burns on the beams, the initials of keepers dead fifty years cut into the stone, the whole room a record no council had ever thought to inventory because it was kept in the wrong medium, by the wrong people, in the wrong place. Ivo had spent his life putting the world onto paper so it could be argued with. These people had spent theirs putting it into stone and story so it couldn't be seized.

"They'll inventory the workshop by week's end," I said. "And when they inventory it, they'll find the coal chute."

"Then by week's end the chute will be empty and the tannery will have had a very busy fire."

"You'd burn them?"

"I'd rather they be ash in the river than evidence in Brandt's drawer." She said it without heat, the way you report the weather. "You kept one out. I can see it in your face. Good. One's enough to hang them, if it ever gets in front of the right eyes. The rest is for keeping *them* from hanging *us*."

I drank the hot something—it was tea the way a shipwreck is a boat, mostly driftwood—and felt, for the first time since the knock, the floor of the world stop tilting. Not steady. Just no longer tilting.

Outside the slit window, the light was going. The bridge stood over me in the dark, all that condemned, unsound, fatal stone, holding. It had been holding all day. It would hold all night. Somewhere above, on the deck no one was allowed to walk, the frost was setting in for the season, and I thought: *they sealed this bridge to kill it, and instead they've made it the one place in Vesper where they never look.*

Sela stood, stretched, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch.

"One more thing," she said. "The ice is forming early this year. Earlier than the old keepers can remember. My father says the river's going to do something come thaw—something big. And when it does, the council's going to need someone to blame." She looked back at me. "I'd start deciding now what your atlas is going to say about that."

The river slid past below the slit window, black and patient, carrying the winter down toward the sea, and I sat in the heart of the bridge they had condemned, holding the truth they had come to take, and began to understand that the eviction had not been an ending.

It had been them, showing me exactly how much they were afraid of.
