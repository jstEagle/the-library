## 3. The Sterling Name

The lie came out of her like water from a cracked pitcher—smooth, cold, and impossible to gather back.

"Sebastian Sterling," she said. "Born in Cincinnati, Ohio. My father was a merchant of dry goods."

Elias Vesper wrote it down in his careful census hand, the nib scratching against the paper the way a mouse scratches inside a wall. He did not look up. That was the worst of it. He simply recorded her answer as though it were a fact about the world, and moved his pencil to the next line.

"Parents living?"

"My mother is dead." True. "My father—" She let a small pause fall into the room, the kind of pause that does a liar's work better than any words. "We are estranged."

That word at least was honest. Estranged. As if they had merely quarreled over the disposition of a sideboard instead of everything else.

Elias nodded and made another mark. The lamplight in the hospital office caught the edge of his jaw, the ink stain on his middle finger, the patient way he held himself, like a man who had learned that most people tell you everything if you only wait. He was not a threatening man. That was what made him dangerous. A provost marshal would have frightened her less; she knew how to stand against a drawn sword. It was this quiet pencil she could not stand against.

"And your occupation before the war?" he asked.

"Nurse. Before that—" *Before that I was Miss Aurelia Sterling of Cincinnati, dressed in watered silk, engaged by contract to a man whose fortune my father needed more than he needed my happiness.* "I kept house for my family."

"Kept house," Elias repeated, writing.

The words sat on the page like two small tombstones.

He closed his folio at last, thanked her for her patience, gathered his coat and his satchel of forms, and let himself out into the snow. Sebastian stood in the office doorway listening to his boots crunch away down the corridor toward Ward Two, where he still had eleven names to collect, and felt the shame arrive on its usual schedule—not all at once but in installments, the way grief came, the way debt came. First the hands. Her hands always shook after she lied, no matter how well the lie went. Then the heat in the face. Then, last and longest, the voice in the back of her skull that sounded disconcertingly like her father's attorney, Mr. Pell, saying: *You lied to a servant of the United States government, miss, and you did it without so much as a tremor. What else are you capable of?*

She went back inside and locked the office door, though there was nothing in it to lock but bandage invoices.

* * *

Cincinnati, in the autumn of 1859, had smelled of pork and river mud and money, and all three smells had been presented to her that September evening as though they were the same thing.

She had been twenty-two years old, seated at the long table under the chandelier her grandfather had brought back from Vienna, wearing a gown the color of claret that her mother had chosen because it flattered her and cost four hundred dollars, and across from her sat Mr. Cornelius Whitmore-Hale, thirty-eight years old, owner of half a pork-packing concern and a third of a bank, widower, childless, and—as he had just demonstrated with a joke about the intelligence of his mill workers—cruel in the comfortable way of men who have never once been contradicted.

"You'll want to see the house on Mount Auburn," he said to her father, not to her. "Eleven rooms. My late wife's furniture is all sound. A bride won't need much."

A bride. As if she were a shipment already crated, awaiting only the bill of lading.

Her father, Josiah Sterling, was in trouble. Everyone at the table knew it and no one named it: two bad years in cotton futures, a note coming due at the Third Bank in March, and Whitmore-Hale's offer standing behind it all like a wall of sandbags—the marriage settled in spring, the loan forgiven as a wedding gift, the Sterling name propped up on the shoulders of a girl who had not been consulted because girls of her class were not consulted; they were *informed.*

"Aurelia," her mother said gently, when the men withdrew to the study and the brandy came out. "Mr. Whitmore-Hale is a solid man. You would want for nothing."

*I would want for everything,* she thought, and said nothing, because she had learned young that silence was the currency of daughters.

That night she did not sleep. She lay in her bed under the Vienna chandelier's borrowed light and listened to the river boats below the bluff, their whistles crossing the dark water like voices calling from a country where women were allowed to answer questions honestly. And somewhere past midnight, unbidden, her brother's name rose in her mind the way names do when a house is too quiet.

*Sebastian.*

Thomas Sebastian Sterling. Dead at seventeen, six years gone now, of the fever that swept the Cincinnati schools the winter she was sixteen. Her younger brother, her shadow, her co-conspirator in every childhood crime against the household order. He was the one who had taught her to swim in the Mill Creek against express orders. He was the one who had dared her to read Tom Paine hidden inside a book cover labeled *Sermons.* He was the one who used to say, sprawled on the nursery floor with his boots on the good rug, "When I grow up I'm going somewhere nobody knows our name, and I'll be whoever I say I am."

He had never grown up. Which meant, she thought in the dark, that the name was going unused. Sitting in some family Bible like a coat left behind in a closet.

By dawn she had a plan, and the plan frightened her so badly that she knew it was the right one.

* * *

It took her five weeks. Five weeks of smiling at Cornelius Whitmore-Hale across dinner tables while he described the discipline he intended to institute in his household ("a wife should no more handle money than a horse should hold the reins"), five weeks of sewing banknotes into the hem of a gray traveling cloak, five weeks of writing letters to hospitals in Louisville and Cairo asking whether they took volunteer nurses without references, and receiving answers that amounted to *no one checks, come.*

On the last night, she took the ferry across to Newport alone, ostensibly to call on an aunt, and from the Newport landing she walked to the depot and bought a ticket south under the name written in her best imitation of her dead brother's boyish scrawl: *S. Sterling.* Not even a lie, properly speaking. Just a name stepping into a pair of boots its owner had left empty.

She remembered the train pulling out, the Ohio River sliding past the window all silver and coal smoke, and her own reflection ghosted over the passing banks—and how she had looked at that reflection and practiced, silently, the new signature. *Sebastian.* The pen strokes of it. The weight of it. By the time the river bent out of sight she had stopped practicing and simply answered to it, the way a soldier answers to a rank.

Her mother found the letter two days later. She knew this because for months afterward, letters arrived at whatever hospital employed her, forwarded through a Louisville stationer who asked no questions—letters in her mother's hand, growing shorter each time, until the last one contained only a pressed violet and the sentence: *Your father says the door is shut. I say doors are only shut from one side.*

She had wept over that violet in a linen closet in Cairo, and then she had folded it into the ledger—the first thing ever entered there, before there were names in it, when it was only a place to keep the one piece of herself she had managed to carry out of Cincinnati intact.

* * *

Present-day again: the snow had thickened outside the office window, sealing the town the way wax seals a letter. Down the hall someone coughed the deep wet cough that meant the pneumonia ward would gain a resident by morning. The clock in the corridor—somebody's donation, a tall case clock that had survived the hotel's conversion and now ticked off the hours of other people's suffering—struck four.

Sebastian unlocked the office and took the ledger out from under the loose floorboard behind the supply cabinet, because she could not help herself, because the lie to Elias Vesper had left a residue on her hands that only work could wash away.

She turned to the front matter, where the violet still lived between the pages, flattened to a brown ghost of itself. Below it, in the columns she kept now with such regularity that Amos Reed joked she kept them "like scripture," were the names. Three added last night. Forty-one in all since November.

She read down the list the way other people prayed.

*Private Daniel Okafor, Company C, 27th—died of wounds received at—omitted from regimental return.*
*Mrs. Letitia Boone, cook, contracted fever in service—omitted from hospital register.*
*Boy unknown, called "Peanut" by Ward Two—died Tuesday—no surname ever learned.*

And so on. The war above stairs wrote its dispatches; down here, under the floorboard, she wrote hers.

But tonight the ledger would not settle her. Tonight every name she read seemed to ask her the question Elias Vesper had asked, casually, terribly, with his pencil already moving:

*And what name were you born under?*

Because here was the thing she had not admitted to anyone, least of all to the flat brown violet: the lie had come too easily. Not the fact of lying—she had been lying for four years, to matrons and surgeons and provost officers, and had made her peace with it as the tax on freedom. No. What shamed her was the *smoothness.* The way "merchant of dry goods" had risen to her lips fully formed, polished, rehearsed in a hundred identical interrogations. Somewhere along the line, Aurelia Sterling had become not a woman in hiding but a woman *edited*—her true self cut out of the record as cleanly as any officer cut a dead private from a regimental return.

She was doing to herself exactly what the war did to its forgotten men.

Sebastian set down the ledger. In the window's gray light her own reflection hovered over the snowbound street, faint as her brother's face in the family Bible must be by now, and she made herself look at it.

"All right," she said aloud, to the reflection, to the violet, to Thomas wherever he was. "No more editing."

Not confession—she wasn't ready for confession, might never be; the door her mother wrote about stayed shut, and Elias Vesper's pencil was still out there in the world, moving line by line toward whatever truth he was paid to find. But resolve. There was a difference, and she marked it the way she marked everything now: in writing. She took up her pen and, beneath the day's entries, added a line that was not a name at all:

*Kept by S. Sterling—who was born under another name and means to die under this one, or under both, whichever honesty requires.*

She blotted it, returned the ledger to the floorboard, pressed the board home with her heel, and had just straightened her apron when the knock came—two raps, apologetic, unmistakable.

Through the frosted glass of the office door, snow-dusted and stamping the cold from his boots, stood Elias Vesper, holding his folio against his chest like a breviary.

"Forgive me, Miss Sterling," he called through the glass. "One question too many. I find I've left a blank on your line, and my supervisor is particular about blanks."

Sebastian closed her eyes for half a second—half a second was all she allowed herself—and opened the door.

"Of course, Mr. Vesper," she said, in the smooth voice she suddenly hated. "What is it you need to know?"
