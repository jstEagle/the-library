## 4. Ward Four Keeps Its Own Records

The fever broke on a Tuesday, and Corporal Amos Reed woke to the sound of a woman writing.

Not the scratch of an army clerk's pen—Amos had heard enough of that in the paymaster's tent at Milliken's Bend to know the difference. A clerk wrote fast and careless, the way men did when the names they set down meant nothing to them. This was slower. Deliberate. The sound of somebody taking care with every letter, the way his mother had taken care with the letters she'd taught him on a slate behind the quarters, back when teaching him was against the law of Kentucky and she did it anyway with the shutters closed.

He lay still and listened. Ward Four was quiet in the way that only came after bad nights. Six beds, four occupied. Private Hollis snoring through the hole in his jaw. The boy they called Chickamaug because he'd lied about his age to get into the 79th Ohio, breathing shallow but steady. Down at the far end, the bed where Sergeant Duffy had been until Thursday stood empty, stripped, the mattress already gone to the burn pile. Nobody had said anything about it. That was how it went. A man emptied out of a bed and the war just rolled over the space where he'd been, like water closing over a stone.

Amos turned his head on the pillow.

She sat at the little table by the window—the nurse, the one the whole hospital called Miss Sebastian, though Amos had figured out early that nobody had ever been told her last name and nobody had ever thought to ask. Lamplight low. A book open before her, and her pen moving across it with that slow, careful care.

He watched her for a long time before she noticed. When she did, she didn't startle or hide the book, which told him something right there. She just looked at him across the dim ward, lamplight catching the tired lines under her eyes, and waited to see what he would do.

"You write letters for the men," Amos said. His voice came out rusted from the fever. "I seen you doing it. So I reckon that's what this is."

"Some of it is."

"And some of it isn't."

She capped her pen. "You've got a sharp eye for a man who's been three days unconscious."

"I've got a sharp eye for a man who's spent two years watching what gets written down and what don't." He pushed himself up against the headboard, and the room tilted, and he let it tilt and then settle. "My company went into the fight at the mill with forty-one men fit for duty. You want to guess how many names come back on the casualty list?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to.

"Twenty-six," Amos said. "Fifteen men, gone. And I don't mean dead—I mean *gone*, off the paper entirely, like they was never issued boots. Captain says colored troops' records go through a separate channel now. Separate channel's been separate for five months. My sergeant's widow down in Cairo can't draw a cent because as far as the army's concerned, Isaiah Pratt never existed." His throat worked. "I wrote to the adjutant general. I wrote twice."

"I know," she said quietly. "I've seen the replies come back. Or not come back."

Something moved through Amos then—not anger, exactly. Anger was a fire you could work beside; he'd been working beside it since Fort Wagner. This was colder. It was the feeling of standing in a graveyard where the stones had all been pulled up and used for a fence.

"What's in the book, ma'am?" he asked. "If you're willing to say."

She was quiet a while. Outside, snow ticked against the glass. Somewhere below, in the kitchen, the freed workers who kept the hospital running were banking the stoves for the night, and their voices rose and fell soft as a hymn without words.

Then she got up, crossed the ward, and set the ledger on the blanket over his legs.

"Read it," she said.

* * *

It wasn't a big book. That was the first thing. All that weight Amos had been carrying—all those names rattling around inside him like shot in a drum—and here was the vessel for them, a plain bound thing no bigger than a hymnal, its cover worn soft at the corners from being handled in secret.

He opened it by lamplight and read.

*Josiah Turner, private, 13th U.S.C.T., died 14 December, fever, Ward Two. Mother: Louisa Turner, freewoman, Ripley, Ohio.*

*Daniel Okafor, cook, contracted hand, drowned 2 November when the ice took him off the landing. No record exists anywhere. He was buried by the river men behind the hotel.*

*Private William Greer, 79th Ohio, died of wounds 19 January. Listed in regimental returns as "deserted at Nashville." He did not desert. I held his hand when he went. He asked for his sister, Mattie, in Chillicothe.*

Page after page. Names with the small hard facts hung on them like coats on nails—who they'd been, who loved them, where the body lay, what lie the official paper told instead. Some entries were a line. Some were half a page. One, near the middle, was just: *Unknown soldier, both legs, Ward Four, died 3 a.m., called himself "Tennessee" and nothing else. Somebody ought to know he laughed easy and gave his coffee away.*

Amos read until his eyes burned. Then he read the front matter, where she'd written, in the same careful hand:

*This book belongs to the men the reports forgot. If I am found out, let whoever finds it carry it on.*

He closed it. He found he couldn't speak for a moment, and when he could, what came out was rougher than he intended.

"You know what they'll call this. If Pike finds it."

"Sedition," she said. "I'm told."

"He told me the same thing. Not about a book." Amos looked down at his own hands, scarred brown and steady on the ledger's cover. "About talking. Back in October, I asked Dr. Pike straight out why my company's muster rolls hadn't reached the pay department, and he looked at me like I'd tracked mud on his floor and said that soldiers' business was officers' business, and if I raised it again he'd note it in my file. Note it in my file. Like I'm a drawer with a loose handle." He exhaled. "I stopped asking after that. Figured the asking was wasted breath."

"And now?"

"Now I'm looking at a woman who didn't stop." He met her eyes. "Ma'am—Miss Sebastian—I got fifteen names in my head that ain't in any book on this earth except maybe God's. Men of Company C, 13th Colored. Fell at the mill, fell after the mill, died in the mud waiting on ambulances that come for the white regiments first. I carried two of them myself. I know their people. I know where they're buried, because me and the chaplain's man dug the graves when the detail wouldn't." His voice cracked and he hated it and kept going anyway. "I been carrying them around like hot coals, waiting on a place to set them down. And here you are with a place."

Sebastian sat down on the edge of the next bed, close enough that he could see her hands weren't quite steady either.

"Tell me," she said.

So he did.

* * *

They worked past midnight. She wrote; he spoke; the snow sealed the windows and the world outside ceased, for those hours, to exist.

Isaiah Pratt, killed 22 November, struck in the chest at the mill, survived two days. Wife, Hester, Cairo, Illinois. Sang bass so low the whole company felt it in their ribs.

Corporal Deacon Moss, killed same day, shot through the thigh, bled out because the tourniquet detail passed his position twice. Had a daughter named for his mother, Ruth, born the week he enlisted. Never saw her.

Private Enoch Bell. Private Thomas Grayson—he wasn't even supposed to be there, he'd swapped duty with a sick friend and caught the bullet meant for another man's luck. Private Samuel Adeyemi, who'd escaped from a plantation in Tennessee and joined up the day the recruiters came through, and whose last words were that he'd finally walked far enough.

On and on. Fifteen names. Sebastian wrote each one the way she wrote all of them—slowly, completely, asking the questions the army never asked. Who waits for word? What should be remembered? Where does he lie?

When it was done, Amos watched her turn back to the beginning of the new pages and add something above the first name, in letters smaller than the rest:

*Recorded by Cpl. Amos Reed, Co. C, 13th U.S.C.T., who was present.*

"There," she said softly. "Now it isn't just memory. Now it's testimony. There's a difference, and someday somebody's going to care about the difference."

"You believe that?" Amos asked. "Truly? That somebody'll care?"

She considered it honestly, which he appreciated. Most white folks answered questions like that with comfort, and comfort was cheap.

"I believe paper outlives intentions," she said at last. "I believe a lie repeated in dispatches is still a lie, and a truth written down once is still true, no matter how long it sits in a drawer. And I believe—" she paused, and something old and guarded moved behind her eyes, something that had nothing to do with the war—"that the people who count on being forgotten make one mistake, always. They assume forgetting is safe. It never is. Somebody remembers. Somebody writes it down. The record keeps."

Amos nodded slowly. Then, because the fever had burned away whatever caution he'd had left, he said what he was thinking.

"You do this every night. Alone. In a building full of men who'd sell you to Pike for a furlough." He shook his head. "Why? What's your stake in my men? No offense meant, ma'am, but I've learned to ask."

"No offense taken. I'd ask too." She closed the ledger and rested her hand flat on its cover, and for a moment she looked less like a nurse and more like a woman standing at a door she'd locked a long time ago. "Because I know what it is to have a name taken off a record. That's all I'll say tonight. Maybe more, someday."

"That's enough," Amos said, and found that it was.

* * *

Word traveled the way it always did in Ward Four—not by telling, but by a kind of weather.

Chickamaug saw the corporal and the head nurse bent together over a book two nights running and drew his own conclusions, which were correct. Private Hollis, who couldn't talk well through his ruined jaw but could listen better than any man in the army, listened. Down in the kitchen, Mama Jinny—who had cooked for the hospital since it was a hotel and who missed nothing that happened under that roof—began leaving the lamp by the stair burning later than regulation allowed, and no one asked why, and no one trimmed it.

By the end of the week, it wasn't a secret so much as a covenant. Men started finding their way to Amos's bedside during visiting hours, casual as anything, and staying a few minutes longer than they needed to. A private from the 79th mentioned that his cousin had died at Stones River and was listed as missing, and could a name maybe find its way into a certain book. A teamster who drove for the quartermaster knew of two drowned deckhands off the *Belle of Louisville* that no report had ever troubled itself to mention. Even the boy who mucked the wards—a freed boy of fourteen called Pip, all elbows and silence—appeared at Amos's elbow one evening holding a scrap of paper on which he'd written, in a laboring hand, the name of his mother, dead on the passage north two winters past, unrecorded anywhere, and laid it on the blanket without a word.

Sebastian entered every name. Word spread further.

And on Sunday evening, with the ward quiet and the lamp low, Amos Reed sat propped against his pillows watching her write, and understood that something had happened in this room that had nothing to do with medicine. A family had formed—not of blood, blood was a lottery—but of witness. People who agreed, without ever saying it outright, that the forgotten would not stay forgotten while they drew breath. He had served two years in an army that could not reliably remember his existence, and here, in a converted hotel on the frozen Ohio, he had found six beds and a kitchen and a lamp left burning late, and a woman with a hidden book, and it was more home than anything the government had ever issued him.

"Miss Sebastian," he said, as she blotted the last entry.

"Hm?"

"The historians. The ones coming up from the capital before thaw. The orderlies been talking about it—big steamboat, official party, going to write the war down proper this time." He watched her face carefully. "You reckon they'll want to hear from folks like us? Folks who were actually here?"

She was quiet a moment too long.

"They'll want to hear from the officers," she said. "From the surgeons. From the men whose version fits the dispatches." She gathered her pen and ink, and her voice was light in the way he was learning meant the opposite. "But paper outlives intentions, Corporal. We'll see whose paper lasts."

She wished him good night and carried the ledger out under her apron, and Amos lay listening to the snow tick against the glass, and thought about steamboats, and thaw, and clocks ticking toward spring.

Two days later, a stranger came up from the landing asking directions to the hospital—a neat, precise man with a census book under his arm and a government look about him, and eyes that stayed a moment too long on everything they touched.

Ward Four watched him come up the walk, and Amos Reed, who had learned to read trouble the way farmers read clouds, felt the whole household go still as one animal scenting wind.
