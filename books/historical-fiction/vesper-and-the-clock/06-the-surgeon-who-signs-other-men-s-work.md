## 6. The Surgeon Who Signs Other Men's Work

Dr. Harlan Pike believed in order the way other men believed in God, and he kept his faith in a leather portfolio that traveled with him from ward to ward like a breviary. Inside it: the day's operations, entered in his own hand, each one initialed, dated, and attributed. *Amputation, left leg below knee — H. Pike.* *Excision of necrotic bone, right forearm — H. Pike.* *Trephination, skull — H. Pike.*

That last one had been young Whitfield's work, all of it. The boy had stood over the patient for forty minutes with a trephine trembling in his grip while Pike took his dinner upstairs, and when Pike returned the hole was bored clean and the man was breathing, and Whitfield had looked up at him with the exhausted gratitude of a dog that has fetched something too heavy for it to carry.

"Good," Pike had said, and made a note.

He did not think of this as theft. Theft implied the taking of something from someone who had a right to it, and what did these half-trained boys have rights to? He had trained them. He had read the books they'd only heard about, corresponded with surgeons in Philadelphia and Vienna, performed—by his own count, which was the only count that mattered—over four hundred operations since the war began. If a man built a mill on his own land, did the grain belong to the farmer who carried it in? The signature at the bottom of an operation report was not a boast. It was a seal. It said: *this work passed through competent hands and is hereby certified as having happened properly.*

The army understood this. The army ran on signatures. Every blanket, every biscuit, every death had to be signed into existence by someone with rank enough to make it real, and Harlan Pike had spent thirty years acquiring exactly enough rank to make things real. That was his gift. Not the knife—his hands had gone unsteady two winters ago, though no one dared notice—but the making real. Other men did the work. Pike did the *recording* of it, and between those two acts lay the whole difference between medicine and history.

He was thinking about history that particular evening because of the letter that had come up from the provost's office on the morning boat, forwarded through channels, stamped twice. He read it again by lamplight in his office on the hotel's second floor, formerly the parlor of the establishment, now furnished with a desk, two chairs, a cabinet of instruments, and a lock on the door that Pike himself had installed with his own screwdriver, because trust was a currency best kept out of circulation.

*Be advised,* the letter said, *that a commission of historians attached to the War Department will arrive in your district prior to spring thaw for the purpose of compiling the official record of medical service in the western theater. You will prepare such reports, rosters, and statistical abstracts as may be required. Discrepancies between unofficial accounts and official records are to be regarded as matters of serious concern.*

Discrepancies. Pike set the letter down and looked at it the way a man looks at weather coming over a ridge. He knew what discrepancies were. Discrepancies were nurses who remembered things wrong. Discrepancies were orderlies with grievances. Discrepancies were the natural sediment that collected around any institution, and it was the surgeon's job—the surgeon's *duty*—to see that the sediment never reached the clear water of the record.

Which brought him, as everything lately brought him, to Miss Sterling.

* * *

He had begun watching her in November, and it was not jealousy, whatever the nurses whispered. Jealousy was a small man's disease and Pike had cured himself of small diseases long ago. No—it was professional instinct, the same instinct that told him a wound was turning before the skin showed color. Something in the woman was turning.

She was, he admitted freely, the finest nurse he had ever employed. Her bandages were textbook. Her dosages were exact. She could hold a hemorrhaging man's artery closed with her thumb for twenty minutes without her hand shaking, and she triaged the incoming boats with a speed that had saved him, conservatively, dozens of lives he would otherwise have lost to chaos. When the inspectors came—and they came often, drawn like crows to any large congregation of suffering—Miss Sterling's wards were always the ones that passed.

But she wrote at night.

He had noticed it first in December. Making his rounds after midnight—he slept badly, a legacy of Vienna, or so he told people; the truth was he slept badly everywhere and always had—he had seen lamplight under her door in the old servants' corridor where the nursing staff quartered, and beneath that light, faintly, the sound of a pen. Not the quick scratch of a nurse jotting a pulse rate. A slow, deliberate, repeated sound. The sound of composition.

Nurses did not compose.

He mentioned it to nobody. Instead he began timing his rounds to pass her door, and three times in January he caught the same light, the same sound. Once, passing the common room below, he had seen her come down near dawn with ink on the side of her right hand—a smear along the edge of the palm, the mark of a person who writes long and leans hard—and she had tucked the hand into her apron pocket when she saw him looking, smooth as a card player palming an ace.

Smooth. Too smooth. Incompetent people were easy; they lied badly and you caught them in a week. Competent people were the danger, because competence meant they understood exactly what was at stake.

And what was at stake, with the historians coming, was everything.

Pike poured himself two fingers of the medicinal brandy—he prescribed it to himself nightly, one of the few prescriptions he filled honestly—and considered the matter as a strategist rather than a man. Suppose the nurse was keeping notes. Private notes. Notes on operations, on deaths, on decisions. Notes that might differ from the record in his portfolio. What would she do with them? Nothing, probably. Women kept diaries the way they kept locks of hair; it was sentiment, not sedition. But *probably* was a word that had hanged better men than him, and the letter downstairs used language like *serious concern*, and serious concern had a way of traveling upward until it landed on the head of whoever was standing closest to the ground.

If there was a discrepancy and it reached the commission through some channel he hadn't sealed, the question asked would not be *why does a nurse keep a diary*. The question would be *why does Dr. Pike's record disagree with it*. And then they would pull the thread, and threads, once pulled, did not stop at the first knot. They went back through four hundred operations, through every signature he had ever set against another man's knife-work, through the whole architecture of his career, which was load-bearing in more places than anyone suspected.

He finished the brandy and decided to act preemptively. Preemption was the whole art of command. You did not wait for the infection; you lanced.

* * *

He found her at half past nine that night in Ward Two, which had been the hotel's ballroom and still had its chandelier, hung now with gauze soaked in carbolic instead of crystal. The ward held thirty-two men. Eleven of them would not see February. She was moving down the row with a tray of dressings, and even from the doorway he could see the economy of her movements, nothing wasted, nothing theatrical—the way she turned a man's wrist to check the pulse without waking him, the way she spoke to the delirious ones in a low voice as if they might answer.

"Sterling."

"Doctor." She did not startle. He noted that. Guilty people startled; innocent people startled; she did neither, which meant she had decided in advance how to receive him, which meant she thought about him, which meant he mattered to her calculations. Good. Men were easiest to manage when they knew they were being managed.

"A word." He nodded toward the corridor and walked, not waiting, letting her fall into step behind him because subordinates fell into step. At the window at the hall's end—the one overlooking the frozen river, black ice running to the far shore, the town's lamps doubled in it—he stopped and turned.

"You've been with me since August," he said.

"Since the boats started coming up from Donelson, yes."

"Your work is satisfactory. More than satisfactory. I have said as much in my reports."

"I'm grateful, Doctor."

"Don't be grateful yet." He let the pause sit. Outside, somewhere down the frozen levee, a team of horses strained at something, harness chains clinking, the sound carrying flat across the ice. "I'm going to speak to you plainly, because I find plainness saves time. There is a commission coming. Historians, from the capital. Before the thaw. Their purpose is to fix the official account of this hospital—its operations, its mortality, its conduct. Everything that happened here will be weighed against what was written here. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Something moved behind her eyes—not fear, he was almost disappointed to note. Calculation. "That we should be careful with our records, Doctor?"

"That there is one record." He said it slowly, the way he explained amputation to a man who still hoped otherwise. "One. Kept by me. Countersigned by the adjutant. Anything outside it—private memoranda, personal journals, informal lists—is not merely irregular. Under the regulations governing military hospitals, the unauthorized documentation of official proceedings constitutes—" he had the regulation number by heart, had looked it up twice, wanting the taste of it— "conduct prejudicial to good order. In wartime, Sterling, that word has a shorter name. Sedition."

The word landed. He watched it land. Her face stayed composed but her breath changed—one small catch, quickly mastered, the kind of thing you only saw if you had watched ten thousand men receive bad news and learned where the body confessed first.

"I keep no memoranda, Doctor," she said. "I barely have time to sleep."

"Mm." He smiled, and knew his smile was the kind that made interns check their own pulses. "Then this is merely a general warning, and you may consider yourself fortunate. But let me add a specific case, since generals are abstract." He stepped closer. Carbolic and lamp oil and, beneath it, the cold coming off the glass. "A nurse in Louisville was found last month keeping a private register of patients. Names, regiments, circumstances of death. Some of the entries disagreed with the attending surgeon's reports." He paused. "She was dismissed without character reference. Her belongings were searched. She was detained for eleven days before the provost released her, and no hospital in the department will take her now. All for paper, Sterling. Ink and paper. Imagine what happens to a woman whose papers contain names that officers have chosen to omit."

"Why would officers omit names?" she asked, and her voice was perfectly level, and that was the most damning answer she could have given, because an innocent woman would have said *how dreadful* and meant it.

"Because omission is sometimes mercy and sometimes policy and always above your pay grade," Pike said. "I am offering you protection, if you want it. Bring me anything you're uncertain about—any entry, any question—and I will tell you whether it belongs in the record. That is what I am for. I decide what belongs in the record. That has been my function for thirty years and I have never once been contradicted, because I have never once been wrong about what the record can bear." He straightened his cuffs. "The men in this hospital trust you. The commission will trust me. Between us we can make certain that what happened here is remembered correctly. Correctly, Sterling. Not completely. Correctly. There is a difference, and the difference is civilization."

She looked at him for a long moment. Down on the ice, the horses won their argument with whatever they were hauling, and the chains went quiet.

"I'll remember what you've said, Doctor," she told him.

"I know you will," said Pike. "Memory is your whole profession. Just make sure it's the sanctioned kind."

* * *

He slept better that night than he had in weeks, which should have warned him. Preemption always felt like victory at the hour of its doing.

In the morning he made his rounds with unusual satisfaction, initialing the night's deaths—four, all expected, all properly documented—and pausing at Ward Four, where Corporal Reed sat propped against his pillows reading a newspaper someone had smuggled in, and gave Pike a look of perfect, unreadable courtesy that Pike chose, for his own peace of mind, to interpret as respect.

It was only at noon, crossing the lower hall with his portfolio under his arm, that he saw it: Miss Sterling at the washstand, scrubbing her hands, and on the edge of her right palm—that same place, that leaning-writer's place—a fresh smear of ink, black as a coal seam, going into the water and coming out again, going in and coming out, as if the stain were deeper than skin.

She saw him seeing it. And this time she did not put the hand away.

She held it there, open, inked, between them, and washed it slowly, thoroughly, meeting his eyes across the basin with an expression he would think about later that night, and the next night, and many nights after the historians' boat came up the river: not defiance, exactly. Defiance he knew how to break.

It was patience. And patience, in Pike's long experience, was the one quality that no regulation in the army had ever figured out how to forbid.
