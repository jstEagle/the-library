## 1. The Ledger

The cold came up through the floorboards first, then through the boots, then through the wool of the socks beneath them, and by midnight Sebastian Voss had stopped believing in warmth as anything more than a rumor told by other men's memories.

The casualty clearing station at Vermandovillers had been a sugar refinery once. He could still smell it under everything else — the ghost of sweetness baked into the brick walls, faint and absurd, like a joke someone had made before the war and no one had laughed at since. Now the great vats were gone and the long room was filled instead with iron cots set so close together that a nurse could pass down the aisle only sideways, hip brushing blanket after blanket, each one hiding some portion of a man that France had decided to take.

Thirty-one of them tonight. The ambulances had come in at dusk in a chain, engines screaming, mud frozen to their flanks in plates like armor, and the orderlies had carried the wounded in on stretchers held level with the kind of care men gave to things they expected to break. Sebastian had stood at the receiving end with his sleeves already rolled, and he had counted them as they passed — not because anyone asked him to count, but because counting was the first thing he could do for them. A number was a shape. It held a man before anything else did.

"Voss." Sister Bernarde, the French matron who ran the night shift with the efficiency of a woman who had buried two husbands and a son and had decided, somewhere in all that, that grief was best administered intravenously. "Bed four is bleeding again. Bed nine wants morphine and won't say so. And the adjutant has been round asking after your paperwork."

"The adjutant," Sebastian said, "can ask the dead for their paperwork."

"He would, if the dead were listed."

He looked at her. She looked back with the flat patience of a woman who had said worse things in worse places, and then she moved off down the aisle toward bed four, her skirts stiffening with cold as she went, and Sebastian understood that she had not been scolding him. She had been telling him, in her way, that she knew. That she had always known. That the knowing was safe with her the way a match is safe in a closed tin.

* * *

Bed nine was a gunner from Yorkshire called Appleyard, twenty years old if he was a day, with a wound through the meat of his shoulder that the surgeons had already dressed twice. He lay with his jaw set and his eyes fixed on the roof beams as though the pain were an argument he intended to win by refusing to acknowledge it existed.

"I'm told you want something," Sebastian said, drawing up the stool.

"Don't want nothing."

"Morphine, I'm told. By someone with better sense than you."

Appleyard's mouth twitched. "It's not — it's not so bad."

"It's bad enough that you're lying about it in front of a professional liar-taker. Give me your arm."

The boy gave him the arm. While the needle did its slow work Sebastian held his wrist, feeling the pulse come down from its gallop, and watched the boy's eyes lose their fixed, defensive stare and go soft and wondering, the way eyes did when the body finally agreed to stop fighting itself.

"They said we'd be relieved Tuesday," Appleyard murmured. "They said."

"I know what they said."

"You're writing it down, are you? What they said?"

Sebastian went still. The boy's eyes had drifted half-closed, but there was a thread of attention in them still, fine as wire, and it was pointed directly at the notebook in Sebastian's breast pocket — the small black ledger whose corner showed just above the pocket's edge, because Sebastian had grown careless, or tired, or both.

"What do you mean?" Sebastian asked carefully.

"Tommy Weller saw you. Back at Corbie. Writing names in a little book when the officer was done with his list. Tommy said you wrote his brother's name down even though the captain said the returns were closed." Appleyard's voice was slurring now, gentled by the drug, drifting toward sleep. "Said you wrote it like it mattered."

Sebastian sat with that for a moment. Around them the ward breathed — the wet rattle of a chest wound in bed three, the low delirium of bed eleven calling for his mother in a dialect Sebastian half-knew, the ordinary machinery of dying running at its nighttime speed.

"It matters," Sebastian said.

But Appleyard was already gone, pulled under by the morphine, his hand slack in Sebastian's grip. Sebastian laid the arm back on the blanket with the care one gives to borrowed things, and moved on down the aisle.

* * *

At two o'clock the shelling started again, far off to the east, a sound less like noise than like weather — a long rolling pressure that came through the walls and rattled the glass in the high windows and made the candle flames lean all in one direction, as if the whole room were turning its face away. The men who could hear it heard it. The ones who couldn't, didn't. There was no way to tell which was the greater mercy.

Sebastian worked. That was the whole of it, most nights: he worked. He changed dressings that came away soaked and heavy as wet sand. He fed broth to men whose jaws were wired or shattered, spoon by patient spoon, wiping chins, apologizing for the coldness of the food as though coldness were his personal failing. He held basins. He held hands. He wrote letters dictated by men who would dictate six lines and then say, *that's enough, don't tell her the rest, don't tell her how it looks now* — and Sebastian would write only the six lines, and keep the rest folded inside himself where such things accumulated, layer upon layer, like silt.

And between patients, in the gaps the night left him — five minutes here, ten there, standing at the little table by the stove where the paraffin heater made its small stubborn argument against February — he opened the black ledger and he wrote.

The rules of it had never been written down anywhere; they lived in him the way his own name lived in him. An officer's report recorded what served the record. A casualty return closed when the clerk's pen lifted. But a man was not a line item, and a death was not a closure, and somewhere in the second winter of the war Sebastian had understood that the difference between those two facts was going to have to be kept alive by somebody, by hand, in secret, in a book small enough to fit in a breast pocket over the heart.

So: thirty-one names tonight.

Not all of them dead. Some were simply *unlisted* — the wounded transferred out in chaos, the missing presumed, the men whose units had been annihilated so thoroughly that there was no one left to report them. Private Ellis, bed fourteen, who had lain unconscious for six hours while his battalion's records burned with the dugout they'd been kept in, so that officially he did not exist until Sebastian found his paybook in his shirt and copied the name out letter by letter. Sergeant Mbala, bed nineteen, a Senegalese tirailleur whose regiment's returns were kept in a language and a system the British clerks couldn't be troubled with, and who had gripped Sebastian's sleeve and said, in careful schoolroom English, *my mother does not know where I am* — and Sebastian had written the name, and the regiment, and the village near Dakar, and the words *wounded, living, treated, will write again.*

And the dead. Always the dead. Lieutenant-Colonel Ashworth's list that evening had named eleven killed from the 2nd Battalion; Sebastian, going cot to cot with the stretcher-bearers' verbal tally still ringing in his ears, had made it thirteen. Two men lost in the arithmetic — one blown beyond recognition, one dead on the table before anyone thought to ask his name, and the orderly who might have known it sent back to the line before morning. Their names existed now in exactly one place in the world: a black notebook, sewn into no pocket but carried always against Sebastian's chest, written in a hand kept deliberately small and plain, without flourishes, because flourishes could be remembered and plainness could be denied.

*Private T. Weller,* he wrote, and stopped.

Weller. Tommy Weller, of Corbie gossip and Appleyard's drugged testimony — the man whose brother's name Sebastian had supposedly written down when the returns were closed. Sebastian turned back three pages, to a night in November, and found it: *Pte. Albert Weller, 2nd Btn., k. in action 14 Nov., unreported — witness: Pte. T. Weller (bro.).* He had written it because Tommy Weller, gray-faced and shaking, had caught his arm in the corridor and said, *they've closed the list, sir says the list is closed, but he's my brother, somebody has to know he happened.* And Sebastian had known — the way you know a thing in your spine before your mind consents to it — that *somebody has to know he happened* was the truest sentence spoken in that corridor all week.

He added Tommy's own name now, in pencil first, then ink: *Pte. T. Weller, wounded, shoulder, stable condition, Vermandovillers CCS.* Not because the army didn't know where Tommy Weller was. Because one day, God willing, someone would want to know, and the wanting should not go unanswered.

That was the whole philosophy of it, if it deserved the word philosophy. Dignity, Sebastian had come to believe, was not a grand thing. It was not medals or speeches or the terrible beautiful language of the dispatches. Dignity was a measure of weight: it was what a man could carry, and what could be carried *of* him, after. A name weighed almost nothing. Ink weighed nothing. A page weighed nothing. And yet a mother in a terraced house in Sheffield, receiving a piece of paper that said her son had existed — had been seen, had been tended, had died among people who learned his name before the end — that paper would have to be carried for the rest of her life, and it would be lighter than the alternative, which was silence. Sebastian could not stop the war. He could not even, most nights, stop the bleeding. But he could make sure that what remained to be carried was the truth, in full, however heavy, rather than the record's convenient fraction of it.

He blew on the wet ink. The stove ticked. Somewhere eastward the shelling rolled on like a tide that had forgotten the shore.

* * *

Toward dawn the worst of it came — the hemorrhage in bed four that Sister Bernarde had predicted, the surgeon fetched stumbling from his bunk, the long grim theater of pressure and ligature and the particular silence a ward falls into when everyone in it understands what is happening at the far end of the room. Sebastian assisted, holding retractors, handing instruments into a palm that never had to look, and when it was over the surgeon said nothing at all, which was its own kind of verdict, and they covered the man's face and drew the screen.

The orderly on duty was new — a boy called Prewitt who had arrived three days ago from a base hospital and still moved like a guest. He hovered at Sebastian's elbow, looking at the shape under the blanket.

"Does he — do we know who he was? For the report, I mean. The adjutant said—"

"I know who he was," Sebastian said.

"But the returns—"

"Give me an hour, Prewitt. Then bring me whatever form they want filled in, and I'll fill it in. Twice, if they like."

The boy blinked at him, sensing something underneath the words without being able to name it, and went off to fetch the forms. Sebastian stood a moment by the screened bed. Under the canvas the dead man's hand lay outside the blanket, and Sebastian took it — cold already, astonishingly cold, cold the way the floor was cold, the way everything was cold except the small heat of living work — and held it while he recited the name silently, once, twice, a third time, the way you'd fix a knot.

Then he went back to the stove, opened the ledger to a fresh line, and wrote:

*Pte. Harold Finch, 2nd Btn., d. 0330, wounds received 21 Feb. Died in surgery, conscious at the end, spoke of his garden. Reported: no. Witnessed: yes.*

Below it, the thirtieth name. Below that, the thirty-first — a Canadian, transferred in at midnight with no tags and no voice, identified only by a laundry mark and a photograph of a girl in a locket taped inside his jacket, *name uncertain, believed R. Okafor or Okafu, Cdn. Inf.*, and Sebastian hated the uncertainty of it, hated it like a splinter, and resolved to go back at visiting hour and try again, because *believed* was not good enough, *believed* was the enemy's favorite word.

Thirty-one entries. He read them over once, top to bottom, the way a priest reads a confession backward, and then he closed the book and pressed it flat between his palms for a moment — a gesture that had become, without his ever deciding it, a kind of blessing — and slid it into his breast pocket, where it settled over his heartbeat like a second, smaller heart.

Outside the high windows the sky had gone the color of dishwater, and the frost on the glass was beginning to fail in slow creeping rivulets, and the first ambulance of the morning could be heard distantly, its engine note thin and complaining across the frozen fields. Another day. Another chain of stretchers. Another set of returns that would close too soon and hold too little.

Sister Bernarde appeared at his shoulder with two cups of chicory, steam rising off them like souls.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"No."

"And the book grew fatter."

Sebastian wrapped his hands around the cup and let the heat hurt. "Thirty-one," he said.

She nodded slowly, and for a moment her face, which was usually a locked door, showed him something old and tired and, unexpectedly, kind. "In my parish," she said, "when someone died, the bell rang for them. Every soul got its bell. Even the poorest. Even the stranger passing through." She drank. "There is no bell here. So you ring it yourself, in ink. I understand this."

"Yes," Sebastian said. "That's it exactly."

"Hm." She set down her cup. "The adjutant is coming back at nine. He is asking why your ward reports never match the battalion returns. He used the word *discrepancies*." Her eyes met his, flat and unreadable and, beneath the flatness, warning. "You should know that discrepancies, in this army, are noticed."

After she had gone, Sebastian stood alone by the failing stove with the chicory cooling in his hands, and felt the ledger's small weight against his chest, and thought about the word *noticed*, and about how many nights a secret could survive being noticed, and about the strange arithmetic of courage — how much of it was spent in vast public agonies, and how much of it was spent quietly, page by page, by a man with cold hands writing down names in the dark.

He finished the chicory. He went back to work.

And it was three days later, in the Red Cross parcel queue, that the world tilted — three days later, when a volunteer thrust a battered cardboard box into his arms and said, "Yours, Voss, from England, someone's been busy knitting you things by the look of it" — and Sebastian, opening it there in the freezing corridor among the crates and the bandage bales, pushed aside the socks and the tinned milk and the newspaper wrapping, and found the envelope.

Found the handwriting.

Found, in a hand he knew better than his own — a hand he had last seen making notes in a trench journal eight months ago, a hand belonging to a man whose name Sebastian himself had written into the black ledger in the section reserved for the confirmed dead —

*Lieutenant E. Hale.*

Alive. Writing. Asking him, in seven hurried lines, to come to a workshop on the Marne.

*Before the ice breaks.*
