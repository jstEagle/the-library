## 1. The Weight of Water

The first rule Noor Thornbury had learned at Halvard's river station was that ice does not care what your name is. It cracks under a factor and a diver alike; it takes fingers from the careless and breath from the foolish without asking for a family name or a reference. This had been, in its way, a comfort. On the river, everyone was equal in the eyes of the cold.

The second rule was learned the same winter: silence weighs less than speech. A word spoken could be recorded against you. A silence could not be entered into any ledger, because no one knew it existed.

Noor had become very good at silences. Seven years good.

* * *

Morning came to the station the way it always did in the last weeks of ice season — greyly, grudgingly, through windows crusted with frost so thick they turned the world outside into something seen through old glass. The bell above the weighing house rang six times, and Noor was already awake, already dressed, already sitting on the edge of the narrow bunk with boots laced, because a clerk-diver who made the others wait was a clerk-diver who got remembered, and being remembered was dangerous.

The bunkhouse smelled of tallow, wet wool, and the particular sourness of men who slept four to a room and dreamed loudly. Tam Voss, the youngest of the divers, was still snoring in the corner like a man arguing with God and losing. Noor stepped over his outstretched feet, took their coat from its peg, and let themselves out into the blue-black cold.

The river lay under its skin of ice, vast and pale and faintly luminous even before dawn, as though it kept its own small light down there among the mussels. Somewhere beneath that white ceiling were the pearls — river pearls, small and often misshapen, but pearls enough that the Halvard Trading Company had built a station here, and a weighing house, and a company store that charged its own workers for lamp oil, and barracks, and a chapel nobody used except when an inspector came south and wanted to see piety.

Noor crossed the yard with their head down and their hands in their sleeves. The cold found every seam in a coat; it always did. But there was a pleasure in this hour too, if a person knew where to look for it — the yard empty, the sky going from black to iron to the faintest bruise of rose at the eastern tree line, the whole station holding its breath before the day began. For ten minutes, the place belonged to Noor alone, and Noor allowed themselves the luxury of walking slowly.

Then the bell finished ringing, and the hour ended, and Noor walked quickly instead.

* * *

The weighing house was the warmest building at the station, which is to say it was merely cold rather than cruel. A stove stood in the corner, fed by peat bricks that Master Corben counted out himself each morning with the tenderness other men reserved for coin. Noor arrived first, as always, and lit the lamps, and laid out the scales, and set the day's ledger open to a fresh page with the date written in their own careful hand: *14th of March, 1847. Ice season, final week.*

Fourteen days, perhaps, before the thaw. Then the last ship would come upriver — or rather, the last ship would leave, carrying the season's pearls south to the markets, and carrying anyone lucky enough to hold a berth. Noor did not think about berths. Thinking about berths was a way of thinking about leaving, and leaving was a door Noor kept firmly shut in their mind, painted over, nailed fast.

The divers came in just after seven, stamping snow off their boots, cheeks scalded red from the morning's cutting of new holes in the ice. They brought their finds in oiled cloth bundles, and one by one they set them on the counter before Noor, and one by one Noor opened each bundle and weighed each pearl and wrote each weight in the ledger while the diver watched.

This was the part of the work Noor loved, though they would have bitten off their own tongue before admitting it. There was a rightness to it. A pearl went on the scale; the needle settled; the number went in the book. The pearl did not argue. The scale did not lie. The book did not change its story overnight. After seven years of watching men say one thing and do another, Noor had come to treasure objects that behaved.

"Big one today," said Tam Voss, unwrapping his bundle with the exaggerated casualness of a boy pretending not to hope. He was nineteen and built like a door, all shoulders, with a gap in his grin where a fist had found him two winters ago. "Come on, Thornbury. Tell me I can buy my mother a stove."

Noor opened the cloth. Inside lay eleven pearls, and one of them — round, cream-white, the size of a marrowfat pea — sat among its crooked sisters like a duchess at a fish market.

Noor weighed it twice, because the first time the needle had seemed too generous, and then a third time because Tam was watching with his heart in his face and Noor wanted to be certain before saying anything at all.

"Forty-one grains," Noor said. "And clean. That's a stove, Voss. That might be a stove and a chimney."

Tam whooped loud enough to startle the lamp flames, and the other divers laughed and thumped him, and for a moment the weighing house was full of noise and warmth that had nothing to do with the stove. Noor wrote the numbers in the ledger and felt something in their chest ease by a degree — not happiness exactly, but the next thing to it, the thing happiness left behind when it couldn't stay.

"There," said Old Marta, the only woman still diving at her age, easing herself onto the bench across the counter. She was sixty if she was a day, with hands like tree roots and a voice like gravel poured slow. "You smiled."

"I did not."

"You did. Little one. Like a crack in a jug." She pushed her bundle across. "It won't kill you, Thornbury. Smiling."

"It's not in my contract."

Marta snorted and began unwrapping her pearls herself, because she'd decided years ago that Noor's rules were a kind of entertainment provided free of charge. "Contract says dive, weigh, obey. Doesn't say don't smile. I've read it. I had your friend read it to me twice."

"My friend."

"The pretty one at the company office. The one who writes letters for people." Marta's eyes glinted. "Imogen Halvard."

"She's not my friend. She's the founder's granddaughter, and she writes letters for divers because her grandfather thinks it looks charitable."

"Mm." Marta laid out her pearls, a poor handful, small and grey as hailstones. "And yet she waited twenty minutes past closing Tuesday so you could check her arithmetic on the quarterly tallies. Charitable indeed."

Noor said nothing, which was rule two, and Marta laughed at the nothing, which was Marta.

* * *

Master Corben arrived at half past eight, as he always did, bringing the cold in with him like a dog on a lead. He was a long man, dry as a September stalk, with a face that seemed assembled for disappointment and a voice pitched precisely between boredom and threat. He hung his coat, warmed his hands at the stove without appearing to notice it was warm, and looked over the ledger.

"Voss," he said, reading upside down with the ease of thirty years' practice. "Forty-one grains. He'll be insufferable."

"He'll be buying his mother a stove," Noor said.

"He'll be drinking half of it at the store and losing the rest at cards, and in the spring she'll have a stove and he'll have debts." Corben closed the ledger. "Which is the natural order of things. You've the tally sheets ready?"

"Ready."

"And the quarterly?"

"Drafted. Waiting your review."

Corben nodded once, which from Corben was applause. He turned to go, then stopped, in the doorway, in the way he sometimes stopped, and Noor felt the familiar tightening behind the ribs, the feeling of weather changing.

"Thornbury." He didn't turn around. "The inspector from the southern office will be up before the ship sails. Company business. He'll want to see personnel records."

"Yes, Master Corben."

"He'll want to see *family* records." Now he turned, and his face was carefully blank, which meant it was not blank at all underneath. "Indenture papers list next of kin. Yours lists—" he paused, delicately, the way a man steps around a hole in the ice, "—a brother. An Elias Thornbury. Currently employed as factor at the Graywater post."

The weighing house had gone quiet. The divers were very interested in their gloves.

"That's what the paper says," Noor said.

"That's what the paper says," Corben agreed. "But papers are one thing and persons are another, and inspectors are paid to confuse the two. If he asks you about your family, Thornbury, what will you tell him?"

That he sold me. That I haven't spoken to him in seven years. That the last time I saw him he was signing his name to my indenture and wouldn't look at me while he did it, and that I have built a whole life out of not looking at that memory, and it holds, mostly, the way ice holds, until it doesn't.

"I'll tell him I'm an orphan," Noor said, "raised by an aunt, with no family connections relevant to company business."

Corben studied them for a long moment. Whatever he was looking for, he apparently didn't find it, or found enough of it, because he nodded again and left, letting in a knife of cold air and letting it shut away behind him.

Marta exhaled through her nose. "He's protecting you," she said quietly, "or measuring you. With Corben it's always hard to tell which."

"Both," Noor said. "It's both. It's always both."

They went back to the pearls. But the words *Elias Thornbury* stayed in the room like smoke, sinking into wool, into hair, impossible to air out.

* * *

Midday meal was barley soup and black bread, eaten standing up in the mess because sitting cost time and time was the company's. Noor ate mechanically, half-listening to the talk around them — Tam retelling his pearl to a fresh audience, someone complaining about the price of lamp oil, someone else claiming the thaw would come early this year because the crows had come back wrong.

"Crows know," insisted Piet, the oldest diver after Marta. "Crows read the river better than any of us. Early thaw, I'm telling you. Three weeks, maybe less."

"Three weeks," said Tam. "Then the ship comes and I'm rich."

"Then the ship *leaves*, you mean, and takes the pearls with it, and we're exactly as rich as we were, which is to say not."

There was laughter, easy and worn smooth by repetition. Noor ate and said nothing and thought about the ship. Every year it was the same: the last vessel of the season, riding low with the year's harvest, and berths aboard it bought and bartered for months in advance. Divers talked about berths the way drowning men talked about shore. Nobody at the station had family money. Nobody had a berth waiting. It was a shared dream, and sharing it made it bearable.

Except that this year, Noor had heard — through the careful listening that was rule three, *hear everything, repeat nothing* — that there would be only one berth sold to station staff this spring. One. And the price quoted was more than a diver earned in three seasons.

One berth. Noor turned the fact over and put it away, the way they put everything away, in the locked room at the back of the mind where the memory of Elias's signature lived.

* * *

Afternoon was tally sheets and lamplight. Noor worked alone in the back office, adding columns of figures that had to match columns in three other books, and finding, as always, that they matched, because Noor checked them the way a diver checks a line before descending — twice, and then once more for the river's sake.

It was during this quiet work that Imogen Halvard appeared, as she appeared most weeks, with a sheaf of letters needing verification and an expression that suggested she'd invented a reason to come and hoped no one would examine it too closely.

"Quarterly cross-references," she said, setting the papers down. "From the office."

"We did those Tuesday."

"These are additional ones." She pulled off her gloves finger by finger, unhurried, and Noor noticed — because Noor noticed everything, it was rule four, *notice everything, admit nothing* — that her hands were unsteady. Not much. Just at the fingertips. "Also, I wanted—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a courier coming up the ice road tomorrow. From the south. Company post, sealed. It goes to Master Corben, but sometimes things get misdirected, and I thought you should know a courier's coming, because you always know couriers mean extra work, and extra work means—"

"Extra hours," Noor said. "Yes. Thank you."

They looked at each other across the desk. Lamplight, ledger lines, the tick of the stove cooling. Imogen Halvard was twenty-three years old and ran the pearl house with a competence that scandalized her grandfather's factors, and she was the closest thing Noor had to a friend, and neither of them had ever said so, because some things named too early break.

"Aunt Sefi sent up smoked fish," Imogen said, more lightly. "From your aunt. She sends it addressed to the whole weighing house, but somehow it's always the pieces you like best that end up near your plate."

"My aunt believes in feeding people strategically."

"Your aunt," said Imogen, "is the finest strategist on this river, and I include the directors." She gathered her gloves, hesitated at the door. "Noor. If a letter ever came here with your name on it — your real name, the whole name — would you want to know? Or would you want it burned unread?"

The question landed strangely, heavily, like a stone dropped through thin ice. Noor looked up. Imogen's face was composed, pleasant, professionally blank — and underneath it, something worried.

"Why would a letter come with that name?" Noor asked. "Nobody's used that name in seven years."

"No reason," Imogen said. "A hypothetical. Clerks deal in hypotheticals, don't they?" And she was gone, boots ringing on the frozen path, before Noor could ask anything further.

* * *

Evening. The walk back across the yard under a sky salted with stars, breath hanging, boots crunching. In the bunkhouse, Tam was already asleep and dreaming his arguments with God. Noor lay on the narrow bunk and listened to the river.

Under the ice, the water moved all night, as it always did — a deep, patient sound, less a noise than a presence, the sound of something enormous going somewhere without hurry. Seven years ago, on the night Noor arrived at the station, that sound had terrified them. It sounded like the world forgetting you. Now it was the sound Noor fell asleep to the way other people fell asleep to a mother's voice, and Noor tried not to think about what that meant, about what it meant that a river had replaced a family, about how a person could get so used to being alone that loneliness stopped registering as pain and became simply the temperature of a life.

Rules, Noor reminded the dark. Rule one: ice doesn't care. Rule two: silence weighs less than speech. Rule three: hear everything. Rule four: notice everything. Rule five, the oldest one, learned from Aunt Sefi before any of the others: *keep something of your own that no one can take.* For Sefi it had been recipes, songs, the names of dead friends recited like a rosary. For Noor it was the numbers — every figure in every ledger, held in the head, untouchable.

Noor was still reciting columns to themself, the nightly litany, when sleep finally took them — and did not hear, far off across the ice road, the faraway jingle of harness and the muffled drum of hooves: a courier riding north through the dark, carrying the sealed letter that would arrive tomorrow, addressed to a name Noor hadn't spoken in seven years.

*The Thornbury heirs.*
