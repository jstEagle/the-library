## 1. The Weight of Paper

There is a particular sound a pen makes when it finishes a map. Not the scratch of the line itself — that you hear for months, years, until it lives behind your eyelids — but the small, wet click of the nib lifted away at the end, as if the ink itself were reluctant to let go. I heard it this morning, just past dawn, with the harbor fog still pressed against the workshop windows like a creditor who hadn't yet learned we had nothing left to take.

The last plate was the estuary of the Vell. Forty-one days of hatching, forty-one days of my hand cramping into a shape I suspect it will keep forever, and then one final stroke along the sandbar where the river gives up its argument with the sea. Master Edvard would have taken three days over that sandbar. He'd have gone out in a rowboat in weather that would shame a drowned man, sounding the channel himself, because he said no surveyor should draw water he hadn't argued with personally. I drew it from his notes instead. His figures were so precise they felt like handwriting — which, I suppose, is exactly what they were.

I set down the pen and flexed my fingers and listened.

Beyond the harbor, the war made its opinion known. A dull thud, then another, spaced out with the patience of artillery crews who have all winter and nothing better to do. The shells weren't falling on Verenholt — not today, not quite — but on the ridge roads north of the city, where the Ninth Army was learning, again, that hills are expensive. The windows shivered. The ink pots on their shelf rattled faintly, a little chorus of tiny bells, and then went quiet.

Three months since Edvard died. Ninety-four days, if you count the way I count, which is obsessively and without mercy.

They called it a fall. He was found at the bottom of the stair outside the survey office on Kessel Street, his satchel beside him, his neck broken, an evening's rain just beginning to pool under him. The watch took statements from two witnesses who swore they'd seen nothing, which is the most anyone ever swears in this city. The guild sent flowers and a card signed by eleven men whose signatures I know by heart, because I've forged most of them at one time or another for practice, and two of them were shaking.

Edvard Lockridge did not fall down stairs. Edvard Lockridge descended stairs the way other men descend thrones — slowly, with dignity, and only when he chose to. He was sixty-eight years old and had spent fifty of those years walking cliff edges in wind with a theodolite on his back. His feet knew stone the way priests know scripture. And he'd been agitated for weeks before he died, muttering about "corrections" coming back from the Ministry with numbers that didn't survive arithmetic, about surveys returned to him annotated by men who had never stood on the ground they were annotating.

"They're drawing the peace before the war ends," he told me once, in November, jabbing his pipe at the window as though the Ministry kept offices out there among the gulls. "Mark me, girl. Borders are decided by whoever holds the pen when the shooting stops. Everything else is theater."

I thought he was being dramatic. He was seventy percent drama by volume; it came with the profession. Now I would give a great deal — my hands, my eyesight, several years of my life I wasn't using anyway — to hear him be dramatic again.

* * *

The atlas lay open on the great table, all fourteen plates fanned out in their final sequence, and I will confess something here that I confessed to no one at the time: I was afraid to close it.

An atlas is a strange kind of corpse. Everything the man was is in it — his patience, his stubbornness, his fury at sloppy lettering, his habit of drawing coastlines with a tenderness he never once showed a living person — and yet it isn't him at all. It's what he left when he went. You can stand over it the way I stood over his coffin, in the guild chapel, surrounded by cartographers pretending not to check each other's work on the memorial plaque, and think: *this is not enough*. This cannot be everything. There must be more somewhere, in a drawer, in a folio, in a pocket.

There wasn't. There was a workshop, an apprentice, and fourteen plates, and now the plates were done.

I walked the table the way I'd walked it a hundred times, checking. The coastal series, first through sixth, Verenholt harbor to the Salt Cliffs, every sounding corrected against Edvard's own field books. The inland sheets, seven through ten, the river country where the fighting had chewed up the roads and our instruments had measured the damage nobody official wanted measured. Eleven and twelve, the disputed marches — the very ground the armistice commissioners would soon be arguing over at Lake Maren, drawn in Edvard's hand with a precision that could end arguments or start them, depending on who read it. Thirteen, the index of place names, half of them restored to their old forms because Edvard believed maps should remember what people preferred to forget.

And fourteen. My plate. The estuary. The sandbar.

My name wasn't on any of it. Of course it wasn't. An apprentice signs nothing; an apprentice is a pair of hands attached to a master's reputation, and when the master dies, the hands are expected to finish the work and then, politely, become invisible. The title page read: *A General Atlas of the Marches and Maritime Coasts, surveyed and drawn by Edvard Lockridge, Master Cartographer of the Guild of Verenholt.* Underneath, smaller: *Completed by his apprentice.*

No name. Just the function. I had been inking myself out of existence for six years and hadn't noticed until now.

Outside, another shell landed, farther off, and the fog swallowed the sound halfway to us. Somewhere below in the street a fishmonger was shouting prices that had doubled twice since autumn, and someone was laughing, because people do, even here, especially here. Verenholt has been besieged, bombed, blockaded, and burned twice in living memory, and its citizens respond to each catastrophe by adjusting their complaints about fish prices. There is a lesson in that, probably. I've never had the leisure to learn it.

* * *

The guild delegation arrived at noon, which meant they'd waited until a decent hour to ruin my morning properly.

There were three of them: Alderman Voss, whose girth preceded him up our stairs like a herald; Master Quennel, who taught projection theory and had the personality of a damp theorem; and a young clerk whose name I never learned and whose function, I gathered, was to stand near the door and look administrative.

"Astrid." Voss removed his hat, revealing the bald patch he usually pretended didn't exist. "You've finished."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway. "This morning. The final plate needs mounting and the binding assembled. Four days' work."

"Two," said Voss.

"The binding alone—"

"The Ministry requires delivery within the week. There are... considerations." He said the word the way men say *tides* or *weather*, as though it were a natural force rather than a decision made by people with names. "The armistice commission convenes at Lake Maren in five weeks. The Ministry wishes the Lockridge atlas entered into the official record. It will be a great honor to your master."

I looked at Quennel, who studied the ceiling beams with the intensity of a man hoping to find God or an exit up there. Quennel had trained under Edvard too, thirty years ago, before some falling-out neither of them ever explained. If anyone in that room might have told me the truth, it was him. He examined the rafters as though they owed him money.

"Honor," I repeated.

"Entered into the record," Voss said, "the atlas becomes the authoritative survey. Every boundary, every sounding, fixed under guild seal. Your master's life's work, Miss Lockridge, enshrined."

Miss Lockridge. I am no relation to Edvard; apprentices sometimes take the master's name informally, the way soldiers take a regiment, and Voss knew perfectly well the distinction. But he said it warmly, deliberately, wrapping me in the name like a coat handed to someone standing in the cold. *You are his legacy. Deliver the book. Be the legacy.*

"The binding takes four days," I said, "if it's done properly. Edvard never bound anything quickly. He said a bad binding ruins a good map faster than fire does."

Something crossed Voss's face at the word *fire* — quick, professional, gone. "Three days, then. The Ministry will send a courier on Friday morning. You'll want everything ready."

They didn't stay for tea. They never do. At the door, Quennel paused, half-turned, and for one absurd moment I thought he was going to say something real. What he actually said was: "Mount the plates on rag stock. The Ministry archives are damp."

Then they were gone, and their footsteps faded down the stairs, and I was alone with fourteen finished maps and a deadline and the distant, patient thudding of a war that intended, apparently, to conclude itself whether or not anyone told it the truth.

* * *

That night I worked late on the binding. This is the part of the craft I love best, though I rarely admit it: sewing the sections, pasting the spine, building the false backing board that gives an atlas its flat, dignified spine — the architecture that makes a sheaf of papers into an object of authority. People trust a well-bound book more than they trust a witness. Edvard taught me that. He also taught me how the false backing works: a second board laid over the spine, hollowed slightly, so the sewing doesn't show and the corners sit square. Every atlas in the guild library has one. It is the least secret secret in the trade.

I was trimming the backing board to fit — knife along the straightedge, the blade whispering through the grey board — when the knife caught.

Not snagged. Caught. There was something under the board's outer skin, something that turned the blade, and I stopped, annoyed, assuming a flaw in the stock, a splinter of wood pulp, a bubble in the paste.

I ran my thumb along the underside of the backing. And felt it: a thinness, a seam, a rectangle of the board that was not board at all but paper, pasted over and painted to match, invisible unless you were cutting the binding apart — which no one ever does, because bindings are cut apart only when they're destroyed, and destroyed only when—

When someone wants to make certain nothing is hidden inside them.

I sat very still. The lamp hissed. Out past the harbor, the guns had gone quiet for the night, and the silence was worse, the way a held breath is worse than a shout.

Edvard had bound atlases for fifty years. He had built this backing himself — I knew his paste, his brushwork, the particular way he feathered paint to match board grain; I'd have known it blindfolded. He had hidden something in the spine of his own masterwork, in the one object the Ministry was certain to demand, sealed and delivered into their hands.

Or — and the thought arrived cold, uninvited, wearing its own coat — the one object he was certain they would seize.

I picked up the knife again. My hand was steadier than it had any right to be.

I slit the false backing open along the seam, and the board peeled away from the spine like skin, and there, tucked into the hollow where the sewing threads ran, flat and pale and waiting: a letter. Sealed with wax. Bearing his mark — the crossed dividers, pressed deep, the way he sealed things he meant to survive.

Addressed, in his cramped, furious hand:

*To whoever finishes this atlas. God help you, it will be Astrid.*

I did not open it that night. I sat with it in both hands for a long time, feeling its weight, which was nothing, which was everything, while the fog pressed against the glass and the war slept its shallow sleep beyond the harbor.

Then I broke the seal.
