## 2. Instructions to Forget

The rain came down like it had a grudge against Philadelphia specifically, and I was the one holding the umbrella that lost its argument with the wind two blocks back. By the time I pulled the Ford up to the drop on Callowhill Street, my collar was a wet rope around my neck and my cap had gone soft as a boiled hat, which is what a cap does when it's been living a lie too long.

Adeline Marsh did not believe in canceling engagements for weather. "Suffrage doesn't melt, August," she'd said that morning, buttoning her gloves with the crisp authority of a woman who had never once been rained on in her soul. So there we were at nine at night, delivering a sealed packet to a printer who owed the movement a favor and a bill he hoped we'd forget.

I left her at the door under the awning and went to move the car. That was the arrangement. Adeline spoke; I drove; nobody looked twice at the driver, because drivers are furniture with engines. I had built a whole philosophy on being furniture. Furniture doesn't get questioned. Furniture doesn't get searched. Furniture gets to keep its hands in its lap and its secrets in its chest, and if you do it right, you can go years without anybody noticing you have a face at all.

I was wrong about that by midnight.

* * *

The packet delivered, I took the long way home because the long way was empty and empty streets let me think. Ernst Keller's letter was still folded inside my shirt, against my skin, where I'd been carrying it since Tuesday. I know what you're thinking—reading other people's mail is a sin. I've made my peace with it the way you make peace with any hunger: daily, and without pride.

Ernst wrote the way men talk when they've decided honesty is cheaper than pride. He wrote about the promise—the one he and Adeline had shaken on in 1913, back when the war was somebody else's bad dream. *If anything happens to any of us,* the promise went, *the others do not vanish alone.* He wrote that his wife had been questioned at the market. He wrote that he still believed America would come to its senses about people like him, people who had only ever argued for peace in a country that had decided arguing was treason.

He wrote, near the end: *A promise is a debt that keeps its own calendar.*

I had read that line maybe forty times. It kept its calendar all right. And here is the thing about reading other people's futures every night by lamplight: after a while you start believing that's all history is—a thing that happens to people with names in print, while people like me hold the lamp.

The rain hadn't let up when I turned onto Juniper Street. The boarding house sat where it always sat, sagging slightly to the left like a tired horse, Bess Okafor's geraniums drowning in their pots on the stoop. And on the stoop, on the third step, in the rain, was a bundle.

My first thought—and I'm not proud of it—was laundry. Somebody's abandoned laundry. Then the bundle moved, and a small voice said something in German, and my heart dropped through three floors of me and landed somewhere near the basement pipes.

It was a boy. Six, maybe seven. Dark hair plastered flat, a coat two sizes too big with the sleeves rolled four times, sitting very straight the way children sit when they've been told sitting straight is the last instruction they'll get. Pinned to his coat was an envelope, soaked through but legible, weighted down with a safety pin gone orange with rust.

I knelt on the step. Rain ran off my cap brim between us like a curtain.

"Hello," I said, and then, because my German lives mostly in my hands, I held out one. He looked at it, then at my face, and did the arithmetic children do faster than adults—deciding whether I was weather or shelter.

"August," I said. "Ich bin August."

Something in him unclenched half an inch. He let me take the envelope.

I knew the handwriting before I opened it. You read a man's letters forty times, you learn his hand the way you learn a walk through a wall—by the sound of it. The tall ascenders. The way he crossed his t's like he was setting them down gently so they wouldn't wake anyone.

*Forget this face,* the note said. In English, in his careful hand. *His name is Tomas. He is seven. If you are reading this, then everything I feared has happened, and I have no one else to ask. Forgive me. Forget this face until it is safe to remember it.*

No address. No signature. Just the hand, and the hand was Ernst Keller's, and Ernst Keller had been declared an enemy alien eleven days ago, his name printed in the paper between a bank robber and a man who'd shouted the wrong thing at a recruiting station.

*Everything I feared has happened.* I stood there on the step getting rained on, holding a child and a confession, and understood that the calendar of promises had arrived at my door without asking whether I could pay.

* * *

Getting him upstairs was its own small war. Bess's boarding house ran on rules the way a clock runs on springs, and rule one was no visitors past eight, rule two was no questions ever, and rule three existed solely to contradict the first two whenever Bess felt like it. But Bess wasn't downstairs. Her light was out. God and the rain were my only witnesses, and God has never once covered for me.

I walked Tomas up the back stairs with my hand on his shoulder, past Mrs. Pruitt's door—she sleeps with the radio low and wakes at anything—and into my room, where I locked us in and stood listening to my own pulse like it was a stranger in the hall.

Tomas stood in the middle of my floor, dripping, taking inventory. My room is not large. A bed, a chair, a trunk, a lamp, and a window that looks at a wall. He looked at the trunk longest. Children know trunks are where the truth gets put.

"You're wet," I said, uselessly, in English, and then dug out the driest things I owned—a flannel shirt of mine that would fit him like a tent, a blanket from the trunk. He changed behind the screen with the methodical obedience of a child following instructions given earlier by someone who loved him, and when he came out he climbed onto my bed without being asked, which told me whoever gave those instructions had also taught him that beds, when offered, are not refused.

"Danke," he said. Small voice. Steady eyes. Ernst's eyes—I'd never seen Ernst, but I'd read enough of him to know his eyes would be steady and sad and watching everything.

"Tomas," I said, pointing at him. He nodded. I pointed at myself. "August." He frowned at the name, tried it, got it mostly right. Then he pointed at the envelope still clutched in my fist, and said something long and urgent in German, and though I caught maybe one word in five, I caught the tone. It was the tone of a child repeating instructions. *Wait. Stay. Someone will come.*

Nobody was coming. That was the whole point of the note.

I sat on the edge of the bed and did the math I'd been avoiding since the stoop. Ernst had known where I lived. Not Adeline—me. Which meant somewhere in the machinery of those intercepted letters, Ernst had learned about the driver, the one who read everything, the one Adeline trusted with deliveries no sane person should trust to anyone. He had known, and he had chosen, and he had sent his son into the rain to the one person in Philadelphia with the most reasons to say no.

Because saying yes meant hiding an enemy alien's child in a suffragist's orbit during a war. Saying yes meant risking the boarding house, the job, the movement, and—if anyone ever looked closely at the driver—the entire architecture of my second self, the one I'd spent six years building brick by invisible brick.

Invisibility protects. That was my creed. That was the whole church of it. Be furniture, be shadow, be the man nobody remembers, and the world will pass over you like weather.

But there was a child on my bed who had already been forgotten once tonight, on purpose, by the man who loved him most, because forgetting was the only protection left to offer. And the creed didn't have a verse for that. The creed just went quiet, like a clock stopping.

A knock came at the door.

I was across the room before I knew I'd moved, putting my body between the door and the bed like that would mean anything, like flesh stops questions.

"Yes?" I said, in my lowest register, the one I keep oiled for exactly this.

"August?" Mrs. Pruitt's voice, frayed with curiosity. "There was a boy on the steps earlier. Bess wants to know if you saw him when you came in."

Bess wanted to know. Which meant Bess already knew more than she was saying, which meant Bess had seen me carry a child up her back stairs in the rain and had gone to bed anyway, filing the information away in whatever drawer she kept for things that might burn the house down.

"No," I called back. "Didn't see anyone."

A pause. The pause of a woman deciding whether to spend a question or save it.

"Well," said Mrs. Pruitt. "If you hear crying in the night, that's cats. There's been cats."

"There's been cats," I agreed, and listened to her slippers shuffle away.

I turned around. Tomas was watching me from the bed, blanket to his chin, and he had heard every word—he understood English better than he spoke it, I'd bet the car on it—and he was looking at me with the particular gravity of a child who has just watched a grown-up lie on his behalf and is deciding what that makes them both.

"It's all right," I told him, and for once the words weren't furniture. They were load-bearing.

I took off my ruined cap and set it on the table, and in the lamplight, with no audience but a seven-year-old who already knew too much, I let my hair down for the first time in front of another living soul in six years. Not because he'd understand. Because I was tired of lying in a room where a child had been instructed to be forgotten, and I couldn't teach him to hide his face while keeping mine hidden from everyone including myself.

Tomas looked at my hair, then at my face, and did his arithmetic again. Whatever sum he reached, he pulled the blanket higher and closed his eyes, which is the closest thing to a signature a child gives.

I sat up all night with Ernst's letter against my chest and the note in my hand, reading *forget this face* until the words stopped meaning anything, and somewhere around three in the morning I understood that they were never instructions to me at all. They were instructions to himself. Ernst Keller had written down the hardest order he'd ever give, and mailed it with his son, because he trusted a stranger he'd never met to disobey it.

The rain stopped before dawn. Down the hall, a floorboard creaked—Bess, awake early, moving toward my door with the deliberate tread of a woman who had decided, sometime in the night, that rule three was about to earn its keep.

Her knock came soft. Twice. Then, low, through the wood:

"August. Open this door. We need to talk about your nephew."
