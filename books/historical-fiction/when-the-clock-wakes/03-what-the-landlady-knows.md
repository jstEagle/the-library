## 3. What the Landlady Knows

There are three kinds of silence in a boarding house, and I had learned to tell them apart the way a sailor tells weather. There was the silence of people asleep, which is soft and even, like snowfall. There was the silence of people pretending not to listen, which has a texture to it, a held-breath quality that presses against your door. And there was the silence of Mrs. Bess Okafor deciding something, which is the loudest of all.

I had been hearing the third kind for two days.

Tomas slept in my bed, curled small as a comma, and I sat at the table under the lamp with his coat across my knees, turning the note over and over as though the paper might confess something new. *Forget this face.* Ernst's handwriting—those careful, upright letters of a man who had learned English from books and trusted it more than he trusted people. The ink had run where the rain had gotten at it, but not enough. Not nearly enough.

A boy cannot live in a room forever. That was the arithmetic I kept arriving at and kept refusing. Food, in a boarding house, is a public event. A child's cough carries through plaster like a telegram. Sooner or later Bess Okafor would climb those stairs on some pretext—the rent, the lamp oil, the state of my gutters—and she would find what I was keeping, and then everything would depend on what kind of woman she was.

I knew less about her than I pretended. She was maybe fifty, built like the idea of patience, ran the house on Vine Street with rules posted by the door in her own round hand: *No liquor. No gambling. No gentlemen above the first floor.* She collected rents on Saturday and forgave them on Sunday, in that order, always. She had come up from somewhere—I'd heard Maryland, I'd heard the Islands—and she looked at me sometimes with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. Not suspicion exactly. More like a woman reading a letter she's already opened.

On the third morning she climbed the stairs.

I heard her before I saw her, that particular tread, unhurried, and I did the only thing available to me, which was to stand in front of my own bed like a fence around a vacant lot.

"Rent's not due till Saturday," I said.

"I know when rent's due." She came in without being asked, which was her privilege and my catastrophe, carrying a plate of cornbread as though it were a warrant. "Brought the boy some breakfast."

The floor did not open. I checked.

"He's—" I began.

"A nephew," she said. "Your sister's boy, come down from New York while she gets situated. Sickly, poor thing, that's why nobody's seen him. That's the story, if you're going to have one. You may as well have a good one."

"Bess—"

"Mrs. Okafor," she said, setting down the plate, "when you want to call me something friendly. Not before." She looked past me at the bed, at the small shape in it, and her face did something complicated and brief, like weather changing over water. Then she turned back to business. "Nephews need papers. School truant officers don't ask much, but the relief office will, and the man at the corner store asks questions just to hear himself talk. I can write a fair hand. Can you?"

"Can I what?"

"Forge, Mr. Sandoval. Keep up."

I should have been terrified. I was, somewhere underneath, the way you're cold at the bottom of a lake. But mostly what I felt was the strange vertigo of a door opening in a wall I'd believed was solid. "You've known," I said. "How long?"

She picked an imaginary piece of lint off her sleeve. "Since you moved in. You hang your wash on the line like a woman who's done it thirty years and hates every minute of pretending otherwise. Your boots are men's but your feet walk in them like they're borrowed. And last winter, when you had that fever, you called out for your mama in a voice no man ever made." She said it all mildly, the way you'd recite a grocery list. "I say nothing, because whatever you are, you pay on time and you carry my coal up without being asked twice, and because a person's business is their own until it walks into my house wearing a child's face."

"And now?"

"Now it's walked in." She took the note from the table—she didn't ask, she simply lifted it, and I let her, which told us both something—and read it twice. Her jaw set. "German boy," she said. "They're taking them, you know. Not officially. Officially it's registration, it's papers, it's *enemy alien* stamped in red. Unofficially, men get pulled off streetcars in Kensington and don't come home. A German child is a dangerous thing to keep." She folded the note along its existing creases, precisely, and handed it back. "So we'll make him not-German on paper. Colored folks' children all look alike to the people who write the laws—that's the one gift this country gives us, and I intend to spend it."

"That's a terrible thing to say."

"It's a true thing to say. Terrible and true travel together in this town." She was already looking around my room with a carpenter's eye, cataloguing. "Ink. Good ink, not that watery stuff. And a birth certificate wants a mother's name. What do we call his mama?"

I hadn't thought that far. I'd thought in nights, not months. "Marta," I said, before I could stop myself. Ernst's wife. It felt like a theft and a prayer at once.

"Marta Sandoval," Bess repeated, tasting it. "Colored women named Marta are a dime a dozen from here to Baltimore. Fine. And the father?"

Dead, I almost said. Dead men ask no questions. But the lie caught in my throat like a fishbone, because I had spent two years reading Ernst Keller's letters and I knew what he believed about dead men—they were how lies got comfortable. "Gone," I said. "Left."

"Gone is good. Gone explains why the boy flinches at men's voices." She wrote nothing down; she didn't need to. Her memory was a ledger with no erasures. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "August."

My breath stopped. Not Sandoval. August. My name, the one I wore under the other one like skin under skin.

"You sleep loud," she said. "When you dream, you say it. Now I know it too, and you know I know, and the world goes on turning." She stepped into the hall. "Cornbread's on the table. Growing boys eat."

Then she was gone, and Tomas stirred, and I sat down on the edge of the bed and discovered that my hands were shaking—not from fear, or not only from fear, but from the sheer unaccustomed weight of being known and not destroyed. Invisibility had rules. It had never once occurred to me that there might be rules for this too.

* * *

From the kitchen below, Bess Okafor listened to the boards settle overhead and allowed herself, for exactly one minute, the luxury of being afraid.

She had known since the first week, of course. Longer than she'd admitted—since the girl had stood in her parlor applying for the room, hat brim low, voice pitched down into a register that fooled nobody who had raised sons and buried a husband. Bess had taken one look and thought: *there but for the width of a hatband.* She had rented the room anyway, because the rent was honest and the eyes behind the brim were honest-er, and because a woman who passes for a man to earn a living is not so different from a woman who charges board and pretends the roof doesn't leak. Everybody in Philadelphia was passing for something. The whole city was a costume ball where half the guests had forgotten what they'd come as.

But the child changed the arithmetic. Bess stood at her stove and counted costs the way she counted everything: coal, flour, risk. A colored woman hiding a German boy in 1918 was not brave, it was suicidal, and bravery was a luxury she had priced out of her life twenty years ago on a dock in Baltimore. If the agents came—and they would come, eventually, because they always came—if they found the boy, they would find the forged papers, and if they found the papers they would find *her* name on them, her handwriting, her neck in the rope alongside the girl's.

And she had written the name Marta on a scrap of paper in her head already. She had already begun.

That was the thing about kindness, Bess thought, scraping the skillet. Nobody warns you it's a debt. You extend it once and it owns you. She had watched the girl—August, *August*, imagine naming a child that and then burying the name alive—for two years, watching her read other people's mail by lamplight, thinking herself invisible, and Bess had understood the hunger in it because she recognized the shape: the longing to matter to somebody's story without paying admission. Well. The admission had come due. It was a child's face and a forged certificate and two women's necks, and there was no refund.

Upstairs, faintly, she heard the boy laugh. A small sound, startled out of him, the first one.

Bess turned off the burner and stood in the quiet kitchen with her hands flat on the table, and made the second decision of the week, which was larger than the first. If they came for the child, they would find a boarding house full of witnesses and a landlady with a memory like a court stenographer. If they came for the girl, same. She was too old for causes. But she was not too old for family, and family, she had learned, was not something you were born into so much as something you agreed to be burned with.

She went to the drawer where she kept the household accounts, and beneath the accounts, wrapped in oilcloth, she took out the bottle of good ink she'd been saving for a will she kept postponing.

Overhead, footsteps crossed to the window. Below, on Vine Street, a federal motorcar rolled past slow, its occupants reading house numbers the way tax collectors read ledgers.

Bess put the ink in her apron pocket and started up the stairs.
