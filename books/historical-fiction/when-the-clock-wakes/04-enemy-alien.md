## 4. Enemy Alien

I had always believed that a woman's home was her last sovereign territory, and I had been wrong about it the way one is wrong about most comfortable beliefs—thoroughly, and in public.

They came on a Tuesday, which I have since decided is the government's preferred day for ruining people. Mondays are for hangovers and regrets; Fridays, for mercy. Tuesday is the day a man in a good coat knocks on your door holding a list, and you understand that the list was typed weeks ago and your name—or someone's name—has been sitting in a drawer in Washington, waiting for a clerk to finish his coffee.

There were two of them. The tall one introduced himself as Agent Colfax of the Department of Justice, and the short one did not introduce himself at all, which told me more about him than a name would have. Colfax had the manner of a man who had practiced looking apologetic in a mirror and gotten quite good at it.

"Mrs. Marsh. Adeline Marsh? Of the Pennsylvania Woman Suffrage Association?"

"Of several associations," I said. "It's a hobby."

He smiled the way a bank teller smiles when you've overdrawn. "We're conducting a routine survey of registered enemy aliens in the district."

"Routine," I repeated. The word had begun to do strange work that autumn. Routine searches. Routine detentions. A whole architecture of cruelty wearing the sensible shoes of paperwork.

"May we come in?"

I could have refused. I knew the law well enough to know what they could and couldn't demand without a warrant, and I knew also that knowing the law is a luxury that protects you exactly as long as it amuses them. I stepped aside.

* * *

The Marsh house on Delancey Street had been my mother's, then mine, and it had learned over forty years how to absorb visitors—the suffragists who came for strategy and stayed for supper, the printers, the widows, the girls from the mill towns who arrived with blistered feet and unshakable convictions. It was a house built for company. On this particular Tuesday it felt, for the first time, like a house built for hiding something, though I could not have said what.

August took their coats.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I had left by the end of that hour. August Sandoval—my driver, my courier, my quiet shadow of three years—took the agents' coats and hung them with the same unhurried competence he brought to everything, and if I had not known him as long as I had, I would have seen nothing at all. But I had spent three years watching August not-watch things, and so I saw it: the half-second pause before the coat left his hands. The way his eyes went to the shorter agent's satchel and away again, quick as a bird taking water.

Colfax settled into the parlor chair my mother had reupholstered during McKinley's administration and produced his list.

"We're looking for a number of individuals. Most are simply required to update their registration." He smoothed the paper on his knee as though it were a menu. "Ernst Keller."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

You must understand what Ernst Keller was to me—not what he became in the newspapers, but what he had been. He had been a correspondent, first: a German-born machinist with a scholar's handwriting and a pacifist's stubborn heart, writing to me in 1913 because our causes had brushed against each other at some congress or other and he believed, with an earnestness that would have been comic in anyone else, that votes for women and peace among nations were the same struggle wearing different coats. I had thought him naive. Then I had met him—at a lecture, at a supper, once memorably at a train station where he talked me out of throwing my umbrella at a heckler—and I had thought him right.

We had made promises to each other in those years, the way people do when the world still seems like a thing that can be arranged. If anything happens, he'd said, meaning war, meaning madness, meaning the century showing its teeth—if anything happens, my family will not vanish alone. And I had said yes, because in 1913 saying yes cost nothing but breath.

Now Ernst Keller was a name on a federal list, and saying yes might cost me everything I had built.

"He isn't here," I said.

"No, ma'am." Colfax consulted his papers. "His registration shows this address as a former correspondence address. We're confirming contacts. Persons who received mail from Keller after April sixth, nineteen seventeen."

"After the declaration," I said. "You're asking whether I corresponded with an enemy alien after the declaration."

"I'm asking whether you received letters."

"The distinction matters to you, I suppose."

"It matters to the law, ma'am."

"The law," I said, "has had a very busy year."

The short agent shifted by the window. August stood by the door with his hands folded, a servant's posture worn like a coat two sizes too large—I had noticed that about him years ago and filed it under *foreign manners*, which tells you how observant I was being—and I saw, in the lamplight, a small muscle jump in his jaw at the word *letters*. Just once. Just enough.

"Mr. Sandoval," Colfax said pleasantly, turning, and I watched August come back into himself the way a swimmer surfaces. "You handle the household post?"

"Some of it," August said. His voice was level, faintly accented, entirely unremarkable. "Letters come for Mrs. Marsh from everywhere. I sort them."

"And do you recall correspondence from a Keller? German name. Writes a fine hand."

"I couldn't say," August said. "I don't read other people's mail."

It was a perfect answer. It was such a perfect answer that it rang slightly, like a coin tested against the counter, and I found myself wondering—for the first time, but not the last—what else August Sandoval did not read.

* * *

They stayed forty minutes. They asked about the movement's finances, its mailing lists, whether we employed any persons of German birth, whether we had ever hosted members of the People's Council, whether I was acquainted with Emma Goldman (I said I admired her posture), whether the suffrage agitation was in any sense funded from abroad. I answered everything with the cheerful exhaustiveness of a woman who has testified before hostile committees for sport, and by the end Colfax looked like a man who had gone fishing and caught only weather.

At the door, he paused.

"For what it's worth, Mrs. Marsh"—and here the mirror-practiced apology returned—"Keller's situation may resolve itself. These things often do." He tipped his hat. "The government appreciates the cooperation of patriotic women."

When the door closed behind them, the house exhaled. So did I, though I hadn't known I was holding anything.

August took down their coats from the hook and held them a moment too long, as if deciding whether to burn them.

"August," I said.

"Ma'am."

"Do you know Ernst Keller?"

The question hung there. I watched him weigh it—I actually watched the scales move, behind his eyes, in the space of perhaps two seconds—and I understood in that interval that whatever answer came next would be chosen, selected from a shelf of possible answers the way I chose which truths to give reporters.

"Everyone knows of him," August said. "His letters used to come regular. Before the war."

"Used to."

"They stopped." He set the coats down. "Shall I bring the motor around? You'll be late for the committee."

And there it was—the deflection, smooth as a dealt card. Three years I had taken that smoothness for discretion, for the natural reserve of a private man in service. Now, with the door still trembling from the agents' exit, it looked like something else. It looked like practice.

"Yes," I said. "Bring the motor around."

But I stood in the hall a while after he'd gone, doing arithmetic I didn't like.

Here is the sum as I worked it: Ernst Keller, pacifist, now enemy alien, had written to me faithfully until the spring of 1917, and then stopped—not gradually, not fading, but cleanly, the way a man stops when someone has advised him to stop. His last letter had contained a promise renewed, and a request: *if the worst comes, remember what we said.* I had remembered. I had done precisely nothing, because there was nothing to do, because the worst had come for everyone at once and my hands were full of my own fight.

Except now the worst had developed a shape and a street address, and it had knocked on my door wearing a good coat, and it had asked me questions while my own driver flinched at the word *letters* like a man hearing his name called in a language he pretends not to speak.

I want to be honest about what I considered, because honesty is the only currency I have never counterfeited. I considered the cause. I considered the amendment, the referendum, the ten thousand women whose names were attached to ours, the newspapers hungry for any stain—a German lover, a foreign gold rumor, an alien in the woodpile—and I calculated, coldly, in my mother's parlor, whether one machinist's freedom was worth the movement's exposure. I was raised to believe that principle is what you keep when keeping it costs nothing, and that everything else is negotiation. For approximately four minutes, I negotiated.

Then I thought of Ernst at the train station in 1913, laughing at my umbrella, saying: *Frau Marsh, a promise is the only country worth defending.* And I thought: if I abandon him, every speech I have ever given is a costume.

The committee could wait. I went instead to my desk, and I began to write down everything I remembered about Ernst Keller—dates, addresses, the names of his peace circle, the contents of letters I had been careful enough to keep and foolish enough to file—and I resolved to find where they had taken him, and what it would cost to get him out, and to pay it.

I was still writing when August brought the motor around and came back in to tell me, and I noticed then what I should have noticed an hour earlier: that he stood in the doorway of my study like a man standing at the edge of a confession, and that his eyes went, once, quickly, to the stairs—up, toward the third floor, toward the narrow rooms where the servants slept.

"Mrs. Marsh," he said. "There's something you should—"

The front bell rang. Long, urgent, twice—the signal we'd agreed on for trouble.

August and I looked at each other, and for the first time in three years, neither of us was passing.
