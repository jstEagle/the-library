## 6. The Raid on Vine Street

My aunt Greta said the men in gray coats were looking for names, and that a name was like a coat: if you took it off quick enough, nobody could say it was yours.

I did not believe her. I had tried to take my name off once, when Papa went away, and it came back anyway. Names are stitched on the inside where you can't reach.

* * *

It was Tuesday because Tuesdays we ate potato pancakes, and Aunt Greta burned them the way Mama never did, and I liked hers better though I would have been skinned for saying so. The whole street smelled like frying and coal smoke and the wet wool of everybody's coats drying on every railing at once. Vine Street is narrow. Vine Street smells like everything at once because there isn't room for the smells to go anywhere else.

Aunt Greta was humming. She hummed wrong notes on purpose, she said, so the right ones wouldn't get lonely.

Then the automobiles came.

I knew they weren't ordinary automobiles because ordinary ones don't come up our street three abreast, and ordinary men don't step out all at the same time, like they practiced it, like it was a dance nobody wanted to learn. Gray coats. Gray hats pulled down low. One of them had a paper in his hand and he read from it the way Pastor Lindt read the sins of the congregation, slow, so each one could be tasted.

"Keller," he said. "Keller. Brandt. Keller again."

The second time he said Keller I felt it in my stomach, the way you feel a stair that isn't there.

Aunt Greta's hand closed on my shoulder. Her hand is small but her grip is not; she used to carry flour sacks up four flights before her back went bad, and I think her hands never got the letter telling them to stop.

"Tomas," she said, very quiet, very fast. "The Kellers' cellar door. Behind the wash line. Go now and don't look at anybody's face."

That last part didn't make sense. But grown-ups give instructions like weather — you don't argue with rain, you just get under something.

So I went.

* * *

Here is what I know about faces, which is more than I want to know:

Papa has a face I could draw with my eyes shut. Mama too. When the government men came for Papa in September, they wrote his face down in a book — I saw them do it, the little photograph pasted next to his name, him looking surprised, like the camera had asked him a question in a language he only half remembered. They put his face in a book and then they took him out of the street, and ever since then I have thought that maybe a face is a thing you can file away. Maybe if your face is in their book, the rest of you goes where the book says.

Aunt Greta said, forget this face. She said it about me, once, the night she put me on the trolley with the note pinned inside my coat. *Forget this face,* written in Papa's handwriting, which meant Papa had written it, which meant Papa was still writing things, which was the only reason I obeyed.

But standing behind the wash line with my heart going like a sewing machine, watching the gray coats walk down Vine Street knocking on doors the way you'd knock on doors you owned, I understood something I didn't have words for yet. I have words for it now.

Forgetting a face is another word for erasing a person.

They erased Herr Brandt first. He owns the bakery. He has flour in his eyebrows permanently, like snow that found a good place to live. Two of them walked him out to an automobile and he kept saying *I am a citizen, I voted for Wilson, look, I have the button,* and one of the gray coats said, "We have the list, sir," in a voice like the list was God. Herr Brandt's face was doing something I'd never seen a grown man's face do. It was asking. Asking everybody. Asking the sky.

Nobody answered him. That's how you know somebody's being erased. Nobody answers.

* * *

I crouched behind the Kellers' wash line with my knees in a puddle and counted. Counting is what I do instead of crying, because crying makes noise and noise makes faces turn toward you, and turned-toward-you faces are how boys get put in books.

Fourteen. Fifteen.

The gray coats stopped at our building. Our actual building. I could see the window of our kitchen, where the pancake pan was still sitting on the stove getting cold and ruined, and I thought, absurdly, *the pancakes.* I thought about the pancakes while they hammered on our door, because thinking about pancakes was easier than thinking about Aunt Greta opening that door.

She opened it. Of course she opened it. You can't not open a door when they hammer like that; the hammering doesn't stop until the door opens, that's the whole design of hammering.

I heard most of it through the cellar door, which is thin, which is why I chose it without knowing I was choosing it.

"Gertrude Keller?"

"Aunt," she said. "I'm the aunt. The mother is — " and here her voice did a thing, a small stumble, like a person stepping off a curb they didn't see. "The mother is deceased."

"The boy? There's a boy registered at this address."

"He went to Philadelphia. To family." A pause. "He is not here."

"Not here," the voice repeated, tasting it. Papers rustled. Then, softer, which was worse: "You are listed as resident and next of kin to Ernst Keller, interned enemy alien. You'll come with us. You can fetch what you need. One bag."

"One bag," Aunt Greta said. "One bag for a life."

"It's more than some get, madam."

I heard our door close. Not slam. Close. Careful, like she was setting down something breakable, and I realized the breakable thing was her voice, and she was saving what was left of it.

I stayed behind the wash line until the automobiles left, which was either ten minutes or a year. Then I sat in the Kellers' cellar among the jars of peaches and did the arithmetic children do, which is not numbers. Papa: taken. Mama: gone before that, the bad lung, the hospital that smelled like lemons and endings. Aunt Greta: in a gray automobile with one bag. Me: in a cellar with peaches.

Every face I had was either filed away or erased. Except mine. And mine was only still mine because a woman I barely knew had lied to men with lists, and a woman I'd met exactly twice had let a stranger's son sleep on her floor.

* * *

I ran.

Not right away — I waited for dark, because dark is a coat that fits everybody. But when the streetlamps came on I climbed out through the coal chute, which sounds brave and was actually just filthy, and I cut through the alley behind the brewery where the smell is so thick you could chew it, and I ran the way Aunt Greta taught me: don't look at anybody's face.

Which is its own kind of trick, because when you refuse to look at faces, you start seeing everything else. Hands wringing in windows. Curtains twitching. Old Mr. Vogel standing in his doorway holding his hat against his chest like the anthem was playing somewhere only he could hear. Whole street full of people practicing invisibility, and me the fastest learner on the block.

Vine Street to the avenue. Avenue to the trolley. I had eleven cents in my pocket and a plan that was mostly just the memory of a route, which is to say I had no plan, I had a shape, and I threw myself into it.

The conductor looked at me twice. Once for the fare, once longer. I gave him my best face, which is the blank one, the one that says *nothing happening here, nothing has ever happened here, move along.* I have been practicing the blank face since September. I am very good at it. I hate that I am very good at it.

* * *

August opened the boarding house door before I knocked.

I don't know how. Later she told me she'd heard my feet, that everybody's feet sound different on stairs, which seemed like a lie made to comfort me, except August doesn't lie to make people comfortable, she lies to keep them alive, which is a different department entirely.

"You're supposed to be indoors," she said.

"They took Aunt Greta."

Something moved across her face, quick, like a bird crossing a window. Then she did a strange thing: she looked over my head, both directions down the hall, the way she does, checking whether the world was watching, and then she pulled me inside by the collar and shut the door with her foot.

"From the beginning," she said. "Slowly. Leave nothing out. Especially the parts you think don't matter."

So I told her. The pancakes. The automobiles. The list with Papa's name twice. Herr Brandt and his voting button. One bag. The peach cellar. The coal chute. The conductor's second look.

She listened the way nobody listens — like my words were freight and she was counting every crate. When I got to the part about the blank face, about practicing it since September, she went very still.

"Show me," she said.

I showed her. The nothing face.

August looked at it a long time. Then she knelt down so we were the same height, which grown-ups almost never do, and she said, "Tomas. Listen to me. That face saved your life. And someday — not today, but someday — you're going to take it off and never wear it again. Do you understand me? It's a coat. Not a skin."

Names are stitched on the inside where you can't reach, I wanted to tell her. Coats come off. Skins don't.

But upstairs, a floorboard creaked, and then another, and then Miss Bess's voice came down the stairwell, low and urgent: "August. Company. Two of them. Gray coats, and they're asking after a boy seen running up Arch Street not twenty minutes ago."

August's hand tightened on my shoulder — the same grip, I realized, the same small strong hand, as if all the women keeping me alive shared one pair of hands between them.

"Cellar," she whispered. "Behind the peaches, if we're lucky."

We were not lucky. The knock came before I reached the bottom step.
