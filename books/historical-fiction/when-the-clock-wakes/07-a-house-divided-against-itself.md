## 7. A House Divided Against Itself

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which I have since decided is the day of the week God reserves for humiliations.

It came on cream stock, expensive cream stock, with the sort of handwriting that had been taught to be lovely by a governess who despaired of it. I knew the hand before I knew the name. Margaret Ellsworth-Croft. President of the Pennsylvania Woman's Suffrage Association's more fashionable wing, chair of every committee that mattered and several that didn't, and a woman who had once told me, over sherry, that the vote was "a door best opened gently, dear, not kicked in by women with opinions about their boots."

I had opinions about my boots. I had opinions about Margaret Ellsworth-Croft generally. But I read her letter twice, and then a third time standing very still in my study while the radiator knocked like a prisoner demanding release, and by the third reading the sherry warmth had gone out of the world entirely.

*Dear Adeline,* it began, in that tone of hers that made every sentence sound like a favor being done at interest.

*It has come to our attention—through channels I will not embarrass by naming—that there is presently residing in your household a child of enemy alien parentage. A boy. German. You will appreciate, I am sure, how this might appear to certain parties in Washington who are already inclined to view our movement as soft on the question of the Hun.*

*The speech you intend to deliver Thursday—your remarks on the disenfranchisement of immigrant women, and I quote your circular, "the vote does not check papers at the door"—has caused considerable distress among our more prudent members. I would urge you, as a friend, to withdraw it. Should you do so, I am confident the matter of the child may be allowed to remain a private sorrow rather than a public embarrassment.*

*I do so admire your work, Adeline. Do not let sentiment undo it.*

*Yours in the Cause,*

*M. E.-C.*

I sat down. Then I stood up, because sitting felt like agreeing. Then I sat down again, because my knees had opinions of their own.

A child. In my household. An alien child, in her phrase, delivered with the delicacy of a subpoena.

The house had been quiet for weeks—too quiet, I had told myself, congratulating us all on our discretion. August drove the car, delivered the letters, came and went with the silence of a well-oiled hinge. Bess Okafor cooked and gossiped and knew everything, as landladies do, and said nothing, as landladies who are wise do. And somewhere above my study, behind a door I had been assured opened onto a storage room, a boy named Tomas had been living on bread and stolen library books since the first week of October.

I had known. I want that understood, by whatever court eventually reads this: I had known, in the way one knows a house is settling. I had chosen not to hear the small footsteps. I had chosen to believe August's flat assurances that everything was in order, because everything in order was what I required, and because I had learned long ago that a movement runs on the fuel of things not examined too closely.

But there is a distance between knowing a thing and being confronted with it in another woman's handwriting, on cream stock, with a threat folded inside like a razor in a napkin.

"August," I called up the stairs.

* * *

She came into my study the way she always did—cap straight, collar buttoned to the throat, hands loose at her sides, a person assembled entirely out of competence. I had hired that competence two years ago and never once, until that moment, asked what it cost her to wear it.

"You received a letter," she said.

"Sit down, August."

She sat. She sat the way soldiers sit, ready to be up again.

"I'm going to ask you a question," I said, "and I want you to consider very carefully whether the answer you give me is the one you've decided I can bear, or the truth. Because I have spent the last twenty minutes being blackmailed, and I find I've lost my appetite for being managed."

Her jaw tightened. I watched her weigh me. I had seen her do it with strangers, with policemen, with the whole city of Philadelphia—measuring exactly how much of herself each of them could be trusted to hold.

"The boy," she said. "Tomas Keller. He's Ernst Keller's son. He was left at my door the night of the rain, the eleventh of October, with a note in Ernst's hand telling me to forget his face. He's been in my room since. Bess knows. Bess helped me make him a nephew on paper." She paused, and I saw her gather something up from a very deep place. "There's more."

"There generally is."

"The letters. Ernst's letters to you—the peace circle correspondence from before the war. I kept copies. I've read every letter I've ever delivered for you, Miss Marsh. Every one. I read them at night, by lamp, because—" and here her voice did something I had never heard it do, something small and furious and ashamed, "—because reading other people's futures was the nearest I ever came to having one."

"August—"

"And the last thing," she said, over me, because she had decided to finish falling and be done with it. "August Sandoval is not my name. It's the name I took when I took the cap and the trousers. My name is August too—Augusta. I'm a woman. I've been a woman driving your car and wearing your movement's buttons and reading your mail for two years, and I'd do all of it again, and I will not apologize for a single day of it."

The radiator knocked. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked under a small boy's careful weight, and we both heard it, and neither of us moved.

I should tell you what I felt, and I will, but honesty requires me to admit the order of it. First came fury—clean, hot, almost restful in its simplicity. She had lied to me. In my house, in my employ, in my cause, she had lied for two years, and her lies had grown a child inside them, and the child had grown a federal offense, and the whole architecture of it was now standing in Margaret Ellsworth-Croft's crosshairs with my name stenciled on the front.

"Two years," I said.

"Yes."

"You let me vouch for you. You let me put my name on your papers."

"Yes."

"You read my correspondence."

"Every word," she said, and lifted her chin, and there it was—the thing I had actually hired, two years ago, without knowing I'd seen it. Not obedience. Not even loyalty, exactly. A spine. "And I would have burned every letter before I let one of them hurt you. You can believe that or not. But you don't get to ask me to be sorry for surviving, Miss Marsh. I've been black a long time and a woman longer, and passing as a man was the first door this city ever opened to me, and I walked through it carrying your banners."

I turned to the window. Below, Chestnut Street went about its Tuesday, women in good coats, men in bad ones, a newsboy shouting something about Wilson that I didn't take in. I had built a life on the principle that a person's worth does not require the government's countersignature. I had said it from platforms. I had said it to reporters. I had said it to my mother, God rest her, who had told me a lady's convictions were like her corsets—necessary, but never to be displayed.

And now here was the bill for all of it, presented by a woman in a man's cap who had trusted me with her life precisely as far as she had seen me trust anyone with mine. Which is to say: not at all. Which is to say: she had learned, from watching me, that trust was a currency one spent carefully.

"Say it again," I said. "The part about the letters."

"They're evidence," she said. "Two years of Ernst writing to you about peace—about arbitration, about the war being a butcher's ledger, about his wife and son. Before the war, when saying such things was merely eccentric instead of criminal. If they take him Thursday, those letters are the only proof he was a man who loved peace in peacetime and never stopped. They're also proof that you knew him, and sheltered his son, and—" she met my eyes in the glass, "—that you employed a woman in trousers. All of it comes out together. It was always going to come out together. I only got to choose the hour, and I chose not to let Margaret Ellsworth-Croft choose it for you."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me like something escaping a burning building.

"She thinks she has a knife at my throat," I said. "Withdraw the speech, and the child stays secret. And all the while you've been sitting upstairs keeping the very evidence that would ruin her leverage—because the moment I'm exposed, I'm exposed for sheltering him, and if I'm exposed for sheltering him, then I'm exposed for *why*, and the why is in your trunk in duplicate."

"That was the idea," August said. "Though I'll admit the idea didn't include her letter arriving before I'd worked up the nerve to tell you."

"And now?"

"Now you have until Thursday," she said, "to decide which scandal you'd rather be. And I have until Thursday to decide how much of me gets exposed alongside you. Because I'm done being invisible, Miss Marsh. Invisibility kept me fed, but it never once kept me *safe*—it just kept me quiet while other people were not safe. I've had two years of reading history. I find I'd like to be in it."

I looked at her for a long time. At the cap, the collar, the hands that had held my life in their competence for two years while I paid them three dollars a day and called it generosity.

"You know what I was doing when I read her letter?" I said. "Standing. Then sitting. Then standing. Because sitting felt like surrender and standing felt like theater, and I could not for the life of me find the posture that a woman in my position is supposed to assume." I turned from the window. "Do you know what I've been doing for thirty years, August? Passing. Passing as a lady. Passing as reasonable. Passing as the sort of woman who opens doors gently, so that the men who own the doors will let me near the handle. I have spent my whole life being *acceptable*, and Margaret Ellsworth-Croft is what acceptable looks like when it writes you a blackmail letter on cream stock."

"Miss Marsh—"

"Adeline," I said. "If we're going to hang, we'll hang on Christian names."

Something moved through her face then—something I suspect she would deny to her dying day, because I have seen her deny tears in a photograph. She looked down at her hands, those competent hands, and when she looked up she was, for one unguarded second, simply a tired young woman who had been carrying a house on her back since October.

"The speech," she said. "Thursday. You're still giving it."

"I have never in my life been less inclined to withdraw a speech. Margaret wants it cut because it's true, and the child is her lever because the child is *mine* to protect, and I find that the two things are the same thing, and always were. The vote doesn't check papers at the door. Neither does decency." I took up my pen. "Here is what will happen. You will go upstairs and tell Tomas that he is not going anywhere. Bess will make enough dinner for three, and we will stop pretending I don't know why there are four settings. And tomorrow you and I are going to sit down with every letter you kept, and we are going to build a case that Ernst Keller loved this country better than the men hunting him love its Constitution."

"And if the agents come before Thursday?"

I looked at her, and she looked at me, and I understood that we had just crossed some border together and could no longer see the country we'd come from.

"Then they come," I said, "and find two women and a landlady and a boy who have already decided what they're willing to lose. Margaret can have her scandal. I've decided which kind I'd rather be."

August stood, and straightened her cap, and at the door she paused—not the soldier's pause, but something older.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I never read your letters to hurt you. I read them because you were the first person I ever met who was fighting for a future she wasn't promised. I wanted to know how that was done."

"You could have asked."

"You'd have said no."

I had to grant her that.

She went upstairs. I heard the floorboards, the small careful weight of a boy moving to meet her, the low murmur of a voice I had mistaken for a young man's for two years and would never mistake again. I took out a fresh sheet of paper and began to write—first to Margaret Ellsworth-Croft, declining her generous offer in terms that would blister her gloves; then to the *Tribune*, to a reporter named Halloran who owed me a favor and had a taste for federal overreach.

And it was while I was sealing the second letter that Bess Okafor appeared in my doorway with a tray of coffee nobody had asked for, set it down, and said, in the tone of a woman laying down a winning hand:

"Miss Marsh. Whatever you're planning—plan it fast. There's a man been walking past the house three times since noon, and he's not reading the numbers on the doors. He's reading the *windows*."

I looked at August, who had come back down the stairs two at a time, and who had gone very still, and very pale, in the way of a woman who has just remembered that the eleventh of October was not the last time Ernst Keller sent word.

"He's been transferred," she said. "The hearing is Friday. Adeline—Friday. They're moving him to the docks for deportation the day after your speech."

* * *

I have since learned that there are exactly two kinds of fear: the fear that makes you small, and the fear that makes you precise. Margaret's letter had made me small. The man at the window made me precise.

"August," I said, "get your trunk. All of it. Every letter."

She was already moving.

"And Adeline—" Bess, at the door, arms folded, a woman deciding something she had evidently decided long ago and was only now announcing. "Whatever's coming through that door Friday—you won't be facing it as two. You'll be facing it as three."

Above us, a small boy's footsteps crossed the ceiling, quick and light, running toward the window to see the man in the street.

And the man in the street stopped, and looked up.
