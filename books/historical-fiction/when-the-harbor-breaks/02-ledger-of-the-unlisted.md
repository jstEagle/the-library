## 2. Ledger of the Unlisted

The ledger lived in a biscuit tin, and the biscuit tin lived under the floorboard beneath Mirabel's bed, and the floorboard had been loose since before he rented the room, which he considered a small act of providence. Landladies never suspected a man who kept his secrets in packaging. They suspected diaries, locked boxes, letters tied with ribbon. Nobody suspected a tin that had once held ginger nuts.

He took it out only at night, by candle, though there was nothing in it that would burn him now — or so he told himself, in the tone of a man rehearsing a lie until it sits down and behaves.

The ledger was not one book but three, sewn together with waxed thread when the first filled up in '17 and the second in '19. The pages were soft as cloth from handling. He knew them the way other men knew their wives' faces: the water stain shaped like Portugal on page forty; the entry where his hand shook so badly the ink had feathered into a small grey bloom, like frost on a window.

*Delacroix, Émile. Private. Fell 14 March 1917, Chemin des Dames. Reported: missing, presumed absent without leave.*

That was the first name. There were four hundred and eleven after it.

Mirabel had been twenty-two years old and a nurse's orderly, which meant he carried stretchers and held basins and learned very quickly which officers counted men and which officers counted paperwork. It was Captain Vasseur who taught him the arithmetic of erasure. A company that reported its dead truthfully was a company under investigation. A company that reported them *missing* was merely unlucky. And a company that reported them *deserted* —

Well. A deserter shamed no one but himself.

"You're a careful lad," Vasseur had said, watching Mirabel copy the casualty return in his neat schoolroom hand. "Careful lads go far."

Mirabel had gone precisely nowhere, which was the point. Careful lads who noticed that the return said *nine dead* when the dressing station had taken in fourteen were not promoted. They were thanked. Quietly. In the tone one uses for a dog that has seen something it shouldn't and had the good manners not to bark.

The fourteenth stretcher that day had belonged to Émile Delacroix, who was nineteen, who had talked ceaselessly about his mother's plum trees, and who had died holding Mirabel's wrist with a grip that loosened by degrees, like a tide going out. When the return came round for copying, Delacroix was nowhere on it. Not dead. Not wounded. Simply subtracted, the way you'd strike a misprinted line.

Mirabel had copied the falsified figure in his neat hand. And then, that night, by the light of a lamp he was not permitted to burn, he had opened the back pages of his own paybook and written, in letters small enough to hide under a thumbnail:

*Émile Delacroix died here. I saw it. I was here too.*

It had felt, at the time, like a mad thing to do. Seditious, almost. But his hand had steadied as he wrote, and something in his chest had unclenched for the first time in weeks, and he understood — with the peculiar clarity of the very young — that this was what he was for. Not the carrying of stretchers, though he did that. Not the holding of basins. This. The writing down. Someone had to be the place where the truth went to live, or it died twice: once in the body, once in the record.

So the ledger began. Officers reported fewer dead than fell, and Mirabel wrote down the difference. Men vanished between the trench and the return, and Mirabel wrote down their names, their regiments, the dates, whatever fragments the dying had left him — a hometown, a sweetheart's name, a joke they'd told that morning. He wrote down the things no form asked. *Afraid of rats. Sang beautifully. Owed me two francs and I forgive him.*

By the armistice the habit was bone-deep. By the time he drifted north to the harbor town — Verchère-sur-Mer, all salt rot and gull argument, a place that smelled of tar and forgiveness — he could no more stop keeping it than stop blinking.

* * *

Verchère had received him the way harbors receive everything: without much interest. He arrived in the spring of 1946 with a census satchel, a forged air of bureaucratic dullness, and a face that people forgot while looking directly at it. Fifteen years of practice will do that. He had learned to stand slightly sideways in doorways, to speak just loudly enough, to laugh half a beat after everyone else so his laughter seemed borrowed rather than owned. Invisibility, he had found, was less a talent than a discipline, like piano.

The town needed counting. That was the official story, and it was even true: the war had chewed through Verchère's registers along with its fishing fleet, and Paris wanted numbers. What Paris did not want — what no form in the empire asked — was the question the widow Gauthier had put to him on Tuesday, standing in her doorway with flour on her hands.

"Do the dead count?"

She hadn't meant it philosophically. Her son had drowned off the point in '43, and the registrar's office had lost the paper, and she wanted to know if her boy existed anymore in the eyes of the republic. But Mirabel, tired and unguarded, had answered the larger question instead.

"They count," he'd said. "They're the ones who count most. It's the living who get sloppy about it."

And then — God knew why, perhaps because her hands were floured like a baker's and it made him think of his mother, perhaps because fifteen years is simply too long — he had added, "I keep my own list. Of everyone the forms miss. If your son's name isn't where it should be, give it to me. It'll be somewhere."

The widow had looked at him the way one looks at a clock that has begun ticking backwards. Then she'd said, "You'd better come in, then," and fed him a cake that tasted mostly of rationing and a little of love, and given him her son's full name, birth date, and the exact color of the sea the morning he didn't come home.

Walking back along the quay afterward, Mirabel had felt the fissure running through him like a crack through ice. One sentence. One honest sentence, and the whole careful architecture of his invisibility groaned. He had spent fifteen years being nobody so that the names could be somebody, and he had nearly undone it over cake.

But here was the trouble he turned over that night, candle lit, tin open, the familiar pages breathing their faint smell of wax and old smoke: the ledger was a promise, and promises are cruel things, built to be impossible.

He could not keep it. Four hundred and twelve names, and every year the list grew — the harbor town was full of men the war had misplaced, deserters and drifters and families with papers like wet tissue, and each one he met went into the book, and each one he failed to meet went unwritten, and unwritten was another kind of erased. He could not write them all. No one could. The war had made more ghosts than any living hand could hold.

And he could not break it. He had tried, in the bad years, to imagine burning the thing — freeing himself, becoming truly nobody, safe. He'd gotten as far as striking the match once, in '31, over a wastepaper basket, and his hand had refused at the last instant with the stubbornness of a mule, and he had sat down on the floor and laughed until he cried, alone in a rented room, holding a biscuit tin like an infant.

Because the promise wasn't really to the dead. The dead were past needing anything. The promise was to whoever came after — some clerk in fifty years, some granddaughter, some stranger asking the widow Gauthier's question, *do the dead count?* — and finding, against all probability, an answer. A hand had been here. A hand had seen.

He blew out the candle and slid the tin home beneath the floorboard, and lay in the dark listening to the harbor work — the winches, the gulls conducting their eternal grievance, the slap of water against hulls. Somewhere out there were people who belonged in his book and weren't in it yet. Somewhere out there, too, were people who would very much prefer his book did not exist.

He thought of neither, particularly. He thought about sleep.

What he could not have known — what no amount of care could have told him — was that the following week, on his rounds, he would pause too long at a certain gate on the boatyard road, gate of weathered blue paint, behind which the yard moved with a quiet industry that didn't quite match its payroll. Nor could he have known that the woman who ran that yard watched her gate the way lighthouse keepers watch the horizon, and had already marked the census taker who lingered.

She would open that gate. She would ask him what he was writing.

And for the second time in fifteen years, Mirabel Halloran would answer honestly — and this time, honesty would look back at him with eyes that had also counted the missing, and refuse to close.
