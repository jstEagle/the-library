## 5. The Auditor Arrives

The telegram came on a Tuesday, which felt fitting. Tuesdays were the day the harbor town had decided, sometime before the war and never afterward revised, to be unremarkable.

Mirabel Halloran was eating breakfast—bread gone slightly stale, coffee that tasted of the tin it had been boiled in—when Odile Ferrant slapped a yellow slip down beside his cup hard enough to make the spoon jump.

"Read it," she said.

He read it. *CORVANE, J. AUDIT OFFICE, MINISTRY OF RECONSTRUCTION. ARRIVING THURSDAY. ASSESSMENT OF FERRANT YARD PREMISES FOR DEMOLITION SUITABILITY. ALL RECORDS TO BE MADE AVAILABLE.*

"All records," Odile repeated, in the tone other people used for words like *plague* or *rat*.

"It's routine," Mirabel said, because someone had to say it, even if neither of them believed it.

"Routine." She pulled out the chair across from him and sat, which she only did when something was wrong enough to require sitting. "They've sent men before. Surveyors. A man with a camera who photographed my cranes like they were criminals awaiting sentencing. But they've never sent an auditor. Auditors don't measure things, Mirabel. They decide them."

He turned the telegram over, as though the back might contain better news. It contained only the printer's mark. He thought about telling her what his stomach already knew—that the name Corvane had landed in him like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling, waiting for the splash—and decided against it. Not yet. Not until he was sure.

He was sure by the time he finished his coffee.

* * *

He spent Wednesday doing what he always did when dread arrived: working. He made his census rounds along the eastern terraces, knocking on doors, asking his gentle questions, writing down the answers the forms had no room for. A widow named Mrs. Tregaskin gave him tea and told him her son wrote every week from a port town Mirabel suspected did not exist. A fisherman reported his household as himself and "the cat, if you're counting souls."

"I'm always counting souls," Mirabel said, and the fisherman laughed, and Mirabel smiled, and inside him the well kept filling.

Because here was the thing about Corvane. Here was the thing Mirabel had carried for fifteen years like a coin sewn into a coat lining: he knew the man's handwriting.

Not his signature—signatures were public things, worn smooth by use. His handwriting. The particular way Corvane formed his sevens with a crossbar too long, as though defending them. The way his reports circled back on themselves, tidy and reasonable, each sentence built like a small brick wall. Mirabel had copied hundreds of those sentences in a requisition office outside Ypres, in a room that smelled of ink and cold tea, transcribing casualty figures that never quite matched what the stretcher-bearers carried in.

Corvane had been a captain then. Efficient. Courteous. He'd once caught young Halloran hesitating over a figure—a number that didn't match the field hospital's count—and instead of reprimanding him, had leaned close and said, in a voice of perfect kindness:

"The report is not a photograph, Halloran. It is a *decision*. Learn which silences keep people safe, and you will go far."

Mirabel had learned. God help him, he had learned so well that he'd spent fifteen years being invisible, keeping a ledger of the very dead those silences had swallowed, and now the man who taught him the lesson was coming to town with the power to erase an entire boatyard full of living people.

There was a symmetry there that would have been elegant in a novel. In life it just felt like a trap closing.

* * *

Thursday arrived gray and windless, the harbor lying flat as poured pewter. Mirabel positioned himself at the corner near the yard gate with his census satchel, pretending to consult his forms, which at least had the advantage of being true work. Beside him, ostensibly checking a mooring line that needed no checking, stood Odile.

"You don't have to be here," she said.

"You don't either."

"My yard. My funeral." She squinted down the road. "Besides, I want to see his face when he steps in the tar."

"There is no tar."

"There will be if I have anything to say about it."

The car came at ten o'clock precisely—a black government motorcar, clean despite the road dust, moving through the crooked streets like a straightedge laid over a drawing. It stopped at the gate. The door opened.

Corvane had aged the way good furniture ages: nothing broken, everything darkened. He was taller than Mirabel remembered, or perhaps Mirabel had shrunk—the invisibility had a way of compressing a man. He wore a gray coat with no insignia, because auditors wore their authority the way ice wore a pond: invisibly, everywhere, underfoot.

His eyes swept the yard—the slips, the half-built hulls, the loft where three families slept—and then swept the street, and passed over Mirabel without a flicker.

Fifteen years of practice, rewarded. He doesn't know me. The relief lasted four seconds, until Corvane turned back toward the gate and paused, head tilting slightly, the way a man tilts his head at a tune he almost remembers.

"Halloran," Corvane said.

The harbor went very quiet. Even Odile stopped breathing, though Mirabel noted, distantly, that she also shifted her weight onto her front foot, like a woman preparing to swing something.

"Captain Corvane." His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "You remember me."

"I remember everyone who copies well." Corvane extended a hand; Mirabel took it. The grip was dry and precise. "Census work now. How the mighty ambitions fall."

"Somebody has to count what actually happened."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—not anger, nothing so useful as anger. Recognition, perhaps, of a debt long unpaid. "Yes," he said softly. "Somebody does. And somebody decides what counting is *for*." He released the hand and turned to Odile, producing papers with a flourish that belonged to an earlier century. "Madame Ferrant. I'll need access to your premises, your books, and your personnel records."

"I keep boats, not personnel."

"Everyone keeps personnel, Madame. Some simply call them crew." He smiled. It was a good smile. It had probably gotten entire villages relocated. "Shall we begin?"

* * *

They began. For six hours, Mirabel watched Corvane move through the boatyard the way water moves through a wreck—patiently, thoroughly, into every compartment. He measured the slips. He examined the crane foundations. He read Odile's accounts with a pencil hovering, marking nothing yet, marking everything eventually.

And all the while, Mirabel stood at the edge of it with his census satchel, doing the arithmetic of catastrophe.

The yard sheltered eleven people who did not officially exist. Tomas, the deserter who repaired engines and flinched at whistles. The Marchetti widow and her two girls, sleeping in the sail loft. Old Brech, whose papers had drowned with his ship. Four others whose names lived only in Mirabel's ledger, hidden in the false bottom of his census case beneath forty perfectly legitimate household forms.

If Corvane found the ledger, it wasn't just the eleven. It was every name in it—two hundred and thirty-one entries, fifteen years of the erased. It was proof of falsified reports with Corvane's own crossbarred sevens at the bottom of the originals. It was, Mirabel understood with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, the end of everything, including possibly Mirabel.

But if the yard fell and the ledger stayed hidden, the eleven scattered into a country with no place for them, and the record died unspoken, and Corvane's silences won again, forever, tidily, in triplicate.

"Halloran." Corvane's voice cut across the yard. He was standing by the great half-built hull, running one finger along the planking. "You're lingering."

"I'm conducting a census."

"Of a boatyard?"

"People live here."

"Do they." Corvane looked at him for a long moment, and Mirabel felt the old requisition office close around them both—the ink smell, the cold tea, the kindness like a knife held flat. "You were a careful man, Halloran. Careful men survive. I'd hate to see carelessness find you after all these years."

"Carelessness finds everyone eventually, Captain. That's rather why I count."

For the first time, something genuine crossed Corvane's face—something that might, in a better man, have been respect. Or warning. With Corvane they had always been the same expression.

He turned back to Odile. "Madame, I'll require your full resident list by Monday. Everyone sleeping on these premises. Names, origins, documentation." He snapped his notebook shut. "I should tell you—I am authorized to offer consideration. Cooperate fully, and the demolition can be handled cleanly. Refuse, and I'm afraid the process becomes... thorough."

Odile Ferrant looked up at him from the keel blocks, this small iron woman with tar on her cuffs, and Mirabel saw her arrive at exactly the same crossroads he had, and saw her choose.

"You'll have your list," she said. "Every soul under my roof, written out fair."

Beside her, Mirabel's heart dropped through the floorboards of the world—

"—in my own hand," she finished, "and you can take it up with whoever reads it, because I promise you, Captain, some names are going to cost you more than they cost me."

Corvane smiled his pond-ice smile and walked to his car. At the door he paused, and looked back—not at Odile, but at Mirabel, and tapped the roof of the car twice, lightly, the way he used to tap the requisition desk when a figure needed correcting.

"Monday, Madame," he said. "Do be complete."

The motorcar pulled away. The harbor breathed again. Odile let out a breath that sounded like a hull taking water.

"He knows," she said quietly. "About your book. He doesn't know where, but he knows."

"Yes," Mirabel said. His hands, he noticed, were shaking. "And now we have until Monday to decide what 'complete' means—for both of us."

He looked down at the census case at his side, at the false bottom where two hundred and thirty-one names slept, and understood, with terrible calm, that whatever happened next, the copying had to start tonight.
