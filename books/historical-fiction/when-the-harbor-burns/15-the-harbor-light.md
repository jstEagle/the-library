## 15. The Harbor Light

There is a particular kind of arithmetic a widow learns, and it has nothing to do with money. It is the counting you do in doorways. How many people are in the room. How many of them you can afford to lose. I had been doing that arithmetic for six years, ever since the navy sent me back a box with my brother's name stenciled on it and nothing inside but his spare shirt and a letter he'd never gotten around to mailing me. Since then I have kept my accounts carefully. The chandlery: four dockhands, one foster-daughter, one disgraced archivist who eats like a bird and argues like a fleet, and — as of this winter — one courier who measured my kitchen in exits before he'd finished his first cup of coffee.

Friday morning I counted five. By Saturday night it would be three, if the plan held, and two if it didn't.

"You're doing the face," Tove said, coming into the boathouse with an armful of lamp oil tins. "The one where you're adding us up."

"I don't have faces."

"You have forty. Ilse catalogued them."

Ilse Vannik, from her corner where she'd been wrapping the false inventory pages in oilcloth with the tenderness other women reserve for infants, said without looking up: "Thirty-seven. The other three are variations of the same face."

I let them have their joke. There would be little enough laughter after this, and none at all if we botched it, so I hoarded what came our way the way Vesper hoarded those little portable things of his — matches, thread, a folding knife no bigger than a spoon. Though lately, I noticed, he'd stopped checking his pockets every hour. Lately his hands hung at his sides like they belonged to him.

* * *

The boat was called the *Solveig*, which was my mother's name, which meant she'd been named by a sentimental man and built by an honest one, because she was sounder than any boat had a right to be after eleven winters of hauling herring through the minefield channel's outer edge. Vesper and I spent Friday afternoon making her lie about herself.

"Fish hold goes here," I said, thumping the deck planking. "Herring, salted, bound for the southern market. Renner rides in the aft compartment with the ledgers sealed in the oilskin packet, under the false floor. If anyone boards, he's a fish buyer from Sorvagen who got caught out by the thaw and is very annoyed about it."

"Can he be annoyed?"

"He's a newspaperman. He can be anything."

Vesper knelt and ran his palm along the false floor seam until he found the catch I'd shown him twice already. He found it on the third try this time. A week ago it would have taken him ten tries and he'd have hated himself for each one. That was the thing about Hartwell nobody guessed, because he wore his competence like a coat buttoned to the chin: underneath, he was a man who believed he was only worth what he could carry off intact. I'd seen the type before. They make excellent couriers and terrible neighbors, because a neighbor is someone you cannot evacuate.

"The channel buoys," he said. "Tove's marks are—"

"Tove's marks are correct. She's run messages past the patrol boat since she was twelve. She knows that water better than the men paid to guard it." I heard my own voice go flat and knew he heard it too. We had been around this buoy before, so to speak. "She pilots to the mouth of the channel and turns back at the second marker. That was the agreement."

"That was your agreement. She signed nothing."

"She's fourteen."

"She's the bravest person in this harbor," he said quietly, "and everyone here keeps telling her to be brave more quietly."

I looked at him then — crouched in the bilge smell and the lamplight, sawdust on his good coat, arguing a child's case against her own guardian — and I thought: there it is. The man under the coat. It had taken him all winter to unbutton far enough to show me, and now that he had, I almost wished he hadn't, because it made the arithmetic worse. You can lose a courier. Couriers come with their losses pre-printed, like ration cards. Losing *Vesper* would be a different entry entirely, one I did not intend to practice writing.

"Help me with the lamp," I said, which was not an answer, and we both let it be one.

* * *

The harbor light sat on the breakwater's end like an old woman who refuses to move out of a house that's falling down around her. Officially it had been dark since the garrison took it over in the autumn — orders, notices, a padlock on the fuel shed that wouldn't have stopped a determined goose. Unofficially, Maren Ostrand had been keeping a key to that shed since before the garrison existed, because the smuggler I married believed in redundancy the way other men believed in God, and some beliefs survive the believer.

We went up at dusk to test the mechanism, Vesper carrying the tools and me carrying the story we'd tell if anyone asked, which was that the widow Ostrand liked to sit by the water and be left alone. Nobody asked. The garrison's patrols had grown lazy and cold, and the town had grown very good at being furniture.

"Fuel line's fouled," Vesper said, head inside the lamp housing. "Someone poured sand in the reservoir."

"Garrison boys. They do it to everything they lock up. Petty as cats." I handed him the wire brush. "It'll burn clean once it's cleared. My husband used to say the light didn't care whose side you were on; it only cared whether you fed it."

He worked in silence a while. Then, without lifting his head: "If the patrol boat comes around the mole early — if Tove's diversion doesn't pull them — how long do I have?"

"Four minutes. Maybe five. Long enough to light it, not long enough to enjoy it."

"And after?"

"After, you go to ground in the coal cellar under the chandlery, and you stay there until either the boat clears the channel or they find you. Those are the only two endings I've budgeted for."

"There's a third ending," he said. "The one where I'm on the boat with them."

I let the wind fill the pause. Out past the mole the ice was going black and rotten at the edges, weeks ahead of schedule, the whole strait turning to open water like a book opening to its worst page. Somewhere out there was the channel, and the mines moored under its skin, and the narrow safe lane that only a handful of living people could steer blind in the dark — and one of them was fourteen years old and currently at home pretending to do sums.

"Hartwell," I said. "Do you know why I agreed to any of this? Not the pouch. Any of it. The room above the chandlery, the meals, the whole winter of you."

He came out of the lamp housing slowly, brush in hand, wary. Men like him always brace when a woman says *I know why*. They assume the next sentence will be a bill.

"When you told me what the pouch actually said," I went on, "about the coastal defenses, about Vaske selling the approaches before the last battle — I wanted to throw you into the strait. I want you to know that. My brother died holding a gun battery that was sold before he ever fired it, and you stood in my kitchen holding the proof in a leather bag, and for one full minute I hated you for carrying it instead of anyone else in the world."

"I know," he said. "You made the coffee very strong that day."

"I make coffee strong every day."

"Not like that day."

Despite myself — despite the breakwater and the dark coming down and the arithmetic — I laughed. Short, ugly, real. "Well. There's your eulogy."

"Maren." He set the brush down. He does that, sets things down deliberately, like a man laying down cards. "When the boat goes, I should be on it. Renner needs the pouch verified, the ledgers need a witness, and I'm the only one who's read both. Staying to light a lamp is sentiment. I've done the calculation."

"Have you," I said. "And what did the calculation weigh?"

"What?"

"What did it weigh, your calculation? Because I've watched you all winter, courier, and every weight you assign to things is wrong. You weigh yourself at nothing. You weigh other people at what they cost you. You weighed this town at zero the day you arrived — a waypoint, a delay, a room above a chandlery — and look at you now, arguing for a fourteen-year-old's courage like it's a national treasure. Your scale is broken. So whatever number you calculated, I'm discounting it."

He stood there in the wind with his mouth slightly open, a man catching his first sight of open water after a life of wells.

* * *

Back at the chandlery, Ilse was waiting up, which was nothing new; Ilse waited up the way lighthouses wait up, structurally, as a matter of design. But tonight she wasn't working. Her table was clear except for one small parcel wrapped in brown paper and tied with the kind of neat archival knot that probably had a name.

"Sit down, both of you," she said. "Maren, you especially. I've rehearsed this and if you interrupt I'll lose my place and start over, and neither of us wants that."

I sat. Vesper hovered, then sat.

"Tomorrow night the historians audit the archive," Ilse said. "Emeric misfiles the true ledgers into the export crate, I feed them the false inventory, and if the discrepancy surfaces, I claim it. All of it. Sole guilt, sole authorship. I am, after all, the archivist of record; it is the one title they've left me, and I intend to use it." She said it the way she read shipping manifests, level and dry, and it was only because I'd known her since autumn that I heard the nail driven flat under every word. "Which means I will not be at the harbor tomorrow night. Which means I am saying my goodbyes now, while there's still paperwork to hide behind."

"Don't talk like—" Vesper started.

"Courier." One raised finger, and he closed his mouth like a drawer. "I spent thirty years keeping records for a government that asked me to bend them, and I refused, and it cost me my career, and I have never once regretted it, because a record is the only immortality ordinary people get. Tomorrow I become a record. I find that acceptable. What I find unacceptable is leaving this house without settling accounts." She pushed the brown-paper parcel across the table toward Vesper. "This isn't yours. It's Maren's to give. I just wanted witnesses, because I'm an archivist, and an unwitnessed transfer is only a rumor."

I looked at the parcel and my chest did something complicated, because I'd gone up to my room that afternoon and taken it down from the shelf where it had sat for six years next to the box with the shirt in it, and I'd told myself I was only dusting it.

"Open it," I said to Vesper, roughly, because if I handled it gently I'd never get through this.

Inside the paper was a compass. Brass case gone soft green at the edges, glass scratched, needle still true. My brother Anders carried it through the naval academy and every year after, and it came home in the box with the shirt, and I had hated it for six years with the special hatred you keep for objects that survived.

"It doesn't point north anymore," I said. "Or it points north fine, but that's not what it's for. Anders used it wrong on purpose. He'd navigate by feel and check the compass after, and when they disagreed he trusted himself, and I used to tell him that was exactly how men like him drowned." My voice snagged on the last word and I let it, because there was no point pretending the word wasn't there. "I'm giving it to you not so you can find your way. You clearly can't — you've been lost since the ferry. I'm giving it to you so you stop measuring. Every time you reach for it, I want you to remember that the needle is not the water. The map is not the road. The calculation is not the man. Put it in your pocket next to your matches and your thread, and when you catch yourself weighing your life in portable things, take it out and watch the needle swing, and remember that you are not cargo, Vesper Hartwell. You were never cargo. Cargo doesn't argue for children. Cargo doesn't learn a town's name."

The boathouse was very quiet. Even the sea seemed to lean in.

"I can't take—" he began.

"You can and you will, because refusing a dead man's compass is bad luck in every maritime nation including the ones that don't exist yet." I stood up before my face could do anything Ilse would later catalogue. "And when the boat clears the channel — *if* you're on it, which we haven't settled — you give it back to me on the far shore. That's the price. It comes home. Everything in this family comes home or we go get it."

He held the compass the way he'd held the pouch the day I met him, and then he didn't — he turned it over, casual, pocketed it like a man pocketing a coin, already part of him. That was the blessing, though I didn't say so. Blessings work best unlabeled, like medicine in bread.

Ilse nodded once, satisfied, a transaction properly witnessed and entered. "Good," she said. "Now. Maren. The false inventory's in the fish crate, Emeric's note is confirmed, and I've written instructions for whoever inherits my catalogs, which will presumably be a historian with poor handwriting and worse intentions. Is there anything else this family needs to settle tonight, or shall we sleep?"

"We sleep," I said.

But nobody slept, really. I lay upstairs listening to the ice groan out in the strait, breaking up early, the whole winter surrendering ahead of schedule like everything else in this country, and I did my arithmetic one last time. Five people in the house. Two on the boat, maybe three. One in a cellar. One in an archive, standing in front of a fire she'd light herself.

And then, near midnight, boots on the stairs — fast ones, Tove's, taking them two at a time, and she burst into my room without knocking, which she only did when the news couldn't wait for manners.

"Aunt Maren." She was breathless, eyes huge, still half-dressed for bed. "The patrol boat. It's moving up the channel *now* — and there's soldiers at the bridge. They're opening it. Tonight. Something's coming through."
