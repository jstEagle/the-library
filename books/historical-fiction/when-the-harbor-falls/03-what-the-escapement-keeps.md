## 3. What the Escapement Keeps

The lamp guttered once, and Mirabel held his breath until it steadied, the way he held his breath over any living thing that might die in his care. A flame was a small engine too. It wanted feeding, trimming, a steady hand. He had learned that at six years old, standing on a stool beside Odell Blackwood's bench while the old man's knuckles—knuckles like walnut burls—turned a screw a quarter-turn at a time.

*Listen before you touch.* The instruction sat in him like a swallowed stone.

He had been listening for two hours now, and the clock had told him everything and nothing.

The observatory's lamp room was above him, dark and shuttered; the clock room itself was a stone drum at the tower's waist, cold as a cellar no matter the season, smelling of graphite and old oil and, faintly, of gull guano that had worked its way through a century of masonry. The master clock dominated the room the way a whale dominates a tidepool: a great oak case taller than two men, its face on the seaward wall, its works open to him through a door that Sable Voss had unlocked with a key on a ring of forty.

"Father says the strike train's gone strange," she had said, before climbing back down to the keeper's quarters. "Strange even for a clock that's already strange. Mind you don't let it bite you. It bit the last man who opened it."

Mirabel had assumed she was joking. He was no longer sure.

He had his tools laid out on a folded blanket in strict order of use—pin tongs, hand-lever, the fine screwdrivers in their roll of green baize, the loupe that had been his master's master's—and he had not touched a single one of them to the clock. Instead he had done the thing that felt, to a journeyman, like standing in a rain with his coat off: he had watched, and listened, and taken notes.

The going train was sound. That much he could certify. The great barrel, the maintaining power, the deadbeat escapement with its ruby pallets—standard, honest, well-made work, the kind that kept time to a few seconds a week if it was loved. The hands on the seaward face had been losing four minutes a day for a year, Sable said, and the army's requisition clerks in the harbor office had been correcting them by hand every morning, which meant the army had been *noticing*, which meant the army cared what time Grayharbor believed it was.

But the going train was not what had woken him at three in the morning in his narrow bed in the workshop loft with his heart going like a flywheel.

It was the strike train. And it was not the strike train at all.

Mirabel took up his notebook—cheap paper, ruled in Odell's hand for the apprentices, *observations first, opinions never* printed across the first page—and read back over what he'd written.

*The strike train does not strike the hour. It strikes a sequence. The sequence repeats every [unclear] hours. The sequence is not 1-2-3-4. The sequence is 7-3-11-2-9-5 and then a long silence, then 1-1-4, then silence, then 12.*

Seven. Three. Eleven. Two. Nine. Five. One-one-four. Twelve.

He had sat in the dark of the clock room with his ear nearly pressed to the oak case, counting hammer-falls in the fog-muffled dark, and the numbers had meant nothing, and then they had meant something terrible, and then he had made himself stop thinking and start counting again, because a clockmaker who theorizes before he measures is a clockmaker who ruins clocks.

The bells above him were the town's bells. Every soul in Grayharbor had heard them all their lives, had set their kettles and their consciences by them, and no one—not the harbormaster, not the parson, not the oldest fishwife on the shingle—had ever remarked that the clock struck seven at six o'clock, or struck twice where once would do. Because the numbers *sounded* like hours. Seven strokes, three strokes, eleven strokes—any ear would file them under *bells being bells*, the way an ear files gulls under *gulls* without counting them.

Someone had hidden a message in plain hearing for—how long? The clock was old. Older than the harbor company's charter, older than Mirabel's own line of Blackwoods in Grayharbor, which went back to his grandfather Elias, and Elias had been a clockmaker before Odell had been, and Odell had been Elias's apprentice before he'd been his successor, and—

Mirabel's pen stopped.

He looked at the open case again. At the strike train, yes, but past it, at the back of the case, where the frame plates met the oak, where he had noticed—had he noticed? or had he imagined?—that one plate was fastened with screws whose slots did not match the others. Every screw in the clock was cut with a slot turned to the same angle, the way a careful man cuts all his screws alike. Except four. Four screws, one at each corner of the rear plate, slotted crosswise.

He had not touched them. *Listen before you touch.* But he had looked at them for a long time, and looking, he thought, was not touching.

He took up the smallest screwdriver, held it over the first crosswise screw, and did not move. His hand hung there, steady as a pendulum, and his heart was not steady at all.

*If it's locked, it's locked for a reason,* Odell always said. *And the reason is either shame or treasure, and you won't know which until you're inside, and by then you're responsible for it. That's the whole trade, boy. Everything you open, you own.*

"I'm already responsible," Mirabel said aloud, to the clock, to the fog, to the dead grandfather he had never met. "You sent for me."

The clock ticked. It did not answer, but it did not stop him either, and he chose to take that as permission.

The screws came out with the reluctance of screws that have not been turned in fifty years—each one groaning, each one shedding a halo of black oxide—and the rear plate, which he had assumed was bolted to the frame, came away in his hands, light as a book cover. Behind it was not oak. Behind it was a second mechanism.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing, and it shamed him, how the beauty arrived before the fear. A compact train of wheels, perhaps a third the size of the strike train, driven—when he traced the linkage with his loupe—by a concealed off-take from the great barrel, geared down so far that its fastest wheel would take an hour to turn once. It had its own escapement, and the escapement had a wheel like nothing in any catalogue, any textbook, any of the plates and drawings Odell kept in the flat cedar chest under his bed.

The wheel had teeth of uneven depth.

Not damaged—Mirabel had seen damaged wheels, wheels with teeth snapped by frost or dropped wrenches, and they wore their wounds raggedly, with bright torn metal and asymmetry. These teeth were deliberate. Each one was cut to a precise depth from a small set of depths—four depths, he judged, maybe five—repeating in a pattern that was not repetition, that was *language*. The wheel was a page. The wheel had been written on.

He sat back on his heels on the cold stone floor and made himself breathe.

Numbers. It had to be numbers. A wheel with variable teeth, driven once per hour or once per some division of the hour, would release the strike hammer in counts determined by the depth of each tooth as it passed the pallet—shallow tooth, few strokes; deep tooth, many. The sequence he'd counted through the night—7-3-11-2-9-5, 1-1-4, 12—was the wheel reading itself aloud, one tooth at a time, once per hour, every hour, for—

He did the arithmetic in his head, then wrote it down because Odell's rule was Odell's rule. If the wheel had—counting by eye through the loupe—sixty teeth, and one tooth was read per hour, the whole sequence took sixty hours to speak. Two and a half days. Then it began again. The same numbers, forever, in the same order, unless the wheel was changed.

Unless the wheel was *written*.

Mirabel took the notebook and wrote the sequence in a column, and beside it he wrote the hours at which he had heard each number, and then he sat looking at the two columns until the lamp oil ticked and the fog pressed at the arrow-slit window and his hands went cold.

Seven. Three. Eleven. Two. Nine. Five. One-one-four. Twelve.

If they were hours, they were nothing. But they weren't hours. They were too small, most of them, too irregular. Debts, then. Sums. Or—his eye went to the 1-1-4, and something in his chest went tight—initials. A name. One-one-four: if the alphabet were folded onto the dial of depths, if A were one and B were two—

He didn't know the cipher. That was the truth of it, and he wrote it down: *I do not know the cipher. I know it is a cipher. I know it has been striking over this harbor every hour of my life and everyone else's, and no one has heard it, and someone meant it to be heard.*

By whom? By whom, and about what?

The harbor ledger. The word came up in him unbidden, the way a fish comes up through dark water, and he could not have said why except that everything in Grayharbor came back to the ledger in the end—the harbormaster's fat book that held every debt, every boat mortgage, every widow's pension, every apprentice's indenture, every inheritance, every name that mattered to every family that had ever worked the water. The book that was copied every year by lamplight and argued over every year at the harbor company's meetings and never, in Mirabel's lifetime, questioned.

His grandfather had been the harbor company's clockmaker. He knew that much; Odell had said it once, in the flat voice he used for facts he didn't want to become stories. *Elias kept the master clock and the company's watches. He was paid in coin and in silence, and he gave them both.*

Paid in silence. Mirabel had taken it for a figure of speech. He took nothing for a figure of speech now.

He looked at the hidden train again, and this time he looked with a clockmaker's eye instead of a grandson's, and he saw the second thing, the thing that curdled the thrill in his stomach into something with a taste like iron.

The train was not merely a record. It was a *mechanism*, and mechanisms could be maintained, and this one had been maintained. The oil in its bearings was fresh—fresh within the year. One pivot had been re-burnished, the work so fine that only someone trained in Odell's own shop would have recognized the polish. Someone had been keeping this secret alive. Someone had climbed this tower in the night, year after year, and fed oil to a wheel full of names while the town slept and the harbor company's clerks wrote their clean copies in their clean hands.

Odell's hands were not steady anymore. Odell had not climbed a tower stair in two years. But the oil was fresh.

*He sent for me. He sent for me the day the army's letter came, and he said, listen before you touch, and he would not tell me why, and he held my wrist too hard when he said it, the way he does when he is afraid for me and will not say so.*

Mirabel closed the notebook. He put the rear plate back, screw by screw, turning each one to its exact former angle—crosswise, all four, so that the next man who looked would see what he had seen—and he sat down on the stone floor with his back against the warm oak flank of the clock, and he listened.

Seven. Three. Eleven. Two. Nine. Five.

Somewhere below, the fog lay on the harbor like a held breath. Somewhere down there were the piers, and the counting-houses, and the harbormaster's ledger, and the families whose names were in it, and the families whose names had been *taken out* of it—because that was the other thing, the thing he had grown up hearing at the tavern in the voices of men who'd had boats and then hadn't, and had learned to say *the company took it* the way you'd say *the sea took it*, as an act of God, no one's fault, no one to answer.

No one to answer. And all this time a wheel in a tower had been answering, hour after hour, to anyone with ears and a key.

The thrill came back, and he let it, because a man should feel what he feels. But under it now was the fear, and he let that too, because Odell's other rule was *a clockmaker who isn't afraid of his work will break something*. The fear said: *you have opened a lock you cannot close. You are now responsible for what is inside it. The army is in the harbor roads. The army requisitions what it requisitions, and it keeps what it keeps, and if a wheel of names is worth oiling in secret for fifty years, it is worth more than that to the men who wanted the silence paid for.*

The clock struck the hour—struck *nine*, the true nine, the going train's nine, four minutes ahead of the hands on the face because the hands were wrong, the hands had been made wrong, the hands were the lie and this cold stone room was the truth—and in the last stroke Mirabel heard, or believed he heard, beneath the bronze, the small dry click of the hidden escapement advancing one tooth. One letter. One number. One name, spoken into the fog, unheard.

He gathered his tools with hands that were not quite steady and did not pretend to himself that it was the cold.

At the door he stopped. He looked back at the great case, at its patient dark, and he said—quietly, because the tower was full of listening surfaces—"I'll come back tomorrow. I'll come back every day. And I'll learn your alphabet if it kills me."

The clock ticked. Behind the rear plate, sixty teeth waited in the dark, each one a syllable, the whole wheel a sentence that had been mid-word for half a century.

He was halfway down the tower stair, the lamp swinging shadows along the spiral wall, when he heard the boats.

Oars, many of them, in unison—the particular muffled rhythm of a navy cutter pulling hard—and then a sound he had never heard in Grayharbor and would never afterward stop hearing: the tramp of boots on the harbor stones, dozens of boots, in step, at dawn, in the fog.

Above him, the clock went on striking its hidden names to an empty sky, and below him, out of the white, the red coats came ashore.

* * *

He reached the harbor road as the first light came up the color of pewter, and the fog was full of soldiers.

They were forming on the quay in two ranks, faces gray under the red, breath steaming, an officer's voice carrying flat and clear through the wet air: *by order of the Crown, the port of Grayharbor is taken into military administration for the duration.* Mirabel stood at the edge of the crowd of fishermen and dockhands gathering along the warehouse wall, and no one spoke, and the fog dripped from the rigging, and somewhere behind him the tower clock, four minutes fast, struck the hour the army had not yet arrived to correct.

A colonel came down the gangway last, gloved, unhurried, the way a man walks into a house he believes he owns, and Mirabel watched him pause at the head of the quay and look up—past the masts, past the warehouse roofs, up at the tower.

At the clock.

The colonel looked at it the way Odell looked at a movement that had been tampered with. Not with wonder. With recognition.

Mirabel's hand went, without his willing it, to the notebook in his coat, to the column of numbers written in his own careful hand—7, 3, 11, 2, 9, 5—and he understood, with the whole cold weight of the morning, that he had written down something worth killing for, and that he was standing in the open holding it.

He turned up his collar, and walked, at a journeyman's unhurried pace, toward the workshop.
