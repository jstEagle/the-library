## 4. The Colonel's Punctual Men

Sable Voss woke before the bells, which had never once happened in her nineteen years, and lay in the dark of the keeper's cottage listening to the silence where the chimes should have been.

The master clock above them was due its six o'clock strike. She knew the sound the way she knew her own heartbeat—the deep bronze voice rolling down from the observatory hill, across the slate roofs and the fish-curing sheds, out over the water where the herring boats would be lifting their nets. The whole town set itself by that voice. Bakers lit their ovens at the first stroke, dockhands gathered at the third, and her father wound the great movement every evening at dusk with a key longer than his forearm, muttering the old words his own father had taught him: *steady hands, honest hours.*

But this morning there was nothing. Only fog pressing against the windowpanes, thick as wool, and beneath it—she sat up slowly—a sound she could not at first name. Boots. Many boots. Not the scattered clatter of fishermen on the cobbles but something rhythmic, deliberate, multiplied. Boots in unison.

She went to the window and rubbed a circle clear in the condensation.

Down in Harbor Street, where the morning market should have been assembling its usual disorder of carts and gulls and shouting women, stood soldiers. Red coats, white cross-belts, bayonets fixed and catching what little light the fog allowed. They were posted at intervals along the pier like fence posts driven into the town's flesh—one at the fish gate, two at the customs house steps, four at the head of the long stone pier where the *Kestrel* was moored. As she watched, a file of twelve more came marching up from the quay road, their boots striking the cobbles in perfect time, and the fog swallowed their rear ranks even as their front emerged, so that they seemed to issue endlessly out of the gray, an army manufactured by the weather itself.

"Sable." Her father's voice from below, strained thin. "Come down. Quickly."

* * *

Edmund Voss stood in the kitchen in his nightshirt with his good coat thrown over it, buttoned wrong, holding his observatory keys in one fist as though he'd forgotten he was holding them. He was not a large man; thirty years of winding clocks and climbing stairs had made him lean and stooped, a question mark of a person. But Sable had never seen him look small until this morning. He looked like a man reading his own sentence.

"They came on the night packet," he said. "Two hundred men, maybe more. They've taken the harbor office already. Marta Corrigan ran up the hill half-dressed to tell me—they put a seal on the door and a sentry either side of it."

"The ledger," Sable said.

Her father flinched. That was answer enough.

The harbor ledger. Every soul in Grayharbor knew what it was even if few had seen it open—a great bound volume kept behind three locks in the harbor office, holding forty years of the town's dealings. Every boat registered, every debt recorded, every inheritance entered in a clerk's careful hand. When old Tam Beale died, the ledger said who got his nets. When the Pell family borrowed against their smokehouse, the ledger held the terms. It was not paper. It was the town's memory, written down so that no man could argue with it.

And soldiers were standing at its door.

"Colonel Ashgrave," her father read from a printed notice he'd taken off the doorpost, his lips moving over the words as if tasting something spoiled. "*By order of the Crown, and in furtherance of the war effort, all port records, accounts, and registers within the district of Grayharbor are hereby requisitioned for military administration.*" He lowered the paper. "Requisitioned. As though it were grain. As though it were powder."

"Will you give it to them?"

He looked at her then, and Sable saw something in her father's face she had never seen before and would spend years afterward trying to forget: the look of a man calculating, honestly calculating, whether obedience might save what defiance would destroy. "What choice do I have? They've got two hundred men and we've got—" He gestured vaguely at the kitchen, at the fog, at everything. "We've got herring forks, Sable."

Below them, faint through the floorboards, the boots went on marching.

* * *

They went down together because her father asked her to stay home and she refused, and because neither of them could bear to watch the other walk into it alone. By the time they reached Harbor Street the fog had thinned to rags and the square before the customs house had filled with townsfolk drawn out of their houses by the same wrongness that had woken Sable. Fishermen still in their oilskins. Women with shawls over their nightclothes. Old Josiah Pell leaning on his grandson's arm, his breath whistling. Nobody spoke above a murmur. Two hundred red coats will do that to a conversation.

At the customs house steps, beneath the Crown's carved arms, stood a tall officer in a coat finer than any uniform Grayharbor had ever seen, gold braid at the cuffs catching the light like fish scales. He wore his hat low and his face higher, chin lifted, eyes moving across the crowd with the unhurried attention of a man taking inventory. Sable had heard the name already, passed mouth to mouth along the street like contraband: *Ashgrave.*

He did not shout. That was the first thing she noticed, and the thing that frightened her most. A shouting man wants you to fear him. This man simply waited until the murmuring died on its own, the way frost kills a garden without raising its voice.

"People of Grayharbor." His voice carried effortlessly, pitched for the back rows as precisely as a bell is tuned. "I am Colonel Reginald Ashgrave, commanding the Second Coastal Regiment. As of six o'clock this morning, Grayharbor is under military administration for the duration of the war effort."

A woman near Sable whispered, "What war? There's no war here."

Ashgrave continued as though the fog itself had spoken. "The port's shipping, warehousing, and records are required for the orderly provisioning of His Majesty's forces. You will find the Crown a fair landlord. Curfew is at eight. Gatherings of more than five persons are prohibited. The harbor ledger—" and here his gaze settled, briefly and deliberately, on Edmund Voss, "—will be surrendered to my officers by noon."

*Surrendered.* Not requisitioned now. Surrendered, the word you used for weapons, for colors, for surrendering men.

Her father stepped forward. Sable wanted to seize his sleeve and could not have said why—to hold him back or to stand beside him—and in the end her hand closed on empty air.

"I am the keeper of the observatory," Edmund said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he steadied it. "The harbor records are held in trust. Under the Port Act of—"

"Under whatever act you please, sir." Ashgrave descended one step, unhurried, and now Sable could see his eyes properly: pale, patient, entirely without cruelty and therefore entirely without mercy. Cruelty at least acknowledged you existed. "The Port Act was written for peacetime harbors. This is a wartime one. I am told the ledger is complete—that it holds every debt, every title, every inheritance in this district going back forty years."

"It does."

"Good." Something moved behind the colonel's pale eyes, quick as a fish turning under ice, gone before Sable could name it. Satisfaction, yes—but also something tighter underneath it, something that looked almost like fear wearing satisfaction's clothes. "Then the Crown is grateful for your thoroughness. Sergeant!"

A sergeant detached himself from the line—broad, weathered, with an accountant's neatness to his movements, Sable thought absurdly, a man built for counting rather than killing—and took position beside her father with a leather satchel open at his side.

"You'll accompany the sergeant to the harbor office," Ashgrave said. "You'll open the locks yourself. And you'll sign a receipt confirming voluntary surrender, so that no one in this town can later claim the Crown stole what was freely given." He smiled then, briefly, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was warm. "Paper protects us all, Mr. Voss. Surely a record-keeper understands that."

* * *

They let the crowd follow as far as the customs house door and no farther. So Sable stood pressed among the bodies at the edge of the square, close enough to see through the doorway when they brought the ledger out.

It came wrapped in sailcloth, borne between two soldiers like a coffin, and her father walked beside it with his hands clasped behind his back so tightly that his knuckles had gone the color of the fog. At the foot of the steps he stopped. The sergeant paused too, satchel open, pen offered.

Sable watched her father look at the ledger. Just look at it, for a long moment, the way a man looks at a child he is giving away. Forty years of Grayharbor in that binding. The Beales' boats. The Pells' smokehouse. Her grandfather's name, entered when he took the keepership. Every promise the town had ever made to itself, written down so that forgetting would be impossible.

And then she watched him take the pen.

His hand shook. She could see it from ten feet away, the nib stuttering across the receipt, and when he finished he did not hand the pen back but let it fall, and the sergeant caught it without comment, and her father stood very straight while the soldiers carried the ledger into the customs house, and only when the door closed did his shoulders come down all at once, like a rigging parting, and Sable pushed through the crowd and took his arm before he could fall in front of the whole town.

"Papa."

"They made me sign," he said quietly. His voice was steady; his arm under her hands was not. "Voluntary. They made me write the word myself."

"I know."

"Forty years, Sable. Your grandfather's hand is in that book. Mine is. And now it sits behind a soldier's lock, and whatever they say it says, that's what it says." He turned and looked up the hill, toward the observatory, its dome just visible above the fog line, and Sable followed his gaze and felt the cold go through her. "There's one record left in this town they haven't touched."

"The clock," she said.

"The clock." He said it the way other men said a prayer. "Your grandfather used to say the ledger was written twice, Sable. Once in ink, for men. Once in brass, for God. I never understood what he meant." He drew a breath that shuddered going in and came out steady. "This morning I think I'm beginning to."

Above them, high on the hill, the master clock struck seven—late, as it had been late for a year now, off by eleven minutes and stubborn about it—and the sound rolled down over the occupied streets, over the red coats and the fixed bayonets, and Sable saw more than one soldier glance up at the sound, uneasy, the way men glance at thunder.

Eleven minutes wrong. Deliberately wrong, she had come to believe. And this morning, watching her father's shaking hands and the sealed doors and the fog full of soldiers, she understood for the first time that those eleven minutes might be the only thing in Grayharbor the Crown did not yet own.

She squeezed her father's arm. "Send for the clockmaker," she said. "Today. Before they think of it themselves."
