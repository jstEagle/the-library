## 6. Time Belongs to the Crown Now

Colonel Edmund Ashgrave had learned long ago that a town could be taken twice: once with soldiers, and once with paper. The first taking was loud and brief and left bruises that faded by spring. The second was quiet, permanent, and required nothing but a table, a pen, and the patience to let men believe they had never owned what you took from them.

He sat at the requisitioned magistrate's desk in Grayharbor's customs house—his customs house now, though he had not yet had the sign changed—and turned the harbor ledger over in his hands before opening it. The binding was salt-stiff, the corners rounded from a century of hands. Somewhere in these pages was his grandfather's name, written in an honest clerk's honest ink: *Ashgrave, one vessel, the *Providence*, debt of forty-one pounds, twelve shillings.*

Forty-one pounds. His grandfather had arrived with nothing but a debt and a talent for making other people pay it.

Ashgrave opened the ledger at random and read. A fisherman named Quill owed nine pounds for net-mending. A widow named Tarrow held title to a smokehouse on the east pier, inherited from her husband, whose own inheritance ran back three generations. The ink was brown and patient. It did not know it was already dead paper.

Outside the window, fog lay on the harbor like a sheet drawn up to the chin. Red coats moved along the piers in pairs, small and deliberate as stitches closing a wound. He had brought two hundred men to take a town of perhaps four thousand, and he had needed fewer than fifty of them. The rest were for the appearance of force. Appearances mattered. Fear, properly administered, was cheaper than violence, and Ashgrave had always been a careful accountant of other people's terror.

A knock. "Enter."

Captain Rell came in with fog still beaded on his shoulders, saluting with the crispness of a man who wanted very much to be somewhere else and knew better than to show it. "The harbor's secured, sir. Piers one through seven. The granary keys are with the harbormaster's clerk—he surrendered them without fuss."

"And the ledgers?"

"Confiscated this morning, sir. Harbor ledger, customs rolls, the shipping company's books." Rell hesitated. "There was some... difficulty at the workshop district."

"Difficulty."

"The old clockmaker. Odell Blackwood. He's dying, by all accounts—the army surgeon looked in on him. But there's an apprentice. Young man, a Blackwood as well, grandson or grandnephew. He was summoned to the observatory yesterday to repair the master clock. He went before we posted the order."

Ashgrave set down the ledger. The word *observatory* moved through him like a fish turning under ice.

"He went," Ashgrave said carefully, "to repair it."

"Yes, sir. On the keeper's request, apparently. The keeper's daughter sent for him."

Ashgrave rose and went to the window. From here he could see the observatory on its hill above the eastern cliffs, a pale dome in the fog like the skull of something buried upright. The master clock beneath it had governed Grayharbor's time for a hundred and forty years. Every tide-table, every shift-change, every chime that told the fishermen when the herring boats went out—it all hung from that one mechanism. And now his grandfather's clock, his family's clock in every way that mattered, was open on its bench with a Blackwood's fingers inside it.

His grandfather. Silas Ashgrave had come to Grayharbor in 1789 with a debt and left it thirty years later owning a third of the waterfront. The harbor company's books had been rewritten twice in those years—everyone knew it, no one could prove it—and Silas had been the company's man from the beginning. When the erasures came, when debts dissolved and titles migrated, Silas Ashgrave's name always appeared afterward where it had not been before, clean as a coin polished by someone else's sleeve.

And if the old stories were true—if the clockmaker Blackwood of that generation had kept a private record, a true ledger hidden in gears—

"Captain," Ashgrave said, "the observatory clock is to be converted to army time. Standard regulation movement, standard striking. I want a garrison time-signal established within the week. The town will keep the Crown's hours or none at all."

Rell blinked. "Converted, sir? It's a working clock. The keeper says it only needs—"

"I am aware of what the keeper says." Ashgrave turned from the window. "The clockmaker will do the conversion. Under guard. And Captain—the workshop district. The buildings are unsound. Fire risk, structural decay, a hazard to the garrison." He said it smoothly; he had composed the sentence years ago, in another town, for another purpose. "Schedule demolition. Three weeks."

"Three weeks, sir? The whole district?"

"The whole district. Give the residents notice and compensation schedules." Compensation would be a fraction of value, paid in scrip, redeemable nowhere. He had done it in Falmouth Reach and in Dunmore Quay, and both towns now flew the Crown's colors and troubled no one.

Rell saluted again and withdrew. Ashgrave stood alone with the ledger and the fog and the sound of his own breathing, which was, he noticed with irritation, slightly too quick.

*Listen before you touch.* He didn't know why the phrase surfaced then—an old fragment, half-remembered, from his grandfather's papers perhaps, or from a servant's gossip decades dead. He crushed it down. Sentiment was a tax he had stopped paying long ago.

But fear was different. Fear was information.

He returned to the desk and drew out a leather case, and from the case a letter, yellowed and brittle, which he had carried through three campaigns folded against his heart like a saint's medal. It was from his grandfather, dated 1811, addressed to Silas's son, and it contained a single paragraph that had fed Ashgrave's ambition and his nightmares in equal measure:

*The Blackwood clock does not merely tell the hour. Old Blackwood built a second heart into it, and if that heart is ever made to speak, it will say things about this harbor that certain families would rather stay drowned. See it silenced, boy, whatever it costs. Some accounts must never be settled.*

Silas Ashgrave had died before he could silence anything. The son—Ashgrave's father—had been a weak man who preferred wine to ledgers and had let the matter sleep for forty years. And so the clock had gone on ticking above the town, keeping whatever secret it kept, while Ashgrave climbed the army's ladder rung by rung, each promotion chosen not for glory but for proximity—to ports, to coastal districts, to paperwork. Men thought him ambitious. He was. But ambition was only fear that had learned to march.

Now he had two hundred men, a seized town, and a demolition order that would level the workshop district to its foundations. The drawings, the tools, the old letters, whatever the Blackwoods had hoarded across three generations—all of it would go into the fire or the sea, and the clock itself would be re-gutted into obedient regulation movement, striking hours and nothing else, forever.

It should have been enough. It was nearly enough.

What gnawed at him was the apprentice. A Blackwood, inside the clock, *now*, with the case open and the guards outside knowing nothing of what they guarded. Ashgrave had seen the boy once, briefly, at the customs house steps—a narrow young man carrying a satchel of tools the way other men carried scripture, with eyes that measured everything and gave back nothing. Clockmakers' eyes. His grandfather had written of them with something close to reverence, and Silas Ashgrave had revered nothing on earth except money.

If the second escapement existed, the boy would find it. Perhaps he already had.

Ashgrave poured himself a measure of brandy from the confiscated stock and did not drink it. He stood holding the glass and watched the fog swallow the observatory dome entirely, and he permitted himself, for exactly ten seconds, the luxury of imagining what the clock might say if it spoke. Names. Debts. Dates. His own name, perhaps, in his grandfather's hand, attached to a theft dressed as a purchase. Forty-one pounds becoming a fortune becoming a colonelcy becoming this desk, this glass, this fog-bound kingdom of seized paper.

Then he put the fear away, as he had learned to do, folding it small and flat and driving it down into the place where he kept everything he could not afford to feel. What remained was procedure, and procedure was armor.

Three orders, then. He dipped his pen.

First: the observatory clock to be converted to regulation army time, effective immediately, the work performed by the local clockmaker under armed supervision. The keeper and his household confined to the observatory grounds—cooperation rewarded, interference punished.

Second: the workshop district condemned. Demolition in three weeks. All contents subject to confiscation inventory. *Inventory*, he wrote, and paused. Inventories invited readers. He struck the word and wrote instead: *All contents forfeit to the Crown.*

Third: the apprentice, Mirabel Blackwood, to complete the conversion personally. If the boy found anything in the mechanism—anything at all—he was to report it directly to the colonel. Directly. No sergeants, no captains. Ashgrave wanted to see the boy's face when he found whatever there was to find, and he wanted to be the last thing the boy saw before the finding stopped mattering.

He signed the orders with a flourish he had practiced at twenty and no longer needed, blotted them, and rang for the adjutant.

Through the window, the fog was lifting off the water in slow rags, and the harbor emerged piecemeal: masts, pier-ends, the humped backs of fishing boats riding at anchor, all of it his now, all of it entered in his columns. Somewhere below, a soldier's boot heels rang on cobbles in perfect regulation time. That was the sound of the future, Ashgrave thought. Not bells—boots. Not memory—inventory. A world where every hour belonged to the Crown and every account balanced in the Crown's favor, because the Crown held the pen.

He allowed himself a thin smile and finally drank the brandy. It was good stuff, better than he'd had in Falmouth Reach. Confiscation improved everything it touched.

Only one detail refused to sit quietly, and it circled him all through the afternoon's paperwork like a moth around a lamp: the timing. The keeper's daughter had sent for the clockmaker *the day before* the army arrived. Coincidence happened, but Ashgrave had built a career on refusing to believe in it. Someone in Grayharbor had known the soldiers were coming. Someone had wanted the clock opened first.

Which meant the Blackwood boy wasn't repairing a clock.

He was reading one.

Ashgrave stood, crossed to the window, and looked up through the thinning fog at the hill where the observatory stood, white and patient and full of teeth.

"Three weeks," he said aloud to the empty room, and the words sounded less like an order than a countdown.

* * *

That evening he walked the piers unescorted, as he always did in a newly taken town—not from courage but from method. A colonel who showed his face taught his men calmness better than any order. The fishermen watched him pass with the flat, shuttered expressions of people counting what remained to them. An old woman spat into the water as he went by, with great dignity, as though performing a civic duty.

At the foot of the observatory road he stopped and looked up. Lamplight showed in the tower's high windows. The clockmaker was working late.

A sergeant of the guard touched his cap. "Quiet day, Colonel. Keeper's cooperative. The girl keeps to herself."

"The girl."

"The keeper's daughter, sir. Sable Voss. She asked when the clock would strike true again."

Ashgrave felt the cold move up his spine and arrive, punctual as anything he commanded, at the base of his skull.

"And what did you tell her?"

"Told her the Crown keeps its own time now, sir."

"Good answer, Sergeant." He smiled, and the sergeant, wisely, did not smile back. "From tomorrow, no one enters the clock room but the clockmaker and a guard I appoint myself. No one. And Sergeant—if the boy removes so much as a single gear from that case, I want to know its number before he knows he's been seen."

The sergeant saluted and went. Ashgrave stood alone on the dark road, the harbor breathing behind him, the tower watching from above with its lit eyes, and admitted to the fog what he would admit to no living soul:

He was afraid of a clock.

Not of armies, not of courts, not of the men in London whose signatures his career depended upon—but of a machine built by a dead craftsman to remember what powerful men had paid generously to forget. His grandfather had feared it enough to write a warning in his final letter. Ashgrave had inherited the fear along with the forged fortune, the way one inherits a house with rot in its beams.

Well. Fear was fuel, if you burned it correctly.

He turned back toward the customs house, boots ringing on the wet stones, and behind him the lamplight in the tower burned on—steady, unhurried, keeping some older time than his.

In three weeks the workshop district would be ash. In one week the clock would strike only the Crown's hours. And if between now and then a young man with clockmakers' eyes found the second heart of the old mechanism—

Ashgrave's hand closed around the letter in his breast pocket, crushing the brittle paper a little further toward dust.

—then the boy would learn what happened to men who settled accounts the Crown had declared closed.
