## 7. Listening Before Touching

The guard at the observatory door had a name—Corporal Pell—and a habit of chewing the same wad of tobacco from dawn until his relief came at dusk. Mirabel learned this on the first morning of his captivity-in-miniature, because he had been raised by Odell Blackwood to learn everything: the wear-patterns in a man's boots, the tremor in a mainspring, the small betrayals of any mechanism, human or otherwise.

"Clockmaker." Pell said it the way other men said *thief*, though there was nothing in Mirabel's hands but a leather roll of tools and a notebook bound in gray board. "You go up. You look. You come down when I whistle. You don't touch nothing without me watching you touch it."

"I was sent to repair the clock," Mirabel said mildly. "The army's own order says so."

"The army's own order says you'll have it keeping army time by Friday fortnight. Until then, you're a pair of eyes and nothing else." Pell spat into the gutter, where the fog drank it. "Go on, then."

Mirabel climbed the observatory stair with the particular slowness of a man who has decided that every step is a sentence in an argument no one else knows he is making. Above him, through the tower's stone throat, came the sound he had been listening for since Odell first pressed the summons into his hand—the master clock breathing. Not ticking. Breathing. A great mechanism does not tick; it inhales and exhales, the escapement releasing the weight of the drive train one measured tooth at a time, and if you laid your palm against the case you could feel the whole tower living around you like a sleeping animal.

He had touched nothing yet. That was the discipline. *Listen before you touch.* His master's voice, thin as wire now, hoarse from the coughing that had begun to hollow him out over the winter. Mirabel carried the instruction the way other men carried coins—counted out each morning, spent carefully, never wasted.

In the clock room, the fog had followed him up, seeping through the louvres in pale ropes. The master clock stood against the east wall like a confessor: nine feet of black oak case, brass face, the pendulum swinging its slow sixty-beat arc behind a glass door gone cloudy with age. And beneath the face, behind a panel that any honest repairman would have opened on the first day—

Mirabel did not open it. He sat down on the floor instead, cross-legged, his notebook on his knee, and listened.

* * *

Three days he spent this way before he so much as removed a screw.

It was not idleness, whatever Corporal Pell believed, and whatever Sergeant Brann—who appeared on the second day and said nothing at all, only watched from the doorway with the stillness of a man counting something—suspected. It was the oldest discipline of the trade. A clock is a confession written in metal. Every irregularity in its voice is a symptom, and every symptom has a history, and if you put your hands on it before you understand its history you will not repair it—you will merely silence what it is trying to tell you.

So Mirabel listened, and wrote, and built in his notebook a portrait of the machine as thorough as anything a painter might have made.

The going train ran true. The striking train ran true. The pendulum's period was correct to within a hair's breadth—he checked it against his own pulse, then against the drip of condensation from the tower roof, which fell with almost mechanical regularity, forty drops to the minute. By every measurement the army would care about, the Grayharbor master clock was a healthy instrument running three seconds slow per day, which was well within tolerance for a municipal timepiece of its age.

But.

There was a hesitation in the strike train. Not a fault—a *pause*. Between the hour count and the final blow of the hammer, the train went quiet for exactly four seconds. Every hour. Four seconds of held breath, and then the last chime fell, and the pause was always the same length, always precisely four seconds, and a fault does not keep time. Only intention keeps time.

And there was the matter of the second escapement. He had found it on the first evening, or rather he had found its shadow: a faint drag in the wheels when the case was cold, a resistance that vanished as the brass warmed and expanded. Something inside the clock was engaged that should not be engaged, some auxiliary mechanism sleeping in the works like a second heart behind the first. He knew where it lived—behind the lower panel, in the space where a clockmaker would keep nothing but dust—but knowing where a thing lived and understanding why were separated by the whole distance between curiosity and courage.

Because he had understood, by the third night, what the hidden escapement meant. Someone had built it deliberately. Someone had wanted the clock to do more than tell the hour. And whoever that someone was, they had hidden their work so well that a year of the army's inspections had missed it entirely—which meant the army was looking, which meant the army feared something, which meant Mirabel was not repairing a clock so much as standing guard over a secret whose shape he could not yet see.

He bought time the only way a craftsman can: by being scrupulous. Each morning he presented Corporal Pell with a written report of findings, in a careful clerk's hand, full of words like *wear* and *tolerance* and *further assessment required*. Each report was true. None of them was complete. He requested parts from the workshop district—oil, files, a new suspension spring—and the requisitions crawled through the army's paperwork at the speed the army reserved for anything that was not marching or shooting, and every day the demolition of the workshop district came a day nearer, and every day Mirabel sat in the clock room and listened and did not touch, and hated himself a little for the slowness, and trusted Odell's instruction more than he hated himself.

On the fifth day, Sable Voss brought him letters.

* * *

She came up the stair with a covered basket—bread, a flask of tea, the ordinary charity of a keeper's daughter toward the workman quartered in her father's tower—and set it down beside him while Pell watched from the doorway with the suspicion of a man who believes all bread is contraband.

"Your master sent these," she said, too loudly, in the voice of a woman discussing weather. "From the workshop. He thought they'd help you with the repair."

"My master can barely lift his head," Mirabel said, matching her volume. "What repair could they help with?"

"He was very insistent." Her eyes, dark and level, said the rest. *Take them. Don't ask here.*

Pell rummaged the basket, found bread and tea and nothing else, and lost interest. The letters lay beneath the loaf, wrapped in oilcloth, six of them, tied with string, addressed in a hand Mirabel knew better than his own—Odell's cramped, upright script, each letter formed like a component machined to tolerance.

He waited until dusk, until Pell's relief whistled from the street below and the corporal clattered down the stairs to hand off his post, until the tower settled into the long hush it kept between the hours. Then he unwrapped the oilcloth by candlelight, and saw that the letters were old—yellowed, folded and refolded many years ago, the ink faded to the brown of dried blood. They were not addressed to him. They were addressed to Odell, and the earliest bore a date thirty-one years past.

He read the first one twice, because the first reading made no sense, and the second made worse sense, and both times his eye snagged on the same line:

*The harbor company has struck eleven names from the ledger this quarter alone. My father's among them. They say the debts were never owed, and so the boats were never owed, and so the boats are theirs. Paper burns, Odell. Metal remembers. Come to the observatory when you're old enough to hold a file, and I will show you what metal remembers.*

There was no signature. There was instead a small mark drawn beneath the final line, and Mirabel's breath stopped in his chest, because he knew the mark. He had seen it stamped inside the case of half the finest clocks on the coast, the maker's sign of a house everyone assumed had died out before Mirabel was born: a wheel with eight spokes, and one spoke cut short.

His grandfather's mark. The grandfather who had left Grayharbor—or been driven from it—before Odell ever took an apprentice, the grandfather whose tools hung at the back of the workshop wrapped in wax cloth, never used, never named. Odell had spoken of him perhaps four times in twelve years, and always in the tone of a man closing a door.

Mirabel read the remaining five letters by the candle down to its stub. They traced a correspondence across two decades—his grandfather writing from wherever exile had taken him, Odell answering, the subject always circling back to the same wound: the harbor company's ledger, the names struck from it, the inheritances dissolved, the fortunes of Grayharbor quietly rerouted into the pockets of men whose descendants now wore red coats and called themselves the law. And threaded through all of it, growing clearer with each letter like a figure emerging from fog, was the clock. The master clock. Built to Odell's grandfather's specification, installed under cover of a municipal contract, its true purpose known to exactly two men alive—one of them dying now in a workshop slated for demolition, the other sitting in the dark with the letters shaking in his hands.

*Metal remembers,* the first letter had said. And Mirabel understood at last why he had been summoned, why Odell—who had apprentices enough and strength for none of them—had sent specifically the boy who had learned to listen before touching. This was not a repair commission. It had never been a repair commission. It was an inheritance, executed by way of a clockmaker's instruction, and the estate was a secret, and the debt attached to it was everything.

He heard footsteps on the stair—soft ones, deliberate ones—and had the letters back under the oilcloth and the oilcloth under his coat lining before the door swung open. Sable stood in the frame with a shuttered lantern, her father's keys at her belt, and looked at him for a long moment with an expression he could not read.

"You read them," she said. Not a question.

"You knew what they were."

"I've known since I was nine years old," she said, coming into the room and closing the door behind her. "My father helped install the second escapement. He's kept the clock's true account running for thirty years, and for the last year the army has been winding it wrong on purpose, and he's too old to climb the tower anymore, and then the soldiers came and took the ledger, and—" She stopped. Steadied herself the way she probably steadied the telescope mounts, with small precise adjustments invisible from outside. "Do you know what's behind the lower panel yet?"

"I know something is."

"Then listen," Sable Voss said, "and when you finally touch it, touch it like it's the last honest thing in Grayharbor. Because it may be."

She left him the candle stub and took the basket, and Mirabel sat alone in the breathing dark of the clock room with his grandfather's letters warm against his ribs, and above him the pendulum swung its patient arc, keeping a time no one in the army had thought to measure.

Four days remained before Friday fortnight. Somewhere below, the workshop district slept under bayonets, and Odell coughed in his bed, and Sergeant Brann walked his rounds past the observatory door, pausing once—Mirabel heard the boots stop, heard them wait, heard them move on—in a silence that felt less like patrol than like a question being asked of the tower itself.

Mirabel blew out the candle and answered it the only way he could.

He listened.
