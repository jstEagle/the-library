## 18. Dawn of the Names

Mirabel had not slept. He had not meant to sleep, and now, with the first grey seeping through the observatory's eastern glass, he was glad of it, because sleep would have stolen the last hour from him, and the last hour was the only hour he had ever truly wanted.

The clock room was cold. It was always cold—the keeper had complained of it for thirty years, and Sable complained of it now, her breath fogging as she stood beside him at the base of the great case, a lantern turned low between them. Above them the works rose through three floors of darkness, wheels within wheels, the pendulum's slow heartbeat descending through the building's bones into the rock of the headland itself. Mirabel had spent ten days inside that heartbeat. He had eaten in it, bled in it, cut his thumb twice and let the blood dry on the brass because he could not spare the moment to wash it. The escapement he had recut sat in its cradle above him like a heart he had rebuilt with his own hands, and he did not know yet whether it would beat.

*Listen before you touch,* Odell had said, and Odell was three days dead, and Mirabel had listened to everything since—every tick, every half-beat of drag in the train, every whisper of the fly fan—until he could hear the clock the way a mother hears a child breathing in the next room.

"It's a quarter to five," Sable said. Her voice was steady, but her hands were not; she kept them folded so he would not see. "They'll change the guard at the pier at five. Brann said the colonel sleeps until the first bell. He likes to be woken by it. He said—" she stopped, and laughed once, a small terrible sound, "—he said he liked the town to know its master rose before it did."

"Then he'll be the first to hear," Mirabel said.

Below them, Grayharbor lay under fog and occupation. From the observatory gallery he could see the whole curve of the harbor: the fishing fleet at anchor, masts bare; the customs house flying the army's colours; the red-coated pickets at the pier heads, small as spilled wax in the blue-grey light. The workshop district was half rubble now, the demolition crews working ahead of schedule, and the smell of burnt lime came up the headland on the wind. Somewhere down there were the index pages Brann had carried through the lines two nights ago, hidden in the observatory's coal cellar, and somewhere down there were six thousand people who had spent a year listening to a clock that lied to them.

Tonight it would tell the truth.

He checked the escapement one last time, though there was nothing left to check. The teeth he had recut were not the teeth his grandfather had cut—Odell's letters had described the originals, and Mirabel had followed the descriptions as a blind man follows a hand on his shoulder—but they were true to the scheme. Each tooth of the count wheel carried a value. Each strike of the great bell would advance the sequence one step: a name, spelled in the old harbor code of long-and-short strokes that every fisherman in Grayharbor had used to mark his nets; a debt, in hundreds; a date, in the company's own ledger reckoning. Ninety-one strikes to the sequence. The first name was *Abernathy, Tomas, boat *Fairwende*, seized 1821, debt 400, heir living.* The last name was *Ashgrave.*

His grandfather had not put the colonel's grandfather at the end of the sequence by accident. Odell's letters had been clear on that. The last name was the nail.

"You should stand away from the case when it starts," Sable said. "If something's wrong—if a tooth shears—"

"Then I'll be close enough to hear which tooth."

"That's not an argument, that's a death wish."

"It's a trade," Mirabel said. "I'm a tradesman. I know my stock."

She almost smiled. Then the fog below them shifted, and through the gap they saw the harbor square, and the soldiers already gathering there—not a picket, a formation, two ranks of them drawn up before the customs house with their muskets shouldered, and at their head, on a grey horse that stamped and tossed in the cold, a figure in a colonel's coat.

"He knows," Sable whispered.

"No," Mirabel said. His mouth had gone dry. "He suspects. There's a difference. A clockmaker lives on the difference."

The first light came up the harbor like a tide.

* * *

At five minutes to the hour, Mirabel climbed the ladder to the striking train and put his ear against the great wheel's frame.

The clock wanted to strike six. It had wanted to strike six for ten days, wound and held, the release lever pinned back by a wedge of oak no bigger than a hymn book. All he had to do was pull the wedge. All he had to do was let a machine do what it had been built—by his grandfather, in secret, in this very room, sixty years ago—to do.

*Listen before you touch.*

He listened. The escapement ticked. The pendulum swung, patient as tide. Somewhere below, the fog lay over the town like a sheet over a face, and beneath the sheet, six thousand hearts.

He thought of Odell in his last week, too weak to lift a file, directing Mirabel's hands across the bench by voice alone, saying *no—there—the angle, boy, the angle is everything, a tooth cut at the wrong angle tells a lie as smoothly as a man does*—and he thought of his grandfather he had never met, cutting the original teeth by candlelight while the harbor company erased a town's memory one ledger line at a time, and he thought: *we are three generations of one long repair, and tonight the clock comes home.*

He pulled the wedge.

For one full second, nothing. The pendulum swung. The fog breathed. Then, high above, the gathering piece dropped, the great wheel took up its load with a groan that Mirabel felt in his teeth, and the first hammer rose and fell against the bell.

The note went out over Grayharbor like a blade of light.

One strike. A long one. Then a pause, and two short strikes, and Mirabel was already counting against the code in his head, *long, short-short—A*—and Sable, beside him, had gone white, because she had grown up with the net-marks on every hull in the harbor and she knew it too.

*A. Abernathy.*

The second name began. *Long, long, short—R.* The bell rolled it out, each stroke falling into the fog and not dying there but carrying, out across the water, off the cliff faces of the headland, back again, the harbor itself repeating the name like a congregation.

Down in the square, the formation of soldiers shifted. Mirabel saw it from the gallery as he ran back to the case: heads turning up toward the observatory, an officer's arm rising, falling. The colonel on his grey horse sat very still.

*Long, short, long, long—B. Brannigan.* The fish-curer on the east pier, struck from the ledger in '29. *Long-short-short—D. Duncannon.* The widow's house on Rope Walk. Name after name, debt after debt, the bell tolling out sixty years of theft in a language the whole town could read, and the fog doing what fog does in Grayharbor at dawn—lifting, thinning, letting the sound through.

"They'll hear it in the fleet," Sable said. Her voice had changed; there was something in it Mirabel had never heard, something between weeping and singing. "Mirabel—they'll hear it across the whole bay."

The first boat moved at the ninth name. He saw it from the gallery: a fishing lugger, unlit, slipping its mooring and standing in toward the customs house stairs, and then another, and another, not in any order, the way a shoal turns, all at once, as if the water itself had decided. Men were coming up from the dockside lanes. He could see them now in the growing light—dockworkers in their night shirts with their jackets thrown over, women with shawls, an old man with a boat hook walking as straight as a parade.

And the soldiers. That was the thing that made Mirabel's hands shake on the gallery rail. The soldiers were not moving. Two ranks of red coats, and they stood, and they listened, and Mirabel understood, with a lurch, that the army drew half its men from the coastal counties—from towns like this one, towns that had their own ledgers, their own seized boats, their own names. He watched a young private turn his head slowly toward his sergeant, and he watched the sergeant not correct him.

The bell struck the fortieth name. *Harrow, Ellen, debt 220, heir living.* A woman in the square below cried out—a short, hard sound, like a stitch tearing—and then another voice took it up, and another, not in fear, in *answer*, the crowd calling the names back at the clock as the bell rang them out, and the sound that rose off the harbor then was not a riot and not a prayer but a town reading its own account aloud after sixty years of silence.

The colonel's horse screamed and reared. Ashgrave fought it down and drew his sword, and even at this distance Mirabel could see the sword was shaking, or perhaps that was the man.

* * *

They came up the observatory stairs at the fifty-eighth name.

Not the colonel—the colonel was still in the square, bellowing orders that the fog and the bell were eating alive. It was the duty picket, six men with fixed bayonets, and at their head, his face grey as the dawn, Sergeant Brann.

Mirabel stepped between the soldiers and the clock case. Behind him, Sable did not move away from the escapement; she put her hand flat on the frame, as if she could hold the sequence in the world by touch.

"Sergeant," Mirabel said. "You're just in time. We're nearly at the K's."

"You were ordered to repair the clock to regulation time." Brann's voice was a strange, flat thing, a man reading out a sentence he had already served. "You were ordered—"

"I was ordered to silence it. There's a difference." Mirabel held the man's eyes. "You've been counting the teeth for ten days, Sergeant. You know what they say. You've known for a week."

The bell struck the sixty-first name, and the whole tower shivered with it, and Brann's jaw worked. One of the soldiers behind him—a boy, sixteen at the outside—said, quietly, not to anyone: "That's my gran's name."

"Stand fast," Brann said, but it came out as a whisper, and no one stood fast and no one moved at all, and the bell struck the sixty-second.

Down in the square, a musket discharged—wild, high, the ball singing off the tower's stonework somewhere above them, and the crowd did not scatter. It *closed*. Mirabel saw it happen: the formation of red coats swallowed by grey coats and shawls and bare hands, not fighting, not yet, just *surrounding*, the way water surrounds a rock, patient, certain of the tide.

"Ashgrave's ordered the clock shot down," Brann said. "He sent for the field piece at the customs house. It'll be here in minutes." His eyes went past Mirabel to the great case, to the works climbing into the dark, and something in his face broke and reassembled itself into a decision. He turned to his men. "Sergeant Hale. Take four men to the stair. No one comes up this tower without my word. No one."

"Whose word, Sergeant?" Hale asked. There was no insolence in it. There was something worse: a real question.

"The town's," Brann said.

The bell struck the seventy-ninth name, and out over the water the whole fishing fleet was moving now, every hull in the harbor standing in for the shore, and from the headland road came the sound of carts, of feet, of people coming up from the villages to hear what the clock was saying.

*Long, long, long, short—A. Ashgrave.*

Mirabel heard the name ring out and felt it in his chest like a struck anvil. Below, in the square, the crowd took it up, six thousand voices, and the name that had been a sentence for sixty years became, in a single morning, an accusation.

The colonel's voice rose over it, thin and furious: *the field piece, God damn you, bring the field piece—*

The bell struck the eighty-first. The eighty-second. The tower shook. Somewhere below, on the harbor road, Mirabel could hear the deep iron-rimmed wheels of a gun carriage, and the hooves of the horses hauling it, coming fast.

"Mirabel." Sable's hand closed on his sleeve. Her face was lit by the lantern and by something brighter than the lantern. "It's going to finish. Whatever they do—it's going to finish."

The bell struck the eighty-fifth, and the gun came around the headland road into the square, and the crowd parted before it and closed behind it, and the last five names of Grayharbor's true ledger rose over the occupied town like a sun coming up.
