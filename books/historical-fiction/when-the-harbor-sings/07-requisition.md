## 7. Requisition

They came for the gallery on a morning so clear the horizon looked cut with a knife.

Paloma saw them first as ants on the cliff road — six soldiers in gray-blue, rifles slung, hauling crates and coiled wire — and she had time to bank the stove, straighten her collar, and put on the face she'd been practicing for three years: the face of a woman with nothing to hide and nothing worth taking.

The knock, when it came, was military in its courtesy. Three blows, evenly spaced, as if the door itself were being processed.

"Madam Okonkwo." The officer at the front was young, square-jawed, with the careful diction of a man who had learned his manners from a manual. "Lieutenant Sorrel. We're establishing a signal station. The army requires use of the gallery and watch room."

"The gallery," Paloma said. "Not the lamp."

"Orders." He produced a paper, crisp as frost. "The light is classified as strategic. It remains in civilian operation — yours, provisionally — pending review of the keeper's credentials."

*Pending review.* The words settled in her chest like cold ballast. She thought of Emeka's license, that absent scrap of paper, and made her own face into a wall.

"You'll find the gallery stairs behind that door," she said. "Mind the third step. It's been loose since before the war."

Sorrel blinked, as though he'd expected argument and been cheated of it. "You're cooperative."

"I keep a light, Lieutenant. The army keeping a flagpole on my roof doesn't change the work." She stepped aside. "There's tea on the stove, if your men want it. It's going to be a long day for all of us."

He hesitated in the doorway — a young man briefly out of his depth, confronted by a widow who offered tea instead of resistance — and then the machinery of his orders caught him again and he turned to bellow at his corporal about the heliograph crates.

* * *

They were installed by nightfall.

The gallery sprouted a signal mast; the watch room filled with a shuttered lamp, codebooks, a telegraph key that chattered half the night like a nervous bird. Sorrel kept two men on rotation and himself slept — when he slept — in the watch room's corner bunk, so that the top of Paloma's lighthouse now belonged, in all but name, to the army of a country she had never chosen to belong to.

The lamp room remained hers. That was the bargain, and she learned its shape quickly: she climbed at dusk, lit the wick, wound the clockwork, polished the lens until it threw its white blade across the water — and every hour of it was performed under the eyes of soldiers who logged her comings and goings in a book of their own.

She had spent three years being invisible. Now she was the most watched woman on the coast.

"You keep it bright," Sorrel observed one evening, standing in the lamp room doorway with his hands behind his back, watching her trim the wick. "Even with the war on. Even with the requisition notices on every post in town."

"A dark light is a wrecked coast," she said. "The army wants this harbor. A wrecked coast is bad for the army."

Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion, exactly. Recognition, maybe, of a logic he couldn't fault and didn't like. "You argue like a quartermaster, madam."

"My husband argued like a keeper. I only learned the parts that keep me fed." She closed the lens door. "Good night, Lieutenant."

* * *

The cellar was the problem.

It had always been the problem, and now it was the problem with soldiers sleeping forty feet above it. The network Emeka had built — the cot behind the coal bins, the false wall of salted fish barrels, the rope ladder to the old storm drain — had sheltered eleven souls in three years, and two of them were in it now: a fisherman named Brandt whose debts had grown teeth, and a boy of maybe nineteen who'd walked out of the coastal militia with his boots and nothing else and would not say his name.

Adaeze managed them the way she managed everything, with a patience that had outlived her own body's strength. She carried down broth at hours when the gallery watch changed, when boots on the iron stairs made enough noise to cover her own. She mended the boy's jacket and told him stories in her low, unhurried voice, and Paloma would hear the murmur of it through the floorboards and think: *she is spending her last coins on a stranger, and calling it nothing.*

"You should rest," Paloma told her one night, kneeling at the cellar hatch with a covered pot.

"I rest when I'm dead, and I'm told the queue is long." Adaeze took the pot. Her hands were thin as winter branches, but steady. "The boy had a nightmare. He was calling for a mother. I sat with him. That's not labor, Paloma. That's just what you do."

Paloma said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't grief, and grief was a currency she was saving.

The discipline of the new life was this: two faces, worn in alternation, never at the same hour. Above, the obedient widow — tea at the right temperature, the lamp lit on schedule, her answers short and her eyes down. Below, the keeper of the other kind of light — extra oil smuggled down in a flour sack, bread baked after midnight, the hatch oiled so it would never, ever creak.

She learned the soldiers' rhythms as she'd once learned the tides. Sorrel's watch turned at nine and three. The corporal, a heavy man named Doss, liked his brandy and slept like ballast. The telegraph went quiet between two and four, and in those hours the cottage was almost hers again — almost, never quite, the word *almost* nailed over every door.

* * *

The first near-disaster came in the second week, on a night of hard rain.

Brandt's cough. It had been building for days, a wet rattle in his chest that Adaeze dosed with honey and gin, and on that night it broke loose into a fit that carried — Paloma knew it even from the lamp room, her heart stopping mid-wind of the clockwork — up through the floor, up the stairwell, into the gallery where young Private Enns stood his watch.

She was down the stairs before she'd decided to move, and found Enns at the top of the cellar steps with a lantern, frowning.

"Heard something."

"Cellar," Paloma said, and her voice came out level, which was a miracle she did not examine. "The drain backs up in rain. I was going to check it."

He looked at her. He was nineteen at most, rain-wet, homesick in the set of his mouth. "You go down alone? In the dark?"

"I've gone down alone in worse than dark, Private."

He hesitated — and then, God bless the boredom of soldiers, he yawned. "Corporal says not to wander. You need a hand, shout."

"Shouting's the last thing I'd do in a storm." She took the lantern from him gently, a theft disguised as a courtesy, and descended.

Brandt was gray-faced against the wall, fist jammed in his mouth, tears of effort in his eyes. The boy — the deserter — had a hand on his shoulder, terrified. Adaeze stood with her back against the fish barrels, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman who had been expecting company.

"Again," Paloma whispered, and Brandt, understanding, gave her one more cough — a real one, torn out of him — and then crushed his sleeve against his face and held still.

She climbed back up, returned the lantern, and smiled at Enns. "Drain's clear. Thank you for the light."

"Ma'am." He touched his cap, and went back to his rain, and she climbed to the lamp room with her legs shaking so badly she had to sit on the stairs for a full minute, in the dark, breathing like a woman who had surfaced from deep water.

That was the arithmetic of it now. Every day, a coin spent. Every day, a coin earned back by luck or by the small mercies of tired boys. She did not know how long the purse would hold.

* * *

Sorrel was harder to read than his men.

He took his supper at her table three nights a week — army regulations, he explained, required the officer to verify the civilian staff's provisioning, a sentence so bureaucratic it could only mean he wanted someone to talk to. He ate everything she served and complimented the fish pie with what sounded like genuine surprise. He asked about the light: its range, its history, the clockwork's eccentricities. He never asked about her husband, which she understood to be a kindness, or a discipline, or both.

"You were born here?" he asked one evening, in the lull between courses.

"On this rock, yes. My father fished. My husband kept this light."

"And you keep it now."

"I keep it now."

He turned his glass slowly on the cloth. "There's a review of keeper's credentials coming. From the district office." He said it flatly, a man delivering weather. "I'm required to tell you. It's nothing — a formality. Paperwork."

"Paperwork," Paloma repeated, and heard in her own voice a note she hadn't intended, some hairline fracture, and Sorrel looked up sharply.

"You've misplaced something," he said. Not a question.

"I've misplaced a great many things, Lieutenant. It's what the sea does." She stood to clear the plates. "More tea?"

He watched her a moment too long, then nodded, and let it go — and she carried the dishes to the basin with her heart hammering and her face composed, and thought: *he sees. He sees something, and he is deciding, night by night, whether it matters to him.*

* * *

The hatch, the flour sack, the oiled hinges. The cot behind the coal. She moved through her treason like a woman kneading bread, hands remembering, mind elsewhere, the work itself become prayer.

Adaeze found her at the cellar door past midnight, the week's oil can in her hand.

"You're thinner," Adaeze said.

"You're one to talk."

"I'm dying, dear. I have an excuse. You're only frightened." Adaeze leaned against the wall, and in the dark her eyes were bright and terrible. "Do you know what I think about, when I sit down here with that boy? I think about Emeka. He used to say — what was it — *the light doesn't ask who's worth saving.* He'd laugh when he said it. He thought it was funny." She paused. "It's not funny, Paloma. It's the truest thing anyone ever said in this house."

"Go to bed, Adaeze."

"In a minute." She touched Paloma's wrist, light as ash falling. "You're doing two jobs and wearing one face. It's wearing through. I can see the shape underneath." A cough took her then, deep and long, and Paloma held her elbow until it passed, and neither of them said what the cough was saying.

"Bed," Paloma said again, softer.

Adaeze went. Paloma stood alone in the dark at the top of the cellar stairs, the oil can cold in her hand, and listened to the house: the telegraph's far chatter above, the two heartbeats below, the sea working at the rocks like a patient creditor.

*Pending review.* The district office. Paperwork. Somewhere in a drawer in the capital sat the absence of Emeka's name, and it was traveling toward her with the slow certainty of weather.

She banked the stove, checked the hatch, and climbed to the lamp room to begin the night's honest work — the one face she never had to wear, the light that didn't ask.

At the gallery door she paused. Sorrel stood at the rail in the wind, looking out at the black water, and without turning he said:

"Storm coming Thursday. Barometer's falling fast. You'll want extra oil."

"Yes," Paloma said. "I'll want extra oil."

And she thought, as she passed him into the lamp room: *so will the men in my cellar, and so will every boat the army's dark orders put on that water — and I will find a way to get it to all of us, or I will burn this whole careful arrangement down trying.*

She lit the lamp. The lens turned. Below, in the dark, the town slept around its notices and its ledger and its coming arithmetic, and Paloma Okonkwo stood her watch with two faces folded away and a third one — the true one, the keeper's — burning steadily behind her eyes.

Thursday was coming. So was the review. So, she did not yet know, was a list of names with berths beside them, and a choice no arithmetic should ever be asked to make.

But the light, at least, was hers until then. She meant to keep it that way.
