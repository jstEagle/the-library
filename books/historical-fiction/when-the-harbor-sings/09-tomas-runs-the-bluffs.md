## 9. Tomas Runs the Bluffs

The message was sewn into the collar of his coat, which meant it mattered.

Tomas knew the hierarchy of his aunt's errands by now. Bread to Widow Marsh at the head of the harbor road — that went in his hands, plain as anything, because bread was only bread. Oil for the boats at the north slip went in a covered pail, and nobody looked twice at a boy hauling oil. But when Paloma sat him down at the kitchen table with a needle and thread and her mouth pressed into that thin line she made when she was frightened and refusing to say so, he understood: this one would cost him if he were caught.

"Say it back," she said.

"The tide's wrong for the army's dredges on Thursday. The *Maren* can run the channel after dark if the fog holds." He recited it flat, the way she'd taught him, no music in it, nothing worth remembering by an eavesdropper. "If there's no fog, wait for the moonless night after."

"And if anyone stops you?"

"I'm gathering gull eggs for Adaeze. She has a craving. Everyone knows sick women get cravings."

Adaeze, propped in her chair by the window with a blanket over her knees despite the mildness of the afternoon, laughed — a real laugh, bright and startling, one of the last ones. "I have never had a craving in my life."

"You do now," Paloma said, threading the needle without looking up. "You crave gull eggs. Terribly."

Tomas held still while she worked the stitches into his collar, small and even, invisible unless you knew where to put your finger. Her hands smelled of lamp oil and salt. He watched the knot disappear under the fabric and thought about how strange it was that the most dangerous thing he owned weighed less than a feather.

"There." She smoothed the collar flat. "The *Maren*'s skipper will be on the bluff past the old signal post, or his son will. You give them nothing with your mouth. You touch your left ear twice if the answer is yes, once if no. That's all."

"What if they ask me something else?"

"They won't. And if they do, you're a boy with gull eggs who knows nothing about tides or ships or anything but eggs." She took his chin in her oil-smelling hand and turned his face toward hers. Her eyes were the same brown as Emeka's had been — he knew that from the photograph on the mantel, because he'd been too young when his uncle died to remember the man himself, only the shape of him leaving rooms. "Tomas. Look at me. Pride is a fine thing on a calm day. On a bad day, it drowns people. If a patrol comes, you run first and feel brave later. Promise me."

"I promise."

"He promises," Adaeze said from the window, "the way all boys promise, which is to say beautifully and not at all."

Tomas grinned at her, and she grinned back, and for a moment the kitchen was warm all the way through, the way it had been before the gray ships came. Then Paloma kissed the top of his head, and the moment closed like water over a stone, and he went out into the wind with the eggs basket on his arm and Thursday's secret stitched against his neck.

* * *

The bluffs south of the harbor were the wild part of the coast, the part the army hadn't bothered to fence yet because there was nothing there to take — no anchorage, no road wide enough for guns, just grass bent flat by the wind and the long fall of black rock to the sea. The path along the top was a sheep track at best. Tomas ran it the way he'd run it since he was six, reading the ground with his feet, knowing where the turf gave way to loose shale, where the gulls nested and screamed their outrage at any passing thing.

He liked this part. He wouldn't have admitted it under torture, but he liked all of it — the running, the messages, the coded touches of his ear, the way grown men at the north slip nodded to him now as though he were a person and not a child underfoot. Before the requisition, he had been the boy who fetched things. Now he was a link in something, a strand in a net that stretched from his aunt's cellar to the fishing fleet to — wherever it stretched. He didn't know the whole of it. Nobody told children the whole of it. But he could feel the shape of it, the way you could feel the shape of a current under a boat, and it made him walk taller.

The trouble was that pride walked tall right into patrols.

He heard them before he saw them — boots, many boots, and the particular clipped rhythm of soldiers' marching, which was nothing like fishermen's rolling gait. They came around the headland below him, six of them and a dog, moving up the cliff path from the cove where the army had been blasting stone for the breakwater. Between Tomas and home lay two hundred yards of open grass and no cover taller than a knee.

The dog lifted its head.

Tomas did not think. Later, lying in the dark of the cave with his heart slamming, he would try to reconstruct the decision and find there wasn't one — just his legs taking over, just the word *run* arriving in his body before it arrived in his mind, exactly as his aunt had ordered. He cut left off the track, down the slope where no path existed, letting gravity have him, the egg basket banging his hip, gulls rising around his head in a shrieking white storm that was, God help him, cover of a kind, because the whole sky went up in noise at once and the soldiers' shouts dissolved into it.

The slope steepened. His feet went out from under him and he rode the scree on his backside, hands out, rocks biting through his trousers, until the ground ended and he saw, three feet below, the slot in the cliff face — the low black mouth he'd known about all his life the way you know about a hole in a fence you've never needed.

He dropped into it.

Inside, the world went from roaring daylight to a darkness so total it felt physical, a hand laid over his eyes. He stood still, chest heaving, listening. Above and behind him, muffled by rock, the boots came on, slowed, stopped. The dog whined. A voice said something he couldn't make out. Another voice answered, bored. Gull eggs, probably. Boys fell down cliffs chasing gull eggs every season; it was practically a local industry, burying them.

Then the boots moved on, and the sea filled the silence, booming somewhere below and ahead of him in the cave's throat, and Tomas Okonkwo sat down on cold stone in the dark and shook.

It took a long time for the shaking to stop. When it did, what replaced it was worse: a slow, spreading heat in his chest that he recognized, with some embarrassment, as pride. He had run when he should run. He had kept the collar silent. Somewhere above him a patrol was reporting a boy and a dog and a lot of gulls, and the *Maren*'s secret was still stitched safe against his neck, and nobody in the world knew where he was except the sea.

He almost laughed. Then his eyes had adjusted enough to show him that he was not alone in the dark.

* * *

The cave was deeper than he'd ever gone. As a smaller boy he'd played in the mouth of it, daring each other to go past the bend where the light failed, and none of them ever had, because beyond the bend the floor dropped away into pools you couldn't see the bottom of, and the sound the sea made in there was the sound of something breathing. Now, with nothing to lose and no way home except forward or up, Tomas felt his way along the wall, one palm on wet rock, counting his steps the way Paloma counted the turns of the lamp mechanism — steadily, without hurry, because hurry was how people died in the dark.

The passage narrowed, then opened, and air moved against his face — sea air, tasting of kelp, coming from ahead. Faint gray light leaked in from somewhere, a crack in the cliff he couldn't see, enough to make shapes. And the shapes resolved, one by one, into things that should not have been there.

Crates. Barrels, small ones, the kind that held lamp oil. A stack of wool blankets wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord gone green with age. Tins — he touched one, then another — hardtack, salt meat, the wax-sealed jars his aunt used for rendered fat. A lantern, rusted at the handle but whole. And against the far wall, half-hidden behind a barrel, a wooden box with a slanted lid, the kind churches kept their alms in, except this one had no lock and no cross, only letters burned into the wood with a hot iron, crude and deep:

**FOR THE RUNNING**

Tomas stood very still.

He knew — everyone in the cottage knew — that the network had come from Emeka. That was the story: Uncle Emeka, dead three years, had started it, and Aunt Paloma kept it going because stopping would have been a kind of betrayal. It made Emeka a legend in Tomas's private mythology, a man he'd never really met who had done large and dangerous things.

But this cache was old. The cord was green. The oilcloth had cracked and resealed itself a dozen times. The hardtack tins were rusted at the seams. Somebody had stocked this cave years ago — five years, ten, more — and somebody had stocked it *well*, with the patience of a person who expected to wait a long time between guests. This wasn't Emeka's work. Or if it was, Emeka had learned it from someone, inherited it the way Paloma had inherited it from him, the way Tomas was inheriting it now, stitch by stitch and ear-touch by ear-touch, a rope handed down hand to hand into the dark.

His fingers found the box lid and lifted it. Inside: coins, a few, worn smooth. A folded paper, stiff with damp, that he didn't dare open in the faint light. And at the bottom, a child's shoe, tiny, leather, long since parted company with its pair.

He didn't know why the shoe undid him. It just did. He crouched there holding a shoe small enough to fit in his two palms together, and understood suddenly that the cave had been doing this work since before he was born — that some other mother, some other aunt, some other boy maybe, had waited in this exact dark while boots passed overhead, and had run afterward along these same bluffs, and had grown old or drowned or escaped, and none of it was written anywhere, and all of it was true. The ledger in town recorded names and trades and claims. The cave recorded nothing. The cave just kept its supplies dry and its mouth shut and waited for the next runner.

*Terror and pride arrive together,* he thought, surprising himself with the sentence, *and you don't get one without the other.* It sounded like something Adaeze would say. He decided to tell her he'd thought of it himself.

He took what he could carry — one tin of hardtack, the paper, careful not to tear it — and left everything else exactly as it lay, because a cache was a promise to whoever came next, and he was not going to be the boy who broke a ten-year promise on his first day of knowing it existed. At the mouth of the passage he paused, looked back at the gray-lit shapes, and did the only salute he knew: two fingers to his left ear. *Yes. Understood.*

Then he climbed.

* * *

The light outside was going gold and long. From the top of the slope he could see the harbor, the gray ships at anchor, the smoke of the army cookfires, and beyond them the lighthouse standing white against the darkening water, its gallery crowded now with the soldiers' signal mast — but the lamp room still his aunt's, still burning its patient eye as the sun went down. Home. Two hundred yards of open grass between him and home, and no patrol in sight, and a secret in his collar and a secret in his pocket and a third secret, the largest one, sitting in his chest where no clerk would ever find it.

He ran the last stretch with the wind at his back, and burst through the cottage door breathless, salt-crusted, bleeding at both elbows, holding the egg basket — four eggs, miraculously unbroken — like a trophy.

Adaeze looked up from her chair. Paloma turned from the stove. Whatever was in his face made them both go still.

"Tomas," Paloma said carefully. "What happened?"

"Auntie." He set down the basket. He pulled the folded paper from inside his shirt and held it out, and his hand was shaking, and he found he didn't care. "How old is the network? Really. Because I found the cave past the bend — the one we never went past — and somebody's been stocking it since before I was born, and there's a shoe in the box, Auntie, a *baby's shoe*, and—" He stopped, out of breath again, and asked the question that had been growing in him the whole run home, the one that changed the size of everything:

"Who was here before us?"

Paloma took the paper. She opened it with hands that were not quite steady either, and read whatever was written there, and when she looked up her eyes were shining in a way Tomas had never seen — grief and recognition tangled together like rope.

"That," she said quietly, "is a longer story than you know. Sit down. Both of you sit down."
