## 22. When the Harbor Sings

The village had no name on any map worth trusting. The people who lived there called it simply *the other shore*, and the children born there called it *here*, and that was the whole of its geography. Paloma learned this in her first week, standing in the cold spring wind above a harbor that ran east to west instead of north to south, everything reversed, the sun rising over open water instead of behind her shoulder, so that for the first month she felt perpetually turned around, as if she were walking backward through her own life.

She did not mind. Backward was a direction too. Backward was how she had come.

The cottage they were given sat on a shelf of gray rock above the water, smaller than the keeper's house had been, with a chimney that smoked when the wind came from the wrong quarter and a door that swelled in damp weather until Tomas had to throw his whole boyish weight against it. Adaeze called the door *stubborn* and claimed it reminded her of someone, and she would look at Paloma when she said it, and Paloma would pretend not to hear, and this was a kind of laughter between them, the first they had managed since the crossing.

Three years had passed since the storm. Three years since the gangplank, since the manifest that allowed only two, since she had stood on the stones and watched the ship's lights dwindle into the black water while she turned back up the hill to a lamp room full of smoke and salt and the smell of burned paper. Three years since a wounded captain had bled on her floor while she stitched him by lamplight and neither of them spoke of ledgers or legality or the word *requisition*. Three years since a small boat had carried her out through the shoals in the storm's dying tail, running dark, running north, with the last of the stragglers huddled in her bilge and the harbor singing behind her in foghorn blasts that she had let herself believe were farewell.

She had thought, in those first weeks on the other shore, that she had left the light behind forever. That was the grief she had not prepared for — not the losing of Emeka, not the losing of the house, but the losing of the work. A woman can survive the loss of almost anything if she still has her hands full. But the new harbor had its own keepers, its own families, its own arrangements older than anyone could remember, and there was no post for a widow from a requisitioned coast, no license, no ledger line with her name on it.

She had learned, slowly, that this was a kind of freedom. And then, more slowly, that freedom without work is only a larger kind of loneliness.

So she had done what she had always done. She had found the boats that went out in weather other boats refused, and she had offered to keep a light for them. There was no official tower — the village relied on a headland fire, tended by rotation, a duty passed around like a communal chore. Paloma began taking more than her share. She took the storm nights. She took the nights when the barometer fell and the old men shook their heads and the young ones went out anyway because fish do not care about barometers. She climbed the headland with her oil can and her matches and she lit the fire and she stood beside it in the wind, and when the boats came home through the black water they steered on her flame, and they began, without anyone deciding it, to call it *her* fire, and the rotation quietly stopped rotating. The headland fire became Paloma's the way the old light had become hers — not by paper, not by appointment, but by the simple, unanswerable fact that she was the one who kept it.

Adaeze grew a garden on the south side of the cottage where the rock held the day's heat. Her illness did not leave her — the sea crossing had cost her a winter she never got back, and the cough came every autumn like a bill come due — but it slowed. It slowed enough. She was there to see Tomas shoot up like a spring tide, to see his voice break and his shoulders fill out, to teach him the recipes in the proper order and the stories in the proper order too, correcting him when he mixed up which grandmother had said which thing, insisting that the order mattered, that a story told out of order was a story half-erased.

"You sound like a clerk," Tomas teased her once, and she threw a dish towel at him, and Paloma, watching from the doorway, felt something in her chest that was not quite pain and not quite joy but lived in the country between them, where most of the true things lived.

* * *

Ivo came in the fourth spring, on a boat that ran dark into the harbor at dawn.

Paloma saw it first from the headland, a shape without lights where a shape without lights had no business being, and her body knew before her mind did — the way it had learned to know, in the old country, the difference between a smuggler's caution and a fugitive's desperation. She was halfway down the cliff path before Tomas caught up to her, and they stood together at the shingle as the boat grounded and a thin man stepped out into the shallows, hatless, gray at the temples now, carrying a leather satchel held against his chest with both arms the way a man carries a child across a river.

"Ivo," she said.

"Keeper," he said, and then, correcting himself with a small, pained smile, "Paloma."

He had not been to prison. He had been worse than imprisoned — he had been *transferred*, shuffled inland to a records office in a city where no one had ever smelled salt, given a desk and a stamp and the task of entering into a new ledger the names from the old ones, the populations of the requisitioned towns, sorted and ranked and reassigned according to the arithmetic of usefulness. He had done it for two years. He had done it neatly, because neatness was the only rebellion available to a man of his temperament, and because, he told them that first night at Adaeze's table, with his hands wrapped around a bowl of soup he barely touched, neat handwriting was the last honest thing left in his life.

"Then one day I understood something," he said. "I had been keeping the ledger. All that time — the requisition, the berth list, the new registry — I had been the one who kept it. And I thought: Ivo, you have spent your whole life learning to keep things. Why do you keep the thing that destroys, when you could keep the thing that saves?"

He opened the satchel on the table.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was a book. It was not beautiful. It was a supply manifest, one of dozens, the kind of record no one audits because no one imagines it matters — the weekly tallies of oil, flour, rope, lamp wicks, issued to the harbor town in the years before the requisition. But in its margins, in Ivo's small neat hand, added over months of quiet evenings while his supervisors believed him to be copying shipping schedules, were names. Hundreds of names. Every person the requisition ledger had tried to sort, rank, and erase — the unregistered, the unlicensed, the debt-fleeing, the deserters hidden in cellars and sea caves, the old women the arithmetic had condemned, the children born after the registration and therefore legally nonexistent. He had gathered them from parish scraps and ferry manifests and the memory of old fishermen, and he had written them down, and then he had walked out of the records office with the book under his coat on the last day before the annual audit, and he had not stopped walking until he reached the coast.

"I couldn't save the town," he said. "I know that. I was there, and I know it. But a name is a small thing to save, and I had a pen, and no one was watching the margins."

Adaeze was crying without noise, the way she did, her hand pressed flat against her sternum as if holding something in place. Tomas, who had been a boy of twelve when the gray ships came and was now a young man who had grown up on stories of that year, reached out and touched the book's cover as if it might be hot.

Paloma did not touch it. She looked at it for a long time, at the oilcloth, at the string tied in a keeper's knot — *her* knot, the one Emeka had used, the one she had taught Ivo without ever noticing she was teaching him — and she understood that this was the true register of the harbor. Not the ledger that had sorted them. This. The record of who had been there. The witness.

"You'll stay," she said. It was not a question. In the old country it would have been a question, because there had never been room, never been safety, never been enough. Here there was a spare room, because Adaeze had been keeping it ready for two years without telling anyone, on the theory — which she had explained to Paloma only once, fiercely — that hope is a thing you furnish in advance.

"I'll stay," Ivo said, and his voice broke on the second word, and Adaeze got up and put her hands on his shoulders, and the soup went cold, and nobody cared.

* * *

They read the names for the first time on the anniversary of the burning.

It was Tomas who suggested it — or perhaps it was not suggested at all but simply happened, the way the truest customs happen, without anyone deciding. It was a raw evening at the end of autumn, the first hard frost, and the headland fire was Paloma's that night, and Ivo came up the cliff path carrying the book, and Tomas carried a lantern, and they stood around the fire with the wind pulling the flame sideways and Ivo opened the oilcloth and began to read.

He read until his voice gave out. Then Paloma read. Then Tomas. They did not read it all — there were too many, and the fire would burn down before they finished, and Ivo said that was right, that was proper, that a register should take more than one night, that a town's dead should take a lifetime. They read the names of the boats lost in the last storm. They read the names of the cellar people. They read the names of the fishermen who had gone out in the darkness she had lit, and come home, and lived, and some of whom had died later in the evacuation, and whose names Ivo had added in the margins of the margins.

When they could no longer feel their fingers, they closed the book, and stood a moment looking at the fire, and below them the harbor made its long slow sound against the rocks — that breathing, that sighing, that voice the old keepers had always said was only wind and water and the shape of the land, but which Paloma had known for fifty years was something else, something that sang to whoever bothered to stay and listen.

"It sounds different here," Tomas said. "The water. Did you ever notice? It's the same sea but it sings different."

"Every harbor has its own song," Paloma said. "It takes years to learn the words."

"And what are the words here?"

She listened. The wind, the rock, the fire, the breathing water, the boy beside her grown tall, the clerk turned keeper of names, and somewhere down the hill a light in the cottage window where Adaeze was waiting up, coughing but alive, alive, alive.

"The words are *you are seen*," she said. "That's all any harbor ever says. That's the whole song."

* * *

The years after that were years the way years are supposed to be — not easy, but *hers*. The village built her a proper light in the fifth year, a small stone tower on the headland, not because any government ordered it but because the fishermen took up a collection and the mason worked through two winters and would accept no pay beyond meals, saying that a man who has been steered home through black water by a flame owes the flame a house. They carved no plaque. Paloma asked them not to. "The light knows whose it is," she said, and that was the only dedication.

Adaeze lived to see the seventh autumn. She died in the cottage with the door that stuck, on a clear night, with Tomas holding one hand and Paloma the other, and her last words were not a story or a recipe or an instruction but a laugh — a small, dry, triumphant laugh, as if at the end she had finally heard the joke the world had been telling all along, and had found it, on balance, funny. They buried her above the tide line where she could hear the water, and Paloma read her name first, every year, at the head of the list, because Adaeze had counted the days and the days had counted her, and no ledger had ever held her, and so the book of names was hers more than anyone's.

Ivo never married. He became, in time, the village's record-keeper — births and deaths and boats launched and boats lost, written in his small neat hand in a great book the villagers paid to have bound in leather, and when children asked him why he wrote everything down he would say, "Because paper is a promise you make to people who haven't been born yet," and they would look at him uncomprehendingly, and he would smile, and write it down anyway.

And Paloma kept the light. Through the storms of every autumn, through the years when her knees began to complain on the tower stairs and she climbed them anyway, through the night when a strange ship ran for the rocks in fog and her flame brought it off, through the long ordinary nights when nothing happened at all, which she had learned — as every true keeper learns — are the nights the light matters most, because the sea is always keeping its own accounts, and the lamp is the only argument against them that never sleeps.

She was old, in the end, older than she had ever planned to be, and on the last evening of the last autumn, she climbed the tower one final time with Tomas beside her — Tomas, who had a boy of his own now, a dark-eyed boy who asked questions about the old country and the gray ships and the fire in the records office, and who had been taught the recipes in the proper order and the stories in the proper order too.

Paloma lit the lamp. She trimmed the wick with hands that shook only a little. And she stood at the gallery rail with the wind in her white hair, looking down at the harbor where the water breathed against the rocks, singing its one song, the old song, the only song — *you are seen, you are seen, you are seen* — and she found that she was not afraid, and had not been afraid for years, and understood at last what Emeka had known all those mornings when he had climbed these same stairs, in that other life, at that other light, and had turned to her at the top and said nothing at all, because there was nothing that needed saying.

The light was lit. The harbor sang. And the names were kept.

Below in the keeper's room, waiting for her to come down, the book lay open on the table to a fresh page, and beside it a pen, and beside the pen, in Ivo's careful hand, a heading already written in wet ink, gleaming in the lamplight:

*The Register of the Other Shore — begun this year, to be kept forever.*
