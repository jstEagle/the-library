## 9. The Living Name

The dead have excellent manners. They arrive on schedule, they stay where you put them, and they never once, in fifteen years of my acquaintance, have come back to ask how I've been.

The living are another matter entirely.

I was re-bandaging a private's knee — a dog's work, the wound gone septic because some lieutenant had decided a fortnight ago that rest was for the faint of heart — when the ambulance came through the gates. I heard it before I saw it: the particular complaint of army wheels on cobblestones, that sound of an institution arriving where it is not wanted. Tamsin looked up from her basin of carbolic with the expression of a girl deciding which officer she'd lie to first.

"Another one?" she said.

"Another one."

They brought him in on a canvas stretcher, four orderlies moving with the exaggerated care men use when they've been told the cargo is important. Major's insignia on the blanket. A decoration bar I could read from six feet away, glinting like a shop window. And below the waist — nothing doing. The kind of paralysis that comes up the spine like frost up a windowpane, sudden and total and permanent as far as anyone in this army would bother to learn.

"Major Rowan Calloway," the transport sergeant announced, consulting his papers with the pride of a man delivering a royal parcel. "Wounded at — " he squinted — "some action upcountry. Decorated twice. The doctors say he's to have the best care available."

The best care available.

I had a basin in my hands and fifteen years of a ledger in my chest, and for one long moment the two were the same weight.

I knew the name before the sergeant finished saying it. Of course I did. I had written it myself, in my own careful hand, by lamplight, in a book that was at that moment sleeping under a chapel floor not two hundred yards from where I stood. *R. Calloway, left at Alder Ford.* Left. As in abandoned. As in struck from the rolls by his own superior's pen, or so the story went — except that I had been there, and the story was a lie stitched over a larger lie, and the man who had done the stitching was now lying on a stretcher in my ward, decorated twice for a war he had officially died in.

Tamsin touched my elbow. "Dom? You've gone the color of the sheets."

"Have I." I set the basin down. My hands, I noted with a kind of detached professional interest, were perfectly steady. It's a nurse's curse, that. The body does its work no matter what the rest of you is doing.

"Take him to bed nine," I said. "The one by the window. He'll want the light."

He would want the light. Fifteen years, and my first instinct was still to give the man the good bed.

* * *

I will not pretend I didn't consider my options. I stood at the ward's far end with a tray of dressings I had no intention of using, and I considered.

Option one: refuse. Inform the transport sergeant that Major Calloway required a different facility, a different nurse, a different century. This would have raised questions. Questions were the one commodity I could not afford. The workshop had been sealed a month; the ledger's absence was a held breath the whole town shared; and a nurse who suddenly developed scruples about a decorated major would be a nurse worth watching.

Option two: the other thing. I won't dress it up. There are powders in a nurse's kit that ease pain and there are powders that ease everything, and in a fever ward, with the mortality running as high as it ran that autumn, who would audit one death among the dozens? I stood there perhaps ten seconds with that thought, and I am not proud of the ten seconds, and I am not going to pretend they didn't happen. A person who tells you they've never weighed such a thing is either lying or has never been betrayed at Alder Ford.

Option three: nurse him. Do the work. Keep my hands professional and my mouth shut and my heart — well. My heart has never once in my life done what I told it.

I picked up the tray and went to bed nine.

* * *

He was thinner than the man I remembered, which is a strange thing to say about a man you last saw being carried away from a burning ford, but grief and years do their carving in different places. His hair had gone grey at the temples — grey, not white, so he'd gone grey honestly, year by year, the way the rest of us had, instead of in one catastrophic afternoon as I had always privately imagined. His hands lay on the blanket like something washed ashore. Hands I had once — but that's a later chapter, as they say, and I'm getting ahead of myself.

His eyes were open. Grey, still. They found me the way eyes do when they've been searching a ceiling for hours.

"Nurse," he said. His voice had the gravel of a long transport. "They told me this was a good ward. They told me there was a nurse here the men trust." A breath. "I didn't expect — "

He stopped. I watched him stop. I watched the recognition move across his face like weather, and I confess I gave him nothing to work with — no flinch, no dropped tray, none of the theatrics fifteen years of novels would have me perform. I set the tray on the stand. I checked his chart with more attention than the chart deserved.

"You didn't expect what, Major?" I said. "A fever ward is a fever ward. We're all of us surprised to be here."

"Dominic."

There it was. My name, in his mouth, after fifteen years, and I will tell you honestly that the sound of it went through me like a splinter — small, deep, and in a place no forceps would reach.

"You're mistaken," I said. "I'm Nurse Thornbury. Everyone here is Thornbury or Hale or Marsh. We're a very repetitive town." I took his pulse. His pulse was steady. Mine, I suspected, was doing something unsuitable. "You've a fever coming, Major. The wound's clean but the spine's a different country. You'll have pain. We'll manage it. You'll want to lie still and let the body do what it does."

"Dominic." He said it again, softer, and there was something in it I hadn't prepared for — not guilt, not yet, not the performance of it, but something rawer. Wonder, maybe. The look of a man who has spent fifteen years believing a thing and then finds the thing standing over him holding a basin. "I thought you were dead. I thought — at Alder Ford, I thought —"

"I know what you thought." I tucked the blanket at his shoulders with the crisp efficiency I reserve for patients I dislike, which is to say, with more care than I give anyone. "You thought a great many things, Major. You'll want to rest now."

"Dominic, please — "

"Bed rest," I said, "means *rest*."

I turned away, and behind me I heard him exhale — a long, careful breath, the breath of a man who has just understood that he has walked into a room where every person in it knows what he did, and only one of them is holding his next dose of morphine.

I did not look back. Looking back is for people with steadier hands than mine, and mine, as I've said, were busy being steady.

* * *

Tamsin found me in the dispensary twenty minutes later, counting the same bottle of iodine for the third time.

"Dom." She shut the door. "Bed nine. That's him. That's the name. The one that made your hands shake in the chapel dark. *R. Calloway, left at Alder Ford.*"

"You've a good memory."

"Don't do that." She came and stood in front of me, this girl I'd half-raised on dock crates and chapel candles, and she had her arms folded and her jaw set, and she looked, in that moment, so much like the veterans she'd grown up among that it hurt. "Don't do the thing where you turn into a locked drawer. He's *here*. The man from the book. The one you said was — " she lowered her voice, though the door was shut and the ward was forty feet away — "the one who did the striking. The officer. He's in your ward and you're going to *nurse* him?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because he's a patient."

"That's not an answer, that's a *habit*."

I looked at her. She's nineteen and fearless in the specific way of people who have never yet lost anything they couldn't rebuild, and I envied it and feared for it in equal measure, which is, I believe, the standard parental condition.

"Tamsin," I said, "when a man comes into this ward, he stops being what he did and becomes what he needs. That's not a habit. That's the whole philosophy. It's the philosophy of the book, if you've been paying attention. *No one is omitted.* Not the cowards, not the deserters, not the men the officers wrote off. Not — " and here my voice did something I didn't authorize — "not the man who taught me the philosophy by violating it."

She was quiet a moment. "You loved him."

"Tamsin."

"You did. It's all over your face. It's been all over your face since the sergeant said his name."

I turned back to the iodine. "What I did at nineteen is not a subject for the dispensary."

"And what he did at Alder Ford?"

"Is in the book," I said. "Where it will stay. Where it *must* stay. Tamsin — listen to me. If he's here, then the ledger is wrong. Not wrong in what it says. Wrong in what it *doesn't* say. A dead man can't be called to account. A living one, lying in bed nine, decorated twice for a war he officially died in — that's not a wound anymore. That's evidence."

She went pale in the way she goes pale — all at once, like a lamp turned down. "The inquiry," she said. "The one everyone's whispering about. If he's alive —"

"Then the record can be challenged. Then the names in the book can be *named*. And the men who struck them are still in command, and the workshop is still sealed, and we are still a town full of people who have been lying to redcoats for a month." I finally set the iodine down. "So we do nothing. We say nothing. He is a paralyzed major with a fever, and I am his nurse, and that is *all* he is, until I decide otherwise. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," she said. And then, because she is Tamsin: "Do you understand *you*?"

I didn't answer that. Some questions are above my rank.

* * *

The fever came that night, as fevers do, punctual as rent.

I sat with him through the worst of it. This was, I told myself, simple triage — bed nine was by the window, the night orderly was needed elsewhere, and a paralyzed man with a rising fever can choke on his own tongue without anyone's permission. All true. None of it the whole truth. The whole truth is that I sat with him because I could not make myself leave, and I have been a nurse long enough to know that the patients who most deserve our absence are often the ones who get our vigil.

He talked in the fever. Not much. Fragments. Ford water and paper and a voice saying *strike them, Rowan, strike them all, that's an order* — and I sat in the dark with a cloth in my hand and listened to fifteen years of my own nightmares spoken in another man's mouth, and learned that he had been having them too.

At three in the morning he surfaced, lucid and grey, and found me in the chair beside him.

"You're still here," he said.

"It's my ward."

"No." He moved his head against the pillow, the small fraction of movement the fever had left him. "You were here at Alder Ford too. In the water. Pulling men out. I watched you." His eyes were wet, and I don't think he knew it. "I struck your name myself, Dominic. Did you know that? They gave me the list and your name was on it and I — I wrote the line through it. I thought if I struck you, you'd be safe. Unrecorded. Off the rolls, out of the war. I thought I was — " He stopped. His throat worked. "I thought a great many things," he finished, echoing me, and there was no mockery in it, only ruin.

I wet the cloth. I laid it on his forehead, because that was the work in front of my hands, and my hands have never once in fifteen years refused the work in front of them, no matter what the rest of me was screaming.

"Sleep, Major," I said.

"Dominic — the ledger. They told me the workshop was requisitioned. All documents. If the ledger —"

The cloth went still under my fingers. He watched my face, this man paralyzed from the waist down who had nonetheless, in one sentence, found the one part of me that could still be paralyzed too.

"You know about the ledger," I said.

"I've known about the ledger since 1884," Rowan Calloway said. "I'm the reason it exists."
