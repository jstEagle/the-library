## 19. The Empty Workshop

The army left the way armies always leave: at dawn, in a great clattering of wagons, with the particular courtesy of guests who have broken your furniture and intend to be thanked for it.

I stood in the doorway of what had been my workshop for fifteen years and watched the last red column wind up the coast road. They had taken their tents, their crates, their flags, their wounded who could be moved and their dead who could be argued about. They had left behind the smell of carbolic, a mountain of ash in my old forge, and a building that looked, from the outside, exactly as it always had — which was the army's final and most thorough lie.

Inside, it was a gutted whale. That was the image that came to me and refused to leave: a whale beached and opened, everything of value rendered out of it, the ribs standing bare. The long ward tables were gone. The partition walls Voss's engineers had thrown up in a week — pine and canvas, cheap as a promise — had been torn down and carted off. The floor where my bench had stood was scarred with boot-heels and stretcher-runners and one long black burn where an orderly had knocked a lamp into a puddle of spirit and very nearly ended us all in November.

I had not been inside since the inquiry closed. Eleven days. I had found reasons. The inquiry itself was a reason; testifying had taken everything I had, and I am not a man with much to spare on the best of years. But the true reason was simpler and less flattering: I was afraid the room would feel like a grave, and I had spent fifteen years learning what graves do to a person who keeps visiting them.

So I stood in the doorway with the ledger under my arm — returned to me the previous afternoon by a clerk with ink-stained cuffs and an expression of profound relief, as though the book had been radiating something — and I made myself go in.

The light came through the east windows the way it always had, in long grey bars across the floorboards. Dust hung in the bars. Everything else was absence.

"Well," I said, to no one, because the room deserved at least one voice even if it was mine. "You've looked better."

The ledger weighed what it had always weighed. I set it on the one surface the army had left — the old scarred trestle by the north wall, too heavy and too ugly to be worth carting — and I walked the room the way you walk a room after a funeral, touching things not to use them but to prove they're still solid.

My bench was gone. They had sawn it apart to make tray-tables, an orderly told me once, with the apologetic pride of a man reporting an atrocity efficiently committed. The drawers where I kept the good needles — gone. The shelf of jars, the mortar and pestle, the little brass scale I'd carried through three campaigns in a canvas bag against my chest like a second heart — all requisitioned, all inventoried, all entered in Captain Voss's immaculate hand and shipped to a depot I will never see again.

The hooks by the door where the veterans hung their coats when they came in of an evening — still there. Nobody had wanted those.

I stood in front of them for a while. There was a nail missing. I knew which coat had hung on it, and whose shoulders had filled it, and I stood there and let that be true, and then I moved on, because that is the whole of the profession, really. You stand in front of the thing. You let it be true. You move on.

* * *

Tamsin found me at noon, sitting on the trestle with the ledger open in my lap, not reading it. She came through the door without knocking, which is her way and always has been; I half raised her and the other half I blame for the state of my nerves.

"They've gone, then," she said, looking around with her hands jammed in her pockets. She was wearing the coat she'd worn all winter, dockworker's canvas gone soft as cloth at the elbows, and she had sawdust in her hair, which meant she'd been working at something before she came, and had abandoned it, which meant she'd been working at something for me.

"They've gone."

"Whole column went up the road before breakfast. Mrs. Petch says she counted forty-one wagons and a mule she'd like to have back."

"The mule was requisitioned fair and square."

"The mule was hers, Dominic."

"The army has a broad understanding of the word *fair*." I closed the ledger. "They left a receipt for it, I expect."

She laughed, but it was the thin kind, and she didn't come further in. She stood just inside the door, in the bar of light, and looked at the room the way I had looked at it — the way you look at a face you knew before it was hurt. She was twenty-three and she had grown up half in this room, and I had never once asked her whether she'd wanted that, and I thought about it now, standing in the ruins, with the whole of the coast open in front of us and the whole of the century open in front of us, and no orders left in the world compelling either of us to stand in this particular doorway.

"They gutted it," she said.

"They gutted it."

"You could get passage south. Mr. Quill said the *Cormorant* takes passengers as far as Wexmouth, and from Wexmouth there's the railway."

"I know what a railway is, Tamsin."

"You've never been on one."

"I've never been on one," I agreed, "because I've never wanted to be anywhere that a railway goes." I turned the ledger over in my hands. Its cover had been scuffed by clerks now, handled by strangers, entered into evidence, read aloud in a room full of people who had spent years not wanting to hear it. It had survived all of that and come back to me looking exactly as it had before, which is more than can be said for anything else in this town, including me.

"Quill also said," she went on, in the tone of a person laying track one rail at a time, "that the *Cormorant* could as easily go north. And there's the ferry, and there's the coach, and there's old Marta's cousin up the coast who's always wanting help with the nets. You could be gone by Thursday. Nobody'd think worse of you. Nobody'd think anything of you at all, which I expect is the point."

"That is very nearly the point," I said.

"And you'd take the book."

"I'd take the book."

"And start somewhere new. Somewhere with no officers in it and no inquiry and no one who knows what Alder Ford means when you say it."

"Yes."

"And the room would stand empty."

"The room is standing empty now."

"Not if I stay."

I looked at her. She had come further in by then, far enough that the dust moved around her in the light, and her chin was up in the way it went up when she was about to be stubborn about something she'd decided before breakfast. I have known that chin since she was nine years old and stealing apples off my sill, and I can tell you that it has never once been lowered in a losing cause.

"Stay and do what?" I asked. "The army took everything. There's no bench. There's no scale. There's no jars, no lamp, no — Tamsin, there's no *roof* over the north corner, if you hadn't noticed. There's a hole you could put a horse through."

"I noticed the hole."

"Then you'll have observed that it is not an improvement."

"I observed," she said, "that the walls are still standing. And the east windows, which you're always going on about. And the well's still good. And the hooks are still there." She pointed at them, the hooks by the door, and I felt that go through me like a splinter, that she had looked at a gutted room and seen the hooks. "And I've got two hands, and Hal's got a cart, and the Petch boys owe every soul on this street a favor going back to the flood year. And there's timber coming up from Wexmouth on the *Cormorant* anyway, because Quill's rebuilding his shed, and he'd not notice a few planks gone missing off the top of a load if a person were careful about it."

"You're describing theft."

"I'm describing *requisition*," she said, with a perfectly straight face, "which is a word I learned from the army, and I'll thank them for the education."

I laughed. It surprised me, coming up out of me like that, the first proper laugh since the hearing, and it hurt in my chest the way laughing does when it's been locked up too long, like a door opening on a stiff hinge.

"Tamsin."

"Dominic."

"If I stay, I stay. That's what you're asking. Not for a visit. Not for the winter. You're asking me to take this room and fill it again, knowing what filling it costs. I've spent fifteen years learning what it costs. I've got the ledger to prove it." I set my hand flat on the cover. "Every name in here cost me something. And the ones that cost most —" I thought of Calloway, sweeping the chapel steps that morning with a broom he'd brought himself, not looking at anyone, not asking anything, his back bent under the particular weight of a man who has signed his own ruin and found it lighter than his silence. "The ones that cost most are still paying."

"I know."

"You don't. You were nine when the war ended. You think the book is a list. It isn't a list. It's a debt, and debts don't stay written down, they come round the house collecting."

"I know," she said again, and this time she came and put her hand on top of mine, on the ledger, the way she used to put her hand on my sleeve when she was small and the storms came off the water and the whole town shook. "Because I watched you pay it. Fifteen years, Dominic. I watched you pay it every night at that lamp. And I watched what it did to the ones whose names were in it — their sisters, their sons, the ones who came to the door because there was nowhere else the names were said out loud. You think the book cost *you*? It was the only thing in this town that ever paid *us*."

The dust turned in the light. Somewhere outside a gull said something rude and went on about its business.

"I'm tired," I said, which was not the argument I'd been building, but was the true one, and she deserved the true one. "I'm sixty-one years old and I'm tired down to the marrow, and every part of me that isn't tired wants to get on the *Cormorant* and be a stranger somewhere. A stranger doesn't get asked to testify. A stranger doesn't get a name read back at him across a courtroom. A stranger can put the book in a trunk and —" I stopped. I heard what I was about to say. *Put the book in a trunk and let it go.* Fifteen years of nights at that lamp, and the end of the sentence was still *let it go*, still there, patient as a hook, and I sat with the shame of it for a long moment while Tamsin watched me and let me.

"You could," she said quietly. "You could put it in a trunk. You've earned the right to do anything you like with it. It's your book."

"It was never my book."

"It was always your book."

"It was *their* book," I said. "I only kept it. That's the whole of what I am, Tamsin — I'm not the author, I'm the keeper. And a keeper who runs off with the keeping isn't a keeper anymore, he's just a thief with a clear conscience." I looked around the gutted room, the whale-ribs of it, the light lying in the empty places where the benches had been. "And a keeper needs a place to keep."

Her chin was still up. But her eyes had gone bright, and she blinked hard and looked at the hooks, and I pretended not to see, because that is also the profession.

"So," she said, when she could. "Thursday, then. The *Cormorant* brings timber up from Wexmouth. Quill won't miss it."

"Quill will miss it."

"Quill will be *compensated*," she said, "in ledger entries. His name in the book. His shed, his planks, his mule if we get her back. That's the new arrangement — everything that goes into this room gets written down. What people gave. What people lost. What people are owed. Not just the dead." She looked at me, fierce and young and entirely certain, in the way that only the young and the entirely certain can be, and I thought: *fifteen years I carried this alone, and it was never meant to be carried alone, I simply never had anyone strong enough to share the weight with until now.* "The living get entries too. That's the whole point, isn't it? That's what you taught me. Nobody gets omitted."

I opened the ledger to the last written page, the one with the typhoid dead in Tamsin's own hand, her letters still learning themselves, still a little uneven, and after them the orderlies, and after them, in a line I had not expected and will not forget, *Capt. E. Voss — gave his career; owed a debt the book cannot pay but should record.* And beneath all of it, blank paper, waiting.

"Thursday," I said.

She grinned — the whole face, the stolen-apples grin, nine years old again for one second — and then she was out the door and down the street at a run, no doubt to commit her first act of organized timber theft, and I sat alone in the gutted room with the ledger open on my knees.

The light moved. The dust settled. Outside, the harbor turned over on its tide the way it had turned for a thousand years and would turn for a thousand more, indifferent and faithful, which is more than most of us manage.

I took out my pencil. And there at the top of the fresh page, under the date, I wrote the first entry of the new book — not a name, not yet, but a note, in my own hand, for whoever keeps this after me:

*Begun again. Same debts. Same keeper, for now. The room is empty. It will not be for long.*

And then, because I heard boots on the cobbles outside, and a knock at the doorframe — a knock I knew, careful and apologetic, from a man who had spent fifteen years not daring to knock at any door — I looked up, and set down the pencil, and said, "It's open."
