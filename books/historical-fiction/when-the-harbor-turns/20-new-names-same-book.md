## 20. New Names, Same Book

The first thing I learned about rebuilding a workshop is that you cannot rebuild it quietly. Every plank has an opinion. Every nail argues with the hammer before it goes in. By the third morning of work, half the town had found reasons to walk past and offer commentary, and by the fifth, they'd stopped offering commentary and started handing me things — a saw here, a bucket of pitch there, a thermos of tea that Old Bram swore was coffee but which tasted like it had been brewed from rope.

"You're holding that level wrong," said Mrs. Pettibone, who had never built anything sturdier than a grudge.

"It's a spirit level, not a spirit," I said. "It doesn't need encouragement."

She sniffed and handed me another nail anyway, which in Pettibone currency was roughly equivalent to an embrace.

The workshop had been gutted twice over — once by the army when they turned it into a ward, once again by the inquiry clerks who'd carted off evidence and left behind the smell of lamp oil and official indifference. The benches were gone. The shelves were splinters. The window Dominic had kept spotless for fifteen years so the light would fall right across the ledger page now looked out on a yard full of army debris and one very determined gull.

But the bones were good. Dominic said so the first day, standing in the doorway with the returned ledger wrapped in oilcloth under one arm like a sleeping child, looking at the ruin with an expression I couldn't read. Not grief exactly. Something more like a carpenter assessing a house that had burned but whose chimney still stood.

"Bones are good," I'd said, echoing what I thought I saw.

Dominic had looked at me for a long moment. "Bones are everything," they said. "Flesh grows back. Ask any nurse."

So we grew flesh back onto the workshop. And somewhere in the middle of the hammering and the hauling and the tea-that-was-not-coffee, something else grew back too, though I didn't have a name for it yet.

* * *

It happened on an evening in late October, when the light came through the new-glazed windows at that low gold angle that makes even sawdust look holy. The veterans had gone home. Dominic was at the far bench, re-inking the faded entries — names that had spent months under a chapel floor and years before that in hiding, their ink going shy, needing to be called back into boldness.

I was sweeping. I am always sweeping. It's my contribution to every enterprise: I show up, I lift heavy things, and I sweep. But that evening the broom handle felt different in my hands, or maybe I felt different holding it, because I stopped mid-sweep and heard myself say:

"Teach me."

Dominic didn't look up. "You want to learn to re-ink? It's tedious."

"No." My heart was going like a dockside drum. "The ledger. Teach me how to keep it."

The pen stopped. In the quiet that followed I could hear the gull outside making its opinions known, and the sea doing what the sea does, which is everything at once and nothing in particular.

"Tamsin." Dominic set the pen down with great care, the way you set down something that might go off. "Do you know what you're asking?"

"I know what I'm asking better than you think I do." I leaned the broom against the bench. "I carried it out under fish crates. I hid it under a chapel floor while an officer asked me if I knew anything about missing documents, and I lied to his face, and I didn't wet myself, which I consider a professional achievement. I've watched you write in it since I was nine years old. I know why each name is in there. You taught me that already, down in the dark, whether you meant to or not."

"That was reading," Dominic said. "Reading is easy. Writing is a vow."

"Then let me take the vow."

They studied me across the lamplit room — this person who had pulled thorns out of my palms when I was ten, who had taught me to bind a wound with one hand and hold a bucket with the other, who had spent fifteen years being the only adult in my life who never once lied to me about anything that mattered. I watched them weigh it. I watched them decide.

"Come here, then."

I crossed the room. They turned the ledger toward me, open to a fresh page, and put the pen in my hand like they were handing me a live coal.

"The rules are simple and terrible," Dominic said. "You write only what you can swear to. You write every name, no matter whose comfort it costs. You write the truth even when the truth is ugly, especially then. And you understand that once ink dries, it doesn't care about your feelings."

"What if I get one wrong?"

"Then you correct it in the margin, dated, in your own hand, and you never pretend you weren't wrong. A ledger that hides its own errors is just gossip with good posture."

Despite everything, I laughed. "Gossip with good posture."

"I'm serious."

"I know you are. That's what makes it funny." I looked down at the blank page. It looked back. "Who goes first?"

Dominic was quiet a moment. Then: "Whomever you choose. It's yours now as much as mine."

And that — that was the moment the floor did its slow drop-out again, except this time it dropped out into something wide instead of something empty. Because I understood, standing there with a pen going cold in my fingers, that Dominic wasn't just teaching me a skill. They were setting down a weight they'd carried alone for fifteen years, and trusting me to carry my share of it, and neither of us was going to say any of that out loud because we were Yarrow Harbor people and we expressed emotion through structural carpentry and insults about tea.

I thought about who should go first. There were so many. The typhoid dead alone filled a winter's worth of pages — the soldiers the officers had written off, the orderlies who'd caught it carrying slop buckets through wards that should never have been built where they were built. I'd held half of them. I'd watched the other half die.

But I knew, somehow, who had to be first. Who had earned it.

I dipped the pen. I wrote:

*Voss, Ellery. Captain. Gave up his career so a book could cross a town under curfew. Misdirected a patrol on the night of the 14th knowing it would end him. Did not ask for thanks. Did not receive much of it either, which is its own kind of record.*

I sat back. My handwriting shook a little, but it was legible, and it was true, and it was mine.

Dominic read it over my shoulder. For a long time they said nothing. Then, quietly: "He'd hate being in there."

"He'd hate more being left out."

A pause. "Yes," Dominic said. "That's exactly right. Next."

* * *

We worked until the candle burned down to a thumb's width. I wrote the typhoid dead — thirty-one names, and I could tell you where each of them was lying when I last saw them, and some nights I still do tell myself, whether I want to or not. I wrote the orderlies. Mrs. Aldous, who sang hymns off-key to the dying and deserved a better tune at her own going. Young Fitch, who was seventeen and lied about it to enlist as a stretcher-bearer and died honest anyway. I wrote them all, and Dominic checked each entry against memory, and twice corrected me gently — a date, a spelling — and both times showed me how to make the marginal correction properly, dated, in my own hand, never pretending.

"Gossip with good posture," I murmured, and got the ghost of a smile for it.

Near midnight, footsteps on the porch stairs. We both tensed — old habits — but it was only Old Bram, letting himself in without knocking because knocking was for strangers and funerals. He looked at the ledger, at the two of us bent over it, at the pen in my hand, and something moved across his face like weather.

"So it's done, then," he said. "The book passes on."

"It doesn't pass on," Dominic said. "It multiplies."

"Hm." Bram lowered himself onto the stool by the stove, joints cracking like green firewood. "In my day we kept such things in our heads and took them to the grave, and everyone praised our discretion, and nobody remembered a damned soul. This is better." He nodded at the page. "Write me in while you're at it. I want to see how you flatter me."

"I don't flatter in the ledger," I said. "Rules are simple and terrible."

"Then write me true and let God sort out the difference."

So I wrote him too — *Old Bram, veteran of three campaigns, keeper of the worst tea on the coast, present at every deathbed in this town for twenty years whether invited or not* — and he read it upside down and pronounced it "adequate," which from Bram was a eulogy, a knighthood, and a marriage proposal all at once.

Word got around, the way word does. Within a week people were coming to the workshop not to be tended but to be *recorded*. A widow wanted her husband's real date of death noted, the true one, not the army's version. A young man who'd served as a cook and never fired a shot wanted it written that courage wasn't only rifles. An old woman brought a list of names she'd kept folded in her Bible for thirty years — men from her village up the coast, struck from the rolls, forgotten everywhere but in her folding hands.

Dominic took each one. Wrote each one. And then — and this is the part I keep turning over like a coin — started handing them to me.

"This one's yours," they'd say. Or: "You knew him better. Write it."

And I would. And the strange thing, the thing nobody warns you about inheritance, is that it doesn't feel like receiving something. It feels like being trusted with something breakable, forever, in front of witnesses.

* * *

On a raw gray afternoon in November, a knock actually came — which meant a stranger. I opened the door to a tall man in civilian clothes that fit him like a disguise, hat brim pulled low, case in hand.

Captain Voss. Former Captain Voss, I supposed, though habits of rank die hard in the spine.

"I heard," he said, in that careful officer's voice, "that records are kept here."

"They are," I said. "Come in before the wind does."

He stepped inside and stood awkwardly among the half-built shelving, a man used to quarters assigned to him and suddenly in a place built by other people's hands. Dominic rose from the bench. The two of them regarded each other across the room — the archivist and the officer who'd hunted the archive, the nurse and the man who'd burned his career to save the patient.

"You're listed," Dominic said.

Something flickered in Voss's jaw. "So I was told. I came to—" He stopped. Started again. "I came to ask that it be accurate. If it's to exist at all."

"It's accurate," I said.

"Then I'll leave you to it." He turned for the door, and I swear on the harbor itself, the man could not leave well enough alone, because at the threshold he paused and said, without turning around:

"My brother's name was Peter. He died at Kessel Ridge. The army recorded him as 'wounded, evacuated.' He was never evacuated anywhere but a ditch." A breath. "If there's a page for men like him—"

"There's a whole book for men like him," I said.

He nodded once, sharp, like a man accepting orders he agreed with for the first time in his career, and walked out into the rain. That evening I wrote Peter Voss into the ledger, and beneath his name I wrote his brother's account of the ditch, dated and sworn, and if my eyes blurred a little over the ink, well, the rules say nothing against that.

* * *

December came down the coast like a debt collector. We worked the roof before the frosts, and the town worked with us — Bram on ladders he had no business on, Mrs. Pettibone directing operations she didn't understand and somehow improving them, the widow Aldous's daughters painting the shutters the deep harbor-blue that Dominic's had always been. The workshop went up around the ledger the way a body goes around a spine.

One evening I found Dominic standing in the finished doorway, watching the light fall across the long bench where the ledger now lived openly, under glass, unlocked — because secrets had nearly killed it, and openness was going to be its armor from now on.

"Do you regret it?" I asked. "Handing it over. All those years keeping it alone."

Dominic considered. "For fifteen years I thought the ledger was a burden I was protecting. Turns out it was a burden protecting me. Gave me a reason to stay upright when staying upright seemed optional." They glanced at me. "It's lighter shared. Not less heavy. Just — distributed. Like ballast."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's a nautical metaphor. Take it or leave it."

I took it. I took all of it — the pen, the rules, the terrible simplicity, the gossip with good posture. Some nights I still wake at three and reach for the ledger's absence beside my bed before remembering it lives in daylight now, under glass, where anyone can read it and anyone can add to it, and that's the whole point, and I made my peace with that the way you make peace with the tide: not once, but daily.

Because here is what I learned writing in that book, the thing Dominic knew for fifteen years and never said out loud: a ledger isn't a record of the dead. It's a promise to the living. It says *someone was watching. Someone counted. You were not omitted.* And a promise like that can't belong to one person, because one person can be silenced, or buried, or simply tired. It has to belong to everybody or it protects nobody.

The town understood before I explained it, the way towns do. They came with their folded lists and their corrected dates and their dead, and the book fattened, and the workshop filled, and on cold evenings the whole found family of us sat around the stove while someone read entries aloud — not mournfully, mind, but the way you read a ship's manifest, checking that everyone boarded.

It was on one of those evenings, close to Christmas, that the knock came again. Real knock. Stranger.

I opened the door, and the wind came in first, and after the wind, hunched and hatless and thinner than the man I'd last seen refusing to certify a lie —

Major Rowan Calloway, demoted, destitute, and standing on our step like a man who'd walked a long way to arrive precisely nowhere.

Behind me, the reading stopped. Dominic rose slowly from the bench.

Calloway cleared his throat. "I'm not here for forgiveness," he said. "I'm here to sweep floors. If you'll have me."

And Dominic, who taught me that a ledger hides nothing and forgives selectively, looked at the ruined man on the doorstep for a long, long moment — and stepped aside.
