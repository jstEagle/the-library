## 2. The Ledger

The widow's house smelled of tallow and wet wool, the way every house in the divers' quarter smelled when the monsoon wind came off the water sideways. I had gone there because that was what you did. Someone drowned, you brought bread or salt or a coin you couldn't spare, and you stood in the doorway being sorry with your hat in your hands. It was a ritual as old as the harbor itself, and like everything old in San Verrano, it existed mostly to keep people from thinking.

I had known Mateo Ferran my whole life, in the way you know a man who works forty feet below you on the same oyster beds — by his silhouette, by the rhythm of his baskets coming up, by the particular way he waved off the leech boats when they drifted too close to his lines. He was not a friend. He was a fixture. And now he was a body laid out on his own table with a candle at his head, and I was standing in his doorway holding a loaf I'd stolen from my own mother's kitchen.

"Come in, Jude Reyes." The widow's voice was steady in the way that frightened me more than weeping would have. Weeping, I knew what to do with. You looked at your shoes. Steadiness required something else.

The house was full of neighbors pretending to pray. In San Verrano, grief was a communal performance with assigned seating, and everyone knew their part: the old women keened softly near the window, the men stood by the door discussing tides in low voices, and the children were banished to the yard where they could be sad without disturbing anyone. I was seventeen years old and technically a child by the company's arithmetic, which meant I should have been in the yard. But the widow looked at me across the room with an expression I couldn't file anywhere, and my feet carried me forward instead of out.

She drew me into the back room, past the curtain of beads her daughter had strung one winter for no reason except that stringing beads was better than crying. The back room held a loom, a chest, and a smell of camphor that got into your teeth. She closed the curtain behind us.

"You were there," she said. "At the shallows. When they pulled him out."

"I helped carry the basket line." This was true. It was also nothing. Half the quarter had been there; drowned men in San Verrano were pulled ashore the way driftwood was, with a certain tired efficiency, everyone knowing their role. The harbor gave back its dead the way a ledger gives back a debt — eventually, and never quite whole.

"You saw what was under his shirt."

I had seen it. A wax-sealed packet, strapped flat against his ribs with a diver's belt, the kind of thing no man carries down to the beds unless he means it to stay with him past drowning. The overseers had cut it free and set it aside while they went through his pockets for company property, and in the confusion of the crowd — in that small, ordinary chaos that I had spent eleven years learning to read the way other people read scripture — it had gone missing. I hadn't taken it. I wanted that recorded somewhere, even if only in my own head. I had simply been standing nearest when it slid off the rocks into the wrack-line, and I had simply picked it up, because leaving company wax seals lying in the open was the sort of thing that got a person asked questions.

"I saw a packet," I said carefully. "I don't know anything about it."

"No," she agreed. "You know nothing about it. That is why I am asking you."

She went to the loom, and her hands — those hands, which had mended nets for thirty years and could tie a bowline blind drunk in a rolling skiff — shook so badly she had to press them flat against the frame to stop them. Then she told me.

Mateo had found the pages three weeks ago, during a salvage run on the wreck of the *Cormorant*, a company barge that had gone down in the channel two seasons back with a cargo of records nobody had bothered to save. The company had written the loss off. The water had kept what the water kept, and among other things it had kept a ledger — or enough of one. Pages showing pearl contracts not as agreements but as traps. Advances issued against future harvests at interest rates calculated to exceed any possible catch. Fines levied for short weights, for damaged shell, for days lost to storm, each fine entered as new debt, each debt rolled into the principal until a man who borrowed forty pesos to repair his boat owed four hundred by the third season and would owe four thousand by the tenth, and his children after him, and their children, forever, amen.

"Bonded," the widow said. "That's the word they use in the pages. Not indentured. Bonded. Like a beast bonded to a plow."

"And Mateo took them because—"

"Because his father died owing eleven hundred pesos to a company that paid him nine pesos a hundred shells. Because he spent twenty years diving to pay a debt that grew faster than he could dive. Because—" and here her voice finally cracked, just once, like ice on a spring pond, "—because he thought if someone read those pages aloud in front of the magistrate, every contract in this harbor would burn."

I looked at the packet in her hands. Wax seal, company red, unbroken. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the anchor chain on the *Marisol*'s biggest barge.

"Give it to the magistrate yourself," I said. "Walk it to Corvo's door. He lives eight streets from here."

"The bridge closes tomorrow at dawn. Brannigan posted the order tonight — monsoon season, harbor sealed, all travel suspended. And Brannigan's men have been at every door in the divers' quarter since dusk asking whether Mateo kept papers, whether he said anything strange, whether his wife noticed him acting secretive." Her eyes came up to mine, and they were dry and terrible. "If I walk toward the magistrate's house carrying anything, I will be searched before I reach the second street. If I send my daughter, she will be searched. They are looking for a grieving family making a move. They are not looking for you, Jude. Nobody has ever looked at you."

There it was. Eleven years of being small and quick and unremarkable, of measuring my life in what fit in two hands, of memorizing which overseers could be bribed with silence and which ones counted the shells twice — all of it suddenly named aloud as a qualification. I had spent my whole life perfecting invisibility, and here was a woman trying to hand me a job posting for it.

"No," I said.

It came out flat and fast, before fear could dress itself up as reasoning. "No. I'm sorry about Mateo. I am. But I have a mother and a sister in this quarter, and if they find that packet on me, they won't hang me — I'm company property, they'll just bill my family for the damages. You want me to carry a firebrand through a sealed harbor into the one building in San Verrano where Brannigan's writ runs thinnest, and you're asking me to do it because I'm good at not being seen. That's not a plan. That's a prayer with extra steps."

"They'll bill your family anyway," she said quietly. "They do already."

And because I am, at bottom, a coward with a good memory, I turned to go. I had the speech ready — the respectful refusal, the condolences, the promise to keep silent. I had one foot through the bead curtain when she said, not angry, not pleading, just tired:

"At least look at what he died carrying. He was your father's partner on the beds for six years. Look, and then refuse, and I'll never speak of it again."

* * *

I looked.

She broke the seal herself, with her thumbnail, like opening a letter she'd been dreading for twenty years. The pages were water-stained at the edges, soft as cloth where the sea had worked them, but the ink inside had been kept dry by oilcloth and stubbornness, and the clerk's hand was beautiful — that was the thing that struck me first, how beautiful it was. Round figures, elegant flourishes, the careful ruling of lines. Somebody had sat at a desk with good light and done this work with pride.

Page by page, she turned them, and I stopped hearing the keening women in the front room.

It wasn't the numbers, though the numbers were bad enough. Nine pesos a hundred. Interest at five percent per month, compounded. Fines itemized with loving precision: *short weight, 2 pesos; shell damaged in transit, 4 pesos; days lost to weather, charged to diver's account.* What gutted me was the names. Every page was a column of names I'd grown up hearing called across the water — Ravelo, Sandoval, Imelda Cruz's husband, the two brothers who ran the skiff out of the north jetty. Men whose debts I could now calculate to the peso, men who would still be paying when their granddaughters were diving the same beds.

And then she turned a page and I saw my own name.

Not mine. My father's. *Reyes, Emilio.* Dated the year I was born. An advance of sixty pesos — boat repairs, the entry said, though our boat had never been repaired in my lifetime, unless you counted the time Teyo's father caulked her with pitch stolen from the customs shed. Sixty pesos, at five percent monthly, against a harvest that never covered it. Below that, the roll-forward entries, year after year, each one initialed in the margin by a company clerk. By the last entry — dated eight months before my father walked into the channel at slack tide and did not come back up — the debt stood at seven hundred and forty pesos.

Seven hundred and forty. Owed by a dead man. Transferable, per clause fourteen, to the estate. To the widow. To the children.

To me.

I had always believed my father drowned owing nothing but a grave. It was one of the few clean things I owned, that belief. I kept it the way you keep a lamp lit in a window — not because it does anything, but because the dark is worse without it. Now a dead clerk's elegant handwriting had reached up out of the *Cormorant* and put it out.

"He never told us," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "He never said a word."

"Would you have?" the widow asked. "With a child in the house? You carry it alone or you drown alone. That's how they built it. That's the whole design, Jude. Not the interest. The silence."

I stood there holding pages that proved my father had been robbed for the entirety of his working life, that his death had been entered in a ledger as a transfer of assets, that I myself was a line item with legs. And the terrible arithmetic of it started turning over in me, the way I turned over shell counts, automatic, unstoppable: if these pages were true, then every contract in the harbor was voidable. If they were voidable, then Magistrate Corvo could void them. If Corvo could void them, then the only thing standing between four hundred bonded families and freedom was a wax seal, a closed bridge, and one unremarkable diver's child who was very good at not being seen.

Fear curdles. That's the truth nobody tells you. It doesn't fade, it sours — turns thick and heavy in the stomach until it isn't fear anymore but something with weight and direction, something that points. I stood in the camphor-smelling dark of Mateo Ferran's back room and felt my own terror turn over like a tide and start pulling the other way.

"If I take this," I said slowly, "and they catch me, my mother pays. You understand that. Whatever happens to me, the company bills her."

"Then don't get caught," the widow said. "You're supposed to be good at that."

Outside, the monsoon wind slammed against the shutters, and somewhere down the lane a company whistle blew the curfew — two notes, long-short, the sound I had obeyed for eleven years without ever once thinking about it. Tomorrow at dawn the bridge would close, and whatever moved through this harbor afterward would move the way the fish moved, under things, between things, unseen.

I wrapped the pages back in their oilcloth. My hands were steadier than hers had been, which surprised me, and shouldn't have. Small, quick hands. Hands made for carrying things that fit in two palms.

"I'll need until midnight," I said. "And I'll need to talk to Teyo."

The widow pressed the packet into my shirt, against my chest, and strapped it there with a length of diver's belt — Mateo's own belt, I realized, still stiff with salt. She buckled it the way you buckle a harness on someone going down into deep water, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because we both understood exactly what she was doing.

She was sending a diver down.

When I stepped through the bead curtain into the front room, the keening paused, and every eye in the house turned toward me — and I understood, with a cold drop in my belly, that the harbor had already begun to watch me differently. Word traveled faster than water in San Verrano. Somewhere out there, Brannigan's men were knocking on doors, asking their questions, and the reward hadn't even been posted yet.

I pulled my cap low and went out into the rain, toward the smuggler's row where Teyo slept, moving quick and small and — for the first time in my life — remarkable.
