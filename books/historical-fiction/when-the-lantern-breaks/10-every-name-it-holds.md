## 10. Every Name It Holds

The archive smelled of cold stone and lamp oil, the way it had a year ago when he had stood in front of the clerk's desk with his hands shaking and his voice steady. Today his hands were quiet. He had learned, in the long months since, that grief did not leave a body so much as settle into it, the way silt settles in a riverbed—still present, still shaping the current, but no longer choking it.

Quentin Vane came to the provincial archive on the first Tuesday of autumn, because that was the day the reading room opened early and the light came through the tall eastern windows at an angle that made the dust glow like something alive. He had made the journey four times now. Each time he told himself he would not come again for a while. Each time he found himself on the road within the month, drawn back the way a tongue returns to the socket of a missing tooth.

The ledger lay open in its display case in the center of the room, under glass, under lock, under the small brass plaque that read: *Casualty Register, Mobile Hospital Nine — Deposited by Testimony, 14th of the Harvest Month.* The clerk, a narrow man named Odell who had once been nothing but a name passed along in Sister Maren's careful whisper, had fought for that plaque. The commission had wanted *Unverified Documents* or nothing at all. Odell had won. It was one of the few victories of that year that had not cost anyone anything, and Quentin treasured it disproportionately.

He stood at the edge of the case and read the open page as though he had not written it, as though he did not know every ink blot and water stain by heart. The flooded field hospital had gotten into the paper that first night—the pages still carried a faint tide line across their lower third, a brown ghost of the water that had risen around the cots while he triaged the wounded and memorized names. He had thought then that the book would be ruined before it was finished. Instead the ruin had become part of the record. Odell said visitors always asked about the stain. Odell said it made them believe.

There were others in the reading room this morning. There almost always were, now. That was the thing Quentin had not anticipated—not the testimony, not the trial of standing before the commission's lawyers while they objected and the historians scribbled, but this: that the ledger would become a place people traveled to. A destination for the bereaved.

An old woman in a gray shawl bent close to the glass, her lips moving. She had been there when Quentin arrived, and she was still there an hour later, working her way down the columns with one gnarled finger hovering above the case, never quite touching. Quentin knew without asking what she was doing. She was looking for a name. They all were. They came from the provinces and the capital and, lately, from across the border—from the countries that had been enemies and were now merely neighbors with bad memories—and they searched the pages for their sons, their brothers, their husbands, their daughters who had put on uniforms and vanished into other people's wars.

The colonial drummer boy was on page eleven. Quentin had written him there in the second week: *Tomas Iriarte, age perhaps twelve, drums, fever, died Thursday before dawn. His officers listed him as stores expended.* The old woman's finger stopped somewhere near that page, and her shoulders shook, and Quentin turned away to give her the privacy of her grief, because he understood that too—that mourning needed walls sometimes, even glass ones.

A young man sat at one of the long oak tables with a notebook, copying names in a schoolroom hand. Copying them slowly, carefully, checking each letter twice. When Quentin passed behind him he saw the heading the boy had written atop his own page: *Men of Harrow Vale Who Did Not Come Home.* Eleven names so far. There would be more. Quentin felt something move in his chest that was not quite pain and not quite pride—something closer to the feeling of watching a seed break soil in ground you had been told was barren.

This was what he had wanted, in the flooded dark, with the pen shaking in his fingers. Not fame—he had lost any appetite for anonymity's opposite during the hearings, when his face appeared in the capital papers beside the word *insubordinate* and strangers wrote him letters both vicious and kind. Not vindication either; the commission had censured him, stripped his nursing certification for two years, and then quietly reinstated it when the public outcry grew inconvenient, which taught him everything he needed to know about how institutions repented. No. What he had wanted was simpler and larger: that the names should exist somewhere outside his own skull. That if he drowned, or burned, or simply forgot—as everyone forgets, eventually—the record would hold.

It held.

* * *

Ada Brannock found him just past noon, as she always seemed to, appearing at his elbow with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had spent years locating people the government insisted did not exist.

"You're standing in my light," she said, though he wasn't, and she sat down anyway at the nearest table and patted the chair beside her until he took it.

"You could have written," he said. "You always say you'll write."

"And you always say you'll answer." She set her bag on the table—a new bag, leather, government-issue, though she'd confided once that she'd requested the plainest one available out of principle—and produced two apples and a knife, and began peeling one in a single unbroken spiral, the way she did when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed. "I've been to see Odell."

"I know. He told me you'd be here."

"He tells you everything these days. I've created a monster." But she smiled when she said it, and slid half the apple across the table to him. "I came about the addendum."

Quentin looked up. "What addendum?"

"The supplementary register." Ada kept her voice low, pitched beneath the old woman's murmuring and the scratch of the young man's pen. "Odell opened a second volume. Anything the commission refused to enter last year—testimony that was struck, depositions withdrawn under pressure, names that surfaced afterward—he's been collecting them. Quietly. With the director's blessing, now that the director is someone else." She paused. "Sister Maren is in it."

The apple went tasteless in Quentin's mouth.

"Her full deposition," Ada went on gently. "From the earlier war. The one that ruined her. Odell tracked down the original court transcript—it was in a sealed box in the military annex, misfiled for thirty years—and he entered it whole. Her testimony, her dismissal order, her letter of appeal. All of it. Under her name. *Matron Maren Ostrevik, Order of Mercy, Third Class.*" Ada's voice caught slightly on the honorific; Maren had never mentioned the class of it, had let them believe she'd been nobody at all. "And a note in her own hand, dated the year of her dismissal. Do you want to hear it?"

He nodded. He could not have spoken.

Ada recited it from memory, because of course she had memorized it; that was what Ada did with things that mattered. *"'They took my position, my pension, and my good name, but they could not take what I witnessed, and I have decided to consider that a professional advantage.'"*

Quentin laughed—a short, broken sound that turned halfway through into something else, and Ada pretended very hard to be interested in her apple peel until he had mastered himself.

"She would have hated the fuss," he managed.

"She would have loved the fuss," Ada corrected. "She'd have hated being thanked for it." She folded the peel into her pocket—she wasted nothing, never had—and looked toward the display case, where the old woman in gray had finally straightened, pressing her fingertips to the glass above some page Quentin could not see. "She found him, I think. The man she came for. She's been coming every week since spring."

"I know. I've seen her."

"You could speak to her, you know. People ask about you. The ones who come regularly—they know your face from the papers, and they want—" Ada hesitated, choosing the word with a census taker's precision. "They want to thank the person who counted. Some of them want to tell you things. About their dead. Things that aren't in any ledger."

"I'm not sure I can carry more names, Ada."

"That's not what they'd be giving you." She touched his wrist, brief and warm. "They'd be giving you the rest of the story. You only ever got the endings, Quentin. They have the beginnings. The middles. What Tomas Iriarte was like before the fever—his mother wrote to the archive last month, did Odell tell you? She sent a photograph. Odell has started keeping a companion file. Letters, photographs, whatever families send. So the ledger isn't the whole of it anymore. It's the door to the whole of it."

Quentin sat with that for a long moment, watching the dust turn in the slanted light. A door. He had built the ledger as a wall against forgetting—a barricade raised in one exhausted night and defended for years—and all this time it had been becoming something else. An entrance. People walked in through those pages now, and found rooms on the other side filled with voices.

"Tomas's mother," he said. "Does she know I—"

"She knows you were the one who stayed with him at the end. She asked specifically. Odell gave her your address." Ada's mouth curved. "Expect a letter. Expect several. She writes the way some people breathe."

* * *

The reading room emptied as the afternoon wore on. The old woman left last, pausing at the door to look back at the case, and Quentin rose impulsively and followed her into the corridor.

"Madam," he said. "Forgive me. Were you—did you find what you were looking for?"

She studied him with eyes gone pale as river ice, and then, slowly, she smiled. "Page eleven," she said. "My grandson's regiment passed through your hospital in the retreat. He was listed as missing in the official report. Missing, for six years." Her hand found his and gripped it with surprising strength. "You wrote *died of wounds, Tuesday, evening, held someone's hand.* That was all I needed. Six years of *missing,* and then one sentence, and I could bury him at last." She squeezed once more and released him. "Thank you for holding his hand, whoever's hand it was. Thank you for writing it down."

She went out into the autumn light, and Quentin stood in the corridor a long while, his throat closed, his eyes burning, until Ada appeared beside him and said nothing at all, which was her particular genius.

* * *

Near closing, when the clerks had begun banking the lamps and the light in the tall windows had gone the color of honey, Quentin returned to the display case one final time. Odell was locking the outer doors; Ada waited by the coat rack, patient as furniture. The ledger lay open to its last written page—the page where the entries stopped, mid-column, because the war had ended before the book was full.

But below the final entry, in fresh ink, a new line had been added in a hand Quentin recognized as Odell's careful copperplate:

*Addendum, entered by direction of the Provincial Archivist: the compiler of this register, having given sworn testimony to its contents, is hereby recorded among those it commemorates.*

And beneath it, waiting, a blank line.

Odell had explained it to him months ago, apologetic, almost embarrassed: the archivists' convention held that a deposited document belonged to its depositor, and the depositor's name belonged in the finding record. Quentin had deferred. He had come back four times and deferred four times, telling himself next visit, next season, when the words were right.

The words were never going to be right. He understood that now. There were no right words for a life; there was only the name, and the willingness to stand behind it.

Quentin took the pen Odell offered—the same make of cheap steel nib he had used in the field hospital, which Odell had sourced specially—and leaned over the case after Odell unlocked it, and wrote, in a hand steadier than the one that had begun the book in floodwater:

*Vane, Quentin. Nurse, Mobile Hospital Nine. Born in the parish of Lowmere, county of Dunmore, in the spring of the famine year. Counted the living. Counted the dead. Was counted, at last, among friends.*

He set down the pen. Ada came to stand beside him, and together they read the line back, and it was not poetry, and it was not enough, and it was exactly enough.

Outside, the bells of the provincial capital rang the hour, and somewhere beyond the rooftops the road ran south toward the hospital, toward the graves, toward the ordinary world where laundry dried and letters arrived and a boy at a copying desk was building a list of his own. Quentin Vane buttoned his coat against the coming cold, and walked out of the archive with his name left safely behind him in the light—

—and did not yet know that the letter waiting at his lodgings, postmarked from the capital and bearing the seal of the Ministry of Records, would ask him to do it all again: a hundred hospitals, a decade of omitted names, a national register, and his signature at the bottom of the first page.
