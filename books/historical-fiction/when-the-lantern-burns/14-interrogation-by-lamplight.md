## 14. Interrogation by Lamplight

The knock came at dusk, and it was polite, which is how I knew it was trouble.

Constables knock like they're breaking the door down out of habit. Neighbors knock twice, quick, apologetic. The magistrate's clerk knocked the way a man knocks when he already knows you're home and wants you to understand that the door was never really yours.

"Mistress Whitlock." Mr. Quill — and yes, that was his name, and yes, the universe has a sense of humor — stood on my step with his hat in his hands and a lantern held by a constable behind him. Snow gathered on his shoulders like he'd been standing there a while, deciding. "Forgive the hour. I wonder if I might trouble you for a conversation."

"A conversation," I said.

"A short one. There's a matter of record that requires clarification." He smiled the way a ledger smiles: balanced, bloodless. "Nothing formal. No charges. Merely questions."

I could have refused him. I want that on the record, wherever records go when they're honest. I could have shut the door and let him stand in the snow, and he would have gone, and he would have come back with a warrant and four men instead of one lantern. I had learned enough in twenty years of Aldous Whitlock's workshop to know the difference between a door you can close and a door that only looks like it closes.

"Come in, then," I said. "Mind the ink on the threshold. It doesn't come out."

* * *

He sat in the good chair, the one I kept for customers, and folded his hands, and looked around the workshop with the unhurried appetite of a man taking inventory. The lamps were lit. I had made sure of that. A dim room invites a search; a bright room invites compliments. Aldous taught me that too, though I didn't know until later that he'd learned it the hard way.

"You've kept the place well," Quill said. "His instruments especially. Most apprentices let them scatter."

"He'd have haunted me."

"Would he?" Something moved behind his eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "I had the impression he wasn't a man who believed in haunting. Only in evidence."

I poured tea because my hands wanted a task and my face wanted something to do besides be afraid. He accepted the cup, thanked me, did not drink it. Constables' tea is a prop; clerks' tea is a hostage. I sat across from him and waited, because with men like Quill, the first one to fill a silence pays for it.

He let me win the silence. That frightened me more than anything else he did that night.

"The matter of record," he said at last, "concerns your parentage."

The tea in my own cup trembled — a small tremor, a hairline crack in the surface — and I set it down before he could read it. I'd had twenty years' practice at stillness. I used all of it.

"My parentage," I repeated. "I was a foundling. Aldous took me in. That's the whole of it. You'd know better than I, sir — it's all in your parish records."

"It is." He nodded, agreeable, terrible. "Foundling, yes. Left at the chapel gate at Merrow's Bend, autumn of the year of the flood. Taken in by the mapmaker Whitlock, who happened to be surveying the valley that same season." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, precise as a draftsman. "A remarkable coincidence, Mistress Whitlock. I have spent my career among records, and I will tell you what records have taught me: there are no remarkable coincidences. There are only stories people agreed to stop asking about."

"You've come a long way to ask about a foundling's christening gown."

"I've come because the magistrate's office is preparing to certify the atlas."

My heart did something complicated. "Certify it. You mean — approve it?"

"Approve it, purchase it, place it in the crown's collection with the magistrate's seal of endorsement." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which was worse. "It would be a considerable honor. For you, and for his memory. A finished work, dedicated properly, its provenance clean. The office would see to that. The office is very good at seeing to things."

There it was. Under the velvet, the shape of the hook.

"And what," I said carefully, "would the office want to see to, in exchange?"

"Only accuracy." He set down the cup. "The atlas as Aldous Whitlock left it is — incomplete. Certain folios missing. Certain surveys unreconciled. The valley sheets, in particular." He watched me the way I'd watched a man read one of my maps for the first time, waiting to see which line his eye went to. "The office would like those reconciled. Completed. By his apprentice, in his hand, which I understand you are more than capable of. And then the originals — the drafts, the field notes, the preliminary plates — surrendered, of course. Working papers. Clutter. No one needs clutter."

"And the final atlas would say what?"

He spread his hands, gentle. "What an atlas says. Rivers, roads, elevations. The flood line, marked as an act of God, which the church has already ruled it was, and which the records reflect, and which no honest mapmaker has ever had cause to dispute."

*No honest mapmaker has ever had cause to dispute.* I turned the sentence over like a coin I suspected was shaved. Twenty years ago, a valley drowned, and the church called it God, and the records called it God, and a mapmaker came to survey it and stayed twenty years teaching an orphan girl to draw coastlines she'd never seen. And now the office sat in my workshop offering me an honor with one hand and holding out the other for the one thing that could prove the honor was a lie.

"You said no charges," I said.

"There are no charges." He leaned forward, and the lamplight made his face kind, and kindness on that face was the most frightening thing in the room. "Mistress Whitlock — Petra, if I may — I am going to speak to you as one keeper of records to another. What do you imagine happens to a woman alone, of no family, no name, holding papers that contradict a ruling of the church and the crown? Nothing dramatic. I promise you, nothing dramatic. She is simply found to have mislaid them. Or they are found to be forgeries, and she is found to have been deceived — perhaps by her late master, whose grief, we know, unsettled him in his last years. She is pitied. She is watched. She loses her license, her trade, her name in the guild rolls. She becomes what she was before he found her: no one." He paused. "Whereas the woman who completes the atlas — the woman the office endorses — becomes the most respected cartographer in the province before she is thirty. A pension. A commission. A name."

"You've thought about my future a great deal."

"It's my profession to think about records," he said, "and you, Mistress Whitlock, are a record. The last one. That is what you have never understood, perhaps because he never told you — and I confess I've often wondered why he didn't. Everything else he did was so careful. So contingented." He almost smiled. "You are the final page of a very old file, and the office would prefer that page read *closed*."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs, in the loft where I had been hiding a fugitive and a set of plates that would hang us both, a floorboard settled with a sound like a held breath released. Quill's eyes flicked toward the ceiling — one flick, smooth, catalogued — and came back to me, and I could not tell if he had heard it or if he wanted me to think he had.

"You keep saying *the office*," I said. "Not the magistrate."

"The office is the magistrate," he said mildly. "The magistrate is the office. It has been a long winter for everyone. He is anxious to have the valley matter settled before the thaw brings — visitors. Inspectors. The usual spring traffic of men with questions." He rose, buttoned his coat, and became once more the courteous gray man from my doorstep. "I'll leave you the night to consider. The offer is generous because the office believes in generosity. Mercy, even. I've always thought of this as mercy, what I do. Tidying. So much suffering comes from untidy records."

At the door he stopped, hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"Your mother's name was Maren," he said. "Maren Ostley, of Merrow's Bend. She was a weaver. She is listed in the flood rolls as deceased, with one dependent, age two months, unaccounted for." The latch clicked. "Unaccounted for, Mistress Whitlock, is a word that has kept you alive for twenty years. Think about whether you'd like it to keep doing so."

Then he was gone into the snow, and the constable's lantern bobbed away down the lane, and I stood in my bright, tidy workshop among my dead master's instruments and understood that I had just been interrogated by a man who had never once asked me a question he didn't already know the answer to.

* * *

I did not sleep. I did not go up to the loft, either, though every stair in me wanted to climb it and put my forehead against the door and tell Elias everything. I sat at the workbench with the lamp turned low and did what I do when the world becomes unspeakable: I drew. A coastline, any coastline. My hand knew the work and the work knew my hand, and for a while the pen was the only part of me that wasn't afraid.

Maren Ostley. A weaver. I tried the name on like a coat cut for someone else and found, horribly, that it fit. The way I hold my head when I'm counting. The way I sing when I think no one can hear — a low, looping tune Aldous once told me to stop humming because it distracted his measurements, and I had never known where I learned it. A weaving tune. A valley tune. Twenty years of careful quiet, and the whole time the quiet had a name, and the clerk had read it off a roll of the dead while I sat there pouring his tea.

He had known. All this time — every visit, every polite inquiry about Aldous's health and the atlas's progress — he had known who I was and let me go on not knowing. That was the cruelest economy of it: he hadn't even needed to threaten me. He had simply waited until the truth would do the threatening for him, delivered at the exact hour it would hurt most and work best. Mercy, he'd called it. Tidying.

And the offer — I made myself look at it straight, the way you look at a map of a place you're afraid of. Finish the atlas in Aldous's hand, surrender the originals, take the pension and the seal and the name, and live. It would be so easy. It would be so *safe*. The plates would go into an office drawer and the drawer would go into a records room and the records room would outlive everyone who remembered Merrow's Bend, and in a hundred years the flood would be an act of God with a beautiful certified map to prove it, and I would be the most respected cartographer in the province, and I would know, every day of my long respectable life, exactly what my beautiful certified lines were standing on.

Aldous had died rather than let that happen. I was only now beginning to understand that he hadn't died *of* the atlas. He had died *for* the option of it — for the chance that someday, someone would finish it honestly. And here was the office, offering to buy that chance from his heir for the price of a pension.

*Unaccounted for is a word that has kept you alive.* Yes. And silence was the word that had kept the magistrate alive, and tidy records were the word that had kept the flood a weather event, and I sat in the lamplight understanding that Quill hadn't come to threaten me at all. He'd come to make me an accomplice. The way I had been an accomplice for twenty years without knowing it — only this time I would know. This time I would sign my name to it.

The lamp burned down. The pen scratched. And upstairs, at last, a floorboard creaked — deliberately this time, twice, our signal — and I set down the pen and looked at the door to the loft for a long moment.

He had heard. Or he had waited. Either way, Elias Row was awake in the dark above me with a satchel of names and a wound and a patience I had never earned, and the clerk would come back at dawn for my answer.

I blew out the lamp, and climbed the stairs.
