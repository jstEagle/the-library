## 15. The Choice of the Ink

*Petra*

The clerk left the workshop the way a cat leaves a room it has knocked something off a shelf — slowly, so you understand he meant to do it, and with the pleasant certainty that he will do it again.

He had sat in Aldous's chair. That was the detail I kept returning to as the door clicked shut behind him and the wind took up its complaining again. He had sat in my master's chair, folded his gloved hands on the drafting table, and offered me mercy the way one offers a beggar a coin one has already counted. Complete the atlas in the magistrate's approved style. Hand over the original plates. Say nothing, ever, about Merrow's Bend or about who my mother was, and live out my life as the respected widow-in-waiting of a respectable trade.

"You've had twenty years of silence, Miss Whitlock," he'd said, and my blood had gone to ice water in my veins, because Whitlock was a name he had no right to say with that gentleness. "It would be a shame to spend the rest of them being difficult."

I had told him I would consider it. I had even smiled. Twenty years of practice will teach a woman to smile with her mouth while her mind is running for the floorboards.

Now the lamplight guttered and the storm outside threw sleet against the shutters like a creditor at the door, and I sat alone with the lie I had just handed him and the truth I had just promised to bury, and I discovered that I was not considering anything at all.

I went to the floorboards.

The boards came up the way they always did — a whisper of grit, the smell of cold earth and linseed — and there they were: Aldous's true plates, the survey of Merrow's Bend, the village that had been drowned on paper before it was drowned in fact. I lifted the topmost sheet into the light. My master's hand, steady and exact, every rooftop and garden plot of a town the official atlas insisted had never existed. And in the margin, small as secrets, the names.

I had spent a week learning that my mother's name lived in that margin. I had spent three days learning to hold a pen again after that.

The clerk wanted me to finish the atlas. Very well. I would finish it. I simply declined to finish *his*.

There is a peculiar freedom in deciding to be ruined. I felt it settle over me like a well-cut coat. Whatever came now — arrest, the fire I half-suspected was already being discussed in the magistrate's warm rooms, the long cold ride to some prison where difficult women were made quiet — it could not be worse than the alternative, which was to spend the rest of my life being the clerk's pen instead of my own.

I was still standing there, plates in hand, wearing my ruin like finery, when I heard the scrape at the back shutter.

I had a knife in my hand before my mind caught up — Aldous's knife, the one for trimming vellum, which is not a weapon but has opinions — and I had crossed the dark workshop and put the blade's edge against a throat before I recognized the shape in the shadows, the shape I had been dreaming of for six nights, the shape I had believed, since the constable took his satchel, was rotting in some cell below the courthouse.

"Elias," I said, and my voice did something humiliating, something between a sob and a laugh.

"Petra," he said, teeth chattering, sleet in his hair, "if you're going to cut me, could you do it inside? It's warmer."

I let him in. I lit the lamp. And then, because I am my master's apprentice and grief has made me honest in the worst ways, I hit him — once, flat-handed, on the shoulder — before I put my arms around him and held on like a woman holding a railing in a flood.

"You're supposed to be in a cell," I said into his coat, which smelled of river and smoke and cold.

"The ferryman," he said, "has a talent for locks and a grudge twenty years deep. There was a diversion. A cart, some brandy, a very convincing argument about a broken axle." His arms came around me, careful of the plates still in my hand. "I came back through the storm. The ice is singing out there, Petra. It's bad. I nearly went in twice."

"Then you're a fool."

"I'm a man with a satchel's worth of reasons."

"You have no satchel. They took it."

"I have a memory," he said, "which they could not take, though I'm told they tried."

And there it was — the thing I had been circling since the night he first told me about Merrow's Bend, the thing that made loving him feel like standing at the edge of broken ice. He had come back not for me. He had come back for the plates.

* * *

*Elias*

I had imagined the reunion perhaps forty times on the walk back through the storm, and in not one of those imaginings did she greet me with a knife. I have learned that Petra will always exceed the imagination, usually in directions that leave marks.

But she held me, after. She held me the way the river holds a boat that has finally come home, and for a few minutes I let myself believe the worst of the winter was behind us. Then she set the plates on the table, and I saw her face in the lamplight, and I understood that the worst of the winter was standing right in front of me, wearing an expression I had last seen on my uncle's face the week before the flood.

"We're leaving," I said.

"No."

"Petra—"

"No," she said again, and began rolling the plates into their oilcloth with the maddening calm of a woman packing for a journey she has already decided against taking. "Listen to me. The clerk came. He knows who I am — he said *Whitlock* like he'd been saying it to himself for years. He offered me the atlas if I give up the originals and keep silent. Which means the magistrate doesn't just want the maps destroyed. He wants a finished, official atlas with his lie bound into it, published, sitting in every magistrate's office in the province. He wants my master's name on a forgery."

"Then we burn the drafts and go. Tonight. The ferryman can take us downriver when the ice breaks—"

"And go where?" She turned on me, and her eyes were wet and furious and the most alive thing I had seen in twenty years of dying towns. "To the capital, with what? A satchel of plates and a story? Who reads a fugitive's satchel, Elias? Who prints it? The clerk told me the truth himself, without knowing it. Paper is the only witness they fear. A map that exists cannot be unmade — Aldous wrote that, or something like it, in every margin of his life. If we run, we're two more drowned names waiting to happen. If we *finish* it — if we finish it in his hand, with the names in the margins, and get it out before they burn this place, because they will burn this place, you know it as well as I do — then it's not a satchel anymore. It's a book. It's a hundred books. It's a thing that gets copied and argued over and hidden in libraries by people who've never met us and can't be threatened by us."

I wanted to shout at her. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the whole point of what we're building. I wanted to shout, because she was right, and because being right was going to cost us everything, and because I had already lost everyone I loved once to a man who understood exactly the power of paper and had used it to erase a village.

"You'd gamble the last of Merrow's Bend," I said quietly, "on a printing house."

"I'd gamble *myself* on it," she said. "The plates I'd wrap in wax and oilcloth the way Aldous taught me, the way that survived a river once already. But I won't run, Elias. Running is what they designed us for. Silence is what they built. Twenty years they let me live in this workshop like a mouse in a wall, because a mouse in a wall threatens nothing. I am done being the mouse."

"And if finishing it kills you?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the storm threw itself against the shutters, and the river ice groaned somewhere in the dark like a thing dreaming of spring.

"Then it kills me doing the one thing that matters," she said. "What did your uncle die of, Elias? What did they all die of? Not the water. The *silence* after it. Everyone who could have spoken, didn't, because it was safer, because it was sensible, because the magistrate was charming and the winter was long. I was raised in that silence. I was *made* of it. And I am telling you that I will not carry it one more winter, not even for you. Especially not for you. If I run with you, I'll be carrying it forever, and I'll come to resent the man I ran with, and I refuse. I refuse in advance. Do you understand?"

I understood. That was the hell of it. I understood so well that my argument collapsed in my chest like a snow-laden roof.

Because here is what I had not told her, what I had barely told myself: I had not crossed the ice in December to survive. I had crossed it because my uncle's friend Aldous had once told me, when I was nine years old and full of questions about the surveying instruments, that a map is a promise. *You draw a thing truly,* he'd said, *and you've promised it will not be forgotten.* The flood took my village. The silence took everything else — the names, the graves, the fact that we had ever stood on that bend of the river and been alive. I had come out of the water carrying a satchel because I wanted the promise kept.

And the woman in front of me, ink to her wrists, grief worn like armor, was the only person left alive who could keep it.

"You'll need to finish it in his hand," I said at last, and my voice came out rough. "The clerk will compare it. Any expert will."

"I know," she said. "I've been practicing his letters for twenty years. It turns out that was the apprenticeship. It turns out he was training his own forger." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, and I loved it. "He knew, Elias. I'm starting to understand how much he knew."

I crossed to her and took her hands — ink-stained, cold, shaking only a little — and I said the thing I had crossed a frozen river twice to be able to say.

"Then we finish it. But you do it my way on one point. When it's done — when the last folio is inked and the copies are out of this building — you come with me. No heroics at the door. No going back for anything. The atlas is the promise. You are not."

"Agreed," she said.

She was lying. I could tell she was lying, the way you can tell river ice is lying about its thickness — by the sound it makes underfoot. And I agreed anyway, because love, I am learning, is the willingness to walk out onto the ice with someone even when you can hear it groaning, and to spend the whole crossing arguing about which of you will save the other if it breaks.

* * *

*Petra*

He stayed. That was the whole of it, the miracle of it, and I was too tired and too full of resolve to be properly afraid of what it would cost.

We worked until the lamp burned low and then lit the second lamp, the good one, Aldous's reading lamp, and the workshop filled with the small industrious sounds of two people committing a crime against a magistrate: the whisper of the burnisher, the scratch of the pen, the soft curses of a man learning to grind ink at midnight because his teacher was a tyrant.

"You press too hard," I told him, over his shoulder, for the fourth time.

"You say that like it's a flaw and not a moral position."

"It's both. Ink is testimony. You don't stab it into the page, you *lay* it down. A name should look like it grew there."

He looked at the margin he was lettering — *Hale, Miriam. Hale, Thomas. Hale, the child, unnamed* — and his jaw tightened, and he lifted the pen and laid the next name down gently, like setting a stone on a grave.

We did not speak of the argument again. There was no need. It had not been resolved; it had been *postponed*, the way storms are postponed, and somewhere in the walls of the night I could hear the postponed weather coming. He would try to make me run when the plates were done. I would refuse. One of us would be right, and the other would be widowed, and I found I could not make myself care which, only that we would not both be wrong.

Near dawn, the storm eased to a hiss, and Elias fell asleep in Aldous's chair with his bandaged shoulder against the wing, and I sat looking at the unfinished folios and thought about my master's locked drawer — the one in the big chest, the one whose key he wore on a cord around his neck until the river took both.

He had known he was going to die. I was sure of it now, sure the way you are sure of a sum you have checked three times. He had trained a forger. He had hidden the plates. He had stayed in the valley, surveying, when any sensible man would have run — because a map that exists cannot be unmade, and he meant to make it exist.

Men like that leave letters. It's practically a professional obligation.

When the light came up grey over the frozen river, I went to the great chest, and I knelt, and I worked the lock of his drawer with the thin blade he'd taught me to use on stubborn vellum — and the drawer slid open, and there, on top, sealed with wax and addressed in his hand, was a letter bearing my name.

*To Petra, when she is ready.*

I sat back on my heels with my heart hammering, the letter in my hands, the storm's last breath rattling the shutters like a knock at the door.

I was not ready. I opened it anyway.
