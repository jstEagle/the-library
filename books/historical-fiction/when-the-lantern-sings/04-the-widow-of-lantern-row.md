## 4. The Widow of Lantern Row

The lantern-maker's widow lived at the crooked end of Lantern Row, where the street narrowed to a throat and the houses leaned together like conspirators sharing a secret. Xanthe had passed her door a thousand times in his life—had counted it, without thinking, the way you count fence posts on a familiar road—but he had never once knocked.

He stood before it now in the blue hour after dusk, his collar up, the copied ledger pages sewn into the lining of his coat like a second, more dangerous skin. Nine pages. Three nights of work by candle stub, copying names and debts and loyalties in a hand small enough to shame a spider. His fingers still smelled of ink. They always smelled of ink. He sometimes wondered if he would smell of ink when they buried him, and whether that would be a comfort or an indictment.

*Knock*, he told himself. *She's just an old woman.*

But she wasn't just an old woman, and that was precisely the problem. Auntie Sable was the woman whose son had been strung up on the magistrate's post six years ago for running messages the garrison didn't like, and who had attended the execution with her back straight as a mast and her face arranged into something so calm that the soldiers had reportedly found it unsettling. She was the woman who still made lanterns—hundreds of them, every festival, beautiful ones, paper skins over bamboo ribs—and who had never once been seen to weep. The town said grief had frozen in her the way the river froze in winter: completely, and with things visible underneath.

Xanthe raised his fist. Lowered it. Raised it again.

"You'll wake the whole street at this rate," said a voice above him, dry as rice husks, "and then I'll have to charge you for the noise."

Auntie Sable leaned out of the upper window, a clay pipe trailing smoke into the cold. In the lamplight behind her her face was all planes and shadows, like something carved rather than born.

"I—" Xanthe began.

"Side gate," she said, and withdrew.

* * *

The side gate opened onto a courtyard stacked with bamboo poles and drying paper, and beyond it a workshop that smelled of glue and tung oil and, faintly, of smoke that had nothing to do with lanterns. Sable lit a lamp, considered him across it, and did not offer him a seat. There were stools available. She simply didn't offer one, and Xanthe understood this was a test he was already failing or passing—he couldn't tell which.

"Zhou's boy," she said. "The quiet one."

"Yes, auntie."

"The quiet ones are always the troublemakers. The loud ones only want attention; the quiet ones want *things*." She drew on her pipe. "You've been walking past my door for three days. I count footsteps the way other women count grandchildren. What do you want?"

He should have lied. Some part of him, the part that had spent seventeen years being no one in particular, reached automatically for the smooth small falsehoods that kept a person safe—I was just walking, I wanted to buy a lantern, my father sends greetings. But the pages pressed against his ribs, and three nights of fear had worn the grooves too deep, and what came out instead was the truth, entire:

"They took our ledgers. Captain Ruan's men. Everything—the resident rolls, the debt books, the birth records." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it. "I copied what I could before they sealed the cases. Nine pages. Names that will hang people if Ruan reads them carefully. And mine—" He stopped.

Sable watched him stop, and let him stop, which was somehow worse than pressing.

"And yours," she said at last, not as a question. She set down the pipe. "Boy, do you know why I make lanterns? Not sell them—anyone can sell them. Why I *make* them, year after year, for a town that watched my son die and then bought my lanterns to celebrate the New Year as if nothing had happened?"

Xanthe shook his head.

"Because a lantern is a promise you can hold in your hands. You put a flame inside a thing made to burn, and you say to the dark: not tonight. Not this one. Tonight this light gets where it's going." She rose, joints cracking like green bamboo, and crossed to a shelf where finished lanterns hung in rows, round-bellied and patient. "My Wei believed that. He carried messages because he thought paper could outrun soldiers. He was wrong about the soldiers. I have spent six years deciding whether he was wrong about the paper."

She turned. Her eyes in the lamplight were the color of tea left standing.

"So don't stand in my workshop smelling of ink and terror and tell me you came for a lantern."

Xanthe took out the pages. His hands were steadier than he expected—copying type all his life had given him hands that did what they were told even when the rest of him mutinied. He laid the nine sheets on her workbench, weighted the corners with bamboo offcuts, and stepped back.

"I need somewhere they can't be found. Somewhere that isn't the shop, isn't our house, isn't anywhere Ruan's men might look if they decide the printer's family knows more than it should. People say—" He hesitated. People said a great many things about Auntie Sable, most of them in whispers: that she knew the old salt-smugglers' tunnels under the town, that she'd helped families disappear during the last conscription, that the garrison had searched her place twice and found nothing but lanterns, though everyone agreed afterward that there had been *somewhere else* to find.

"People say," Sable prompted, with the ghost of amusement, "a great deal. Go on."

"That you know the town underneath the town."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she picked up the topmost page, read it with the unhurried attention of someone who had once read everything her son died carrying, and set it down again.

"Old Zhou's debts to the Chen brothers," she murmured. "The grain weights at Wu's mill. Little Peony Lin's real age—she's been paying child tax on a girl registered as a boy, clever woman." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "And page seven. The Zhou birth records."

Xanthe's ears went hot. "You don't have to—"

"I have eyes, boy, and I had a friend who was a midwife. Nobody in Anshui is entirely what their papers say—that's half of why the ledgers are dangerous and half of why nobody wants them read aloud." She tapped page seven once, lightly, the way you'd touch a bruise to learn its shape. "Your father kept this secret longer than most men keep their tempers. It must have cost him."

"It cost us all," Xanthe said quietly. "Every day. Quietly."

Something moved in the old woman's face—not softness exactly, but recognition, the look of one mason seeing another's chisel marks in a wall.

"Very well." She swept the pages into a lacquered document box, the kind used for wedding contracts, and slid it beneath a false bottom in her paper chest among twenty identical boxes. "They'll sleep under the festival stock. If the garrison searches, they'll find lanterns, lanterns, and more lanterns, and die of boredom before they find anything else."

"Thank you, auntie. I'll repay you somehow—"

"Stop." The word cracked across the workshop like a bamboo pole breaking. Sable turned on him, and for the first time he saw the thing under the frozen river: not rage, exactly, but a heat that had been banked so long it had become structural. "Don't thank me. Thanking me makes this a kindness, and kindnesses end. This is a *debt*, do you understand? Trust is a debt with interest, and I am lending you something I cannot afford to lose twice."

"I know about your son," Xanthe said. "I'm sorry. I—"

"Don't be sorry. Be careful." She came close, and up close she smelled of oil and ash and, faintly, of the chrysanthemum tea she was famous for never serving anyone. "Wei trusted a courier from downstream. One man. He sold the route for thirty silver pieces and a pardon he never lived to use. So hear the terms of our arrangement, printer's boy, and hear them fully."

She held up one finger. "One: these pages stay here. You don't visit, you don't send notes, you don't look at my door when you pass it. If you must think of me, think of me as a lantern shop and nothing more."

A second finger. "Two: if you're taken—if Ruan puts you in a room with a brazier and a pair of tongs—you will not know my name. You will have hidden the pages yourself, in the river, in a hole in the temple wall, anywhere but here. Swear it."

"I swear."

"Three." The third finger came up slowly, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which frightened him more than the rest. "Three: when this ends—however it ends—you come back, and we settle accounts. Maybe you'll owe me. Maybe I'll owe you. That's how it should be between people who intend to see each other again."

Xanthe blinked. "That's... the terms?"

"That's the terms." She allowed herself, briefly, the shadow of a smile. "Did you expect a contract? Your father prints enough of those. No—a debt means I expect to see you alive to pay it. That's the whole point of lending anything, boy. You only lend to people you mean to keep."

The words landed in his chest with a weight he hadn't anticipated. He had spent his life being kept by two people—his father and Mei—and both of them were bound to him by blood, by obligation, by the accident of a shared roof. This was different. This was a stranger choosing, with open eyes, to be findable.

"Auntie," he said, "why? Truly. You owe my family nothing."

Sable looked at him for a long moment. Then she went to the shelf, took down a small lantern—plain, unglamorous, the kind carried by night workers and midwives—and lit it from her lamp. Its glow filled the space between them, warm and unsteady.

"When they took Wei," she said, "your father came to me. Not with words—he knew better than to offer words. He came with his press and printed, free, five hundred funeral notices with Wei's name set large and proud, the way it would have been set if he'd died a respectable man. The garrison forbade it. Your father printed it anyway and delivered them himself, at night, to every house on this street." She held out the lantern. "I hung one in my window for six years. The soldiers made me take it down four times. I put it back five."

Xanthe took the lantern. It was lighter than he expected. Most things that mattered, he was learning, were.

"He never told me," Xanthe managed.

"He wouldn't. That's the trouble with quiet men—they do their loving in the dark and wonder why their children grow up thinking love is scarce." She waved him toward the side gate, brisk again, the moment folded away like paper. "Go home. Walk the long way. And boy—if a soldier stops you, you're a fool who bought a lantern for his sweetheart and paid too much. Nothing is more believable than a young man being foolish about a girl."

"There isn't a girl," Xanthe said, before he could stop himself.

"No," said Auntie Sable, and this time the smile reached her eyes. "There certainly isn't. Off you go."

* * *

He walked the long way, as instructed, along the embankment where the river ran black and slow under a sky rinsed clean of stars by cloud. Across the water the garrison fires burned in their neat military rows, orderly as punctuation, and somewhere a sentry called the hour in a voice bored past all feeling.

The lantern swung gently from his hand. He ought to snuff it—it was reckless, conspicuous, the exact opposite of invisible—but he found he couldn't. It felt less like a light than like a handhold.

His father would be awake, coughing in the way he tried to hide. Mei would be awake too, listening to the coughing and pretending not to, the way she pretended not to notice everything else about him that needed not noticing. Between the three of them they had built a house out of careful silences, and Xanthe had always thought that was what family was: people who agreed, together, not to say the true things.

Tonight, for the first time, he wondered if it could be otherwise. An old woman with a frozen face and a warm stove had looked at page seven—had looked at *him*, the whole buried architecture of him—and had not flinched, had not pitied, had simply filed him away among the things worth keeping. *You only lend to people you mean to keep.* He turned the sentence over like a piece of type, feeling its weight, its reversed and waiting sense.

At the corner of Lantern Row he paused and looked back. In Sable's upper window, small and defiant, a light was burning. He was almost certain it had not been burning when he arrived.

He was halfway home, cutting through the alley behind the dye-works, when he heard the footsteps—two sets, unhurried, falling into step behind him with the particular rhythm of men who wanted to be heard. He tightened his grip on the lantern and did not turn around.

"Printer's boy," said a voice he knew, and his stomach dropped through the cobbles. "Captain Ruan would like a word about your father's inventory. Tonight, if it's convenient."

It was Corporal Deng. And he was not alone.
