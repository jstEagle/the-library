## 9. Lanterns for the Dead

The fire woke the town before the bells did.

Xanthe came out of sleep all at once, the way a fish comes out of water, and was at the window before he knew he was moving. Orange light pulsed against the low clouds beyond Lantern Row, and the smell arrived a breath later—smoke and something sweeter under it, lacquer and bamboo, the particular perfume of Auntie Sable's workshop burning.

"Mei." He shook his sister's shoulder. "Mei, get up. Sable's shop."

She was on her feet before the sentence finished, pulling her coat over her nightclothes. Below them, in the back room, their father coughed twice and then, mercifully, went on sleeping. The doctor's medicine had finally begun to hold him through the nights, and Xanthe thought, with a strange clarity, *let him sleep through this one. Let him sleep through everything.*

They went out into streets already filling with neighbors in mismatched clothes, everyone carrying something useless—a bucket of water that would be a spoonful by the time it arrived, a blanket, a chicken that had opinions about being carried. The garrison had turned out a patrol, more to watch than to help, Xanthe noticed. Captain Ruan's men stood at the mouth of Lantern Row with their rifles slung and their faces lit orange, and not one of them moved toward the flames.

"They're not even pretending," Mei said under her breath.

"They're counting," Xanthe said. "Look. They're counting who comes."

He counted too, but for a different reason. He counted until he found her: a small square figure in a padded jacket, standing in the snow with her arms crossed, watching her own life burn with the expression of a woman grading a student's work. Unimpressed. Unhurt. Alive.

The relief hit him so hard he had to grab the wall.

* * *

By dawn the workshop was a black ribcage against the grey sky. Sable had saved almost nothing—two crates of finished lanterns she'd dragged out the back before the roof went, and the iron frame of her workbench, which sat in the snow like the skeleton of some patient animal. She stood over the ashes with her hands in her sleeves, and when Xanthe reached her, she spoke without turning.

"Third crate from the left," she said. "The one with the chrysanthemum stencil. Take it home. Don't look inside it in the street."

"What's in it?"

"Things that were under the floor." She finally turned, and her face was soot-streaked except for two clean crescents around her eyes, so that she looked like a raccoon who had seen the end of the world and found it mildly tedious. "The floor is gone now. So the things are in the crate. Follow the logic, boy, it's not complicated."

"The tunnels—"

"Gone. Or worse than gone." She said it flatly, but Xanthe saw her jaw tighten. "The fire started in the back corner. Not the stove corner. Not the lamp corner. The back corner, where the trapdoor was, where the oilcloth was, where a fire would need to be *started* if someone wanted the tunnels to burn and the rest of the town to say *ah, poor old woman, careless with her lamps.*" She spat into the ashes. "I've been careless with lamps for forty years. I've never once burned down my own floor."

Xanthe looked at the patrol at the end of the street. Still watching. Still counting.

"Who knew about the trapdoor?" he asked quietly.

Sable's eyes came around to him, and they were not warm. "You knew. Mei knew. The boy who carried my bamboo knew. The widow Chen knew, and her sister, and the sister's husband, who cannot keep a secret the way a sieve cannot keep water." She held up a finger. "And whoever else those people told, because that is what people do with secrets, they set them down like heavy bags and walk away lighter, and they never once wonder where the bag goes."

"You think it was one of ours."

"I think," Sable said, "that the fire was very well fed, and that the patrol arrived very promptly to watch it, and that Captain Ruan did not send a single man to fight it, because a man does not fight a fire he has paid for." She turned back to her ashes. "Take the crate. And boy—until we know whose mouth it was, you trust no one. Not the widow Chen. Not the sister. Not the sister's husband especially."

"Not even Deng?"

She considered this longer than Xanthe liked. "The soldier is a coin still spinning," she said at last. "I don't trust a spinning coin. But I don't trust a landed one either, because it always lands the way the hand that flipped it wanted." She waved him off. "Go. You're standing in a dead woman's doorway making conversation."

"You're not dead."

"Not for lack of someone's effort." And there, under the soot, was the ghost of her old grin. "Whoever it was will be very annoyed to hear that. I plan to be extremely annoying about it."

* * *

The crate, opened behind the locked door of the print shop's back room, held the copied ledger pages—every page Xanthe had risked his neck to make—and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth, a small arsenal of Sable's other life: a bundle of fuse cord, a tin of slow-match, three brass fittings that Xanthe did not ask about, and a child's shoe, worn through at the toe.

He didn't ask about the shoe. Some crates have two bottoms and you only lift the one you're given.

Mei stood over the pages with her arms folded. "If she's right, if someone talked—"

"Then whoever it was knows the pages existed. They may not know where they went."

"They know *she* had them."

"They knew she had tunnels," Xanthe said. "Pages could be anywhere. Ash, by now, if she'd had time." He looked at the neat stacks, Sable's handwriting on the wrappers in her crabbed, beautiful script. "She had time. That's the strange part. A fire in the back corner, and she had time to pull three crates out the back door. Almost like she was awake. Almost like someone warned her."

Mei went very still. "You think she was warned?"

"I think Auntie Sable does not survive forty years in this town by sleeping through the sounds a fire makes before it starts." He repacked the pages. "I think someone ran to her first, and then the fire came anyway, and now she's alive and furious and sleeping in the widow Chen's back room, and hating every minute of it."

"Poor widow Chen."

"Poor everyone in that back room. That woman snores like a mill."

It was the first joke either of them had made all morning, and it landed softly, the way a hand lands on a shoulder. Mei almost smiled. Then the almost fell away.

"Xanthe. If there's an informer, they know you came to her. They know the pages went somewhere. When Ruan doesn't find them in the ash—"

"Then he starts looking at houses," Xanthe finished. "Yes. I've done that sum."

"And the shop is already his. We're already living inside the search."

Xanthe set his hand flat on the crate lid, as if he could press the danger down into the wood and keep it there. "Then we move them again. Tonight. And this time nobody knows where, including us, until we're already carrying them."

"That's not a plan, that's a panic with extra steps."

"It's the family tradition," he said, and this time she did smile, briefly, tiredly, before the fear came back and settled in like a lodger.

* * *

Corporal Deng arrived at midday with the inventory ledger under his arm and the expression of a man performing normalcy as a difficult craft. He checked the type cases. He checked the paper stock. He checked the ink levels with the thoroughness of a man who had checked them yesterday and the day before, and whose real errand was standing in the doorway of the back room with soot on his boots.

"Heard about the lantern woman," he said, not looking up from the ink ledger. "Terrible. Old people and fire. Bad combination."

"Very bad," Xanthe agreed. "She's fine, by the way."

"Is she." Deng wrote something. The something was almost certainly not about ink. "The patrol report says the fire started by accident. Lamp oil. Carelessness."

"The patrol report was written before the fire was out."

Deng's pen paused. He looked up then, just for a moment, and his eyes did the thing they did—went flat and careful, a door closing most of the way but not all. "That's the kind of observation that gets people invited to dinner," he said. "The captain's been inviting people to dinner all week. Nobody enjoys the food."

"Is that a warning?"

"It's an inventory," Deng said. "Of things I have said, and things I haven't." He closed the ledger. At the door he stopped, back still turned, and spoke to the frame. "The fire crews were ordered to stand down. All of them. Before the fire was even reported." A beat. "Funny thing to order, if you didn't know it was coming."

Then he was gone, and Xanthe stood in the doorway a long moment, holding the crate's weight under his arm and the soldier's words in his chest, turning them over like coins. A spinning coin. Sable had said it exactly. He didn't know which way Deng would land, and the not-knowing had begun to feel less like hope and more like standing on ice and listening to it talk.

* * *

They moved the pages that night, in the way Xanthe had promised: badly, frantically, and with no plan beyond motion. Mei took half, sewn into the lining of a grain sack. Xanthe took the other half, wrapped around his belly under his coat, where they made him look, he reflected bitterly, like the well-fed boy he had never once been. They went out the back at different times, through different alleys, and met at the old kiln behind the pottery works, where the clay dust covered everything and their own footprints vanished behind them.

It was there, crouched in the kiln's cold throat with the pages against his ribs, that Xanthe understood the real damage. Not the tunnels. Not the workshop. The damage was this: he crouched in the dark listening for footsteps, and every footstep he imagined belonged to someone he knew.

The widow Chen, who had held Sable's hand at her son's execution. Her sister. The sister's husband, who laughed too loud and too often, and had Xanthe ever actually wondered *why* a man laughed like that, all the time, at everything, unless he was afraid of what silence would make him say?

Old apprentice Ho, who had left the shop in autumn and taken a clerk's post with the garrison, and who had wept at leaving, or so everyone said. Everyone said a lot of things.

Even Mei. Even his own sister, out there in the dark with half the pages, who had been out at night more and more this winter, who came home smelling sometimes of lamp oil and sometimes of nothing at all, and who was his sister, his sister, and the fact that the thought had even arrived in his head made him feel sick enough to be ill right there in the kiln.

*That's what an informer does,* he thought. *It doesn't burn one building. It burns every building you ever stood in, after.*

He stayed in the cold until his hands were too numb to be useful, and then he went home the long way, and lay awake listening to his father's slow breathing and Mei's quiet one, and hating himself for counting them, and counting them anyway, because that was what the fire had taught him: you count who comes out. You always count who comes out.

* * *

Sable did not stay hidden. This surprised no one who knew her, and visibly disappointed the patrol, who had perhaps hoped grief would keep her indoors. Two days after the fire she appeared at the print shop's front counter like a customer, bought one sheet of the cheapest paper, and conducted an entire conversation in the language of commerce.

"One sheet," she said. "The thin kind. The kind that takes ink well."

"We're low on the thin kind."

"Then I'll wait for your next shipment. When does it come?"

"Thursday, if the road's open."

"Roads close," she said. "Roads are the first thing to close, in my experience. A wise shopkeeper keeps his stock in more than one place." She paid for the sheet with exact change, counted out coin by coin, and met his eyes on the last one. "The widow Chen's back room has too many people in it. And one of them hums when she thinks nobody's awake. I've had forty years of my own company and I find I prefer it. So I'm taking a room above the teahouse on Bridge Street, under the name of my late husband's cousin, who was a real person and is conveniently dead. If anyone asks, I'm his widow, which is close enough to the truth that I can say it without spitting."

"You shouldn't be seen here."

"I'm buying paper," Sable said. "Old women buy paper. If that's suspicious, then the whole town is guilty and Ruan can arrest us in alphabetical order, which will at least be orderly." She tucked the sheet under her arm. Then, quieter, with the counter between them and the street door open to the cold: "I've been thinking about who it was. I've made a list. It's a short list. Lists like this get shorter, one way or another."

"Sable—"

"Boy." Her voice dropped to almost nothing, and her eyes, over the counter, were the eyes of a woman who had watched her son kneel in a snowy square and had never once looked away from anything since. "Whoever fed that fire knew my floor. Knew my corners. Knew where the oilcloth was. That's not a stranger's knowledge. That's family knowledge." She straightened her shawl. "And I have been somebody's family for a very long time, to a great many people, and it turns out that is exactly the kind of thing that gets you burned."

She left. The bell over the door gave its small, stupid, cheerful ring, and Xanthe stood behind the counter with his heart going like a drum in a procession, and thought about the list, and the shortness of it, and the word *family*, and how it had begun to sound, this winter, less like shelter and more like a door that swings both ways.

That evening, Mei came home late again. She smelled of cold air and, faintly, of lamp oil.

She said she'd been at the widow Chen's, sitting with Sable.

Xanthe said, "That's good," and smiled, and helped her off with her coat.

And then he went to his room and lay in the dark and did not sleep at all, because Auntie Sable's workshop was ash, and the tunnels were gone, and someone in this town had fed the fire—and now, for the first time, Xanthe had begun to make a list of his own, and his sister's name was on it, and he would have burned his own hands off before he let anyone see it.
