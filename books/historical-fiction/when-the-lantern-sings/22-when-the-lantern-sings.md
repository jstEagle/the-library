## 22. When the Lantern Sings

The letter came on a morning that smelled of thawed earth and fresh ink, which Xanthe would later decide was the correct smell for beginnings, even though it had arrived months after the beginning had actually happened.

It was spring proper now—true spring, the kind Anshui hadn't seen in two years—and the river ran high and brown and loud outside the shop windows, carrying sticks and petals and one extremely indignant duck downstream. The ice was a memory. The soldiers were a memory. Captain Ruan was a memory the town preferred not to speak of at all, except Auntie Sable, who said things like "that man burned like wet straw and deserved no better" whenever she'd had plum wine, which was often.

Xanthe was setting type for the shop's first free edition when Mei came down the stairs holding an envelope as if it might hatch.

"It's from Father," she said. "The seal's broken. I couldn't wait. Don't be angry."

"I'm never angry before breakfast," Xanthe said, which was a lie they both let stand.

Their father's handwriting had changed. It was larger now, looser, the strokes of a man who had learned to hold a brush again after his hands had spent a winter forgetting how. He wrote from the downstream town where he'd convalesced through the cold months, in a house belonging to a cousin of a cousin who asked no questions, which seemed to be the national pastime of everyone who had survived the war.

*My children,* the letter began, and Xanthe felt Mei go still beside him, because their father had written *children*, plural, without flinching, and then he wrote:

*I have thought all winter about what to say to you both, and I have decided that fathers should say less and carve more. So I have carved you something. It is enclosed. Do not drop it.*

Something rattled in the envelope's depths. Xanthe turned it over. Out slid a wooden block, hand-carved, wrapped in oilcloth—and inside the oilcloth, a full set of new type sorts, each character cut with a flourish he had never seen before. The radicals curled upward at their tips like flames caught mid-flicker. The strokes tapered the way smoke does when it rises off a lantern.

Mei picked up one sort—a *ming*, bright—and held it to the window light.

"He made us a font," she said. Her voice did something complicated. "He spent his whole recovery carving us a font."

"Not us," Xanthe said quietly. He could see it now, once he looked. Every character in the set carried the same signature: the little flame-tongue at the crown of each stroke. "This is yours, Mei. This is your name-shape. Look—the fire radical. He built the whole alphabet around it."

Mei stared at him. Then at the type. Then out the window, toward Lantern Row, where Auntie Sable was already climbing her ladder with an armful of paper lanterns, because it was nearly festival season again and some traditions refused to die even when empires tried to kill them.

"Well," Mei said finally, in the tone of someone accepting a gift too large to lift. "Then we'd better print something worth lighting."

* * *

They printed the first free edition of the *Anshui Almanac* over three days, and it went like this:

Day one, everything went wrong. The press frame had warped over the winter—it had been used as a garrison office, then briefly as a chicken coop during the occupation's chaotic final week, and the chickens had opinions about typography. Deng, whose shoulder wound had healed into a stiffness he complained about with the dedication of a man who had found his life's true subject, spent the morning planing the frame back true while narrating his grievances.

"In the army we had discipline," he said, shaving a curl of wood. "Structure. A chain of command."

"You deserted twice," Mei reminded him.

"Once," Deng said with dignity. "The second time doesn't count, I was walking toward something instead of away."

"And what were you walking toward?" Xanthe asked, ink to his elbows at the stone.

Deng considered this with the seriousness he brought to all matters, including soup. "Toward a shop where nobody asks what I'm carrying and everybody assumes I can read." He tested the frame, nodded once, and added, "Also the pay is worse here, which proves it's honest work."

Day two, everything went wrong differently. The first pull of the new flame-font came out smeared because Xanthe had mixed the ink too thin—he blamed the humidity, Mei blamed his distraction, and both accusations were accurate. But the second pull came out clean, and when Xanthe lifted the sheet and held it up, the whole room went quiet.

The characters glowed. That was the only word for it. Cut shallow and wide, catching light along their flame-tipped strokes, the page looked less like text than like a street of lanterns seen from a hill at night. Their father had not merely carved letters. He had carved the feeling of being lit from within.

"That old man," Auntie Sable said from the doorway, where she had appeared with the silent suddenness she reserved for moments requiring witnesses. She crossed the shop, took the proof sheet in both hands, and held it a long time. "His son died for printing words like these," she said at last. "And now look. His other children print them in daylight, on the main street, where anyone can read." She sniffed, which from Auntie Sable was the equivalent of weeping openly. "I'm going to hang lanterns over your door so aggressive that travelers will think the whole town is on fire. In a good way."

"There's a bad way?" Deng asked.

"You'll know it if you see it," she said, and left to begin.

Day three, they printed four hundred copies, and the town took every one.

* * *

The almanac itself was modest, deliberately so: planting dates, river levels, market days, a recipe for pickled mustard greens contributed by the widow Chen that would have gotten the previous edition arrested. But tucked inside, on the second page, in the smallest type, was a list. Names. Forty-one names—the hidden families of Anshui, the ones who had spent the war in cellars and tunnels and borrowed identities, printed now in plain sight, in type shaped like flames.

Not a confession. A celebration. The heading read: *Citizens of Anshui, accounted for, accounted with, home.*

Old Man Qiu found his own name and stood in the street holding the page against his chest for so long that Mei had to bring him tea. The ferryman Lin, who had rowed deserters across the frozen shallows at risk of his hands, laughed until he cried reading his name in flame-type. And a boy of perhaps ten, born during the worst winter, pointed at the page and asked his mother, "Is that me? Is that my real name?" and his mother said yes, and the boy read it aloud three times, practicing, like a word in a language he was only now permitted to speak.

Xanthe watched all of this from the shop doorway and understood, with a fullness that sat warm under his ribs, that this was what the ledger had been for—not the imperial one, with its debts and betrayals, but this one. Names were not dangerous because they exposed you. Names were dangerous because they belonged to you, and there were people who wanted to take them. Printing them was just the taking-back.

"You're doing the face again," Mei said, appearing at his shoulder.

"What face?"

"The one where you're having a feeling and pretending it's a headache."

"It *is* a headache," Xanthe said. "A warm one."

* * *

On the evening of the small festival—not the great autumn one, but the spring rite when lanterns are hung for the living rather than the dead—Auntie Sable transformed Lantern Row into something between a street and a constellation.

She had spent two weeks making them, and it showed. Paper lanterns in every color the dye pots allowed, but each one different, each one marked: a fish for the ferryman, a plum branch for widow Chen, a small fierce rooster for Deng, who pretended to be embarrassed and then walked past his lantern eleven times that evening. For the Zhou family she had made three—one shaped like a printing press, absurd and perfect; one shaped like a boat, for their father, far downstream and healing; and one plain white lantern, unmarked, hanging highest of all.

"For whom?" Xanthe asked.

"For whoever needs it next," Sable said. "There's always someone next. That's why I keep making them."

The wind came up the river valley at dusk, as it always did, and the lanterns swung and settled, swung and settled—and then, as the breeze threaded through the bamboo ribs and the paper mouths, they began to hum. Not loudly. A low chord, dozens of voices, rising and falling with the wind like the town itself had been given breath and didn't quite know what to do with it.

"They've never done that before," Mei whispered.

"They're singing," Sable said, entirely unbothered, as though lanterns sang every spring and she simply hadn't mentioned it. "Paper remembers what it's for. You of all people should know that, printer."

Deng stood in the middle of the street with his head tipped back, listening, and for once he had nothing to inventory, nothing to misfile, nothing to flee. "In the army," he said slowly, "we had songs. Marching songs. They were terrible. This is much better."

"You're crying," Mei told him.

"I'm checking my eyes for defects," Deng said. "It's a military habit."

* * *

Later—much later, when the street had emptied and the lanterns still hummed their low patient chord overhead—Xanthe locked up the shop and stood a moment in the dark with the key in his hand.

Through the window he could see the press, cleaned and oiled, the flame-font sorted into its case with each character in its place. On the counter lay tomorrow's work: orders for a school primer, a collection of Sable's late son's letters (she had finally agreed, saying "print them ugly, he wrote them ugly"), and a single sheet already set in type, waiting for morning.

It was a letter to their father, and it began the only way it could:

*Dear Father. Your type arrived safe. We printed with it today, and the whole town read its own names by lantern-light. You asked in your letter what we would do with the shop now that no one is hiding. Here is our answer: everything. We are going to print everything.*

He signed it with both names—the one he had been given and the one he had chosen—and found, as he always did now, that they fit together the way a well-cut joint fits, no gap, no wobble, no lie in the grain.

Outside, the wind shifted, and the lanterns above the door changed key.

Xanthe listened for a moment, smiling, certain that somewhere downstream their father could hear it too.
