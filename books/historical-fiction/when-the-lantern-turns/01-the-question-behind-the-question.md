## 1. The Question Behind the Question

The census taker came on a Tuesday, in the grey hour between the morning's last delivery and the luncheon Miriam Vale never ate.

Astrid saw him first from the kitchen window, where she stood with her hands still wet from washing the carriage lamps. He was a narrow man in a coat the colour of porridge, standing at the iron gate with his hat held politely to his chest and his eyes already working — she could see them working — over the house the way a clerk's finger moves down a ledger. Not looking at the townhouse so much as *counting* it. The windows. The chimneys. The smoke that rose from only one of them, though there were four fireplaces kept ready below stairs.

She dried her hands slowly, giving herself time to arrange her face into the mild blankness she wore like an apron. Then she went to answer the door before Mrs. Quill could shuffle up from the basement and say something unguarded. Mrs. Quill said everything unguarded; it was her one talent.

"Good morning," the man said. He had a voice like dry paper folding. "Mr. Ellery Spatch, assistant enumerator, Borough of Kensington. I've come for the household schedule." He produced a leather folio and opened it against his forearm with the ease of long practice. "Mrs. Vale is listed as head of household?"

"She is."

"And you are?"

"Astrid Crane. I drive for Mrs. Vale." She let the word *drive* do its work. It was a good word, plain and masculine enough to close doors behind it. People heard *coachman* and stopped looking.

"Crane," he repeated, writing it. His pen made a small scratching sound, like a mouse in a wall. "And you reside here?"

"I have the room above the stables."

"Convenient." He did not look up when he said it, which was how she knew the word was not a compliment but a note. "And the rest of the household? The schedule requires every soul who slept under this roof on the thirty-first of March. Every soul, Miss Crane. Parliament is very firm on souls."

"The mistress. Myself. Mrs. Quill, who cooks. And Mr. Fenwick, who sees to the garden and the coal."

"Fenwick." The pen paused. For the first time he raised his eyes fully to hers, and they were pale, patient eyes, the eyes of a man who fishes because he likes waiting more than catching. "That would be the young gentleman? I understand there's a young gentleman in this house."

The wind moved through the plane trees along the street, and Astrid heard it, and heard also the small cold click inside her own chest, like a latch settling into place.

"I couldn't say what gentlemen are in this house," she said. "I sleep above the stables. I'm up at five and abed by nine. The house keeps its own hours."

"Mm." He smiled, and it was a courteous smile, well-made, the sort sold in shops. "Only, I ask because of the photograph."

Astrid did not move. She had learned — from Miriam Vale, though Miriam never knew she was the teacher — that the body betrays you fastest in the half-second after a blow, and that the trick is not to feel nothing but to feel it somewhere no one is watching. So she felt it in her fingertips, in the wet-raw skin around her nails, and kept her face as smooth as a shut door.

"Photograph," she said, making it a question with nothing under it.

"The attic one." He turned a page in his folio, unhurried. "You can see it from the street, you know, if you know to look. Through the gable window. There's a frame on the sill — silver, or something that shines like silver. A young man in it. Dark hair, high collar. Standing beside a motor car, or one of those new machines, anyway." He glanced past her shoulder, into the dim hall with its black-and-white tiled floor and its hat stand wearing Miriam's good coat like a headless sentry. "I mention it only because the schedule asks after boarders, lodgers, servants' kin — anyone resident. And a photograph is a kind of residence, isn't it? In a manner of speaking. People keep photographs of the people they're keeping."

*The people they're keeping.* Astrid turned the phrase over and found it double-edged all the way down.

"That window's been papered over since autumn," she said, which was true. "The glass cracked in the November gales. Mrs. Vale doesn't hold with draughts."

"Then I'll take your word for the window." He wrote something — three words, perhaps four; she counted the scratches — and closed the folio with a soft clap. "I'll want Mrs. Vale's signature, of course. Shall I call again Thursday? Or does the lady prefer to come to the parish office? Some do. It saves me the walk, and it saves the lady the questions."

"I'll tell her Thursday suits."

"Thursday suits," he echoed pleasantly, and put on his hat. At the gate he paused, one hand on the iron, and looked back up at the house — at the third floor, at the small dark square of the gable window, papered now, blind now, but photographed already in whatever careful memory he kept behind those pale eyes. "Good day, Miss Crane. Mind the roads. They say we're in for a hard spring."

He went off down the street without hurrying, folio under his arm, a man with many doors left to knock on and all the patience in the world for knocking.

* * *

Miriam Vale received the news standing at her study window, in the posture she used for bad tidings and dispatches alike: spine straight, hands folded, chin lifted as though the world were a crowd she intended to address.

"Spatch," she said, tasting the name. "Do you know him?"

"No, ma'am."

"Do you know *of* him?" She turned. Miriam was fifty-one and handsome the way a courthouse is handsome, all stone and intention, and her eyes missed less than anyone supposed. "Enumerators are hired clerks, Astrid. They are not hired to notice photographs through gable windows. Someone has spoken to someone."

"Or he noticed on his own. Men like that are paid to count. Counting makes some men curious about what won't be counted."

Miriam considered this, and Astrid watched her consider it, and understood suddenly that she was watching a woman perform deliberation the way other women performed embroidery — the pattern decided long before the needle moved.

"Thursday," Miriam said. "I'll sign his schedule Thursday. You'll bring the carriage round at eleven, and we will be very ordinary together. Can you be ordinary, Astrid?"

"I've been ordinary for six years, ma'am."

Something flickered across Miriam's face then — too quick to name, gone before it landed. "Yes," she said quietly. "So you have." And she returned to her letters as though the morning had contained nothing but weather.

* * *

But Astrid could not leave it, and the house would not let her.

That evening, with Mrs. Quill's radio-less silence rising from the kitchen and the gas lit low in the back stair, Astrid climbed to the attic. She told herself it was to check the papering of the window, that the seal had held, that the draught hadn't loosened a corner. It was even true. But truth is a generous currency; it will buy almost any errand.

The attic smelled of dust and camphor and old rope. Trunks stood along the eaves like sleeping cattle. And on the sill of the gable window, behind the brown paper pasted carefully over the glass, sat the frame.

She had dusted this room. She had cleaned this room, in the ordinary course of her duties, forty times if once. And in forty cleanings she had never once looked properly at the photograph — because it faced the room, not the window, and because a servant who studies her employer's belongings is a servant collecting her own dismissal. She had catalogued it without seeing it: *frame, silver, portrait of a young man.* A fact filed among facts.

Now she took it up and held it to the lamp.

The young man was perhaps twenty. Dark hair swept back from a forehead that seemed always about to frown. High collar, dark jacket, one hand resting on the mudguard of an automobile — one of the new machines, just as the census man had said, all brass and arrogance. He was not smiling. He was looking slightly past the camera, at something or someone just out of frame, with an expression Astrid could only call *impatient to begin.*

She did not know him.

That was the thing that sat down heavily inside her, heavier than fear: she did not know him, and yet the house knew him. The paper over the window knew him. The census man's folio knew him. Somewhere in this townhouse that she had served and studied and believed she understood down to its joists, there was a whole chamber she had never entered, and its door had just been described to her by a stranger with a porridge-coloured coat.

She turned the frame over. The backing was stiff card, sealed with old tape. On the tape, in pencil, faded nearly to nothing, someone had written a single word:

*Harrow.*

Not a name. An address, maybe. Or a warning. Or a key.

Astrid set the frame back exactly where it had stood — angle, dust-shadow, everything — and went down the stairs with the lamp held steady and her heart going like a trotting horse.

Invisible, she had thought herself. Careful, thorough, six years invisible. But invisibility, she understood now, standing in the dark hall with the house breathing around her, was not a wall. It was a curtain. And curtains have cracks, and cracks have light behind them, and tonight a man with a folio had put his eye to hers.

Thursday, Miriam would sign the schedule. Thursday, the questions would come again, softer and worse.

And tomorrow — tomorrow she would fetch the post, as she did every morning, and read what she always read, and deliver what she always delivered.

Unless the post had something to say about Harrow Lane.
