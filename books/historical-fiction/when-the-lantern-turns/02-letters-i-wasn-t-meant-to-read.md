## 2. Letters I Wasn't Meant to Read

The first letter Astrid Crane ever read that was not addressed to her was her mother's.

She had been fourteen, and the letter had arrived three days after the funeral, in a hand she did not recognize — a small, upright script, pressed hard enough into the paper to be read from the back. It sat on the hall table of the house in Bermondsey for two days while relatives came and went and spoke of her mother in the past tense, as though grief were a room one could tidy. On the third evening, alone, Astrid picked it up. She told herself she would only look at the signature. She looked at everything.

*My dear Lena,* it began. *The committee has voted. Your name will go forward with the others. You would have laughed at how long it took them.*

Her mother — quiet, careful Lena Crane, who mended shirts for the mill girls and never raised her voice above the kettle's whistle — had been on a committee. Had been going *forward*, wherever forward was. The letter said other things too, things about subscriptions and safe houses and a woman called Vale who could be trusted absolutely, and by the time Astrid finished reading it her hands were shaking and the candle had burned down two inches without her noticing.

She put the letter back exactly as it had lain, angle and all. Then she sat on the stairs in the dark and understood, with the terrible clarity available only to children, that her mother had been someone else entirely — or rather, had been herself entirely, and had shown Astrid only the hem of it.

She never mentioned the letter. But she began, after that, to read things. Not out of malice. Out of hunger. Her mother had left her a locked door, and Astrid had spent six years finding keys.

* * *

By 1899 the keys had multiplied until they hardly felt like theft anymore.

It began innocently enough, the way all corruptions do. As Miriam Vale's driver, Astrid collected the post each morning from the box on Chancery Row — a duty that had simply fallen to her because Miriam disliked the walk and the clerks at the sorting office had learned her face. She would carry the letters home in a leather satchel, set them on the hall table, and ring for tea. And somewhere between the satchel and the tea tray, a letter or two would come open under her thumb. Old glue, she told herself. Damp weather. A flaw in the manufacture.

Then it was not an accident at all, and she knew it, and she did it anyway.

She had rules, which made her feel better about having none. She read nothing that looked personal — no love letters, no letters from mothers, though there were precious few of either in Miriam's post. She read only what concerned the movement: circulars, minutes, appeals for funds, the occasional furious note from some provincial branch whose treasurer had absconded. She read them standing up, quickly, the way one takes medicine, and she sealed them again with the spirit gum she kept in the carriage box, warmed against the small of her back in winter so it would flow smooth. In four years she had never once been caught. In four years she had learned more about the movement than half its members, who received their information secondhand and were grateful for it.

Astrid told herself this was loyalty. She was not certain she believed it, but the telling helped.

On the morning the letter came, London wore fog like a dirty bandage, and the hansom's lamps did little against it. Astrid drove back from Chancery Row with eleven letters in the satchel and her collar wet, and she went through them in the kitchen while Mrs. Pettibone clattered upstairs with the coal scuttle, because the kitchen was the one room in the townhouse where the light was honest.

Ten of the letters were ordinary. The eleventh was not ordinary in any way she could name at first, which is perhaps why she noticed it at all.

It was addressed to *Mrs. E. Marsh* — one of Miriam's older names, retired now for nearly a year, kept alive apparently in at least one correspondent's address book. The paper was cheap, greyish, the kind sold loose in stationers' shops along the river. There was no return direction. And the handwriting —

Astrid stood very still over the kettle's low hiss.

She knew that handwriting. Of course she knew it; she had grown up inside it. It was the hand that had written *gone to the fair, back late, don't wait up* on the kitchen slate a hundred times. It was the hand that had drawn her a map of the constellations when she was nine, the letters leaning slightly rightward like men walking into wind, the *t*'s crossed with a single impatient stroke that always overshot the stem. Their father had written like a schoolmaster. Tomas wrote like a boy running somewhere.

Tomas had been dead for six years.

Officially. Legally. With a coroner's form and a pauper's grave at Woking and a constable's hat held respectfully to his chest. Dead in the Millbank fire of 1893, one of four souls lost when the factory floor gave way beneath him — that was the story the newspapers printed, the story her father had carried to his own grave two years later like a stone he could not put down. Astrid had been seventeen. She had not wept at the funeral because someone had to sign the forms, and signing forms requires dry eyes.

She opened the letter. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she would have expected them to shake.

The text was ciphered — not heavily, not the work of a professional, but in the simple substitution the movement used for addresses and names, the one Astrid had cracked within a month of starting to read the mail, because whoever devised it had a fondness for patterns and no imagination whatsoever. Most of it was routine: a confirmation of quantities, a date, a warning that a certain premises was being watched. Routine, routine — and then, near the bottom, in plain figures because even the cautious grow careless:

*Tell the boy at Harrow Lane the plates are ready. He'll know what they're for.*

Astrid read it twice. Three times. The kettle went from hissing to shrieking and she did not move to lift it, and when Mrs. Pettibone came down the stairs the older woman took one look at her and said, "Miss Crane, you've gone white as your apron," and Astrid heard the words from a great distance, through water, through six years of earth.

Harrow Lane. The boy at Harrow Lane.

There was no boy at Harrow Lane. There was a man of twenty-four who should have been dead, whose handwriting crossed its *t*'s like a boy running somewhere, and Astrid was holding proof of him in her two hands, in a kitchen that smelled of coal smoke and yesterday's bread, and the world had quietly come off its hinges.

* * *

She resealed the letter with hands that were not quite her own. The spirit gum flowed smooth — she had warmed it against her ribs without thinking, the body remembering its training while the mind was elsewhere — and she laid the envelope on the hall table third from the left, precisely where it would have fallen naturally among the others, and then she went and sat in the carriage in the mews and let the shaking come.

Because here was the thing she could not get past, the thing that turned her cold and then hot and then cold again: if Tomas was alive, then everyone had lied. Not just the coroner and the constables and the newspapers — those lies she could forgive, those were the world's ordinary machinery. But her father. Her father, who had grieved for two years and died of the grieving, who had gone every Sunday to Woking with cut flowers he could ill afford — had he known? Had he knelt at an empty grave knowing it was empty? Or worse, had he believed it truly, and was Tomas's survival a secret kept even from the man who loved him most?

And if her father hadn't known — then who had? Who had arranged a death so complete that even Woking held a grave for it? People did not simply stop being dead. Someone had built the lie, plank by plank, and someone was maintaining it still, and that someone wrote letters in cheap grey paper and called her brother *the boy at Harrow Lane* as casually as one names a delivery.

And Miriam. Miriam, who had taken Astrid in at nineteen, given her the carriage and the rooms above the stable and a wage generous enough to save from, who trusted her with the movement's comings and goings — did Miriam know? Astrid ran backward through four years of overheard conversations, sealed letters, half-glimpsed meetings, looking for Harrow Lane the way one looks for a face in a crowd once it has been described. Nothing surfaced. But then, nothing ever surfaced; that was the whole architecture of Miriam Vale's life. Secrets did not hide in her household. They were furnished, fed, and given respectable aliases.

The fog thickened toward evening. At six Miriam came down the steps wrapped in her grey coat, sharp-eyed and unhurried as ever, and paused at the carriage door with her hand on the frame.

"You're pale, Astrid."

"The fog, ma'am."

"Mm." Miriam looked at her a moment longer than the answer required — Miriam always did, that was her particular genius, she made evasion expensive — and then settled into the seat beside her instead of opposite, which she did only when something was on her mind. "We've a letter coming tomorrow I'll want carried myself. Not read into the book. Directly to me." She said it lightly, arranging her gloves. "You understand."

"Of course, ma'am," Astrid said, and her voice did not betray her, and she hated how good she had become at that.

They rode home through the gaslit murk, past shop windows already dressed for the new century — paper garlands, tin bells, a grocer's window spelling out 1900 in candied fruit — and Astrid watched the crowds stream by, warm and unknowing, and thought: my brother is alive, and he is near enough to have an address, and I am the only person in London who knows both things at once.

That night she waited until the house slept. Then she took from her writing desk the last letter Tomas had ever sent her — posted the week before the fire, full of jokes about his foreman and a girl at the bakery, signed *your loving pest* — and laid it beside the memory of the morning's envelope. The same lean. The same overshooting stroke. Six years, and the hand had not changed; only the paper had grown cheaper, and the name at the top of the page had grown false.

Harrow Lane. She said it aloud once, to the dark, testing whether saying it made it more real or less.

It made it more real.

In the morning she would drive Miriam to her committee, and smile, and hold the reins steady, and be invisible in the way she had trained herself to be. But tomorrow, or the day after — tomorrow, if she could manage it — she would find a reason to take the carriage east, alone, toward the river and the printing districts, toward a street she had never heard of until yesterday and would not forget now if she lived to be a hundred.

Her brother was alive. And Astrid Crane, who had spent six years learning to read what was not meant for her, was finally going to read the one letter that mattered: whatever Tomas Crane had to say for himself.
