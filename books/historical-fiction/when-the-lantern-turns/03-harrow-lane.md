## 3. Harrow Lane

The letter had said *Harrow Lane*, and Harrow Lane was nothing. That was the first thing Astrid understood about it, standing at its mouth on a grey Tuesday with the rain coming down in threads so fine they seemed less like weather than like the city exhaling. It ran off the Mile End Road behind a chandler's shop, a crooked seam of brick no wider than an alley, and if you blinked while passing it — as every person she asked had plainly done for years — you would never know it existed.

*The boy at Harrow Lane does his work by lamplight and asks no questions of the post.* She had copied the sentence out three times before she could make her hand stop shaking, and even now, folded against her heart inside her glove, the transcription felt less like evidence than like a pulse.

Six years. Six years since the constables had come to their mother's door with hats in their hands and the word *deceased* arranged carefully among their condolences, the way undertakers arrange lilies. Six years since Astrid had stood at a graveside in the rain — this same fine, threadlike rain, she thought absurdly, as though the sky had been rehearsing — and watched a coffin go into ground that could not possibly have held her brother, because Tomas Crane had been many things but he had never once been still.

She had told herself, for six years, that grief was simply a room she lived in. You furnished it. You learned where the furniture was in the dark. And then a letter had arrived in Miriam Vale's morning post in handwriting that leaned to the right the way a runner leans into wind, and the room had opened a door, and through the door was an alley called Harrow Lane.

She went in.

* * *

The lane smelled of ink and tallow and wet stone. Laundry hung between the upper windows like signal flags from some defeated nation. Halfway down, wedged between a coffin-maker's and a house that had given up pretending to be anything but a ruin, there was a doorway with a painted board above it: **CRANE & VALE, JOB PRINTERS** — and Astrid stopped so abruptly that a woman behind her walked into her back and swore.

Not Crane. It could not be Crane. Unless —

Unless someone had wanted it to be. Unless someone had chosen the name the way you choose a coat: not for warmth but for meaning, a private joke played at the world's expense, or a message played at hers. Her mother's name and Miriam's, joined by an ampersand, over a print shop in an alley that did not exist. Whoever had painted that board had known exactly whom it would one day stop cold in the street.

The window was fogged from within. Through the blur she could see the amber smudge of a lamp, and shapes: the great grey slab of a press, racks of type, and a figure bent over a composing stick with his sleeves rolled up, setting letters one by one into a line the way a man sets stones into a wall — patiently, precisely, as though the order of the world depended on it.

Tomas had always set the table that way. Fork parallel to knife, glass at the top right, everything squared to the table's edge. Their mother used to say he'd been a printer in another life. Astrid had laughed then. She did not laugh now. Now she stood in the rain outside a window in Harrow Lane and watched a dead man's shoulders move, and understood that she had known those shoulders the way she knew her own hands, and that no false name, no six years, no grave with a stone on it could change what her body knew before her mind consented to.

She pushed open the door.

A bell rang — a small, cheap, tin bell, the kind of detail that would stay with her afterward, that the whole enormous machinery of death and resurrection should announce itself with a penny shop's bell — and the man at the case did not look up.

"Closed," he said. "Orders by post only. There's a card in the—"

"Tomas."

He did not drop the composing stick. That was the strange thing, the thing she would turn over for nights afterward: he finished the letter he was setting, seated it into the line with his thumb, and *then* turned around, as though even now, even at this, the line must be completed before the man could be. And when she saw his face — thinner, bearded, older around the eyes in a way that had nothing to do with age — it was her brother's face wearing six years like a mask he had learned to sleep in.

"Astrid." He said it very quietly, the way you say a word in a language you're afraid you've forgotten. "You shouldn't have come."

"You're dead," she said. "I buried you. I stood in the rain at Abney Park and I was nineteen and I buried my brother, so I think we can dispense with what I shouldn't do."

He set the composing stick down. Carefully. Everything squared away. Then he came around the press and stopped three feet from her, close enough that she could see the scar through his eyebrow — the one from the bicycle, the summer they were children, the one she had forgotten she remembered until this moment — and he raised his hands slightly, palms out, like a man showing he carried nothing.

"I couldn't tell you," he said. "If you knew, you were dangerous. Not to me. To yourself. They question people who love fugitives, Astrid. They question them for hours, and they're good at it, and you—" His voice caught on something. "You've never been able to lie with your face."

"And you have?"

"I've had practice."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the lane a cart passed, wheels grinding the wet cobbles, and neither of them moved until the sound had gone entirely, as though they were two animals who knew that movement is what draws the eye.

"The fire," she said. "At Whitlock's mill. The foreman who died. Tell me you didn't—"

"I didn't."

"Say it properly. Say it looking at me."

He looked at her. His eyes were their mother's eyes, brown going soft at the edges, and they did not waver, and that was the thing that undid her more than the beard or the thinness or the six years — that he could look at her and hold still, this boy who had never once held still, and let her search him.

"I did not set that fire," Tomas said. "I swear it on Mum's grave. On the grave you put me in, if you like. I wasn't even in the city that night — and I know how that sounds, I know exactly how that sounds, everyone says they weren't there — but I didn't set it, Astrid. I was arrested for it because I was convenient. Because I'd organized the mill girls, because I'd written the leaflets, because a man who'd already decided what happened needed someone to have happened it." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as lint. "They were going to hang me. My solicitor told me, privately, that the jury would take eleven minutes. So I ran. And running looked like guilt, and guilt is a coat that fits anyone once they've decided you're wearing it."

"The foreman. Mr. Aldous. He had a wife."

"I know." The words came flat and heavy, a stone laid on a stone. "I've had six years to know it. Whatever happened in that building, a man died, and his wife writes him letters at a dead address, and none of it is mine and all of it is attached to my name. That's what being accused is. It isn't a charge. It's a weather system. It follows you everywhere and it rains on everyone near you." He glanced toward the window, toward the street, and lowered his voice. "Which is why you have to leave. Now. Today. You took a hansom? Walked? Someone saw you come down this lane?"

"No one sees anything on Harrow Lane. That appears to be its entire purpose."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking. I'm standing in a print shop run by a dead man under the names of two women, one of whom pays my wages, and you want me to be solemn about it." But her voice cracked on *wages*, because it landed just then, all of it landing at once: Miriam's careful silences, the letters addressed in that leaning hand, the way Miriam had looked at her sometimes across the breakfast table with an expression Astrid had filed, without examining it, under *pity*. "Does she know? Does Miriam know who I am to you?"

Tomas was quiet a moment too long.

"Tomas."

"She knows what she needs to know," he said. "Ask her the rest yourself, if you must. But not today, and not angrily, and not—" He stepped closer, and now his voice dropped to almost nothing, urgent, the old Tomas urgency, the voice that had talked their mother into letting him keep pigeons on the roof. "Listen to me. The census man came to the house. I heard it from the post. If Dreever has opened a file on that household, then every thread in it gets pulled eventually, and I am a thread, Astrid. One letter, one witness, one clerk who remembers a face from a courtroom sketch — and it isn't just me they take. It's her. It's the whole machine she's built. Do you understand? They wouldn't hang me twice. They'd ruin her instead, and they'd enjoy it."

"Then we run. Tonight. There are boats—"

"And go where? On whose money? With what papers?" He shook his head, and for a moment he looked suddenly, unbearably young, the boy who had cried at their father's funeral and denied it ever after. "There's a plan. There's always a plan; that's what she's for. But it isn't tonight, and it isn't yours to make, and the first part of the plan is that you walk out of here and you don't come back."

"I just found you."

"I know." And his face did break then, briefly, the mask slipping enough to show the thing underneath, which was not courage at all but the same naked relief she felt, the same starving joy, two orphans finding each other across a printed page. "I know. Do you think I haven't imagined this? Every night for six years I've imagined this, and in every version you cry, and I tell you it will be well, and neither of us believes me." He took her hands. His were ink-stained to the second knuckle, black in the creases, the hands of a man who worked by lamplight. "So believe this instead. I didn't kill Aldous. I loved Mum. I love you. And I am going to get out of this alive, because I refuse — do you hear me — I *refuse* to make your grief a true thing after all these years of it being a lie."

Outside, the rain thickened. Down the lane, unhurried, a man in a constable's greatcoat paused at the mouth of the alley, looked along it with the mild, cataloguing attention of a man taking inventory, and did not enter — but did not leave, either. He stood beneath the chandler's awning, sheltering, or waiting, or both.

Tomas saw him over her shoulder. She felt it happen — felt his hands go still around hers, felt the whole warm room of the reunion contract to a single cold point.

"That's him," Tomas breathed. "That's Dreever."
