## 4. What Miriam Knows

Miriam Vale had spent thirty years learning to read a room the way other women read letters — quickly, silently, and without being seen to do it. She had read drawing rooms full of hostile aldermen, church halls thick with hecklers, the parlour of a newspaper editor who smiled at her while his typesetters set her words on fire in print. She could read a room the way a surgeon reads a body: for what was wrong beneath the surface.

So when Astrid Crane came down the stairs on the morning after the census man's visit, Miriam read her at once. The girl moved differently. Her chin was up, her hands were steady as she laid out Miriam's gloves and the day's correspondence, but there was a wire pulled taut somewhere behind her eyes, and Miriam knew — with the sick certainty of a woman who had been lying professionally for three decades — that the census man's questions had landed where he'd wanted them.

He had asked about the photograph. Of course he had. Men like that always went for the photographs; they understood instinctively what the movement took years to teach its own members — that a household is a story, and every story has a figure in it that doesn't fit.

"Mrs. Vale," Astrid said, arranging the letters with unnecessary precision, "the man yesterday. From the census."

"What about him?"

"He asked about a young man. In the attic photograph." Astrid's voice was level. Too level. "I told him I didn't know who it was."

"I heard you. You were very good." Miriam drew on one glove, then the other, buying herself three seconds. Three seconds was often all a lie of omission required, if the person you were deceiving trusted you enough to fill the silence themselves.

But Astrid didn't fill it. She stood there with the letters in her hands like a woman holding evidence, and she said, quietly: "Who is he?"

And there it was. Thirty years of careful architecture, and a girl of twenty-three had found the load-bearing wall in a single afternoon.

Miriam considered — briefly, almost wistfully — the old answer. *A cousin. A former lodger. No one.* She had used all three before, on better interrogators than a loyal chauffeuse with honest eyes. But something stopped her, and it was not sentiment. It was arithmetic. The census man would come back, or his superiors would, and Astrid would be standing between them and whatever Miriam told her to stand behind, armed with nothing but a lie she hadn't chosen. A lie you don't believe in will betray you faster than the truth ever could.

"Sit down, Miss Crane," Miriam said.

Astrid sat. She did not put down the letters.

"Before I answer," Miriam said, "you should understand what you're asking me to do. I am about to hand you something that can be used to hang a man. Once you know it, you carry it. There is no setting it down again. Do you understand that?"

"Yes," Astrid said. And then, because she was her mother's daughter: "Yes, ma'am."

*Her mother's daughter.* That was the whole of it, really. That was why the room tilted slightly when Miriam looked at this girl — not because of any resemblance of face, though there was some, in the stubborn line of the jaw, but because Eleanor Crane had stood in this same parlour twenty years ago with the same terrible honesty in her eyes and said, *Miriam, either we fight for everyone or we are only fighting for ourselves.*

Eleanor had died owing debts. Miriam had spent six years paying one of them.

"The young man in the photograph," Miriam said, "is named Tomas Crane."

She watched the name go into Astrid like a blade going into water — no sound, no splash, just the sudden absence where something solid had been.

"He is your brother," Miriam continued, because there was no kind way to say it and cruelty lay only in delay. "Officially dead these six years. Convicted, in absentia, of the arson at the Halloway Mill that killed foreman Daniel Brice. He has lived in this city under another name since 1895. And he has worked as a printer for this movement for two of them."

The clock on the mantel ticked through four full seconds. Astrid's face did something Miriam had seen perhaps five times in her life: it came apart and reassembled itself in the same breath, grief and fury arriving together like twins.

"You knew," Astrid said. "You've known all along. You let me drive past him. You let me sort his letters—"

"I let you live your life," Miriam said, "which is what your mother asked me to do, and which I have done imperfectly, as one does."

"My mother is *dead*. What could she possibly have—"

"She asked me the winter before she died. We sat where you are sitting." Miriam removed her gloves now, finger by finger. It was time to stop performing composure and start spending it. "Tomas had written to her from hiding. Not to ask for money — he never asked for anything, which was how I knew he was frightened. He wrote to tell her he was alive, so that she would not grieve twice. And Eleanor — God forgive her, she was braver than wise — she wanted to bring him home. To hide him here. In this house. With you upstairs and the constabulary already sniffing at our meetings."

"And you refused."

"I told her the truth, which is that this house is watched, that a fugitive here endangers every woman whose name appears in our ledger, and that the movement cannot afford—" Miriam heard her own phrasing arrive and hated it even as she said it, because she had said it so many times, in so many rooms, about so many people. "The movement cannot afford the cost."

"But you took him anyway. Two years ago."

"Eleanor died in March of '97. Tomas surfaced that autumn, half-starved, working under a false name at a print shop on Harrow Lane. He came to me because I was the last person alive who knew his mother's handwriting well enough to verify the letter he carried." Miriam folded her hands. "He brought one of hers. I would have known it anywhere. She pressed too hard when she crossed her T's, as though the words might otherwise escape her."

"Why keep him secret from *me*?"

"Because you were nineteen and grieving, and because a secret shared is a secret halved, and because—" Miriam paused. This was the part she had rehearsed for two years and still could not make clean. "Because I believed, and still believe, that you could not know and remain safe. Not physically safe. Safe in the way that matters. If you knew, you would look at him. And people who look at a thing too long begin to be seen looking."

Astrid stood. She walked to the window, and Miriam watched her back do the work her face refused — shoulders rising once, hard, and settling.

"The census man," Astrid said finally. "He wasn't fishing. He knew."

"He suspected. There is a difference, and the difference is currently sitting in an office at the constabulary deciding whether suspicion is worth a warrant." Miriam rose and joined her at the window. Below, the street went about its morning: the milk cart, the lamplighter making his rounds early against the shortening days, a boy selling papers shouting something about the mill trial. "His name is Dreever. Constable Dreever. He has visited three times now — you saw the first. He asks small questions. Where does the cook buy fish. How many bedrooms. Whether the attic is storage or lodging. Small questions are how patient men build large cases."

"What does he want? Tomas?"

"He wants leverage." Miriam turned from the window. "Understand what we are, Miss Crane. We are not yet respectable. Half the country thinks us hysterics and the other half thinks us criminals, and a trial — a real trial, with our name attached to a fugitive from a capital crime — would give both halves everything they want. They would not merely convict Tomas. They would convict *us*. Every branch, every chapter, every woman who signed a petition. The vote in two provinces hangs on testimony we are arranging right now, and that testimony dies in a courtroom that smells of arson."

"So we give him up." Astrid said it flatly, without accusation, which was somehow worse. "That's the arithmetic. One boy against the century."

"That is the arithmetic I have been refusing to do for two years," Miriam said, "while telling myself I was doing strategy instead." She heard the crack in her own voice and let it stand; there was no use pretending to a girl who could hear a lie in the pause between two words. "Your mother made me promise two things. That I would protect her children. And that I would never let the movement become the thing we were fighting. I have discovered, in my fifty-eighth year, that these promises cannot both be kept. That is what I know, Miss Crane. That is what I know."

Astrid was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone somewhere Miriam hadn't heard it go before — past fear, past anger, into a flat clear country beyond both.

"Harrow Lane," she said. "The print shop. That's where he is."

Miriam closed her eyes. *There it is,* she thought. *The moment the secret halves itself, exactly as promised.* "Yes."

"Then I'll need the motorcar," Astrid said, reaching for her coat, "and you'll need to decide, Mrs. Vale, whether to tell me to stay away — knowing I won't."

Miriam did not tell her to stay away. She was a strategist, after all, and a liar, and a keeper of promises — and she recognized, watching the girl button her coat with her mother's jaw set like a door against the wind, that the decision had already left the house without her.

But as the motor coughed to life below the window, Miriam sat down at her desk, drew out a sheet of paper, and began — for the first time in two years — to draft the letter she had always known she would one day have to write: the one that decided which promise she intended to keep.

Outside, the first flakes of December snow came down over Harrow Lane, soft as ash.

* * *

She had written three lines and crossed out four when the knock came.

Not the cook's knock, nor the postman's arrhythmic double-tap. Three slow blows, evenly spaced, delivered by a man who wished it known that he was in no hurry and that neither, henceforth, was she.

Miriam opened the door herself. Constable Dreever stood on the step with his hat in his hands and snow melting on his shoulders, and he smiled the smile of a man who has all the time in the world and knows that you know it.

"Mrs. Vale," he said pleasantly. "Forgive the hour. I find myself in the neighbourhood more and more, these days. Curious thing, this street." He glanced past her, down the hall, toward the coat rack where Astrid's scarf had hung an hour before. "Might I ask you a few small questions?"
