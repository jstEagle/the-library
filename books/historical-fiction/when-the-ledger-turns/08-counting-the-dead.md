## 8. Counting the Dead

A census taker learns early that the dead are the easiest people to count. They don't argue about the number of rooms. They don't insist their daughter counts as a separate household because she "keeps her own accounts now." They don't stand in doorways with their arms folded, radiating the particular resentment of a man who believes he is being assessed for taxation when in fact he is being assessed for something far worse. The dead simply are, in whatever column you write them, and they stay written.

I had been counting the dead of Kesselmark for eleven days.

Not all of them, of course. Most of my rounds were the ordinary arithmetic of a provincial town: the Habermans and their nine goats, the widow Oster and her lodger who was definitely her nephew, the cobbler whose workshop had mysteriously gained a second chimney on paper. But the occupation had added a new category to my forms, printed in that heavy bureaucratic gothic that the victors used the way other people used shouting, and the new category was *deceased since last enumeration*, and the space where a cause was to be entered was small, and the ink they supplied was thin, and I was beginning to understand that all three of those facts were deliberate.

Because the dead of Kesselmark had been dying in numbers that did not match the requisitions, and the requisitions could not be adjusted, so the numbers would have to be.

* * *

Let me be precise about the morning it happened, because precision is the only religion I have ever kept, and I want to record my own apostasy accurately. A woman who has spent her life insisting that documents tell the truth owes the truth at least one document, even if the document is only her memory, and even if the truth is that she lied.

It was the fourth of the frost month. The fog off the river had come up the hill streets and settled into Kesselmark like a tenant who pays no rent. I was working the eastern lanes, the ones where the houses lean together over the gutter as if sharing a secret, and my ledger bag was heavy with forms, and my hands were cold, and I had already been lied to four times before noon, which was two below the daily average and therefore a good day.

The fifth house was the Brandt house. I knew it as the Brandt house the way I knew every house in my district, which is to say I knew its tax valuation, its roof material, its number of hearths, and the name of its head of household as it had stood in the census of three years past. Anselm Brandt, cooper. Wife, Greta. Three children, the eldest a boy of fourteen.

I knocked. Nobody answered, which was itself an answer, because in Kesselmark nobody failed to answer a knock from the census office. We had become, in the space of a season, the most feared institution in town, feared above the soldiers, feared above the requisition officers, feared, I suspected, above God, because God at least did not carry forms in triplicate.

I knocked again, and a woman's voice said, "Come in, then, if you're going to," in the tone of someone who has stopped believing that closed doors are a meaningful category, and I went in, and that was the moment my professional life ended, though it took me the better part of an hour to notice.

* * *

Greta Brandt was not the woman I had on my forms. My forms said Greta Brandt, thirty-six, and the woman in the kitchen was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with the swollen knuckles of a laundress. She was sitting very still at the table with her hands folded, the way people sit when they have decided that stillness is the only defense left to them, and behind her, half-hidden by the doorframe of the back room, stood a boy of about fourteen with his mother's jaw and his father's frightened eyes.

Anselm Brandt's eyes. I had seen Anselm Brandt twice in my career, once to enumerate and once to re-enumerate after a fire, and I remembered his eyes because they had been the eyes of a man who counted things for a living too — staves, hoops, barrels — and trusted the count.

The requisition officers had taken him in the autumn. That much I knew from the district lists. Taken with eleven other men for "labor service in support of the occupation," which was the phrase, and which meant, according to what came back down the river with the autumn barges, labor service of the kind from which men did not return to be enumerated again.

The boy in the doorway was supposed to have gone with him. That was the order: men and boys over fourteen. The boy had not gone. The boy had been hidden — in the smokehouse, in the reed beds, in the cellar of the church during the weeks when even the church was not safe — and now the boy was here, in the back room of a house that was on my list as a household of four and was in fact a household of three, unless you counted the dead man, whom the forms still counted, because the forms had not been told he was dead, because the forms had been told something else entirely.

"Your papers say the boy was taken," I said. It was not a question. I am not sure why I said it at all. Perhaps because saying it out loud was the last act of the woman I had been that morning.

"The papers say what they say," Greta Brandt said. Her hands stayed folded. "Papers are good at that."

And there it was. The whole philosophy of the occupation, delivered by a laundress in a kitchen that smelled of cabbage and smoke, in eleven words. *Papers say what they say.* The victors had understood it from the beginning. The requisitions said the town could feed the garrison, therefore the town could feed the garrison, therefore the grain existed, therefore if the grain did not exist it was hidden, therefore the town was lying, therefore more requisitions. The numbers did not describe the town. The numbers *were* the town, now, and the town had to arrange itself around them or be arranged.

I had known this, in the way one knows the water is cold — abstractly, from the edge, from other people's reports. I had even written about it in my reports, in the careful administrative language that is designed to say everything and commit to nothing: *discrepancies noted between requisition figures and observed household capacity.* I had filed my discrepancies and gone home and eaten my supper, and I had told myself that this was integrity, that a census taker's duty was to the record, and the record would speak, someday, when someone was ready to listen, and my job was only to make sure the record was accurate.

But the record was not accurate. I knew that now, standing in Greta Brandt's kitchen. The record had been rewritten before I ever touched it. The dead Anselm Brandt was alive on my forms, because the garrison's labor lists said he had been delivered to the works at Grunwald, and the works at Grunwald had confirmed receipt — I had seen the confirmation, a neat stamped slip, *forty-one souls received, condition satisfactory* — and a dead man cannot be received anywhere, condition satisfactory or otherwise, so either the lists lied or the works lied, and either way the lie had been stamped and filed and was now load-bearing in the architecture of the occupation's paperwork, and my little accurate forms were being fitted into a building whose foundations were fraud.

The boy shifted in the doorway. He had his father's habit of standing like a stave, straight and thin.

"Name," I said, and took out my pen.

Greta Brandt went very still, in a new way. Stillness, I have since learned, has many dialects.

"Ma'am," she said, "whatever you're going to write —"

"I asked the boy's name. The one he uses. Not the one on your papers. The one he uses."

A silence, in which the fog pressed against the window and the cabbage pot ticked as it cooled, and then the boy said, "Joss," in a voice that had not yet decided what it would become.

"Joss," I repeated. "Joss what?"

"Joss Merle," said Greta Brandt, quickly, too quickly, and I understood that Merle was a name she had ready, a name she had been keeping in her mouth for months like a coin under the tongue, a name belonging to some cousin or dead neighbor or nobody at all, a name prepared for exactly this knock at exactly this door.

I wrote it down. I am writing it down again now, and my hand is as steady as it was then, which is the part that frightens me. I wrote: *Joss Merle, age 14, lodger, nephew of householder, no labor status, transferred from district seven.* District seven had burned its records in the autumn, in the confusion of the retreat, so district seven could confirm nothing and contradict nothing, and I knew this, and I used it, and the pen did not shake.

I wrote a boy into existence who had never existed, and I wrote him into existence in the space where the forms demanded a dead man's son, and when I finished I looked at the entry and it was clean. It was accurate. Every figure on the page was internally consistent, cross-referenced, defensible. It was a perfect lie, and it was the truest thing I had written all year.

* * *

You will want to know how it felt. Everyone always wants to know how it felt, as though the feeling were the event, as though the event were merely the occasion for the feeling. I will tell you, since I am telling everything: it felt like nothing. That is the confession underneath the confession. I had expected — the night before, when I lay awake rehearsing the morning, if in fact I had rehearsed it, and I think some part of me had been rehearsing it for weeks without admitting it — I had expected the pen to feel heavy, the paper to feel like a wound, some grand moral sensation, the earth tilting. Instead the nib scratched, the ink took, and I felt the ordinary mild satisfaction of a form properly completed, the same satisfaction I felt writing *nine goats* at the Habermans', and it was only later, walking down the lane with the fog thickening around me, that the enormity arrived, and it arrived not as horror but as a kind of vertigo, a sense that the ground of my whole life had been revealed as paper all along, and that paper, it turns out, can be written on from either side.

Here is what I had believed, and I believed it the way one believes in the load-bearing walls of a house one has never left: that the record was a wall between the town and the state. That as long as the record was true, the town was *somewhere*, was counted, existed, could not simply be erased or invented at the state's convenience. My whole profession rested on that belief. The census was the town's witness. And I had just learned that the witness had been bought, or rather had never been for sale because it had never been on the town's side at all — the record was the state's memory, not the town's, and a state's memory is a very selective organ, and when the state's memory and the town's reality disagree, it is the town that is found to be mistaken.

So if the record was not the town's witness, then it was only a weapon that happened to be shaped like a witness. And a weapon, picked up, can be pointed.

I am making it sound very decisive. It was not. For three days afterward I did nothing but my ordinary rounds, and I told myself the Merle entry had been an aberration, a single lapse, forgivable, even — and this is the phrase I actually used to myself, God help me — *necessary.* I balanced my books. I reconciled my figures. I drank my coffee in the census office and listened to Supervisor Hahn complain about the new forms, the ones requiring us to certify household loyalty alongside household size, and I said nothing, and my silence felt very heavy and very safe, the way a coat feels heavy and safe when you are wearing someone else's.

* * *

Then, on the seventh day, the Oster house.

Widow Oster I have mentioned. The lodger who was definitely her nephew. The lodger was named Pavel Oster, and he was not her nephew, and I had known he was not her nephew for two years, because I had enumerated him under a different name in a different district before the war moved the borders and the borders moved the people, and I had said nothing then either, because the man he had been was not a criminal, he was only a man from the wrong side of a line that had been redrawn, and the census was not supposed to be an instrument of that kind of arithmetic. That had been my line, then. That had been my *record*, my private one, the entries I never made.

The soldiers came for Pavel Oster on the seventh day, at dusk, and they did not come to enumerate.

I was two streets away. I heard it — the boots, the door, the widow's voice going up and up and then cutting off in the middle of a word, which is the worst sound a voice can make. And I stood in the fog with my ledger bag on my shoulder and I did the arithmetic I had been refusing to do for a week: that every entry I had ever written truthfully had been fed into the machine that had just kicked in the Osters' door. My accurate forms were the soldiers' map. My diligence was their intelligence. The record I had served did not protect people from the state. It delivered people *to* the state, neatly, by household, by hearth, by name, in triplicate.

They took Pavel Oster out between two soldiers, and he did not struggle, and I want to tell you that this was dignity but I know better; it was the stillness of a man who has understood the paperwork of his situation. He looked down the street as they put him in the wagon, and the fog was thick, and I do not know whether he saw me. I have decided he did not. I am permitted one falsified entry of my own.

* * *

That night I sat at my table with the district ledger open under the lamp, and I did not light a second candle, because a second candle would have meant ceremony and I could not afford ceremony. I went through the forms the way I go through all forms, which is to say thoroughly, and I found what I was looking for, which was the Reiter family of Mill Lane: mother, two daughters, and a son of seventeen who had been entered in the autumn as *deceased, fever* — entered by me, on the mother's own sworn statement, because the mother had begged me, because the boy had fled the labor levy and a dead boy on paper was a boy the lists would not look for.

A dead boy on paper. I had written it without hesitation, that autumn, and told myself it was mercy, and then I had told myself it was the only such entry I would ever make, and then Pavel Oster had been taken out of his aunt's house between two soldiers, and here I sat.

I turned to the Reiter page. And I understood, finally, with the clarity that only comes at the end of a long refusal, that the question was never whether I would falsify the record. I had been falsifying it for months — by accuracy. Every true figure I entered was a true figure in the service of a false total. The occupation did not need me to lie. It only needed me to be precise. Precision was the treason; the lie would be the loyalty.

So I made the boy alive again. I amended the entry — amendments require a reason, and I gave one: *clerical error, duplicate report, death unverified by parish register* — and the amendment was even true, in its way, because the parish register had burned, and nothing had been verified, and everything in Kesselmark now rested on unverified paper, including the occupation itself, including the annexation proclamation with its seals and ribbons, all of it unverified, all of it paper all the way down.

I wrote the boy back into the world of the countable. Then I sat for a long time with my pen dry in my hand, feeling not guilt — I record, for the archive of my own conscience, that guilt did not come, or has not come yet — but something colder and cleaner, like the first hard frost, which kills the soft things and makes the ground firm enough to walk on.

I had stopped serving the record. I saw that plainly. What I had not yet seen — what I would not see until the audit, until the man Brenner arrived with his smooth voice and his ministry credentials and his questions like fishhooks — was what serving the people in the record would cost, or where it would lead, or that it would lead me, of all places on this earth, to a podium in the town square with the true terms of the peace in my hands and four hundred pairs of eyes waiting on my voice.

But that night, all I knew was this: the ledger had turned. Mine had. And there was no column in any form ever printed for what I was now.

I blew out the lamp. In the dark, I began, for the first time in my professional life, to keep a second set of books — the true one, the one that existed nowhere but in my head, in which Anselm Brandt was dead and Joss Merle was alive and a boy named Reiter walked the reed beds under the fog, and in which the census taker of Kesselmark had finally asked herself the question that would define her: *whose count is this, and who is it counting for?*

The answer arrived the next morning, in the form of a telegram on Supervisor Hahn's desk, marked urgent, from the ministry: an auditor was coming. His name was Brenner. And he was asking, the telegram said, after a missing ledger.
