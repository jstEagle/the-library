## 5. A Knock in the Dark

The winter taught Jude the sound of the map house.

Not the wind — the wind was the same everywhere, worrying at the shutters, finding the loose pane above the drying racks that no amount of putty would seal. Not the river either, though the frozen Vell groaned some nights like a ship changing its mind. What Jude learned was the house's own vocabulary: the tick of the copper boiler cooling after midnight, the sigh of paper absorbing damp and giving it back, the particular creak of the third floorboard on the stairs, which the master had always meant to fix and had instead mapped, once, in a marginal note on a survey of his own hallway — *loose; will attend to it* — a note dated eleven years before he died.

Jude knew all these sounds the way you know your own pulse. Which was why, on the night of the knock, every one of them went silent at once.

It was the twenty-third day of the closed season. Jude had begun counting because the master's ledger had a column for everything else, and someone ought to keep the books now. Twenty-three days since the chains rattled across the bridge mouth and the town settled into itself like an animal into snow. Twenty-three days of working by lamplight until the fingers stiffened, cataloguing a dead man's hand, copying plates under the eye of Sergeant Brandt, who came each morning at nine with her boots ringing on the cobbles and her questions wrapped in courtesy so thick it might have been armor.

That night there was no moon worth mentioning. The lamp burned low. Jude was tracing the master's contour lines along the disputed reach — the stretch of valley both nations called theirs, drawn twice in the atlas, once as the ministry wanted it and once, faintly, in pencil beneath, as the theodolite had found it — when the scratching started.

At first Jude took it for a branch. There were no branches near the loading door; the master had them cut years ago, saying sightlines were a cartographer's first duty. Then it came again: not wood on wood but something smaller, duller, patient. Fingernails. Or the thing fingernails become after a long time in the cold.

Jude sat very still. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself. Down in the town a dog began to bark and thought better of it.

Three more scratches. Then — barely a voice at all, flattened by the door, by the wind, by whatever distance a person crosses between being someone and being a rumor —

"Jude."

One word. But Jude had spent two years learning to read handwriting, and a voice is only handwriting made of air. He knew it the way he knew the third floorboard.

He crossed the studio without deciding to. Later he would try to reconstruct the decision and find there wasn't one — just his body moving ahead of his mind the way water moves ahead of ice, filling the shape of something before the cold can claim it. The loading door's bar was oak, cut for a siege. It lifted heavier than memory said it should.

Cold poured in like a second person.

Tomas was folded against the doorframe as if the frame were holding him up out of charity. Frost had built a gray rime along the collar of his coat and in his eyebrows, so that he seemed already half monument, half boy. His lips were the color of the slate Jude mixed ink on. One hand was pressed flat against the wood where he'd been scratching, and the fingers did not look like they would ever scratch anything again.

"You're supposed to be across the river," Jude heard himself say. Stupid words. The truest ones available.

"The river is the road," Tomas said. "That's the whole problem with rivers."

Then his knees went, and Jude caught him — caught all of him, the coat crusted stiff, the body underneath burning cold in that wrong way cold bodies burn — and hauled him inside, and put the bar back down, and stood in the dark listening to his own heart while the house resumed its sounds around them, one by one, as if deciding nothing had happened.

* * *

Getting the frost out of a man is slower work than getting it out of a coat. Jude learned this in the loft above the map drawers, where the master used to store vellum because the heat rose there gentle and even. He stripped Tomas's boots — the left one had to be cut, the leather frozen to the skin beneath — and wrapped him in the surveyor's blankets that smelled of lanolin and lamp oil and, underneath it all, of the master's tobacco, which was gone from the world now except in wool.

Tomas shook for an hour. Not shivering exactly; something deeper, tectonic, the body renegotiating its terms. Jude held the tin cup to his mouth and watched him drink broth Jude had boiled from the bones of a chicken bought, days earlier, with flour money — flour that Sergeant Brandt had already noticed came in quantities too large for one apprentice, and had said nothing about, and the remembering of this made Jude's hands go unsteady on the cup.

"You're going to tell me," Jude said, when the shaking had thinned to a tremor, "that you walked across the ice."

"I waited for the hard freeze. Three nights below ten degrees." Tomas's voice was wrecked, sanded down to gravel. "There's a channel near the far pilings that doesn't close. I found it with my father's auger. You test as you go. You drill, and if the core comes up thin, you go around." He almost smiled. "He taught me that when I was nine. He said ice is just a map that redraws itself nightly."

"And your father?"

The word *father* landed on Tomas like weather. He looked down into the cup.

"They came at night. Two weeks ago. Not soldiers — clerks, mostly, and one officer who kept apologizing. That was the worst part. He apologized the whole time they were going through the cabinets." Tomas's hands tightened around the tin. "They took the correspondence. Every letter your master ever sent him. Forty years of letters between two men who drew the same valley from opposite banks, and now every one of them is evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

"Of friendship," Tomas said. "Which in wartime is the same crime as treason. They arrested him as an enemy agent. An *agent*, Jude. The man hasn't crossed the river in six years. The last time we were all in one room was your master's fiftieth birthday, and they argued about projection methods until dawn and then embraced on the doorstep like brothers." He stopped. Breathed. "They let me go. That's how little I mattered. And then the notices went up — family of detained persons are to remain within the district pending review — and I understood that *pending review* is a place you can live your whole life inside of, if they let you live it."

"So you ran."

"So I ran toward the only border I trust." He looked up then, and his eyes were fever-bright and utterly level. "You said it yourself. At the funeral. Under the larches, when everyone else had gone to the wine."

Jude remembered. Of course Jude remembered; he had been trying not to remember it for twenty-three days, because promises made over graves have a weight that ordinary promises don't, being load-bearing.

"If you ever need—" Jude had said, and hadn't finished, because finishing promises at funerals is bad luck, and Tomas had gripped his wrist through both their gloves and said:

"—the door opens. Any door. The loading door. I'll know the way in. Your master showed me once, years ago, when our fathers were still speaking. He said a map house should have one entrance that isn't on any map."

"He said that?" Jude asked now, from the loft floor.

"He said a great many things to me that winter. I think he knew what was coming. I think he'd known for years." Tomas set the cup down with the care of a man handling an instrument. "Jude. There's something else. Something I crossed the ice to tell you, and I'd have told you even if you'd turned me away at the door."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the boiler ticked its slow midnight syllable.

"The garrison didn't arrest my father for the letters alone. The officer — the apologizing one — he read from a list. Survey discrepancies. Dates. Coordinates. Your master's field notes against the official record, matched line by line. Someone gave them the comparison." Tomas's voice dropped. "Someone here. On this side. Because those notes aren't published. They're not anywhere except—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. Jude looked past him, down through the loft's open rail, at the long table where the atlas lay open under its weights, at the dead man's hand still moving across it in ink and pencil, at the sealed letter in the drawer marked *for the one who finishes this* — the letter Jude had carried for three weeks like a stone in the pocket of every coat, unable to open it, unable to leave it.

"—except here," Jude said.

"Except here," Tomas agreed softly. "And they'll come for it. Maybe not tonight. Maybe not this month. But Major Voss requisitioned this building for a reason, and Sergeant Brandt walks past your flour sacks every morning counting them, and sooner or later one of them is going to open that drawer." He reached out — slowly, the way you reach toward a bird — and closed his hand around Jude's wrist, the same grip, the same spot, gloveless now, warm at last. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry to bring the war across the ice with me. But you promised me a door, and I'm asking you now for the other thing you promised. Finish it. Finish it before they do."

Outside, the wind turned, and the river groaned beneath its lid of ice, redrawing its map in the dark.

* * *

Jude did not sleep. Tomas slept the way the drowned are said to sleep, absolutely, alarmingly, and Jude sat cross-legged beside him in the lamplight watching the frost melt out of his eyebrows and thinking about doors.

Because here was the arithmetic Jude could not stop running: a fugitive behind the bar of the loading door. Flour for two. Boot prints in the loft dust. A sergeant with kind eyes and a soldier's patience arriving at nine o'clock sharp. And beneath it all, the drawer, the letter, the pencil lines under the ink lines — the truth lying under the lie the way the surveyed border lay under the official one, waiting for someone with the nerve to lift the top sheet.

History, Jude had believed all winter, happened elsewhere. In capitals. In conference rooms where men moved borders with rulers and never felt the cold of the ground they were dividing. But history had scratched at the loading door tonight, half-frozen, calling Jude's name in a voice like handwriting, and now it was asleep in the master's blankets breathing the master's air, and elsewhere was here.

Near dawn, Tomas stirred and said, without opening his eyes, "You should open the letter."

"I know."

"It's addressed to you. Whoever finishes it. That's you now."

"I know," Jude said again, and reached for the drawer, and the letter lay there with its wax seal unbroken, and upstairs, faint and punctual as conscience, came the sound of boots on cobbles — early, too early, an hour before nine — stopping at the loading door.

Sergeant Brandt knocked. Twice. Politely.

And she had never, in twenty-three mornings, come to the loading door before.
