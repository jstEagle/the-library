## 7. Sergeant Brandt's Rounds

The orders came on a Tuesday, typed on a card the size of a ration slip: *Sergeant I. Brandt to conduct weekly inspection of the map house, Okafor premises, report irregularities to Major Voss directly.*

Irregularities. Ilsa read the word twice and almost laughed. She had spent four years in a uniform where *irregularities* meant a village that stopped answering the telephone, meant a barn that was warm when it shouldn't be, meant the smell of lime under fresh snow. She knew what the word was for. It was for finding things out without having to say what you were looking for.

The map house sat at the end of Cooper's Lane like a held breath. Two stories, stone on the ground floor, timber above, its loading door big enough for a hay wagon and its front door painted a green that had once been fashionable. The sign above the bell pull still read *HALLORAN & OKAFOR, SURVEYORS AND ENGRAVERS* in gold leaf gone dull, and beneath it, smaller, the master's name alone: *A. Halloran.* Someone would have to decide whether to paint over it. Nobody had.

She knocked the way the manual said to knock — three, firm, spaced — and heard the small domestic sounds of someone putting a life away before answering. A drawer shut. A chair pushed back. Footsteps that tried to be unhurried and weren't.

Jude Okafor opened the door in a smock spotted with ink, and Ilsa watched the boy's face do the thing faces did: the half-second calculation of *how much trouble am I in* smoothed over by *I am a citizen going about my lawful work.* He was what, nineteen? Twenty? Old enough that the war had taken his classmates and young enough that he still stood like someone waiting to be told the joke.

"Sergeant." He stepped back to let her in, because the orders entitled her to entry and they both knew it, and courtesy was cheaper than resistance.

"Apprentice Okafor." She stamped the snow off her boots on the mat, thoroughly, because she had learned that thoroughness in small things made people relax about large ones. "Weekly inspection. Nothing personal."

"Is anything, these days?"

She looked at him. He had the grace to look briefly ashamed. "No," she said. "Nothing is."

* * *

The ground floor was the workshop, and it smelled the way Ilsa imagined churches would smell if churches were honest: linseed, paper, stone dust, and under it all the sharp mineral bite of inks mixed by hand. Long tables under north-facing windows. A wall of shallow drawers, each labeled in a hand so fine it looked typeset. A press. A drying rack hung with sheets like laundry, like flags of a country that didn't exist yet.

Jude trailed her through it at a polite distance, hands folded, the picture of cooperation. She let him trail. She had done inspections of bakeries, of the tannery, of the Widow Marsh's boarding house, and in every one the inspected person trailed her the same way, and in every one the thing worth finding was in the place they walked her *past* rather than the place they walked her *to.*

So she went where he didn't point.

The master's desk sat in the corner, under a dust sheet. She lifted a corner of the sheet without asking. Jude's breath did something small.

"Requisitioned materials," she said. "I'm entitled."

"I know." His voice was flat and careful. "It's just — that was his chair. Nobody's sat in it."

"I'm not going to sit in it." She lifted the sheet the rest of the way off, folded it, set it on the desk's corner. The desk itself was oak, scarred, its surface a palimpsest of compass punctures and knife-cuts where a blade had been drawn across paper a thousand times. An inkwell, capped. A magnifier on a folding stand. A letter tray with three items in it, and she read the tray the way she'd learned to read a room: the top item was a supply invoice, the middle item a blank envelope, and the bottom item — the bottom item was a letter addressed in a hand she didn't know, and the seal was unbroken, and the paper had yellowed at the edges the way paper does when it has been handled and put down and handled again, many times, over years.

She didn't touch it. That was the discipline. You didn't touch the thing until you'd decided what touching it would cost.

"His effects," Jude said. "I'm cataloguing everything. For the garrison inventory. You'll get a copy."

"I'm sure I will." She set the dust sheet back. "How is the atlas coming?"

"Slowly." Honest, at least. "The master did the surveys. I'm doing the fair copies. It's different work. He drew like he was arguing with the paper. I draw like I'm apologizing to it."

Despite herself — and she noted the despite, filed it — Ilsa almost smiled. "That's not nothing, knowing the difference."

"My master used to say a map is a confession." Jude said it lightly, but his eyes went to the desk, to the sealed letter, and came back. "That every line admits what the drawer wanted."

"Did he say that before or after the war?"

The question landed harder than she'd intended. She watched it land. Jude's face went through something quick and private. "Before," he said. "He said everything before the war. He died before he could say anything after."

* * *

She worked the upper floor next, because the manual said ground to top and because the loft above the map drawers was the kind of space her eye went to the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. Narrow stair. Low ceiling. A cot that had belonged to the master's late clerk, unmade but not slept-in — she pressed her palm flat to the blanket and it was cold, room-cold, no warmth held in it. A washstand. A jug. A window overlooking the lane, its latch stiff with fresh oil.

Fresh oil. She stood very still and looked at the latch. Someone had oiled it recently, and Jude Okafor did not strike her as a boy who oiled latches on windows he never opened. She looked at the sill. Dust, fine and grey, undisturbed in a band along the frame — but below the window, on the floorboards, the dust was thinner in a patch roughly the size of a man standing, looking out.

Or a man standing, watching the lane, waiting to see if the coast was clear.

She said nothing. She wrote nothing. She stood in the cold loft for a full minute, long enough that Jude's footsteps on the stair below went quiet with the particular silence of someone listening, and then she came down and said, "Roof looks sound," and watched him decide whether to believe her.

* * *

It was on the way out that she saw the flour.

The provisions ledger was kept at the garrison commissary, and Ilsa had made a habit — a professional habit, she told herself, though she no longer entirely believed the telling — of reading the town's small accounts the way other sergeants read dispatches. Flour, salt, lamp oil, coal: the ledger of a town under occupation was a confession in its own right, and it told you things no informer could. Who had money. Who had none. Who was feeding more mouths than they admitted to.

Jude Okafor bought flour every ten days. Half a sack. That was correct for one person, generous even, a boy doing heavy work who ate the way the young eat. But he also bought, every ten days, at the same visit, a second half-sack — and he had begun, three weeks ago, buying dried peas, a twist of tea, and a tin of rendered fat.

One person did not eat like that. One person ate flour and whatever the town doled out. Two people ate peas and tea and fat, because two people eating hidden in a house ate carefully, ate things that kept, ate things that didn't smell.

She stood in the commissary doorway with the ledger in her hand and the snow coming down outside, and she did the arithmetic she did not want to do.

The loft window, oiled. The patch of thin dust below it. The second half-sack of flour.

Someone was in the map house.

* * *

That night she sat in her billet — a requisitioned room in the customs house, a bed, a chair, a stove that ate coal and gave back half of it as smoke — and cleaned her rifle she did not need to clean, and thought about her brother.

Lukas had died in the second autumn of the war, holding a stretch of trench outside a town whose name had since been given to the enemy and then taken back and given away again, so that when the casualty list reached her, the place he died no longer officially existed. That was the thing she had never managed to explain to anyone who hadn't served. Not that he died. That the *where* was unmade. That men were fed into a line on a map — a line drawn in an office in the capital by men who had never been cold — and the line was treated as a fact of nature, like a river, like a ridge, and her brother was a rounding error in it.

*The line is drawn first,* she had thought, standing at the mass grave in the rain with a folded flag that wasn't a flag, it was just a flag, they'd run out of the right kind. *The line is drawn first, and then the boys are sent to become it.*

And here she was, in a town whose whole fate was sitting in a dead man's drawers. A border. Ink on paper. Major Voss wanted it the way a man wants a loaded weapon he's been told isn't his — with a hunger he dressed up as duty. She had seen Voss review the atlas commission, had stood at attention in his office while he turned the master's survey reports over in his long fingers, and she had watched his face do the thing faces did: the half-second calculation of *how much trouble am I in* smoothed over by *I am an officer going about my lawful work.*

The same face as the boy's. That was the joke of it. The same face. One of them was nineteen and ink-spotted and afraid, and one of them was forty-five and decorated and afraid, and both of them were going about their lawful work, and somewhere between them was a valley full of people who would learn, when the armistice delegation crossed the bridge in spring, exactly what their homes were worth on paper.

She racked the rifle. She did not write her report.

* * *

The first inspection, she wrote: *Premises secure. Apprentice cooperative. Inventory in progress. No irregularities.* She signed it, dated it, and walked it to Voss's office herself, and stood while he read it, and watched him look up at her with the flat patience of a man who has decided to believe his own instruments.

"No irregularities," he said.

"No, Major."

"The master's papers. The letter tray on his desk. What was in it?"

So he had been in the studio too, before her, or he had eyes that had been. She kept her face at parade rest. "A supply invoice. A blank envelope. A sealed letter, unbroken, addressed in a hand I didn't recognize."

"Addressed to whom?"

"It didn't say. Just a seal."

Voss turned his pen twice between his fingers. "If it's opened, I'm to know first. If it's copied, I'm to have the copy. The atlas is a matter of state now, Sergeant. The boy is a custodian, not an owner. Make sure he understands the difference."

"He understands, sir."

"Does he." The pen stopped turning. "You'll understand something too, Brandt. I've read your file. Your brother was in the — " he named the regiment, named the action, named the ground that no longer existed — "and I'm sorry for it. It's why I chose you for this posting. You know what lines cost. You won't sentimentalize the people on the wrong side of one."

*No, Major,* she said, which was not yes, which was not no, which was the word she had learned to make her whole body.

* * *

The second inspection was a Thursday, snowing hard, the lane drifted to the shins. Jude met her at the door with tea — two cups, and she noted that, noted the second cup set out and then, at the sight of her, not poured — and she made her rounds with the methodical slowness of a woman who had decided, somewhere between the commissary ledger and Voss's pen, that she was going to look as hard as she could and see exactly as much as she chose to see.

The loft again. The window latch. The thin patch of dust — thinner now, or was she imagining it? A thread caught on a nail by the cot: coarse wool, undyed, the kind of yarn that went into workmen's socks on the far side of the river. She held it to the light for a long moment. Then she let it fall back into the dust and pressed her boot sole over it, gently, the way you'd press soil over a seed.

Downstairs, Jude was waiting with the inventory pages, and his hands were steady, and his eyes were not.

"You've been eating well," she said, and watched him go white and then careful. "The peas. The tea. Good for the concentration, copying work. My mother swore by it."

"I — yes, Sergeant. Eating properly. As ordered."

"As ordered." She took the inventory pages, squared them on the table, and looked at him across the master's empty chair, and for a moment — one moment, unprofessional, unforgivable — she wanted to say it plainly. *I know. Whoever it is, I know. And I have not said. And you should understand what that is costing me, and you should understand that I am not doing it for you.* She wanted to tell him about Lukas. She wanted to tell him that she had stood at a grave for a line on a map and that she would not easily help dig another.

But she had seen what happened to sergeants who said things plainly. They were thanked, transferred, and replaced by someone who said nothing.

"Next inspection, I'll want to see the fair-copy pages," she said instead. "The border sections. The Major has questions about the master's survey methods, and I'd rather he asked me than you."

"Of course." Jude's relief was so naked it was almost worse than fear. Almost.

She buttoned her coat. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and said — lightly, the way you'd remark on the weather — "The latch on your loft window sticks. In weather like this, a stiff latch will freeze solid. You'd want it working smoothly. If you ever needed to open it in a hurry."

She did not look back to see his face. She walked out into the snow, and the green door closed behind her, and the town went on holding its breath.

* * *

That evening Voss sent for her. The major's office was warm in the way garrison offices were warm — coal without limit, the occupied town cold so the occupiers need not be — and he stood at the window with his hands behind his back, watching the river, watching the bridge, watching the ice that would hold until the engineers said otherwise.

"Brandt." He didn't turn. "The delegation comes when the bridge opens. You understand what's at stake in that house."

"I understand, sir."

"Then you'll understand this too." Now he turned, and his face was the patient face, the lawful-work face, and it frightened her more than anger would have. "If anything leaves that building that shouldn't — a page, a plate, a person — I will not be asking the town where it went. I'll be asking you. And you should know that I have already written the report I'll file if your answer disappoints me. It's in my desk. It's dated. All it needs is your name in the wrong place."

*Yes, Major,* she said.

Walking back through the frozen streets, past the shuttered shops and the snow-buried lampposts, past the bridge chains locked for the season, Ilsa Brandt did the only arithmetic left to her. Two half-sacks of flour. One sealed letter. One boy eating for two. One report already written, waiting, like a line drawn in advance across ground where people were still living.

She had held a line once, in the way that soldiers hold things — with their bodies, briefly, badly. She had buried her brother under a line. And now she was walking a round she had chosen to walk blind, and every week the blindfold cost more, and spring was coming, and the ice would not hold forever.

In the loft of the map house, she was almost certain, someone was listening to boots on the lane and learning to tell hers apart from all the others.

She found she was counting on it.
