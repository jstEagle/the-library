## 13. Arrest at the Mill

The cough started on a Tuesday, small and dry, like paper being folded.

By Friday it had teeth.

Tomas heard it through the floorboards of the loft, where he'd spent six weeks learning the geography of nine square meters — which board creaked near the window casement, which nail head held warmth from the stove flue below, how many steps Jude took between drafting table and ink cabinet (eleven, always eleven, the man measured his own grief in paces). He knew the map house better blind than his father had known it with both eyes open. And now he lay in the dark listening to that cough climb out of Jude's chest and rattle around the studio like a loose compass needle.

"You're sick," Tomas said, when Jude climbed up with the supper tray and set it down wrong — an inch too far left, no joke about the geometry of soup.

"I'm fine. It's the cold."

"It's the cold *in your lungs*, which is what sickness is." Tomas pressed the back of his hand to Jude's forehead, a gesture that had become, over the weeks, almost casual — almost — though neither of them had ever quite managed to make it casual enough. "You're burning."

"It's a stove room. I work over a stove."

"Jude."

Jude sat back on his heels. In the candlelight his face had the gray undertone of old vellum, and there was a shine of sweat at his hairline that had nothing to do with heat. "It's nothing. A few days' rest and—"

"And you'll rest when?" Tomas gestured at the room below them, visible through the open trapdoor: the drafting table buried under the garrison's copy, the true plates wrapped in oilcloth under the loose floorboard, Major Voss's requisition notice still pinned to the door like a threat nailed up in a frontier town. "You haven't rested since the solstice. You cough all night. Last week you drew the Vistula tributary upside down and blamed the master's notes."

"That was the master's notes."

"They were *fine*. I checked them against my father's copies before you were even awake."

Something moved across Jude's face — the old argument, the one they kept having in smaller and smaller pieces: whose maps, whose fathers, whose fault the war had been. But it passed, because Jude was too tired to hold it, and instead he just looked at Tomas with an expression that made the loft feel suddenly too small and too warm.

"There's a chemist," Jude said. "On Mill Street. Old Ferenc. He doesn't ask questions. He sold my master laudanum for his hands for eleven years and never once wrote it down."

"No."

"The fever could turn. It turned for the master, in '17, and he was stubborn about it and lost a whole winter of surveying."

"*No.*"

"Tomas—"

"You want me to walk out that door." Tomas heard his own voice go flat and hard, the voice of arguments won in schoolyards a lifetime ago. "Down your stairs, past your sergeant's rounds, out into a town where every soldier between here and the bridge has seen my face on a warrant. For *cough syrup*."

"For medicine. And because if this fever turns and I can't work, Voss comes up those stairs himself, and then we're both finished, and so is everything under that floorboard." Jude's hand found his wrist in the dark. His fingers were hot. "I'm not asking you to be brave. I'm asking you to be quick."

They argued until the candle burned down to its collar. That was their way — they had never once resolved anything by agreement, only by exhaustion, and whoever yielded last did it silently, as a gift rather than a defeat. It was nearly midnight when Tomas finally said, "Day after tomorrow. Market day. The streets will be full, and Brandt does her long route on market days — she walks the river wall first, I've watched her from the window for a month."

Jude stared at him. "You've been watching her routes."

"I've been watching *everything*," Tomas said. "It's what I'm for. My father used to say a cartographer is just someone who watches so carefully it starts to feel like a sin."

He regretted mentioning his father the moment he said it. He mentioned him more lately, not because it hurt less but because silence had begun to feel like a kind of lying. Jude reached over and blew out the candle, and in the dark his hand found Tomas's again and stayed.

"Day after tomorrow," Jude said. "Market day. In and out in twenty minutes. Ferenc's shop has a back door onto the tannery yard."

"I know. I've drawn it."

Of course he had. In the loft, by matchlight, over six frozen weeks, Tomas had drawn the whole quarter from memory — every alley, every courtyard, the millrace, the tannery, the three ways in and out of Ferenc's street — on the back of a spoiled proof sheet Jude had smuggled up to him. It hung behind the flour sacks now, a secret map of a town that had become, without either of them deciding it, a home worth escaping from and never leaving, both at once.

"Twenty minutes," Jude said.

"Twenty minutes," Tomas agreed, and neither of them slept much after that.

* * *

Market day came up white and loud.

Snow had fallen overnight — a fat, windless snow that muffled the town and gave everyone an excuse to move slowly, and the square was thick with sledges and shouting fishwives and soldiers who were colder and more bored than vigilant. Tomas went down the outside stair at half past ten, dressed in Jude's spare coat, which smelled of ink and lamp oil and, underneath that, of Jude himself, a smell Tomas had decided privately he would know anywhere in the world, in any crowd, at any distance, forever, which was either love or a liability and probably both.

The plan was simple because simple plans survive. Twenty minutes. Cap low. Walk like a man going somewhere dull.

Mill Street ran downhill toward the race, and the noise of the water covered everything — the wheel turning slow and heavy under its casing of ice, the saws stopped for the holiday week, the whole street smelling of wet stone and cut lumber and, faintly, the tannery's sharp reek. Ferenc's shop sat in the middle of the row, its window full of brown bottles and a stuffed owl that had been dying by degrees since Tomas's childhood.

Tomas counted to one hundred in the doorway of the cooper's shed across the street, watching. An old woman went in. A boy with a sledge went out. No uniform in sight. The millrace churned green-black under its ice shelves.

He crossed.

The bell over Ferenc's door rang the same cracked note it had rung when Tomas was nine years old and his father had sent him in for camphor. Ferenc himself looked up from his ledger, and for one long second the old man's eyes went from Tomas's face to the door to the window and back, and Tomas watched seventy years of discretion weigh itself against a warrant.

"You're Havel's boy," Ferenc said quietly. Not a question.

"My father is a prisoner," Tomas said. "And the man I live with is dying of a cough. I need something for the lungs — moss, honey, whatever you have. Quickly."

Ferenc looked at him a moment longer. Then he turned to his shelves without a word, and his hands, moving among the bottles, shook only a little.

It was going perfectly. That was the thing Tomas would remember afterward, the thing that would keep him up nights for years: it was going *perfectly*. Ferenc had weighed the moss and was wrapping it in waxed paper; the bell hadn't rung; the street outside was empty except for a dray horse steaming at the rail. Ninety seconds left of his twenty minutes, and he was already composing the report he'd give Jude, already seeing the loft, the candle, the hand on his wrist—

The bell rang.

Two soldiers came in arguing about cards, shaking snow off their shoulders, and one of them — young, red-faced, his collar open like the war was a pub crawl — stopped dead.

Tomas knew him. That was the horror of it. Not a stranger, not a patrol passing through: a boy from the garrison's winter mess, someone who had stood in line behind him at the bakery in September, in another life, when Tomas's father still had a shop and a name and the border was just lines on paper. The soldier's mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"Havel," he said. "You're Havel's son."

The world went very simple and very fast.

Tomas moved before thought caught up — shoulder through the space between the counter and the shelf, bottles going over behind him, Ferenc shouting something, the waxed paper packet clutched against his chest like a heartbeat. Through the back door, into the tannery yard, over the low wall where the lime pits steamed, boots skidding on frozen hides hung out like flags of some terrible nation. Behind him the shop door banged and boots hit the yard, and a whistle went up — two short, one long, the garrison's alarm, a sound he'd learned to dread from the loft window without ever expecting to hear it aimed at him.

He ran the way he had drawn it.

That was the only reason he lived through the next four minutes: that for six weeks he had traced these streets in matchlight until they lived in his hands. Left at the lime pits, where the smell alone would stop a pursuer raised on clean barracks. Down the service stair behind the cooperage, taking the steps three at a time while the packet crinkled against his ribs. Along the millrace, on the narrow maintenance ledge where the ice grew teeth and a man had to run bent double under the wheel housing — and here he heard them behind him, two sets of boots, the young soldier's voice cracking with excitement: *"He went the water side! Cut him off at the weir!"*

So Tomas did the thing that was not in any drawing.

At the weir, where the race narrowed to a wooden sluice gate furred with ice, he went over the rail and dropped — four feet, five — onto the ice shelf below, felt it hold, felt it groan, and ran out onto the frozen channel itself, boots punching through the powder crust into black glass beneath, arms out, the whole frozen river sliding and shifting under him with a sound like a struck bell. The soldiers stopped at the rail. They were sensible men. Sensible men do not chase fugitives onto river ice in front of witnesses.

He came off the ice at the boathouse steps, soaked to the knee, lungs full of knives, and went the last hundred meters through the tannery's drainage tunnel — drawn it, drawn it, thank God he'd drawn it — and came up through the coal chute into the map house cellar at 10:52, twelve minutes late, bleeding from one shin, half-frozen, holding a paper packet of lungwort moss like a man carrying fire out of a burning city.

Jude was waiting at the bottom of the ladder. He didn't shout. He pulled Tomas up the last rungs with both hands, checked him over with the brisk terror of a man counting wounds, and then simply held on, both of them shaking, in the dark that smelled of coal and ink and the iron breath of the river.

"You're late," Jude said finally, into his hair.

"Twelve minutes."

"I counted."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the sound Tomas had been dreading since the weir: boots. Many boots. Moving through the quarter in the organized, patient pattern of a search that has all the time in the world, because it knows exactly what it's looking for and roughly where.

* * *

Major Voss arrived at the map house at four o'clock, which told Jude everything: the major had not come running. He had let the machine work. He had spent the afternoon confirming, cross-referencing, assembling — a cartographer of men, building his case the way the master built a survey, layer by layer, until the picture was undeniable.

He stood in the studio doorway with snow melting on his greatcoat and Sergeant Brandt a pace behind him, and he did not look at the drafting table or the atlas or the floorboards. He looked at Jude. Only at Jude.

"A fugitive was seen in Mill Street today," Voss said. "Recognized. Chased. Escaped across the river ice, which took nerve, I'll grant him." He removed one glove, finger by finger. "Havel Ondráček's son. Your father's rival's boy. Who, I am told, was close friends with you as children — the ferryman remembers you two fishing off the pilings, summers."

"The ferryman remembers a lot of things," Jude said. His voice held. Inside, he was a bell struck too hard, ringing and ringing.

"He was seen coming off the ice at the boathouse steps. Which is two streets from here." Voss set the glove on the drafting table, precisely parallel to its edge, and Jude understood that the gesture was the message: *I put things where they belong. Everything in this room will end up where it belongs.* "So. Either the son of an enemy agent swam the frozen river in January for the excellent fishing, or he is sheltered within a hundred meters of this building. Possibly within it."

"Then search it," Jude said. "Search it now. Search it twice."

"Oh, I will." Voss smiled, and it was the worst thing Jude had seen all day, worse than the boots, worse than the whistle — because it was patient. "Tomorrow. With dogs, and men who know how to look, and all the time in the world. Tonight—" he picked up his glove, tapped it once against his palm, "—tonight I seal the quarter. Curfew at dusk. Checkpoints at every corner. No one leaves the map house, no one enters it, and Sergeant Brandt moves in effective immediately. She'll sleep downstairs." He paused at the door. "Your cough sounds terrible, apprentice. I'd see the chemist. Oh — but Ferenc's shop is closed. Arrested, pending questions about what he sold whom."

The door shut behind him. In the silence, upstairs, through the floorboards, Tomas began to cough — small and dry, like paper folding — and Jude stood in the middle of the studio looking at the loose floorboard that hid the truth of a valley, and understood that the noose had not tightened around the map house.

It had closed.

And in the doorway, not yet gone, Sergeant Brandt stood a moment longer than she needed to, her eyes moving — quickly, professionally, and for reasons Jude could not read — from the flour sacks in the corner to the trapdoor in the ceiling above.
