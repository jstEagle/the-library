## 20. Water-Soluble

They gave me a room with a long table and too many chairs.

The garrison's council chamber had been scrubbed for the occasion — Ilsa told me later the soldiers had spent half the night polishing the floorboards until they shone like river ice — and at the head of the table they'd set out a green felt runner, the kind used for signing things. Pens in a row. Inkwells in a row. A carafe of water, sweating, catching the morning light that came hard and white through the east windows.

Beyond those windows, the river ran open. The engineers had broken the ice two days early with dynamite and long poles, and now the water moved the way it always moved in March, brown and muscular and indifferent, carrying the last rotten pans of winter downstream toward the lowlands. On the far bank, the delegation's barges were already tied up. I could see the delegates' coats from here — grey, grey, and one coat the color of wet slate — small figures descending the far stair to the ferry that would bring them across.

The bridge stood open above them, chains slack, planks gleaming. It had been closed four months. It would open for good this afternoon, after the signing. The whole town had turned out on both banks to watch, the way people watch a hanging or a wedding, which are often the same crowd.

I stood at the foot of the long table with the atlas in my arms and my heart going like a drum in a parade, and I thought: *history doesn't happen elsewhere. It happens in rooms like this, at tables like this, to people who would rather be anywhere else.*

Master Aldric had taught me the opposite. He'd said it the first week of my apprenticeship, tapping the border of a map of a country I'd never seen: *We draw the world, boy, but we don't make it. The making is done by men in rooms we'll never enter.* He had been wrong, or he had been lying to himself, or he had changed his mind in the last year of his life — the year he surveyed the valley in secret and drew a line in ink that dissolves.

The door opened. Major Voss came in first, as he came into everything, as if the room had been waiting for him and he was doing it a courtesy by arriving. Behind him, two soldiers. Behind them, Ilsa Brandt, in her best uniform, her face arranged into the particular blankness I had learned to read over a winter of flour receipts and boot prints and one burned piece of evidence.

Voss wore his dress grey. His medals sat on his chest like punctuation. He looked at the atlas under my arm the way a man looks at a key he has finally pried from a drowned man's fist.

"Apprentice Okafor." He said my name as if it were a place he intended to occupy. "You'll present the atlas when the delegation is seated. You'll open it to the border folios. You will speak only when asked a question. Do you understand?"

"I understand," I said.

What I understood and what he meant were different countries. Between them ran a river.

* * *

The delegation crossed on the ferry at half past nine. There were five of them: two from the northern commission, two from the southern, and a chairman — a small, dry man with spectacles and the patient expression of someone who has spent eleven months listening to men shout about rivers. His name was Corvel. He shook hands the way a banker counts coins.

They were seated along the felt runner. Voss took the place of honor at the chairman's right. I was given a chair at the table's end, the atlas before me, and I noticed — because I had spent four months noticing everything in that room — that the carafe of water had been refilled and set at my elbow. Someone had thought of it. I did not let myself wonder who.

"Major Voss," Chairman Corvel said, "your dispatches indicate that the survey work conducted in this valley constitutes material evidence relevant to the boundary question."

"It does, sir." Voss's voice was smooth as a planed board. "The late Master Aldric Ferrane conducted a complete resurvey of the disputed reach over the final eighteen months of his life, at the request of —" a pause, delicately weighted, "— parties in this town. The resulting atlas establishes, with a precision no prior survey achieved, the true course of the boundary. I have the atlas here. The apprentice who completed it will present it."

Every face turned to me. Ilsa stood by the door with her hands clasped behind her back, and she did not look at me at all, which was how I knew she was looking at me with everything she had.

I opened the atlas.

I had rehearsed this. Not the words — there were no words to rehearse, only the act — but the hands. All night in the icehouse, by the light of one candle Tomas shielded with his body, I had practiced turning to the right folio with steady fingers, the way Master Aldric had taught me: *a mapmaker's hands confess him, Jude. Keep them boring.* So my hands were boring. I turned the brass clasps. I lifted the cover. I turned past the title page, past the general survey, past the valley in spring, in summer, in autumn, in winter, each one drawn in the master's hand with a delicacy that made even Voss's soldiers lean slightly to see.

I turned to the border folios.

The first was there: the northern reach, the river's course as the old commission had drawn it, the line running along the eastern bank, putting the ferry landing, the mill district, and the town's water rights on the northern side. Voss's side. The line that, confirmed by the master's authority, would let the garrison claim the valley's grain, its timber, its bridge — and would make every soul in this town, on the wrong side of a pen-stroke, subjects of a government they had spent a war bleeding against.

"Here," Voss said softly, "you see the boundary as—"

"The second folio is missing," Chairman Corvel said.

The room went very quiet. The river filled it, a low brown murmur through the glass.

I had turned past it. I had turned past it so smoothly that only a man who had spent his life reading maps would have caught the skip in the sequence — and Corvel had caught it, because Corvel had spent his life reading maps, or at least reading men who lied with them.

"The southern reach," Corvel said. "The resurvey of the disputed reach itself. The whole purpose of this evidence. Where is it?"

Voss's head turned toward me slowly, the way a gun traverses.

"Apprentice," he said. "The folio."

"There is no folio," I said.

My voice came out steady. I have no idea how. Inside, everything was the icehouse at night, everything was Tomas's hand on the candle, everything was the master's letter: *a line drawn in ink becomes a grave dug in advance.*

"The master completed the survey," I said. "He completed the drawing. I completed the atlas. And then, before he died, Master Aldric Ferrane did the last thing a mapmaker can do to a map."

"Which is?" Corvel asked. He was leaning forward now, spectacles flashing.

"He made it honest."

I reached for the carafe.

* * *

Here is what I did, and I will set it down plainly because everything else in this account is plain:

I lifted the carafe of water. I opened the atlas to the space where the second border folio should have been — the binding still held the ghost of it, a pale strip where the plate had been tipped in and removed — and I set the carafe down beside it, and from inside my coat I drew out the page.

I had carried it against my chest all morning. The true border. The master's final survey, the one that would have condemned the valley — the one that showed the river had moved, that the old line ran through what was now open water, that the boundary, drawn truly, gave the ferry landing to the north and the mill district to the south and split the town's heart between two flags like a wishbone. The page that would have let Voss stand before this table and say *behold, the truth* while the truth did what truth does to small places, which is break them.

The ink was the master's own formula. He'd shown me once, years ago, in a mood I'd taken for a joke: gum arabic and oak gall and something bitter he wouldn't name, ink that went down black as any ink and held for years in a dry drawer — and surrendered everything to water. *For corrections, boy,* he'd said. *The river is the only honest editor.*

"Major Voss will tell you," I said, "that this page is the most important document in this valley. He is right. It is the master's final border, surveyed at cost of his life's work and, I believe, his life. It is accurate. Every measurement in it is true."

"Apprentice—" Voss began, and there was something new in his voice, something I had not heard in four months of his polite traps: urgency, naked and human.

I poured the water.

I poured it the way I'd been taught to wash a plate for correction, a thin steady stream, and I watched the line go. It went the way the master must have dreamed it would: not all at once but in a slow bloom, the black border softening, browning, feathering out into the fibers of the paper like a root system letting go of the soil, until there was no line at all, only a faint grey stain where a border had been, and then not even that. Just paper. Just the valley, drawn beautiful and whole, with no line through its heart.

The water ran off the page onto the felt runner and spread in a small dark continent among the pens and inkwells, among all the instruments assembled for drawing lines.

"No line," I said. "No grave."

* * *

Voss was on his feet. I want to record, because it is the only honest thing I can say about him, that for one moment — before the outrage, before the command voice — his face showed something almost like grief. He had spent a war believing that lines were the only thing standing between order and the wolf, and I had just washed one away in front of the only men who could restore it.

"Do you understand what you have done?" he said. "That was evidence. That was *property of the commission*. That boy—" and his hand cut toward the door, toward the guardroom where Tomas had been held since the night raid, since Ilsa's search, since the boot prints she never found, "—that prisoner's fate was tied to the disposition of that atlas. You had a bargain."

"I had a promise," I said. "It was made first."

"Sir." Voss turned to Corvel, and I will grant him this: he rebuilt himself in a breath, the officer reassembling over the man like ice over water. "The apprentice has destroyed commission evidence. He has done it to protect a fugitive — an enemy national found concealed in this town, in this building. I have him in custody. I have the apprentice as an accessory. The survey can be reconstructed from the field notes, which I also hold. The line will be drawn, sir. If not today, then—"

"Major." Chairman Corvel had not stood. He had not even raised his voice. He had taken off his spectacles and was cleaning them with a grey handkerchief, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "Sit down."

Voss sat.

"Four months ago," Corvel said, putting his spectacles back on, "I was in a room very like this one, and a major very like you presented me with a map, and on the strength of that map we drew a line, and on the strength of that line two villages were evacuated, and I have spent the winter reading the casualty returns." He looked at the atlas, at the blank space where the folio had been, at the water still darkening the felt. "I have spent eleven months listening to men tell me what the river *should* have done. You are the first to show me what it did. And then the first to admit that the truth, in this valley, is a thing that kills whoever holds it."

He turned to me.

"Young man. The master's survey — the measurements. Were they true?"

I thought about the ferryman on the frozen bank, and the terrible arithmetic: the line gave the valley to neither side but split it, ruin either way. I thought about Tomas in the loft, arguing that the atlas was a weapon. I thought about the master's grave, and what I'd said to the headstone: *I don't know how to finish you.*

"The measurements were true," I said. "The line was true. That's why it's gone. A true line, in that valley, is a grave dug in advance. My master understood it. He wrote it down. He drew the line so it could be unmade."

Corvel was quiet a long moment. Through the windows the barges rocked on the open water, and somewhere on the far bank a crowd made the low sound crowds make when they have waited all winter and can wait a little longer.

"The armistice," Corvel said at last, to the table, to the room, "provides amnesty for enemy nationals detained in the occupation zone upon signature. The signature occurs this afternoon." He looked at Voss. "Major, is there an enemy national in your custody?"

Voss's jaw worked. I watched him weigh it — the reconstruction from field notes, the appeal, the long game — and I watched the room take the weight out of his hands, delegate by delegate, chair by chair, the way a river takes a bank.

"There is," he said. "He will be released at signature."

"And the apprentice's act," Corvel continued, "in destroying a document whose publication would have condemned an entire valley to partition — I rule it an act of conscience under the humanitarian articles, and I note it in the record as such." He almost smiled, a dry small movement. "The commission has, after all, spent a year drawing lines. It can survive one morning watching a line wash away. Perhaps it is the most useful thing anyone has shown us."

* * *

They signed at two o'clock, on the bridge itself, at a table carried out to the middle of it, because Corvel said — and I heard him say it — that a boundary should be signed where the two sides can see each other's faces. The town crowded both banks. The chains that had rattled shut in November were unlocked and dragged open with a sound I felt in my teeth, and the first people crossed in both directions, and someone started ringing the church bell, and the bell was answered from the far side by a bell I had never heard before, which was the enemy's bell, which was just a bell.

Tomas was brought out of the guardroom as the ink on the signatures was drying. He looked thinner than the icehouse had left him, and he blinked in the daylight like a man surfacing, and then he saw me across the crowd and the whole winter went out of his shoulders at once.

Ilsa Brandt stood at the guardroom door and watched him go, and she did not salute, and she did not look away, and when our eyes met she touched two fingers to her breast pocket — where the boot prints had been, where the evidence had burned — and then she turned and went back inside, because there was still a garrison to run and she was still, for one more day, its sergeant.

Voss did not watch the signing. He stood in the council chamber window with the atlas's empty binding open on the table before him, and he looked at the river for a long time, and I have thought since about what a man like that sees when he looks at moving water. Perhaps a line. Perhaps the place where a line used to be.

Tomas reached me at the foot of the bridge. He didn't say anything. He just put his hand flat on my chest, over the place where the page had ridden all morning, where the paper still left a faint damp ghost against my heart, and left it there.

"The studio," he said finally. "There's something we should do with the blank page."

"I know," I said. "I've had all winter to think about it."

And below us, under the planks of the bridge, the river went on carrying the winter away — brown, muscular, indifferent, and, for the first time in my life, entirely unmarked.
