## 6. The Reckoning List

The list went up on the market cross at dawn, nailed there by a corporal with a hammer and the bored efficiency of a man hanging a butcher's notice.

Beatrix heard of it before she saw it. News traveled through Ashcombe the way water travels through chalk—silently, invisibly, surfacing where it was least wanted. By the time she crossed the square with her master's mended watch in her pocket, a crowd had already gathered beneath the cross, and it made no sound at all. That was the worst of it. Thirty names read by thirty pairs of eyes, and not one voice raised above a whisper.

She shouldered through to the front, past the fishwife Marta Cullen whose hands were shaking so badly her basket of herring trembled like something alive, past old Hobbes the ratcatcher who had gone the color of tallow. And there it was, written in the clerk's neat copperplate on army paper, headed with the royal arms:

*Persons of Ashcombe and environs adjudged vagrant, seditious, or without lawful settlement, to be transported to His Majesty's plantations in America aboard the* Providence *, sailing upon the tide of the twenty-ninth.*

Thirty names. She counted them twice because the first time her eyes refused to cooperate.

*Aldous Winterbourne, clockmaker.*

Her breath went out of her as if she'd been struck in the sternum. There it was in black ink, his name among felons and vagabonds, listed between a poacher from the moor and a woman who'd been overheard singing a Jacobite air in her own kitchen. The petition he'd been drafting with his guild seal and his careful arguments—the one that would buy him appeal time, that would prove his settlement of forty years—it might as well have been written in water. The army had not waited for guild standing or magistrate's mercy. They had simply taken the ledger's silence about certain households, added the testimony gathered at Ferris's trial, and made their arithmetic.

She read on, her lips moving though she made no sound.

*Marta Cullen, widow.* The fishwife beside her made a small noise, like a stitch tearing.

*Tobias Renwick, dockhand.* Beatrix's stomach dropped through the cobbles. Tobias Renwick—big, slow-smiling Tobias, who had carried Nell on his shoulders through the spring fair, who had given the girl his dead sister's name when the parish asked who claimed her. Nell's only protector in all the world. If Tobias was shipped across the ocean, Nell would be nobody's child again. Worse than nobody's. A child with no entry in any book could be bound out to anyone, sent anywhere, and no paper in Ashcombe would rise up to say otherwise.

And then, near the bottom, in the same indifferent hand:

*Nell Renwick, orphan, age uncertain.*

They had put her on the list. Eight years old, perhaps nine—she didn't know herself—and they meant to pack her into the hold of the *Providence* with convicts and debtors and send her to die slowly in a tobacco colony, because no ledger said she belonged anywhere.

Beatrix became aware that Marta Cullen was gripping her arm, hard enough to bruise.

"They can't," the widow whispered. "Tobias has worked the quay since he was a boy. He's paid his dues. He—" Her voice cracked and reassembled itself into fury. "His only crime is that he took in his sister's brat without paying the parish for the privilege."

Beatrix said nothing. Her mind had gone cold and precise, the way it did when a mechanism jammed and every second of panic cost her a gear tooth. She looked at the list again, and this time she read it not as a sentence but as a specification—as she'd read a thousand drawings of things that did not yet exist but could be made to.

At the very bottom, beneath the thirty names, a final line in smaller script:

*The vessel carries accommodation for thirty-one persons. The thirty-first berth is reserved by order of Colonel Hesse, commanding.*

Reserved by rank. Someone's servant, someone's mistress, someone's profitable cargo of a different kind—one berth held back while thirty souls were measured for shackles. Unless someone acted, that berth would carry nothing but an officer's comfort across the Atlantic, and Nell Renwick would sail in the dark below with the rest.

Beatrix stepped back from the crowd. Marta was still talking—planning, raging—but Beatrix was already walking, fast, toward the magistrate's temporary quarters in the requisitioned assembly rooms, because there was one thing left that a person without a name in any book could still do.

She could ask.

* * *

Colonel Hesse received petitions in the assembly rooms between ten and noon, and by half past eleven the queue stretched down the steps and into the street: wives of the condemned, mothers, employers vouching for servants, all of them clutching papers they'd paid scribblers to write. Beatrix waited two hours on those steps with the wind cutting through her coat, watching each petitioner emerge looking smaller than they'd gone in.

When her turn came, a sergeant with ink-stained fingers waved her into a room that still smelled faintly of its former life—wax and lemon polish under the new smell of pipe smoke and wet wool. Colonel Hesse sat behind a table that had clearly belonged to the town council, a comfortable man with a comfortable face and eyes like coins.

"Name?" said the clerk beside him.

"Beatrix Winterbourne. Apprentice to Aldous Winterbourne, clockmaker, of Wheelwright Lane."

"Apprentice." The clerk's pen paused. "Your indenture is enrolled with the guild?"

"It was never formally recorded." She kept her voice level. "My master took me in when I was seven. I have worked at his bench eleven years."

"Mm." The colonel turned a page. "You're here regarding?"

"My master appears on the transport list. Also a child, Nell Renwick, who—"

"The child." Something flickered behind the coin-bright eyes—not interest exactly, but recognition, the look of a man consulting an inventory. "Yes. No settlement. No parentage. No record whatsoever. Under the act, such persons are liable to be transported for the good of the colony and the relief of the parish."

"She is eight years old."

"Children thrive at sea, I'm told." He said it mildly, almost kindly, the way one remarks on weather. "Better prospects than the streets of a garrison town, certainly. The plantations take them young and make workers of them. It's a mercy, if you consider it properly."

Beatrix considered it properly. She thought of Nell three nights ago in the workshop, asleep in Aldous's own chair with her thumb in her mouth and her boots still on, small enough that Beatrix had carried her to the cot without waking. She thought of the girl learning to hold a file the way you'd teach a bird to perch—gently, patiently, laughing when it went wrong.

"I'm here to petition for her berth," Beatrix said. "I understand the ship carries thirty-one places. Thirty are filled by your list. I ask that the last be struck from whatever private use it's reserved for, and given to the child—or rather, that the child be removed from the transport list entirely, and lodged with me. I'll stand surety. My master's shop, my wages, my person."

The clerk wrote none of this down.

Colonel Hesse leaned back in the councilman's chair and regarded her with something that was almost warmth, which was how she knew the answer before he gave it.

"You misunderstand the nature of these proceedings, Mistress Winterbourne. This is not a matter of berths being *given*. Berths are allocated according to order and priority. The thirty-first place is reserved by my order for purposes of the service. As for the child—" he spread his hands, a gesture of beautiful helplessness— "the list is drawn. It has been posted. To alter it now would require new orders from London, and London does not concern itself with the domestic arrangements of a town like Ashcombe."

"Then I ask that her case be reviewed. She has a protector. Tobias Renwick—"

"Is himself on the list." The colonel smiled. "You see the difficulty. The girl's protector is a vagrant by our reckoning, which makes her the ward of a vagrant, which makes her a vagrant. Paper is a wonderful thing, Mistress Winterbourne. It tells us exactly who people are." He tapped the ledger—*her* ledger, the parish's ledger, lying on his table like a captured flag. "This book says who belongs in Ashcombe. You, I note, are not in it."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere upstairs, boots crossed a floor.

"So you see," Hesse continued pleasantly, "your surety is worth precisely nothing. A person who does not exist cannot stand bond for another. I admire your feeling—I do, genuinely; it does you credit—but feeling is not settlement, and settlement is the whole of the law." He picked up his pen, a small man concluding small business. "Sergeant will show you out. And Mistress Winterbourne—a word of advice, offered kindly. Grieve quietly. Loud grief in a garrison town attracts attention, and attention, just now, is dangerous currency."

She walked out past the queue of waiting faces, past the wives and mothers clutching their useless papers, and down the steps into the wind. Nobody stopped her. Nobody needed to. She had been dismissed with perfect courtesy, the way one dismisses a beggar or a changeable weather—a thing of no account, a person who did not exist.

* * *

Aldous was at the bench when she came in, though "at the bench" had come to mean sitting propped in his chair with a movement laid out before him that he hadn't touched in an hour. The cough had carved him down another size since the trial. When he looked up at her, she saw he already knew.

"You've seen the list," he said.

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, as if confirming a calculation. "Thirty years I've kept every promise the town ever asked of me. Repaired the church clock for nothing. Trained four apprentices proper. Paid my rates, served my turns, buried my wife in consecrated ground." He turned the untouched movement over with one finger. "And one afternoon's arithmetic puts me in the hold of a ship with poachers. Because a printer I never met wrote my name in a dying man's mouth."

"Aldous—"

"I'm not afraid of the colonies, girl. I'm too old and too sick for them to want me anyway; half of us won't survive the crossing and the army knows it and counts it as freight loss." He coughed, deep and wet, and rode it out with his eyes shut. "It's the principle that galls. That a book can decide it. That everything I built counts for less than what some clerk failed to write down."

Beatrix stood in the doorway with the colonel's courtesy still ringing in her ears—*a person who does not exist cannot stand bond for another*—and felt something shift in her chest, the way a mainspring shifts when it passes its breaking tension. Not despair. Despair had been on the courthouse steps, in the queue, in the colonel's warm coin-bright eyes. This was something harder, something that set its teeth.

Paper told them who people were. Very well.

Paper could be made.

She thought of the ledger lying open on the colonel's table, unguarded except by a sergeant's boredom. She thought of the clerk's copperplate, which she had studied by candlelight until she could reproduce the capital R's curled tail in her sleep. She thought of sealing wax and the impression she'd taken weeks ago from a discarded letter, pressed into her memory the way she memorized gear trains. She thought of the observatory clock, whose escapement she knew better than any living soul, and of what a clockmaker could do to a town's sense of time if she chose.

"Master," she said quietly, "how long does the *Providence* lie at anchor before she sails?"

"Six days. Why?"

"Because a book decides who belongs." She pulled off her coat and hung it on its peg, and her hands were perfectly steady. "And books are only mechanisms, Aldous. Wheels and springs and locks. And I have spent my whole life learning how mechanisms break."

Aldous looked at her for a long moment, and then, slowly, he began to smile—the first time she had seen him smile since the red coats came—and reached beneath his bench for the drawer where he kept the tools he'd never shown her.

"Then we had best begin," he said, "while there's still darkness enough to work in."
