## 10. A New Page

The soldiers left on a Tuesday in March, in the grey hour before the fish carts came down to the quay. Beatrix watched them from the workshop window, the glass cold against her forehead, and counted them out of Ashcombe the way she had once counted their arrival—by companies, by wagons, by the drumbeat that had kept the town awake for half a year. The last wagon carried the records chests. The parish ledger went with it, bound in its oilcloth, and for a moment she felt her heart climb into her throat as if a part of her own body were being carted away.

Then the wagon turned at the market cross and stopped, and a clerk—not the clerk whose hand she had learned by candlelight, but a younger one with ink-stained cuffs—climbed down and handed the ledger across to the vicar like a man returning something he had borrowed and did not wish to discuss.

The army had audited itself, in the end. There had been questions asked in London about the trial of Thomas Ferris, about the transport list, about a certain irregularity discovered in the records of one garrison town. No one had ever come to the workshop door. No one had ever looked twice at the apprentice clockmaker with the steady hands. The forgery sat in the restored ledger now like a rivet set flush into a plate: invisible unless you knew where to press.

She knew where to press. She pressed it every day, in her mind, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

* * *

Aldous Winterbourne took his breakfast by the window these days, wrapped in a blanket even when the fire was lit, because the cold had gotten into him the way damp gets into a clock case—slowly, and then all at once. His cough had settled from the racking storms of winter into something quieter and more permanent, a dry ticking deep in his chest that Beatrix could hear across the room when the shop was silent.

"You're staring," he said, without looking up from the plate of bread and honey Nell had cut for him. She always cut it too thick. He never said so.

"I'm looking at my master," Beatrix said. "It's permitted."

"It's wasteful." He tore the bread with fingers that had gone thin at the knuckles, hands that had once held a graver so fine it could cut a tooth for an escapement wheel. "There's work wanting. The Quaker woman's mantel clock still runs slow."

"The Quaker woman's mantel clock runs slow because she lets her grandson wind it wrong. I've told her. Twice."

"Then tell her a third time, and charge her for the telling." He smiled, and the smile cost him breath. "You'll make a poor tradesman, Bea. You keep giving the work away."

"Then I'll be a poor tradesman," she said, "and rich in everything else."

He looked at her then—really looked, the way he had looked at her the night he confessed what he'd done with the ledger thirty years ago, writing a dead child's name over hers to keep her out of the workhouse. That look had frightened her once. Now it only made her throat ache.

"Nell's at the harbor," she said, to break it. "Widow Marsh is teaching her knots. Proper ones, not the kind that strangle."

"A useful education." He nodded slowly. "And you? What will you teach her?"

Beatrix considered the question honestly, because Aldous deserved honesty. "That a thing can be mended," she said at last. "Most things. Not all. But most—and it matters which things you choose to mend first."

He closed his eyes briefly, as though she had handed him something heavy and precious. "Better than anything I taught you," he said. "Go on. The shop won't open itself."

* * *

The ledger came back to the parish chest on a Thursday, and Beatrix was there when the vicar unlocked it, because she had arranged to be there—had offered, in fact, to help carry it, in the way of a person who has no official business but every unofficial reason in the world.

The new pages were clean. The old ones had been copied out by a London hand, fair and impersonal, and Beatrix read down the columns with her heart in her mouth until she found what she was looking for.

*Winterbourne, Aldous, clockmaker, resident.* His entry stood unaltered—the altered record had held, had been copied forward as truth, and whatever appeal time she had bought him that terrible autumn had stretched into the winter and past the withdrawal, and now it simply didn't matter anymore. He would die in Ashcombe, in his own bed, above his own shop. It was the only mercy she had ever stolen that had come true completely.

And beneath it, in the same fair hand:

*Winterbourne, Nell, ward.*

Not Marsh. Not Ferris. Not any name invented in a records office by candlelight while a sentry's boots scraped outside the door. Ward—to Aldous, legally, which meant ward to the house, which meant ward to her. The widow Marsh had insisted on it that night at the docks, gripping Beatrix's wrist with rope-hardened fingers: *My place, girl. Give it to the child. I've buried enough; God knows where I belong already. But that one belongs somewhere, and you're going to see she stays there.*

The widow had been transported after all—in the end she'd chosen it, waving from the deck like a woman boarding a pleasure yacht, calling out that the colonies couldn't be worse than Ashcombe in February. Her last letter had come in January, cheerful and misspelled, asking after Nell and complaining about the heat.

"She's real," Beatrix said aloud, to the vicar, who blinked at her. "The entry. The girl. She's real, and she's mine—or I'm hers, I've never quite worked out which."

"That's how it works," the vicar said, with the mildness of a man who had spent forty years watching this town argue with its own paperwork. "Were you wanting to enter anyone else?"

And there it was. The question she had carried since autumn, folded small and kept warm.

"Yes," Beatrix said. "Me."

* * *

They brought the ledger to the workshop that evening, by arrangement, because Aldous could not easily climb the churchyard hill anymore and because—though no one said this—it seemed right that it should happen under this roof. The vicar sat in the good chair. Nell perched on the workbench, legs swinging, having been told this was history and having decided to treat it accordingly, with great solemnity and a honey-sticky thumb.

Aldous signed first, adding his mark beside the copied entry—a shaky W, witnessed by the vicar, confirming what the paper already knew. Then the pen came to Beatrix.

She had imagined this moment all winter, and in her imagination her hand had trembled. It did not tremble. Her hand was as steady as it had been in the records office with the sentry ten feet away, as steady as it was setting a balance spring, because this too was fine work, and fear had taught her the difference between shaking and precision.

She wrote:

*Winterbourne, Beatrix, clockmaker, resident of Ashcombe, apprenticed to Aldous Winterbourne, entered this day of her own knowledge and consent.*

Her own knowledge and consent. Not smuggled in under a dead child's name. Not forged in the dark. Written in full light, in her own hand, in ink she had mixed herself because the parish ink was watery and she had standards.

"There," the vicar said, when the ink had dried. "Now you exist."

"I always existed," Beatrix said. "I just wasn't written down." She blotted the page carefully. "The difference matters less than people think. But it matters."

Nell slid off the workbench and put her chin on Beatrix's shoulder to read upside down, lips moving through the words. When she reached *ward* she stopped. "That's me."

"That's you."

"In a book."

"In the book. The book of who belongs here." Beatrix turned and looked at her—the sharp-faced dockhand's orphan who had appeared at the workshop door hungry two winters ago and had never entirely left. "Do you understand what that means?"

Nell considered it with the gravity of nine years old. "It means nobody can ship me away."

"It means more than that," Aldous said quietly from his chair. "It means somebody chose to write you in. Remember the difference, girl. Books can be burned. Being chosen—that stays."

* * *

Spring came into the workshop the way spring comes into a clock: not all at once, but gear by gear, each small motion catching the next. The light lasted longer in the evenings. The harbor ice broke up. And Aldous declined, gently, the way he did everything—without fuss, without complaint, marking his diminishment the way he marked his clocks, precisely and without drama.

By April he no longer worked at the bench. By May he came down to the shop only in the afternoons, to sit in the chair by the window and watch Beatrix's hands and occasionally say, "Slower," or, "You're forcing it," or, best of all, nothing at all, which meant the work was good.

One evening in late May she found him standing before the tall case clock in the corner—the old regulator, the one he'd built the year she was born, which had run faithfully through occupation and trial and forgery and grief without missing a beat.

"It's two minutes fast," he said.

"It's been two minutes fast since November. I've been meaning to get to it."

"You've been meaning to get to it since November." He lowered himself into the chair with a sound like a settling house. "Get to it."

She opened the case. The movement gleamed inside, brass and steel, ordered and patient. She stopped the pendulum, corrected the rating nut, listened to the tick find its true interval again—that deep, even heartbeat she had fallen asleep to as a child in the loft above this very room.

When she closed the case, Aldous was watching her with an expression she couldn't name until suddenly she could: it was the expression of a man checking his work and finding it finished.

"You know," he said, "when I falsified that entry for you, thirty years ago, I told myself it was a kindness. A small crime, a small mercy. I never thought—" He coughed, waved the thought onward. "I never thought you'd take it further than I dared. A whole page. A whole life. You built what I only sketched."

"You gave me the tools."

"I gave you pliers." He smiled. "You gave yourself the rest."

Outside, the town bell rang the hour, and the regulator answered it a half-second later, true at last, and Beatrix thought: *this is what belonging sounds like. Two voices, keeping the same time.*

* * *

He died in July, on a morning so clear the harbor looked cut from tin. He died in his sleep, in his bed, above his shop, with the ledger downstairs in the parish chest bearing his name in fair ink, resident of Ashcombe, and if there is a better death than that, Beatrix thought afterward, standing in his room with the sunlight on the coverlet, she did not know what it was.

The funeral filled the churchyard. Half of Ashcombe came—the Quaker woman, the dockhands, the families whose names had been gathered like kindling at Thomas Ferris's trial and had somehow, miraculously, not burned. The vicar spoke of a good tradesman. Nell cried and pretended she hadn't. Beatrix stood at the graveside with her hands folded the way she did when she needed them still, and felt the strangest thing: not emptiness, but inheritance. As though a key had been pressed into her palm by a hand that was no longer there.

The will was short. *To my apprentice Beatrix Winterbourne, the workshop, the tools, and the goodwill of the trade, which she has earned thrice over.* The lawyer read it in the shop, surrounded by clocks, all of them ticking, all of them keeping different hours, and Beatrix signed her name beneath it in her own hand and noticed, without surprise, that it no longer shook.

* * *

On the first of August she opened the shop under her own sign. WINTERBOURNE & WARD, the painter had written, at Nell's insistence, and Beatrix had let her have it, because a found family deserved lettering.

She hung the sign herself, on a ladder, in the morning sun, while Nell held the ladder and offered loud technical criticism. Below in the street, Mrs. Quill the Quaker woman raised her hand in greeting. Somewhere toward the harbor a ship's bell rang. In the parish chest across town, a ledger lay closed, holding two names written in one hand—one stolen long ago by a dying man's mercy, one given freely by her own—and Beatrix understood, finally, what Aldous had tried to tell her with pliers and silence and thirty years of patience:

Belonging was not granted. It was built. Gear by gear. Name by name. Page by page.

She climbed down, took up her apron, and opened the doors.

The first customer was already waiting.
