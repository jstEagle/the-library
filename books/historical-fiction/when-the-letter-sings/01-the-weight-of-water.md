## 1. The Weight of Water

The bell at the compound gate rang before the sky had decided what color to be, and Paloma Underhill was already awake.

She had been awake for some time, in fact — since the moment the wind shifted in the night and came off the water instead of the land, carrying that particular mineral smell she had never learned to sleep through. Seven years on this coast had taught her body things her mind preferred not to know: that a change in the wind meant a change in the swell, that a change in the swell meant the overseers would be counting every shell twice, and that days when the sea was difficult were days when it paid to be small, quick, and unremarkable.

She dressed in the dark without lighting the lamp. The lamp oil cost against her account — everything cost against her account, from the salt in her food to the mending thread in her clothes to the very cot she slept on, itemized monthly in a hand so fine and patient it might have been written by a spider. She knew the arithmetic of her life better than she knew her own face. Four hundred and twelve meridian marks remaining, less interest, less rations, less the lamp she did not light. At her current rate of clearing debt, she would be free in eleven years, assuming the beds held out, assuming her lungs held out, assuming nothing went wrong.

Nothing ever went wrong for people like her. That was the whole design of it.

The dormitory breathed around her — forty women in the long room, some stirring, most still sunk in the heavy sleep of the exhausted. Paloma moved down the row of cots with the practiced silence of someone who had learned that making noise drew attention and attention drew questions and questions were how girls got noticed by men whose noticing cost more than lamp oil. She braided her hair wet, because dry hair caught the light and light caught the eye. She ate her ration of cassava standing up, quickly, tasting none of it. And then she was out into the blue-black morning, joining the river of divers flowing toward the boats, one dark shape among many, indistinguishable, invisible, safe.

* * *

The dawn boats went out in order of seniority, which meant they went out in order of debt, which meant Paloma's boat was always third. She took her place on the thwart without being told, as she always did, wedged between Marisol the elder, who was fifty and still dove deeper than anyone, and a girl called Nena who could not have been more than fourteen and who shivered even in the windless season. Nobody spoke. Speech before the first dive was considered bad luck by the older divers and wasteful by the younger ones, and so the boat slid out over the sleeping reef in a silence that was almost holy, if you didn't think too hard about why it existed.

Paloma thought about it anyway. She thought about most things anyway. That was the trouble with her, or would have been, if anyone had ever bothered to look closely enough to find the trouble: she had a mind that kept working whether she wanted it to or not, turning over observations like stones, looking for what lived underneath. She had learned young that the trick was not to stop thinking but to stop showing it. A face like still water. Eyes that watched without appearing to watch. Hands that did exactly what was expected and never a fraction more.

The sea east of the compound turned from iron to pewter to a pale, bruised gold as the sun hauled itself up behind the drying sheds. The sheds again — always the sheds, their long shadows lying across the water like fingers. Paloma looked away from them. There was no reason to look at them. There was never any reason to look at anything the company did not explicitly require her to look at.

"Down at the second marker," said the boatman, Ferro, a company man with a voice like gravel in a tin cup. "Overseer says the bed's been stripped shallow. He wants deep work."

Marisol spat over the side, an old woman's commentary that everyone understood and no one acknowledged. Deep work meant bottom time past comfort. Deep work meant ears that ached for days and blood sometimes in the spit afterward. Deep work also meant heavier tally, and heavier tally meant the debt moved, however slightly, in the right direction.

Paloma checked her stone weight, checked her knife, checked the mesh bag she'd repaired herself with twine bought against her own account. Then she waited, as everyone waited, for Ferro to ring the small brass bell that gave permission.

The bell rang. The world tilted. And Paloma went under.

* * *

There was a version of the dive she had never told anyone about, not Marisol, not Tomas, not the priest who came quarterly on the mail sloop and heard confessions through a curtain of mosquito netting. In that version, the descent was not labor but arrival. The water closed over her head and the noise of the world — the bells, the overseers, the ledger, the debt, the name she did not say aloud — all of it pressed flat and fell away, and there was only the blue going green going black, and the pressure folding around her like a hand, and her own heartbeat slowing to match the rhythm of the swell above.

She was good. She knew she was good, though she would sooner have cut off a finger than let anyone see her know it. Good was dangerous. Good got you promoted onto the crews that worked the deep beds exclusively, which got you more money per shell and more damage per lung. But there was a difference between being good and seeming eager, and Paloma had spent seven years learning to walk that line the way a rope-walker works a high wire: perfectly, invisibly, with death waiting on either side.

Her body knew the work the way a bird knows air. She dropped feet-first along the anchor line, equalizing her ears in small deliberate swallows, and at thirty feet the reef opened beneath her like a country. The pearl beds lay in the sandy hollows between coral heads, oysters clustered thick as cobblestones, and she worked them with quick economical sweeps of her knife, prying, twisting, bagging, moving. No wasted motion. No flourish. The other divers from her boat scattered along the bed like dark stars, and above them all, faint and far, hung the hulls of the boats, and beyond those, the pale disc of a sun she would not feel properly again until evening.

At the edge of the bed, where the sand shelved away toward deeper water, she saw something that made her go still.

An oyster — a great one, old-shelled, crusted with weed — sat just past the boundary stone that marked the limit of the company's leased ground. Beyond the stone, the shells belonged to whoever held the next concession, and taking one was theft, and theft by a debtor was not punished with fines. It was punished with years. Her contract had seven more on it already; she did not intend to add to the sum.

But the oyster sat six inches inside the line. She measured it three times with her eyes, floating there in the cold green half-light, her heart ticking slow and loud. Six inches. It was the kind of shell that held a pearl worth a season's tally, maybe two. It was also the kind of shell that a certain kind of overseer liked to use — a shell planted deliberately past the line, or near enough to claim it was past, a trap baited for divers who were doing too well, growing too skilled, becoming too valuable to control without a reminder of who owned whom.

Paloma had seen a girl named Adela take such a shell four years ago. Adela had cleared her debt entirely that day, and had been free for exactly one afternoon, and had then spent three years on the drainage crew at the salt pans, from which no one had yet returned with their health intact.

Paloma left the oyster where it lay. She turned back to the safe bed, pried ordinary shells from ordinary rock, and filled her bag with the patient, unremarkable labor of a woman who intended to survive.

* * *

The tally book came out at midday, when the boats grounded on the shingle and the divers formed the line they always formed, wet and shivering, each holding out her bag for counting.

Overseer Bracken did the counting himself, as he always did, a narrow man with a clerk's hands and a flogger's memory. He wore his coat buttoned to the throat despite the heat, and he counted with two fingers, fast, dropping each shell into the company crate while his assistant — a soft, sweating boy called Pell — entered the figures in the tally book with a pencil worn to a stub.

"Underhill." Bracken's voice found her the way it always did, flat and precise, neither warm nor cruel. That was the thing about Bracken that made him worse than the cruel ones: he was nothing at all. He was a column of figures wearing a man's body. "Forty-one."

"Forty-one, sir." Her voice came out low and level, trained to carry no further than necessary.

"Thirty-one credited." He wrote it. She watched the number go down against her name and felt the familiar sensation, which was not quite anger and not quite grief, of watching part of a day of her life get folded away into a stranger's pocket. Ten shells off for the boat, for the gear, for the breakage allowance, for the levy, for the hundred small taxes that lived between the diving and the crediting like parasites between the scales of a fish.

"Sir." Nena's voice, beside her, thin with the effort of keeping it steady. "I count thirty-eight in my bag. He has thirty-eight."

Pell looked up. Bracken did not. "Thirty," he said.

"But I—"

"You have thirty, girl, unless you'd like to explain to the magistrate why your arithmetic is better than the company's." Still no inflection. Still nothing. He moved on down the line, and Nena stood there with her mouth open and her eyes bright and furious, and Paloma, without turning her head, without changing her expression at all, reached sideways and touched the girl's wrist. One touch. Let it go.

Later, walking back toward the compound, Nena muttered, "He shorted me eight."

"Yes."

"Aren't you angry?"

Paloma considered the question with genuine care, because Nena deserved care, and because the answer mattered. "Anger is expensive," she said finally. "I can't afford it."

Nena stared at her as though she'd spoken in a foreign tongue, and perhaps she had — the language of the survived, which took seven years to learn and could not be taught, only caught, like a fever.

* * *

The afternoons belonged to the compound. Paloma's assignment was the mending shed, where nets were repaired and shell was sorted and the damaged records of the counting house came to be patched — a task given to her two years ago when it was discovered she could read, a discovery she had regretted every day since, because literacy was useful and useful people were noticed.

She sat now at the long table with a ledger open before her, its spine broken, its pages swollen from damp, and she smoothed and pressed and copied with a steady hand while the other women talked around her in the low current of compound talk: rations, debts, a wedding on the San Aurelio road, a diver from the fourth boat who'd come up bleeding from the ear and been sent back down anyway.

"Tomas says the beds are dying," Marisol said, not looking up from her netting. "Says the company's dredging the shallows so hard there'll be nothing left in five years. Says that's why they're pushing the deep beds."

"Tomas says a lot of things," said another woman, and there was a careful blankness in her voice that Paloma recognized. Careful blankness was the compound's native dialect.

Tomas. Paloma's hands kept their rhythm on the page. Tomas Reyes, twenty-two, broad-shouldered and loud-hearted, who had been the first person on this coast to learn her real laugh and the last person she allowed herself to trust. He was forever saying things — about the company, about the beds, about the way the debt accounts never seemed to shrink no matter how many shells went into the crates. He said these things in the dormitory yard, in the queue at the ration house, once, memorably, directly to Overseer Bracken's face, and every time he said them, Paloma felt the cold slide of fear down her spine, because she remembered — she made herself remember, though remembering hurt — what had happened the last time people on this coast had said things together, loudly, in groups.

She had been twelve. She remembered her father's voice saying *keep quiet, little fish, keep quiet and stay deep*, and she remembered not understanding, and she remembered understanding later, all at once, in a way that could never be undone.

She pushed the memory down where it lived, in the flooded dark below thought, and kept copying. The ledger she was repairing was an old one, five years dead, full of names she half-knew. Her eye — treacherous, restless, always looking for what lived under stones — snagged on a column heading she had seen a hundred times before without ever truly reading it:

*Debt Accounts, Continuing.*

Continuing. An odd word for a closed ledger. Debts continued; that was normal. But these entries had dates of origin going back years, and amounts accruing, and against several of them, in the margin, in that same fine patient spider-hand, small annotations she could not quite make out through the water stain.

"Underhill." Pell stood in the doorway of the mending shed, sweating, apologetic, holding out a chit. "Overseer wants this year's damaged volumes finished by week's end. All of them."

All of them. That was new. That was more than new — that was strange, because the damaged volumes had sat untouched for months, and Bracken was not a man who cared about tidy records, and something in the phrasing of the order, in the boy's nervous delivery of it, suggested the instruction had come from above Bracken. From the counting house proper. From Director Calloway's floor.

"I'll have them done," Paloma said.

Pell lingered a moment, shifting his weight. "There's — talk of an audit coming," he offered, in the tone of a boy handing her a gift he wasn't sure she wanted. "From the mainland. Someone's coming to check the books. So everything has to be square."

Everything has to be square. Paloma nodded once, and Pell fled, and she sat alone at the table as the light went amber through the shed's slatted walls, looking down at the ruined ledger and its continuing debts, and felt — without being able to name it, without wanting to name it — the first faint tremor of the thing that was coming for her, the way divers feel a storm swell hours before the boats do: a long, low pull in the water, far offshore, gathering.

* * *

Evening came down over the compound like a lid. The women queued for rations, ate, washed, and went to their cots, and the lamps were doused, and the compound settled into its nightly silence — a different silence from the daytime one, looser, fuller of murmurs and coughing and the sea's endless work against the shingle.

Paloma lay awake on her cot, as she often did, doing the arithmetic. Forty-one shells today, thirty-one credited. Four hundred and nine marks remaining. Eleven years, give or take the interest, give or take the levies, give or take the sea.

Eleven years. She would be thirty. She tried to imagine thirty and could not; the future refused to hold still for her, the way a face refuses to resolve underwater. This was the bargain she had made without ever signing anything: safety now, in increments, purchased with silence, purchased with the oyster left on the sand, purchased with Nena's eight stolen shells swallowed like seawater. It was not a noble bargain. She did not pretend it was. But it was hers, and it had kept her alive through seven years that had killed or broken better people than she was, and she clung to it the way a drowning woman clings to a spar — not because the spar is glorious, but because the alternative is the bottom.

Somewhere across the compound, a door banged. Voices — men's voices, unfamiliar ones, near the counting house. She registered them the way she registered everything, briefly and completely, and filed them away, and rolled toward the wall.

A stranger's voice, she thought, on a coast where strangers arrived only with the mail sloop or the magistrate's barge. A stranger, at night, asking directions to the counting house.

She closed her eyes. Whatever it was, it was none of her business. Nothing was her business except the beds and the tally and the long slow climb toward a freedom so distant it functioned mostly as a rumor.

She was very good at believing that. She had had seven years of practice.

But sleep, when it finally took her, was shallow and full of water, and somewhere in it — though she would not remember this in the morning — a voice she had spent seven years not listening to sang three notes of a song, and stopped.
