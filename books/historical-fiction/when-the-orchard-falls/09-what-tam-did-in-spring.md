## 9. What Tam Did in Spring

The frost came early that year, and it came mean.

Tam Corvane felt it first in his hands — the old break in his left knuckle aching the way it always did when the river dropped and the mud went hard as iron. He stood at the edge of the Corvane wharf at dawn, watching his breath hang over the black water, and told himself for the hundredth time that he had come back to Aldermoor for honest reasons. Winter work. The ferry chains needed greasing before the ice took them. His father needed help hauling the boats above the tideline. Simple things. Repairable things.

But honesty had never been the currency of this town, and Tam had spent most of his life paying in the other kind.

She was already at the shop when he passed it that morning — he made himself pass it, made himself look, because flinching away was a habit he refused to feed. The lamp burned behind the map shop's fogged window, and her shadow moved across it, bent over the great table, patient as a nun at prayer. Jude Ravensworth. Even her silhouette looked like an accusation.

Five years since he'd stood in the magistrate's court with his cap wrung in his fists and said the words that sent Nathaniel Ravensworth down the river and out of all their lives. Five years, and the words hadn't faded. They lived in him like a splinter worked too deep to draw — every winter it ached, every spring it festered, and no amount of salt water or hard labor ever brought it to the surface where he could pull it free.

He was still standing there like a fool when the shop door opened.

* * *

"You'll catch your death standing in the street," she said. Not a greeting. Not an invitation either. She had her shawl pulled tight and her chin up, and her eyes went through him the way a gimlet goes through green wood — clean, unimpressed, leaving a hole.

"I came about the chart," Tam said. It was true, as far as it went. Everything he'd said to her lately was true as far as it went, which was the whole trouble with him.

"Then come about it inside, or don't come about it at all. I won't discuss Crane's papers on my own doorstep like a woman selling eggs."

Inside, the shop smelled of ink and tallow and the ghost of pipe smoke that five years of scrubbing hadn't lifted from the rafters. Elias Crane's chair sat empty behind the counter, pushed in neat as a coffin lid. Tam had never been able to look at that chair without seeing the old man in it, quill behind his ear, saying *measure twice, boy, cut once* — advice that applied to maps and apparently not at all to lives.

Jude didn't offer him a seat. She stood on her side of the worktable with the lamp between them, and the light caught the ink stains on her fingers, three of them dark to the second knuckle. She'd been working since before dawn. Grieving, probably, though she'd have bitten him for saying so.

"The third checkpoint," she said. "You were going to tell me how the quay gate is watched."

"And I will." He turned his cap in his hands. The wool was worn thin at the brim; he could feel every thread of it. "But there's something I have to say first, and if I don't say it now I'll keep finding reasons not to."

Her face closed like a shutter. "If this is more of your apology—"

"It isn't an apology." He heard his own voice go flat and strange, the way it did when he was steering into rough water. "An apology asks for something. Forgiveness, or absolution, or just to be let off the hook. I'm not asking for any of those. I'm going to tell you what happened in the spring of '42, all of it, in order, and then you can do whatever you like with it. Hate me cleaner, if nothing else. Hating a man for what he actually did is better than hating him for a story."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out on the river a gull cried, thin and hungry, the sound of a world that didn't care what got confessed in riverside towns.

Jude folded her arms. "Say it, then."

* * *

"In the spring of '42 I was seventeen," Tam began, "and my father owed money to men you didn't want owing money to. Not Wren — worse than Wren, the ones behind Wren, though I didn't know that then. The ferry had a bad season. Ice took one boat, the tolls took the rest, and there was a debt of forty pounds sitting on us like a millstone, and the men who held it had already taken our second skiff as surety and were talking about taking the wharf.

"Wren sent for me. Me, a ferryman's son, seventeen years old, and the magistrate of the whole valley sends a clerk to say *his worship would like a word.* I thought — God help me, I thought it was about mooring fees. I walked up to that fine brick house on the hill with my heart going like a drum, and he gave me tea. Tea, in a cup so thin you could see through it. And he asked me about the Ravensworths."

Tam stopped. The words after this point were the ones he'd rehearsed a hundred times on the river at night, saying them to the water because the water didn't flinch, and they came out now the way they always did — too fast, like men escaping a burning building.

"He asked whether Nathaniel ran cargo after dark. Whether we ferried for him. Whether we'd ever landed goods at Miller's Stair below the high-water mark. And I told him no, because it was true — Nate kept his own counsel, ran his own landings, never trusted the ferry with anything. And Wren smiled at me the way a pike smiles, and he said, *that's a pity, boy, because I already know it's true, and the only question is whether your father's name appears on the writ alongside his.*

"They had papers. forged, I understand that now, but I was seventeen and they had wax seals and dates and my father's mark on a landing manifest from Miller's Stair — a night, a tide, a cargo of French brandy that never touched our boat. Forty pounds of debt on one side of the scale and my father swinging from a rope on the other, and Wren leaning back in his chair saying, *or there is another way. A son's testimony weighs lighter than a father's gallows. Tell me what you've seen, and the Ravensworth matter resolves itself quietly, and certain debts of yours might resolve themselves too.*

"I told him I'd seen nothing. He said think again. I came home and I didn't sleep, and my father — who knew nothing, who was innocent of everything except being poor — asked me why I looked like a drowned man, and I couldn't tell him, because telling him would have been the thing that killed him. And the next morning the clerk came again. And the next day. And on the fourth day Wren had my father's boat seized on some pretext about harbor dues, and I understood that the squeezing had started and it wouldn't stop until either I broke or my family did.

"So I broke."

The word cracked in the quiet shop like ice giving way underfoot.

"I went to Wren and I said what he'd written out for me. That I'd seen Nathaniel Ravensworth take delivery of untaxed goods at Miller's Stair. That I'd helped row them upriver. Every word of it a lie except the rowing, which I'd done once, two years before, for honest wages, moving legitimate timber — but it sounded true, that's the devil of it, it sounded exactly like the truth, because I was a waterman and I *had* rowed for Nate, and a lie built on a true bone stands up straight in court.

"And Nate —" Tam's voice went, and he let it go, and waited until he could trust it. "Nate never denied it. That's the part I couldn't understand for years, Jude, and maybe you can't either, but I've had five winters to turn it over. He stood in that dock and he looked at me — seventeen, shaking, sick with it — and he never once said *the boy is lying.* Because he knew. He'd known for months that Wren wanted the cutters broken and the route buried, and he knew that if he called me a liar the trial would widen, and the widening would reach my father, and the forged manifests were already sitting in Wren's drawer waiting for their moment. Nate took the exile to close the wound small. He chose where it would cut. I've hated myself every day since, and I will hate myself every day until I die, but I want you to know — I need you to know — that the man who informed on your brother was a boy with a knife at his family's throat, and the hand holding the knife belonged to the same man whose seal is stamped on Elias Crane's stolen pages."

Silence. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself.

Jude had not moved through any of it. She stood with her arms folded and her face white as fresh paper, and when she finally spoke, her voice was very quiet, which was infinitely worse than shouting.

"My mother died believing Nate was guilty."

"I know."

"She died asking him — in her letters, at the end, when she was past caring about pride — she wrote and asked him to tell her it wasn't true. And he wrote back, because he's Nate, and he said *it doesn't matter what's true, Mam, only what's bearable,* and she read that as a confession. She read those words as her son telling her yes. She died with it." Jude's arms tightened. "And you stood in that court and you watched her face while you said it. You watched her."

"Yes." There was no defense and he offered none. "I watched her."

"And Wren paid your father's debt."

"Wren tore up the debt. That was the payment. Forty pounds for a family's good name, and I signed the testimony, and he shook my hand in front of his clerk so I'd understand the bargain was witnessed." Tam set his cap on the table at last, because his hands had begun to shake and he didn't want her to see it. "I'm not telling you this to be forgiven. I told you that. I'm telling you because we're about to do something that could hang people, and you deserve to know exactly what sort of man is standing beside you when we do it. And because—" he stopped, started again, "because Wren made me a liar once by squeezing, and I have spent five years learning that the only thing a squeezed man owns is what he refuses to give up. So I refuse. Whatever comes at the quay gate, whatever he offers me or does to me — I refuse. That's the only thing I have that's worth anything, and I'm putting it on your table."

* * *

For a long moment Jude said nothing at all. She looked at him, and Tam endured it, because he had decided on the walk over that he would endure whatever she gave him — contempt, fury, the door, all of it — and that enduring it was the last honest act available to him.

When she spoke, her voice had steadied into something level and cold and terribly clear.

"I believe you."

His breath went out of him.

"Every word," she said. "That's the worst of it, Tam. I believe you, because I've read Crane's marginalia, and I've seen Wren's seal on pages that should never have carried it, and I know now what that man does to boys with debts and daughters with reputations and old mapmakers with dangerous knowledge. I believe you were a knife in someone else's hand." She unfolded her arms and laid them flat on the table, and her knuckles were white. "And it changes nothing."

"Jude—"

"No. Let me finish, because I've had five years too, and mine weren't spent rowing boats." Her eyes were bright and dry, which meant she was holding herself together with both hands. "You want me to hear that you were coerced and feel the hatred drain out of me, and I've sat here and listened and I *do* understand it, God rot me, I understand it completely, and the hatred hasn't moved. Do you know why? Because understanding isn't mercy. My brother understood too — Nate understood exactly what you were, even in that courtroom, even while you destroyed him, he understood and he took the weight to spare your father. And knowing that has never once made it hurt less. Understanding is just pain with better manners."

She came around the table slowly, and stopped an arm's length from him, close enough that he could see the ink stain at the corner of her jaw where she pushed her hair back with dirty fingers.

"So here is what I can give you, and it's more than I thought I'd ever give you, so don't ask for a farthing more. I believe you. I will stand beside you at the checkpoints, and if it comes to it I'll swear to your character in front of any court in the county, because it's true and because the truth is the only coin I deal in now. But forgiveness is a different country, Tam Corvane, and I don't live there. Maybe someday. Maybe when my brother is standing on English soil again. Maybe never. You'll get my trust before you get my pardon, and you'll earn neither cheaply, and if that bargain doesn't suit you, the door is behind you."

Tam looked at her for a long time. The lamp threw her shadow huge against the charts on the wall — coastlines, soundings, the careful hand of a dead man — and he thought that she was the most frightening and most decent thing this valley had produced in his lifetime.

"That suits me," he said. "It suits me fine."

"Then we're agreed." She turned back to the table, businesslike again, grief and mercy both locked away where he couldn't reach them, and perhaps where she couldn't either. "The quay gate. Tell me."

He told her. Four guards at the gate, two at the chain, a rotation at midnight and again at four. The customs officer who took bribes and the one who didn't. The gap in the patrol between the second bell and the tide, twenty minutes wide, closing fast once the freeze set the schedule in stone. She listened the way Crane used to listen — saying nothing, marking everything — and when he finished she was silent a moment, looking at the hidden chart spread under the lamp, the smugglers' cut drawn in her dead master's hand, and something in her expression shifted, some decision settling into place like a keel finding water.

"There's one more thing," Tam said, shrugging his coat back on. "You should hear it before you sleep, not after."

"Go on."

"Halloway's been asking after me. Down at the Anchor, through the landlord. Asking when the Corvane boy started drinking regular, who he talks to, whether he's been up the hill to see anyone." He met her eyes. "Wren's not the only one who knows how to squeeze, Jude. And I think Halloway already suspects what we're carrying — and I think he suspects it because someone's been talking. Someone close to this shop."

Jude went very still. Outside, the frost was thickening on the window glass, feather by feather, sealing the town in silence — and somewhere in that silence, Tam thought, a watcher was already awake.

"Lock your door tonight," he said. "All three bolts. And Jude — don't trust anyone who loved Elias Crane too loudly."

He let himself out into the blue dark, and behind him the lamp in the shop window burned on, small and stubborn, visible from half the street — to anyone who might be looking.
