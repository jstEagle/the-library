## 13. The Toll House Fire

Tam Corvane had set fires before. That was the shame of it — the ease with which his hands remembered the work.

He had been eleven the first time, burning stubble on his father's field while the ferryman drank himself blind in the house above the water. He had been fifteen when he burned a letter he should have kept, and seventeen when he burned the one thing that might have saved him: his own testimony, spoken aloud before Magistrate Wren and half of Aldermoor's respectable men, condemning a boy who had never done him harm. Words could burn too. He had learned that early. Everything Tam touched, it seemed, turned to ash eventually — his father's boat, his mother's patience, Jude Ravensworth's family, name by name by name.

So it was fitting, he thought as he crouched in the reeds below the toll house road, that fire would be the thing he offered now. Fitting and foul. A man pays his debts with whatever coin he has.

The toll house squatted at the narrow throat of the valley where the river road pinched between the water and the cliff, a low stone building with a slate roof and a lamp burning yellow in its single window. Two guards were quartered there for the winter — Tam had counted their comings and goings for three nights, learning their habits the way he'd once learned the moods of the tide. The older one, a heavy man with a red nose and a soldier's slouch, took the first watch and drank through most of it. The younger one — Pike, they called him, though Tam didn't know if that was name or nickname — was different. He read by the fire. He fed a stray dog scraps from his ration. Twice Tam had seen him stand at the door looking out at the dark river with an expression Tam recognized from his own mirror: the look of a young man far from anywhere he wanted to be.

*He's just a boy*, Tam thought, and hated himself for thinking it, because thinking it was a way of softening what he'd already decided to do.

The plan was simple, which is to say it was the kind of plan that only works until it doesn't. The toll house checkpoint stopped every soul on the river road after dusk; no cart, no foot passenger, no reason given or accepted. But a fire — a real fire, visible from the church checkpoint up the hill, loud enough to pull the second guard from his post — would empty the road for exactly as long as it took the town to remember itself. Jude needed minutes. Minutes to walk the ledger past the darkened toll house in the smoke and confusion, wrapped in oilcloth beneath her coat, moving like any frightened woman running toward a fire instead of away from it. No one questions a woman running toward a fire.

Tam had argued for doing it alone. Jude had refused with that flat, immovable look she'd learned from her dead master, and in the end they'd split the night between them: hers to carry, his to burn.

He checked the flask of lamp oil at his belt, the bundle of tarred rope and dry gorse tucked under his arm, and told himself, for perhaps the hundredth time, that no one would be hurt. The fire would take the wood store beside the toll house, not the building itself. The guards would smell the smoke, see the flames against the sky, and run for buckets and bells like every man in Aldermoor had run since childhood. It was theatre. It was noise. It was a lie told in flame, and lies, Tam knew better than anyone, always cost more than the liar budgets for.

Above him, on the ridge, the moon went behind cloud. He waited for it. He had promised himself he would wait for the dark, as if darkness made the act cleaner, though he knew it made nothing cleaner. Darkness only made things harder to see. That was rather the point.

He moved.

* * *

The wood store leaned against the toll house's eastern wall, stacked cordwood under a lean-to of old planks, and it took the fire greedily. Tam worked fast and without pleasure — oil soaked into the gaps between logs, the tarred rope coiled deep where it wouldn't show, the gorse piled to catch the first lick of flame and throw it upward. His hands did the work while his mind stood apart, watching, the way it had watched at seventeen when he opened his mouth in the magistrate's court and let Wren put his words in it.

*You're good at this*, said a voice in his head that sounded unpleasantly like his father's. *Always were. Good at the small betrayals.*

"This isn't betrayal," he whispered to the dark. "This is the opposite of it."

But his father's voice had never much cared for answers.

He struck the flint twice before it caught. The spark leapt into the gorse and the gorse flared up bright and sudden, a bloom of orange in the black, and Tam stepped back into the reeds with his heart going like a drum and watched the fire find its feet. It climbed the cordwood fast — faster than he'd reckoned, the wood bone-dry from months under cover, the wind off the river feeding it — and within a dozen breaths the whole lean-to was roaring, throwing sparks up the cliff face, painting the toll house window in shifting gold.

"Fire!" The older guard's voice, thick with drink and alarm. The door banged open. "Pike! Pike, wake up — the store's alight!"

Tam pressed himself flat in the reeds as the two men came stumbling out, buckets in hand, shouting. And here was the moment the plan required: the older guard running for the water butt, the younger one — Pike — hauling the alarm bell rope that would bring the church checkpoint's men down the hill, the road left empty behind them, the smoke rolling thick across the river road in a grey curtain.

It happened almost exactly as planned. Almost.

What the plan had not accounted for was the dog.

It came out of the toll house behind the guards, the stray, all legs and panic, and instead of fleeing it ran *toward* the burning store, barking at the flames as though they were an enemy it could drive off. And Pike — young Pike, reading-by-the-fire Pike, scraps-for-the-dog Pike — turned back for it.

"No," Tam breathed. "No, no —"

The boy got the dog by the scruff and hauled it clear, and he was nearly clear himself, one stride, two, when the lean-to roof came down.

It went all at once, the old planks surrendering together in a sheet of orange collapse, and a burning spar — no thicker than a broom handle, Tam would tell himself later, over and over, *no thicker than a broom handle* — swung out of the ruin and caught the boy across the shoulder and the side of the head. He went down without a cry. The dog stood over him screaming in its animal voice, and the older guard dropped his bucket and roared for help, and the night filled up with light and bell-sound and running feet, and Tam lay in the freezing reeds with his face in the mud and understood that the arithmetic had already failed. He had budgeted for noise and bought a wound instead. He had promised himself no one would be hurt, and someone lay unconscious twenty feet from him with blood on the stones, because of him, because of his hands and his flint and his long practice at setting the world alight.

They carried Pike inside. Voices swarmed — men from the church checkpoint coming down the hill, buckets passing hand to hand, someone crying for the surgeon, someone else crying that the fire would spread to the toll house roof, douse it, douse it, mind the wall. The road, for those few golden terrible minutes, was empty.

And through the smoke, small and swift and hooded, came Jude Ravensworth.

She didn't run, which was the brilliance of it; she walked, quick but unhurried, a woman drawn by disaster, her shawl pulled up, her eyes forward. She passed within ten feet of Tam's hiding place in the reeds. He saw her glance at the toll house door — saw her see, through the open doorway, the boy laid out on the table inside, the blood, the women bending over him — and he saw her step falter, just half a step, before she fixed her gaze on the middle distance and walked on, up the road, past the checkpoint post with its unattended lantern, into the dark beyond.

She didn't look back. She had promised him she wouldn't, and she didn't.

Tam stayed in the reeds until the fire was beaten down to smoking timber and the crowd began to thin, until the cold had worked so deep into his bones that his teeth chattered in time with the distant bell. Then he rose, mud to the eyebrows, and slipped away along the water path with the dog's cries still ringing in his ears and one image burned into him brighter than any fire: the boy on the table, young and far from home, undone by a stranger's war he'd never enlisted in.

*This is what your loyalty costs*, said his father's voice. *Other people's blood.*

For once, Tam couldn't answer.

* * *

Jude was waiting in the boathouse below the ferry steps, the one place in Aldermoor where lamplight wouldn't show at the windows. She had the ledger out of its oilcloth already, checking it by candle-stub, page by page, her fingers steady in the way he'd come to recognize as effort rather than calm — steadiness maintained the way one maintains a difficult note in singing.

"It's through," she said, not looking up. "The second checkpoint. One remains."

"I know."

"The quay gate at dawn. Halloway will expect us there, so we'll go where he isn't, and arrive where he does. Tam—" She looked up then, and the candlelight found her face, and whatever she saw in his must have stopped her, because she set the ledger down. "Tam. What happened?"

"There was a guard." His voice came out sanded flat. "Young one. He went back for a dog."

"And?"

"He's hurt. Bad, maybe. I don't know. They sent for the surgeon." He heard his own breathing, too quick. "I told myself no one would be hurt. I built the whole thing on that. Dry wood, wet night, fire away from the door — I measured everything, Jude, I measured it like a chart, and the chart was wrong, because charts don't account for a boy loving a dog."

Jude was quiet a long moment. In the candlelight her eyes were the colour of the river at dusk, and he braced for judgment — for the look she'd worn across three years of silence, the look that had named him traitor without a word spent on it.

But what she said was: "Did you mean for it to happen?"

"No."

"Then it wasn't murder. It was war, and war keeps its own accounts." She picked the ledger up again, holding it against her chest like something living. "Wren hurt that boy the day he sealed the valley and bribed his superiors and hanged four cutters on a false charge. We're just collecting the debt. It doesn't feel like justice — I know it doesn't. Justice never does at the time. It only looks like justice afterward, if it ever gets the chance to look like anything at all."

"That's a comfortable sermon."

"I'm not comfortable. I used a grieving widow this week as a shield and called it strategy." Her mouth twisted. "We're both stained, Tam. The question is whether the stain buys anything. Come dawn we'll know."

He wanted to believe her. He wanted it the way a drowning man wants air — desperately, uncritically, knowing the wanting itself was dangerous. He looked at her standing in the boathouse dark with Elias Crane's dead masterwork held to her heart, this woman whose family he had broken and whose cause he had somehow come to carry as if it were his own, and he thought: *if she can hold it, I can hold it. If she can walk past a bleeding boy without stopping, I can live with having put him there.*

Outside, the tide was turning. He could hear it in the river's changed voice, the low suck and shift of water deciding which way to go.

"We sleep two hours," Jude said. "No more. Then we sew the ledger into my coat, and you teach me the quay gate's rhythm — the changing of the watch, the gap you found. And Tam—"

"Aye?"

She hesitated. Whatever it was, she swallowed it, and what emerged instead was practical, clipped, her armour back in place: "Be at the fish cellar by four. Don't be late. After tomorrow none of this matters anymore — one way or the other."

She took the candle and went up the steps toward the town, and Tam stood alone in the boathouse listening to the tide turn, and it was only then, in the full silence, that he let himself say aloud the thing he'd been carrying since the reeds:

"He was just a boy. God forgive me. He was just a boy."

The river said nothing. Rivers never do. But far off across the water, where the burnt toll house smouldered and the surgeon's lantern burned late, a bell rang once — short, sharp, urgent — and Tam's blood went cold, because he knew that pattern. Every child in Aldermoor knew it.

It was the signal for a deathwatch.

Someone had died at the toll house tonight. And come morning, when the town asked how the fire started, Tam Corvane would discover whether the answer was written anywhere but in his own conscience — or whether Mr. Halloway, clever and patient Mr. Halloway, had already begun to read the ashes.
