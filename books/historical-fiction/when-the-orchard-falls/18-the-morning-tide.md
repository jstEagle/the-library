## 18. The Morning Tide

The fog came off the river before dawn, thick as wool, and Jude walked through it as though through a held breath.

She had sewn the ledger into her coat in the small hours, by candlelight, with stitches borrowed from the sampler she had once hidden a chart inside — the same running stitch, the same doubled thread. Her fingers had been steady then. They were not steady now. They trembled as she buttoned the coat to her throat, as she pulled her hood up, as she stood in the doorway of the shop and looked back at the dark interior where Elias Crane's atlas lay open on the bench, the blank final page waiting for a hand that would never write it.

*You would have hated this,* she thought to him. *Or you would have done exactly the same.*

The streets of Aldermoor were empty in the way streets are empty when everyone has been told to stay inside. Halloway's notices had gone up at dusk — plain paper, plain words: *The quay is closed by order of the Crown until further notice. All persons found upon it will be detained.* The town had read them and gone quiet, and the quiet was worse than the checkpoints, worse than the toll house fire still smouldering in the town's memory, worse than Wren's visit with its honeyed offer of pardon and position. A town that has learned to be afraid makes a particular silence, and Aldermoor made it now, and Jude walked through the middle of it with treason stitched against her ribs.

The river announced itself before she saw it — a smell of weed and tar and cold iron, then the sound, that low muscular lap of water against pilings that she had heard every day of her childhood and never once listened to until this winter. The fog thinned near the water. It always did; the river breathed it away. And through the parting grey she saw the *Kestrel* at her berth, riding low with cargo, her rigging furred with frost, her deck lamps burning small and orange like a row of held candles.

And she saw the men on the quay.

Six of them, maybe seven, in the King's grey, muskets shouldered, breath smoking. Halloway stood among them at the foot of the gangplank, and he was not pretending anymore. The polite surveyor with the visiting cards was gone. What remained was a tall man in a plain coat with his hands clasped behind his back, waiting, the way a man waits at a door he knows will open.

Jude stopped in the shadow of the last warehouse and made herself breathe.

*Tam should be here by now.*

That had been the plan, made in the orchard two nights past with frost in their hair and their voices low: Tam would go first, loud and visible, the known smuggler's son, the boy Wren had always said he was — he would give them a reason to look at him and not at a woman in a hooded coat walking the long way round by the salt sheds. He had not wanted her to come at all. She had not given him the choice.

*If I'm taken,* he had said, *you walk away. You don't stop. You don't turn round. You put that ledger on that ship if it costs you everything else.*

*And if you're taken, what do I cost?* she had asked, and had hated herself for asking, and had hated him more for not answering.

The fog moved. Somewhere behind the warehouses a dog began to bark and was silenced. Jude pressed her back against the cold boards and waited, counting her own heartbeats, and on the fortieth beat she heard what she had been listening for: voices at the far end of the quay, raised, angry, familiar.

Tam Corvane came out of the fog like a man walking out of a fire.

He was not sneaking. That was the terrible, magnificent thing about it — he came down the quay in full view of the soldiers, coat open, hands visible, calling out in a voice that carried over the water: "You'll want to search me, then! Go on! You'll want to search me properly, because I've come to sail on the *Kestrel*, and we all know what the *Kestrel* carries, don't we, lads? We all know what the Corvanes carry!"

The soldiers turned. All of them. Halloway turned last, and Jude saw his face in the lamplight — not surprised. Expectant. As if a door he had been waiting at had finally opened, and the wrong person stood behind it, and he was deciding whether that mattered.

"Master Corvane." Halloway's voice was mild as milk. "How good of you to deliver yourself."

"Deliver myself?" Tam laughed, and it was a good laugh, a real one, and Jude understood with a lurch that he had practised this, that he had stood somewhere alone in the dark rehearsing his own surrender so it would sound like defiance. "I've paid my toll and bought my passage same as anyone. Or is the quay closed to Aldermoor men now? Is the river closed? Because I don't recall the river being anyone's to close."

"Search him," Halloway said.

They did. Thoroughly, and in full view, turning out his pockets, running their hands along his seams, and Tam stood for it with his arms out and a sneer on his face that Jude knew was for her benefit — *look at me, look at me, not at her* — and there was nothing on him. Nothing but a knife, a purse, and a letter of passage signed with a name that was probably false. The soldiers stepped back. Halloway studied him the way he had once studied maps, and Jude, pressed against the warehouse wall with her heart hammering, understood that Halloway was not fooled. He was waiting. He had known the ledger would come to the quay this morning, and he had six muskets and all the time in the world, and Tam was the decoy and they both knew it.

"Take him to the toll house," Halloway said. "He can explain himself to the magistrate when the magistrate arrives."

"You'll have to carry me," Tam said pleasantly.

They took him by the arms, and he went, and at the last moment — Jude would remember this for the rest of her life, would wake some nights years later with it bright behind her eyes — he turned his head and looked directly at the shadow of the warehouse where she stood, and did not see her, or pretended not to see her, or saw her and gave the smallest shake of his head: *walk away, don't stop, don't turn round.*

She turned toward the ship instead.

It was madness and she knew it. Halloway was ten yards away with his back half turned and the gangplank unguarded for the length of time it takes a man to give an order, and Jude Ravensworth had spent her whole life being careful, being quiet, being the girl who inked coastlines and asked no questions, and she walked out of the shadow into the open with her hood up and her head down and her legs moving as if they belonged to someone braver, and the fog closed behind her, and no one shouted.

The gangplank groaned under her feet. The *Kestrel* rose and fell beneath it, patient, alive. At the top a deckhand — a woman, broad as a barrel, with rope burns across her palms — looked at Jude's hood, at her face beneath it, and said nothing, and stepped aside.

"The captain," Jude whispered. "I have to speak to the captain. It's about the cutters. The ones they hanged. It's about the ones they hanged."

The woman's face did not change. But her eyes did — something moved behind them, some old grief or old anger, and she jerked her chin toward the stern. "Aft cabin. And walk soft. He's in one of his moods."

Jude had taken four steps along the deck when the fog behind her said, in a mild and terrible voice:

"Miss Ravensworth."

* * *

She did not turn at once. That was the strange thing — her body simply refused, some animal part of her insisting that if she did not turn, it was not happening, that she was still a woman walking toward a ship's cabin on ordinary business. Then she turned, and Halloway was at the foot of the gangplank with two soldiers flanking him, and his hands were still clasped behind his back, and he was smiling the way winter smiles.

"You'll forgive the informality," he said, mounting the plank. "But I find I've grown rather fond of you this winter, and I should hate for this to be done by strangers."

The deckhand had melted away. The soldiers came up behind Halloway, muskets in their hands now, not shouldered. The quay below was emptying of everything but fog. Somewhere in the town, a bell was ringing — the toll house bell, Tam's fire or Tam's arrest or both, she could not know — and the sound came flat across the water and died.

"Mr. Halloway," Jude said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "You're standing on private deck."

"I'm standing on Crown business." He stopped an arm's length from her, close enough that she could see the frost melting on the shoulders of his coat, close enough to smell the ink on him — surveyor's ink, the same iron-gall smell that lived in her own fingers. "I'm going to ask you once, and then I'm going to ask the soldiers to ask you, and they ask with their hands. Where is Crane's ledger?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," he said softly. "I don't suppose you do. You've been very good at not knowing all winter. It's the family talent, isn't it? Your brother didn't know anything either, when they asked him. Your master didn't know anything, right up until the night he ran." He tilted his head. "You know he ran. You've known since I told you. Old men don't die on the London road with their boots off, Miss Ravensworth. They die running from men like the men standing behind me."

The ledger burned against her ribs. She thought of Tam at the toll house, being carried away, buying her this — these minutes, this fog, this conversation she was losing. She thought of Wren in her shop, offering her brother's pardon like a man offering a sweet. She thought of the widow, kneeling that morning among her husband's papers, saying *let it cost what it costs.*

"Search me, then," Jude said. "Since your men ask with their hands."

"Oh, I will." He sighed, almost regretful. "But not here, and not quickly, and not before the ship sails, because the ship is not sailing. The *Kestrel* is detained. Her cargo is detained. Her captain will be detained the moment he shows his face, and he will show his face, because captains always do." He smiled again. "You've run a very long race, Miss Ravensworth. But you've run it into a cul-de-sac, and the only question left is whether you make it painful for both of us."

And that was when the cabin door opened, and Captain Merrow came out.

She had imagined him, in the fever of the last two days, as some greybeard smuggler out of Crane's stories — scarred, sly, half-legend. He was none of that. He was a short, broad man of perhaps fifty with a face like a knot in oak and hands like mooring rope, and he came out of his cabin pulling on a coat and looked at Halloway the way a man looks at weather he has sailed through before.

"Mr. Halloway," he said. "Still chasing other men's work, I see."

"Captain Merrow." Halloway's smile thinned. "The *Kestrel* is detained by order of the Crown."

"The Crown detains a lot of things." Merrow came down the deck slowly, and Jude saw that the deckhand had reappeared at the rail, and that two more shapes had come up out of the forecastle, and that the fog on the quay below was not as empty as it had been — dark figures at the foot of the gangplank, four of them, five, dockmen with boat hooks and barge poles, standing in a loose, easy line that was not a mob and not quite not one. "Crown detains cargoes. Crown detains papers. Crown even detains cutters, sometimes. Six of them, wasn't it? Six cutters, hanged at Aldermoor quay on a cold morning very like this one, on the word of a magistrate who'd sold their route out from under them." Merrow stopped, and looked at Halloway, and his voice did not rise at all. "I knew those men, Mr. Halloway. I carried their goods for eleven years. I carried their letters. I carried one of their widows to Falmouth when she couldn't bear to stay. And I have waited a long time for the morning when what was done to them could be said out loud on this quay."

"This is sedition," Halloway said.

"This is arithmetic," said Merrow. "Count my crew. Count yours. Count the men on my gangplank, who are not on your payroll, and who have long memories and short tempers." He turned his head and looked at Jude — looked *through* the hood, at her, and there was nothing soft in his face but there was something steady, something that had been waiting for her without knowing her name. "You're Crane's girl. He wrote to me about you. Last letter he ever sent, he said: *if anything happens to me, there's a girl in Aldermoor who finishes what she starts.*" He held out one rope-ruined hand. "So. Do you start, or don't you?"

Jude's hands went to her coat buttons, and they were not shaking anymore.

She unpicked the stitches she had sewn herself, there on the deck of the *Kestrel*, in front of Halloway and his muskets, and she drew the ledger out from against her ribs — Elias Crane's true final page, the surveyor's original, Wren's treason in ink and figures and a seal — and she put it into Captain Merrow's hand.

"Take it to London," she said. "Not to the Admiralty — they're bought. Take it to the Commissioners of Inquiry. Take it to the newspapers if the Commissioners are bought too. Take it anywhere it will be read by someone who didn't hang those men."

"Miss Ravensworth—" Halloway began, and made a motion, and the soldiers made a motion, and the deckhand stepped off the rail with a boathook in her fist, and the men at the gangplank came up it, and for one long moment the whole quay was a held breath, muskets and boat hooks and fog and frost, the river slapping at the pilings below like a slow heart.

Halloway looked at the arithmetic. Jude watched him do it — watched him count, watched him arrive at the sum, watched the surveyor in him measure the ground and find it against him. His hands unclasped. He stepped back.

"You understand," he said quietly, to her, as the ledger vanished into Merrow's coat, "that this doesn't end it. Wren will fall, perhaps. And someone will be sent in his place, and that someone will be me or someone like me, and the valley will be sealed again, and the maps will be drawn the way the Crown likes them drawn. You've won a morning, Miss Ravensworth. Mornings end."

"I know," Jude said. "I've read the tide tables."

Something crossed his face then that was almost, almost respect. He turned, and went down the gangplank, and his soldiers went with him, and the dockmen let them pass with a silence more eloquent than jeers, and the fog took them.

"Cast off," said Captain Merrow.

* * *

They sailed on the tide, as the sky went from black to iron to a thin, cold gold.

Jude stood at the stern rail and watched Aldermoor come apart into fog. The church tower went first, then the warehouses, then the quay where Tam had been carried away in irons an hour before, then the last dark line of the valley hills, and then there was only the river, wide and grey and moving, carrying her away from the town that had made her and the man she had — she did not have a word for what Tam Corvane was to her, and suspected she never would, and suspected that was its own kind of honesty.

"You'll want to come back this way in spring," Merrow said beside her. He had appeared without sound, the way sailors do. "When the Commissioners are done, if they're honest, you'll want to see the faces in the town when the truth comes home."

"I don't know if I'll be welcome in it."

"Welcome's overrated." He handed her something — a mug of something hot and bitter, ship's coffee, thick as mud. "Crane wrote that about you too. Said you'd rather be right than comfortable. Said it like a complaint." The old man almost smiled. "Sounded like a eulogy to me."

Jude drank the coffee and watched the river, and the triumph she had imagined — the soaring, the relief — did not come. What came instead was a great quiet tiredness, and beneath it a grief with no name: for Crane, who had died running; for the six cutters who had died waiting; for the widow who had given her name to the fire; for Tam, in a cell at the toll house, who had walked into chains so a book could board a ship. The ledger was safe. The truth was at sea, moving toward the light. And it felt, standing in the cold gold morning with the town dissolving behind her, less like a victory than like a funeral that had finally, finally been held.

The *Kestrel* ran with the tide, and Aldermoor fell away behind her, and Jude Ravensworth did not know — could not know — that in the town she was leaving, the toll house bell was still ringing, and that by noon the whole valley would know what had sailed, and that Magistrate Wren, hearing of it in his own hallway, would go very still, and pour himself a glass of wine, and begin, with the calm of a man who has survived worse, to bury what could still be buried.
