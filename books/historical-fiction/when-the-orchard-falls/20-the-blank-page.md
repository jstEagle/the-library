## 20. The Blank Page

The printing house on Candlewick Street smelled of hot lead and lamp oil and the particular sweetness of paper stacked too long in a damp room — a smell Jude had come to think of as the smell of the future being made, sheet by sheet, whether anyone was ready for it or not.

She had been in London eleven days. Long enough for the bruise of Aldermoor to fade from vivid purple to something duller, something she could press a thumb against without flinching. Long enough for her hands to stop smelling of the river. Not long enough — she suspected it would never be long enough — for her to stop waking at dawn with the tide in her ears, listening for a ship that had already sailed.

Mr. Quillfeather's printing house occupied the ground floor of a narrow building that leaned, very slightly, toward its neighbour, as though after forty years of pressing words into paper it had grown tired of standing straight. Through the fogged windows she could see the great iron press, black and patient as a beetle, and men moving around it with the unhurried competence of people who made their living turning other minds into ink.

She stood outside for a long while before going in.

In her satchel, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the atlas. Elias Crane's atlas. *A Compleat Survey of the Southern Coasts and Inland Waters*, begun in 1719 and finished — though *finished* was a word she still could not say without tasting iron — in the winter of 1747, by hands that were not his.

By hers.

*You'll take it to Quillfeather,* the widow had said, pressing the parcel into her arms on the quay, after the ship had gone out with the tide and taken the ledger and the proof of everything with it. *He owed Elias a debt from the old days. He'll do it right.* And then, quieter, with the look of a woman who had spent a lifetime reading other people's silences: *And you'll decide what name goes on it. That decision is yours now. He'd have wanted it that way, even if he never said so.*

Jude had thought, on the journey down from Aldermoor — three days by carrier's cart, watching the valley fold itself away behind the hills like a letter being closed — that she knew what she would do. She had rehearsed it. She would tell Quillfeather the truth: that the atlas was Crane's design but her completion, her inking, her four years of labour over coastlines and soundings and the small patient work of making another mind legible to the world. She would say it plainly. She had earned the saying of it.

But plans are things we make about the people we were, not the people we become, and somewhere between the last checkpoint and the sea, between Tam's arrest and Wren's fall, between the town that had whispered and the town that now applauded, she had stopped being the girl who needed her name on things.

She went inside.

* * *

Quillfeather was smaller than his reputation. A round man, bald as an egg, with ink-stained fingers he made no attempt to hide and spectacles that sat low on his nose so that he looked at the world over them rather than through it, as though most of what the world offered wasn't worth the trouble of proper focus. When Jude unwrapped the atlas on his scarred oak table, he put those spectacles on properly, and she understood at once that she was seeing him work.

He turned the pages slowly. His breath came shorter at the coastal charts. At the survey of the Aldermoor cut — the smugglers' route, drawn now clean and honest, stripped of its secrets because the secrets no longer needed keeping — he stopped entirely, and took off his spectacles, and polished them on his sleeve for a longer time than spectacles require.

"Crane," he said at last. "Elias Crane. I corresponded with him twenty years ago. He wrote the finest hand I ever set type against." He looked up at her over the spectacles. "This is finished."

"Yes."

"I mean—" He tapped the page. "I mean it is *finished*. The later charts. The inland waters. This is not his hand."

Jude had prepared for this. She had prepared a dozen versions of it on the road, arguments and confessions and half-truths arranged like tools on a bench. What came out instead was simply the truth, worn smooth by the journey:

"No. It isn't."

Quillfeather waited. He was, she would learn, a man who understood that silence was also a kind of press — that if you held it steady over a person long enough, they gave up their shape.

"The master died in October," Jude said. "Before the work was done. I completed it. The final surveys, the inking, the—everything after the Exe estuary is mine."

"And your name?"

"Is not on it."

He studied her. Outside, a cart rattled past, and somewhere in the back of the house an apprentice dropped something metal and swore with the creativity of the young.

"Miss Ravensworth," Quillfeather said — she had given him her name; there was no hiding from a printer, whose whole trade was names — "an atlas of this quality will be reviewed. Discussed. There are men at the Royal Society who will want to know who drew the Saxon Shore chart, because it is better than anything the Admiralty has produced in a decade. Names get found out. They always do. Would you rather we found you, or invented someone?"

"You may invent no one," Jude said. "Let it be Crane's alone. Let it be his finished work, published posthumously, completed—" she paused, and chose the word carefully, and felt it settle into place like a keystone — "*under his direction.*"

"That's a lie."

"It's a dedication."

Quillfeather took off his spectacles again. He looked at her for a long moment, and then he did something she did not expect: he laughed, short and dry, without much humour in it but with a great deal of recognition.

"I've been in this trade thirty years," he said. "Do you know how many people have stood where you're standing and begged me to print their name larger? To make it red? To put it on the spine and the title page and every third page besides?" He laid one ink-stained finger flat on the atlas. "And here you are, asking me to make yours smaller than small. To erase it entirely." He shook his head slowly. "Either you're the humblest soul in England or the wisest, and in my experience the two look identical from behind a counter."

"It isn't humility," Jude said. And then, because he had earned it — because he was about to give her gift permanence, which is the only kind of gift that matters — she told him some of it. Not all. Never all. But enough: the hidden pocket in the spine. The chart that shouldn't exist. The magistrate, the hanged cutters, the ledger that went to sea. Enough that Quillfeather's face went grey and he sat down heavily on his stool and said, "Christ's bones, girl," in the voice of a man revising his entire understanding of a quiet river town.

"Why hide yourself?" he asked finally. "After all that. After you *won*. Why give him even the shadow of credit for pages he never saw?"

Jude looked at the atlas open on the table. At the coastline running along the bottom of the page, true and complete, every inlet named, every sandbar marked. At the margin, where Crane's notes marched in his beautiful hand — and beside them, in places, her own smaller script, correcting his soundings, finishing his sentences. Two hands on every page. One dead, one living, and only one of them about to be remembered.

"Because I finally understand why he hid his work," she said. "All those years. Everyone in Aldermoor thought Master Crane was a modest man, a careful man, a man who kept his best charts locked away out of caution or eccentricity. But that wasn't it." She touched the edge of the page, where the paper was softest, worn by handling. "He hid it because a name is a handle. You put your name on a thing and anyone who wants to move the thing takes hold of the name. Wren couldn't hang a chart. He could hang a cartographer. Halloway couldn't burn an atlas, but he could ruin the woman who signed it." She met Quillfeather's eyes. "Crane spent his whole life making himself weightless so the work could be heavy. I'm doing the same. Let the atlas carry everything. Let me carry nothing."

Quillfeather was quiet for a long time. Then he rose, and fetched a fresh sheet of paper, and dipped his pen, and wrote at the top of it in letters large enough to read across the room:

*A Compleat Survey of the Southern Coasts and Inland Waters. By Elias Crane, of Aldermoor. Published from the author's final materials.*

"Final materials," he said. "That's as close to honest as a title page gets. It claims nothing and conceals nothing. Anyone who looks will see two hands in the plates — good printers always see — and anyone who asks will be told the author directed the work to its end, which is true, because the dead direct us more thoroughly than the living ever manage to." He glanced up. "Will that serve?"

Jude found, unexpectedly, that her throat was tight.

"It will serve," she said.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon. Quillfeather was a tyrant of detail and Jude discovered she loved him for it — loved the way he argued about the weight of paper as though lives depended on it (perhaps they did; perhaps they always had), the way he rejected three of her plates for smudging she couldn't even see, the way he handled each original drawing as though it might break, which nothing else in his rough trade seemed to merit.

It was near dusk when he paused over the last leaf.

Every atlas has a final page, and Crane's had always been blank — a leaf of good paper left empty at the end, as mapmakers sometimes do, either for future additions or from superstition or simply because paper was bought in quires and one leaf must fall where it falls. Jude had inked nothing there. Four years of work, and she had left the last page white.

Quillfeather tapped it. "Do we bind this in? Most would cut it for waste."

"Leave it."

"A blank page at the end of a survey? Reviewers will call it an error."

"Call it what you like in the prospectus," Jude said. "But leave the page."

He raised his eyebrows, but he was a man who had learned, over thirty years, when an author's oddness was load-bearing. "May I ask why?"

Jude considered the question. She thought about Crane's shop in the weeks after his death, the dust settling on the instruments, the light moving across the wall the way it had moved when he was alive to complain about it. She thought about the hidden pocket in the spine, and the chart folded inside it, and how a secret is just a truth that hasn't found its hour yet. She thought about the morning tide, and Tam's face through the quay gate bars as they led him away — not afraid, not sorry, *certain*, in a way she was still learning how to be. She thought about her brother, somewhere beyond the sea, alive because a ledger had crossed three checkpoints sewn into a coat, and coming home in the spring whether she was ready or not.

"Because a map is never finished," she said. "That's the first thing Master Crane taught me. Every chart is a lie we agree to believe until the coastline changes — and the coastline always changes. Rivers move. Sands shift. Towns burn and rebuild and forget. A finished map is a small arrogance." She touched the blank leaf. "He left this page empty on purpose. I'm sure of it now. It's the only honest thing in the whole book. It says: *here ends what we know. Begin.*"

Quillfeather wrote something in his jobbing book. When she craned to see, it read: *bind all leaves per instruction. The client understands the work.*

The client. She almost laughed. After everything — the checkpoints, the fire at the toll house, the magistrate's courteous voice in her doorway offering her brother's pardon for her silence — to be reduced, safely, mercifully, to *the client*. Anonymity, she was discovering, was not a wound. It was a door, and she had walked through it, and on the other side the air was astonishingly clear.

* * *

She stayed to watch the first forme set. The compositor — a silent man with forearms like hawsers — picked the type letter by letter, upside down and backwards, building Crane's name in mirror-writing that would come out right in the press. Jude watched *ELIAS CRANE* assemble itself in reverse and thought: this is how memory works. Everything true arrives backwards, and only the printing makes it legible.

"First pull tomorrow, if the ink behaves," Quillfeather said. "You'll want to see it?"

"No," Jude said, and surprised herself with the ease of it. "Print it. Send the first copy to the vicarage at Aldermoor, to Mrs. Harrow. She'll know what to do with it."

"And the rest?"

"Let them find their own way. Maps do."

She gathered her satchel. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the press standing in the lamplight — the great iron beetle, patient, indifferent, ready to bite Crane's name into a thousand sheets that would outlive them all.

"Mr. Quillfeather. If anyone ever asks — and someone will, someday, some scholar with too much time — who finished the atlas. What will you tell them?"

Quillfeather polished his spectacles, and looked at her over them, and she saw that he had understood her completely and would keep the understanding the way he kept everything: in type, unspoken, durable.

"I'll tell them no one," he said. "No one finished it. It finished itself, the way rivers find the sea." A pause. "Unless you change your mind."

"I won't."

Outside, London was lighting its lamps, one window at a time, a coastline of small fires against the dark. Jude pulled her cloak close and walked into it, feeling lighter than she had since October — lighter, in fact, than she had since she was a girl, before the testimony, before the exile, before any of it. Erasure, she had learned, comes in two kinds: the kind that is done to you, and the kind you choose. The first is a grave. The second is a garden. She had spent her life in the first kind. She intended, from here forward, to live in the second.

She was halfway to her lodging when she heard her name called — her real name, spoken aloud in a city where no one should have known it — and turned, heart hammering, to see a boy running toward her through the lamplight with a letter in his fist, salt-stained and creased, postmarked from across the water.

The handwriting on the front was her brother's.
