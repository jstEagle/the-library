## 7. Two Maps, One Valley

Edith Vane had been drafting maps since she was nine years old, and in all that time she had learned one iron law of the profession: never share a table with another cartographer unless you were prepared to be judged.

The company had given them the old customs house on the mill road — a room with one long window facing the orchards, two desks pushed together into a single working surface because Captain Vane's quartermaster could not be bothered to find a second. So there they sat, shoulder to shoulder, morning after morning: Edith with her company-issued instruments and her company-issued conscience, and the master's apprentice — Blackwood, that was the name, Felix Blackwood — with a hand like a signature and eyes that missed nothing.

It was maddening.

Not the sharing itself. Edith had shared tables before, at the survey office in Corvane, with men who chewed tobacco and breathed through their mouths and drew rivers as if the rivers owed them money. She could endure bad habits. What she could not endure was *good* work.

Blackwood's line work was exquisite. That was the trouble. Every stroke carried weight exactly where it should — firm on the ridge lines, feathered at the floodplain, confident without being showy. It was the kind of hand you only got from years under a demanding teacher, and it was also, unmistakably, *familiar*. Edith had spent three nights turning that familiarity over in her mind like a coin she couldn't quite read, and on the fourth night she'd sat up in bed with the answer arriving whole:

She had seen that hand before. In the margins of Aldous Merrick's published county atlas, in the little decorative flourishes around the compass roses — the way the north point curled like a fern frond unfurling. She had copied those flourishes herself as a girl, practicing them by candlelight until her father told her to stop wasting candles on pretty nonsense.

Merrick's apprentice. Of course. The boy who had drawn the prettiest compass rose in three counties.

Except the boy had a way of sitting that no boy she'd ever met possessed — shoulders drawn slightly inward, always aware of the space his body occupied, adjusting his coat when he reached across the table in a manner that suggested the coat was a costume he hadn't finished learning to wear. And except that when Blackwood looked at the orchard valley, he didn't look at it the way a cartographer looks at terrain. He looked at it the way a person looks at a wound.

Edith kept this knowledge folded up inside her like a letter she wasn't ready to post. There would be a time for it. Or perhaps there wouldn't. She found, to her considerable irritation, that she rather liked not knowing yet.

* * *

The official map was called *Survey of the Aldermoor Valley, Prepared for the Company Road Commission*, and it was a lie from its border inward.

Not an obvious lie. Edith was too good at her work for obvious lies, and besides, the valley was genuinely there — the river genuinely bent where it bent, the mill genuinely stood where it stood. The lie lived in the details. The cluster of farmsteads along the eastern slope was rendered as "abandoned holdings." The scorch-darkened section of orchard was marked "blight damage, 1844." And the low ground near the mill, where the grass grew strangely lush and the farmers crossed themselves when they passed, appeared on the map as nothing at all — blank green, innocent as a child's drawing of summer.

"Your blight notation," Blackwood said on the sixth morning, tapping the map with a pencil worn down to a stub. "Blight doesn't burn in straight lines."

"Blight damage per the company assessment," Edith said pleasantly. "I don't write the assessments. I draw them."

"You're drawing them right now."

"I am." She kept her eyes on her work. "And I'm drawing them very accurately, considering."

Blackwood made a sound — half laugh, half exhale — and went back to the river bend. They worked in silence for a while, the good kind of silence, the kind Edith usually only found alone. Then, without looking up, Blackwood said, "Your contour intervals are inconsistent between the third and fourth sheets."

Edith's pencil stopped.

They were not. She checked them twice nightly. She checked them again now, heart thudding with something that was absolutely not alarm, and found them perfect — which meant Blackwood had invented the error, which meant Blackwood wanted her attention, which meant—

Which meant the apprentice was playing a game, and had just served the first ball over the net.

"I'll look into it," Edith said gravely. "Thank you for flagging the discrepancy."

"Don't mention it." A pause. "Also your scale bar is charmingly crooked."

"It is *hand-drawn*."

"Mm. Charmingly."

Edith bit down on a smile so hard it nearly hurt. She had not been charmed by anyone in four years, and she resented it immediately and completely.

* * *

By the second week they had developed a language.

It had no words, or almost none. It lived in the margins of the working drafts — the rough sheets that would be destroyed once the fair copy was made, or so the regulations claimed, though everyone knew the company kept everything, filed everything, buried everything in the archive vaults like bodies in a peat bog.

It began, Edith thought later, with the church.

The parish church of Aldermoor stood on the map, small and correct, with its bell tower noted at forty-one feet. But the churchyard — the churchyard was wrong. The official survey showed six grave plots. Edith had walked the ground herself; there were six stones visible, yes, but the earth told a different story. The ground sank in rows beyond the wall, out toward the orchard fence, in the pattern ground sinks when it has been opened often and filled hastily.

She drew the true extent of the churchyard into her draft — a small extension of the boundary line, unremarkable, the sort of thing any careful surveyor might add — and beside it, in her smallest annotation hand, she wrote: *Ground disturbance suggests additional interments. Recommend investigation.*

It was a test. She told herself it was a test of Blackwood's professional honesty, nothing more. If the apprentice reported the note to Captain Vane, Edith would know whose side the famous Merrick training actually served. If the apprentice ignored it, she would know the boy was merely timid.

What happened instead was this: the next morning, the note was still there, but beneath it, in a hand so fine it seemed embarrassed to exist, someone had written: *Investigation already conducted, 1845. Findings suppressed. See boundary dispute file, Aldermoor parish, ref. 12-C.*

Edith read it four times. Then she took out her eraser, removed both annotations with the tenderness of a woman cleaning a wound, and redrew the churchyard boundary at its true extent anyway — silently, permanently, in the ink layer that would survive every rough-copy purge.

That evening, when Blackwood gathered the rough sheets for filing, Edith watched him pause at the churchyard sheet. He looked at the corrected line. He looked at her. His face did something complicated and then settled into perfect professional blankness.

"Nice catch on the churchyard," he said.

"I do my best."

* * *

After that, the margins became a correspondence.

Nothing dangerous — not at first. A correction here, a query there, all of it dressed in the impeccable grammar of survey practice. *Elevation reading at the mill race appears ten feet high.* Answered: *Verified against Merrick's 1846 level notes. Your instrument runs hot in cold weather; try shading the bubble vial.* *The ford at Willow Bend is mislabeled seasonal.* Answered: *It is seasonal. The season is approximately eleven months long. I have adjusted the label to 'impassable most of the year,' which the commission may find less useful but is at least honest.*

Edith began looking forward to mornings. This was a problem. She had arranged her entire adult life so that she looked forward to nothing, because looking forward to things gave other people leverage, and she had been raised by a father and a brother who understood leverage the way sharks understand blood. Yet there she was, waking early, dressing quickly, walking to the customs house in the blue dark with her breath steaming, because somewhere in last night's rough copies there might be a note waiting for her.

The notes got braver. One morning she found, sketched tiny in the corner of a field sheet, a perfect little drawing of a wren perched on a fencepost — rendered with such affection that it could only be a portrait, not a study. Beneath it: *Common in this district. Uncommonly opinionated.*

Edith wrote back: *So I've heard. The whole household sings its praises.* And then, because she was apparently determined to ruin herself: *You've settled in well, I think. Mrs. Calloway feeds you properly?*

The reply came the next day, and it was just one line, but Edith read it a dozen times: *Better than I have eaten since my master died. They are kind people. You would like them, if you let yourself.*

*I don't let myself,* she nearly wrote back. Instead she wrote: *My brother says kindness is a currency and the Calloways are spending beyond their means.* And beneath that, after a long moment, smaller: *He would say that. He's never been poor a day in his life.*

She erased that last part. Then she redrew it. Then she erased it again. The sheet went into the file with only the first exchange intact, and she hated herself a little, and worked anyway.

* * *

On the twelfth day of their collaboration, Captain Vane came to inspect the progress.

He did it the way he did everything — loudly, filling the doorway first with his shoulders and then with his voice, asking questions he already knew the answers to and answering questions no one had asked. He leaned over the fair copy for a long time, and Edith watched her brother's face the way she had learned to watch it as a child: tracking the weather behind his eyes.

"This is good work," he said finally, which from Ambrose was not praise but inventory. "The commission will be pleased. Clean valley. Nothing in it that shouldn't be."

*Nothing in it that shouldn't be.* Edith felt the words land on the map like frost.

"The valley is what it is, Captain," Blackwood said evenly.

"The valley is what we say it is," Vane said, with a smile that had never once reached past his teeth. "That's what maps are for." He clapped Blackwood on the shoulder — Edith saw the apprentice go rigid under the touch, saw the flinch mastered in half a heartbeat, and wondered how many other people had noticed that Blackwood could not bear to be touched — and swept out to terrorize the quartermaster about nails.

The silence he left behind had a ringing quality.

"He believes that," Blackwood said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Ambrose believes whatever is most convenient at the moment. At present, convenience requires a clean valley." Edith aligned her pencils with unnecessary precision. "Belief is flexible. Paper isn't. That's why he wants yours burned and ours kept."

Blackwood turned to look at her fully for the first time in days, and Edith understood she had said far more than she intended — that she had just told the master's apprentice, in plain terms, that she knew about the burning, that she knew what the company did to records it disliked, that she was telling him this while standing inside the machine that did it.

"Why are you telling me that?" Blackwood asked.

Because you draw wrens with love in your hand. Because you flinch when touched and I want to know who hurt you. Because I falsified a boundary line in Corvane four years ago and a family lost their grazing rights and I have thought about it every single day since and nobody knows, nobody has ever known, and the secret is a stone in my chest and you are the first person I have met who might understand why it's heavy.

"Professional courtesy," Edith said. "Between rival surveys."

Something moved through Blackwood's expression — disappointment, maybe, or relief, or both wearing each other's coats. "Of course," the apprentice said. "Courtesy."

They worked until dusk. When Blackwood left, Edith stayed behind with a lamp and the day's rough sheets, and in the margin of the orchard sheet, next to the neat notation *blight damage, 1844*, she drew — very small, very fine, deniable to anyone but a trained eye — a single scorched apple tree, and beneath it, in annotation script: *Trees do not burn in patterns. People do.*

Then she sat for a long time, lamp guttering, listening to the river move in the dark, and did not erase it.

* * *

The reply came two mornings later, and it was not a correction.

It was a full page — the longest entry yet — written in that fine, guarded hand, tucked into the margin of the mill sheet where no clerk would ever think to look. Edith read it standing, then sitting, then standing again, because her legs would not decide.

*You asked once why I stay at the Calloway house when I could have crossed the bridge before it closed. Here is the answer I could not give. On the eleventh of October, 1844, something happened in this valley that the company's map calls blight. I have spoken with people who remember it. I am going to put what they remember into this atlas — into the contours, the boundaries, the marginalia — where it cannot be erased without destroying the map itself. I am telling you this because you keep correcting my errors honestly even when honesty costs you, and because yesterday you drew a burning tree in a company document, which is either madness or courage, and I have decided to believe it is courage.*

*I am not asking you to help me. I am asking you to look away at the right moments. That is all. That is already more than most people would risk.*

*— F.B.*

Edith read it until the words wore grooves in her. Then she took out her pen, and her hand — steady through every exam, every inspection, every one of her brother's interrogations of her loyalty — shook.

She thought of Corvane. Of the Hollis family and their lost grazing land, of the boundary line she had moved because her supervisor told her to and she had wanted to keep her position, of the letter she'd received a year later from a woman named Hollis thanking her — *thanking her* — for the fairness of the hearing, because the woman had never known the map itself was the knife. Edith had kept that letter. She kept it in her instrument case, folded small, and she took it out sometimes at night like a person pressing on a bruise to check whether it still hurt.

It still hurt.

She dipped her pen. In the margin beneath F.B.'s page, she wrote:

*I will not look away. Look away is how the Hollis field happened. I will do better than that, if you'll let me, and I will explain someday what I mean by Hollis field. Not on paper. Paper is theirs.*

*But hear this now, and believe it: I know what my brother's road is built on. I have suspected since the day we arrived, and I confirmed it Tuesday, and I have been sick about it since. Whatever you are building in these margins — build it well. Build it so that a trained eye cannot miss it and a careless one cannot find it. That is the craft we share, you and I. Let us see whose map survives.*

*— E.V.*

She blotted it, filed the sheet among thirty others, closed the vault-style portfolio, and walked home through the falling snow with her heart going like a drum.

At the Calloway gate she paused. Through the kitchen window she could see lamplight, and figures moving — the old man Tam in his chair, the mother laughing at something, Wren Calloway with her sleeves rolled, and Blackwood among them, leaning toward the fire, wearing that borrowed name like a coat that finally, tonight, fit.

Edith stood in the snow a moment longer than was sensible.

Then she went inside to write her evening report to her brother — accurate in every particular, complete in every detail, and silent as a held breath about everything that mattered — and it was while she was sealing it that Wren Calloway knocked at her door, stamping snow off her boots, and said, with a farmer's terrible directness:

"You'll want to come tomorrow. Grandfather's going to talk. About the night of the fire. And I think—" Wren glanced back toward the house, toward the map room's lit window, and lowered her voice. "I think he means to tell it *all*."
