## 13. Night Surgery

The mill kitchen had been a bakery once, before the company bought the water rights and the ovens went cold. Felix knew this because the hearth was built for loaves, wide and low, and she had to kneel at it like a penitent to keep the water at a rolling boil.

She had been kneeling for four hours.

"More linen," she said, without turning. A strip was pressed into her hand before she finished the sentence — Wren, who had not sat down since they'd carried Tam up from the burning mill and laid him on the long table that had been carried into the kitchen from the parlor, because it was the only surface long enough and the only surface they were willing to ruin.

Tam Calloway lay on his back with his arms spread like a man making an angel in snow, except the snow was scorched linen and the angel was seventy-one years old with burns down his left side from shoulder to wrist, where he had wrapped the testimony pages against his chest and turned his back to the fire. The pages had survived. Felix had counted them twice, by candlelight, her hands shaking so badly she'd had to set the count aside and start again. Fourteen leaves. Old Tam's account of the massacre night, encoded in contour lines and boundary notes, the ink blistered at one corner but whole.

The man who had shielded them was not whole.

"Boil's ready," Wren said.

"I know. I'm thinking."

"You've been thinking since midnight. It's nearly three."

"Thinking is the work," Felix said, and heard how it sounded — the voice of her master, who had once spent a full afternoon deciding which of two identical blues to use for a river, and had defended the decision to Felix as though his life depended on it. *The drawing is the easy part, boy. The deciding is the map.*

She dipped the linen in the boiled water, wrung it, and laid it along Tam's forearm as gently as she knew how, which was not very. The old man made a sound like a door in a windstorm.

"Sorry," Felix breathed. "Sorry, sorry—"

"Do that again," Tam said through his teeth, "an' I'll haunt ye specific. Not yer family. *You.* Every map ye ever draw, I'll smudge one line. One. Ye'll never know which."

Despite everything — the smell of burned wool and cooked flesh, the candle guttering, Wren's mother asleep sitting up in the corner chair with a Bible she wasn't reading — Felix laughed. It came out of her like something escaping.

"There it is," Tam said, satisfied. "Knew ye had it in ye."

"You should sleep."

"I'll sleep when the burn does. Go on. Tell me what ye're doin' to me, so I can complain about it proper."

So Felix narrated. She had learned this trick from her mother, long ago, over a fevered brother — *name the thing and it shrinks* — and it worked on Tam the way it had worked on a nine-year-old Felix: not by lessening the pain but by making it a story, and Tam Calloway would endure nearly anything that came to him in the shape of a story.

"Cool water, to draw the heat out. Then the goose grease and the comfrey, which Mrs. Calloway made while pretending not to cry, which I will never mention to her. Then clean linen, loose, so the skin can decide what it wants to be when it grows back."

"Ye've done this before."

"My brother broke his arm falling out of an apple tree he'd been told twice not to climb." Felix smoothed the linen down Tam's wrist. "I set it. I was eleven. I did it wrong, and it healed wrong, and he could never quite straighten the hand after. So I learned to do it right, after, in case there was a next time."

"And was there?"

"There's always a next time. That's the first law of geography."

Tam's laugh turned into a hiss, and Felix apologized again, and the night went on.

* * *

The house had emptied by degrees. Wren's father had gone at midnight to stand watch at the bridge — what remained of the bridge, its broken span jutting into the current like a snapped bone — and the younger Calloways had been marched to bed by their mother with the kind of quiet ferocity that Felix was beginning to understand was the true engine of the household. That left the kitchen, the hearth, the table, Tam, Wren, and Felix.

And the secret, which sat among them like a fourth person.

Felix had been aware of it all night — not the testimony, which was dangerous but known, but the other thing, the older thing, the thing she had carried since she was sixteen and had cut her hair with sewing scissors and taken her dead brother's name like a coat that didn't fit and never would. It sat in her chest the way the atlas sat in its oilcloth: wrapped, weighted, waiting.

Wren knew about the testimony. Wren had helped carry Tam. Wren had stood beside Felix all night without being asked, tearing linen, boiling water, holding the candle steady when Felix's hands weren't, and Felix had understood somewhere around the second hour that Wren was not staying for Tam's sake alone. She was staying because Felix was there, and Wren Calloway did not leave people alone with hard things.

It was, Felix thought, the most dangerous quality she had ever encountered in a person.

"The grease," Wren said, holding out the little crock. "Before the linen sets."

Felix took it. Their fingers touched on the crock's rim — they had touched perhaps a hundred times that night, passing things back and forth, and each time Felix had performed the small arithmetic of withdrawal, the half-second delay, the angle of the wrist that read as indifference. She performed it now.

Except Wren was watching her hands. Not her face. Her hands.

"You've got ink in the burn line," Wren said. "On your fingers. You've been drafting since we got Tam upstairs — no. Since before. Since the mill. You went back in for the pages and you had the pen in your pocket and you've been holding it the whole time, haven't you. Through the fire and after."

Felix looked down. There it was: a dark stain along the side of her right hand, the cartographer's callus, the ink that never fully came out no matter how she scrubbed. She had been holding the pen when she'd run into the burning mill. She had been holding it when she'd found Tam facedown in the smoke with the pages crushed under him. She was, apparently, holding it now — it was in her pocket, she realized, and her hand was in her pocket, curled around it like a talisman.

"I didn't notice," she said.

"No," Wren said. "You wouldn't."

* * *

Toward dawn, Tam finally slept. The burns had been dressed, the fire had been fed down to embers, and Mrs. Calloway had been persuaded — by her son, arriving bleary from the stairs — to go up to bed, which she did while informing the room that she would be down again at first light and expected to find everything exactly as she left it, only better.

The kitchen went quiet in the particular way kitchens do when the work stops and the quiet has nowhere to put itself.

Felix stood at the basin, washing. The water had gone gray. She scrubbed at the ink on her fingers out of habit rather than hope, and behind her she heard Wren moving — banking the hearth, folding linen, the small competent noises of a person who had been raised in a house where resting before the work was done was a moral failing.

"You should sleep," Felix said. "You've been up as long as I have."

"I will."

"You said that at midnight. And one. And—"

"I know what I said." Wren came and stood beside her at the basin. Not touching. Close. In the ember-light her face was half gold and half shadow, and there was soot on her jaw where she'd pushed her hair back with a dirty hand, and Felix found that she had to look at the water instead, because looking at Wren had begun to feel like looking at the sun through a spyglass — possible, but not without cost.

"Your hands are shaking," Wren said.

"They've been shaking since the mill."

"No. They shook *at* the mill. These are different." Wren reached over and turned off the water — a small tyranny, gently done. "These are the shaking of someone who's been holding something up all night and knows they can't set it down."

Felix said nothing. The basin dripped.

"Give me your hand," Wren said.

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to hold it, and if you ask why again I'll think you're being difficult on purpose."

And Felix — who had spent six years becoming a person who did not flinch, who had perfected the flat stare and the level voice and the handshake that was firm but not *too* firm, who had learned to occupy the space of a young man the way one occupies a borrowed room, carefully, without leaving marks — Felix gave her her hand.

Wren held it the way she seemed to do everything: without ceremony. She turned it over in both of hers, and began, of all things, to clean it. A cloth from somewhere, dipped in the clean water she'd set aside for the dressings, working along Felix's fingers with the same unhurried thoroughness she'd brought to Tam's dressings, tracing the ink out of the lines of her palm.

It should have been nothing. It was, technically, nothing. A hand being washed.

But no one had held Felix Blackwood's hand in six years. And no one had ever held it like this — as though it were a hand, and not a piece of evidence, and not a disguise to be maintained, and not the thing that would undo her if anyone looked too long. Wren held it like a hand that had done a night's hard work and deserved looking after, and something in Felix's chest that had been clenched since the age of sixteen began, very slowly, to consider unclenching.

"You've a callus here," Wren said, thumbing the side of her forefinger. "Odd place. Drafting?"

"Yes."

"And here." The base of her palm. "From pressing down hard."

"I press hard when it matters."

"Mm." Wren worked the cloth along her thumb, and then paused. Felix felt the pause the way one feels a change in wind — a pressure shifting, the air deciding something.

Then Wren turned Felix's hand over again, gently, and pressed her thumb into the soft part of the palm, and said, in a voice that was not quite casual:

"You know what I can't work out? Your hands. I've watched them all night. They're quick, and they're sure, and they're kind — you've the gentlest hands of anyone in this house, and that's saying something, my mother's in the room. But they're *small.* And I keep thinking: for a draftsman, fine. Draftsmen come in all sizes. But for a young man of — what are you, twenty-two? — they're small, Felix. And your wrists. And the way you sat on the stairs this evening, knees together, like my grandmother. And the way you flinched — just now, not before, just now — when I took your hand, like nobody'd held it in years."

The kitchen was very quiet. An ember settled in the hearth with a sound like a sigh.

"I'm not saying anything," Wren said. Her voice had gone soft, and steady, and she had not let go of Felix's hand. "I'm saying I've been watching all night, and I've been watching since the orchards, and I add things up. It's what I do. You said so yourself — you said I corrected your measurements like I was reading your mind. I wasn't reading your mind. I was reading your *hands.*"

"Wren—"

"I'm not asking." She said it quickly, firmly, like closing a gate before something could bolt through it. "Whatever it is, it's yours. I'm not asking, and I'm not telling, and if anyone in this house ever asks me about Felix Blackwood I'll tell them he's the best draftsman in the county and a terrible singer, and that's all I've got. But—" and here her voice did something Felix had never heard it do, something small and unguarded, a crack in the competence, "—I wanted you to know that you don't have to carry it alone in *this* kitchen. Whatever it weighs. There's room for it here. That's all. That's the whole of what I'm saying, and if you'd rather I'd never said it, we'll never say it again, and I'll—"

"Wren."

Felix had not meant to say it that way. She had meant to say it lightly, deflectingly, in the voice of Felix Blackwood the apprentice, and what came out instead was the voice underneath — the one she hadn't used in six years, the one that was neither her brother's name nor her master's training nor her own careful architecture, but just *her*, frightened and tired and so grateful it was a kind of pain.

Wren went still.

Felix turned her hand over — her own hand, in Wren's — and let the firelight fall on her palm, and said nothing at all, because there was nothing to say that the hand didn't say better.

The silence lasted four heartbeats. Felix counted them, because counting was what she did when the world tilted: one, two, three, four.

Then Wren exhaled — a long breath, as though she'd been holding it since the orchards, since the ferry, since the first moment a stranger's precise measurements had snagged on something in her that never quite let go — and she did not step back, and she did not let go, and she did not say *I knew it* or *I always thought* or any of the things Felix had rehearsed defenses against for six years.

She said: "Does it hurt? Your side. Where the beam caught you. You've been favoring it since the mill and you think nobody's noticed, but Tam noticed, and Tam's half asleep with his own skin cooked."

Felix blinked. "What?"

"Your side. Sit down. I'm getting the grease."

"You — that's — *that's* what you—"

"You held a man's whole testimony against a fire tonight," Wren said, steering her toward a stool with the gentle tyranny Felix was rapidly learning not to resist. "You're allowed to be looked after. Sit."

Felix sat. Wren knelt, lifted the hem of Felix's shirt with a matter-of-factness that was somehow more devastating than any tenderness, and began cleaning the bruise along her ribs with the same cloth, the same unhurried hands, humming — off-key, terribly, wonderfully — the same song the family had sung round the fire two nights ago.

And Felix Blackwood, who had not been looked after since she was sixteen years old, who had built herself out of straight lines and steady hands and a dead boy's name, sat in a bakery kitchen at dawn with her shirt half lifted and her secret lying open between them like a map with the margins finally filled in, and wept — silently, furiously, without changing her breathing, the way she'd taught herself to do — while Wren Calloway hummed and worked and pretended, with immense kindness, not to notice that too.

* * *

The light was coming up gray over the frozen fields when Tam's voice came rough from the table.

"Girl."

Both of them turned. The old man's eyes were open a slit, fixed on the ceiling, his burned arm laid out with the care of a relic.

"Ye'll want to know," he said, "before the household's up. Before anyone's up." He swallowed, and Felix saw the pain move through him and get put away, the way he'd been putting things away for fifty years. "The mill fire weren't chance. I saw 'em. Before the smoke took me proper. Company coats, an' they weren't there to put it out. And the one that struck the light—" his jaw worked, "—he had papers in his hand already. Dry papers. Knew which shelf."

The words settled into the kitchen like cold water into boots.

"He knew what he'd come for," Tam said. "An' that means somebody told him what to look for. An' I'll tell ye somethin' else for nothin'." His eyes moved — slowly, painfully — from Felix to Wren, and back. "Somebody who's been inside these walls."

Felix's hand found Wren's without her deciding anything at all.

Outside, down the white lane, a rider was coming toward the house through the early light — moving fast, and alone, and wearing company gray.
