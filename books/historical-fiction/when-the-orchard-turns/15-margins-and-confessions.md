## 15. Margins and Confessions

The map room at the Calloway farm had been a pantry once, and it still smelled faintly of dried apples and lamp oil, which Edith Vane found she did not mind at all. There were worse smells to think by. She had spent three years thinking by the smell of the company survey office in Corvane — tallow, wet wool, and the particular sourness of men who believed the world owed them its shape.

Now she bent over Felix Blackwood's drafting table by lamplight, and the world was being shaped back at her.

"You've put a boundary stone where there isn't one," she said.

"There is one. It's under the hawthorn at the north fence. Tam remembers it from before the company pulled the markers."

"Then we draw the hawthorn."

"We draw the hawthorn," Felix agreed, and made the small note in the margin — *hawthorn, N. fence, stone beneath, per T. Calloway* — in a hand so small and so neat that no clerk skimming the atlas would ever pause on it.

That was the whole art of it, Edith thought. Not hiding. Hiding was for smugglers and liars, and they were neither, whatever the law would say if it ever got the chance to say anything. What they were doing was *placement*. A map was read by men in a hurry, and men in a hurry read only what was bold, what was centered, what was labeled in a fine confident hand. Everything else — the contour lines, the boundary notes, the little annotations of soil and drainage and fence condition — was scenery. And scenery, Edith had learned long ago in her brother's offices, could hold an enormous amount of truth if you were patient enough to tuck it in.

She had learned that the way one learns most things worth knowing. Badly, and too late, and at someone else's expense.

"Pass me the blue," Felix said.

Edith handed her the ink pot. Their fingers did not touch, which Edith noted with a small private disappointment and then put away, the way one puts away a letter one cannot yet answer.

Outside, the thaw was doing its slow, noisy work. The eaves dripped in a rhythm that was almost musical, if one had low standards for music, which the Calloway household emphatically did. Somewhere below them, Wren was teaching the younger Hollis boy a song about a drowned miller, and the boy was singing it in a key that did not exist in nature. Edith had lived in barracks and officers' messes and had believed herself inured to bad singing. She had been wrong. She found, to her considerable surprise, that she did not want it to stop.

"The grave sites next," Felix said. "The east orchard first. There are eleven."

"Ten," Edith said. "The eleventh is the one under the well. Tam counted ten in the rows and one they couldn't reach."

Felix's pen stopped. For a moment the only sound was the eaves, and the boy below hitting a note that made a dog somewhere start barking.

"Under the well," Felix repeated quietly. "He never told me that part."

"He doesn't tell anyone that part. I had to ask him twice, and the second time I brought him cider, which I recommend as a general technique with old men." Edith turned the master sheet toward her. "We'll mark it as a water feature. Depth, flow, condition of the lining. And then—" she took the fine pen herself, "—we note that the lining was repaired in the autumn of '46 with fieldstone, *per T. Calloway*, who supplied the stone."

Felix looked at her for a long moment. "You're going to make the well itself the testimony."

"I'm going to make the well itself the testimony," Edith agreed. "A well is the last thing in the world a company clerk looks at closely. They've got rivers to misname and forests to shrink by a third."

That earned her the thing she had been working toward for two weeks without quite admitting it: Felix Blackwood laughed. It was a quiet laugh, more breath than sound, but it changed her whole face, and Edith had to look back down at the map very quickly and pretend to be fascinated by the drainage of the east orchard, which was, in fairness, genuinely interesting, though not *that* interesting.

* * *

They worked until the candle needed replacing, and then Felix went down to fetch a fresh one and to rescue the Hollis boy from Wren's musical ambitions, and Edith was alone with the atlas and with the confession she had been carrying around like a stone in her boot.

She had rehearsed it, in the way one rehearses things one knows one must say. She had rehearsed it walking the fence line in the grey mornings, rehearsed it to the horse, who was a poor audience but a discreet one. And now the moment had arrived, the candle was being fetched, and she understood with perfect clarity that if she did not say it tonight she would find a reason tomorrow, and the day after, until the atlas was gone downriver and the moment was gone with it.

Felix came back up with the candle and two cups of Tam's cider, which was less cider than it was a rumor of cider, and set one down at Edith's elbow without comment.

"I falsified a map once," Edith said.

Felix went still. Not alarmed, Edith noted — attentive. There was a difference, and the difference mattered more than Edith could say.

"The Kesswick survey. Four years ago. A river boundary dispute between two farming parishes and a company that wanted the water rights." Edith kept her eyes on the map. It was easier. "I was twenty-two and the company cartographer was my instructor, and he told me the survey had been *revised* and that my job was to fair-copy the revision. I fair-copied it beautifully. I was very proud of it. It won me my posting."

"And what did the revision do?"

"Moved the river." Edith turned the cup in her hands. "Not much. Thirty feet on paper. Enough. Two hundred people lost their grazing rights, and one of them — a man named Aldous Petrie — drowned himself in the very river we'd moved, which I have always thought was either a terrible coincidence or the most pointed message anyone ever left me, and I have never decided which."

The eaves dripped. Below, mercifully, the singing had stopped.

"I didn't know what the revision was when I copied it," Edith went on. "I found out a year later, and I did nothing, because by then I was comfortable, and my brother was a captain, and it is astonishing how much a person can not-see when not-seeing comes with a salary." She finally looked up. "I am not telling you this so you'll forgive me. I'm telling you because when we finish this atlas, and it goes down the river, and someone in the capital holds it up to the light — I need you to know that the hands that drew it are hands that have drawn lies before. And that this one is the amends. Not the apology. The amends. There's a difference. An apology is for the person wronged. An amends is for the map."

Felix was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the way it went careful when she was working something out rather than avoiding it.

"Your brother wrote the order," she said. "For the massacre. The one you copied for us."

"Yes."

"And you fair-copied that too."

"Yes." Edith's mouth twisted. "I appear to have made a career of it."

"And now you're un-copying it." Felix set down her pen. "Edith. I spent six years passing as a man so I could hold a pen professionally. Do you imagine I don't know what it costs to do the wrong thing for the right reasons, or the right thing for the wrong ones, or any of the thousand arrangements a person makes with a world that wasn't built for them? I'm not in a position to forgive you for Kesswick. The people of Kesswick are. But I can tell you that this atlas will be better because you're drawing it, and that when I sign it, I'll be glad to sign it beside your work."

Edith had prepared herself for anger. She had prepared herself for the cold, polite withdrawal she knew from her brother's officers, the kind that ended careers. She had not prepared herself for *glad*, and she found she had to blow her nose on her sleeve, which was not at all the mannerly thing, and Felix had the grace to study the east orchard's drainage very intently until she was finished.

"Well," Edith said at last. "That's the confessions done, then. Shall we bury the captain's order under the mill pond?"

"Under the mill pond," Felix agreed, and there was the laugh again, and Edith thought: *I would burn every map I ever made to hear that regularly,* and then thought: *no, actually, I'd burn them just to hear it once more tonight,* and then got firmly back to work, because the atlas would not finish itself and the thaw would not wait.

* * *

They worked through the deep hours, and the work had a rhythm to it that Edith came to love the way one loves a song one has heard all one's life without noticing.

The testimony went in as contour lines. Old Tam's account of the massacre night — the torches coming up the valley road, the shots from the ridge, the eleven who did not run fast enough — was encoded as elevation readings, each figure a date or a count, each interval a name. Felix had devised the cipher, and it was elegant, and Edith had improved it, and the two of them argued about it with a ferocity that would have looked, to an eavesdropper, exactly like two cartographers arguing about contour intervals, which was the entire point.

"The third interval is wrong," Felix said at one point. "If we space them evenly, a trained eye will see the pattern."

"Then we space them *unevenly*, but according to a rule. Every fourth interval compressed. It looks like terrain. It reads like a clock."

Felix stared at her. "That's — yes. That's exactly it." She made the adjustment, then paused. "You know, in Corvane they say the Vane siblings are the two finest technical minds in the company survey corps."

"They say that about my brother too?"

"They say your brother is the finest *political* mind. They say you're the finest technical one. I'm beginning to think they had it backwards."

Edith felt that one somewhere under her ribs and elected to respond by demanding they re-examine the fence line at the south field, which Felix did, and which was, as Felix had insisted all along, drawn correctly, and which Edith pretended to correct anyway just to have something to do with her hands.

The grave sites went in as soil annotations — *dark loam, recently turned, per local informant* — eleven of them, plus the well. The burned farmsteads went in as *former structures, condition: ruins, cause of ruin: unspecified*, which Edith found she enjoyed enormously. *Unspecified* was going to do more work in this atlas than any word had ever done. The company's own historians, when they came sniffing, would find every fact in the atlas technically accurate and unimpeachable, and only when you laid the whole thing end to end and read it as it was meant to be read — the way a farmer reads weather — did it say what it said.

*They came in the night. They burned the east rows. Eleven are in the ground and one is in the well, and the company called it blight.*

All of it, in soil notes and contour lines and the condition of a well lining.

"Read it back to me," Felix said, near dawn. "The whole margin. As a reader would."

Edith read it. The valley took shape under her voice: the orchards, the fences, the hawthorn with its stone, the well with its fieldstone lining, the ruins, the eleven dark patches of turned loam, the contour intervals ticking like a clock down to the night of the ninth of October. When she finished, neither of them spoke for a moment.

"It holds," Felix said finally.

"It holds," Edith agreed. And then, because the hour was late and the cider was gone and confession apparently bred confession like rabbits: "Felix. When this is done. When it's in the capital and the record and all the rest of it. What happens to you?"

Felix looked up from capping the ink. In the candlelight her face was unreadable and then, abruptly, entirely readable, which was a trick Edith suspected she alone got to see.

"I don't know," Felix said. "I've been someone else for six years. I'm not entirely sure who's left under the name."

"Well," Edith said, "whoever it is, I've a professional interest. I've worked with them for weeks and I'd like to keep the acquaintance." She stood, and stretched, and gathered her courage the way she gathered everything, briskly and before she could think better of it. "When we're finished. After the river. I'd like — that is. There's a tea house on Chandler Street in the capital that serves a disgraceful number of pastries, and I thought—"

The door opened.

Wren stood in it, lamplight behind her, and Edith had never in her life been so grateful for an interruption or so furious at one. Wren's face was pale and set in a way Edith had learned, these past weeks, to read as *trouble*.

"You need to come down," Wren said. "There's a rider at the gate. Company colors. He's asking for the household by name — all our names." She paused, and her eyes went from Edith to Felix to the atlas lying open on the table, and back. "And he's got a clerk with him. With a document case."

Edith looked down at the finished margin, at the well and the eleven graves and the order hidden in the contour lines, all of it finished not four hours too soon and one night too late.

"Tell him," she said, "that the survey team will receive him in the kitchen. And Wren — put the kettle on. Nobody in the history of the company has ever managed to be menacing over a offered cup of tea, and I intend to find out if that holds."
