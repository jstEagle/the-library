## 16. What Wren Saw

The lamplight in the map room had turned the color of cider, and Wren had been watching Felix draw the same contour line for the better part of an hour.

She didn't say anything about it. That was the thing about Felix — she thought her silences were invisible, and they were anything but. When Felix was frightened, her pen got lighter. When she was lying, her letters got smaller. When she was carrying something too heavy, she held her breath in little parcels, letting it out through her nose like a woman counting coins she didn't want anyone to see.

Tonight the pen was light as a feather and the letters were tiny as seeds, and Wren had watched enough to know what that meant.

"You've drawn that hill three times," Wren said, from her chair by the stove, where she was ostensibly darning a sock and actually doing nothing of the kind.

"I'm checking my elevations."

"Against what?"

"Against themselves."

"Mm." Wren pulled the thread through. "And how do they compare?"

Felix set the pen down with the exaggerated care of a person who wanted very much to throw it. "They're consistent."

"Then why are you sweating?"

"I am not sweating."

"You're sweating like a cheese in July, Felix."

That earned her a look — the look Felix got when Wren used her name too familiarly, too knowingly, in a room where anyone might pass the door. But it was late, past midnight, and the house had gone quiet around them. Tam was sleeping with his bandaged arm propped on a pillow Ma Calloway had sewn from a flour sack. The mill fire was nine days behind them, and the smell of smoke had only just left everyone's hair.

Felix rubbed her eyes under her spectacles. "The testimony," she said, finally. "Tam's. It's in. The contour lines carry it, the boundary notes carry it, the grave sites are marked the way we agreed. Edith's page is sewn into the back board. There's only—" She stopped.

"Only the part that's been sitting in this room for sixteen years," Wren said, "wearing a dress and pretending to be a sock."

Felix looked at her.

"I was there," Wren said.

The stove ticked. Outside, the thaw was working at the ice on the eaves, a slow dripping like the house clearing its throat.

"You were a child," Felix said carefully.

"I was nine."

"You told me you remembered the fires."

"I told you what everyone tells. I told you the version that fits in a sentence at a funeral." Wren set down the sock. It was a terrible sock anyway; she'd been darning the same hole for three nights, and the hole had won every time. "I was in the east orchard. Under the Crawford apple, the big one, the one with the split trunk. I'd run there because Tam said run, and I didn't run far enough, or maybe I did. I don't know. I've never known, because I've never once said any of it out loud, and a thing you never say out loud doesn't have a shape. It just has weight."

Felix had gone very still. She did that — went still the way water goes still, not empty but gathered. Wren had learned to read it as attention, not fear.

"I can write it down," Felix said quietly. "The way we did Tam's. Encoded. No one would ever—"

"I know how your codes work. I've watched you make them. I helped you make them." Wren stood, because sitting suddenly felt like lying down in a grave she hadn't dug yet. "I don't want it encoded, Felix. Not mine. Tam's story needs hiding because Tam would be killed for it. Mine needs saying because I've spent sixteen years being killed for it anyway, slowly, by keeping it."

Felix took off her spectacles. It was something she did when she wanted to see a person instead of a problem, Wren had noticed. Without them her face was younger and more frightened and more stubborn, all at once.

"Then say it," Felix said. "And I'll write it plain. And if the plainness of it kills us both, at least we'll be legible."

"That's the worst comfort anyone's ever offered me."

"It's the only kind I stock."

Wren almost laughed. That was the trick of Felix — she made you laugh at the edge of the drop, and the laughing was what kept you from looking all the way down.

* * *

They moved to the kitchen table because the map room felt too much like a church and Wren wanted to do this somewhere with crumbs in it, somewhere human. Ma Calloway left a lamp and a pot of tea and, without being asked, a heel of the brown loaf with butter, because in the Calloway house no testimony was given on an empty stomach. That was practically scripture.

Felix sat across from her with fresh paper — not the atlas paper, not yet. Plain paper first, she said. Plain words first. The atlas could wait for the truth to be finished being true.

"Start wherever it starts," Felix said. "Not where it's most important. Where it starts."

Wren wrapped her hands around the tea. The cup was chipped on one side, the way all their cups were chipped, the way everything in this valley was chipped and still in use.

"It starts with the pears," she said. "That's the foolish part. That's the part I've never told anyone, because it's foolish, and I thought if I told the foolish part first, no one would believe the rest."

"I'll believe the rest."

"I know." And the strange thing — the thing Wren would remember later, when she told this story again and again, in print, in ink that outlived them — was that she did know it. Felix had a face you could believe things at.

"The pears were ripe early that year. Early September, warm as August. Da said we'd lose them to wasps if we didn't pick the south row, so he sent me up the ladder because I was small and light and didn't complain about the wasps, or rather I complained in a register the wasps couldn't hear." She took a breath. "I was up in the tree when the riders came. I saw them first as dust on the north road. That's the truth of it — the massacre starts, in my memory, as dust. A brown smudge. I remember thinking, that's a lot of dust for one cart."

Felix's pen was moving. Wren could hear it, that small dry whisper, and found it steadied her rather than frightened her. Every word she spoke became a mark on paper. Every mark on paper meant it had happened outside her skull, in the world, where things could be checked and answered.

"Da came out of the barn and stood in the yard with his hat off, which he never did, not even for the reeve. And the dust resolved into horses. Twelve of them. Company green. And a wagon behind, with the tarpaulin on it, and I remember — I remember this exactly, I've carried it exactly for sixteen years like a stone in a sling — I remember that one of the horses was gray with a white sock on its near hind leg, and I thought, *I know that horse.* Because I did. It was the horse that pulled the miller's cart to market. It had been borrowed, or taken, or bought, and it didn't know whose hands were on its reins now. It was just a horse doing its work."

"Write that down," Wren said, before Felix could ask. "The horse. People will want to skip the horse. Make them not skip the horse."

Felix wrote: *the horse.*

"Da asked them their business. I was too high up to hear the answer, but I heard the tone of it, and the tone was the answer. Then Tam came out — Tam was younger then, still had all his hair and most of his temper — and there was shouting, and Da put his hand on Tam's chest like, *not now, not here,* and the officer — I never saw his face, the brim of his hat was low and the sun was behind him, which I think was not an accident, I think men like that learn to stand with the sun behind them — the officer pointed at the tarpaulin wagon, and men got down off horses and started unloading it, and what they unloaded was oil."

The kitchen was very quiet. The thaw dripped. Somewhere upstairs the house settled on its joists, the old timbers sighing the way old houses and old people sigh when the young ones are talking about hard things.

"They went barn to barn," Wren said. Her voice had gone flat and she noticed it going flat and made herself put the curve back in it, made herself sound like a person and not a ledger. "Not the houses. The barns first. The granaries. The cider press at the Crawfords'. They were not burning houses, Felix. They were burning *food.* Do you understand what I'm telling you? They came to make the valley starve, and the shooting — the shooting came after, and it came after because some men ran for what was burning, and you cannot let men run for what's burning, because if men can run for what's burning then burning doesn't work."

Felix's pen had stopped.

"Keep writing," Wren said. "Please. If you stop I'll stop, and I can't stop, because I don't know if I can start again."

The pen started again.

"Da got me down out of the tree — I don't remember the getting down, I remember the bark, I remember the smell of pear bruising under my knees where I'd knelt on the fruit in the crook of the branch. He put me in the root cellar under the kitchen with the hatch shut and the crocks around me, and he said, *count the jars, Wren, count every jar, don't stop counting till I come.* And I counted jars. I counted two hundred and eleven jars of pears and beans and plums, and I counted them again, and I counted them a third time, and somewhere in the third counting the screaming started."

She had thought this part would break her. She had spent sixteen years assuming this part would break her, which was the whole reason she'd never said it. You keep a thing shut because you believe the door is load-bearing.

But Felix was across the table with her pen moving and her eyes up, meeting Wren's eyes, not looking away, not flinching, not weeping — and that was its own mercy, because pity would have broken the spell. Pity would have made Wren a thing to be sorry for instead of a thing to be *recorded.*

"I heard the shots the way you hear thunder," Wren said. "Counted, spaced. Not a battle. A battle has a sound like a cloth tearing. This was — one, then a while, then two close together, then a long while. Men being *selected.* I counted the shots too. I couldn't help it. I was counting jars and then I was counting shots and the numbers got mixed together and for years afterward I couldn't see a crock of plums without doing arithmetic I didn't want to do."

"How many?" Felix asked, very softly.

"Seventeen shots that I counted. The official record says eleven dead, all *brigands and their confederates.*" Wren's mouth did something that was not a smile. "I counted seventeen. And there were men they took away alive in the wagon, and I don't know what number those were, because I was in a root cellar, and that is the limit of what I know, and I want the record to say that. *The witness heard seventeen shots. The witness does not know the full number. The record should be honest about the size of its own blindness.* Can you write that?"

Felix wrote it. Wren watched her write it and saw her hand shake only once, at *blindness,* and steady again.

"Da didn't come," Wren said. "I counted jars till the counting ran out of numbers in me, and then I pushed the hatch, and the kitchen was full of smoke that wasn't kitchen smoke, and I went out into the yard, and—" She stopped. She picked up the tea and found it cold and drank it anyway, because her mouth needed a job. "I'll say this part fast. Fast is how it happened."

"Go on."

"The barn was standing. The barn is still standing. But the men were lying in the yard like — like the way apples lie after a bad wind, everywhere, wrong-angled, too many. And Da was among them. And Tam was kneeling by him, and Tam's hands were — Tam was trying to arrange him, do you see, trying to make him *lie properly,* and a man can't lie properly after that, there's no properly, and Tam couldn't stop trying. I remember thinking that was the worst thing I'd ever seen, Tam trying to fix it. And then I saw something worse, which was the officer on his horse at the gate, writing in a little book. Writing in a *little book,* Felix. Standing in our yard with our dead and writing in his little book, and I thought, even at nine, even with my whole mind gone white as ash — I thought, *he's making a map of it. He's making the lie first. He's here to write it down wrong and we're all just standing around letting him.*"

Felix had stopped writing again. This time Wren didn't tell her to continue. This time she reached across the table and put her hand flat on the page, over the fresh wet words, and said:

"That's why the atlas matters. That's the whole of it, right there, that's the seed of the whole thing. He had a little book and we had nothing. Sixteen years, and the little book won, because it was the only book. So we make another book. A truer one. And mine goes in it, and Tam's goes in it, and the horse with the white sock goes in it, and the seventeen shots go in it, and the pears go in it, because they were ripe early that year and no one will ever write that again if I don't."

Felix was crying. She was crying the way she did everything, quietly and while pretending not to, and Wren found — this astonished her, this would astonish her for the rest of her life — that Felix's tears were not breaking her open but *closing* her shut, sealing her, the way wax seals a letter. Her testimony was becoming a letter. It was becoming a thing with an address. It was becoming a thing that would be *delivered.*

"Say the rest," Felix said, voice wrecked and steady at once. "All the rest. The names. You knew them all."

"I knew them all," Wren said. "I'll give you every name, and their farms, and where each one fell, because I walked the yard after, I walked it before they let us bury anyone, I was nine and furious and nobody thought to stop a child from looking, and I *looked,* Felix, I looked at all of it, and I have kept every name in a room in my head with the door shut for sixteen years, and I am about to open that door, and I want you to understand that I am not doing it because it's safe. I'm doing it because the door was never protecting me. It was only ever protecting *them.*"

* * *

They worked until the lamp burned low and the thaw-drip outside turned to birdsong, because the birds in this valley had apparently decided spring was a rumor worth starting early. Wren gave names: Crawford, and Crawford, and the two Hedley brothers, and the miller's son who'd only been home from the coast a week. She gave places. She gave the gray horse with the white sock. She gave the seventeen shots and the two hundred and eleven jars and the officer with the sun behind him, and Felix took it all down in plain hand on plain paper, the plain truth before the encoding, and twice she read passages back and Wren corrected her — not the facts, the *weight* of them. "You've made it sound like a report. Make it sound like a girl in a pear tree."

By the time the east window went gray and then gold, Wren felt something she had no proper word for. Lighter was the wrong word; nothing about it was light. *Carried* was closer. For sixteen years she had been carrying the thing alone, and now it was in ink, and ink could be handed to other people, and a thing handed to other people is a different thing entirely.

Felix blotted the last page and looked at her across the ruins of the bread and the cold tea.

"You didn't break," Felix said. "I kept waiting for it. You didn't break once."

"I know," Wren said, and found she was grinning, actually grinning, at the end of the worst night of her life. "That's the joke of it. Sixteen years I thought the telling would kill me. Turns out the *not telling* was doing that fine on its own."

Felix laughed — a wet, cracked, real laugh — and began squaring the pages into order.

And that was when the knocking started.

Not the polite knock of a neighbor. Not the hammering of a panic. It was a measured, official, patient knocking, three blows and a pause, three blows and a pause, the knock of men who had all the time in the world and all of it was yours.

From the stairs, Ma Calloway's voice, low and sharp as a drawn wire: "Company riders. Six of them. And Felix—" a breath, "—they're asking for the mapmaker by name."

Felix went white. Wren put her hand over the fresh pages, over her own words, seventeen shots and two hundred and eleven jars and the gray horse with the white sock, and held them down against the table as though the wind of what was coming might take them.

"They can't know," Felix whispered.

"Then they suspect," Wren said, "and there's a difference, and we've got about a minute to make it count."
