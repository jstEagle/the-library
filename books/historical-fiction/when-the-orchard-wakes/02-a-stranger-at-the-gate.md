## 2. A Stranger at the Gate

The rain came in off the fields the way it always did in late October—not falling so much as arriving, sideways and grey, as though the weather itself had somewhere to be and no patience for the land it crossed. Ada Eriksen stood in the third row of the orchard with the pruning saw still in her hand, watching a figure come up the cart track from the road.

She had seen him first as a smudge, then as a coat, then as a man: tall, stooped against the wind, hatless, with the particular lurching walk of someone who had been walking for a very long time and had stopped pretending he was dry. He moved the way soldiers moved when they'd stopped being soldiers but hadn't yet remembered how to be anything else—weight forward, eyes doing the work of a scout even on a road where the only enemy was mud.

Ada did not move. That was the first discipline she had learned, in a ward at Étaples with shells walking their thunder across the coast: when something new appears, be still, and count. She counted his steps. Forty-one from the bend in the track to the gate. She had counted men like that once, carried in on stretchers, and the habit had never left her, the way a burn never leaves the skin.

The orchard was hers in the way that ruins belong to the people who refuse to leave them. Her father had planted these trees before she was born—Bramleys and Ribstons and a row of old Costards down by the wall that nobody grew anymore—and when the war took him and then took her brother Nils and then, in its slow indifferent way, took her mother, it had left Ada standing among eleven hundred trees with a pair of shears and no one to tell her what to do with them. The village had opinions, of course. The village always had opinions. But opinions did not prune, and so the orchard had gone half-wild, and Ada had let it, because a wild thing keeps its own counsel and asks nothing of you.

The man reached the gate. He stopped, one hand on the rail, and looked up at her through the rain, and she saw that he was younger than his walk—thirty perhaps, with a beard that had been trimmed once, long ago, by someone who cared, and not since.

"Fröken Eriksen?" he called. His Danish was flat and coastal, a Jutland accent worn smooth by other countries.

"I'm not receiving anyone," Ada said. She said it in Danish, then, because he had given her Danish. It was a courtesy and a wall in the same breath.

"I've walked from the ferry," he said. "From Helsingør. Two days on the road because the buses don't run where I'm going, and where I'm going is here, and I'd very much like to get out of this rain."

"Then you should have started sooner."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile, but the memory of one. "So I've been told. By my mother, by the ferry clerk, and by God, presumably, though He hasn't spoken to me directly."

She should have sent him on. Every instinct she had honed over five years of careful, deliberate, invisible living told her to send him on: strangers in autumn meant questions, questions meant the magistrate, the magistrate meant the ledger, and the ledger—Ada's hand tightened on the saw's handle—was not for the world. But there was something in the way he stood at the gate without trying it. He did not rattle the latch. He did not lean. He waited as though the gate were a border and he had already been refused at better ones.

"State your business," she said, "and then walk back the way you came."

"My business is a letter." He reached inside his coat, and Ada's whole body went taut, the old reflex, the one that had her reaching for a man's pulse before she knew his name. But what he drew out was not a weapon. It was an envelope, oilskin-wrapped, sodden at one corner despite his care, and he held it out across the gate the way a man offers a bird—open-palmed, ready for it to fly.

"I was paid in Copenhagen," he said, "to put this in your hand and no one else's. Not the post, not a neighbour's box, not under the door. Your hand. The man who paid me was very clear about that, and I have found in my life that when a man is very clear about something, it is usually because his life depends on the clarity."

Ada did not move.

"Your name is on it," he said. "Ada Eriksen, the orchard outside Vesterby. There's no other address. I've been asking after you for two days, and I'll tell you what the village said, because it was instructive. The woman at the bakery said she'd heard of you. The man at the inn said you kept to yourself. The schoolmaster said—" he paused, choosing—"the schoolmaster said you were the war's widow, though you never married. And the ferryman, who was drunk, said you talked to trees. So. You're either mad, or you're the only person in Vesterby who minds her own affairs, and I have a letter that says you're the second."

The rain ticked against the oilskin. Somewhere behind Ada, in the wet grass between the rows, a blackbird scolded and went quiet.

"Who wrote it?" she asked. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later, that it came out level.

"I don't know. I can't read it—the hand's not mine and the words aren't either. I was paid to carry, not to read. The man who hired me said the sender was a friend of a friend, in the north, and that the letter must reach you before the month was out." He shifted his weight. Water ran off his chin. "Fröken Eriksen, I have been standing in your rain for four minutes, and I have been in rain for two days, and I will stand here for four more if I must. But that letter is getting damp, and whatever it is, it was not meant to drown."

Ada set the saw down on the low wall beside her, deliberately, the way one sets down a weapon one intends to return to. Then she crossed the wet grass, took the envelope from his open hand, and turned away from the gate.

"Come to the barn," she said. "Not the house. You can dry by the stove and be gone before dark."

* * *

The barn had been a dairy once, in her grandfather's time, and still held the ghost of that order: the stone trough, the iron ring in the wall where a chain had hung, the high small window that let in a blade of grey light. Ada had put the stove in herself, second-hand, and a chair, and a table, because there were afternoons when the house felt too much like a held breath and she needed a room that asked nothing of her. She lit the stove now, watched the man hang his coat on the nail she indicated, watched him lower himself onto the stool with the careful descent of someone whose knees had opinions.

Then she stood at the table with the envelope in both hands, and did not open it.

She knew the handwriting before she knew she knew it. That was the vertigo of it—that recognition arrived through her fingers before her mind could intervene, the way you know a stair is missing in the dark. The address on the front was written in a hand that slanted slightly leftward, the letters tall and narrow, the crossbar of the *t* struck with a little upward flourish at the end, like a man in a hurry to finish a sentence and begin the next. The ink was brown where the damp had found it. The paper was cheap. But the hand—

Ada had seen that hand every day for three years. On prescriptions he'd written for men who couldn't read them, translated aloud in her ear in the ward at Rouen while the orderlies argued about coal. On the margins of her nursing ledger, where he'd corrected her Latin—*not febris, Ada, it's septicaemia, say what kills them or the next nurse won't know what to fight*. On the back of a photograph of the orchard she'd sent him in the spring of 1916, on which he'd written: *Your trees are better company than my colonel. Tell the Costard by the wall I haven't forgotten her.* On eleven letters that had stopped coming in April of 1917, and on the printed card that came in their place, from an office that did not know him and had spelled his name wrong: *We regret to inform you that Private H. Dahl, Royal Danish—* no, not that regiment, the confusion of nations had been part of the tragedy—*We regret to inform you.*

Officially dead since 1917. A cross in a field near Ypres that someone else had paid for, because the Danes who fell in other armies' uniforms got whatever cross was nearest. She had grieved him the way you grieve in wartime—thoroughly, privately, and without the luxury of a grave to stand at. She had grieved him for six years, and then she had done what grief requires if you intend to go on living: she had folded it up small and put it somewhere load-bearing, and built her days on top of it.

And now the handwriting was in her hands, slanting leftward, alive.

"Fröken Eriksen." The man's voice, gentle. "You've gone very white."

"Be quiet."

He was quiet. He was, she noted distantly, good at being quiet, which was not a quality she associated with couriers or with men.

She opened the envelope with her thumb, because her fingers were not steady enough for anything finer. Inside: one page, folded twice, and a smaller slip of paper that slid out and lay on the table between them. She did not look at the slip. She looked at the page, and the room, the rain, the stove's small heat, the stranger on the stool—all of it receded to the edges of her vision like a tide going out, and the handwriting came forward, and it said:

*Ada. I don't know if the name still means anything where you are, or if they let it mean anything at all. I have been dead for six years, which is longer than I was alive to some people, and I have used the time badly. I will not waste this page on apology. I will waste it on facts, because facts are what you trusted in, and I am asking you to trust them one more time.*

*I did not die at Ypres. I was buried by a shell that killed the two men beside me, and I woke under the earth, and what happened after is a longer story than this page, and I will tell it to you or I will die with it untold, but not here. The short of it: I was pulled out, and I was broken, and I was passed from hospital to hospital like a parcel with the address smudged, and when I could finally walk and speak and say my own name, the name I said was the wrong name for the country I was in. The border moved, Ada. It moved over me while I lay in a bed in a town that used to be one thing and is now another, and the men with the new maps looked at my papers and my accent and my face, and they did not see a Dane who had bled for the wrong king. They saw an enemy alien. That is the phrase. It is written about me in an official hand, and I have learned that an official hand can make a living man into a category.*

*I am to be deported. Put on a ship and sent to a country that is not mine either, in the spring of their convenience. There is a hearing, of a kind. There is a clerk, of a kind. There is no one, Ada, of any kind, who knows what I was before the maps moved—except you.*

*The slip of paper in this envelope has a name on it and a date. The name is the ship. The date is the tide. I am not asking you to come. I am asking you to answer. You made me a promise in a garden, and I have thought about that garden every day for four years, and I am reaching, Ada. I am reaching the only way a man in my position can reach.*

*If this letter reaches you, then someone found you, and if someone found you, then you can be found, and if you can be found, then the world is not entirely made of what they write about us.*

*Your trees. Tell the Costard by the wall I still haven't forgotten her.*

*Henrik*

The stove ticked. The rain worked at the roof. Ada stood with the page in her hands and discovered that she was not breathing, and made herself breathe, the way she had once made herself breathe over men whose lungs were filling, because panic was a luxury the patient could not afford her.

The garden. She was there before she consented to be—the convalescent garden at the rear of the hospital at Trouville, June of 1919, the roses past their best and the air smelling of salt and cut grass. Henrik in a chair with a blanket over his ruined knee, thin as a lath, his hair grown back wrong, laughing at something she'd said about the head doctor's moustache. And then not laughing. And then his hand coming across the arm of the chair toward hers, slowly, the way you reach toward a bird, or a flame, or the last thing you will ever be allowed to want—and his voice, not asking, never asking, only stating the terms of it like a man laying a card face-up on the table: *If I ever reach for you, Ada—wherever it is, whenever it is, however badly the world is arranged around us—if I ever reach for you, will you answer?*

And she had said yes. She had said yes the way you say yes to a thing you already are.

The slip of paper lay on the table. She picked it up at last. A ship's name, in the same leftward hand: *MS Astrid, out of the northern port, sailing on the morning tide.* And a date. She counted, because counting was the discipline that held her together, and the number she arrived at made the barn seem suddenly very small and very far from everywhere.

Eleven days.

"Fröken Eriksen." The courier had risen, or half-risen, his hands hovering as though she might fall and he might catch her, or might not be permitted to. "The man who paid me said you might take it badly. He said—" Tomas stopped, recalibrated. "He said, *tell her the hand is real. Tell her to check the *t*.*"

Ada looked up.

"The crossbar," the man said, uncertain now, a messenger who had carried his message and found the message was a live thing. "On the *t*. He said you'd know."

She looked down at the page. The crossbars of every *t* struck upward at the end, quick and confident, like a man reaching for the next word. Like a hand reaching across the arm of a chair in a garden in June.

The past did not knock, Ada thought. It did not knock. It waited at the gate in the rain, patient as weather, and when you finally opened up, it came through the floorboards, through the ledger's hiding place, through six years of careful silence, and it stood in your barn dripping, and it said: *I am reaching. Answer.*

She folded the letter along its old creases. Her hands had steadied. That was the strange thing—somewhere between the first line and the last, the tremor had gone out of them, replaced by something harder and older and far more dangerous.

"What's your name?" she asked the courier.

"Lindqvist. Tomas Lindqvist." He hesitated. "You'll want to know how to reach me, I suppose. In case."

"In case," Ada agreed. She looked at him—at the soaked coat, the road-worn boots, the eyes that had done the work of a scout on an empty road—and she understood that her life, the small invisible life she had built plank by plank over the grave of her heart, had just been read its summons.

"Mr. Lindqvist," she said, "you'd better tell me everything about the man who paid you. And then you'd better tell me exactly how you found my gate."
