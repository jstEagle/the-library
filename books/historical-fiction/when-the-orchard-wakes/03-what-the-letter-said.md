## 3. What the Letter Said

The letter lay on the kitchen table between them, and for a long moment neither of them touched it.

Rain ticked against the window. The fire in the stove had been fed an hour ago and had settled into that low orange murmur that meant the house was warm enough to forget it was cold outside. Ada stood with her hands still damp from the basin where she'd washed the road's mud from Tomas Lindqvist's coat, and she looked at the envelope the way she had once looked at unopened field post—knowing that whatever was written inside had already happened, that the paper was only the slowest part of the news.

"You're certain it's for me," she said.

"The name on it is Ada Eriksen, Ravnsgaard Orchard." He had the flat, careful diction of a man who had rehearsed a thing too many times. "I was paid to see it into your hands. Nothing more."

*Paid.* The word snagged on something in her, some old nurse's instinct that sorted men into categories by the way they said money. But his eyes were honest enough, or honest the way weather is honest—it does what it does regardless of what you'd prefer.

She sat down. The chair complained, as it always did, and the sound was so ordinary, so much a part of the grammar of her evenings, that for a moment the letter seemed impossible. Letters like this belonged to other houses. To houses where someone had died properly, with a grave and a date and a government's apology folded inside.

She picked it up. The paper was thin, cheap, the kind sold in station kiosks along the border lines where men who had no country bought stamps. The handwriting on the front stopped her breath.

She knew that hand. She had watched it for two years—on charts, on the backs of cigarette packets, on the margins of a book of poems he'd carried in his breast pocket until a piece of shrapnel nearly carried the pocket and the book and the man all away together. Henrik Dahl wrote his letters the way he did everything: slightly too fast, the ascenders leaning forward as if impatient to arrive, the *r*'s with a small stubborn flourish like a fist half-raised.

*Henrik Dahl.* Dead at Ypres in the autumn of 1917. Dead in every record that mattered, dead on the memorial stone by the church, dead in the mouths of the village women who said *that poor Dahl boy* in the past tense for six years until the tense itself wore smooth as a river stone.

She broke the seal.

* * *

The hospital at Rouen had a garden that was mostly mud and mostly hope, and in the spring of 1919 the hope was winning.

Ada had been there eleven months by then. She had learned to read a man's chances from the way he held his cigarette, to measure convalescence in the slow return of appetite, and to keep her face still when the matron read out the names of those who would not see the summer. She was twenty-four and she had stopped counting the dead individually because the arithmetic had grown too large to carry in any single mind, and so she had begun, in a small notebook meant for supply inventories, to write down the names the officers omitted. The men who died of wounds that were technically survivable. The men whose families would receive the wrong information because a clerk had transposed a regimental number. The men nobody claimed.

Henrik had been in the third bed from the window for six weeks, his left arm in a cage of splints, his humor intact in a way the doctors found medically suspicious. He was a Dane who had enlisted in the British service for reasons he explained differently every time she asked—adventure, a girl in Hull, a bet, the simple fact that the war was there and he was a young man and the world in 1914 had seemed to belong to whoever showed up for it.

"You're writing again," he said that afternoon, nodding at the notebook. "More ghosts for your collection."

"They're not ghosts. They're names. There's a difference."

"Is there?" He shifted, wincing, and made it into a joke by the time the wince reached his mouth. "In my experience a name is just a ghost that hasn't been introduced yet."

"Then let me introduce them properly. Somewhere. To someone. Someday."

He looked at her then—not the ward-round look, not the flirtation he rationed like everything else, but the look of a man reading something. "You'll wear yourself out, Ada Eriksen, carrying other people's dead."

"Better than carrying my own."

The garden smelled of wet earth and the chemical sweetness of the antiseptic works downstream. A plane went over, high, and neither of them flinched, which was itself a kind of recovery. His good hand lay on the blanket between them, and after a while—she would remember the length of the after, the way the after stretched like taffy—he turned it palm up.

"If I ever reach for you," he said, "answer."

It was not a proposal. It was not poetry, or not only poetry. It was the specific grammar of men who had learned that the future was a rumor, who asked for things in the conditional because the indicative had betrayed them too often. She understood it completely.

"I will," she said. "Every time. Even if I'm asleep. Even if it's twenty years."

"Twenty years," he said, and laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle the way young men's laughs did in those days, the way a floorboard cracks over a hidden weight. "I'll be an old man. You'll pretend not to know me."

"I never pretend."

"No," he said. "That's the whole trouble with you."

* * *

She read the letter standing up. She didn't remember standing; her body had decided on its own, the way bodies of nurses decide things, rising when the news requires a steady hand.

*My Ada. If this reaches you, then Ingrid found you, or found someone Ingrid trusts, which is nearly the same miracle.*

*You will have heard I am dead. I heard it too. It was in the papers, in the lists, and I have read my own name in the past tense so many times I have begun to think of myself in it. Here is what happened, as briefly as I can, because the brief is all they allow me.*

*I was not killed at Ypres. I was taken. A prisoner, then a hospital in Germany, then a camp, then—when the war ended and they sorted us all like seed grain—the wrong side of a line. The new border. The one drawn by men with rulers. My village is on the German side of it now, Ada. My mother's church, my father's grave, my own name in the parish book—all of it, overnight, in a country I never belonged to and that does not want me. I am a Dane by blood, a British soldier by service, and by the arithmetic of the new maps I am an enemy alien in the place where I was born.*

*They are sending me back. Deportation, they call it, as if I were a letter addressed wrongly. A ship, from a port whose name I will write below. Eleven days from the date of this letter, which will already be fewer by the time you read it. I know what you will do with this information, because I know you, and so I ask you—no. I don't ask. I promised myself I wouldn't ask.*

*Only this. In the garden you said you would answer. I have reached for you across six years and two deaths and a border that did not exist when we met. I am reaching now, Ada, with everything I have left, which is not much and is all yours.*

*If you cannot answer, I will understand. I have learned to understand things I should never have had to. But if you can—*

*—then I will know that the war ended for me too, someday, somewhere, in the handwriting of one person.*

*Your Henrik*

*P.S. — Ingrid has money. She will find a way to send it. Do not let pride make you refuse it; she has more stubbornness than coin, and both are at your service.*

The letter trembled. Not the paper—her hand.

He was alive. The fact arrived in her the way floodwater arrives in a cellar, finding every seam, filling every space she had thought was solid. Six years. Six years of the memorial stone, of the past tense, of her own careful grief that she had filed alongside the other griefs in the ledger beneath the floorboards, and all of it wrong, all of it a clerical error, all of it—

Alive.

And then, following the water, the second fact, colder: *deportation. Eleven days. A ship.*

Hope curdled in her chest like milk left out in a heat wave—still recognizably what it had been, but changed at the molecular level into something that would poison her if she swallowed it whole.

"Read it to me?" Tomas asked quietly. "No. Don't. It's not mine to hear."

"Eleven days," she said. "It says eleven days."

"From when it was written." He glanced at the postmark, or where a postmark should have been; the stamp was foreign and the cancellation was a smudge. "Roads are slow. Borders are slower. I'd count on eight. Maybe seven."

Seven days. She did the arithmetic the way she had once done morphine dosages, quickly, without allowing herself to feel the weight of each number. Seven days to reach a port she'd never seen. Seven days to move a man off a ship that three governments had agreed he belonged on. Seven days for a woman whom the village knew as *the Eriksen girl who keeps to herself* to become someone who could stand in front of authority and be believed.

"Who paid you?" she asked. "You said you were paid."

"A woman in Copenhagen." He hesitated, and she saw the hesitation the way she'd once seen a patient hide a wound—by the way the rest of the body arranged itself around the concealment. "His sister. Ingrid Dahl."

"And the money was for?"

"The letter." He met her eyes and did not flinch away from the question underneath the question. "That's what I was told to deliver. The letter."

*What I was told to deliver.* Not: *that's all it was for.* She filed the distinction away with all the other small observations she had never had anyone to share them with.

She walked to the window. The orchard stood black and dripping in the rain, the old trees holding their shapes against the grey like men who had learned to stand at attention through anything. The oldest tree—Henrik's tree, the one he had grafting-knifed with her the summer before everything—was barely visible now, just a darker darkness, a presence she knew by faith rather than sight.

He was alive. He was alive and he was on a ship in eleven days—seven—and the last time she had seen him she had said *twenty years* and meant it as a joke, the way you joke about things too enormous to say plainly.

"Frau Eriksen." Tomas had risen too. "What will you do?"

The question hung in the warm kitchen air. What would she do. She thought of the ledger beneath the floorboard in the corner of this very room—forty-odd names, dates, testimonies, the archive of men the war had misfiled. She thought of Magistrate Holm, whose face she had seen at church and whose reputation for correctness she had heard described in the tone villagers used for weather they didn't like. She thought of the border, the port, the ship, the machinery—enormous, indifferent, oiled with procedure—that had already decided Henrik Dahl's future and needed only to execute it.

She thought: *I answered him in a garden in 1919 and I have been answering him ever since, every morning I got up and kept this orchard alive, though I didn't know to whom.*

"I'm going to need to know about the ports," she said. "The schedules. Which ships, which lines, who inspects the manifests. Everything you know."

Tomas Lindqvist looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes—she would understand it later, though not tonight—that was either respect or the recalculation of a debt.

"That," he said slowly, "may be more than I was paid for."

"Everything worth doing is," Ada said, and reached for her coat.

* * *

Outside, the rain had softened to a mist that hung in the orchard like breath. Ada walked the rows without her lantern, because she knew the ground the way she knew her own hands, and she stopped beneath the oldest tree and put her palm against its bark, wet and cold and alive.

"Seven days," she said to it, or to him, or to the dark. "Hold on."

The tree said nothing. Trees never do. But somewhere under the wet bark the sap was already moving, already climbing, the way it did every year without permission from anyone—without papers, without a country, without asking the magistrate whether it was allowed to rise.

She stood there until the cold drove her in, and then she went inside, lifted the floorboard in the corner of the kitchen, and took out her ledger.

It was time, she thought, to find out what forty names weighed.

And whether they were heavy enough to stop a ship.
