## 6. The Sister's Money

The inn at Källby had a mirror above the bar, and Tomas Lindqvist had spent three days learning not to look at it.

It was a habit from the road. A courier learned early that his face was a document, and documents could be read. So he kept his eyes on his beer, on the window, on the rain stitching itself down the glass, and he told himself the discomfort in his chest was only the damp working into his lungs. It had been a wet autumn. Wet autumns did this to a man.

But the mirror was patient, and eventually a man had to shave.

He looked up once, briefly, the way a man checks a lock he already knows is fastened. The face that looked back was forty-one years old and had the particular weathering of a man who had spent two decades being paid to carry things that were not his: parcels, letters, sums of money, other men's apologies. His hair was going grey at the temples in a way he suspected women found dignified. His hands, resting on the bar, were steady. They were always steady. That was the whole of his trade, really—steady hands and a face that gave nothing back.

*You kept her money,* said the face in the mirror, in his mother's voice, dead these nineteen years and still finding new ways to disappoint her.

*I kept a fee,* Tomas answered, in his own voice, which was the voice of a reasonable man. *Couriers keep fees. It's how couriers eat.*

The mirror declined to argue. That was the trouble with mirrors. They only ever showed you the reasonable man.

* * *

He had met Ingrid Dahl in a back room of a Copenhagen boarding house that smelled of boiled fish and church candles, and he had known within four minutes that she was the kind of woman who counted her spoons.

Not mean. He wanted to be fair about it, even now, alone with his beer. Not mean—*precise*. She had laid the money out on the table in bundles tied with string, and she had written down the amount in a little notebook, and she had made him sign for it, and she had watched him sign with the flat, unblinking attention of a woman who had learned in the last six years that every person who smiled at her wanted something and every person who wanted something would lie.

"Two hundred and forty kroner," she said. "Half now. Half when the letter is in her hands."

"And the other half of the errand?"

"There is no other half of the errand. There is the letter, and there is the money, and there is a lawyer in Malmö named Bergström whose card is in the envelope. The money is for the lawyer. The letter is for the woman. You will not confuse them, Mr. Lindqvist, because I will know if you confuse them."

"And how will you know?"

She had looked at him then with an expression he had seen only twice before in his life—once on a customs officer in Flensburg, once on his brother Nils, the night before Nils walked out of their mother's house for the last time. It was the expression of a person who had already imagined the worst and found it survivable.

"Because I am poor," Ingrid Dahl said, "and poor people learn to count."

* * *

He had kept thirty kroner.

Not at first. That was the thing he wanted the mirror to understand, if mirrors could understand anything. In Copenhagen he had been honest to the krone. He had carried the envelope against his chest through Malmö, through the ferry crossing, through two days of rain on roads that turned to porridge, and he had thought of the money the way a priest thinks of the host—something carried, not owned. He had even, in a moment of weakness outside Ystad, taken the lawyer's card out and read it under an awning: *Advokat Bergström, Stortorget, Malmö. Foreigners' cases. Repatriation. Deportation appeals.* A whole trade in it now, he'd thought. A whole profession grown up around the new lines on the map, like mushrooms after rain.

Then he had reached the village, and found the woman Eriksen, and delivered the letter, and seen her face do the thing that faces did when the dead came back through the floorboards—and something in Tomas had quietly shifted its weight, the way cargo shifts in a lorry on a bad curve, and by the time he noticed it had shifted, it was too late to shift it back.

Thirty kroner. He had told himself it was expenses. The road from Copenhagen in November was not free; the inn at Källby was not free; a man could not keep his hands steady on an empty stomach. Thirty kroner out of two hundred and ten was not theft, it was *accounting*.

But last night, lying in the narrow bed with the rain working at the shutters, he had taken out the envelope and counted it again, and his thumb had paused on the second bundle, and he had thought: *the lawyer only needs half of this to take the case on appeal. Bergström's card says he takes foreigners' cases. A man who has built a trade on deportation appeals knows which cases pay and which cases are charity, and he will take half now and the rest when it's won, because that is how lawyers count too.*

And underneath that thought, quieter, in the voice he did not let himself hear in daylight: *and the woman will never know the difference, and the sister is four hundred miles away, and you could be in Hamburg by Friday.*

He had put the envelope back under the floorboard of his room—every old inn in this country had a loose floorboard; it was practically a national institution—and he had lain in the dark and listened to the rain, and he had felt the particular nausea of a man who has caught himself mid-sentence in a lie he has not yet decided to tell.

* * *

The trouble—the real trouble, deeper than thirty kroner or three hundred—was Nils.

Nils Lindqvist, younger by four years, taller by an inch, better with horses and worse with money, who had gone out in '14 with the second wave and come back in '19 through a tangle of paperwork that no one in the family had ever managed to unpick. That was the polite way to say it. The impolite way, the way their mother had said it every night for six years until it killed her, was that Nils had come back *wrong*—come back across a border that had not existed when he left, into a country that now spelled his name with a different set of suspicions, and the paperwork that should have made him a returned soldier had instead made him, in the eyes of some clerk with a rubber stamp, a man of the wrong category. *Category C. Enemy origin. Pending review.*

Pending review. Six years of pending review. Nils had waited out the six years the way a man waits out weather, in a room at the back of a cousin's farm, doing work that paid in food, keeping his head down, learning to be invisible in the way that men of the wrong category learned it—thoroughly, permanently, until invisibility stopped being a tactic and became a personality.

And now Nils's name was on a list.

Tomas had seen the list. That was the part that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone. In Malmö, waiting for the ferry, he had passed the shipping office and seen the notice pasted in the window—the *Elsbeth*, sailing on the morning tide, passenger manifest posted for objection, and among the names, printed in the same indifferent type as all the others: *Lindqvist, N., labourer, repatriation order, category C.* His brother's name, published like a price. Eleven days. He had stood in front of that window in the rain for a long time, long enough that a constable had drifted over to look at him, and Tomas had smiled the smile that gave nothing back and walked away, and had gone and delivered Ingrid Dahl's letter as though the world were a place where a man could do one honest errand at a time.

The ship that would carry Henrik Dahl away from the woman who loved him was the same ship that would carry Nils Lindqvist away from the last country that would tolerate him. One tide. Two men. And Tomas Lindqvist, professional carrier of other people's burdens, standing in a village inn with thirty stolen kroner and a conscience that had begun, inconveniently and against all his commercial interests, to speak.

* * *

He went out in the late afternoon because the walls had gotten too close. The rain had thinned to a grey drifting mist, the kind that didn't fall so much as accumulate, and he walked up past the church and along the lane where the Eriksen orchard began, its gate leaning, its rows of apple trees running up the slope like a congregation that had stopped believing but still showed up on Sundays.

She was out there. He could see her small figure between the trunks, moving with a purpose he recognized—he had spent enough years around farms to know the difference between a woman doing chores and a woman planning something. Ada Eriksen was planning something. She moved through the trees the way he moved through train stations: eyes down, hands busy, everything noted.

He stood at the gate a moment, not entering. He had no reason to enter. His errand was done; the letter was in her hands; the money was—well. The money was where it was. A courier's obligations ended at delivery, and everything after that was other people's weather.

But he stood there anyway, and he let himself do the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since Malmö.

The letter said eleven days. The lawyer was in Malmö. The lawyer was four hours' train from here and one day's work from the shipping office where the manifest hung in the window, and Tomas knew that office, knew the clerk who kept it—a soft, frightened man named Poulsen who owed Tomas a favor from the winter Tomas had carried a certain sealed envelope to a certain address and had not, despite considerable offers, asked what was in it. Discretion was a currency. Tomas had a small purse of it.

And Nils was on that ship, and Nils did not know that Tomas knew, and Nils would board it on the morning tide with his one suitcase and his category-C papers and his face that had learned to expect nothing, and he would be carried across the water to a country that had been redrawn out from under him, and that would be the end of it. Not a dramatic end. The worst ends never were. Just a slow fading, a man in a room in a town where no one knew his name, writing letters that took four months to arrive, until the letters stopped.

*You could tell him,* said his mother's voice. *You could go to Malmö and tell him you know, and put the money in his hand, and—*

*With whose money?* said the reasonable man. *The sister's money is for the sister's brother. There is a lawyer's fee in that envelope with a name on it, and the name is not Lindqvist.*

*There are two brothers in this story,* said his mother's voice, which was unfair, because his mother had never in her life been unfair, which made it worse. *You have counted one of them all your life. Count the other.*

Tomas turned away from the gate. In the orchard, Ada Eriksen had stopped moving and was standing very still beside the oldest tree at the top of the slope, her hand resting on its bark the way a person touches a door they are afraid to open. He watched her for a moment—watched her the way he had watched the manifest in the window, reading what was written there—and then he walked back down the lane through the thinning rain, and the arithmetic followed him like a dog.

* * *

That night he took the envelope out from under the floorboard and set it on the bed, and untied the string, and counted it a third time, and this time he did not lie to himself about what the counting was for.

Two hundred and ten kroner. Thirty already gone into the road and the room and the beer. A lawyer's fee that would take half of what remained and buy one appeal, for one man, in one case, and the shipping office would stamp its papers and the clerk Poulsen would shuffle his files and somewhere in the machinery of the new Europe a name would be lifted from a list and set carefully aside—*pending review*, the gentlest words in the language, the words that meant *not yet*.

And there would be nothing left over. Not for Nils. Not for the second name on the second list. Unless.

Unless a man who had spent twenty years carrying other people's burdens decided, at forty-one, to carry one of his own. Unless he went to Malmö not as a courier but as a brother, and stood in Poulsen's office, and spent the last coin of his discretion—the sealed envelope, the unasked question, the whole small purse of it—on one name struck from one manifest. And unless he then found the money to pay for it from somewhere, or someone, or some margin that existed only if a lawyer's fee could be made to stretch, the way fees could stretch, if a man was honest with everyone except the arithmetic.

He sat on the bed with the money in his hands and the rain at the shutters and he understood, with the clarity that comes only to men at night, that he had already decided. He had decided it on the ferry, or in the shipping office window, or perhaps six years ago in his mother's kitchen watching Nils eat in silence like a man who had been taught that appetite was a luxury. The thirty kroner had never been about thirty kroner. It had been the first crack in the dam, and the water behind it was six years deep.

*The woman Eriksen,* he thought. *She is going to do something. She is planning it up there among her trees, and she has eleven days, and whatever she does she will need a man who knows the roads and the offices and the price of silence.*

And he thought: *and I will need a reason to be near her when she does.*

He retied the string. He put the envelope back under the floorboard, and pressed the board down with his heel until it sat flush, and lay back in the dark, and for the first time in three days he did not avoid the mirror on the wall across the room, because in the dark a mirror was only a window with the lights off, and a man could be anyone he had not yet decided to be.

Tomorrow, he would go back to the orchard gate.

Tomorrow, he would tell Ada Eriksen that the ship's schedule was not the only schedule that mattered—that there was a manifest in a window in Malmö with names on it, and a clerk who owed him, and a brother he had never once said aloud he was trying to save—and he would watch her face and decide, in the way couriers decided everything, how much of the truth she could carry.

And if she asked him where the money had gone, he would have to look her in the eye, the sister's money warm and guilty under his floorboards, and answer her.

The rain kept on. Eleven days. Ten, now, if you counted the way Tomas counted—by tides, by trains, by the shrinking distance between a man and the thing he could no longer not do.
