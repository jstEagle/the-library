## 8. The Magistrate's Tea

The pretext Ada chose was rates. It was the dullest thing she could think of, and dullness, she had learned long ago in the wards at Étaples, was the best disguise a woman could wear. Nobody watched a woman who had come about the drainage assessment on the orchard road. Nobody remembered her an hour later. She put on her good coat—the grey one with the mended cuff she turned under—and walked the four miles into Vesterby with the letter's words folded inside her like a splinter working toward the heart.

*They have classified me as an enemy alien. There is a ship on the eleventh of the month.*

Eleven days when Tomas had said it. Ten now, if you counted honestly, and Ada counted honestly; it was one of the few virtues the war had left her.

The magistrate's house stood at the end of Kirkevej, behind a hedge that had been allowed to grow wild since his wife died. Ada remembered Mrs. Holm from before—remembered the way she'd deadheaded the roses every August and handed the trimmings over the fence to children who hadn't asked for them. Now the hedge swallowed the gateposts whole, and the windows had the blind look of rooms no one bothered to light until dark made it necessary.

She knocked. A long pause, long enough for her to rehearse her opening sentence twice, and then Holm himself opened the door—not a housekeeper, not a clerk, just the man, in carpet slippers and a waistcoat buttoned wrong by one.

"Mrs. Eriksen." He knew her name. Of course he did; he knew everyone's name within his jurisdiction, which was precisely what made him dangerous. "You've come about the road assessment."

"The drainage on the orchard lane," she said. "It floods where the culvert's collapsed, and I'm told the parish is responsible."

"And you're told correctly." He stepped back to admit her, and there was something in the gesture—a small tired courtesy—that reminded her of doctors at the end of a long shift. "Come in. I'll make tea. My girl has Thursdays off and I refuse to feel sorry about it."

* * *

His study smelled of pipe smoke and cold ash. Books everywhere, but not arranged—accumulated, the way driftwood accumulates against a sea wall. On the desk, beneath a paperweight of green glass, lay a stack of files squared with a precision that contradicted everything else in the room. Ada's eyes went to them the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

Holm filled the kettle in the little alcove off the study rather than summon anyone, and she understood this too: a man who made his own tea was a man who did not want witnesses to his conversations.

"You keep the old Dahl place now," he said, his back to her. "The orchard above the mill."

"I do."

"Henrik Dahl's family." He said the name the way you'd set down a heavy object—carefully, to see whether it would roll. "He was at Ypres."

"He was."

"They sent us the notice in '17. His mother took it hard. Ingrid came to my office twice afterward about the pension paperwork, and both times she cried in my waiting room, and both times I could do nothing for her except let her cry somewhere warm." He lit the burner. The kettle began its low gathering hum. "That is most of what a magistrate does, Mrs. Eriksen. Letting people cry somewhere warm while the paperwork proceeds."

Ada folded her hands in her lap and waited. She had learned patience from men dying slowly; a kettle would not defeat her.

He brought the tea—brown earthenware, no saucer for her, only a cup—and sat down across the desk from her instead of behind it, which she noted as either kindness or tactic and could not yet decide which.

"Now," he said. "The culvert."

"The culvert," she agreed, and they spoke of drainage for six full minutes. He knew the lane, knew the fall of the land, knew that the mill race backed up in wet Novembers. He wrote nothing down. And through all of it, Ada felt the stack of squared files sitting at the edge of her vision like a dog that hasn't barked yet.

When she rose to go, thanking him, promising to send measurements, he said, quietly, without looking up from pouring the last of the pot into his own cup:

"There was a letter."

The room did not change. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, a cart went by on the church road, and somewhere a dog gave two uninterested barks. But inside Ada's chest, everything tilted, the way a floor tilts when the ground beneath it has decided otherwise.

"A letter?" she said.

"From across the border. Circulating." He set down the pot. "The border authorities flagged it at Flensburg. They flag everything now—it's cheaper than reading anything—but this one they read, because it mentioned a name on their lists. A repatriated man. Wrong side of the line when the border moved, which happens to be the story of half the district, though you wouldn't know it from the way we behave." He looked up at her then, and his eyes were bloodshot at the rims, the eyes of a man who slept badly. "The letter was addressed to a courier. The courier passed through here."

"Tomas Lindqvist," Ada said. The name left her mouth before she had decided to spend it.

"Yes." Holm turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, aligning the handle with the desk edge, some small ceremony of order. "And the letter concerned Henrik Dahl. Who is dead."

"Who is *dead*," Ada repeated, and heard how flat her voice came out, and hated herself for the flatness, because flatness was a confession to anyone listening properly, and Holm was listening properly. That was the whole trouble with him. He was not clever in the way of men who trap you; he was clever in the way of men who simply see what is in front of them and are too tired to pretend otherwise.

"Sit down again, Mrs. Eriksen."

She sat.

* * *

For a while neither of them spoke. The fire in the grate had burned down to a red seam along one log, and the light through the window had gone the colour of weak tea itself, afternoon surrendering early the way it did in October.

"Do you know what my instructions are?" Holm asked at last. "Regarding such a letter?"

"No."

"To identify any party in correspondence with persons classified as enemy aliens. To record names. To forward those names." He recited it in the voice of a catechism learned by a boy who no longer believed in the catechism. "I have done it eleven times since spring. Eleven names forwarded. Do you know what happened to them? Nothing visible. One man lost his coal allotment. One woman's brother was refused a travel permit to attend their father's funeral. Small things. Administrative things. The kind of things that don't look like punishment because there's no form that says *punishment* on them." He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger. "I write the letters myself. My own hand. Eleven times."

"Why tell me this?"

"Because you came into my house about a culvert," he said, "and we both know you didn't, and I am fifty-eight years old and too tired for the part where we pretend." He leaned back. "Here is what I know, and I will say it once. A letter exists. It is in a handwriting the border office believes belongs to a dead man, which makes it either a forgery or a miracle, and they have no procedure for miracles. A courier carried it and is presently drinking his way through the credit at the Harborside Inn. And the widow Eriksen—" he corrected himself, gently, "—the *missus*, forgive me, habits die harder than men—Miss Eriksen, who has not set foot in my office in five years, chooses this particular week to ask after drainage."

The trap, Ada thought. Not sprung. Simply described, patiently, in front of the fire, with tea going cold between them. That was somehow worse than irons. A man like Holm didn't need to close anything. He needed only to show you the shape of the closing, and let your own fear do the work.

"If there were a letter," she said carefully, "and I'm not saying there is—"

"Of course."

"—what would happen to whoever received it?"

Holm was quiet for a moment. He got up, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at his dead wife's hedge.

"When I was a young clerk in Schleswig," he said, "before all of it, before the redrawn maps—I processed emigration papers. Whole families. And there was a rule even then, and the rule was that the file must be complete, and I remember thinking, a complete file, what a fine thing, everything in its place. Then the war came and I learned what gets filed. Death notices. Deportation orders. The names of eleven people whose lives I made smaller with a pen." He turned around. "What would happen? An inquiry. Questions put to you, formally, in a room with a stenographer. Your circumstances examined. Whether you aided communication with a detained person, whether money changed hands, whether you intend to depart yourself." He paused. "And your orchard, Mrs. Eriksen. Land held by a single woman of foreign-born parentage is not a thing this district protects. You must know that better than anyone."

She did know it. She had known it every day for five years, walking her own rows like a ghost on someone else's property, invisible and therefore safe, and here was the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the letter arrived: invisibility was not safety. It was only safety on loan, at interest, callable at any hour by any clerk with a flagged letter.

"I see," she said.

"I don't think you entirely do." Holm came back to the desk but did not sit. He stood over the squared files, one hand resting on them—not protectively, she thought. More the way a man rests his hand on a coffin lid. "Because here is the other thing I will say once, and then we will speak of culverts for the benefit of God and the hedge. I am required to report that a letter circulated. I am *not* required to report every name I suspect of having received it. Suspicion is not evidence. Evidence requires more than a woman asking about drainage." His jaw worked. "But the border office writes to me weekly now. They want confirmation of local contacts. They want the courier questioned. And if they come themselves—and they will, they're coming, probably within days—then it leaves my hands entirely, and I cannot protect so much as your garden fence from men who carry their own forms."

The kettle ticked. Down the hall, a clock struck four with the reluctance of an old man climbing stairs.

"Then I'll say something once, Magistrate," Ada said, and heard her own voice steady, the ward-voice, the one she'd used to talk men through the worst nights of their lives. "If a man survived Ypres and spent six years on the wrong side of a line drawn by politicians—if he is to be put on a ship and sent further from everyone who ever loved him, for the crime of surviving in the wrong place—then somebody ought to be asking questions in a room with a stenographer. And it oughtn't to be him."

Holm looked at her for a long time. Something moved behind the tiredness—not warmth exactly, but recognition. The look of one exhausted animal seeing another across a clearing.

"You were a nurse," he said. "At the front."

"I was."

"I thought so. You have the voice." He straightened the files on his desk, squaring what was already square. "Go home, Miss Eriksen. Prune your trees. Mind your culvert. And if a letter should ever reach you—which I am not saying it has—do not answer it in writing. Do not answer it through any channel a clerk can hold in his hand." He opened the study door for her, and the hallway beyond was already dimming toward evening. "Answer it the way people answered things before there were offices. In person. Quickly. While the answering is still possible."

"Is it still possible?" she asked. "The ship—"

"Sails on the morning tide of the eleventh," Holm said. "Or so the schedule says today. Schedules are the first casualty of any bureaucracy, Miss Eriksen; I have watched them move dates the way weather moves clouds, and never once in the direction of mercy." He held out his hand, formal, correct, and she took it, and his grip was dry and light and brief. "Good afternoon. Send me your culvert measurements. I mean that. I will actually process them. It will be the most honest work either of us does this week."

* * *

She walked home in the blue hour, the hedgerows black on either side of the lane, her breath beginning to show. Behind her the village lit its windows one by one, each square of yellow a household deciding, as it did every night, to mind its own fire and its own supper and none of the world's business. Ahead of her the orchard waited, invisible in the dark, five hundred trees holding their silence.

Ten days. Perhaps fewer, if schedules moved like clouds.

Holm had told her nothing, and in telling her nothing had told her everything: the border men were coming, the courier would be questioned, and whatever she meant to do would have to be done in person, quickly, through no channel that could be held in a clerk's hand. *In person.* Which meant the port. Which meant leaving the orchard, leaving the ledger's hiding place, leaving the careful invisible life she had built plank by plank over her own floorboards.

At the gate she stopped. Somewhere down the valley a train whistled—the evening goods run, hauling sugar beet toward the coast, toward the same stretch of shoreline where a ship waited with Henrik's name on a manifest. She stood in the cold listening to it fade, and thought: *he taught me grafting in this orchard; he reached for my hand in a hospital garden; and I have ten days to cross forty miles of checkpoints with nothing but a dead man's handwriting and my own name, which is now, as of this afternoon, spoken aloud in a magistrate's study.*

Inside, she lit the lamp, knelt, and lifted the loose board.

The ledger lay where it always lay. But as her fingers closed on it, she heard Holm's voice again—*they want the courier questioned*—and thought of Tomas Lindqvist drinking his credit at the Harborside Inn, a stranger who knew her secret, carrying a letter the authorities had already flagged, and she understood with a cold settling certainty that the next knock would not be hers to choose.

It would come to the inn first.
