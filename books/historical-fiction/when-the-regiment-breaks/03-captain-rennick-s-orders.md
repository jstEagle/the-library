## 3. Captain Rennick's Orders

The orders arrived at four in the morning, which told me everything I needed to know before I'd read a single word.

Important orders come at civilized hours. They arrive with clerks and witnesses and the faint smell of coffee, because the men who issue them want them executed properly and on the record. Orders that arrive at four in the morning, carried by a lieutenant who won't meet your eye and a courier who's already turning his horse around, are the kind that want to be executed *quickly* — before anyone has time to think, least of all the officer holding them.

I stood in the doorway of the requisitioned customs house with the envelope in my hand and watched the courier's dust settle over the empty market square, and I thought: *well, Rennick. Here it is.*

I've been a soldier for nineteen years. I can read the army the way a farmer reads weather. And the weather, that morning, was turning.

* * *

The envelope was sealed with the double eagle of the Provisional Command, which was itself a small joke, since the Provisional Command had spent the last month behaving less like a provisional government and more like a man burning his own house down to keep the neighbors from inheriting it. Inside were two pages. The first was typed. The second was handwritten, which was worse. Typed orders are policy. Handwritten orders are a man's name attached to a decision, and men who attach their names at four in the morning are either very confident or very frightened.

The typed page was straightforward enough. All civic records in the requisitioned territory — registries, ledgers, correspondence, municipal archives, anything held by the town clerk, the tax office, the parish, or, and here the sentence paused in a way I would later think about for a long time, *any licensed courier house* — were to be seized, catalogued, and transported under escort to Command headquarters at Breche. Nothing was to be left behind. Nothing was to be copied.

The handwritten page was shorter. It said, in a slanting, impatient script I did not recognize: *Anything of an inflammatory nature — lists, denunciations, compilations of names — is to be destroyed on site. Destruction to be confirmed by the seizing officer personally. No inventory required.*

No inventory required.

I have burned things before. Every officer has. You burn a bridge, a granary, a uniform that would fit the enemy. Burning is a soldier's verb; it's honest, in its way. But *no inventory required* is not a soldier's phrase. That's a lawyer's phrase. That is a phrase written by someone who wants to be able to say, later, with a straight face, that no one knows what was destroyed because no one was supposed to know what existed.

I read both pages twice, folded them, and put them in my breast pocket, where I could feel them against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Then I woke my sergeant.

* * *

Sergeant Dax Olem has been with me for eleven years, which means he has been with me through the retreat at Corven, the winter at Salles, and the business at the foundry that we do not discuss. He is a large, deliberate man who moves through the world the way a barge moves through a canal — slowly, with immense displaced consequences. He read the orders over my shoulder by lamplight and said nothing for a long moment.

"Courier houses," he said finally.

"I saw it."

"That's new."

"It is."

Olem set the orders down on the table with the care of a man handling something that might go off. "Courier houses keep names, sir. That's their trade. Who carried what, to whom, when." He rubbed his jaw. "Army doesn't usually care about the *post*."

"The army cares about what the post carried." I poured the last of the coffee — requisitioned, like everything else in this town, down to the cups we drank it from — and didn't drink it. "There's a ledger, Dax."

"There's always a ledger."

"Not like this one. The clerk's office sent a manifest last week, before the requisition finalized. Standard procedure. Most of it's the usual — tax rolls, birth registers, grain accounts." I turned the cup in a slow circle. "But there was one item listed under sealed deposit. A courier house holding. Described as a *consolidated record of names*, compiled by the town's licensed couriers over — I don't know, years. Decades, maybe. The kind of thing they keep so that when a letter goes missing, or a man disappears, somebody, somewhere, can prove he existed."

Olem looked at me. Olem is very good at looking at me in a way that makes me finish sentences I'd rather not.

"And Command wants that," he said, "burned."

"Command wants anything *inflammatory* burned. And they've defined inflammatory as lists of names."

"Every record in a town is a list of names, Captain."

"Yes," I said. "I'd noticed."

* * *

Here is the thing I have not told Olem, because I have not fully told myself.

I recognized the seal.

The manifest from the clerk's office had included, as manifests do, a description of the sealed deposit's authentication: the courier house's registration mark, the town's countersignature, and the seal of the archive it was destined for — Vellacourt, the neutral archive, the great cold library where documents go to become history instead of becoming ammunition. And beneath all that, pressed into the wax of the outer wrapping, a secondary mark. A courier's guild seal. The old one. The one from before the guilds were reorganized, before the war, back when I was nineteen years old at the academy at Marren and a gray-haired instructor with a limp made us memorize authentication marks the way other instructors made us memorize artillery ranges.

*You will see a hundred thousand documents in your career, gentlemen,* he'd told us, tapping the wax with his cane. *Most of them will be lies with good handwriting. The seal is the one honest thing on the page, because a seal is a promise made by an institution, and institutions are the only liars with something to lose.*

I was nineteen and thought this was very funny. I am thirty-eight and no longer do.

The seal on that manifest was the Marren guild mark. The old one. Which meant the ledger had been authenticated under the old system, by the old hands, by people who had been taught — as I had been taught — that a record is a promise. And someone at Command, at four in the morning, had decided that this particular promise was *inflammatory*.

* * *

We moved at dawn, because the orders said to move at dawn, and because I have learned that the best way to survive an order you don't like is to execute it so carefully that no one can accuse you of anything except thoroughness.

The town was already awake in the way towns are awake after a requisition — that is, shutters closed, streets empty, and every eye behind every shutter tracking our boots. Requisitioned towns have a particular silence. It isn't peace. It's the silence of people counting your soldiers and deciding, for now, not to run. I've been on the other side of that silence once, early in my career, in a village whose name I've made a discipline of forgetting, and I have never since been able to walk through it without my neck prickling.

We took the clerk's office first. That was easy. Clerks are soldiers of a different army, and when the other army wins, they hand over their files with a certain professional dignity, as if to say: *we kept the records; what you do with them is your dishonor, not ours.* The town clerk, a dry woman named Perrin with ink on three of her fingers, watched my men stack her life's work into crates and said only, "You'll want the courier house done before the light's good. The couriers keep odd hours."

"Is that a warning, madame, or a scheduling suggestion?"

"Both, Captain." She looked at me over her spectacles. "They're the only people in this town who know where everything is. Including, I suspect, things you'd rather they didn't."

I thanked her, because it was true, and because I was already beginning to understand that the orders in my pocket had less to do with the clerk's office than with whatever the courier house knew.

* * *

The courier house sat on the Rue des Postes, a narrow building with a wide door, the kind of door built for riders coming in fast. There was a lamp burning inside. At six in the morning. In a requisitioned town. Either someone had forgotten to put it out, or someone was awake, and I had a feeling I knew which.

I posted Olem at the door with four men and went in with two more, because I have found that searches go better with fewer boots. The front room was exactly what you'd expect: sorting tables, pigeonholes, a wall of cubbies with faded labels in three languages. The back room was the registry — the courier house's own records, the logs of what had been carried where, by whom, for whom. And there, on a shelf behind the registry desk, in a place of honor that was also a place of perfect ordinariness, sat a row of bound volumes. Tax abstracts, by the look of the spines. Municipal summaries. The kind of thing an inspector would flip through and put back in under a minute.

I pulled the third one down and opened it, and the hair on my arms stood up.

Because it was a tax abstract. It genuinely was. The pages were full of assessments, exemptions, grain levies — real entries, in a real clerk's hand. But the binding was wrong. The stitching was the guild's old cross-stitch, the kind they'd taught us to recognize at Marren, and the pages were too thick, and when I tilted the volume against the lamp I could see the ghost of a second text showing through the paper — a denser text, a text of columns, of names.

Someone had built a shell around the ledger. A false book, honest on every page, hiding the one page that mattered. Hidden in plain sight among the tax records, in a room the army was about to search.

I stood there holding it for what felt like a long time. My orders said to seize all civic records. My orders said to destroy, personally, anything inflammatory, no inventory required. And the volume in my hands was both things at once — a civic record and a sealed deposit, an honest shell and a dishonest core, exactly the kind of object that had been designed, by someone clever and frightened and very good at their trade, to survive precisely this morning.

"Captain?" One of my men, at the door. "The clerk from the registry's here. Says the deposit manifest needs your countersignature."

"Send it in."

The manifest was the same one I'd seen a copy of. Sealed deposit, consolidated record of names, destined Vellacourt. And beside the entry, in fresh ink, the courier house's registration mark — and beneath it, pressed deep and clean, the old Marren guild seal. The promise. The institution's honest thing.

My instructor's voice, nineteen years old and gray-haired: *a seal is a promise made by an institution.*

And Command's voice, four in the morning, slanting and impatient: *no inventory required.*

* * *

I did what the orders said. I want that on my own record, since no one else is keeping one: I did what the orders said.

We seized the clerk's office entire. We seized the parish registers and the tax office and the notary's cabinet. We seized the courier house's registry — the logs, the manifests, the cubbies, everything. It was catalogued, because the typed page said to catalogue, and cataloguing is a slow, honest, soldier's work, and I made my men do it slowly and honestly, and if that took until nearly noon, well, thoroughness is a virtue.

The sealed deposit I carried out myself, in its honest shell, and I did not burn it, and I did not open it, and I did not record in my report that the shell existed at all. My report says: *sealed deposit recovered intact per manifest, transported under personal escort.* Every word true. I have learned that the most dangerous sentences are the ones that are entirely true and arranged to mean something else. It appears the army has been teaching me its own language.

Olem asked me about it that evening, in the customs house, over the last of the requisitioned wine.

"The courier house," he said. "The deposit. You carried it out yourself."

"I did."

"Orders said destroy anything inflammatory."

"It's a tax abstract, Sergeant. I checked."

He looked at me for a long time. Olem has carried men off battlefields and lied to colonels for me and once, at Salles, held a door shut for six hours with his shoulder while the rest of us got a wounded lieutenant out, and he has never once asked me to explain myself. He wasn't going to start now. But he looked at me the way you look at a bridge you're about to cross and aren't sure will hold.

"You know what happens," he said, "when Command opens that book and finds a tax abstract."

"I do."

"And you know what happens to the officer whose countersignature is on the manifest."

"I do," I said, "which is why my countersignature will say I recovered exactly what the manifest described. A consolidated record of names." I turned the glass. "Let them open it at Breche. Let some major with clean hands read the names and decide what's inflammatory. If it's going to be burned, Dax, I find I'd rather not be the one holding the match."

"That's not obedience, Captain."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

He drank his wine and said nothing else, which from Olem is a benediction.

* * *

I sat up late that night with the two pages of orders, the typed one and the handwritten one, and I thought about the word *inflammatory*.

It's a medical word, originally. It means a wound that's festering. The army has borrowed it to mean a document that makes the wound visible — and the treatment, apparently, is not to heal the wound but to shoot the messenger who describes it. Somewhere in a warm office at Breche, a man with a slanting hand has decided that the peace will hold better if the names burn. And maybe he's right. Maybe that's what I can't stop turning over. Maybe armistices are built on forgetting, the way bridges are built on pilings, and maybe every nation that ever healed did it by agreeing, together, to stop looking at the scar.

But I was at Marren. I was nineteen. I was taught by a limping old man with a cane that a record is a promise, and that the men who burn the records are never the men the records are about. The records are about the people in them — the ones who were taxed, and registered, and carried out of their town in the backs of army trucks this week, and who will, if the slanting hand has its way, have existed never.

I put the orders in my breast pocket again, against my ribs, where they beat like a second heart.

Tomorrow the convoy leaves for Breche, and I ride escort, and the sealed deposit rides in my own wagon, in its honest shell, three feet from my knee the whole way. The route passes through the mill valley. It passes, if the maps are right, within sight of the Vellacourt road.

I have not decided anything. I want that understood. A soldier can carry a thing a long way without deciding anything at all.

But I found, falling asleep in the customs house with my boots on, that I had already memorized the Vellacourt road.
