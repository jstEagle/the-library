## 5. Three Checkpoints

Lena had learned the port the way her father had taught her to read water: not by looking at it, but by feeling where it pushed back.

The Meridian compound sat on the harbor's throat like a cork in a bottle. Everything that left this coast — pearls, copra, tin, and now, apparently, a baby — had to pass through three doors before it reached the magistrate's white veranda on the hill. Three doors, and behind each one a man with a stamp and a salary and no reason in the world to let a laundress walk through carrying contraband.

She stood at the drying lofts' open window with a pencil stub and the back of an invoice sheet, drawing the route as if she were charting a dive. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Inside, some small animal was screaming continuously, but her hands — her father's hands, broad-palmed, patient — did what they were told.

*Door one: the harbor gate.* Old Tamu sat there most days, a guard so ancient that half the port claimed he'd been present at its founding and the other half claimed he *was* the founding. He'd known Lena since she was small enough to steal mangoes from the company trees. He waved her through every morning with the enthusiasm of a man greeting weather. But he also noticed things. That was the trouble with people who loved you; they looked at your face.

*Door two: the customs house.* A long stone room smelling of ink and mildew, where clerks opened crates and closed their eyes, depending on whose crate it was. Madame Reyes ran the inspection bench. Lena had watched her work for six years without once speaking to her. The woman dove for pearls before the company bought the beds out from under the divers — everyone knew that much. Everyone also knew she'd stopped diving the year her husband's boat came back without him. There was a look she gave the young divers who came through with their fathers' belts cinched too tight around too-small waists. It was not pity. Pity would have been easier to survive.

*Door three:* and here Lena's pencil pressed hard enough to nearly tear the paper. *The counting-room checkpoint.* Meridian's own gate, inside the compound walls, between the ledger room and the world. Brandt's territory. Two guards, a logbook, and a head clerk who could smell an irregularity the way sharks smelled blood — except sharks, in Lena's experience, were more honest about what they wanted.

Three doors. And between her and door one, forty yards of open dock where anyone might see a woman carrying something that cried.

Because that was the problem, wasn't it. She could sew a ledger page into her diving belt until it was indistinguishable from sweat-stiffened leather. Pages didn't cry. Pages didn't need feeding at hours designed by some malicious spirit to coincide with the moments of maximum danger. Pages fit in fish crates.

Babies did not fit in fish crates.

She looked over at him, asleep in the laundry basket among her spare smocks, one fist curled against his cheek, utterly unconscious of the fact that he was currently the most dangerous object in the colony. His mouth worked at some dream. Probably milk. Everything about him was milk and grievance and the grey-green eyes she refused, so far successfully, to think about.

"All right," she told him quietly. "Here is the arithmetic."

Arithmetic was how she prayed. Her mother had taught her figures before letters, saying numbers don't lie to you the way words do, they only tell you truths you don't want. So Lena laid it out like sums.

Sum the first: she could not keep him. An indentured laundress keeping an unregistered infant in a company loft was not a guardian; she was a thief stealing from Meridian twice over, and when they found out — *when*, not if, because Brandt counted everything eventually — they would take the boy and her wages and probably her freedom, and the page would go into a drawer, and her father's name would stay dead twice.

Sum the second: she could not leave him. She had tried, for approximately four hours, to imagine walking away from the basket, and her body had rejected the premise entirely, the way lungs reject water. Some refusals live below thought.

Therefore: the magistrate. Therefore: three checkpoints. Therefore, the problem reduced itself, as all problems did, to breath and timing.

You didn't fight a long dive, her father used to say, crouched on the gunwale with his knife loose in his hand. You broke it into short ones. One breath for the descent. One for the work. One for the way up. Never think about the whole distance — the whole distance will drown you. Think about the next breath.

One breath: get past Tamu. One breath: get past the customs house. One breath: get past Brandt's own gate. Then the hill, then the veranda, then done.

She just needed help with the middle part. Specifically, she needed someone who owned a fish crate and owed a debt.

* * *

Sefa's cookhouse squatted at the edge of the dock like a toad that had decided, after long consideration, to become a building. It was built of driftwood, corrugated iron, and thirty years of smoke, and it produced food that dockworkers swore by and doctors pretended not to know about. Sefa himself was a mountain that had learned to move quietly — a big Tuvaluan man with forearms like mooring posts and a laugh you could hear over engines.

He was alone when Lena slipped in through the back, which was unusual enough to make her nervous. Usually the place crawled with stevedores. But the afternoon tide had pulled them all out to a grounded cargo lighter, and Sefa was standing at his great iron stove stirring a pot of whatever-it-was with the tenderness other men reserved for infants.

Which was, she supposed, fitting.

"Sefa." Her voice came out smaller than she'd planned. "I need to talk to you."

He turned. His eyes went to her face first — everyone always looked at her face first, looking for the drowned man in it — and then, with a cook's unerring instinct, to the bundle under her shawl.

The bundle chose that exact moment to hiccup.

Sefa set down his spoon with great ceremony. "Ah," he said. "So that's why you've come through my back door instead of buying my fish soup like a respectable person." He wiped his hands on his apron, which was already beyond the reach of cleanliness, and nodded toward the stool by the wall. "Sit. Eat something. You look like a rope that's been asked to be a knot."

"I can't eat. I can't stay. I need—"

"You need," Sefa said, lifting the edge of the shawl with two fingers and peering in at the boy with an expression that made Lena's chest hurt, because it was soft, soft as her father's voice, "about nine hours of sleep and someone to tell you that you're doing the right thing. I can give you the second one. The first one you'll have to steal later, like everything else we steal in this life."

"You know what he is?"

"I know what he isn't. He isn't yours, or you wouldn't be shaking. And he isn't nobody's, because nobody leaves a nobody at a warehouse door with—" Sefa paused, and something moved behind his face, some old weather. "With anything sewn into his blankets. Word travels, girl. The night watchman talks more than he watches."

Lena's stomach dropped. "Who else knows?"

"Who else matters?" Sefa said. "Half the port knows the watchman drinks. Nobody believes him further than they can spit uphill." He took the boy from her with a competence that suggested decades of nieces and nephews, settling him against one enormous shoulder, where the baby blinked once and decided, apparently, that this was acceptable furniture. "Now. Tell me what you're planning, so I can tell you which parts will kill you."

And she did. She told him about the page — not what was on it, not yet, that truth still burned to touch — but that it named names, that it had to reach the magistrate, that the boy couldn't be found anywhere near her when it did. She told him about the three checkpoints, drawn in the dust of his floor with the same pencil stub, and Sefa listened the way good listeners listen, which is to say he kept cooking while she talked, as though her plan were an ingredient being added to the pot.

When she finished, he was quiet for a long moment, turning something over. Then:

"Your father," he said, "once carried me on his back through a channel at night with a broken ankle, because I'd been fool enough to argue with a boat propeller and lose. Four miles of black water, and he sang the whole way so I wouldn't hear how scared he was. Worst singing I have ever heard. Like a gull being murdered politely." He looked down at the boy. "I have owed that man since before you were born. I thought the debt died with him. Debts don't die. They just wait for somebody to come collect."

"So you'll help me."

"I'll give you a fish crate," Sefa said. "A good one, with a false bottom I use for the good rum when the excise men come. The boy rides under the fish, the fish rides under ice, and ice buys you maybe five hours before it starts to melt and drip and make people curious. Five hours is enough, if nothing goes wrong."

"Nothing ever goes wrong," Lena said, "except everything."

"There it is. Your father's daughter." He handed the boy back, and his voice dropped into a register she hadn't heard from him before — low, careful, the voice of a man stepping onto thin ice deliberately. "But hear me, Lena Nystrom, because I'll say it once. Whoever left that child at your door wanted that page delivered. Ask yourself why he didn't deliver it himself. A man with a plan uses a laundress the way a fisherman uses bait. You're not carrying the truth to the magistrate, girl. You may be carrying the trap."

"He said to forget his face."

"Of course he did. That's what men say when they want you to remember everything else." Sefa stirred the pot again, though it needed no stirring. "I'll hide the boy tonight and tomorrow, when you're ready. I'll lend you the crate and my nephew's cart for the run to the customs house. But I'm telling you now, so it's said: I owe your father the delivery. I don't owe him your life. Don't spend both on one trip."

It should have frightened her more than it did. Instead she felt something settle in her chest, cold and clean, like water closing over her head. Fear was still there — fear was permanent, fear was a tenant who paid no rent — but it had changed shape. It wasn't a screaming animal anymore. It was a current, and currents could be read.

* * *

That evening she walked the route. Not all of it, and not suspiciously — a laundress walking home along the dock was invisible, that was the whole architecture of her life — but with her eyes working, cataloguing, the way her father had taught her to catalogue a reef before ever entering it. Where the shadows pooled. Where the sightlines broke. Which corners held watchers and which held only cats.

The harbor gate at dusk: Tamu lighting his little lamp, humming to himself, waving through a fisherman with a wave so automatic it was practically a reflex of the wrist. Door one, she thought. One breath.

The customs house, windows going amber with lamplight: through the glass she could see Madame Reyes at her bench, stamping papers with the unhurried precision of a woman who had never once been rushed by a man in her life. As Lena passed, the woman glanced up — not at her, exactly, but at the window, at the street, at the general movement of the world — and for one absurd moment Lena was certain she'd been seen straight through, shawl and secret and all. Then the glance moved on. Probably. Almost certainly.

Door two. One breath.

And then the third door, which she saved for last, standing across the compound yard in the blue dark, looking up at the counting-room checkpoint where the gas lamp burned all night over Brandt's logbook. Two guards. A gate that required a reason. A head clerk who signed his name the way other men sharpened knives.

Her father had walked through that gate every day for eleven years. Had stood under that lamp. Had signed his name in that book, and somewhere inside, in a locked drawer, his death was already written down as a kindness the company had done him.

Lena stood in the dark and breathed the way he'd taught her — slow in, slower out, past the point where the body began to complain, past the point where it began to panic, into the deep still water underneath panic where the truth lived.

She could do this. Three doors, three breaths. People had done harder things with less reason. People did harder things every day in this port and nobody wrote songs about them.

Behind her, from the direction of the drying lofts, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that rearranged all her careful plans: the baby, awake, announcing his opinion of the evening with the full force of his small furious lungs.

Lena closed her eyes.

Five hours of ice, Sefa had said. She looked at the counting-room lamp, then back toward the lofts, and began, very calmly, to recalculate.
