## 6. The Boy Who Doesn't Cry

The drying lofts sat above the warehouse like a hat nobody wore anymore — a long room of slatted racks and salt-stiffened rope, built for pearl shell that hadn't come through the doors in years. Lena had chosen it the way her father had taught her to choose a dive spot: not for what it offered, but for what it kept out. No foot traffic. One ladder. A window facing the harbor fog, which was less a view than a curtain.

The boy slept in a fish crate lined with sacking, and he did not cry.

That was the thing she couldn't stop noticing. Babies cried. She had heard them all her life in the lanes behind the compound — thin furious howls at dawn, at noon, at three in the morning when the night shift stumbled home. Babies cried because crying was the only language they had, and this one had been handed a ledger page instead of a lullaby and had apparently decided, at some age too young to have opinions, that language was overrated.

He lay on his back with one fist curled near his chin and watched her with an expression she could only describe as professional patience.

"You're going to be trouble," she told him quietly. "I can tell already. You're going to be exactly the kind of trouble that doesn't make noise."

He blinked at her. Grey-green eyes, the color of shallow water over old coral.

Lena looked away first.

* * *

She had work to do, and work was the oldest trick she knew. Busy hands, closed mouth — her father's catechism, though he'd never said it as a catechism. He'd said it as instructions, once, when she was small enough to sit on the gunwale while he checked his weights: *A diver who is busy is a diver who comes up. A diver who talks about what she sees down there drowns twice.* She'd thought he meant pearls. She was beginning to suspect he'd meant everything.

So she worked. She inventoried the drying racks because inventing inventory was safer than thinking. Rack seven: eleven shells, cracked, worthless. Rack nine: coiled line gone stiff as wire. Rack twelve: a gull had gotten in at some point in the last decade and left its opinion on the boards.

The baby slept through all of it. Of course he did. He didn't cry even when the floorboards groaned under her feet, even when a rat the size of her forearm crossed the room with the unhurried confidence of something that paid no rent. He slept like a man who had signed off a shift.

At midday she fed him the milk Sefa had pressed into her hands before dawn — "from my sister's goat, don't ask which sister" — warmed against her skin inside her shirt, which was a sentence she never intended to say aloud to anyone as long as she lived. He took it from the cloth-wrapped spoon without fuss, without greed, watching her face the whole time with those eyes.

And it was there, in the middle of the feeding, with goat's milk on her thumb and the fog burning off outside, that the thought arrived — the one she'd been holding underwater since the warehouse door, the way you hold a breath past fear, not because you want to but because surfacing means knowing.

Those were her father's eyes.

Not similar. Not *reminded her of*. They were the exact grey-green of the water in the shallows off Meridian Point, the color Lena saw every time she looked in the polished tin that served her as a mirror, the color that had made the head clerk squint at her the day before and mispronounce her father's name while deducting her mother's debt from wages her father had technically earned.

"No," Lena said aloud.

The baby, unimpressed by argument, finished his milk.

"No," she said again, softer, because the first one had sounded frightened and she did not do frightened. "You're nobody's. You're a bundle somebody dumped. People dump bundles. It's a whole industry."

But her hands, traitorous busy hands, were already turning him gently toward the window light, tilting his small face, checking — the way you check a shell for the flaw that tells you whether it's worth anything or nothing. The light came through the slats in stripes and lay across his cheek, and there, above his left ear, under a whorl of dark hair: the same cowlick her father had, the one that grew backward no matter how the wind argued with it. She used to press it flat with a wet thumb when she was six and he would laugh and say it always grew back, everything true grows back.

Her hands were shaking. She put the baby down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and walked to the window, and stood there breathing fog until her lungs remembered their business.

It proved nothing. Cowlicks were common. Eyes were common. Half the port had grey-green eyes; the sea got into everyone eventually; it was practically municipal policy. There were four hundred people within a mile of her who could plausibly be related to an abandoned infant if relatedness were decided by eye color, which it was not, which was fortunate, because otherwise half the dockworkers owed each other inheritance.

Except.

Except Voss had said *forget the face*, and men did not risk the counting-room checkpoint at midnight for bundles they felt neutral about. Except the page sewn into the swaddling had been stitched with a diver's palm-needle stitch, tight and even, the kind of sewing you learned with your hands in cold water. Except her father had drowned eleven years ago and she had never once, not once, been allowed to see the body — *recovered and processed*, the company letter had said, *shares settled per schedule* — and she had been fifteen and indentured and busy, busy hands, closed mouth, and she had let them process her own father like cargo because processing was all the port understood.

Lena turned around. The baby was awake again, watching her with the calm of deep water.

"Don't look at me like that," she said. "I'm not your sister. I don't have a brother. I have a rack of dead shells and a debt I didn't sign."

He made a sound then — not a cry, nothing so demanding. A small conversational syllable, the verbal equivalent of a raised eyebrow, and it was so precisely the sound her father used to make when she lied to him about eating the last of the dried fish that she had to sit down on a crate and laugh, or something adjacent to laughing, wet-eyed and graceless, alone in a loft full of rope.

"All right," she said finally. "All right. But we're going to settle this properly. With evidence. Because I am my father's daughter and I don't believe anything I haven't counted twice."

* * *

Her mother's letters lived in the bottom of Lena's sea chest, wrapped in oilcloth, beneath the diving belt she wasn't supposed to own and the knife she wasn't supposed to have inherited. Her mother had written them to no one — or to herself, or to the sea, depending on which letter you read. After her mother died, Lena had read them all in one night and then not touched them for four years, because grief keeps better in the dark, like pearls.

Now she knelt on the loft floorboards and unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that smelled of goat's milk, and the smell of the paper itself rose up — salt and camphor and something floral gone ghostly — and for a moment she was fifteen again and the world still had a floor.

There were perhaps forty letters. Her mother had written in a careful, looping hand, the handwriting of someone taught letters late and determined to make up for lost time with decoration. Lena sorted them by year, the way she sorted ledger entries, because sorting was a form of breathing.

The early ones were from before her birth. Complaints about rent. A recipe for pickled lime. One entire letter devoted to a neighbor's rooster, described with such sustained literary venom that Lena smiled despite everything — her mother had ended it, *if that bird outlives me I want it noted I predicted this.*

Then the letters from the year Lena was two. And here the handwriting changed. Tighter. Faster. As if the pen were being chased.

*Elias has taken the night boat twice this month and will not say where,* one began. Elias. Her father's name, spelled correctly, which was more than Brandt had managed. *He comes back smelling of another woman's cookfire. I know the smoke. It is the smoke from the eastern shore, where the divers' families keep their fires different. He thinks I cannot tell smoke apart. I raised oysters and a daughter. I can tell everything apart.*

Lena's mouth went dry.

*I asked him straight,* said the next one. *He sat a long time. Then he said: if anything happens to me, the boy goes to Tomas. Tomas knows. I have arranged it. Don't ask me more, Ada. Some arrangements are life jackets. You don't inspect them, you just trust the knot.*

Tomas.

Voss's given name was Tomas. She had seen it once, years ago, on a requisition form she'd carried between offices — *approved, T. Voss* — and she had thought nothing of it, because why would she, because names floated past her all day like debris and she had trained herself not to catch any of them.

The boy went to Tomas. If anything happened to me.

Her father had drowned. And eleven years later, a man named Tomas Voss had stood in the fog outside a warehouse door and handed her a bundle and said *forget the face*, and the bundle had her father's eyes and her father's impossible cowlick and her father's talent for silence, and somewhere in the eastern shore a woman had cooked dinners over a fire whose smoke her mother could identify blindfolded, and that woman had borne a son, and the son had been hidden, and the arrangement had held for eleven years until whatever had broken it broke.

Lena sat among her mother's letters and felt the vertigo come up through the floorboards like a swell under a boat. Her grief — eleven years of it, worn smooth as beach glass, carried daily like ballast — had been bookkeeping. That much she'd known since the page. But now it was worse and better at once: her grief had also been a family. There had been another child. Another fire on another shore. Her father had loved someone else and had loved her mother too, apparently, in the complicated human way that ports teach everyone, and he had spent his last years arranging life jackets for both his children, and the knot had held, barely, crookedly, through one drowning and one indenture and eleven years of falsified pages.

Family. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed hook.

Because it was a wound, obviously. It was her father's secret laid open on the loft floor, and it hurt in the specific way that learning a parent was a full person hurts — the discovery that he had fears and plans and a private arithmetic she'd never been shown. But it was also a weapon, and Lena was honest enough with herself to see that too. If the boy was Elias Nystrom's son, then the page in her belt wasn't just paper. It was inheritance. It was standing. Every falsified death entry stopped being an abstraction and became theft from a named child — from *this* child, asleep in a fish crate, whose shares had been absorbed by Meridian before he could walk.

They couldn't dismiss him the way they dismissed her. She was indentured; her testimony weighed what the company said it weighed. But a fatherless heir with a forged ledger — that was a different animal entirely. That was the kind of thing magistrates liked, because it let them be righteous without being inconvenient.

The boy stirred and looked up at her, and Lena realized she was smiling, and stopped, and then decided not to.

"I'm sorry," she told him. "I'm sorry I called you a bundle. You're a life jacket. My father tied you himself, and apparently the man couldn't tie a knot that didn't mean something."

He reached up and grabbed her finger with his whole hand, grip astonishing, and held on.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the harbor, and the afternoon shift bell rang below, and Lena Nystrom — invisible, punctual, the woman with busy hands and a closed mouth — sat in a dead room full of drying racks, holding her brother's hand, doing sums.

One page in her belt. One name in her mother's letters. One man in the counting room who had kept a promise for eleven years and was clearly terrified of what keeping it had cost.

Two days, she thought. Sefa could hide the boy for two days. Two days to decide what a life jacket was actually for — whether you wore it yourself, or threw it to someone drowning, or, as her father had evidently believed, both at once.

Below, boots on the warehouse stairs. Not the shift bell's rhythm. Slower. Heavier. Someone climbing toward the lofts who had no ordinary reason to climb.

Lena blew out the lamp, gathered the letters into her shirt, and picked up her brother.
