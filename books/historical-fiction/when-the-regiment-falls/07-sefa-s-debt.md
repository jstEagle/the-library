## 7. Sefa's Debt

The cookhouse smelled of charcoal and ginger before the sun cleared the breakwater, and Sefa liked it that way. A man who woke before his fire had time to think, and a man who thought too much was a man who eventually said something stupid to somebody dangerous. Sefa had spent forty years in this port perfecting the art of saying nothing at great volume — laughing loud, ladling generous, remembering everything.

So when Lena Nystrom came through his back door at an hour no honest person came through anyone's back door, carrying a fish crate that mewled like a cat and wearing the face of a girl who had already decided something terrible, he did not ask what was in the crate.

He asked if she'd eaten.

"Sefa." She set the crate down on his flour table without ceremony, which told him how far gone she was. Nobody put anything on Sefa's flour table. He had once chased the harbormaster out of his kitchen with a ladle for resting a boot on it. "I need you to look."

He looked. Inside the crate, on a bed of ice chips melting into someone's good shirt, lay a baby. Not a newborn — three months, maybe four, fat in the way of babies who had been fed regularly right up until they hadn't been. Grey-green eyes, wide open, regarding Sefa with the solemn disapproval of a customs inspector.

"Well," Sefa said. "He's got your face."

"He's not mine."

"Nobody said yours. I said your face. There's a difference, and the difference is currently sleeping in my flour." He lifted the boy out — gently; whatever else Sefa was, he had raised six children and buried two wives, and his hands knew what to do even when his mouth was still negotiating — and turned him toward the lamp. The eyes caught the light and went green as shallow water over sand.

Lena went very still.

"You see it," Sefa said quietly.

"I don't know what I see."

"You see exactly what I see, and that's why you came here instead of anywhere else." He bounced the boy once, twice, expertly, and the child made a sound like a small contented kettle. "Sit down. Eat something. Then tell me whose face he has, and don't lie to me, because I fed your father for eleven years and I can taste a lie the way I taste too much salt."

* * *

Here is what Sefa remembered about the night Anselm Nystrom died, and he remembered all of it, because that was the curse of men like him: the port forgot, but the cookhouse did not.

It was the monsoon season, nine years back. Rain coming down so hard the harbor sounded like applause for something nobody wanted to watch. Sefa had been closing up late because a Meridian boat had come in off the pearl grounds early — early and wrong, riding high in the water, too few men on her deck. He'd been frying plantains for the crew because hungry sailors tell you things, and one of them, a boy from upriver with a split lip, sat at Sefa's counter shaking and ate nothing.

"They went down past the second shoal," the boy said. "Nystrom told them not to. Current was wrong. He told them."

"And?"

"And the company man said dive anyway. Shares don't wait for weather." The boy wiped his mouth. "Nystrom went down first so the others would follow him somewhere safe. That's what he was doing. Finding them a safe way down."

Sefa had heard enough dock talk to fill a churchyard. But then the door opened and the rain walked in wearing a coat, and the coat was Voss.

Voss was not yet the gray ghost of the counting rooms in those days. He was younger, harder, a man who still rolled his sleeves. He crossed the cookhouse like a man crossing a courtroom he owned, took the boy with the split lip by the shoulder, and said, "You'll come with me. The company wants your statement."

"The boy already gave me his statement," Sefa said, before his brain could stop his mouth. "Free of charge. With dinner."

Voss looked at him. It was not a threatening look, which was what made it memorable. It was tired. "Cook," he said, "the current was wrong, the weather was wrong, and the boat went out anyway because the schedule said so. That is what happened tonight. If you feed men and let them talk, you will bury more of them than the sea does. Help me make sure the story is one that keeps boats on the water and divers alive. Or keep your statement, and I promise you the next boat goes out worse, because the company will be looking for someone to blame, and they will find the crew."

And the boy with the split lip had stood up and gone with Voss, and Sefa had let him go, and in the morning the official account said Anselm Nystrom drowned in open water due to sudden squall, heroism noted, debts forgiven by the generosity of the Meridian Trading Company.

*Debts forgiven.* Sefa had chewed on those two words for nine years like a gristle he couldn't spit out. You didn't forgive a dead man's debt out of generosity. You forgave it because a debt needed a ledger line, and a ledger line needed a reason, and somewhere in the counting rooms of Meridian there was a column where drowned men stopped being men and became arithmetic.

He had never said any of this aloud. He had fed the funeral crowd — forty people, and he charged none of them, which in Sefa's economy was a eulogy — and he had watched a ten-year-old girl stand at the waterline with her mother's hand on her shoulder, dry-eyed, jaw set like a cleat, and he had thought: *that one will either die young or outlive everyone.* He had not guessed she would do both at once, in a way.

* * *

"So," Sefa said now, setting down the bowl of congee Lena had barely touched. "The page."

She flinched. Good, he thought. Still human under all that competence.

"How did you—"

"Cookhouse, little sister. Sailors talk. Last night somebody saw a man at the warehouse door in the fog, and this morning somebody else says the drying lofts were warm when they shouldn't be, and this evening you walk into my kitchen with a baby in a fish crate and your father's eyes sitting in its face asking me for breakfast." He shrugged. "I don't need the page read to me. I've been reading this port for forty years."

"I need two days." Her voice was steady, which cost her; he could hear what it cost. "Maybe three. There's a route to the magistrate and I have to walk it carefully, and I can't walk it carrying him. Sefa — if Meridian finds him, or finds me with him—"

"If Meridian finds him, they won't hurt him." Sefa turned the boy gently in his arms. "Babies are only evidence until they're leverage. Then they're worth more than pearls." He said it plainly because Lena deserved plainness; sugar-coating was for customers. "But yes. Two days. Maybe three."

Her whole body loosened, and then, being Lena, immediately reassembled itself into caution. "What do you want for it?"

Sefa laughed, loud, the laugh that filled the room and told the neighbors through the wall that everything in here was normal, nothing to see. "I want you to eat that congee. Cold congee is an insult to my ancestors."

"Sefa."

"Lena." He shifted the baby to one arm — the boy had gone heavy and trusting against his chest, the way children do when they've decided you're furniture — and looked at her straight. "Your father ate at my counter every Sunday for eleven years. He paid in stories and he paid in pearls he swore were rejects, and once he paid by fixing my roof in a typhoon because he said a man who feeds the harbor deserves a dry kitchen. When he died, I let a man in a wet coat walk out of here with the truth, because he frightened me with a good argument, and I have regretted it every monsoon since." He tapped the table once, softly, so as not to wake the boy. "This isn't a debt you're calling in. This is a debt I'm paying. Don't insult us both by haggling."

Lena's eyes did something complicated. She looked down at the congee and ate it, all of it, which was her way of saying thank you without the vulnerability of words. Sefa approved. Words were for people with fewer pots on the fire.

* * *

But later — after he'd shown her the loft above the smoke room where the heat would cover a baby's cry, after she'd left the way she came, silent as tide — Sefa sat alone with the sleeping boy and told him the rest of it, because a man should practice honesty on somebody.

"Here's what your big sister doesn't want to hear," he murmured, rocking the cradle-barrow with one foot. "That man in the fog. Voss. He didn't leave you at that door out of love."

The baby slept on, unimpressed. Wise child.

Sefa had seen Voss twice since that rainy night, really seen him. Once at the company wharf, watching divers go down with the expression of a man watching his own hands do something he hadn't authorized. And once, last year, at the edge of the cookhouse queue, where Voss had stood holding a bowl of Sefa's fish stew and said, quietly, not looking at him: "Do you ever think about that night, cook?"

"Every monsoon," Sefa had said.

"Hm," Voss had said, and finished his stew, and left the bowl clean, which among certain kinds of men is a confession.

A man like that doesn't save a baby. A man like that saves himself, and if a baby comes along for the ride, well — ballast floats too. Voss had spent nine years inside the machine that turned Anselm Nystrom into a ledger line. Whatever he was doing now, leaving infants in fog and trusting a diver's daughter to carry fire to the magistrate, it wasn't redemption. Redemption walks in the front door and gives its name. This was a gamble, and the stake was a child, and the table was Voss's own guilt, and Sefa had watched enough card games at the back tables to know that the man who bets with other people's lives always keeps the best seat.

But — and here was the thing Sefa had learned about debts, the thing he'd pay real money to unlearn — you don't get to choose which debts are clean. He owed Anselm Nystrom. The interest had compounded for nine years. And the payment was currently drooling on his apron.

"Two days," he told the boy. "Three if the wind's wrong. After that, your sister comes back for you or I start making noise louder than she'd like, and believe me—" he grinned at the sleeping face, grey-green eyes shut now, one fist curled around Sefa's thumb with the grip of a man who intended to hold on to things, "—nobody in this port has ever accused me of being quiet."

Outside, the harbor breathed. Somewhere across the compound, lamps were burning in a ledger room where a girl was teaching herself to steal, though she didn't know it yet — didn't know that once you palmed the first page, the second came easier, and the third easier still, until one morning you looked at your own hands and couldn't remember whose they'd been before.

Sefa fed his fire, kept his voice low, and waited for the wind to change.
