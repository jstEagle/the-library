## 8. Inventory

The knock came at half past seven, which was wrong in three ways.

First, the ledger room was never disturbed before eight. Second, nobody knocked — they simply entered, because a room full of indentured clerks was not considered a place where privacy could be meaningfully violated. Third, and most damning: the knock was polite. In Lena's experience, politeness at the Meridian Trading Company preceded trouble the way green water preceded a storm.

"Inventory," said the man in the doorway, as though announcing weather. He was small and round and sweating through a linen jacket that had been white once, in another country, in another life. "Mr. Halloran, company auditor. You will continue your work. I will observe."

*Observe.* The word sat in the room like a cat that hadn't decided whether to stay.

Lena kept her eyes on her column. Diver's name. Date of death. Share value. Disposition. The same four words she wrote forty times a day, the same four words that had stopped being words sometime around the night she'd found the page sewn into an infant's swaddling and become instead a kind of arithmetic she could no longer perform without tasting salt.

To her left, old Maren — sixty if she was a day, hands like driftwood, hired before anyone remembered why — let out a wheeze that might have been a sigh or might have been laughter. Maren had survived three head clerks and one fire in the copra shed, and her philosophy was simple: whatever was coming, it would come faster if you watched for it.

Lena did not watch for it. Lena copied figures.

But under the desk, her left hand rested on her knee, and her knee was pressed against the drawer where, folded to the size of a communion wafer inside the hollowed spine of an old tide almanac, lay the first page. And beneath the false bottom of the ink cabinet — she had pried it loose with a diver's fingernail three nights ago, in the dark, with the boy asleep against her collarbone in the drying lofts above — there was room for a second.

Because here was what she had learned from the first page, lying awake while the child breathed his small warm breaths against her throat: one page was an anomaly. A clerk's error. A thing that could be explained away, dismissed, disbelieved. But two pages — two pages in different hands, different months, the same lie — were a *system*. And systems could be proven.

She knew which entries she needed. She had known since Tuesday, when she'd stayed late copying the duplicate index and memorized, the way her father had taught her to memorize reef passages — not by looking hard but by looking *once*, completely — the folio numbers of every diver whose death share had been "forgiven" between 1904 and 1908. Her father's name appeared twice in that span. She intended to hold both pages in her hand when she stood before the magistrate, and she intended for them to be pages Brandt could not call forgeries, because they would still smell of the counting room's lamp oil and bear its watermark, and because one of them would have been taken from under an auditor's nose.

That last part had not been the plan, originally. The plan had been patience: wait for the audit to end, wait for the dark, take the page at leisure. But plans, her father used to say, are what divers make so the sea has something to laugh at.

* * *

Halloran began at the far end of the room, with Maren, who received him the way one receives a visiting bishop — with deference and absolutely nothing resembling cooperation. He asked to see her day book. She produced it. He asked about discrepancies in the copra weights. She explained, at length, with references to humidity, the phases of the moon, and the moral character of the copra buyers, until his eyes glazed and he moved on.

Lena measured him as he worked, the way she'd once measured current off the point: direction, strength, the places it slackened. He checked totals against totals. He checked signatures against signatures. He did not, she noted, check the actual folios — the bound original ledgers chained to their stand in the corner, the ones only Brandt and the senior clerks could unlock. Which meant he was auditing the *copies*. Which meant either he was incompetent, or someone wanted him auditing copies.

Both possibilities interested her very much.

The morning crawled. The ceiling fan turned with the enthusiasm of a dying thing. Sweat made rivers down Halloran's back and he grew shorter-tempered and more thorough, and by eleven o'clock he had worked his way down the row of desks until he stood directly behind Lena's chair, close enough that she could hear the wet whistle of his breathing and smell the camphor he used against the port's insects.

"Your columns," he said. "They're unusually clean."

"Thank you, sir."

"That wasn't a compliment." He leaned over her shoulder. His shadow fell across her page like a bird's. "Most clerks your age make errors. Errors tell me where to look. You've made none. That tells me you're either very good or very careful, and in my experience the second is worse than the first."

Lena felt the strange double pulse of fear and something adjacent to it — the diver's thrill, the moment the reef drops away beneath you and the blue opens up and you understand that you are now committed, that turning back costs more than going on. Her father had called it *the lean*. You feel it in the chest before a deep dive. Most people mistake it for terror. It is actually the body telling you it knows exactly what you're about to do.

"I was trained by the sea, sir," she said. "The sea doesn't forgive errors."

"Hm," said Halloran, and moved on.

It was then that she saw it.

He had set his case on the corner of the ink cabinet — the cabinet with the false bottom, the cabinet holding the almanac, the almanac holding the first page — and in shifting his weight to consult a paper, he knocked loose the brass catch she had not quite finished disguising. It was a small thing. A glint of bright metal where dull metal should have been. But Halloran was a man who noticed clean columns, and Lena understood with cold clarity that if he looked down at that moment, everything ended: the page, the boy, Sefa's cookhouse, all of it, into the harbor with stones in its pockets.

So she did the only thing available to her. She dropped her pen.

It rolled — she sent it rolling with her heel, a small practiced flick she'd learned keeping crabs off the drying racks — and it came to rest precisely beneath Halloran's boot.

"Oh — forgive me, sir—"

He stepped back to let her reach for it, away from the cabinet, and she came up from under the desk with the pen in one hand and, in the other, in the space of a single heartbeat, with the almanac's spine opened and closed again, the wafer-thin fold of paper transferred from hiding place to sleeve in a motion so smooth it frightened her. Because it hadn't felt frightening. It had felt *easy*. That was the part that scared her — not the risk, but how naturally her hands had done it, how little they asked her permission anymore.

Her father's hands had been like that. Forty feet down, working a shell loose, no thought required. She had always believed competence was the cleanest inheritance he'd left her.

She was beginning to suspect he'd left her others.

* * *

The near-catch came after lunch, and it came wearing Brandt's face.

The head clerk arrived at two o'clock without warning, which was itself a warning, and stood in the doorway surveying the room with the expression of a man checking whether his house had been robbed in his absence. His eyes went desk to desk. They paused on Maren. On the empty chair of the junior clerk who was, at this hour, conveniently running an errand to the customs house. On the ink cabinet.

On Lena.

"You," Brandt said. "Nystrom."

Not *Nyström*. He had mispronounced her father's name the first day and had declined, through two years, to correct himself, and Lena had long ago decided this was not ignorance but policy — a small daily erasure, the company reminding her that even her name was on loan.

"Sir."

"The auditor reports your work is unusually accurate." He crossed the room slowly. Behind him, Halloran packed his case with the weary dignity of a man who had found nothing and knew he'd been meant to find something. "Accurate clerks interest me. Accurate clerks get given responsibility." He stopped at her desk. Up close his skin had the grey undertone of a man who slept badly, and his eyes did the thing they always did — flicked, briefly, to her left side, to her sleeve, as though drawn by some instinct he didn't examine. "You'll stay late tonight. There are folios to recopy. Alone."

"Alone, sir?"

"Is there a difficulty?"

There was a difficulty. The difficulty was that alone in the ledger room after hours was either a trap or a gift, and she would not know which until she was inside it. The difficulty was that the fibers of a certain grey blanket — washed twice, boiled once, hidden in the lofts — clung still to the wool of her sleeve, three pale threads just above the wrist, and Brandt's gaze had already touched them once and touched them again now, lingering, the way a man's gaze lingers on a stain he can't yet identify.

She saw the exact moment he saw them.

It was very small. His eyes narrowed — not in suspicion, not yet, but in the particular way of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. Blanket fiber. Wool. Company-issue blankets were cotton; the servants' were jute; the dock families wove palm. Grey wool of that grade came from exactly one source: the infant blankets issued, in the old years, to the company maternity ward, discontinued in 1906, the remainder stored in — she watched him arrive at it — the archive. The same archive where duplicates were kept. The same archive where a missing page would have lived.

"Those threads," Brandt said quietly. "You've been in the loft stores."

"The drying lofts, sir. I mend the sails sometimes. Off hours." True. Verifiably true. She had mended sails there for two years, and half the harbor could confirm it, and she thanked God and her own tedious habits for it.

"Mm." He reached out — slowly, giving her time to object, which was somehow worse than speed — and pinched one thread between finger and thumb. Held it to the light. Lena kept her face arranged in its usual expression, the one she wore like a diving mask: pleasant, empty, slightly dim. Busy hands, closed mouth. Inside, her heart was going like a fish in a bucket.

"Wool," Brandt murmured. "Odd choice for sailcloth." He let the thread fall. For a moment neither of them moved, and the fan turned, and somewhere outside a winch groaned under a load of copra, and Lena understood that the next ten seconds would decide whether she spent the rest of her indenture in this room or in a cell, and possibly whether the boy spent his childhood an orphan twice over.

Then Halloran cleared his throat from the doorway.

"My case, Mr. Brandt. And my receipt for the inspection. The boat to the capital leaves at four, and I am told the harbor master does not wait for auditors."

Brandt's eyes held hers one beat longer. Whatever lived behind them — suspicion, exhaustion, the first cold drop of a fear he would spend the rest of this story feeding — he folded it away, and became again the grey man with the bad sleep.

"Tonight," he said. "Seven o'clock. Don't be late."

* * *

She waited until his footsteps faded down the iron stair, until Halloran's boat-horn sounded flat across the water, until Maren had gathered her shawl and shuffled out with a backward glance that contained, in its single raised eyebrow, an entire conversation Lena chose not to have.

Then she sat down at her desk and put her head in her hands and shook.

Not weeping — she'd forgotten how, mostly — but the body's own ledger settling its accounts: the tremor in her fingers, the sweat cooling along her spine, the wild urge to laugh that she strangled in her palms. Because she had done it. Under his nose, under both their noses, she had taken the second page, and it was against her ribs right now, sewn that morning into the lining of her bodice where no auditor looked because auditors, like everyone else, did not look at indentured girls.

Two pages. Both her father's name. Both in Meridian's own hand, on Meridian's own paper, bearing Meridian's own watermark.

She took them out — carefully, in the empty room, holding them the way you hold a shell you know is inhabited — and laid them side by side in the lamplight. The same lie, written twice, eighteen months apart. *Share forgiven. Debt absorbed.* Her father drowned twice on paper, and both times the company caught him falling.

And underneath the triumph, quiet and unwelcome, sat the other thing. The thing she'd felt when her hands moved without asking. She had stolen today. Not bread, not freedom — paper, yes, but theft all the same, and it had felt easy, and ease is a door that does not close again behind you. Her mother had been honest to the bone and it had bought her a debt and a grave. Her father had been honest underwater and it had bought him a ledger entry. Honesty, Lena was beginning to understand, was a luxury priced beyond an indentured girl's wages, and she could no longer afford to be the person she had been a month ago.

She folded the pages away. She thought of the boy in the lofts above, asleep in his impossible grey wool, with his grey-green eyes that were her eyes, that were their father's eyes, and she thought: *I will be a thief, then. If that's the price of you.*

At seven o'clock she climbed the iron stair to the ledger room, alone, as ordered, and found on her desk a single folio and a note in Brandt's precise hand:

*Recopy folio 114. Burn the original when done.*

Folio 114. She knew that number. Everyone who had read the index knew that number.

It was the folio with her father's name in it — the third entry, the one she hadn't yet counted, the one dated six weeks *after* his drowning.

Someone had recorded her father dying twice. And Brandt wanted the proof burned, and wanted her — of all people — holding the match.
