## 9. The Face She Must Forget

From the counting room window, Elias Voss could see the drying lofts, the fish market's striped awnings, and — if he leaned far enough to the left, which he did now, for the fourth time in an hour — the narrow stair where Lena Nystrom came down each morning with her hair bound tight and her face arranged into nothing at all.

She was good at that face. Better than her father had ever been. Tomas Nystrom had worn his whole heart on the outside of his skin like a man who'd never learned the sea kept secrets. Lena wore hers somewhere below the waterline.

But she had looked up. Yesterday, crossing the yard with a crate of shell, she had glanced at the counting room window — one flick of the eyes, quick as a fish turning — and Voss had felt it land on him like a stone dropped down a well.

*She knows I know,* he thought, and poured himself tea he did not want, because a man standing idle at a window was a man thinking, and a man thinking in Meridian House was a man already halfway to being watched.

The tea was terrible. It was always terrible. Brandt ordered it from the same supplier who watered the company's lamp oil, and Voss suspected the two economies were not unrelated. He drank it anyway. There was a certain discipline in drinking bad tea without complaint that he imagined would serve him well in whatever afterlife he was currently earning.

Behind him, the counting room hummed its morning hymn: nibs scratching, Brandt clearing his throat in the particular rhythm that meant *I am about to be insufferable*, and the great iron safe ticking as it cooled from the night.

"Voss." Brandt did not look up from his duplicate ledger. "You've been at that window four times."

"Admiring the harbor."

"The harbor is a mudflat and forty rotting hulls."

"All the more admirable for surviving them."

Brandt sniffed — a small, precise sound, like a page being torn very carefully — and returned to his columns. Voss turned back to the glass.

There she went. Down the stair, across the yard, a bundle of grey-green shawl over one arm that had not been there last week. His stomach performed a slow, professional turn.

He knew what was in the shawl. He knew because he had put it there himself, three nights ago, in the fog, at the warehouse door, with hands that shook so badly he'd nearly woken the child before he meant to. *Forget the face.* That was all he'd managed to say. Eleven years of planning, four years of saving, one night of courage, and his entire speech had been three words delivered to a girl who had every reason to hate him.

It had been, he reflected, possibly the worst-orchestrated operation of his career, and his career included the year he and Tomas had tried to smuggle a piano to a leper colony on a boat with a hole in it.

* * *

They had been partners once. Not the paper kind — the kind where two men own one boat between them and argue about whose turn it is to bail.

Tomas Nystrom had been twenty-two and built like a rope, all fiber and no give, when Elias met him on this same quay. Elias had been the clerk's son from the administrative quarter, sent down to the water to learn "the practical side of the trade," which everyone understood to mean *learn how the other half drowns*. He'd stood at the edge of the shallows in boots worth more than a diver's boat, holding a notebook, and Tomas had swum up out of the green like an accusation.

"You're going to write down what we do?" Tomas had said, dripping, grinning.

"I'm going to write down what you *earn*."

"Then write small. We don't earn much."

That was the joke they'd built everything on. Write small. They'd carved it into the mast of their shared boat, the *Two Brothers* — a name chosen by Tomas, who had a brother drowned, and Elias, who had none, and who had liked the idea of inheriting one. For six years they ran pearls together. Tomas dove; Elias sold. Tomas held his breath past fear; Elias held his numbers past temptation. And for six years the arrangement worked because each man guarded the thing the other couldn't count.

Then Meridian came. Meridian always came. It arrived the way weather arrives — first the distant pressure, then the men in clean linen saying *partnership* in voices that made the word mean the opposite of itself. They offered contracts. They offered credit. They offered, most dangerously of all, respectability: a counting room with Elias's name on the door, a foreman's wage for Tomas, security instead of salt.

Tomas had said no. Tomas had read the contract twice, found the clause where the inheritance shares became "administrative fees," and thrown it in the harbor, where it dissolved with more dignity than most documents manage.

Elias had signed.

He told himself, then, that he signed to protect Tomas — that a man inside the machine could shield the man outside it. And for a while it was even true. When the quotas tightened, Elias adjusted them. When the death ledgers began to fill, Elias moved names around like furniture so the widows got something. Small mercies, written small. The old joke turned into a code, and the code turned into a cage, and by the time Elias understood that Meridian hadn't bought him so much as *absorbed* him — the way the sea absorbs a body, gently, completely, without any need to hurry — Tomas was dead, and the ledger said *debt forgiven*, and Elias was the one who had taught the clerks the trick of writing it that way.

That was the arithmetic he carried. Not guilt exactly — guilt was too warm a word, too much like grief, and he had no right to grief. It was colder than that. It was the knowledge that he had invented the pen that wrote his friend's erasure.

So when the boy was born — Tomas's son, by Amara, whom Elias had loved in the dumb, sideways way he loved everything he refused to look at directly — Elias had done the only brave thing available to a coward with a key to the archive. He had torn the true page from the ledger, sewn it into swaddling cloth, and carried the child through the fog to the one person alive who had Tomas's eyes and Tomas's stubbornness and no idea yet what either was worth.

*Forget the face.*

God. If he lived to be ninety he would never forgive himself those three words.

* * *

At midday he took his usual walk — the walk every clerk took, past the drying lofts, past the cookhouse, unhurried, unremarkable, a man stretching his legs. Sefa's cookhouse smelled of fried plantain and woodsmoke, and Sefa himself stood in the doorway wearing the expression of a man who has been asked to keep a secret by someone he does not like and is deciding how expensive it will be.

"Elias Voss," Sefa said. "Still walking places you have no business walking."

"I'm admiring your smoke."

"You're admiring my smoke the way Brandt admires my accounts." But Sefa stepped aside to let him pass, which was, from Sefa, practically an embrace.

Inside, among the hanging baskets, Voss saw what he needed to see: a fish crate, freshly lined, sized for something larger than fish. And beside it, on the bench, a folded square of grey-green wool.

His chest did something complicated.

"She eats here at noon," Sefa said quietly, not looking at him. "Sits in the corner. Doesn't talk. Feeds herself like the food might be watching."

"She gets that from—" He stopped. *From her father* was a sentence that opened doors he'd nailed shut years ago.

"From nobody," Sefa said. "People like that make themselves. I've fed enough of them to know." He turned the plantains with unnecessary force. "Voss. Whatever you've put in motion — and don't tell me you haven't, you have the face of a man carrying a piano downhill — understand something. That girl trusts me because of her father. Not because of you. If your plan costs her, there is nowhere on this coast I won't help her find you."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's generous. Fair would be feeding you to the crabs." Sefa handed him a plate he hadn't asked for. "Eat. You look like a ledger with legs."

Voss ate. It was, he noted with some bitterness, better than anything served in Meridian House, and probably cooked with stolen company charcoal, and he respected that enormously.

* * *

The trouble with watching someone, he discovered, was that eventually you saw too much.

By evening he had catalogued it all from the counting room window: the way Lena now took the long route past the harbor gate, casual as Sunday, though Sunday was four days away. The way she paused at the customs house steps without going up. The way she'd begun sewing at night in the lofts — he could see the candle through the slats, and the small, rhythmic movement of her shoulders that was not mending but *stitching*, deliberate, structural, the stitching of a woman attaching something to something else.

To her diving belt, if he had to guess. And he did have to guess, because guessing was all he had left. He'd burned his ability to simply ask her anything on the night he said *forget the face* and vanished like a stage magician with a guilty conscience.

"She has the page," Brandt said behind him.

Voss did not jump. It cost him something not to, and he paid it gladly rather than confirm anything with his spine.

"What makes you say that?"

"The duplicate books." Brandt laid the twin ledger open on the desk between them, and there it was: the same entries, the same forgiving hand, the same names — including, entered twice, *Nystrom, T., diver, debt forgiven* — recorded in the duplicates that never left the building. "A page is missing from the working ledger. The duplicates are intact. Which means whoever took the page took it believing the truth died with it." He smiled the way a lock smiles. "It didn't."

"No," Voss agreed mildly. "I suppose it didn't."

"We'll conduct an inventory tomorrow. Full audit of the ledger room staff." Brandt tapped the name column. "Starting with the indentured ones. They steal out of desperation, and desperation leaves fingerprints."

Voss looked at the window. At the lofts. At the single candle still burning in the slats, steady now, the shoulders still moving, stitch after stitch after stitch.

He should warn her. He knew the routes she'd have to take — harbor gate, customs house, and then the third checkpoint, the one nobody outside the company remembered existed: the counting room itself, where every claim against Meridian passed under the eyes of the men who'd falsified it. She was walking toward the mouth of the machine with its own evidence strapped to her body and its own blood asleep in a fish crate, and tomorrow morning Brandt would begin closing the net, and the net closed fast, because Brandt was not loyal to Meridian — Brandt was terrified of his own signature, and terror moved quicker than loyalty ever had.

And yet.

If he warned her, and she ran, and she was caught running, the warning would hang around his neck like an anchor. If he stayed silent, she might walk straight into the audit. Either way he was gambling, and the stake was the only promise he had ever kept longer than a contract: *I'll see the boy safe, Tomas. However it has to happen. Even if it can't be clean. Especially then.*

He had promised it at the graveside, to a hole in the ground, which is the easiest place to make promises and the hardest place to keep them.

Below, the candle in the lofts went out.

Voss stood a moment longer in the dark glass, a middle-aged man reflected faintly over a sleeping port, holding a secret in each hand and discovering they weighed different amounts.

Then he took out a sheet of company paper, and — because he was, whatever else, still a clerk, and clerks believe in the power of the written word even when it has ruined them — he began, very slowly, to draft a note he was not at all sure he would have the courage to deliver:

*Lena. Tomorrow they count the pages. Trust the woman at customs. Do not trust the gate. And when you reach the third checkpoint—*

He stopped. Looked at the sentence. There was no honest way to finish it except *come to me first*, and every instinct he possessed — the good ones and the self-preserving ones, which had been sharing an office for thirty years — screamed that she would sooner swim to the magistrate through shark water than walk into the counting room and ask him for anything.

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a dog barked twice and stopped, the way dogs do when a door opens that shouldn't.

Voss folded the unfinished note into his breast pocket, over his heart, where he kept everything he'd never sent, and went to find out which door had opened.
