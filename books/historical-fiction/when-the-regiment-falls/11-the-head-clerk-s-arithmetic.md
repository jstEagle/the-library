## 11. The Head Clerk's Arithmetic

Elias Brandt had been head clerk of the Meridian Trading Company for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned one truth more reliable than any other: a man's character was written in his hand.

Not his handwriting — though that too, of course; the slouching scrawls of the divers who marked their marks at the bottom of wage receipts told him everything he needed to know about them — but his *hand*, his actual hand, the instrument with which a man signed his name. Brandt's own signature was a small masterpiece. He practiced it Sunday evenings, after the compound emptied, with a good steel nib and the company's blackest ink. The *B* stood upright like a man at attention. The final *t* crossed with a single decisive stroke, neither timid nor flamboyant. It was the signature of a man who could be trusted with other people's money.

Which was precisely the problem.

Brandt sat alone in the counting room an hour past midnight, the lamp turned low because a low lamp felt like discretion, and looked at the duplicate ledger open before him on the green baize. Entry 447. Dated the fourteenth of March, four years past. *Share of A. Nystrom, diver, deceased — remitted to company account per standard provision.*

His own signature beneath it. Upright *B*. Decisive *t*.

The trouble was that Nystrom had not died on the fourteenth of March. Nystrom had died on the second of February, and the share had been entered as *remitted* six weeks before any man could have been said to have remitted it, which meant that somewhere between the drowning and the entry, a death had been invented, or a date had been moved, or both, and Brandt's hand — that honest, upright, trustworthy hand — had made it official.

He did not remember signing it. That was the thing he clung to, turning it over like a coin of doubtful metal. Eleven years of entries, thousands of them, and no man could be expected to remember each one. He had signed what Voss put before him. Voss prepared the pages; Brandt certified them. That was the division of labor. That was how a company functioned. If every clerk refused to sign anything he hadn't personally verified from the seabed upward, commerce itself would collapse, and civilization with it, and Brandt did not think he was exaggerating.

And yet.

* * *

He had found the twin page by accident, which was somehow worse than finding it by design. An accident meant the universe was paying attention.

It had happened Tuesday, during the quarterly reconciliation, a task Brandt performed himself rather than delegate, not from diligence but from a private superstition: that if he personally touched every number once a season, the numbers would refrain from betraying him. He had been matching the duplicate books against the working ledgers — page against page, figure against figure, the pleasant mechanical rhythm of it, like rosary beads — when his finger stopped.

Working ledger, folio 112: *Nystrom, A. — debt forgiven, share absorbed.* Torn out. The stub remained, ragged at the gutter, and someone had trimmed the stub neatly, which meant whoever took the page had taken it with a blade and a steady hand, not torn it in passion or panic.

Duplicate book, folio 112: the same entry, whole. Untouched. Sitting there in its duplicate immortality like a man who believed himself unobserved.

Brandt had stared at it for perhaps four minutes. He knew this because when he finally looked up, the clock over the door said twenty past four, and he remembered beginning the reconciliation at sixteen minutes past.

Four minutes is a long time to look at your own signature.

* * *

By Wednesday morning he had constructed three theories, and by Wednesday noon he had discarded two of them, and the one that remained was so unwelcome that he spent his lunch hour reorganizing his ink drawer purely to avoid thinking about it.

Theory the first: clerical error. A page removed by mistake, misfiled, lost. But pages were not removed with blades and steady hands by mistake, and the duplicate had been reconciled and initialed — his initials, *E.B.*, small and neat in the corner — which meant the removal postdated the last audit, and audits were not lost by mistake either.

Theory the second: theft for gain. Someone selling company secrets, or blackmailing a family whose debts had been forgiven. But the families whose debts were forgiven were dead, mostly, and dead men's children were indentured, and indentured children did not blackmail; they kept their mouths shut the way oysters keep their shells shut, which was to say absolutely, and only under mortal provocation.

Which left theory the third, and theory the third had a face, and the face belonged to Voss.

Brandt had never liked Voss. This was not a moral judgment — Brandt tried to reserve those, finding them expensive to maintain — but a professional one. Voss had been with the company longer than anyone, since before Meridian had absorbed the old partnership houses, and he carried himself with the particular serenity of a man who knows where all the bodies are filed. He was courteous. He was punctual. He was, Brandt suspected, entirely without fear, and a man without fear in a counting house was like a man without a sense of smell in a tannery: constitutionally unsuited to the work, and dangerous to stand downwind of.

Because here was the arithmetic that Brandt performed now, nightly, in the privacy of his own skull, where the figures were less tidy than any ledger:

If the falsified entries came to light, who burned?

Not Voss. Voss had signed nothing. Voss had *prepared* — and preparation was a mist, it dispersed, it could not be produced in evidence. Every incriminating line bore the certification of E. Brandt, Head Clerk, in his beautiful upright hand. Voss would step back into the fog from which he'd come, and Brandt would stand alone in the dock holding eleven years of signatures like a man holding eleven years of knives, all of them pointing inward.

Therefore: the missing page must be recovered. Therefore: the person who removed it must be identified. Therefore—

Therefore, Brandt admitted to himself, in the smallest voice he owned, the voice he used only for truths that could not survive daylight: therefore the girl.

* * *

He had noticed Lena Nystrom the way one notices a well-kept account: with approval, and without interest. She was quiet, punctual, accurate. Her columns balanced to the half-cent. In eleven years she had asked him exactly two questions, both about filing conventions, both answerable in under ten words. She was, in short, the ideal employee, and Brandt had long ago formulated his one genuine principle of management: watch most closely the ones who give you nothing to watch.

It was Thursday evening when he confirmed it. He had begun, quietly, keeping the ledger room open later than usual, inventing small tasks — a re-sorting of the correspondence files, a recount of the stamp drawer — and on Thursday he had watched her, through the doorway, pause at folio 112's stub. Not long. Three seconds, perhaps four. Her fingers did not touch it. Her eyes did not even linger, not obviously. But there was a quality to stillness that Brandt had spent a career learning to read, the way a moneylender reads a debtor's face, and the girl had gone still the way people go still near a grave they recognize.

Then she had turned back to her columns, and her pen had moved, and her figures had balanced, and she had been perfectly invisible again.

But Brandt had seen. And having seen, he could not unsee, and the knowledge sat in him like a swallowed hook.

* * *

What troubled him — what genuinely troubled him, keeping him at his desk past midnight with the lamp low — was not the girl. Girls went to the magistrate or they didn't; either way there were procedures. What troubled him was the direction of his own thoughts, which had begun running, without his permission, toward a solution he did not want to examine.

Because there were two ways to close the net.

The clean way: report the discrepancy upward, to the resident director, initiate an internal inquiry, recover the page through proper channels. And the clean way required Brandt to write, in his own hand, a memorandum stating that folio 112 had been falsified, dated, and certified — thereby creating, in writing, in his own beautiful hand, the single document that would hang him. The resident director was a decent man, in the way that men are decent who have never had to choose. He would be horrified. He would demand explanations. And when the colonial office eventually descended, as it eventually would, the resident director would remember, with perfect sincerity, that Brandt had brought the matter to his attention — that Brandt had *known* — and knowing, in the colonial arithmetic, was indistinguishable from doing.

The other way required no memoranda. It required only that the page be recovered before it reached any magistrate, and that the person who took it be handled quietly, and that the duplicate book be — Brandt's pen hovered over the thought the way a man hovers over a high dive —

— retired.

Duplicates were destroyed routinely. That was the beauty of the system. Old books were pulped on a five-year rotation, and folio 112's twin was four years and some months old, and if the rotation schedule were advanced by a season — if a clerk noted, credibly, that the archive was damp and the paper foxed and the binding rotting, all of which was even true — then the entry would pass out of existence cleanly, legally, with paperwork of its own, and the only remaining copy in the world would be whatever the girl had sewn into whatever the girl had sewn it into.

And then, if the girl were searched — if her quarters were inspected, as the quarters of indentured staff were periodically inspected, entirely within regulations — and the page were found on her person, why, then the story wrote itself. Theft of company property. Attempted fraud. A lone servant, embittered, stealing records for purposes unknown. No inquiry into folio 112 necessary. The matter closed itself, tidy as a ledged column, and the only signature required was Brandt's, on a search authorization, which was routine, which was administrative, which was nothing.

He told himself it was nothing for the better part of an hour. Then he blew out the lamp, because even a low lamp felt suddenly like a witness, and sat in the dark listening to the harbor sounds — the water slapping pilings, a drunk singing somewhere near the fish market, the creak of the drying lofts — and understood that he was not protecting the company.

The company, he reflected, would survive a scandal. Companies were like sharks in that respect, and like sharks in most respects. What would not survive was Elias Brandt, whose entire existence consisted of his signature's good name, and who had, upon inspection, nothing else whatsoever inside himself. Strip away the ledgers and the initials and the decisive crossing of the *t*, and what remained was a thin man in a rented room who owed his position entirely to the fact that he had never once refused to sign.

Cowardice, Brandt thought, is not the absence of courage. Cowardice is an engine. It burns fuel like anything else, and the fuel is other people, and it runs very efficiently indeed, and it will carry you anywhere you ask it to, and it never, ever stops asking to be fed.

He lit the lamp again. He took out a fresh sheet. And in his beautiful, upright, trustworthy hand, he wrote the search authorization for the quarters of L. Nystrom, indent #2214, effective dawn tomorrow.

* * *

There remained the matter of Voss.

Brandt was not a brave man, but he was a thorough one, and thoroughness demanded that he understand his opponent before moving against him. So on Friday morning he did something he had never done in eleven years: he walked to Voss's desk in the outer room and stood over it, hands clasped behind his back, wearing the expression of mild administrative concern that he reserved for misfiled invoices.

"Voss," he said. "Folio 112."

Something crossed the older man's face — not alarm, Brandt noted with disappointment, nothing so useful as alarm. Something almost like sadness, or perhaps like patience, the look of a man watching a slow cart finally arrive.

"Folio 112," Voss repeated. "Is there a difficulty with the reconciliation?"

"The page has been removed from the working ledger."

"Has it." Voss returned to his correspondence, unhurried, dipping his pen. "How careless. You'll want to check the duplicates, I imagine."

"I have checked the duplicates."

"And?"

Brandt hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a fraction of a second, but he saw it land on Voss like a fly settling on meat, and he understood, with a cold clarity that would have served him well eleven years ago, that he had just answered the question. That Voss now knew the duplicate existed, and intact, and that Brandt had found it, and that Brandt's next move would be dictated not by justice but by self-preservation — and that Voss had known all of this before Brandt opened his mouth, had probably designed the whole arrangement around it, had counted on the fact that a coward, once shown the cliff edge, could always be relied upon to walk himself toward it.

"You'll want to speak with the girl," Voss said mildly, not looking up. "Nystrom's daughter. She keeps late hours. And Brandt—" he blotted his letter, folded it, and only then raised his eyes, which were grey and calm as harbor water in fog, "—do be careful with the fire regulations in the archive. Paper's temperamental stuff. One spark and eleven years of records could go up before anyone could save what mattered."

He returned to his letter. The conversation, by every convention of the counting room, was over.

Brandt walked back to his office on legs that felt borrowed. He sat down. He looked at the search authorization, already signed, already dated, effective dawn tomorrow.

Dawn tomorrow. And between now and dawn, a night shift, and a warehouse full of shadows, and a girl who had gone still beside a grave she recognized, and somewhere out in that fog — he was certain of it now, certain the way one is certain of weather — Voss was already three moves ahead, feeding the coward his lines and waiting to see whether the fool would read them aloud.

Brandt reached for the bell cord to summon the night watchman, and his hand, for the first time in eleven years, was not quite steady.
