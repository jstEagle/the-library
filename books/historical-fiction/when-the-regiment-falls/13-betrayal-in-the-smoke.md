## 13. Betrayal in the Smoke

Sefa had learned to count in a language that had no numbers.

It was the counting his mother had done before him, and her mother before her, back along a line of women who had never owned anything worth weighing but who could tell you, to the grain, what a household held. A sack of rice was not a sack of rice. It was four days of quiet, or two days of loud, depending on how many mouths stood at the fire. A chicken was not a chicken; it was a Sunday, or it was an apology owed to a neighbor, or it was medicine bought from the Chinese apothecary when the fever came up the alley like tide water.

So when Sefa stood at his cookhouse door on the morning the Meridian men came, he did not see three men in grey company jackets. He saw arithmetic.

The tall one in front — Okonkwo, they called him, though Sefa suspected the name belonged to some other man entirely, some man whose debts were paid — held a folded paper in one hand. Behind him stood two younger ones with the particular emptiness of men hired by the hour, their faces like doors painted shut.

"Sefa." Okonkwo said it the way you'd say *dog* or *table*. "Company business."

"Everything here is company business," Sefa said. "The fish is company fish. The smoke is company smoke. Even my cough is company cough — I caught it off a company diver." He stepped aside from the doorway, because refusing entry was a number too large to pay. "Sit. Eat. A man who talks on an empty stomach says nothing true."

They did not sit. That told him something too. Men who sat and ate wanted small things. Men who stayed standing wanted your whole house.

* * *

The cookhouse sat where the dock road bent toward the drying lofts, which meant everything that happened on that stretch of harbor passed through Sefa's nose before it passed through anyone's eyes. He smelled the tides. He smelled the ships' moods — coal ships arrived sour, pearl boats came home smelling of money and salt and sometimes, faintly, of blood. He had smelled the night Lena Nystrom's father drowned, years ago, though he had not known then what the smell meant. He had only known that Voss had come into the cookhouse past midnight, white as a boiled fish, and ordered rice for two and eaten none of it, and that in the morning there was a story about a cramp, a current, a regrettable accident, and everyone in the port had agreed to the story the way you agree to weather.

Sefa had cooked for divers for thirty years. He knew what fear tasted like when it came back on a boat: it tasted like men chewing without swallowing.

And now here was Okonkwo, standing among his pots, asking after a fish crate.

"A crate," Okonkwo said. "Delivered night before last. Heavy for its size. The porter remembers it because the label was wrong — marked for the lofts, but the lofts don't take deliveries at night."

"The lofts take whatever I say they take," Sefa said. He turned a fish on the grill so his hands would have work. Busy hands, closed mouth — the girl Lena had learned it somewhere, and people assumed she'd learned it from her father, but Sefa knew better. Her father had been all open hands and open mouth, laughing, generous, careless as a monsoon. The girl had built herself out of the opposite timber. Probably out of watching what openness cost.

"Company property has gone missing," Okonkwo said. "A ledger page. And we have reason to believe it left the compound in the company of—" he consulted the paper, though Sefa would have bet his whole cookhouse the paper said nothing of the kind— "a child."

Sefa laughed. It surprised even him, how easily it came out, warm and round as a dumpling. "A child. You've lost a page and found a baby. This is why I cook and you do paperwork."

Okonkwo did not laugh. The two painted doors behind him did not laugh. Somewhere under the grill, fat dripped and flared, and the smoke rose straight up through the roof vent the way smoke does when the air is holding its breath.

"Mr. Brandt," Okonkwo said, "has authorized compensation for information leading to recovery of company property. Compensation" — and here he named a figure, and Sefa's hands went still on the fish, because the figure was not a bribe. It was a sentence. It was the exact sum of a cookhouse, a lease, a wife's medicines, two daughters' school fees, and the small debt Sefa still carried to the widow of a man who had once pulled him out of the harbor with a boat hook and a great deal of swearing.

That was the unbearable mathematics. Not that the number was large. That the number was *correct*. Somewhere in a grey office, a man with wet eyes and tidy cuffs had sat down and calculated precisely what Sefa Okafor's loyalty weighed on the open market, and had written that number down, and had been right.

"I'll ask around the docks," Sefa said.

"You'll do better than ask." Okonkwo set the folded paper on the cutting board, weighted it with a stone salt dish, and left it there. "You have until sundown. After sundown, we stop asking and start looking, and Mr. Brandt has explained to me what happens to establishments that obstruct company inquiry." He looked around the cookhouse slowly, taking inventory the way Sefa took inventory — but counting only what could be burned, fined, or confiscated. "A shame. Good smoke. You can smell it from the magistrate's steps."

Then they were gone, and the cookhouse was full of their absence, and Sefa's wife Amara came out of the back room where she had been listening with both ears and neither breath.

"You will tell them," she said. Not a question. She had her mother's counting too.

"I will tell them," Sefa said.

"And you will not tell them true."

"And I will not tell them true."

She nodded once, went to the window, and began moving the medicinal jars off the sill — the ones that would look like contraband to men who didn't know ginger root from gunpowder. That was the other half of the mathematics, the part no ledger ever recorded: that survival was not a single choice but a hundred small ones, made quickly, in smoke, by people who would carry each one like a stone in the shoe for the rest of their lives.

* * *

Sefa spent the middle of the day doing what he did best, which was feeding people while measuring them.

The dock crowd came through as always — stevedores wanting pepper soup, a customs runner wanting extra plantain and giving nothing in return but gossip, which was its own currency. From the gossip, Sefa assembled the day's ledger: Brandt had locked the duplicate archive and posted his own clerk outside it, which meant Brandt was afraid of paper, which meant the paper mattered. Madame Reyes at customs had gone off shift angry, which meant someone had tried to move something through her station and she'd let it move anyway, or hadn't, and either way her anger had a shape Sefa recognized — the shape of a woman protecting somebody. And the girl Lena had not been seen since morning, which meant she was either hidden or running, and Sefa had promised her father's ghost, in the private chapel of his own kitchen, that she would be neither if he could help it.

He owed the dead man. Not the way the company kept accounts — a debt as a chain — but the old way, the way his mother had counted: a life pulled from the water weighs against a favor owed on land, and the scales stay tipped until you die or until you balance them, whichever comes first, and usually it's death, because favors are heavy.

But owing the dead man and owing his own family were two weights on the same beam, and this morning the beam had begun to tip the wrong way, and Sefa stood at the fulcrum sweating.

In the back room, behind the sacks of dried shrimp, the fish crate sat with its lid loose. He had moved the boy at dawn — not into the crate, God forbid, the crate was the thing they'd be shown — but into the space beneath his own sleeping platform, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of smoke and star anise, with a gourd of sweetened water and instructions delivered in the universal language of a finger pressed to lips. The boy had regarded him with those grey-green eyes, solemn as an auditor, and had not cried. That was the worst of it. Babies should cry. A baby who doesn't cry has already learned the first lesson of this coast: that being heard is dangerous.

At midday Sefa lifted the platform skirt and checked on him. The boy was awake, silent, watching the light through the floorboards as if reading something written there.

"You know what today is?" Sefa whispered to him. "Today I sell you. Loudly. To men with bad shoes. And tonight you are still here, and they are still fools, and we are both alive, and nobody thanks anybody, because that is how it is done."

The boy blinked. It was, Sefa felt, a fair review of the plan.

* * *

They came back at sundown, as promised, and this time they brought the smell of the counting room with them — ink and lamp oil and the particular sourness of men who have signed things.

"Well?" Okonkwo said.

Sefa wiped his hands on his apron with great ceremony, buying three seconds, because three seconds was a number too. "I asked," he said. "I asked the way you'd want. Quietly. And here is what the docks say." He let the pause stretch until Okonkwo leaned in. People always leaned in. It was the oldest trick in cooking — make them hungry first.

"The crate went through here, yes. Night before last. Marked for the lofts." He watched relief begin to bloom on the young one's face and killed it. "But it never reached the lofts. It went onto the *Kestrel* — the coastal packet — as deck freight, bound south. First stop Tanjung Selat, then down the straits. If your page and your child are anywhere, they're eating salt air and getting farther away by the hour."

It was beautiful, the lie. It had weight and draft and a wake behind it. It used real names and real schedules, which is what made lies seaworthy. The *Kestrel* had in fact sailed that morning — Sefa had smelled her coal at dawn — and by tomorrow noon she'd be beyond any rowboat Meridian could hire, and every hour the company spent telegraphing Tanjung Selat was an hour the girl wasn't spending in a cell.

Okonkwo stared at him for a long moment. Then he took out a stub of pencil and wrote on his palm, which told Sefa the man couldn't be trusted with his own notebook, and asked, "The porter. The one who remembered the crate. His name?"

"Ah." Sefa shook his head with genuine sorrow. "That one drinks. By now he won't remember his own name, let alone yours. But I can find him tomorrow. Tomorrow I'll bring him to the compound myself, sober, and you can hear it from his own mouth."

Tomorrow. Another number. Every tomorrow he sold them was a brick in the wall between the boy and the grey jackets, and Sefa intended to sell them tomorrows until the market closed.

They left. Amara came out from behind the curtain, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the cookhouse ticked and settled around them like a ship at anchor.

"They believed you," she said finally.

"The tall one did. The young ones believe whatever he believes." Sefa untied his apron and hung it on its nail, and his hands, which had lied steadily all evening, shook only now, when there was no one to perform steadiness for. "That's the trouble with this coast, Amara. Everyone survives. Nobody sleeps."

He went to the back room and knelt and lifted the platform skirt. The boy was asleep at last, curled small, one fist closed around a thread from the blanket — a blue fiber, the same blue as the diving belt Sefa had helped stitch a page into two nights ago, though the boy couldn't know that, and wouldn't, please God, for years.

"Your sister is out there," Sefa whispered, "doing something brave and stupid, and I am in here doing something cowardly and useful, and between us we might just get you to Thursday."

The boy slept on. Outside, far off toward the compound, a bell began to ring — not the harbor bell, not the dinner bell, but the thin urgent clanging of the counting-room alarm, and Sefa rose slowly to his feet with the boy's blue thread still caught in his fingers, and understood, with a cook's certainty, that somewhere in the grey heart of Meridian, something was burning.
