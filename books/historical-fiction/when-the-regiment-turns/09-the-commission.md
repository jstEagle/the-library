## 9. The Commission

The hall had been a granary once, before the war took the harvest and left only the building. Now its rafters held banners instead of grain, and the dust that sifted down through the slatted light was the dust of officialdom—chalk, ink, the powdered residue of men who had spent fifteen years polishing a lie until it shone.

Gideon sat with hands folded in a plain grey coat, the copied pages sewn into the lining over his heart. They had weighed almost nothing when Marta stitched them in, her needle steady, her mouth a hard line. Now they weighed like a millstone.

The commission of inquiry sat on a dais beneath the regimental colours: five officers in dress uniform, and at the center of them, Colonel Ashcombe—no longer colonel, Gideon reminded himself. General now. Promoted twice on the strength of Harrow Fen, his chest hung with the honours of a battle he had won on paper and lost in every other way. He had aged well. That was the obscenity of it. His hair had gone silver at the temples like a portrait of statesmanship, and his eyes, when they swept the hall, held the mild patience of a man who had never once doubted the arithmetic of other people's lives.

At the counsel's table sat a thin, precise man named Verlaine, who had spent the morning doing what Gideon had watched him do to three witnesses already: taking a human being apart along the seams and laying the pieces out until the hall saw nothing but thread.

The widow Perrin had gone first. She had seen the artillery fall on her own company's position; she had said so in a voice like a cracked bell. Verlaine had asked her, gently, whether grief might not have confused her memory of *which* battery had fired, whether the trauma of loss did not so often distort—whether, in fact, she had not already told a different version to a different clerk in a different year. By the end she was weeping and apologizing, apologizing, as though it were her shame that the truth had grown tangled in her keeping.

Then the surveyor's apprentice, now a grey-bearded man, who confirmed under Verlaine's questioning that maps could be copied, altered, recopied; that memory of a signature was not proof of a signature; that the document produced by the defense—a clean, confident report in Ashcombe's hand, dated three days after the battle—was the *official* record, and the official record had a way of outliving everything else.

*That is the weapon,* Gideon thought, watching. *Not the lie. The filing of it.*

Marta sat beside him, her hands still as stones in her lap. Tomas was two rows back, his shoulder bound beneath his coat, his face the colour of tallow. He had insisted on coming. The bullet in him had been dug out by lamplight four days ago, and he had spent the intervening time refusing, with a Wren-like obstinacy, to stay in bed.

"—and so the commission must ask itself," Verlaine was saying, closing his case against the last witness, "whether we are here to litigate a battle won fifteen years ago by the memory of the grieving, or to confirm the record of the men who fought it. I have no further questions for this witness. Indeed—" he turned, and his gaze found Gideon across the hall like a hook finding flesh, "—I understand the commission wishes to hear from one final witness. A nurse. Gideon Thornbury."

The name in that room, in that voice, was like cold water down the spine. Gideon rose.

* * *

The oath was administered. Gideon took the stand and gave name, rank, years of service—fifteen years, regimental nurse, present at the field hospitals of four campaigns, at Harrow Fen itself in the aftermath, sorting the dead.

"You kept records," Verlaine said. It was not a question.

"I kept records."

"Official records. Casualty returns. Death ledgers. All of them countersigned, all of them filed."

"All of them."

"And yet." Verlaine smiled the way a surgeon smiles before the first incision. "We have heard testimony this morning about a *private* ledger. A secret book. Kept without authority, without oversight, in which this witness is alleged to have recorded names omitted from the official returns. Is that correct?"

"It is correct."

"You understand that a document kept in secret, unverified, uncountersigned, has no standing before this commission."

"I understand what it has," Gideon said. "It has names."

A stir ran through the hall. On the dais, Ashcombe's expression did not change, but his stillness changed—a stillness like held breath.

Verlaine pressed on, smooth as water. "Names alleged by whom? Recorded on the testimony of whom? This commission has already heard how grief distorts, how memory invents. Why should we trust a book compiled in the dark by a man with an obvious grievance?"

"Because you can check it," Gideon said. "That is what a ledger *is*, sir. Every entry has a date, a company, a wound, a witness where there was one. I did not write it in the dark. I wrote it in the wards, by lamplight, with dying men dictating and living men correcting me. It was kept in secret because the truth was not safe in the open. That is not an argument against the ledger. It is an argument about what this army had become."

Verlaine let the silence stretch, then turned a page with a small, dry sound. "Let us speak of what else you wrote in the dark. We have examined the death returns from the winter of the second campaign. There is a boy listed there—Private Ansel Ferrow, seventeen years of age, convicted of desertion, sentenced to execution. And yet Private Ferrow, we find, did not die. There is a death record in your hand, countersigned by a chaplain who has since conveniently passed beyond questioning. And Private Ferrow, we are told, is alive. Living under another name. In another country." Verlaine looked up. "You falsified a death record. You freed a condemned man. You are a forger, a liar in the eyes of the army, and you stand here asking this commission to accept your *other* records as gospel."

The hall had gone very quiet. Gideon had known this moment was coming; had rehearsed it in the nights before, lying awake while Marta slept down the hall. And still, hearing it spoken aloud, felt the old fear rise—the fear of the ward at night, the fear of the knock at the door.

"Yes," Gideon said. "I falsified it."

The admission landed like a dropped instrument. Somewhere in the gallery a woman gasped.

"I was twenty-nine years old," Gideon went on, and the voice came out steadier than expected, the voice of the wards, the voice that had said *hold still* and *breathe* and *you are not alone* ten thousand times. "Ansel Ferrow was seventeen. He had run because his sergeant was sending boys to walk a minefield ahead of the column, and the official report called those deaths combat losses. His court-martial lasted eleven minutes. I know because I timed it against the drip of the still. Eleven minutes, and a boy's life. So I wrote a record that said he had died of his wounds, and I put him on a supply cart, and he lived. And I have never once lost sleep over it." Gideon turned, deliberately, to face the dais. "But I will tell the commission what that falsification taught me. It taught me that a record is a weapon. Whoever holds the pen decides who lived and who died—not at the front, but afterward, forever. Colonel Ashcombe held the pen at Harrow Fen. And for fifteen years, every man who died in that artillery strike has been recorded as a casualty of Captain Wren's incompetence. That is the falsified record this commission should be examining. Mine saved one boy. His buried forty."

"Forty—" Verlaine began.

"Thirty-eight by the official count," Gideon said. "Forty by mine. Two were never found. Their names are in my ledger. Their names are nowhere else on this earth."

Verlaine turned to the dais, and Gideon saw it—the flicker, the first true crack, when counsel realizes the trap has closed on the wrong hand. "The commission will disregard the witness's editorializing—"

"I will hear it," said the chairman, an old major-general with a face like a dried riverbed, who had not spoken all morning. "Continue, Thornbury. The ledger. Produce it."

* * *

Gideon stood, and Marta rose beside him, and together they walked to the long table before the dais where the exhibits lay in their official folders. Gideon unpicked the lining of the grey coat with fingers that did not shake—fifteen years of wards had taught these hands their business—and laid the copied pages out one by one, smoothing each flat, reading each name aloud into the hall.

"Private Dole, Marcus. Company B. Killed by our own artillery at Harrow Fen. Recorded as: died of fever, second week after the battle."

"Corporal Henn. Company B. Killed by our own artillery. Recorded as: deserted, fate unknown."

"Private Sallow. Company B. Seventeen years old. Killed by our own artillery. Recorded as: *not listed*."

The names went into the hall like stones into deep water, and the ripples went out and out. In the gallery, the widow Perrin had stopped weeping and was sitting very straight. An old sergeant near the door had closed his eyes and was moving his lips, counting.

Then the map. Tomas had carried it from Harrow Fen at the cost of his blood; now Gideon unrolled it and weighted its corners, and the surveyor's ink—faded, authentic, *dated before the battle*—showed the artillery coordinates in Ashcombe's own hand, and the correction, initialed and misfiled, that had never reached the guns.

"Read the date," Gideon said. "Read the initials. This is not memory, sir. This is not grief. This is paper."

Ashcombe rose. "Madam Chairman—gentlemen. This is a fabrication. Any clerk could—"

"Then produce the filing record," said the riverbed-voiced chairman. "Produce the counterfoil for the correction order. If the general's version is true, the paper trail will show it."

Silence. The particular, terrible silence of a man whose paper trail has been eaten.

And into that silence, from the back of the hall, came the sound of a door.

* * *

Elias Wren walked the length of the granary hall leaning on his brother's arm, and Gideon watched the room understand what it was seeing. He was wrecked—there was no other word. The illness had hollowed him; the fifteen years had written themselves across his face in lines like trench-maps. His uniform coat, what remained of his old one, hung on him like a flag on a broken mast. But he walked upright, and his eyes, when they found Gideon's, held something that was neither thanks nor farewell but a third thing, older than both: *we are on the record now.*

"I am Captain Elias Wren," he said, when they reached the table, and his voice carried further than his body had any right to let it. "Reported dead at Harrow Fen. I have come to correct the record."

What followed Gideon would remember afterward in fragments, the way one remembers a bombardment—the whole, and then the pieces. Wren's voice, level and ruined, giving the order as it was given, the coordinates as they were read, Ashcombe's face as the guns fired wrong and the colonel's eyes went cold with arithmetic. The cover-up assembled within the hour. The report dictated before the dead were counted. *He looked at the smoke and he looked at the promotion, and he chose, gentlemen. That is all I am here to say. He chose.*

Verlaine rose once, twice, and sat down twice. There was nothing to dismantle. The man on the table before them was the record.

And then, mid-sentence—Wren was saying *and the men of Company B deserved to have their names—* his hand, flat on the table, slid. He went down without a cry, the way the dying go, and Gideon was over the barrier before the sound of the fall finished, hands finding the wrist, the chest, the heart stuttering like a lamp in wind.

"Captain Wren is unwell," Gideon said to the hall, to the dais, to history, kneeling on the boards with one hand pressed flat over that failing heart. "He has been dying for some time. He came here anyway. I ask the commission to weigh why a man would spend his last strength on this room."

The chairman was on his feet. The hall was in uproar. And through it, faint as a pulse under Gideon's fingers, Wren's eyes opened and found Gideon's, and his mouth moved—no sound, only the shape of a word.

The word was *stay*.

Gideon looked up at the dais, at Ashcombe's face going grey as ledger-paper, and heard the chairman's voice cut through the noise like a gavel through a riot:

"The commission will adjourn for one hour. Colonel Ashcombe will remain. This inquiry is *not* concluded."
