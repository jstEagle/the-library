## 3. Requisition

The morning the army came to Ashden Vale, I was mending a tire on the Daimler and thinking about Mrs. Pettigrew's teeth.

This requires explanation, though at the time it required nothing of the sort. It was simply Tuesday. Edith Marlowe had asked me to deliver a letter to the dentist in Tavistock — an innocent-looking envelope that was, knowing her, anything but — and while I waited for the vulcanizer to do its work on the spare, I had turned my mind to the problem of Mrs. Pettigrew, who owed money to three people in this town and was owed by two, and whose false tooth had recently begun to click when she lied. The clicking was new information. I had filed it under *Pettigrew, M.*, between a debt to the fishmonger and a promise she'd made to keep quiet about the vicar's nephew.

I kept a ledger. Not the parish ledger, not the mill ledger, not any ledger with a spine and an official stamp. Mine lived in a oilcloth packet inside the spare wheel well of the Daimler, which is the one place in England no man will look, because looking at a spare tire suggests doubt about one's own driving. In it I recorded what I heard and read: names, debts, promises, threats, the small economies of silence that keep a town like Ashden Vale from eating itself alive.

I did not think of it as dangerous work. I thought of it as plumbing. You don't notice the pipes until they burst.

The army arrived at eleven o'clock, in two motorcars and a lorry, and they came up the Tavistock road past me without slowing, which told me everything before anyone said a word. Men who slow down are curious. Men who don't slow down have already decided.

* * *

By noon there were papers nailed to the church door, which struck me as a poor choice of venue given how the last set of documents nailed to church doors had worked out, but then, armies are not famous for their sense of irony.

I stood in the crowd outside the Green Dragon and listened to Mr. Cobbett, the parish clerk, read aloud in his dry, apologetic voice, as though apologizing to each sentence individually:

*"By order of the War Office, and pursuant to the Defence of the Realm Consolidation Act, the lands known as Ashden Green, together with the premises of Vale Mill and appurtenant water rights, are hereby requisitioned for His Majesty's service..."*

"Requisitioned," said Tom Hedley, the blacksmith, tasting the word like milk he suspected had turned. "That's a polite word."

"It's a legal word," said Cobbett, "which is worse."

There was more. The green was to become a training camp. The mill would store fodder and boots. And then came the paragraph that made the hair on my arms stand up, though I gave no sign of it, because giving signs is a habit I gave up around the age of nine:

*"...and all books, registers, rolls, and records of the parish shall be delivered into the custody of the camp adjutant for the duration."*

All records. Every record.

"Now see here," said Hedley. "The baptismal register? The burials?"

"Copies will be made available," said Cobbett, in the tone of a man reading terms and conditions he has not written and does not endorse. "In due course."

"In due course," repeated old Mrs. Fenwick, who had been saying "in due course" about the drainage on Fore Street since 1898 and had come to regard the phrase as a personal enemy.

I looked across the square and saw Edith Marlowe standing outside the post office with her arms folded, watching the officers' motorcars the way a chess player watches a board after a bad move — not with surprise, but with recalculation. She caught my eye. She didn't smile. That frightened me more than the papers did.

* * *

The surrender of the records happened on Thursday, in the vestry, and I attended in my official capacity, which is to say I stood by the door holding my cap and being invisible, a skill at which I am, if I may say so, exceptional.

The camp adjutant was a Captain Boyce, young, pink, and meticulous, with the air of a man who alphabetizes his socks. He sat at the vestry table with a receipt book open before him, and one by one Cobbett brought out the parish's memory and laid it down like a widow surrendering jewelry.

"Vestry minutes, 1861 to present."

Boyce wrote it down. "Condition?"

"Damp at the corners," said Cobbett. "It's Devon."

"Register of baptisms, 1843—"

"Marriages too," said Boyce. "Burials. Poor relief accounts. School attendance. Tithe schedules. Rate books." He said each item the way other men say grace — mechanically, thoroughly, without meaning any harm by it, which is somehow the most frightening way to say things.

And I stood by the door doing arithmetic. Because every one of those books contained names, and every name was a thread, and I had spent eleven years quietly learning where all the threads led — who owed whom, who feared whom, whose daughter had left for Bristol with no wedding ring and a story nobody believed. My ledger was built on top of theirs, the way ivy grows on a wall. Take away the wall and the ivy doesn't fall, exactly. But it hangs differently.

Then Cobbett went to the bottom drawer.

Now. Every clerk has a bottom drawer. It is a law of nature, like gravity or the reluctance of committees. And I knew — because I know things; it is my affliction and my trade — that Cobbett's bottom drawer held certain papers that had never been entered in any index. Letters from the year of the mill strike. A statement signed by six men who had been promised anonymity. A settlement the Ashden family had paid to keep a death from becoming an inquest. Thirty years of the town's private weather, kept off the official map.

Cobbett hesitated. It was only a moment — a pause you could have missed in a sneeze — but I saw his hand hover over the drawer, and I saw him look at Captain Boyce's pink, patient face, and I saw him make the calculation that small men have made before every catastrophe in history: *surely they won't notice.*

He surrendered the drawer's contents. All of them. He even helped Boyce list them.

And here is the thing that I have thought about every day since, the thing that sits on my chest at night like a cat that won't be fed off: as Cobbett lifted the last bundle out, a loose page slipped free and drifted to the floor, and neither of them saw it, and I could have picked it up. I was three steps away. It lay there face-down on the flagstones, plain as a fallen leaf, and I stood by the door with my cap in my hands and let it lie, because picking it up would have meant explaining why I wanted it, and invisibility, I have learned, survives only so long as you never reach for anything.

Boyce stamped his receipt, thanked Cobbett with genuine warmth — that was the worst part, the warmth; he truly believed he was performing a routine administrative function and not carrying off thirty years of other people's lives in a canvas sack — and the records went out to the lorry in boxes marked with chalk numbers.

The page stayed on the floor until Cobbett swept up that evening. I checked. I always check.

* * *

I drove Edith home along the back lane, because she asked for the long way, and the long way with Edith Marlowe always means she wants to talk where the hedges can't testify.

"They took the poor books," she said. "The whole history of everyone who ever needed help. Poor relief, orphan lists, the maternity fund subscriptions. Do you understand what that is, Bram? That isn't administration. That's a map of every family in this valley that has ever been desperate. They've requisitioned desperation."

"I'd noticed," I said.

She turned in her seat to look at me properly. Edith has a way of turning in seats that makes drivers wish for higher walls. "You've been very quiet since Thursday."

"I'm always quiet. It's my best feature."

"Bram." She used my name the way she used facts in a speech — precisely, and to effect. "When they inventory those records, some clerk is going to cross-reference them against something else. That's what clerks do. Cross-referencing is their whole religion. And when they do—" She stopped herself, which she almost never does, and looked out at the hedgerow. "There are people in this town whose safety depends on certain connections never being written down anywhere."

I kept my eyes on the lane. There was a pheasant crossing ahead, and I slowed for it, partly out of mercy and partly because it bought me four seconds to decide how much of the truth to tell.

"The connections are written down somewhere," I said finally.

"I know. In the parish books."

"No," I said. "Not only there."

The pheasant made it across. The car rolled on. Edith was silent for so long that I began composing apologies in my head, arranging them in order of dignity, and then she said, very quietly:

"How many names?"

"A hundred and forty-odd. Debts, promises, things people need kept quiet and things other people need remembered. It's how I keep the town safe. Or it was." I gripped the wheel. "It's how I've been keeping it safe for eleven years, miss, and today I watched a boy in a good uniform put somebody else's books in a sack, and I understood that mine goes in the same sack the day they find it. Everything I've built. Every secret I've kept for other people's protection. It's all just — paperwork now. It's all just waiting to be read by the wrong man."

Edith was quiet again. When I glanced over, she wasn't looking at me with pity, which I'd have resented, or alarm, which I'd have expected. She was looking at me the way she looks at a speech that needs restructuring — with respect for the material and grave concern for its author.

"You'll move it," she said. Not a question.

"I'll move it tonight."

"And then what? Bram, listen to me. A ledger in a wheel well protects secrets. But we're past secrets now. They've taken the town's memory into custody, and they'll issue it back to us in whatever form suits them. Copies, they said. *In due course.*" She laughed, short and hard. "They've stolen our past and issued us a receipt. And receipts, Bram, are only as good as the honesty of the issuer."

We came down into the vale as the light went amber behind the tor, and there they were: tents going up on the green like pale mushrooms after rain, sentries already walking the perimeter, a flag on the mill roof where the Ashden crest had been for a hundred years. A boy was leading a horse across what had been the cricket pitch. Everything looked the same and belonged to someone else entirely.

"There's one more thing," Edith said as I stopped at her gate. "The colonel commanding them. Havelock. He called on me this afternoon while you were in Tavistock."

My stomach performed a maneuver I did not authorize. "Did he."

"He was charming. He brought his compliments and a request — that I inform him, as a prominent local resident, of 'any persons whose conduct might prove detrimental to discipline.'" She opened the door but didn't get out. "He said it twice, Bram. As though testing how it sounded. And then he smiled and said he found that in a well-run community, most people were only too happy to talk."

She stepped down, smoothed her skirt, and paused with one hand on the gate.

"He shook my hand," she said. "And his palm was dry, and his grip was exactly right, and I thought: this is a man who has practiced shaking hands the way other men practice swordsmanship. Goodnight, Bram. Move your ledger somewhere better than a wheel well. Somewhere he'd never think to look because it's too obvious, or too humble, or too much trouble."

She went up the path. I sat in the car a moment, watching the campfires begin to dot the green like a rash, and I thought about the word *requisition*. How it sounds like a request. How it means *we have decided, and the deciding is done.*

Then I drove home through the dark, and I took the ledger out of the wheel well, and I held it in both hands under the gas lamp — a cheap exercise book in oilcloth, filled with my careful hand, a hundred and forty names, eleven years of other people's silences — and I understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only after the door has already closed, that I had not been keeping a ledger at all.

I had been building a weapon. I simply hadn't known yet whose hands it was waiting for.

Somewhere across the dark green, a bugle sounded the first note of the regiment's evening call, thin and clear and utterly indifferent to us, and I got to work finding a hiding place worthy of what the book had become.
