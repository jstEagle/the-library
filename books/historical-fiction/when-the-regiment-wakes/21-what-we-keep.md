## 21. What We Keep

The frost came early that January, silvering the millpond and turning the lane to Marlowe House into a corridor of small, brittle sounds. I drove the motor slowly, because the roads were treacherous and because I wanted the last half-mile to last. In eleven years of driving I had learned that arrival is a thing you can ration.

Edith Marlowe stood on her own front step in a coat too thin for the weather, and she did not wave. She simply watched me come up the drive with her hands folded, as though she had been standing there since dawn and intended to stand there until I arrived, whenever that might be, and that this was a perfectly reasonable use of a Tuesday.

"You look thinner," she said, when I stopped the car and got out.

"You look like a woman who has spent three weeks eating prison bread."

"I have. It builds character." She looked past me at the motor, at the grey sky, at the bare elms along the wall. "It also builds an appetite. Is there anything in the house? Don't answer — of course there isn't. Mrs. Petch has been keeping it shut like a tomb. Come inside and we'll see what a tomb has in its larder."

Inside, the house smelled of cold ash and lavender, and every surface wore its dust like a held breath. She went through the rooms touching things — the mantel clock, the spine of a book, the piano lid — not checking that they were there so much as greeting them. I built a fire while she talked, which was how she preferred to talk: sideways, with her back half-turned, saying true things to a room rather than to a person.

"They let me go on a technicality," she said. "The magistrate decided the documents I burned were not proven military property. He said it with his mouth full of regret, as if he were apologizing for the weather. Havelock's tidy account fell apart the moment two of his own clerks contradicted each other about dates." She turned then, firelight catching her face. "Your doing. Your testimony made them afraid of being consistent."

"Being consistent was never their difficulty," I said. "Being believed was mine."

"And yet." She sat down across from me, and for once she did not arrange herself for effect. She just sat, tired, sixty-one years old, a woman who had been arrested for burning paper in her own parlor. "Three newspapers, Bram. My cousin in Cheltenham sent me the clippings. They spelled your name wrong in all three — *Bramwell Okafor*, as though you were a solicitor — but they printed every name you read out. Every one. Do you know what that means?"

"It means a typesetter in London worked a long shift."

She laughed — a short, cracked laugh, the first I'd heard from her since before the arrest — and pressed her hand briefly over her eyes. "It means they can't unprint it. That's all I ever wanted, you know. Not victory. Un-erasability." She lowered her hand. "How is Sergeant Pryce?"

I told her. There wasn't much good to tell.

* * *

The court-martial had taken four hours. The theft of a regimental document by a serving soldier, in wartime conditions — or what the army chose to call wartime conditions, though no war had been declared and none would be for some months yet — carried a maximum of penal servitude. Pryce got eighteen months and dismissal with ignominy, which is the army's way of saying *you were never one of us* in language formal enough for the record.

He was alive. I reminded myself of this daily, the way one takes a medicine. Alive, at Aldershot, in a cell he'd leave in eighteen months with a record that would follow him into every civilian job he ever applied for. Eighteen months bought by four hours of my hesitation outside the camp gate, the night I'd stood with the ledger under my coat and chosen testimony over rescue, arithmetic over loyalty, and hated myself with a thoroughness I have never quite managed since.

But hatred, I have found, is a poor clerk. It keeps bad books. So I did what I knew how to do instead.

I wrote to his mother.

It took me six drafts. The first was too legal — names, charges, dates, the whole machinery laid out like an inventory. The second was too comforting and therefore dishonest. The third I burned out of sheer disgust. What I finally sent was this:

*Dear Mrs. Pryce,*

*Your son Thomas is sentenced to eighteen months at Aldershot. He is well. He is fed. He asked me to tell you that the fault was entirely his own and that you are not to believe otherwise, and I am telling you this because he asked, but I am also going to tell you something he did not ask me to tell you, because I believe you have a right to it.*

*What your son took was a book of names belonging to the people of Ashden Vale — names the army was using to frighten them. He took it so that those names could be spoken aloud where they could be heard. One of them was a man called Alfred Dunn, who disappeared from the camp in October and whom the army called a deserter. Your son helped make sure that Alfred Dunn's name is now printed in three newspapers, and that lawyers in London are asking questions the army cannot conveniently answer.*

*He will tell you himself that he is ashamed. I have seen him ashamed, and I want you to know that what I saw was not shame. It was a man who understood exactly what a thing cost and paid it anyway. When he comes home, whoever he is when he comes home, he will be someone you can be proud of without having to explain anything to anyone.*

*I enclose the newspaper clippings. Keep them somewhere safe. Paper keeps better than memory, and memory keeps better than official reports.*

*Yours faithfully,*
*Bram Okafor*

I posted it from the village rather than the camp post office, and then I wrote a second letter, shorter, to Pryce himself, telling him what I'd done, so that nothing reached his mother that he hadn't been warned of. Even now — especially now — I could not bear to be a man who handled other people's words without their leave. I had spent too many years reading letters I was never meant to open. The habit of confession had to start somewhere.

His reply came eleven days later, a single sheet, the handwriting of a man writing on his knee:

*Okafor —*

*Mam writes that she has pinned the clippings inside the hymn board at chapel, behind the numbers, where nobody looks. She says the congregation sings the hymns and the truth sits behind them, which she considers very fitting.*

*Don't write again. They read everything here. But if you happen to be passing Aldershot on a Tuesday, the canteen allows visitors between two and four, and I hear the tea is terrible, which I find I miss.*

*T.P.*

I passed Aldershot on a Tuesday. The tea was terrible. We talked for forty minutes about his mother's garden and the correct way to prune roses, neither of us knowing the first thing about roses, and it was the best conversation I had that winter.

* * *

Nell Ashcroft came to the cottage on a Thursday, in the blue hour between lamplighting and full dark, with her son's cap in her hand the way she always carried it — as though the boy might at any moment need it produced, or hidden, or defended.

I had known Nell for nine years. Her secret was the first entry I had ever written in the old ledger: *Nell A., son b. 1904, father gone to Canada, no letters expected.* Nine words that had kept a boy legitimate in the eyes of a town that liked its legitimacy documented, because I kept the documentation and the documentation said what the town needed it to say. For nine years those nine words had been a locked drawer. Then Colonel Havelock had opened the drawer and held it out to her like a gift, and she had taken it, and told Edith's movements to save her boy, and wept while she did it, and nobody in Ashden Vale had blamed her except Nell Ashcroft, who blamed herself with the efficiency of a professional.

She stood in my doorway and didn't come in.

"I've brought you something," she said.

She handed me a notebook. Cheap, marbled, the kind schoolchildren use, with a rubber band around it to keep it shut. Inside, in her small, careful hand — the hand of a woman who had learned to write late and practiced the way other people practice piety — was a fair copy of the ledger's most damning pages. The disappearances. The payments. The threats, transcribed word for word from what Havelock had said to whom. She had copied them during the raid on Marlowe House, unasked, unknown, and hidden them in the flour sack I still hadn't fully accounted for.

"I don't need it back," she said. "You've got yours. But I thought — two copies is better than one. That's what you're always saying. Paper keeps better than memory."

"Nell—"

"Don't thank me." Her chin came up, and for a moment she looked remarkably like Edith Marlowe, which surprised us both. "I informed on that woman to save my Charlie, and I'd do it again tomorrow, and I'm not sorry, and I'm sick to death of being sorry. But I'll not be a woman who only ever gave this town a betrayal. So there's my copy. And there's one more thing."

She came inside then, finally, and sat down on the chair nearest the door, the chair of a woman who does not intend to stay long, and put Charlie's cap on her knee.

"The new ledger," she said. "Edith tells me you've started one. Truths witnessed, she said. Things to be opened when the world's ready." She smoothed the cap with her thumb, over and over, a small circular motion like polishing. "I want Charlie's name in it."

I waited. With Nell you learned to wait; the words came up from somewhere deep and arrived in their own order.

"Not the old entry," she said. "Not *father gone to Canada, no letters expected*. That was a silence, and you kept it, and I'll bless you for it till I die. But this new book — you said it's not for silences. So I want it written plain. *Charles Frederick Ashcroft, born the ninth of March, 1904, son of Nell Ashcroft, who raised him alone and is not ashamed.*" She looked up at me, eyes bright and level. "Father's name included or left out, whichever you think serves him best. But the rest of it written plain, so that when the world's ready and somebody opens that book, my boy reads that his mother was not ashamed. Because I wasn't. I was frightened, and I was careful, and I lied to protect him, and none of those things are shame. Nobody ever taught me the difference until I saw you keep all our secrets like they were worth something. Worth something, Mr. Okafor. Not shameful. Worth something."

The fire ticked. Somewhere down the lane a dog announced a cart and thought better of it.

"Nell," I said, "you understand that a book like that — if anyone reads it before the world is ready —"

"I know what it costs. I've seen the bill presented." She almost smiled. "I'm not asking you to promise to protect me. I'm asking you to write it down anyway. That's the whole difference, isn't it? The old book was secrets kept. This one's truths kept. Keeping's the same work either way. You just stop flinching while you do it."

So I got the new ledger down from the shelf — a proper bound volume, bought in London, heavy paper, sewn spine, built to outlast its keeper — and I wrote, in my best hand, while she watched:

*Charles Frederick Ashcroft, born the ninth of March, 1904, son of Nell Ashcroft, who raised him alone and is not ashamed. Witnessed and recorded this day by Bram Okafor, at her request, openly.*

"There," she said softly, when the ink had dried. "Now it exists twice. Once in the world and once in the book. Harder to kill a thing that lives in two places."

She stayed for tea after all, in the end — the door-chair abandoned for the one by the fire — and told me about Charlie's scholarship examination in the spring, and how he wanted to be a surveyor, and how she'd told him about the ledger herself, all of it, the night after the inquiry closed, because she refused to let him learn his own history from a gossip. "He said," she reported, with fierce satisfaction, "*Mum, does that mean Mr. Okafor knows everything about everybody?* And I said yes. And he thought about it, the way he does, chewing his sleeve, and then he said, *Is he kind about it?* And I said yes. And he said, *Then he's the most important man in Ashden Vale,* and went back to his sums."

When she'd gone I sat for a long while with the new ledger open on my knees, reading backward through the entries I'd made since New Year's Eve. Alfred Dunn, alive in London under a lawyer's protection. Sergeant Thomas Pryce, eighteen months, the truth of it pinned behind the hymn numbers at a chapel in Wales. Edith Marlowe, released, already drafting a pamphlet titled *What the Inquiry Did Not Ask*. Page after page of things that had nearly been erased and weren't.

And now Charles Frederick Ashcroft, entered openly, at his mother's request, the first name in the book that had walked in the front door instead of being smuggled in through a keyhole.

Outside, the frost was hardening toward morning, and far off — or perhaps not so far off, perhaps only at the edge of hearing, perhaps only in my own chest — I could feel the year gathering itself. February papers spoke of Serbia in tones that had changed. The colonel's replacement drilled his men on the green with a new briskness, as though rehearsing for something larger than manoeuvres. Edith had begun saying *when the regiment marches out* instead of *if*, and Edith was rarely wrong about the direction history intended to travel.

I dipped my pen and turned to a fresh page, and began the next entry.
