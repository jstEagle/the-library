## 6. Her Mother's Hand

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Odile understood what she was looking at.

She sat at the workbench in the back room of the Ravensworth print shop, the ledger open before her, its pages soft as cloth from handling. Outside, Vellmarin slept under a thin rain that ticked against the shutters like a compositor tapping sorts into place. She had been awake for — she checked the clock on the mantel and was startled to find it past two — nearly seven hours. Her father believed she'd gone to bed. Anselm believed she would burn the thing. Both of them were wrong.

She had begun the night with the intention of destruction. That was the honest truth of it. She had carried the ledger downstairs wrapped in an oilcloth, set it beside the stove, and stood with her hand on the iron door for the better part of ten minutes. The fire was banked but alive; one push of the panel and the names inside would be ash before they could become evidence. It would have been simple. It would have been safe. Her father's shop, her father's life, her own neck — all of it argued for burning.

But she had opened it instead. Just to look once more at her mother's handwriting before she fed it to the flames. That was all she told herself she wanted: a last look.

She was still looking.

* * *

The ledger's entries were not written in any alphabet Odile knew, and yet every mark on them was familiar. That was the horror of it, or the wonder — she could not decide which word fit. Each page was ruled into columns, and each column held strings of small characters: hooks, stems, serifs, dots suspended above strokes like accents over foreign vowels. At first glance it might have been shorthand, some merchant's speed-writing, a stenographer's private script. But Odile had grown up in a print shop. She had cut type since she was tall enough to see over the composing stone. And what she saw now, page after page, was not writing at all.

It was typefaces.

Not whole faces — no, nothing so obvious. Fragments of them. A letter here that curved in exactly the way the Bodoni italics curved, with that knife-thin transition from thick to hairline that old Monsieur Garamond's foundry had made fashionable twenty years ago. A numeral there whose flag terminated in the precise blunt serif used only in the shop's jobbing face, the one her father bought secondhand from a bankrupt stationer in the year Odile lost her first tooth. The characters in the ledger were not letters of any language. They were *letterforms*, lifted from a dozen different fonts, arranged in sequences, and the sequence itself — not the shape alone, but which face each glyph came from, thick-stemmed or hairlined, bracketed or unbracketed, old-style or modern — that was the message.

A cipher built out of typography. Built out of the very thing Odile did with her hands six days a week.

She sat back and pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw stars. When she lowered them, the ledger waited, patient as lead.

Only a cutter would ever read this. Not merely a printer — plenty of men in Vellmarin could tell a Caslon from a Didot at arm's length. But a cutter, someone who shaped the matrices herself, who knew the faces not as products but as habits, as tendencies of hand, as the small betrayals of a punchcutter's wrist — only such a person would notice that the *g* on page eleven descended with a leftward flick that existed in exactly one font in the city, and that font lived in the Ravensworth shop. Only someone who had spent years learning why letters leaned the way they leaned.

Her mother had built a code that only Odile — or someone like her — could break. And she had hidden it inside the machine, the temperamental composing machine that Odile had oiled and mended and cursed for half her life without once suspecting what slept beneath its false panel.

*Maman,* she said aloud, to the empty room, to the rain. *What were you?*

* * *

There had been games. Of course there had been games. Odile had forgotten them for fifteen years, buried them under grief and routine the way one buries anything too bright to look at, but now they came back with the force of floodwater.

She was five, maybe six, sitting on her mother's lap at the type case, and Marguerite Ravensworth — though even the name felt provisional now, a coat borrowed for the weather of a respectable life — held up a piece of printed waste paper torn from the day's spoiled impressions.

"Find the impostor," her mother said. It was their name for the game. "One of these letters doesn't belong. Look closely, little wren."

And Odile would squint and point, and be wrong three times, four times, and then suddenly see it — an *e* among *e*s whose crossbar tilted a degree too high, a spy from another case hiding in plain sight — and her mother would laugh, that low warm laugh like water over stones, and kiss the top of her head and say, "There. You have eyes like a jeweler. Never let anyone tell you different."

Another game: *Name the Face.* Her mother would set a single character before her — just one letter, cast in metal — and Odile had to say where it came from. "Jobbing," she'd guess, and her mother would shake her head. "Look at the foot serifs. Brackets. Who brackets their serifs?" "Old Fontaine." "Old Fontaine," her mother agreed, "who never met a corner he didn't want to soften." And they would both laugh at the joke, because in their household the typefounders of Europe were old acquaintances, gossiped about like neighbors over the fence.

She had thought it was play. She had thought her mother simply loved the trade, loved it the way Odile loved it, and wanted to share that love with her daughter. And perhaps she had. But now, turning the ledger's pages by candlelight, Odile understood that the games had been something else entirely. They had been instruction. Deliberate, patient, disguised instruction — a mother teaching her daughter to read a language that did not officially exist, so that someday, if someday ever came, the daughter could do precisely what Odile was doing now.

*Someday* had come. Someday had come while her mother lay dying of the lung fever, saying ordinary things, leaving ordinary instructions about the shop and the accounts and Papa's melancholy, and never once hinting that a false panel waited in the machine, that a ledger of condemned names waited behind it, that her daughter's childhood games had been the key to all of it.

Unless — Odile turned the thought over slowly, carefully, the way one turns a fragile sort — unless her mother *had* told her. Unless the games themselves were the telling, delivered years in advance, trusting that memory would keep the message safe until the messenger was needed. Trusting her. Trusting the child with eyes like a jeweler.

Odile put her head down on the workbench, on the cool wood beside the ledger, and wept — not the clean grief of the funeral, which had at least been permitted, but a ragged, astonished grief braided through with something that felt disgracefully like pride. Her mother had been a conspirator. Her mother had been *good* at it. Whatever else Marguerite Ravensworth had been — and God only knew what else remained to be discovered — she had been magnificent, and she had chosen her daughter as her heir.

* * *

By three o'clock the tears had dried and the work began.

Deciphering was slow, maddening, exquisite labor. Odile fetched the type cases one by one and set them along the bench like witnesses at an inquiry. The body text of the ledger, she determined within the hour, ran in a modified old-style — the entries' basic skeleton — but every third or fourth character switched faces, and the switches carried the meaning. A thick-stemmed modern where an old-style belonged meant one thing; a hairline italic meant another; a swash form, a reversed letter, a character deliberately miscut with its serif clipped — each variation was a word, or part of one. There were rules. She could feel the rules underneath the surface, consistent as grammar, and she could feel her mother's mind in them: economical, playful, ruthless about precision. Some of the substitutions were jokes. She was almost certain one recurring cluster — a fat-faced display capital wedged absurdly into running text — was her mother being funny, some private pun Odile couldn't yet translate.

She worked the way she cut type: hunched, breath shallow, the world reduced to the space between her fingertips and the page. Neighbors surfaced first, the easy words. *Boulangerie.* *École.* *Rue des Tanneurs.* Then trades, coded as she'd guessed from the start — *compositeur*, *relieuse*, *imprimeur* — and then, with a lurch, whole sentences began to assemble themselves out of the fog.

*Meeting. Thursday. Cellar of the —*

The rest was in a substitution she hadn't cracked yet. She wrote it down anyway, copied it faithfully into her notebook with a cutter's exactness, and moved on.

Toward dawn she deciphered a passage that stopped her heart:

*If you are reading this, you are mine. No one else could be.*

She stared at it for a long time. The candle guttered. Rain kept its small percussion on the shutters. The sentence was either a message intended for whoever broke the cipher — and who else could break it? — or it was nothing, coincidence, her own exhausted mind arranging marks into comfort. But no. It was deliberate. She could see the deliberation in the letterforms, in the care of the spacing, in the way the final line sat on the page with the balance of a finished plate. Her mother had known. Had planned for this night, or a night like it, years before Odile was born, perhaps. Had sat at some other bench, in some other city, under some other name, and cut these very matrices with her own hands, thinking — of what? Of a daughter not yet imagined? Of a shop not yet rented? Of a quiet man named Ravensworth who would ask no questions because he loved too much to risk the answers?

*I am yours,* Odile whispered back to the page. *I always was. Tell me what to do.*

But the ledger was not a letter. It was a list. And lists do not answer questions; they demand action.

* * *

The light was gray at the windows when she heard the bolt of the street door turn below. Footsteps on the stairs — her father's uneven tread, and a second step she knew by now, lighter, more hesitant. Anselm. Of course. He had said he would come back today, and census men, she was learning, were creatures of terrible punctuality even when off duty.

She had perhaps a minute. She looked at the ledger, at the scattered type cases, at the notebook full of half-translated names, and made the decision the way her mother must have made a hundred decisions before her: quickly, completely, without looking back.

The ledger went back into its oilcloth. The oilcloth went under the loose floorboard behind the flour bin — a hiding place so obvious it was nearly invisible, because no one hides things in the place a fugitive would think of first, except that everyone does, except that her mother had chosen it, and her mother had chosen everything else correctly so far. The type cases went back to their racks. The notebook she slid into her apron pocket, against her hip, where it beat faintly against her body like a second heart.

When Anselm knocked — he knocked now, she noticed; yesterday he would have entered as an officer of the state enters, entitled — she opened the door with ink still on her fingers and exhaustion sitting honestly on her face, and let him see both.

"You've been up all night," he said. Not a question. His eyes went past her shoulder to the workbench, to the candle stub, to the faint ghost of type dust on the wood.

"I've been working," she said. "Type doesn't set itself."

"No," Anselm said quietly. "I imagine it doesn't." He hesitated in the doorway, and she watched him wrestle with whatever he had climbed the stairs to say. "Mademoiselle Ravensworth. Odile. I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide what I deserve."

Behind him, through the window at the stair's landing, the rain had stopped and the morning lay flat and pale over the rooftops, and somewhere across the city a bell began to ring the hour — six strokes, unhurried, indifferent — and Odile thought of the cellar on the Rue des Tanneurs, of Thursday, of how many days remained between now and the arrests the ledger promised, and she stepped aside to let him in.

"Tell me," she said. "And then we're going to need a map."
