## 7. Three Checkpoints

Odile had walked the route to the river a thousand times and never once seen it. That was the thing about streets, she thought as she stood at the corner of the Rue des Halles with a market basket on her arm and nothing in it but a cabbage and a length of ribbon: you could live inside a city your whole life the way you lived inside your own skull, and never turn around to look at the back of it.

Now she was looking. Now she could see nothing else.

The market gate stood at the end of the street like a held breath. It had been there since before her father was born — two stone pillars, an iron arch, the city's crest hammered flat by a century of weather — and she had passed beneath it every market day since she was tall enough to carry a basket. It had never been more than architecture. This morning it was a mouth, and it was open, and there were soldiers standing in the throat of it.

Three of them, where ordinarily there would be one bored gendarme waving through carts of onions. These wore the grey coats of the garrison, not the blue of the municipal watch, and one of them had a bayonet fixed in the pale morning light, which was either a formality or a message. A trestle table had been dragged across the cobbles, and a corporal sat behind it with a ledger of his own — the thought arrived before she could stop it, a ledger, everyone in this city was keeping ledgers now — and beside him a second man turned over the contents of a fishmonger's cart with a long stick, lifting the canvas, prodding the straw, tapping the barrels and listening to them the way her father tapped a forme to hear whether the type was locked up tight.

Odile bought her cabbage. She bought her ribbon. She adjusted the basket on her hip and walked toward the gate the way she had walked toward it ten thousand times before, at the pace of a woman thinking about dinner, and she let her eyes do the work while her face did nothing at all.

The table. That was the first thing. Papers were demanded at the table — she watched a carter hand over a folded slip, watched the corporal read it without hurry, watched him look up and compare the man's face to the face on the paper as though the two might have quarreled overnight. The carter was waved through. A woman with geese was not asked for anything at all; the geese were apparently beneath suspicion. But the fishmonger's cart was emptied to its boards, and the fishmonger stood in the road with his hat in his hands while a soldier's stick went through his ice and his salt and his morning's catch, and when nothing was found the soldier did not apologize, and the fishmonger did not expect him to. He simply repacked his cart with the movements of a man who had done this before, or who understood without being told that he would do it again.

So: papers at the table, faces checked against papers, carts searched. Goods spilled. Time taken — the line was already backing up toward the square, and the soldiers did not care, and the delay itself was a kind of weapon, because a woman standing in a queue for an hour had an hour in which to tremble.

Odile turned down the lane behind the tanneries and let the gate fall out of sight. Her heart was going like a treadle. She put her hand flat against a wall to steady herself and felt the brick cold through her glove, and she made herself stand there and count, the way her mother had taught her to count when she was small and frightened of storms — *one, two, three, four, the lightning is already past* — except that the lesson had never been about storms at all, had it. She understood that now, standing in a stinking lane with a cabbage going soft against her ribs. Every game her mother had played with her had been this: the counting, the memory rhymes, the little cipher puzzles scratched in chalk on the kitchen slate that her mother had called *arithmetic for clever girls*. Her mother had been building her. Brick by brick, in a print shop on the Rue des Halles, she had been building a daughter who could stand in a lane and count her way back to calm.

*You knew,* Odile said to the air, to the dead, to the woman who had signed a false name on a marriage register and a true one nowhere at all. *You knew I would need this.*

She walked home the long way, past the customs house.

It sat on the river's edge like a fat spider, the customs house, all pale stone and dark windows, and she had never given it a second's thought in her life because nothing she had ever carried was worth taxing. Now she stood across the quay from it with her basket and studied it the way her father studied a page for errors before the press came down. Guards at the water door. Guards at the land door. A covered yard behind, where carts went in empty of inspection and came out stamped. And on the quay itself, two gendarmes walking a slow circuit, and between them, at the foot of the customs stairs, a table — of course a table — where a clerk in a green coat examined the papers of every soul crossing from the river district into the old town.

Three checkpoints. Anselm had said it first, drawing on the back of a census form with the stub of a pencil: the market gate, the bridge, the customs house. Three doors between the Rue des Halles and the printers' guild hall on the far bank, and behind every door a table, and behind every table a man whose whole trade was the difference between one name and another.

She was still standing there when a hand closed on her arm — not hard, but certain — and she did not scream, and did not run, and only afterward understood that her mother's counting had held her upright even then.

"You're making a spectacle of yourself," Anselm murmured. He was in his census coat, his ledger under his arm, the very picture of the state's mild paperwork made flesh. "A woman stares at the customs house for a quarter hour, someone notices. Someone always notices. That is the entire principle on which my profession rests."

"Then I'm fortunate it was you who noticed."

"You are fortunate I was counting you among the things I am no longer willing to count for them." He said it quietly, without drama, and drew her along the quay with a hand at her elbow, two clerks, two gray and unremarkable people, invisible in the way that only the state's own servants could be invisible. "Come. We should not be seen standing still anywhere in this city anymore."

* * *

They mapped it that evening in the print shop, after her father had gone up to bed, with the shutters closed and the lamp turned down to a coin of light, and the composing machine — her mother's machine, the machine that had kept its secret for nine years behind a false panel no wider than a hand — sitting on its bench like a witness who had finally decided to speak.

Anselm drew the city. He drew it quickly and well, and Odile realized with a small cold shock that he had drawn it from memory: every street, every alley, the curve of the river, the three bridges, and the checkpoints marked with small careful crosses, like graves.

"The market gate first," he said. "It's the softest. Market days they're looking for smuggled flour and unlicensed meat, not people. Papers, a search of whatever you carry, and through. The danger is the queue — an hour, sometimes two, standing still with it on you." He did not say *with the ledger on you*. He did not need to. The ledger lay between them on the bench, sewn now into the heart of a bolt of linen, and every so often Odile's eyes went to it the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

"The bridge," she said.

"The bridge." Anselm set down the pencil and rubbed his eyes, and in the low light he looked suddenly older than his years, a man worn down to the wire. "The Pont-Neuf is the only crossing they're not yet watching foot by foot, and that will not last. There's a sentry post at the near end and a second at the far. They stop carts. They stop anyone whose papers name a district on the far bank. And—" he hesitated, and she watched him decide to finish the sentence, "—and my superior, Commandant Vasseur, has taken a recent interest in my ink requisitions. If Vasseur is at the bridge on the night we cross, then the bridge is the hard one."

"Then we don't cross on the night we cross. We choose the night after we see where he is."

"You sound like a general."

"I sound like a compositor. You plan the forme before you lock it. You don't discover your errors under pressure." She heard her father in the words, and her mother underneath him, and did not know whether to laugh or grieve, so did neither.

"And the customs house last." Anselm tapped the far bank, where the guild hall waited on the Rue de l'Imprimerie like a promise. "It's the worst of the three and there is no way around it. The river district has one land approach and the customs house sits on it like a cork. Full search. Papers opened, persons searched, goods unwrapped. If they take the linen bolt off your shoulder and cut the stitching, it's finished — not just for you. For every name in it."

"Then the linen has to be boring," Odile said. "Boring enough to be unwrapped and found to be nothing but linen." She had been turning this over for two days, and it came out of her now whole, the way a line of type comes out of the stick whole when the justification is right. "A bolt of shirting for the shop. Ordinary, invoiced, dull. My father's name is known at the customs house — thirty years of paying his duties on time, never a dispute. A Ravensworth bolt of linen is not worth a second look, *if* the papers are perfect and *if* my face is boring too."

"And if they ask you a question the papers can't answer?"

She had thought about that as well. She had thought about almost nothing else. "Then I answer it," she said. "Whatever it is. I've been Odile Ravensworth, daughter of Aloïs Ravensworth, my whole life. That much of me is true. I can stand inside the true part and let them look at it."

Anselm looked at her for a long moment across the coin of lamplight. "You've changed," he said, and it was not a compliment exactly, and not a warning either. It was the tone a man uses when he notices the river has risen overnight.

"So have you," she said. "You altered the first permit tonight. I saw your hands while you did it. They didn't shake."

"They shook afterward."

"Mine too. Afterward is allowed."

He almost smiled. It was a small, exhausted thing, that almost-smile, but it was the first one she had seen on him since the census visit, and it made the room feel briefly like a room where two people lived rather than a room where two people were hiding.

They worked until the candle guttered. He assigned the bridge to himself — *my papers are real papers, altered; yours are forged from nothing, and a real thing bent is harder to detect than a false thing made* — and she did not argue, though she wanted to, though some stubborn part of her wanted to carry it across all three doors herself and dare the city to stop her. That was not courage, she told herself. That was pride wearing courage's coat. Her mother's ledger deserved better than her pride.

By the time they finished, the plan lay on the bench in pencil and crosses: the gate, the bridge, the customs house. Three tables. Three men with three ledgers. One bolt of linen, one census satchel, one night, and somewhere at the end of it a press that could turn names into something a bayonet could not unpick.

"Terror at the margins," Anselm said quietly, looking at the map. "That's what this is. The center of it is only walking. It's the edges that will kill us."

"Then we stay in the center," Odile said, "and we walk."

* * *

It was Anselm who left first, slipping out through the yard with his collar up, and Odile stood a while in the dark shop after the door had closed, listening to the city. Somewhere a patrol's boots went by in rhythm. Somewhere a dog, and a shutter, and the river working at its pilings the way it had worked at them all her life, indifferent and eternal.

She put her hand on the composing machine. The iron was cold and faintly greasy, and beneath her palm she could feel the seam of the false panel, invisible to any eye that did not know, and she thought: *she hid you inside me too.*

She was still standing there, in the dark, with her hand on her mother's machine and her mother's counting running in her head like a treadle that would not stop, when she heard a soft scrape at the front door — not a knock, nothing so honest as a knock — and a chalk line being drawn, she would swear it, a chalk line on the wood.

She lit the lamp. She opened the door.

Lucien, the baker's son, stood in the street with chalk dust still on his fingers and terror and resolve fighting openly in his face, and he said, "I know my father's name is in something. I've come to help you carry it."
