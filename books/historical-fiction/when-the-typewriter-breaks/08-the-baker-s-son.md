## 8. The Baker's Son

The bell above the shop door rang at half past four, in that dead hour between the afternoon deliveries and the closing of shutters, when the light through the front window went the color of weak tea and Odile's fingers ached from six hours of sorting fourteen-point bodoni.

She did not look up at first. Customers came rarely to Ravensworth & Son — her father had let the trade dwindle to funeral cards, invoices, and the parish notices for three churches that paid late but always paid — and when they did come, they came with the apologetic shuffle of people who knew they were asking for something small. She assumed this one wanted a memorial card. They always wanted memorial cards in autumn.

"Mademoiselle Ravensworth."

The voice was young, and it cracked slightly on the second syllable of her name, the way voices do when they have been rehearsed in front of a mirror and still fail their owner at the door. She looked up over the type case.

Lucien Marchand stood just inside the threshold, cap in his hands, flour dusting the dark wool of his jacket at the shoulders where he'd wiped his palms without thinking. She knew him the way one knows everyone in the Rue des Fondeurs: by sight, by trade, by the smell that preceded him into any room. The Marchands' bakery sat eleven doors down, close enough that on baking days the whole street breathed yeast and woodsmoke whether it wished to or not. He had been a boy who raced hoops under the eaves when she was already apprenticed at the stone; now he was perhaps nineteen, broad through the chest from hauling sacks, with his father's heavy jaw and his mother's quick, unhappy eyes.

"Lucien," she said. "Your father wants the Advent notices reset? Tell him I can't promise before Sunday — the twelve-point is spoken for—"

"It isn't the notices." He turned his cap a quarter-turn. His knuckles were white around the brim. "Could we — is there somewhere we could speak? Not out here."

The shop was not large. There was the front room with its cases and its press, the composing stone beneath the window, the narrow stair to the living quarters, and behind everything the back room where her father kept the paper stock and his melancholy in equal measure. Her father was upstairs. She could hear him moving — that slow, dragging tread he had developed these two years since her mother died, as though grief had added weight to his boots.

Odile set down her composing stick. Behind her own eyes, unbidden, the ledger opened itself: *M. Marchand, baker, Rue des Fondeurs — Thursday evenings — the cellar on Vasseur Lane — carries messages to the river.* She had read the entry four times now, trying to make it mean less than it meant. Trying to make it a stranger's father, some other Marchand, some other street.

"There's nothing to speak about privately," she said, and heard how badly it came out — too fast, too high, a door slammed before anyone had knocked. "I mean. What is it you want?"

He looked at her then, fully, and she understood that he had already seen the answer in her face, and that he had come anyway, because hope is stupid and loyal and does not read faces well.

"My father," Lucien said. "Is his name in something?"

*In something.* As though the ledger were a book, a story, a piece of gossip passed hand to hand. As though names were ink and ink were only ink. She thought of what Anselm had said to her two nights ago across the kitchen table, his census ledger closed between them like a wound: *Names are not ink, Odile. A name on that list is a cell in the Fort Célestin and a trial that lasts nine minutes.* And she thought of how she had argued with him anyway, because arguing was easier than agreeing, and agreement meant the world had changed shape overnight and no one had told her.

"I don't know what you mean," said Odile. "'In something.' In a book? We print books. We print everything. If you want something printed—"

"You've gone pale," Lucien said quietly.

"I'm always pale. I work indoors. Look at me — I have the complexion of a mushroom."

It was meant to be light. It landed in the room like a dropped plate. Lucien did not smile. He stepped closer to the composing stone, and his eyes went — she watched them go, helpless to stop them — to the drawer beneath it. Not the right drawer. The ledger was not even in the shop anymore; it lay wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of Anselm's document case, three streets away, and still her eyes flicked to the drawer and still Lucien saw them flick, and she understood with a cold sliding feeling that she had just confessed everything, in the only language that mattered, without saying a word.

"You have something written down," he said. "About my father."

"Lucien—"

"The gendarmes were at the Vasseur Lane cellar on Tuesday. My father thinks I don't know. He thinks I don't notice anything, because I'm the son and sons are furniture." His voice stayed low, but it had a tremor running through it now, a wire pulled taut. "There are men who come to our house on Thursdays. They eat in the cellar and they talk low and my father calls them 'cousins from the country.' I counted seven cousins last month. Seven cousins, and none of them look alike, and all of them leave before dawn." He swallowed. "I'm not furniture, mademoiselle. I know what Thursdays are."

"Do you?" Her heart was going hard against her ribs. "What are they, then? Say it. Say it aloud in a print shop with the window open onto the street."

He glanced at the window. Closed it himself, with a deliberateness that frightened her more than anything he'd said yet, and drew the bolt.

"They're men who believe the city should be governed by the people in it," he said. "And someone has written their names down somewhere, and you know where, because you look like my mother looks when the knock comes after curfew and it's only the neighbor drunk again. That particular look. Like a person waiting for weather."

* * *

She made him tea because her hands needed an occupation and because the ritual of it bought her time she did not know how to spend. He sat on the paper crates in the back room among the smells of size and glue, holding his cup awkwardly, too large for the chair of himself he was trying to occupy. Upstairs her father's tread crossed and recrossed. Outside, a cart went by; a woman called a child home; the ordinary city performed its ordinariness.

"If I tell you nothing," Odile said at last, "you'll go away and start asking questions elsewhere. You'll ask at the chandlery. You'll ask old Madame Ferrand, who would sell her confessor for a bottle of brandy. Is that what you'll do?"

"Yes," Lucien said simply.

"And if I tell you to burn your Thursdays, tell your father the cousins must stop coming, move the cellar, change everything—" She stopped. Because that was the argument she'd had with Anselm, inverted: Anselm had wanted to burn the ledger and save the names by erasing them; she had wanted to carry it; and here was the son of one of those names asking her to do the third thing, the impossible thing, which was to save everyone by making them never have existed at all.

"Would that help?" he asked. "Truly? Or would it just be slower?"

She looked at him. Nineteen years old, flour in his eyebrows, sitting on reams of unsold paper in a dying print shop, and asking questions like a man twice his age who had already buried something.

"No," she admitted. "It would be slower. The list exists. Moving the players doesn't unpublish the play."

"Then show me."

"I can't."

"Because you don't trust me."

"Because trusting you endangers you. Don't you see that? Every person who knows is a person who can be made to talk. I'm not being cruel, Lucien, I'm being—" She searched for the word and found the one she hated. "Careful. I'm being careful. It's the only thing keeping those names alive."

"Whose names?" he asked. "All of them? Or—" and his voice did the cracking thing again, worse this time, "or is my father's name among them? Just tell me that. Not where the thing is, not who wrote it, not any of your careful secrets. Only: yes or no. Does my father's name appear in whatever it is you're hiding from me?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the walls a pipe ticked as the day cooled. Odile held her teacup and felt the heat of it and could not drink.

She thought: if I lie cleanly, he goes away. If I lie cleanly, he goes away and asks Madame Ferrand, and Madame Ferrand asks a gendarme cousin of hers, and by Friday the Marchand household is watched, and by Sunday the cellar on Vasseur Lane is kicked in at four in the morning while the bread proofs. A bad lie kills faster than a truth.

And she thought: I am my mother's daughter, whoever my mother was, and I have discovered in the last week that lying is apparently the family trade.

"No," she said.

Lucien set down his cup. Very carefully, the way a man sets down something when his hands are shaking and he does not wish them to be seen shaking.

"That was the worst lie I have ever heard spoken aloud," he said. "My aunt lies better about the price of eggs."

"Lucien—"

"You said no the way Papa says the oven's fine when the loaf's gone black." He stood up. He wasn't angry — that was the terrible part, he wasn't angry at all, he looked like a man standing in a doorway watching a house burn and understanding that the fire had started long before he smelled smoke. "You looked at the drawer. You couldn't even look at me when you said it. And you've been pale since I walked in, and you bolted the window with your own hand, and you're holding that cup like it's the last solid thing in Vellmarin." He picked up his cap. "His name is in it."

Odile said nothing. What was there left to say that her face hadn't already typeset?

"How many?" he asked.

"I'm not telling you how many."

"So more than one." He laughed — one short, awful syllable of a laugh, with no humor anywhere in it. "More than one. Seven cousins from the country and a schoolmistress and God knows who else, and it's been sitting in this street the whole time, in the print shop, under the noses of—" He stopped. Turned. "Whose handwriting is it? Whose list is it? Someone made it. Lists don't make themselves."

"Lucien, please."

"Was it the census man? The one who comes every autumn with the little book? People say he lingers."

"The census man," Odile said, before she could stop herself, "is the reason any of us have until Saturday instead of tomorrow."

She heard it land. She watched it land. And she understood that she had just spent another secret to buy control of a conversation she had already lost, and that this, apparently, was going to be the shape of her entire life from now on: a woman paying out truth like rope to a man descending a cliff, hoping the knot holds.

"Saturday," Lucien repeated.

"Dawn raids. Possibly sooner. Possibly tonight, if the registry flags faster than we think." She put down the cup at last and stood, and found that she was almost his height, which surprised her; she had been thinking of him as a boy, and boys are smaller. "So you can stand there demanding answers, or you can go home and warn your father, which will accomplish nothing except teaching the gendarmes that the Marchands knew, or you can help me. Those are the choices. I didn't make them. I would give a great deal to unmake them."

"What kind of help?"

"The kind that gets you arrested."

"I said, what kind."

She studied him. The flour at his shoulders. The jaw that was his father's jaw, that same heavy stubborn architecture she had seen a hundred times across the counter when old Marchand collected the parish notices and complained about the price of lead. A name on a list. A face over a name. All week the ledger had been a weight in her arms, a bundle of terror, an abstraction wearing oilcloth — and now it had flour on its sleeves and a cracked voice and it was standing in her back room refusing to leave.

"Saturday before dawn," she said slowly, "something has to cross the river. Something that cannot be found on me or on the man helping me. Papers get searched. Bodies get searched. But a delivery boy carrying bread to the guild kitchens on the far bank—" She stopped herself. Shook her head. "No. No, I'm not doing this. Forget I said it. Go home, Lucien."

"Carrying what?"

"Bread. Actual bread. Forget it."

"You need it carried across the checkpoints and you need a carrier who isn't you and isn't the census man, and you need him respectable, and you're looking at the only baker's son in the Rue des Fondeurs." He put his cap on. It gave him a sudden authority, absurd and total, like a crown. "I've been through the market gate every morning of my life. Old Bressac waves me through before I've even shown the basket. He's waved me through since I was nine."

"Bressac won't be the gate forever. And Saturday the gate will be doubled."

"Then I'll bring double the charm." He was already moving toward the door, and there was something in his walk now that hadn't been there when he entered — a purpose that straightened him, that made the bakery boy into something else, something being forged in front of her at speed. "Tell me one thing first. Truthfully. You owe me one truthful thing."

"If I can."

"My father. In your — in whatever it is." His hand rested on the bolt. "Does the list say what happens to them? Or only who they are?"

And that question, of all of them, went through her like a burin through soft lead, because she knew the answer, had known it since the night she decoded the columns: the ledger recorded names, trades, meetings, couriers — everything except fates. Her mother had catalogued the living with a compositor's precision and had left the future blank, as blank as an unwritten page, as though she had believed until the very end that the ending hadn't been set yet. That it could still be reset.

"Only who they are," Odile said. "That's all it says. Who they are, and where they meet, and who carries what. Nothing about what happens to them. That part isn't written yet."

Lucien nodded once, slow, absorbing it. Then he drew the bolt and paused with his hand on the door.

"Thursday supper," he said. "My father expects you — he ordered the good loaves for the Ravensworths, he mentioned it this morning. Come at seven. Sit at our table. Look him in the eye and decide whether you can do what needs doing." A ghost of his father's jaw set itself. "And mademoiselle — if you lie to him, lie better than you lied to me. He raised me. He'll see straight through the both of us."

The bell rang behind him. Through the window she watched him go down the Rue des Fondeurs, cap pulled low, shoulders squared, a boy carrying nothing visible at all — and she stood among her cases of type with her heart hammering out the only sentence it knew:

*It has a face now. God help me, it has a face, and it eats supper at seven.*
