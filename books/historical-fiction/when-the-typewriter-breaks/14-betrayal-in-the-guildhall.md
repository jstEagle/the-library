## 14. Betrayal in the Guildhall

The guildhall smelled of hot metal and tallow, and Odile had loved it since she was a child.

Her father had brought her once, years ago, when the guild still admitted printers' families to its open days. She remembered standing beneath the vaulted ceiling with its painted motto — *THE WORD OUTLIVES THE HAND* — and watching men in leather aprons pull sheets from a press older than the city walls. She had thought then that a guildhall was the safest building in Vellmarin, safer than a church, because churches kept God and guildhalls kept the truth.

Now she stood in its shadow across the square, hood up, the ledger's weight distributed through the false lining of Anselm's document case, and tried to remember that buildings did not keep anything. People did. And people could be bought.

"Third window on the left," Lucien murmured beside her. "That's where Master Corvel keeps his private office. I delivered flour there every Thursday for two years. He always paid in coin counted twice."

"You're certain he received my mother's message?"

"As certain as a man can be who watched his own father's name get copied out of a book." Lucien shifted the empty flour sack on his shoulder — their cover, one last delivery to the guild kitchens. "He read it three times. Then he burned it in the stove and nodded at me like nothing happened. That's how careful men say yes."

Odile looked up at the third window. Lamplight moved behind the glass — Corvel's silhouette, pacing. Waiting for them, she told herself. Waiting to hide them.

Anselm stood at her other shoulder, and she felt him trembling, though his voice was level when he spoke. "Once we're inside, we don't leave until the ledger is in the hands of at least three masters of the guild. Not Corvel alone. Three. That was the condition."

"Corvel *is* the condition," Lucien said. "He's Second Chair of the Pressmen. If he can't protect it, no one in this city can."

"That's what worries me." Anselm adjusted the strap of the case. "Men who are indispensable are also men who know exactly what they're worth."

They crossed the square in the gray hour after vespers, when the lamplighters were still working their way down the far streets and the cobbles held the day's last warmth. Odile kept her eyes on Corvel's window and her hand on the case, and she recited to herself the route they would take afterward — the safe route, the one Anselm had walked twice already: out through the guild's cart-yard, along the tanners' wall, over the footbridge at Millrace Lane where the watch never posted a man because the smell drove them off. Two hundred paces of stink and safety.

She was so busy reciting it that she almost missed the wrongness.

It was small. A cart in the cart-yard that shouldn't have been there — a government courier's cart, blue-liveried, parked in the shadow of the guild's loading arch as if it belonged. And near the kitchen door, a man in a flour-dusted apron who stood too straight for a baker's boy, whose eyes swept the square the way soldiers' eyes sweep a street.

Odile stopped walking.

"What is it?" Lucien whispered.

"The cart." She turned her face away, toward a shuttered chandler's shop, and studied its peeling door as though it fascinated her. "Guild carts are green. That one's blue."

Lucien glanced sideways, quick and practiced. When he looked back, all the color had gone out of his face. "Courier corps," he breathed. "Those are arrest warrants. My father got taken in a cart like that."

"Maybe it's unrelated," Anselm said, but his hand had gone white on the case strap. "Warrants go out every week. Maybe—"

"Through the kitchen door," Odile said. "If Corvel has already given them something, we need to know before we walk into that room. If he hasn't—" She swallowed. "If he hasn't, then we're early, and we can warn him."

"And if he has?" Lucien asked.

She didn't answer. She was already moving, keeping to the wall, counting doors.

* * *

The guild kitchen was a cavern of copper and steam, empty now between meals except for a scullery girl scrubbing pots with her back to them. Odile slipped through it the way she'd slipped through her father's shop a thousand times — reading the room, finding the gaps. A passage behind the ovens led to a back stair; the back stair rose past a landing where the guild's apprentices slept; and from the landing, a narrow corridor ran to the private offices, each door marked with a brass plate.

CORVEL — SECOND CHAIR. The plate was polished bright. Someone took pride in this office. Someone believed in it.

From inside came voices.

"—no reason to search the office itself, Captain. The guild's records are open to you whenever you wish. We are loyal tradesmen, not conspirators." Corvel's voice, warm and rolling, the voice of a man who had talked customs officers into good humor for thirty years.

And then another voice, flat and official: "We have information that documents of interest were conveyed to this building today. Conveyed by a baker's delivery boy, among others. You will open this office, Master Corvel, or we will open it for you."

Odile's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with fear for herself. *A baker's delivery boy.* They knew about Lucien. Which meant they knew about the message. Which meant—

"Documents of interest," Corvel repeated, and now she heard it, the thing she had missed all these weeks because she had wanted so badly to trust him: the pause before the phrase, the little hitch of a man choosing words someone else had taught him. "Captain, I am sixty-one years old. I have set type for four governors. Whatever 'information' reached you, I promise you it is mistaken."

"It reached us with remarkable precision," the captain said. "Down to the hour. Down to the number of persons expected. You understand, Master Corvel, that precision of that quality does not grow on trees. It must be purchased. And purchased things want paying for."

Silence. In the silence Odile heard the thing that broke her heart before any confession did: Corvel breathing. Slowly. Carefully. The breath of a man composing a sentence he had rehearsed.

"I don't know what you mean," Corvel said.

"We pay informants well," the captain said. "But we do not pay them twice for the same information. So I will ask once more, plainly: are the documents here, or are they not?"

Lucien's hand closed on Odile's arm — not to hold her back but because his legs were failing him. Through the gap between the door and its frame, where the wood had warped away from the jamb, she could see a slice of the office: Corvel behind his desk, upright, dignified, his hands folded like a man at prayer. The captain standing opposite, hat under his arm, patient as winter.

And on the desk, between them, a purse. Heavy. Leather. The kind of purse that changed lives.

"No," Corvel said.

The captain waited.

"No," Corvel said again, louder, and for one wild moment Odile let herself believe — and then he said, "Not here. Not in my office, with my people in the building. The documents will be found in the cellar archive, third shelf, behind the bound minutes of 1831. Wrapped in oilcloth. I put them there an hour ago. Now pay me, and be gone before the night shift comes down."

The world tilted. Odile gripped the doorframe.

*Behind the bound minutes of 1831.* She knew those volumes. Every printer's child knew them — the fat gray annuals nobody opened. Her mother's ledger, wrapped in oilcloth, sitting twenty feet below her feet, and above it a man selling it for the price of a house.

"Your name goes in the report as a confidential source," the captain said, and there was something almost gentle in it, which made it worse. "You'll be protected. The guild need never know."

"The guild," Corvel said, "will know exactly what I tell it: that soldiers searched our archives and found nothing. And in a month, when this business has blown over, I will take my family to the coast." A pause. "My daughter is ill. The reward is for her. You may judge me however you like, Captain, but judge me knowing that."

"I don't judge informants," the captain said. "I use them. Where is the delivery boy?"

"Gone home hours ago. I sent him off myself."

Odile felt Lucien flinch against her. *Gone home.* Corvel was lying to protect him — or lying to keep the captain from sweeping up loose ends before the payment cleared. There was no way to know which, and no time to learn.

Boots crossed the office floor. The captain was leaving — going down, going to the cellar, going to the shelf behind the minutes of 1831.

Odile pulled back from the door. Her mind, trained by ten thousand hours of type-sorting, did what it always did under pressure: it stopped feeling and started arranging. *Cellar. Back stair. Oilcloth. Twenty minutes, perhaps less, before they find it — unless it isn't there, unless Corvel lied about the hiding place too, unless this whole conversation is theater staged for ears in the corridor.*

No. The tremor in Corvel's voice had been real. Greed is real. Fear for a sick daughter is real. Men lie beautifully about everything except the things they've already sold.

"Stairwell," she mouthed. "Now."

* * *

They went down the back stair ahead of the soldiers, taking the steps in silence, Odile leading by memory of a building she had visited once as a child and Lucien leading by knowledge of it as a deliverer of flour. The cellar archive was exactly as guild cellars always were: cold, dry, smelling of paper dust and stone. Rows of shelving stretched into the dark, and somewhere ahead a lamp burned low.

"There." Lucien pointed. Third row. Third shelf. Gray spines, *MINUTES OF THE GUILD, MDCCCXXXI*, and behind them, just visible, the corner of oilcloth.

Anselm reached it first. His hands shook so badly he fumbled the wrapping, and Odile took it from him — took the whole bundle, ledger and oilcloth both, and pressed it against her chest beneath her coat. It was warm somehow, or she imagined it was, the way you imagine a dead person's hand might still hold heat.

"Out the coal chute," Lucien whispered. "There's a chute at the far end — deliveries come down it in winter. It opens into the cart-yard behind the woodpile."

"The yard with the blue cart," Anselm said.

"The yard with the blue cart," Odile agreed, "which is why they won't look at us. Nobody hides next to the wolf."

Above them, a door banged. Voices descended — the captain and two men, boots ringing on stone, unhurried, certain. Certain men walk slowly. Odile had learned that much in three days of being hunted: certainty is loud, and loud things are easy to track.

They flattened themselves behind the last row of shelves as the light swung toward them. Odile pressed her palm over the ledger's shape against her ribs and thought, absurdly, of her mother's hands guiding hers over the type cases — *feel for the notch, little bird, every letter has one, even in the dark.*

Even in the dark. Even now.

The lamp passed within six feet of them. The captain stopped at the third shelf. There was a silence long enough for Odile to count her own heartbeats — eleven, twelve, thirteen — and then the sound of books sliding aside, and the captain's voice, flat with satisfaction:

"Well, Master Corvel. You may send for your daughter's physician in the morning."

* * *

The coal chute was narrower than it looked and ended in a drop of eight feet onto frozen ground. Lucien went first and caught Odile; Anselm came last, landing hard enough to crack the ice in a puddle, and for a moment all three of them crouched in the black shadow of the woodpile, listening to the muffled triumph above them.

Then they ran.

Not the safe route — the tanners' wall was burned now, Corvel had known it, had sold it along with everything else, and Odile understood with a clarity that felt like a wound opening that every road they had planned, every bridge and gate and alley Anselm had memorized, was already written down in a report on some captain's desk. Corvel had known the route. Corvel had been entrusted with the route. That was what trust meant, she realized — it meant handing someone the map of your own destruction and believing they wouldn't copy it.

They cut north instead, through the rag-and-bone yards, Lucien in front because he had grown up in these streets the way rats grow up in walls. Behind them the guildhall blazed with sudden lantern-light as the search expanded, and Odile heard the thing she had been dreading since the kitchen: a bell. Not the guild's own bell — the watch bell, from the tower by the customs house, ringing the pattern she had only ever heard in drills.

General alarm. Curfew moved forward. Every gate closing early.

They stopped in the lee of a bone-boiler's shed, all three of them gasping, and Odile finally let herself feel it — not the fear, that could wait, but the betrayal, arriving all at once like water through a broken dam. Corvel's warm voice. Corvel's polished doorplate. Corvel's folded hands. Thirty years of setting type for governors, and he had sold them for a purse and a story about a sick daughter — and the worst of it, the part that made her press her fist against her mouth, was that the daughter might really be ill. That he might be a good father and a traitor in the same body, and that both things could be true, the way her mother had been a compositor and a conspirator, the way Anselm was a servant of the state and a savior of names.

"People aren't one thing," she said aloud, without meaning to.

Lucien stared at her. "What?"

"Nothing." She straightened. The ledger pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "Listen to me. The safe route is dead. The guild is dead. Anyone else who knew about the ledger — anyone my mother trusted, anyone the network touched — we have to assume they're either arrested, terrified, or bought. All three, if we're lucky enough to be surprised."

"So where do we go?" Anselm asked. His face in the dark was gray. "Every plan we had went through the guild. That was the whole architecture. Deliver it to the Pressmen and let the guild's presses do the rest."

"Then we build new architecture," Odile said. "Tonight. Before the curfew bells finish ringing."

"With what? We have no contact, no safe house, no papers that will survive a real inspection—"

"We have Lucien." She turned to him. "You grew up in the tanneries. You told me once — the night we met properly — that there are ways under the river that the watch doesn't know because the watch won't walk through the smell. I thought you were boasting."

Lucien's jaw worked. "I wasn't boasting. But Odile — those tunnels haven't been used since the smuggling trials. My uncle went down them and came out the other side of a prison sentence. Half of them are flooded. Half of the other half are collapsed. And the ones that are neither—" He hesitated.

"Say it."

"The ones that are neither belong to people. Smugglers' people. Tannery people. People who charge, and people who talk, and people who'd sell us to the watch for less than Corvel got." He looked at the ledger's outline under her coat. "Taking that thing underground is a different kind of gamble than anything we've done."

Above them, the watch bell rang on, and somewhere in the distance a new sound joined it: the rhythmic tramp of a patrol column, moving toward the river district. Toward the bridges. Toward every way across that wasn't underground.

Odile thought of her mother's handwriting in the ledger, each name set down like type in a forme, locked tight, waiting for the press. Names didn't get to choose their hour. They just had to survive until the printing.

"Then we gamble differently," she said. "Take us to your uncle's people. And Lucien — whatever they ask, whatever it costs—"

"I know." He pulled his collar up and stepped out into the dark. "Follow me, and don't speak past the third arch. After that, the walls have landlords."

Behind them, the guildhall's windows went dark one by one, and Odile did not look back.
