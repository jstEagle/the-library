## 9. The Bridge

The ice spoke before dawn.

I heard it from the doorway of Maren's loft above the tannery — a long groaning from the river, deep as a ship's timbers taking water, and beneath it a high thin singing, the sound water makes when it is deciding to become stone. By tonight the Vell would be sealed. By tonight the bridge would be a road to nowhere, and the magistrate on the far side might as well be on the moon.

The page was against my chest. I had sewn it there myself, three nights ago, inside the lining of my coat, in oilcloth Maren had given me — *wax the edges, boy, water is the honest man's enemy too* — and beneath the oilcloth it was only a sheet of paper, misprinted and worthless to anyone but a magistrate who could read a signature date and understand what it meant. My father's life, folded twice, resting over my heart like something I had stolen from a church.

Maren stood at the window with her back to me, watching the bridge through a gap in the shutters. She had been watching it for an hour without speaking, and I had learned in these weeks not to fill her silences. She was a small woman, rope-muscled, with a face like a knot in old wood, and she moved the way water moves — always toward the gap, always without argument.

"They've doubled the guard," she said at last. "Four at the gatehouse. Two on the span. And the captain's there himself — I saw his gray coat." She turned. "That's the one from the customs house. Voss."

I knew the name. I had slept eight feet from the man two nights ago while the blizzard screamed, had listened to him talk about my father over bad wine — *the printer, the clever one, he'll tell me where the press is or I'll take his hands one knuckle at a time* — and I had kept my face still and my breath even and hated him in a way that frightened me, because it was so easy. Hat with a human face. That was the lesson of the customs house, the one I hadn't wanted.

"He'll be looking for a boy," I said. "He described me. He described my father through me."

"He'll be looking for a boy carrying paper." Maren crossed the room and took my shoulders. Her grip was like a vice that had learned gentleness late in life. "So we don't carry paper. We carry type."

She had spent the night breaking apart a sort-case — one of ours, from the shop, salvaged from the first fire — and in the bottom compartment, beneath the broken ligatures and the cracked quads, she had built a false floor. The page would lie flat under a layer of lead. A boy carrying type across the bridge was a boy doing his father's errand. A boy carrying type was nobody.

"Say it back," she said.

"The fish market is closed. The customs house burned its manifests. I'm taking the case to Herr Brandl in Ostmark, who owes my father money, because my father would rather be paid in coin than in gratitude." I said it flat, bored, the way she'd drilled me. "I don't know anything about the guild. I don't know anything about the governor. I'm a cutter's son and I want my supper."

"Good." She let go of me and looked at my coat, my boots, my face. Something moved behind her eyes — I would understand it later, but not then — something that looked like an accountant finding the column doesn't balance. "Your father has your chin," she said. "Don't stand in the light."

* * *

We went down separately, she first by ten minutes, me after, through the tannery yard where the hides hung like flayed ghosts and the smell of lime covered everything, even fear. The streets were empty in the way streets are empty before snow — swept clean by anticipation, every shutter closed, every window holding one pair of eyes.

The bridge rose out of the fog at the end of Bridge Street like the spine of some enormous drowned animal. I had crossed it a thousand times in my life and never once looked at it. Now it was the only thing in the world. Five arches, stone, older than the city's walls, and at the near end the gatehouse with its new timber barricade and its four soldiers in gray, their breath standing around their faces like small ghosts of their own.

Maren was already at the barricade, arguing. I heard her voice — the smuggler's voice, aggrieved and reasonable at once — complaining about tolls, about the weight of her cart, about her rheumatism, and the soldiers were half-annoyed, half-amused, the way men are with an old woman they've decided is harmless. She was buying their attention the way you buy bread: openly, in small pieces, so no one notices the sum.

I came up behind a fishmonger's boy hauling a barrow, close enough to seem attached to him, and when the soldiers waved him through I was simply there, a boy with a sort-case on his shoulder, next in line.

"Name."

"Sebastian Ishikawa. My father's the cutter, off Bridge Street — the shop with the blue sign."

The soldier wrote it down. The soldier writing it down was the worst moment, because names were what they were collecting, names were the harvest, and I was handing them mine like a tithe. Then: "Open it."

I set the case on the barricade's flat stone. My hands did not shake. I had spent my whole life learning stillness — cutting type, binding pamphlets, passing — and all of it, every invisible year, had been rehearsal for these ten seconds. I lifted the lid. The type lay in its compartments, broken and honest. I began lifting trays, one by one, the way a boy would who had nothing to hide and knew his father's property.

The third tray was the false floor.

I felt it before I saw it — the slight give, the wrongness of weight — and I heard boots on stone, and then a voice I knew from a blizzard and bad wine, from a night I would carry longer than the scar this day would leave me.

"The Ishikawa boy."

Captain Voss stood in the gatehouse light with the fog behind him, and up close his gray coat was not a uniform so much as a skin. He looked at me the way a man looks at a ledger he has been trying to balance for months. "Turn out your pockets. And lift that bottom tray — all the way."

My hands were on the tray. Under my palms, through wood and lead and oilcloth, the page. My father's name on the arrest list. The governor's signature, dated nine days after the crime it was meant to have answered. All of it under my fingers, one motion from the light.

I lifted.

* * *

Later — in the years after, when I told this story, and I told it often, because it was the story that made me — I would try to explain the arithmetic of that moment. How much time passed between the tray beginning to rise and what happened at the barricade. A breath. Half a breath. The tray came up an inch, and the false floor shifted, and the edge of the oilcloth showed at the seam like a white thread in dark cloth, and Voss leaned in —

— and Maren's cart went over.

Not tipped. *Went over*, all at once, the axle snapping with a crack like a musket, and the cart spilled across the barricade road: dried fish, rope, a hundredweight of tannery salt, and — this was the genius of it, the part I only understood much later — a bundle of printed sheets, seditious pamphlets, guild pamphlets, scattered in the wind and slapping against the soldiers' boots like living things.

"Smuggler!" someone shouted, and the world broke into motion. Voss straightened. Two soldiers went for Maren; she fought them the way I imagine she had fought everything her whole life, small and ferocious and foul-mouthed, and I heard her voice over the noise, pitched high, pitched *for me*: "Well, don't just stand there, boy! Take your father's case and go, before they tax you for the delay!"

And I — this is the part that costs me still, the part that will cost me forever — I picked up the case and I walked.

I did not run. Running was the one thing they would stop you for. I walked through the gatehouse arch with the tray still half-raised in my mind's eye, with the white thread of oilcloth showing at the seam, and no one looked at me, because every eye in Vellmara was on the old woman in the road, on the pamphlets fluttering like black birds, on the gray coat bending over her.

At the center of the bridge I stopped. I couldn't help it. I turned, and I looked back, and what I saw I will see when I am old and blind: Maren on her knees in the spilled salt, her arms wrenched behind her, her face lifted — not to the soldiers, not to the crowd gathering on Bridge Street — but to the bridge, to the middle of the span, to me. And she nodded. Once. The way you nod to a customer whose account is settled.

Then a soldier's hand closed on her collar and the fog took them both, and I turned and walked on, because that was the price, and the price was exactly what it should be, and she had known it when she named it, weeks ago, in a tannery loft, before either of us knew what we were trading.

* * *

The far side of the bridge was a country I had never lived in, though it was half a mile from my bed. The snow was already starting — fat, slow, deliberate flakes, the first of the season, the ones that mean *no turning back*. Behind me I heard the gatehouse bell ring the closing: three strokes, the last crossing of the year, and then the great timber doors swinging, and then the sound of the Vell freezing over a city I had chosen, finally, to belong to.

I stood at the bridge's far foot with the case on my shoulder and let the snow land on my face. Somewhere behind me my father was in a cell. Somewhere behind me an old smuggler was in a worse one. Somewhere behind me the smoke of a burned print shop was still rising, and every soul in Vellmara now knew whose child I was.

Ahead, up the hill, a light burned in a window. The magistrate's house. The magistrate who could read a signature date.

I shifted the case higher on my shoulder and started walking.

I did not yet know that by morning the governor's men would be in Ostmark too. I did not yet know that the page in my coat was about to be worth more than my life — or cost it.

But the snow was falling, and the light was burning, and for the first time in my life, I was not hiding from anyone.

I climbed.
