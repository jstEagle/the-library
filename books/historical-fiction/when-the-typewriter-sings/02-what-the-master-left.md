## 2. What the Master Left

The inventory of a dead man's life, Rosalind discovered, was mostly paper.

Not the grand documents one might expect from a master cartographer — not yet, anyway. It was the small things that accumulated: receipts for India ink in quantities that suggested Albrecht had been drinking it; a tailor's bill unpaid since October; forty-one pencils, sharpened to varying degrees of surrender, arranged in a clay pot like a bouquet of grey flowers; and a note in his cramped hand reading *remind R. about the meridian on Plate XIV* — which was useless now, and which Rosalind had therefore pinned above the workbench anyway, where it could remind them of nothing but him.

Three days after the funeral, the workshop still smelled of him. Linseed oil and pipe smoke and the particular chalky scent of the whiting he dusted across finished plates before wrapping them. Rosalind had opened every window at dawn to let the river air through, then closed them again by mid-morning because the cold came in with the light and the inks began to sulk. February in a river town was not a season for sentiment. February was a season for keeping your fingers supple enough to hold a pen.

"You'll want to do this properly," Tomas had said, when he'd helped carry the last of the master's crates up from the wharf office. "List everything. The guild will want an accounting."

"The guild," Rosalind had said, "will want an accounting of what they can sell."

Tomas had laughed, then stopped laughing when he saw Rosalind's face, and had said something kind instead, which was Tomas's way. He was a wharf clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a habit of standing too close to stoves, and he was the only person in town who knew that the coastlines everyone admired in Albrecht's atlas were half-drawn by an anonymous hand. He'd sworn secrecy years ago over two cups of terrible coffee, and had never once broken it, though he did enjoy saying things like *the guild will want an accounting* purely to watch Rosalind bristle.

So Rosalind made the accounting.

It took the better part of three days, working through the master's effects drawer by drawer, shelf by shelf, while the ice on the river groaned outside like an old man turning over in bed. The atlas itself lived in the great flat cabinet under the north window, twenty-two completed plates wrapped in tissue and resting in their slots, each one a small country of patient labor. Rosalind unwrapped each in turn, checked it against the ledger, rewrapped it, and set it back, handling them the way one handles eggs or grief — carefully, and without letting oneself think too much.

Plate I: the river's mouth, where it met the sea in a fan of brown water and argument. Done. Plate IV: the narrows at Kessel Bend, with its treacherous gravel shoal that shifted every spring flood and which Albrecht had surveyed himself, wading in hip boots, swearing cheerfully at the current. Done. Plate XI: the town itself, rendered so lovingly that you could count the chimneys, done. On down the list, plate after plate, until Rosalind reached the end of the cabinet and found the two empty slots.

Twenty-two done. Twenty-four promised.

The contract with the shipping guild called for twenty-four plates. The final two — the upper reaches, past the mill race, where the river narrowed into the hills and the charts mattered most of all to the barges that would run them come thaw — existed only as field notes, pencil surveys, and a half-finished fair copy of Plate XXIII that sat on the drafting table where the master had left it, its western shore inked, its eastern shore waiting.

Rosalind stood looking at those two empty slots for a long time.

Two plates. It didn't sound like much. Two plates was perhaps six weeks of work, if the weather held and the light held and Rosalind's hand held, and there was no reason any of those should fail. But the empty slots had a way of looking at a person. They were the shape of everything unfinished, and the master had left a great deal of himself in exactly that condition.

"Well," Rosalind said aloud, to the cabinet, to the room, to nobody. "Well."

This was another habit of the anonymous life: talking to rooms. When no name is attached to your work, you develop a certain freedom with your voice. Rosalind had once delivered an entire scathing lecture on the inferiority of copperplate engraving to an empty workshop, and had enjoyed it immensely, and had told no one, and had been, in that moment, the most honest critic in the province.

* * *

It was on the third day, moving down the row of drawers beneath the master's desk, that Rosalind came to the locked one.

Everyone in the workshop had known about that drawer the way everyone knows about a locked door in their own house — as a fact of architecture rather than curiosity. It was the bottom-left drawer, deeper than the others, fitted with a small brass lock gone dull with handling. In eleven years, Albrecht had never opened it in Rosalind's presence. Once, early on, young and foolish, Rosalind had asked what it held, and the master had looked up from his loupe with an expression that was not anger but something adjacent to it — a wariness, almost, a man checking the horizon — and had said:

"Paperwork."

And that had been the end of that, because Albrecht's silences were load-bearing walls; lean on them and the whole conversation collapsed.

Now Rosalind knelt in front of the desk with the inventory ledger open on one knee and tried the drawer out of pure thoroughness. Locked, naturally. Rosalind jiggled it once, felt the mechanism shift with a small brass click that meant nothing, and sat back on their heels.

Here was the thing about inventories: they demanded completeness. A ledger with a gap in it was worse than no ledger at all; it announced its own dishonesty. And here was the other thing, the thing Rosalind admitted only to the knees of their trousers and the dusty floor: eleven years of trust deserved better than a hairpin and a prayer, but eleven years of trust also entitled a person to know what they were inheriting.

Rosalind did not pick the lock. That is worth stating plainly, because later, when the guild asked, certain parties would suggest otherwise. What Rosalind did was go to the pegboard by the door where the master hung his keys — the studio keys, the flat-press key, the key to the poison cabinet where the corrosives lived — and find, hanging behind them all on its own bent nail, a small iron key that matched no lock Rosalind had ever seen used.

It fit the bottom-left drawer the way a sentence fits its meaning.

Inside: papers. Not many. A bundle tied in string, a leather folio, and at the top, face-down, a letter sealed with wax — good wax, the expensive red kind the master kept for correspondence he considered worthy of ceremony. The seal had been pressed with a signet Rosalind didn't recognize: not Albrecht's monogram, but a device of some kind, geometric, a pattern of interlocking lines that might have been decorative and might have been anything at all.

The letter was addressed in a clerk's careful hand. Rosalind turned it over and read the direction, and felt the floor of the ordinary world tilt very slightly, the way a barge tilts when a crate shifts somewhere below the waterline.

*To the Marchetti family.*

Their own name. Their father's name, their mother's name, written in a stranger's hand and sealed under a stranger's signet, lying in a dead man's locked drawer.

Rosalind sat back against the desk leg and looked at it for a long moment. Outside, the ice groaned. Somewhere down the street, the baker's boy was arguing with a horse, unhappily and at length, in the eternal negotiation between boys and horses.

There were perhaps thirty defensible responses available. Read it. Don't read it. Take it home. Leave it. Summon the guild. Summon a priest. Summon Tomas, whose counsel was free and usually sound and always delivered while standing too close to a stove.

What Rosalind actually did was put the letter back exactly as it lay, retie the string on the bundle without examining it, close the drawer, turn the key, and hang the little iron key behind the others on its bent nail.

Then Rosalind stood in the middle of the workshop, hands folded, and thought: *I will decide this tomorrow, when my hands are steady.*

It was, in retrospect, the single most cartographic decision of Rosalind's life. When the ground shifts, mark your position first. Do not move until you have fixed your bearings. The survey can wait until morning; the mistake is made by the one who walks on in the dark.

* * *

Herr Voss arrived the following afternoon, which was either remarkable timing or none at all, depending on how much one believed in coincidence, and Rosalind believed in coincidence approximately as much as one believes in a tide table — useful, but never entirely to be trusted.

He came up the stairs without knocking, having already established with the downstairs tenants that he was expected, which he was not. He was a compact man in a good coat, grey-eyed, with the sort of pleasant, unlined face that seems designed to be forgotten. He carried his hat in one hand and a walking stick he plainly did not need, and he looked around the workshop with the unhurried attention of a man pricing a house.

"Signor — ah." He paused delicately on the blank space where a surname should have gone, and smiled as though the pause were a courtesy. "Forgive me. The guild's correspondence refers to you simply as 'the assistant.' How refreshing, in this age of self-advertisement."

"Rosalind," Rosalind said. "I keep the workshop. How may I help you?"

"Herr Voss." He extended a hand that was dry and cool and brief. "I represent certain commercial interests — shipping concerns, principally, out of the south. We were patrons of your late master. I confess I arrived hoping to pay respects, and find instead—" he gestured gently at the inventory ledger, the open cabinets, the shrouded press, "—a household in mourning. My condolences. Albrecht was a considerable talent."

"He was."

"And the atlas?" Voss drifted along the north window, hands clasped behind his back, reading the spines of reference volumes without appearing to read them. "The great river survey. I understand it nears completion. A monument, truly. The guild must be eager."

"The guild is patient."

Voss laughed — a short, genuine-sounding laugh, which was somehow worse than a false one. "Patient. Yes. The guild is famously patient, like a glacier." He turned, and his eyes went over the room again, and this time Rosalind watched them work: the drafting table, the plate cabinet, the locked desk, the pegboard of keys. Not admiring. Measuring. "You know, I have seen some of the proof impressions. Remarkable linework. One hears the assistant's hand in it — forgive me, one hears *a* hand in it, a second hand, very fine. Albrecht spoke of you."

The words landed somewhere below the ribs. *Albrecht spoke of you.* Eleven years of anonymity, and the first stranger to walk through the door knew of the second hand at once.

"He was generous," Rosalind said.

"Mm." Voss lifted the corner of the tissue on Plate XVII — the confluence, the loveliest plate in the set — looked at it for perhaps four seconds, and laid it back with the care of a man returning someone else's child to its cradle. "May I ask a practical question? Purely practical. If the atlas were to be purchased — completed or uncompleted, terms negotiable — who would hold the right to sell it? The guild holds the commission, certainly, but the plates themselves, the physical work... one assumes these remain with the estate. And the estate's executor being..."

He let the sentence trail off into the room, into the silence where a name should have been, and smiled his forgettable smile, and waited.

Rosalind's hands, folded neatly on the inventory ledger, were perfectly steady. This required a certain amount of lying, since inside them the pulse was going like a telegraph key.

"The matter is under review," Rosalind said. "By the guild. And by the family of the deceased, such as it is."

"Of course. Of course." Voss replaced his hat, which seemed to be a signal that the visit had concluded according to some schedule only he possessed. At the top of the stairs he paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back with an expression of mild, reasonable regret.

"For what it is worth," he said, "my principals admire the work enormously. Enough to be generous. Enough, even, to be *patient*. But patience, Signor Rosalind, is like the river ice. It looks solid for months. And then one morning—" he shrugged pleasantly, "—it isn't."

His footsteps went down the stairs, unhurried, measured, counting the treads.

Rosalind stood in the quiet workshop a long while, listening to the ice groan on the river, and thinking about a sealed letter in a locked drawer, addressed in a stranger's hand to a family that had never done anything in its whole careful life worth sealing in red wax.

Then Rosalind took down the little iron key from its bent nail, and unlocked the drawer, and drew out the letter, and broke the seal.
