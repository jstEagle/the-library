## 10. The Missing Plates

The trouble with a habit of counting things is that you cannot stop counting them.

I had counted the atlas's plates so many times over the winter that the number had worn a groove in me, the way the river wears a groove in its banks: forty-one copper plates, each wrapped in oiled cloth, each slotted into the cedar cabinet Albrecht had built with his own hands and guarded with his own particular brand of suspicion. Forty-one. I could have said it in my sleep. Some mornings I think I did.

So when I unwrapped plate thirty-eight on a gray Tuesday in late February and found beneath it not plate thirty-nine but an empty groove lined with nothing but dust and my own rising dread, I knew — before I checked, before I counted properly, before I made myself do the thing twice as Master Albrecht always insisted — I knew.

Thirty-nine was gone. And so was forty.

* * *

I stood there for a long moment with the oiled cloth in my hands, listening to the workshop breathe around me. The stove ticked. The wind worried at the shutters. Somewhere out on the river, the ice groaned its long slow note, the one Tomas said sounded like a whale with opinions. Inside the cabinet, two absences sat where two plates should have been, and absences, I have learned, are louder than presences. A missing thing does not sit quietly. It fills the room.

"Right," I said aloud, to nobody, because saying things aloud to nobody is another habit I cannot break. "Think."

Thinking, in that workshop, meant doing what Albrecht would have done, which meant making tea first. He held that no conclusion reached before tea was worth the paper it wasn't written on. So I filled the kettle, set it on the stove, and while it heated I walked the room the way he'd taught me to walk a chart: slowly, edge to edge, letting the details come to you rather than chasing them.

The cabinet itself showed no violence. No pried lock, no splintered wood, no scratched paint. The lock had been turned by someone who either had the key or had the patience of a saint and the fingers of a pickpocket. The window latch upstairs was fastened. The cellar door was barred from within. Which left the front door, which locked behind me every evening with a click I had performed so many hundreds of times it had become less an action than a reflex, like blinking.

Which meant, and here the kettle began its low whistle as if announcing the finding, that whoever took the plates had done it in daylight. In working hours. While I was here.

My hands were steady pouring the tea. I noted this with a kind of detached pride, the way you might note that a roof is holding in a storm. Inside, of course, everything was sloshing.

* * *

Let me tell you what those two plates were, because their absence was not random, and understanding *why* those two went missing was half of understanding everything.

Plate thirty-nine was the lower river reach — the stretch below the town where the channel braided around the shoals at Miller's Bend. Plate forty was the delta approaches, the last true word on where deep water lay before the river gave itself up to the lake. Between them, they contained the most commercially valuable miles of water in the entire atlas. Every barge captain, every grain merchant, every syndicate man with a ledger full of futures depended on knowing precisely where the channel ran.

And more than that — I had only understood this fully a fortnight ago, hunched over Albrecht's marginalia by lamplight — those two plates carried the heart of his testimony. His pencil notes, worked into the linework in strokes so fine they read as mere texture, documented the discrepancies between the syndicate's published charts and the river as he had surveyed it himself, soundings taken in his own boat, in his own weather, over eleven patient years. Where the syndicate's charts moved the channel north of the shoal — conveniently past land the syndicate had quietly bought up — Albrecht's plates showed the truth, and showed the falsification, in ink that would outlive everyone who tried to argue with it.

Whoever took those plates took the atlas's teeth.

And whoever took them had to know exactly which ones to take. Not the title plate, not the decorative wind-roses, not any of the thirty-seven others. Two specific plates, chosen with the precision of a surgeon or a thief who had spent an afternoon asking very polite questions about which parts of an atlas mattered most.

I drank my tea. It was too strong. Albrecht would have said that was the correct response to bad news.

* * *

By midday I had searched the workshop three times, on the theory that I might be wrong, or mad, or that the plates had simply wandered off together like elderly gentlemen after too much brandy. They had not wandered. Cedar cabinets do not misplace things; people do, and I had not. I catalogued my own movements of the past week in a little notebook until the exercise became absurd — *Tuesday: inked coastline of plate 12; Wednesday: ground pigment; Thursday: wept briefly over letter, continued working* — and concluded what I had known since morning.

Herr Voss had been in this workshop four times since his arrival. Each visit had been a masterpiece of courtesy. He admired my linework — "remarkably assured, remarkably like the late master's" — he asked after my health, my methods, my hours. He had stood beside the cedar cabinet twice, once with his hand resting on its top as though it were an old friend, and had remarked on what fine craftsmanship it was, and had asked, so lightly, whether the master had kept his plates in order.

I had answered him honestly, because I had nothing yet to hide and a lifetime's habit of answering questions plainly. Yes, I'd said. In order. All forty-one.

All forty-one. I had handed him the inventory myself, wrapped in politeness, tied with a bow.

I sat down at my desk and put my face in my hands and allowed myself — briefly, in the privacy of my own fingers — to feel like the largest fool between the mountains and the lake. Then I lifted my head, because Albrecht used to say that self-pity was like ink spilled on a chart: understandable, human, and absolutely fatal to the work if you let it spread.

* * *

Here is the shape of it, as I worked it out that afternoon, pacing the length of the workshop with my arms crossed and my boots wearing a path Albrecht would have objected to:

Voss wanted the atlas silenced. That was the syndicate's whole errand — erase the routes, erase the evidence, let the falsified charts stand unchallenged and the profits flow unimpeded. But he could not simply burn the workshop, not yet; a fire draws questions, and questions draw the guild, and the guild still believed — because I had told them so, and because it was true — that the atlas neared completion. An unfinished atlas could be dismissed. A finished one, delivered to the guild and copied and distributed, could not.

But now the town suspected my family. The letter under wax had seen to that; some councilman had read our name aloud in the guild hall, and suspicion, once spoken, spreads like damp through plaster. Voss had not needed to arrange that — the letter had done its work on its own — but he would have been a fool not to see the opportunity lying there like a tool on a bench.

So: take the two plates that matter most. Let the atlas remain visibly incomplete — two gaps, obvious to any examiner, damning to the workshop's reputation and to mine. And then make the offer. The offer he had already hinted at twice, all patience and silk: sell me the atlas unfinished, Frau Marchetti — or should I say, shall we speak of your family's difficulties? I can make those difficulties disappear. Names can be cleared as easily as they are muddied. For a price.

The price being silence. The price being the truth of the river, drowned in oiled cloth and burned, and my father's name laundered not by innocence but by arrangement.

He was not stealing the atlas. He was building a lever. And he had used my own honesty, my own plain answers, to find where to cut.

I stopped pacing. Outside the window, the light had gone the color of pewter, and down on the river a barge sat frozen in the ice like a fly in amber, waiting with the whole town for the break-up. Everyone in this place measured time by the ice. Voss had booked passage on the first barge out — Tomas had told me that, with the particular frown he reserved for men he didn't trust, which was a short list containing, currently, one name.

The first barge out. Which meant Voss was on a schedule too. Which meant the offer — the bargain, the threat dressed in its Sunday coat — would come soon. Perhaps tomorrow. Perhaps tonight.

* * *

There is a peculiar clarity that arrives when you stop wondering *whether* someone is your enemy and begin instead studying *how*. I had spent weeks half-doubting myself, turning over the letter, turning over my father's shadow, wondering if some Marchetti sin waited in the family past to be discovered. But theft has a signature, and this one was signed clearly enough. An honest examiner would have taken the whole atlas or none of it. A frightened thief would have taken anything. Only a man executing a plan takes exactly two plates — the two whose absence hurts most, the two whose return can be sold at the highest price.

Certain of Voss's guilt, I discovered, is a warmer feeling than I expected. Terrible, but warm. Like standing very close to a stove in a cold room because the alternative is freezing in the middle of the floor.

I unlocked the drawer where the letter lay hidden — inside a folio of blank practice charts, the one hiding place in a room full of hiding places that no one ever checks, because no one believes a mapmaker would file something among blanks — and I read it again. *The Marchetti family, complicit in the alteration of river charts...* Blackmail shaped as revelation. My father had been dead nine years; the dead make convenient conspirators. But the letter named dates, and the dates were wrong, and I had begun, quietly, methodically, to build the case that would prove them wrong — if I lived long enough and the ice held off long enough.

Then I did something new. I took a fresh sheet of paper and wrote, in my best disguised hand — the hand I used for labeling practice charts, deliberately unlike my own — a short record of the day. *Two plates missing. Nos. 39 and 40. No sign of forced entry. Taken during working hours. Suspect: H.V.* I folded it small and slid it into the hollow spine of Albrecht's oldest survey ledger, where I had already begun keeping such notes. If anything happened to me, or to the workshop, or to the atlas, there would be a trail. Dead men spoke through ink in this town; I saw no reason the living shouldn't start.

* * *

Tomas came by at dusk, stamping snow off his boots in the doorway and bringing with him the smell of the wharf — tar, rope, frozen wood, and the faint fishy sweetness of the river under ice. He was carrying a paper-wrapped parcel of fried dough from Marta's stall, which was his way of announcing that he had heard something and wished to discuss it under cover of chewing.

"Marta says Voss asked her about the break-up pool," he said, around a mouthful. "You know. When the barges can move. Whether the ice-master thinks it'll be early."

"The ice-master thinks it'll be late," I said. "He always thinks it'll be late. He's been wrong six years running."

"He's not wrong this year. Ice is thick as church floors." Tomas set the parcel down on my clean bench, then thought better of it and moved it to the dirty one, which is the closest thing to manners he possesses. "Rosa. What's wrong? You've got your counting face on."

"I don't have a counting face."

"You have four faces. Counting, inking, stubborn, and the one where you're about to do something foolish. You're wearing counting, and I can see foolish coming up behind it like weather." He looked past me toward the cedar cabinet, and I watched him notice what I had noticed all day — that I had left both doors of it open, which I never do, which was either carelessness or a message I hadn't known I was sending. "Something's gone."

I told him. All of it — the empty slots, the locked-and-unforced cabinet, the arithmetic of who could have done it and when. Tomas listened the way he listened to everything, entirely, with his whole body, frowning at the floor as though the floor had personally disappointed him.

"So he's going to trade you," Tomas said finally. "Your family's good name for the atlas's silence. And everyone in town will watch you take the deal and call it sensible, because what else would a Marchetti do?"

"That," I said, "is the elegant part. From his side of it."

"And from yours?"

I looked at the cabinet. At the two empty grooves, patient as graves. At the lamplight lying along the bench where I had inked coastlines all winter for a man who couldn't thank me, in a room where my name appeared nowhere except inside my own head.

"From mine," I said, "he's made a mistake. He thinks those two plates are the only copies of the truth. He thinks the atlas is the testimony." I touched the survey ledger on its shelf, with its hollow spine and its growing cargo of small folded papers. "But Albrecht kept field notes, Tomas. Soundings, dates, bearings — eleven years of them. Everything in those plates exists twice. Once in copper, and once in pencil."

Tomas's frown turned itself upside down, which on Tomas was roughly equivalent to another man's grin. "So he stole the wrong half."

"He stole the half that was easiest to steal." I blew out a breath, and with it, some portion of the day's dread. "Now I only have to prove it, redraw two plates from memory and notes before the ice breaks, survive whatever bargain he offers, and clear my family's name in front of a town that's already decided otherwise."

"Foolish face," Tomas confirmed solemnly, and pushed the fried dough toward me. "Eat something. Conspiracies go better fed."

We ate in the lamplight while outside the river groaned its long note, patient and enormous, keeping its own count of the days. And I caught myself almost smiling, there in the cornered dark of it — because cornered, I was learning, is not the same as beaten. Cornered simply means the exits are visible now. And I had just located one that Herr Voss, for all his silk and patience, did not know existed.

What I did not yet know — what I would learn within the week, in the worst possible way — was that Voss had already made his next move, and that it would arrive not as an offer at my door, but as a summons with the guild's seal upon it.
