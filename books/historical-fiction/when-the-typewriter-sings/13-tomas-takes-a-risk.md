## 13. Tomas Takes a Risk

Tomas Beck had been a wharf clerk for eleven years, and in that time he had learned three things with certainty: the river always won, the harbor master's temper was shorter than the winter days, and paperwork, if you squinted at it correctly, could tell you anything you wanted to know.

He had also learned a fourth thing, though he'd have denied it under oath: Rosalind Marchetti was the best friend he'd ever had, and he would do something foolish about it eventually.

The something foolish arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside a barge manifest.

* * *

The *Marta-Lena* had come down from upstream the night before, riding low with pig iron and salt pork, her captain in one of his moods. Tomas sat in the customs shed with a pot of coffee gone bitter as regret and worked through her papers by lamplight. Manifests were his favorite part of the job, though he'd never admit that either. There was a pleasure to it — the tidy columns, the honest arithmetic of a cargo hold, everything accounted for and squared away like well-behaved children.

This manifest did not behave.

It was the third sheet of five, the one covering the stern hold, and someone had pasted a paper over the lower corner of it. Not stamped, not initialed — pasted, with flour glue gone hard as mortar, the kind the chandlery sold in little pots to boys who couldn't afford proper wax. Tomas picked at the corner with his thumbnail. It came up reluctant, taking a shred of the manifest with it, and beneath it lay a rectangle of heavy paper wrapped in oilcloth, tied with twine.

Tomas stared at it for a long moment.

He knew what an atlas plate looked like. Everyone in this town did; it was practically the town crest. Half the shop windows displayed Albrecht engravings of the river bend, and Tomas had once spent a pleasant hour watching Rosalind explain why the hatching on the shoal markers had to go a particular way, and why anyone who drew them differently deserved to be pushed into the water. He hadn't understood a word of it, but he'd liked watching Rosalind talk about it.

He unwrapped the oilcloth just far enough to see the edge of copperplate engraving, fine as spider silk, and the ghost of a river channel drawn where no channel ought to be.

His stomach dropped through the shed floor, through the pilings, into the black water below.

Voss. It had to be Voss. The man had been sniffing around the workshop since before the ice locked in, all polished boots and polite questions, and now here was a plate — one of the stolen ones, everyone said two were missing — hidden in a barge manifest bound for downstream. Bound, Tomas realized with a fresh chill, for wherever Voss intended to be when the river opened.

Tomas rewrapped the plate. He looked out the frost-furred window at the wharf, at the men chipping ice off the barge rails, at the ordinary gray morning going about its business as though the world were still a place where manifests behaved.

Then he put the plate inside his coat, against his chest, where it sat like a slab of cold conscience, and finished the manifest as if nothing were amiss.

* * *

Here is what Tomas knew about risk, learned from a father who'd lost two fingers to a capstan and never once complained about it: there is calculated risk, which is gambling, and there is the other kind, which is knowing exactly what you stand to lose and deciding it matters less than what you stand to protect.

He spent the rest of the day being aggressively normal. He checked cargo. He argued with the harbor master about a miscounted barrel of tar. He ate lunch standing up in the customs shed and tasted none of it. Three times he caught himself touching his coat front to make sure the plate was still there, and each time he turned the gesture into patting his pockets for tobacco, so that by mid-afternoon the other clerks believed he'd developed a nervous habit and offered him advice on willpower.

The danger wasn't dramatic. That was the thing nobody told you. The danger was that Herr Voss seemed to know every clerk on the wharf by name, tipped generously, and asked friendly questions about shipping schedules with the air of a man collecting small, harmless facts the way other men collected stamps. If Voss had paid someone to hide that plate — and someone had; plates didn't paste themselves into manifests — then someone on this wharf had done it, and that someone might mention, over a beer tonight, the odd business of the clerk who patted his pockets all day like a man who'd lost his mind along with his pipe.

At four o'clock, the harbor master sent him to inventory the salt pork. Tomas inventoried the salt pork. At half past, a boy ran a message down from the guild hall, and Tomas read it twice because it concerned the suspension of the Marchetti workshop's license pending inquiry, and the words *pending inquiry* sat in his chest like swallowed stones.

That settled it. Tomorrow the inquiry might come to the wharf asking questions. Tonight, the plate went home.

Or rather — not home. Rosalind's lodgings were above the cooper's shop on Tanner's Lane, and Tomas knew the route there the way he knew the river channels: by heart, and with a healthy respect for the hazards.

* * *

Rosalind opened the door looking like a candle burned at both ends.

"Tell me you've brought food," Rosalind said. "I've been redrawing the upper bend from memory since noon and I believe I've forgotten what eating is."

"I've brought something better than food." Tomas stepped inside, dripping snow. "Though now that you mention it I could eat a table."

The room was small and fierce with industry. Candles stood everywhere — real candles, hoarded like treasure since the guild had cut off the workshop's lamp oil — and every surface bore the pale ghost-lines of unfinished coastlines. In the corner, weighted down with a teapot, lay a half-finished plate that made Tomas's breath catch, because it looked for all the world like the one warming against his ribs.

"What is it?" Rosalind said. "You've got your face on. The one you make before you say something ridiculous."

"This face," Tomas said, "is my serious face. This face has won me arguments with the harbor master."

"Your serious face once won you an argument about whether a duck could outrun a goose. You were wrong, incidentally."

"The duck had a tailwind." Tomas stopped pacing. He took the oilcloth bundle from inside his coat and set it on the table between the candle stubs, and the room went very quiet, the way rooms do when something important has entered them.

Rosalind looked at the bundle. Then at him. "Where did you get that?"

"It was pasted into the *Marta-Lena*'s manifest. Stern hold, third sheet. Flour glue, chandlery grade." He unwrapped it, hands steadier than he felt. "Someone hid one of the stolen plates on a barge heading downstream, Rosa. And I stole it back before it could get wherever it was going."

For a moment Rosalind didn't touch it. Just looked, the way Rosalind looked at anything true — straight on, without flinching. Then careful fingers lifted the plate to the candlelight, and Tomas watched a whole weather system move across that face: recognition, grief, fury, and underneath it all, something that looked almost like relief.

"This is the lower channel plate," Rosalind said quietly. "Look here — see how the channel bends east of the middle shoal? That's the falsified route. Voss wants this version to survive and mine to die." A short, unsteady laugh. "He's holding it hostage. Return his silence for our name."

"Will you trade?"

"No." It came flat and immediate, no deliberation in it at all, and Tomas felt warmth spread through his chest that had nothing to do with candles.

"Good," he said. "Because I've thought about it, and trading with that man is like negotiating terms with the ice. It'll agree to anything right up until it drowns you."

"You shouldn't have taken it." Rosalind set the plate down and turned to face him fully. "Tomas. Do you understand what you've done? If anyone learns that plate passed through your hands—"

"I'm a wharf clerk. Paperwork passes through my hands the way water passes through a net. Nobody'll trace it." He shrugged. "And anyway, I didn't take it for the atlas. I took it for you."

The silence after that had a different texture entirely. Rosalind looked at him for a long moment, and something in the set of those tired shoulders eased — not much, but visibly, like ice shifting in its bed.

"My mother used to say loyalty is inherited," Rosalind said at last. "Family looks after family, blood answers blood, all of it. But she never explained what to call it when someone chooses you instead." A pause. "I don't have a word for it, Tomas. I only know I'd trust you with the last plate in the world."

"Well." Tomas cleared his throat and studied the ceiling with great interest. "Don't tell anyone. I've got a reputation as a selfish man to maintain. The clerks think I hoard the good ink."

"They're right, too. I've seen your desk."

They laughed together, briefly, and the laugh broke something loose in the room. Rosalind poured tea from a kettle kept warm on the stove, and they drank it standing up, and Tomas told the story properly — the manifest, the glue, the day of aggressive normalcy — and Rosalind listened with the attention of someone committing it to memory, the way mapmakers commit coastlines.

"There's something else," Rosalind said finally. "If this plate was traveling downstream, Voss means to leave on the first barge after break-up. He's not planning to stay for whatever the inquiry decides. Which means—" Rosalind stopped.

"Means what?"

"Means he intends the atlas to leave with him. Or its absence to speak for him." Rosalind's hand rested flat on the stolen plate, protective as a hand on a child's head. "The inquiry sits next week. If the council wavers, if Voss produces something against us—" The fingers curled slightly. "I need to move the remaining plates somewhere safe. Somewhere he wouldn't think to look."

Tomas set down his cup. "Say the *Marta-Lena* runs light cargo on the break-up tide. Say a certain clerk with access to the manifests finds space in the forward hold for a crate marked 'survey instruments.' Say nobody checks a crate that thoroughly, because the clerk who stamped it is trusted." He met Rosalind's eyes. "Say the atlas sails ahead of Voss and arrives downstream before he does, in the hands of people who'll keep it safe."

"That's smuggling."

"That's *clerking*," Tomas corrected. "There's a difference. Smuggling gets you transported. Clerking gets you commended, if the paperwork's clean enough." He grinned, then sobered. "Rosa. Listen to me. When the ice breaks, everything moves at once — barges, accusations, that man in his polished boots. Whatever you mean to do, decide it soon. The river doesn't wait for anyone to be ready."

Rosalind looked at the plate glowing in the candlelight, at the false channel drawn so confidently across honest paper.

"No," Rosalind agreed softly. "But it carries whoever's brave enough to ride it."

* * *

Tomas walked home late, snow squeaking under his boots, the river a vast dark presence beyond the wharfs — frozen solid still, silent as held breath, but underneath that silence he could feel it waiting. Eleven years on the water had taught him to hear what a river meant to say before it said it.

He touched his coat pocket out of habit, found it empty, and smiled.

Loyalty chosen, not inherited. His mother would have boxed his ears for risking a clerkship over a name that wasn't even theirs. His father would have understood without a single word.

In the morning, he would forge — no, *prepare* — a clean manifest. Tonight, he let himself imagine spring: open water, the first barge riding free, and somewhere on it a crate full of truth sailing steadily away from the reach of men who bought silence by the pound.

Above the cooper's shop, a candle still burned.

Downstream, the ice groaned in its sleep, and began, very faintly, to sing.
