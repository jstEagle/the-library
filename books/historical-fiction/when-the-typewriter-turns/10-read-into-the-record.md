## 10. Read Into the Record

The clerk's name was Ellery Finch, and he had been reading aloud for six hours.

His voice had gone thin as wire. The tribunal chamber in the capital — a former customs house with tall windows and a ceiling painted with allegories of Commerce and Navigation, both now water-stained beyond saving — held perhaps two hundred people, and every one of them had stopped shuffling papers. Even the defense counsel had given up objecting. There was nothing left to object to. The words were on the record now, all of them, typed in a slightly uneven elite typeface on forms that were never meant to carry anything but numbers.

Finch adjusted his spectacles and read on.

*"Item forty-one. On the night of the fourteenth of March, the patrol boat Vireo foundered off the point. The light was kept burning until dawn though the keeper had been ordered to extinguish it. Nine men of the coastal service were brought ashore by villagers officially recorded as 'statistically negligible.' One of those men, Able Seaman D. Corrigan, is present in this chamber today."*

A man stood up in the third row. He did not say anything. He did not have to. He stood there in his good coat with his cap in his hands, an old man made old too early, and the chamber looked at him, and then at the clerk, and then at the forms stacked three feet high on the reading table.

The presiding magistrate said quietly, "Thank you. Please continue."

* * *

Coral Amberdale had not wanted to come to the capital.

She lived now in a market town three days inland, in rooms above a stationer's shop, where she kept ledgers for the shopkeeper and grew geraniums on a sill that faced away from the sea. She had not seen the ocean in eleven years. She told people she didn't miss it, which was true in the way that most comfortable lies are true: she missed it constantly, but she had learned to set the missing down the way she once set down barometric readings, hour by hour, until the day was accounted for.

She might not have come at all if the summons hadn't included a single sheet of paper — a photostat of one census form, blank except for the printed headings and one line of typed text:

*Item 1. The light at Amberdale Point was manned without interruption from 1901 to 1939. I know because I kept it.*

Her own words. Her own machine. She had read the line eleven times on the train, holding the page in both hands, because some things you measure by what fits in two hands, and that fit exactly.

Now she sat in the gallery between strangers, an old woman in a gray coat, and listened to her life read back to her in a stranger's dry voice.

It was strange, hearing it aloud. She had typed most of those entries herself, in the small hours, while the tower full of displaced families slept around her — the Okafors in the oil room, Marta and her goat in the storeroom, Pip curled wherever Pip decided to curl that night. She had typed fast and imperfectly, and she could hear the imperfections now, the places where the ribbon had been nearly spent and the letters came up pale, where her finger had slipped and struck twice. The clerk read them faithfully, even the slips. That was the thing about testimony, she thought. It keeps your mistakes. It keeps everything.

*"Item sixty-three,"* the clerk read. *"They came at low tide with trucks. They gave us twenty minutes. Old Marta would not leave the goat. They took the goat first."*

In the row behind Coral, someone began to cry. Not loudly. Just the sound of a woman breathing wrong, over and over, and the man beside her putting his hand flat against her back and leaving it there.

Coral knew that voice. She turned. A heavyset woman, gray-haired, wearing a fisherman's knitted scarf decades out of fashion. Tomas Okafor's wife. Nella. Coral had fed her soup out of the same pot for two winters and had resented her for most of the first one.

Nella saw her and went still. Then she mouthed something across the space between rows — no sound, just the shape of it — and Coral understood it without needing to hear: *You wrote us down.*

*I did,* Coral mouthed back. *All of you.*

* * *

Idris Vane attended the tribunal every day and said almost nothing.

He sat near the back with his hat on his knee like a man waiting for a train that had been delayed for years. He had testified in the second week — four hours under examination, his voice level, his answers precise, the same precision that had once served the other side. He had produced what remained of his field notebooks, cross-referenced against shipping manifests, and walked the chamber through the arithmetic of erasure: how many households, how many souls, how many villages struck from the maps before a single truck ever rolled.

"Statistically negligible," the examining counsel had repeated. "Is that a phrase you used?"

"It is a phrase I was given," Vane had said. "I used it for two years. Then I met the people it described, and I stopped."

Today he watched the gallery more than the bench. He watched the survivors recognize their own sentences. He had learned, during those winters at the lighthouse, that this was the whole secret of Mrs. Amberdale's method: she never wrote anything the people themselves couldn't confirm. Every entry had a witness. Every witness had a name. The state had erased the names; she had simply written them back in, on their own forms, in their own words, and mailed them into the machine disguised as its own paperwork.

Four thousand census forms. He had helped carry the goat cart down through the tail of the storm himself. His hands remembered the weight.

On the table below, the clerk turned another page.

* * *

Pip was thirty-one years old and worked as a compositor for a small press that printed, among other things, hymnals and seed catalogs. He had never spoken. The doctors called it many things over the years; none of the names mattered much. Words had simply never agreed to live in his mouth.

But he had learned letters young, sitting in a lamp room above a storm, watching a woman's fingers move across a typewriter like something flying. And when the clerk read the item about the boy who carried oil up ninety-two steps without being asked, Pip felt the sentence go through him like weather.

That was him. That was the winter of the worst storm, the inspectors' boots on the stairs, the whole village hidden in the lamp room among the lenses and brass, everyone silent while the light turned and turned. He had carried the oil because carrying was easier than being afraid. Someone had written that down. Someone had thought it was worth writing down.

After the session, in the corridor outside the chamber, he waited with the crowd until he saw her — the old woman in the gray coat, smaller than memory, her hair gone white as gull feather. He had a card in his pocket. He had printed it himself, set the type by hand, because if he was going to say anything to Coral Amberdale it was going to be in ink.

He pressed it into her hand.

She looked down. The card bore a single line of type, centered, in a face she recognized as a decent old Caslon:

*You taught my hands. They remember.*

Coral read it twice. Then she took the boy — the man, she corrected herself, the grown man with ink stains on his cuffs — by both shoulders, and kissed his forehead, and said, "Ninety-two steps. I counted them every time you climbed them. I put it in the log."

He smiled. It was, several witnesses later agreed, the only time anyone had seen him do it.

* * *

The reading ended on the fourth day, near dusk, with the last items.

*"Item one hundred and nine. The light was extinguished at 1400 hours by order of the naval district. The keeper requested permission to log the exact time. Permission was granted. The officer who granted it has been identified and is expected to testify."*

*"Item one hundred and ten. The tower was demolished in the spring. The stone was used for a breakwater. I am told the breakwater is good. I am told ships are safer now. I record this without comment, as is proper in a document of record."*

A ripple went through the chamber — not laughter, exactly, but the ghost of it, the sound of two hundred people recognizing a dead woman's irony and honoring it. Because Coral Amberdale had died three years ago, in her sleep, in her rooms above the stationer's shop, and everyone in that hall knew they were listening to a voice from beyond any grave the state had ever managed to dig.

The presiding magistrate removed her spectacles.

"This tribunal will recess for findings," she said. "Before it does, let the record reflect the following. The documents entered as Exhibit Four Hundred Eleven — four thousand one hundred and six census forms bearing testimony of the coastal evictions of 1934 through 1939 — are hereby accepted as authentic, corroborated, and admitted in full. The claim of the respondent ministry that these communities were 'already abandoned' is contradicted by the evidence and is rejected." She paused. "Further, let the record reflect that the typewriter which produced these documents has been located, restored to working order, and will itself be entered into evidence. The court wishes to thank whoever kept it."

Coral's nephew, who had inherited the rooms above the stationer's, had kept it. He had carried it to the capital in its old case, wrapped in a wool blanket, and surrendered it to the court clerk with the solemnity of a man handing over a relic.

* * *

They let the public view the exhibits on the final afternoon, before the findings were sealed.

Coral's nephew stood beside the glass case where the typewriter sat — a black iron Underwood, keys worn to the shine of use, a faint salt bloom still ghosting the carriage. People filed past it slowly. Some of them touched the case. Some of them wept. A schoolteacher held up her class, one by one, so each child could look.

Nella Okafor stood a long time. So did Idris Vane, hat in hand, saying nothing, having said everything years ago at the cost of his career and nearly his liberty. Pip came last, after closing, when the guards were readying to lock the hall, and the guard on duty — a young woman with a sensible manner — looked at his compositor's ink-stained fingers and at the machine behind the glass, and stepped aside.

Pip did not touch the typewriter. He opened his case instead, and took out a broadsheet he had printed that week, and laid it against the glass where the next morning's cleaners would find it.

One line, centered, in careful Caslon:

*THE LIGHT WAS MANNED WITHOUT INTERRUPTION.*

Below it, smaller: *Item 1. I know because I kept it.*

And below that, in letters small enough to require leaning close, the line that would be set again and reprinted in pamphlets and newspapers and, eventually, in the histories that got things right at last:

*Memory, once set down, cannot be fully confiscated.*

Outside, the capital's bells rang the hour. Somewhere beyond the rooftops, far to the west, the sea was doing what the sea does — turning, indifferent, keeping its own vast and unreadable record. But in the chamber behind him, four thousand forms lay in their cases, each one a small lamp lit against a dark that had lasted exactly as long as it took for someone to write the truth down.

Which is to say: not long at all. Not long at all.
