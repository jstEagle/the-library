## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The observatory sat on its hill like a man who had been told all his life that he was important and had come to believe it. Lena Underhill had thought this more than once on the long climb up from the tram stop, but she had never said it aloud, because saying things aloud was a habit she had trained out of herself the way one trains out of biting one's nails — patiently, painfully, and with occasional relapses.

Her tool case weighed eleven pounds. She knew this because her father had made her weigh it every morning for three years, until the number lived in her wrist the way scales lived in a musician's fingers. *A clockmaker who doesn't know her tools is a clockmaker who guesses,* he used to say, and then he would tap the case twice, which meant *and guessing is lying with extra steps.* She was twenty-six years old now, and her father had been dead four years, and she still tapped the case twice before opening it. Grief, she had found, was mostly habit with nowhere left to go.

At the observatory's side door she presented the work order to a young man in a wool coat far too fine for the weather, who read it as though it were written in a language he disapproved of.

"You're the — " He consulted the paper again, hoping perhaps it would improve on second reading. "The Underhill apprentice."

"Lena Underhill," she said. "I'm the one who'll be fixing your clock."

"Ah." He said it the way other men said *ah* when a dog did something clever. "Mr. Halloran's people were very insistent. The master clock has been losing time, and the Director wants it seen to before the year-end observations. There's rather a lot riding on it." He held the door open with the air of a man granting a favor he intended to mention later at dinner. "You'll want to mind the third stair. It's loose."

The third stair was not loose. The third stair was worn into a shallow dish by two hundred years of learned feet, and Lena noted, without saying so, that the astronomers of the Royal Observatory could measure the parallax of a star but had never once noticed their own staircase. This was, she had come to understand, the whole of astronomy in miniature: eyes fixed on heaven, shins bruised by the furniture.

* * *

The clock room was at the heart of the building, a stone chamber kept at a temperature chosen by dead men and defended by living ones. The master clock stood against the north wall, seven feet of mahogany and brass, older than the hill itself, and it was ticking — but wrongly. Lena heard it from the doorway the way a mother hears a wrong note in a child's humming: instantly, and with a small cold drop in the stomach.

"It's been losing about four seconds a day," said the young man, whose name she had already decided not to learn. "Dr. Pemberton says it's the cold. These old mechanisms are temperamental in winter."

"These old mechanisms are never temperamental," Lena said pleasantly. "They are only honest. Something has changed, and the clock is telling us what."

He blinked at her. She got the impression that honesty, in this building, was considered a service provided by instruments and not by persons.

She set her case down on the felt cloth she always carried for the purpose — *never let your tools touch another man's surfaces; you are a guest in his machine* — and opened the case door of the clock itself. The smell came out to meet her: brass polish, old oil, dust warmed by the pendulum's patient breathing. Her father's ghost arrived with it, as it always did, standing just behind her left shoulder with his hands folded, watching, saying nothing, tapping twice.

Four seconds a day. She listened for ten minutes without touching anything, which was the part of the trade no client ever appreciated and which separated repairers from parts-swappers. The tick was clean. The escapement was releasing evenly. The pendulum's arc was true. Which meant the fault was not in the going train at all.

It was in the winding.

The great weights hung low in the case, lower than they should have been for a clock wound on schedule. Either someone had stopped winding it properly — unlikely; the observatory staff wound it like priests tending an altar flame — or something inside the case was dragging. Friction where there should have been none. A weight moving through honey instead of air.

Lena fetched her lamp, angled it up into the dark throat of the case, and looked.

There was a bundle wedged against the interior wall of the case, above the left weight, bound in oilcloth and tied with waxed string, sitting precisely where a falling object dropped through the gap behind the movement would come to rest. It had been there a long time. The oilcloth had gone stiff and amber, and a fine grey fur of dust lay over it like frost.

Someone had hidden something inside the master clock of the Royal Observatory, and the something had been heavy enough, or badly enough placed, to foul the weight's travel by exactly enough to cost four seconds a day.

"Well," Lena said to the empty room, because her father had also taught her that talking to oneself in a machine room was not madness but method. "You've been making the poor thing lie."

She should have called for Dr. Pemberton. She knew that even as she reached for it. An object found in the fabric of the Observatory's most sacred instrument belonged to the Observatory, and to the Director, and to the chain of men whose names were on the door downstairs. A proper apprentice would have stepped back, dusted her hands, and announced the discovery in a voice pitched to carry.

But Lena Underhill had spent her whole life learning which questions never to ask, and the same instinct that kept questions unasked apparently kept hands still while they worked things out. She untied the waxed string — it crumbled at the second knot, patient as everything else in this room — and unwrapped the oilcloth on her felt cloth, under the lamp, alone.

Inside was a letter. One letter, sealed, the wax unbroken, pressed flat between two boards of thin cedar to keep it safe. The seal was stamped with a crest she didn't recognize — a ship above three stars — and across the face of it, in a careful copperplate hand faded to the color of weak tea, was a single line:

*To be opened only if the clock is ever opened by an honest hand.*

And beneath that, dated in the same hand: *March 1884.*

Lena sat back on her heels. The pendulum swung behind her, tick, tick, tick, four seconds a day short of the truth, and the warmth of the room seemed to thin all at once, as though a window had opened somewhere in the walls.

An honest hand. Whoever wrote that had expected a stranger, or a successor, or perhaps no one at all. They had not expected — she turned the envelope over, looking for anything further, and found nothing but the date — they could not possibly have expected her.

Because beneath the copperplate instruction, smaller, added later in different ink, in a hand she knew better than her own reflection — a hand that had written her name on school copybooks and medicine labels and one unfinished letter that still lived in the drawer of her workbench — was a second line:

*If it must be Lena, then God forgive me, and forgive me twice if she reads it.*

Her father's handwriting.

The room did not spin, exactly. It was quieter than spinning. It was the particular silence of a mechanism that has run smoothly for years and suddenly, deep in its train, met a tooth that does not fit. Lena knelt on the cold stone floor of the Royal Observatory with a sealed letter in her hands and understood, with the wordless certainty of her trade, that something in her life had just slipped a cog, and that nothing she owned — not her tools, not her name, not the careful invisibility she had worn like a second coat since childhood — would ever run quite true again.

She did not open it. Not yet. Her hands were not steady enough, and a letter fifteen years in the dark deserved steadier hands than hers were. She wrapped it back in its oilcloth, and then, after a moment, did not retie it with the broken string but slid it instead into the inner pocket of her coat, against her ribs, where it lay with a weight out of all proportion to its few ounces.

Then she stood, brushed off her knees, took up her tweezers and her screwdrivers, and went to work on the clock.

It took her two hours. She freed the fouled bearing, trued a slightly bent arbor she suspected had been bent deliberately — bent, she realized as she worked, by someone who knew clocks well enough to know that a four-second daily error would be blamed on winter and never investigated — cleaned, oiled, adjusted, and reset the beat. By the time she closed the case door, the master clock was ticking with the flat, serene perfection of a thing that has never once doubted itself.

The young man in the fine coat reappeared to watch her pack up, visibly disappointed that the repair had involved no drama he could report upward.

"Fixed, then?"

"Fixed," Lena said. "It will keep perfect time now. Better than it has in years."

"That's what we like to hear." He walked her to the door, holding it with the same air of conferred favor. "Mind how you go down the hill. It's icy after dark."

Outside, the winter sun was setting over the city in bands of copper and slate, and the gas lamps were being lit along the observatory road one by one, like a row of small obedient clocks all agreeing on the hour. Lena stood on the top step with her eleven-pound case in one hand and her father's secret against her ribs, and she thought about the phrase *an honest hand*, and about the fact that in March of 1884 — the spring she turned eleven, the spring her father started checking the locks twice and taking the long way home from work — someone had trusted the future to keep a promise.

The letter pressed warm against her side all the way down the hill.

She would open it tonight, in her workshop, with the door bolted and the lamp trimmed. She told herself she might not. She knew she would. Her father had taught her that too, though he'd never said it in words: you can leave a mechanism alone for only so long before the spring winds itself tight, and then it opens whether you're ready or not.

Better, then, to choose the hour yourself.

The tram bell rang below, patient as a pendulum. Lena Underhill went down the hill toward home, invisible as ever to everyone she passed, carrying time in her case and something heavier in her coat.
