## 7. The Underground Press

The address Marguerite Osei had given Lena was written on the back of a laundry ticket, which struck Lena as either very clever or very careless, depending on how many people in this city sent out their washing.

*No. 9 Coriander Yard,* it read, in a hand so small and precise it might have been set in six-point type itself. *Ask for the woman who mends books.*

Lena went at dusk, when the gas-lamps were being lit and every doorway in the city filled with a shadow that could plausibly be watching her. She had spent three days deciding whether to come at all. She had spent those same three days sleeping badly, eating worse, and catching herself reading the *Gazette* with her heart hammering at nothing — at shipping notices, of all things, at the little announcements of arrivals and departures that had once been as boring to her as weather.

The letter from the clock case sat against her ribs now, wrapped in oilcloth inside her bodice, where it made a small rectangular heartbeat she could feel whenever she breathed too deeply.

Coriander Yard turned out to be an alley behind a chandler's shop, entered through an archway that smelled of tallow and river mud. Number 9 was a narrow brick house with a bookbinder's sign hanging crooked over the door: *M. OSEI — BINDING & REPAIRS.* The window was dark. The door, when Lena tried it, was locked.

She knocked. Nothing. She knocked again, and a voice came through the wood, low and unhurried.

"We're closed."

"I'm told you mend books," Lena said. "I have one that's coming apart at the seams. It's been in my family fifteen years, and I've only just discovered what's written inside it."

A long silence. Then bolts — two, then a third she hadn't heard before — and the door opened onto lamplight and a woman about Lena's mother's age, broad-shouldered, spectacles pushed up into grey-threaded hair, appraising her with the expression of someone who repaired books and had strong opinions about people who didn't take care of them.

"You'd better come in before the whole street hears your poetry," said Marguerite Osei.

* * *

Inside, the front room was exactly what a bookbinder's should be: presses, bone folders, jars of paste, stacks of paper smelling of size and glue. It took Lena perhaps four seconds to notice what a less trained eye would have missed — that the floorboards under the big press were worn in a semicircle much wider than any bindery work required, and that the smell of ink beneath the glue was not the ink of repair but the ink of composition. Fresh. Black. A great deal of it.

Marguerite saw her noticing and did not pretend otherwise. "You're Underhill's girl," she said. "The clockmaker."

"Apprentice clockmaker," Lena said, out of habit, and then heard how it sounded and hated it. "Yes."

"Your father bought type from me once. Nineteen years ago. Wanted a single line set — a maker's mark for his clock cases, something elegant." Marguerite's mouth did something complicated. "He paid cash and never gave me his name until the third visit. Careful man."

"He was," Lena said. Her throat tightened. "That's rather the problem."

She unwrapped the letter there at the workbench, under the lamp, and laid it flat among the bone folders like a patient on a table. Marguerite put on her spectacles, bent close, and read. She read the way Lena worked on a movement — completely, without hurry, missing nothing.

When she finished, she straightened up and took off the spectacles and looked at Lena for a long moment.

"Tidal records," she said. "Stellar observations. The *Meridian*, wrecked off the point with all hands, and the logs adjusted afterward so the company wouldn't drown in lawsuits." She tapped the page. "And this is in whose hand?"

"The observatory's first astronomer. There are initials countersigning each alteration. My father's are here." Lena touched them lightly, the way one touches a bruise to confirm it still hurts. "He witnessed it. He may have done more than witness it. The letter doesn't say, and he's dead, so he can't say either."

"And the Halloran name, and—" Marguerite paused at the crest drawn in the margin, a shield with a chevron and three stars. "That's a magistrate's family arms. That's Vane."

"Yes," Lena said.

"You know the Vane boy. The inspector. Half the neighborhood knows you know him; he walks past your shop on Thursdays like a man who has forgotten what he came out for."

Heat climbed Lena's neck. "Is there anything in this city that isn't common knowledge?"

"The truth," said Marguerite dryly. "That's rather the entire difficulty. Everything else is common knowledge. Sit down, Miss Underhill. We're going to talk about what you're asking me to do, and I'm going to be honest with you, because you've brought me something worth being honest over."

* * *

What Marguerite said, while she poured tea into two cups that had seen better decades, was this:

"I print a broadsheet. Eight hundred copies some months, twelve hundred when the news is good and the constables are slow. It has no name you'd recognize and no office you could raid, because the office moves. Last year it was above a fishmonger in Salt Lane. Before that, a chapel vestry. At present it is through that door." She nodded at a panel behind the drying racks. "I have been arrested twice, tried once, and acquitted once because the jury contained three dockworkers and a widow who reads everything I print aloud to her lodgers. The second time I was not tried, because the witnesses forgot they had ever seen me, which cost me forty pounds in gratitude and was worth every shilling."

Lena held her tea and listened.

"If I set this story — the wreck, the falsified logs, the names, the crest — it will not be a broadsheet. It will be an event. Halloran's lawyers will descend like gulls on a dropped pie. The constabulary will want the printer, and they will want the source, and the source is a young woman with a workshop, a trade license, and a Thursday gentleman caller who happens to wear a badge." Marguerite set down her cup. "Publication means warrants, Miss Underhill. For both of us. Not maybe. Certainly. The only question is whether the truth is worth what they'll take from us for printing it, and that is a question I cannot answer for you. I can only tell you my own arithmetic."

"Which is?"

"I'm fifty-three years old. I have buried a husband, outlived two brothers, and watched thirty years of respectable newspapers print the shipping magnates' weather and call it the sky." She said it without heat, the way one states the price of bread. "My arithmetic says a lie that big, left standing into a new century, becomes furniture. People stop seeing it. You don't get another chance to knock it over until somebody else dies of it. So yes. I'll set your story. But you should go home tonight and sleep on it, and if tomorrow you come back and tell me no, I will respect you for it and we'll speak of it never again."

Lena looked down at the letter. Her father's initials. Fifteen years of his careful silences, his *some questions, Lena-girl, are like loose springs — you don't go poking them*, his whole architecture of survival built on the principle that small people stay small and live.

She thought of Fenn, the disgraced clerk, saying *they're writing the history now, girl, with the ink still wet on the crimes.* She thought of the master clock at the observatory, running four minutes slow, lying to every ship in the harbor by a little and a little and a little.

"No," Lena said.

Marguerite raised an eyebrow.

"No, I won't sleep on it. If I sleep on it I'll find a reason, the way my father found reasons for fifteen years, and the reasons will be sensible and I'll hate every one of them." She pushed the letter gently across the bench. "Set the story."

For the first time, Marguerite Osei smiled fully, and it transformed her face from that of a wary tradeswoman into something fierce and delighted, a woman who had been waiting years for good news and had learned not to expect it.

"Well then," she said. "Fetch the lamp. We start with the headline."

* * *

They worked through the night.

The back room behind the panel was low-ceilinged and warm, lit by two lamps and crowded with a hand press, cases of type, and twenty years of accumulated ingenuity — Marguerite had rigged a bell to the yard gate, a mirror angled to show the alley door, and a loose brick under which, she mentioned casually, a crowbar lived.

Lena had expected to be useless. She knew gears, not letters. But within an hour she discovered that typesetting was a cousin of her own trade: a case of type was a movement, each piece machined to fit its neighbors exactly, the whole depending on every smallest part sitting true. She was slow, and she dropped a capital Q twice, but she was not clumsy, and by midnight Marguerite was grunting approval instead of corrections.

"*HALLORAN LOGS FALSE,*" Marguerite read aloud, composing stick in hand. "Too long. '*THE MERIDIAN LIE*'?" She considered. "Shorter is louder. Shorter is always louder."

They argued about the headline the way old friends argue, though they had known each other six hours, because shared purpose compresses acquaintance wonderfully. They argued about whether to name the inspector's family outright (yes, said Lena, with a steadiness that surprised her) and whether to name her father (also yes — *if I hide him, I'm still keeping his secrets, and I'm done keeping secrets; let him stand in the record as he stood in the room*). Marguerite wrote the copy in her tiny exact hand; Lena set it; the galley grew column by column, and with every line of lead the thing stopped being a secret and started being an object, real, weighty, inevitable.

It was near three in the morning, the lamps burning low, when Lena paused over the compositor's stick and realized her hands had stopped shaking.

They had been shaking since the clock case. Three days of tremors she'd blamed on cold or tea or lack of sleep. And now, here, in an illegal cellar, setting seditious type with a warrant certain as sunrise — now they were steady. Because terror alone was a weight with nowhere to go, but terror with a task attached became something else entirely. Purpose, she supposed, was just fear that had found employment.

"You've gone quiet," Marguerite said, not looking up. "Quiet workers are either thinking or panicking. Which is it?"

"Thinking," Lena said, and found it was true. "I was thinking that my father spent his whole life making sure I'd never be brave, and it didn't take."

"It rarely does, in the second generation. Fear wears out, like anything else. Courage is simply fear that's been maintained." Marguerite slid a line of type home with a click. "Your father maintained his fear beautifully, from the sound of it. You'll maintain the other thing."

Lena laughed — actually laughed, at three in the morning, in a conspirator's cellar — and reached for the next piece of type.

It was nearly dawn when they finished the first galley and Marguerite called a halt, banking the lamps. "We pull proofs tomorrow. Full run the night after. Sleep, eat, walk your usual streets at your usual hours, and look bored." She fixed Lena with a look over her spectacles. "Especially with the inspector. Bored, Miss Underhill. Can you manage bored?"

"I've had a lifetime of practice," Lena said. "Being invisible. I'm merely changing professions."

But walking home through the blue-grey hush before sunrise, the letter gone from her bodice now — left in Marguerite's safekeeping, copied, distributed between three hiding places — Lena felt the absence of it like a lost tooth, and felt, underneath that, a new and colder thought.

Elias walked past her shop on Thursdays. He was investigating the break-in at the observatory. He was dutiful, and thorough, and he knew her handwriting, her habits, her face better than anyone living.

Sooner or later — and knowing Elias, sooner — he would follow the thread from the clock case to the alley to the press. And he would find her name on a warrant beside Marguerite's, and his duty and his heart would finally be handed the choice they had been circling all winter.

Lena pulled her collar up against the cold and quickened her pace, telling herself she had until Thursday.

She had until Wednesday.
