## 9. River Narrowing

The ice came in from the east like a rumor that would not die.

Sable Voss stood at the stern rail of the *Merrow* on the third morning out of Vellmark and watched the floes thicken — first scattered plates the size of serving trays, spinning slow in the current, then rafts wide enough to carry a horse, grinding against one another with a sound she felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears. A low complaint, a bone-deep groan, as if the river itself were being asked to remember something it had spent all autumn trying to forget.

She had been awake since four. She was always awake by four now. Sleep had become a country she could visit but not live in — too many checkpoints at its borders.

Her breath fogged the rail. She wiped it with her glove and looked down at the water sliding black beneath the hull, threaded with white. Somewhere under that water was the border they were racing toward: the neutral port of Callow's Reach, where the republic's writ ended and the world's indifference began. Twelve hours behind schedule already. The captain had posted no notice, but Sable had learned to read ships the way other people read faces — by what they stopped doing. The *Merrow* had stopped taking on coal at the last stop. The stokers had stopped singing. The engine had stopped trying to make up time and settled into a steady, stubborn churn, the rhythm of a man walking fast through snow he knows will win.

Behind her, the deck was nearly empty. The passengers who could afford cabins stayed in them; those who couldn't huddled in the saloon around the single permitted stove, playing cards with marked decks and telling stories about themselves that were better than the truth. Sable preferred the cold. Cold kept her sharp, and sharpness was the only luggage she'd been allowed to bring.

*You are a census clerk,* she reminded herself, the way she did each morning, fitting the name over her face like a mask still damp from the last wearer. *You are traveling to audit shipping manifests for the Bureau of Population Records. You are unremarkable. You are tired. You are nobody.*

It had worked for years, this litany. It was working less well now, and she suspected she knew why, though she refused — in the disciplined way she refused most things — to look directly at the reason.

The reason was currently asleep two decks below her, in berth eleven, breathing under a coat that never seemed to leave their shoulders even in the overheated saloon.

Lior Eastwick.

Sable turned the name over in her mind the way a jeweler turns a stone toward the light, looking for the flaw. There was always a flaw. Ten days of watching had taught her the shape of this particular stone: the way Lior sat with their back to a wall and their eyes on the door; the way they counted exits without seeming to; the way they touched the left breast of that coat — twice an hour, sometimes three times — a small, unconscious gesture, the gesture of a person confirming that something sewn against their heart was still there.

People carrying nothing did not check their chests.

She had filed her observations in the neat mental ledger the Bureau had trained into her, and the ledger said: *courier*. It said: *the codes are aboard*. It said: *your orders stand*.

But ledgers, she was beginning to understand, were written by people who believed the columns balanced. Hers had not balanced since Vellmark, since the boarding house, since the moment a young man with careful eyes had lied to her about the year of the crossing so smoothly that she'd known instantly he was lying, and known almost as instantly that she didn't want him caught.

That was the trouble. That was the whole rotten trouble. She had come aboard hunting a set of stolen identity papers — papers bearing a name the Bureau had erased twenty years ago, a name that belonged, by every legal fiction the republic possessed, to no living soul, which meant it belonged to her — and instead she had found herself standing at a rail watching ice form on a river, thinking about a stranger's face, feeling something she had no column for.

The ship shuddered. Not the engine — the hull. A floe the size of a hay wagon had shouldered past beneath them, close enough to scrape paint. Sable watched it drift astern, white and indifferent, going wherever rivers told it to go.

*Must be nice,* she thought, and immediately despised herself for the smallness of it.

* * *

The telegram found her at noon, or rather she found it, because telegrams do not find people; people find telegrams, if they know how to look.

She knew how to look. The purser's office aboard the *Merrow* was a closet off the main corridor, staffed by a harried sub-officer named Drell who took his meals at fixed hours and his brandy at unfixed ones. Between the second sitting of lunch and the first round of evening drinks there was a window — forty minutes, maybe fifty — when the office stood empty but unlocked, because Drell believed locks invited scrutiny and scrutiny invited questions, and Drell's entire philosophy of survival consisted of inviting neither.

Sable had made a point of learning Drell's philosophy on the first day out. She had also made a point of being seen near the office at random hours, chatting about weather, asking after mail, establishing herself as a harmless bore with an interest in postal schedules. Harmless bores could stand outside any door in the world without raising an eyebrow. It was one of the few advantages of being nobody, and she had cultivated it the way farmers cultivate poor soil — patiently, without illusions.

Today she waited until Drell's boots receded toward the mess, counted to sixty, and let herself in.

The wire basket on the desk held the usual dross: a shipping notice from the coal concern at Brast, a personal message for one of the twins — she noted, without much interest, that it was from their mother and contained the word *disown* — and a flimsy yellow slip with the Bureau's crest pressed into its header, addressed to the *Merrow*'s master, marked URGENT and ROUTED VIA RELAY STATION KESS.

She read it standing up, because her legs had decided sitting was no longer among the day's options.

> TO MASTER SS MERROW STOP REPUBLIC PROVISIONAL GOVT DISSOLVED 14TH STOP MINISTRIES CEASED FUNCTIONING STOP ALL CREDENTIALS BUREAU ISSUED VOID AS OF THIS DATE STOP NO FURTHER INSTRUCTIONS WILL ISSUE STOP ACKNOWLEDGE AND ACT ACCORDINGLY STOP

There was more — port advisories, a warning about militia activity on the north bank — but she read the middle of it three times and the rest not at all.

*Dissolved.*

The word sat on the page, small and bureaucratic, wearing its full stop like a period at the end of a very long sentence. Twenty-two years of the republic — the congresses, the censuses, the purge, the counter-purge, the forms in triplicate, the names entered and erased, the whole vast machinery of paper that had decided who existed and who did not — and it had ended the way everything ended now: with a relay station clerk typing *STOP* and meaning nothing by it.

Sable put the telegram back in the basket exactly where it had lain, angle preserved, edges aligned. Her hands were steady. She noticed this with a kind of clinical detachment, the way she'd once noticed, as a child, that she could hold her breath longer than the other children in the ward. Steadiness was just another kind of holding your breath.

Then she walked out of the purser's office, along the corridor, up the stairs, out onto the deck, and stood at the rail in the gray light and let it hit her properly.

She served a country that no longer existed.

The thought arrived not as grief but as vertigo — a lurch, as though the deck had tilted five degrees and everyone else had somehow failed to notice. All her life there had been the republic underneath things, the way bedrock is underneath things: unseen, assumed, load-bearing. The Bureau had been monstrous, yes — she knew its teeth better than most, having grown up in the gap between them — but it had been *real*. Its forms had been real. Its erasures had been real. When the Bureau erased a girl named Sable Voss from the rolls of the living, that erasure had weight, consequence, reality. Someone had signed it. Someone had filed it. The machinery had ground forward over her name and left a smooth flat place where a person used to be, and she had spent twenty years pressing her thumb against that flat place, waiting for the machinery to acknowledge the dent.

Now the machinery was gone. Dissolved. Ceased functioning. And the dent —

The dent was still there. That was the terrible joke of it. The republic died, but its damage survived it, the way a dead man's debts survive him. She was still erased. The papers she'd been sent to find — the forged documents built on the ruins of her own name — were still forged, still stolen, still warm against some courier's chest. But now there was no Bureau to return them *to*. No ledger to correct. No clerk at the end of the long corridor of procedure who could stamp a page and say *restored*, and make it true.

If she completed her mission — if she recovered the codes, closed the file, delivered the last report of the Bureau of Population Records — she would be delivering it to a corpse. She would be the faithful servant of an office that had ceased to exist, guarding the legitimacy of a government that had none left to guard.

And the codes. God, the codes.

She understood them now, finally, in a way she hadn't at Vellmark. Diplomatic cipher keys weren't secrets; they were *proof*. They were the republic's signature, the thing that let its last ambassadors walk into neutral ministries and be received as representatives of something rather than refugees from nothing. While the codes lived, the republic lived — technically, legally, in the thin afterlife of recognized governments-in-exile. Burn them, lose them, surrender them, and the republic became what it had in fact already become: a memory with borders that existed only on old maps.

Somewhere below her feet, in a coat lining, against a careful heart, rode the last living piece of a dead country.

And she was supposed to take it.

*Act accordingly,* the telegram said. Accordingly to what? To whom? The authority that issued her orders had dissolved like sugar in rain. Every hour she spent wearing the census clerk's mask, she was impersonating a government. She laughed — one short breath of a laugh, swallowed at once — and a gull somewhere downstream answered her, mocking or commiserating, impossible to say.

* * *

In the afternoon the captain called them together.

He did it in the old style, standing on the saloon stairs with a speaking trumpet he clearly didn't need, because the saloon was small enough that he could have whispered and been heard in the engine room. Captain Orla Henn was a broad, gray woman with a face like a river stone — worn smooth, hard to read, older than her years by several decades of water. Sable had catalogued her early: honest, competent, loyal to the ship above all flags, which made her, in this season, the rarest kind of person aboard.

"Listen," Henn said, and the room went quiet the way rooms went quiet now — quickly, hungrily, the way people listened when listening might keep them alive. "I'll give it to you straight, because you'll get it wrong anyway if I don't."

She let that land. She was good at letting things land.

"The freeze is coming down the valley faster than the almanac says it should. Ice master says the channel at Sorrel Narrows closes within the week — maybe four days, maybe six. We're twelve hours behind and losing. If we make Callow's Reach before the river sets, we tie up, you disembark, and this becomes somebody else's problem." She paused. "If we don't, we winter here. In the ice. This ship can survive frozen in — I've done it, twice — but she can't feed forty souls for four months on the coal and stores she's carrying. So hear me plainly: if we stop, we stop short. And stopping short means choices."

Nobody asked what choices. Everybody understood.

"The purser will have the boat manifest posted by tomorrow," Henn went on. "Four berths in the longboat, provisioned for a crossing overland to the Reach on foot if the ice takes us early enough to walk it. Priority goes by verified identity — papers checked, names confirmed. That's regulation, and regulation is the only law left on this river, so I suggest you all become very fond of your paperwork."

A sound went through the room — not quite a murmur, more a shifting, like ice settling. Sable watched faces. The defected judge, Justice Aldery, sat with his hands folded and his eyes closed, a man rehearsing serenity. The twins sat apart from each other for the first time since Vellmark, a visible inch of air between their shoulders, and Sable thought: *there it is, the crack, already running.* The singer, Corvane, was watching the purser's door with the fixed professional attention of a man pricing a commodity. And near the stove, wrapped in a gray coat that never left her shoulders, Lior Eastwick sat perfectly still, hands in lap, face arranged into the mild blankness of a person with nothing to declare.

But Sable saw the fingers. Left hand, resting over the left breast. Pressing. Once. Twice.

*Checking.*

Henn finished — provisions, rations, discipline, the usual catechism of shrinking worlds — and the crowd broke apart into knots of whisperers. Sable stayed by the wall and watched Lior Eastwick rise, exchange a glance with Maren Eastwick — the mother, the iron-gray woman with the purser's ear and the guilty eyes — and leave the saloon alone.

Sable gave them thirty seconds and followed.

* * *

She found them — found *her*, the courier, the stranger, the person whose name might or might not be the name Sable had come to reclaim — at the stern rail again, where the ice was thickest now, plates locking together astern in a widening white seam, as if the river were closing a book behind them.

For a while Sable simply stood, three paces off, watching the floes. She was aware of being watched back. Neither of them spoke. This had become, over ten days, a strange and wordless arrangement between them: proximity without approach, attention without accusation. Two people circling the same fire, each pretending to warm their hands.

"The captain's arithmetic," Sable said at last, because someone had to break it. "Four berths. Nine claimants. Verified identity."

"Mm," said Lior.

"You have papers, I assume. Good ones."

"I have papers." A pause, delicate as a scale balancing. "Don't you? A census clerk ought to have excellent papers."

"Oh, mine are flawless," Sable said. "That's rather the tragedy of them."

Lior turned then, and for a moment their eyes met across the freezing air, and Sable felt again that vertiginous tilt, that sense of standing on a deck that had shifted five degrees without anyone else noticing. There was something in Lior's face — fear, yes, carefully rationed like everything else about them, but under the fear something else. Recognition. The look of a person seeing their own loneliness walking around outside their body, wearing different clothes.

"The republic fell today," Sable said quietly. "Did you know?"

Something moved behind Lior's eyes — a door closing, or opening; Sable genuinely could not tell which. "Ships hear things," Lior said. "So do clerks, apparently."

"So do couriers."

The silence that followed lasted four seconds. Sable counted them, because counting was what she did instead of screaming. Four seconds in which either of them could have stepped across the gap — could have said *I know what you're carrying* or *I know what you're hiding* or simply *who are you, truly, tell me, tell me, the whole world has ended anyway, what does it cost now* — and neither of them did. The moment passed the way ice passes under a hull: bearing weight, for now, groaning.

"Whatever you're carrying," Sable said, and watched Lior's hand twitch toward the coat and stop itself, "I hope it's worth what it's going to cost. Everything costs, on this river, now. Even staying silent. Especially staying silent."

"And what are you carrying, Clerk Voss?" Lior asked. Their voice was light. Their eyes were not.

Sable looked at the ice. The floes had locked solid astern now, a white plain growing backward from the stern, reaching for the ship like a hand closing finger by finger.

"A name," she said. "One that was taken from me before I could miss it. I've been sent to find it." She smiled, and the smile hurt her face. "Though I'm told the office that sent me no longer exists. So I suppose I'm looking for it on my own recognizance now. Isn't that something? Even my errand is an orphan."

Beside her, Lior Eastwick had gone very still — a stillness different from composure, a stillness with a heartbeat hammering audibly under it, or so Sable imagined, or so Sable hoped, because some traitorous part of her wanted the courier to flinch, wanted the words to land somewhere soft, wanted — absurdly, unforgivably — to be *known*.

"That sounds lonely," Lior said at last, barely above the grind of the ice.

"It is," Sable said. "How's yours?"

Lior didn't answer. Downriver, far ahead, the last open water gleamed like a strip of steel between the gathering whiteness — narrow now, narrowing, a door that was closing at the speed of winter. Sable watched it and did the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do all day: four berths, nine claimants, one longboat, one frozen river, and somewhere between her and that strip of open water, a coat with two hearts' worth of secrets sewn into its lining.

Tomorrow the manifest would be posted. Tomorrow, verified identities would begin deciding who lived.

Tonight, Sable Voss — clerk of a dissolved bureau, heir of an erased name, servant of a country that had stopped existing at 1400 hours by relay wire — went below to decide, for the first time in her life, whom she was working for.

The answer, when it came, frightened her more than the ice did.
