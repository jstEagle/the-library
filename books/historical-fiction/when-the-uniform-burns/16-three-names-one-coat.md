## 16. Three Names, One Coat

The ship had stopped speaking.

That was the only way Lior could describe it — the *Merrow*, which had groaned and hissed and clattered for six days like a living thing, had gone quiet in the way of animals deciding whether to fight or die. The engines turned over at half steam, enough to keep the boilers from freezing, not enough to pretend they were going anywhere. Twelve miles of pack ice between them and the neutral shore, and the captain had stopped saying *when* and started saying *if*.

Lior found Sable Voss where she always was now: the aft rail, third stanchion from the port side, where the fog came off the ice in long white breaths. She stood with her hands folded on the rail the way a woman stands at a graveside, patient, unimpressed by weather.

"You'll freeze," Lior said.

"I've been cold for twenty years." Sable didn't turn. "Another hour won't change the architecture."

It was the kind of sentence a person said when they had already decided something. Lior had heard it before — from the judge, the night before the twins took him off at Grayhaven Reach, one twin weeping and the other staring straight ahead while the militia boat bumped against the hull. The judge had shaken every hand on deck except the informer's. He'd looked at Lior last, longest, and said nothing at all, which was worse than anything he could have said.

Lior had been carrying that silence for two days. It sat in the chest like swallowed river water.

"Sable."

"Now you use my name." A ghost of a smile. "You've been avoiding it since the boiler room."

*Since the boiler room.* Since the heat and the hammering and Sable's voice pitched low under the noise, telling a stranger her own history as if it were someone else's crime: *They took my name when I was four years old. There was a broker. There was a fire. There was a girl who got to be real because I stopped existing.* And Lior, wearing that name stitched into every document Maren Eastwick had ever signed, had stood in the boiler's orange light and said nothing, and the nothing had grown teeth.

"I have to tell you something," Lior said. "And after I tell it, you're going to want to put me off this ship. I'd help you. But you should hear it from me before you hear it from him."

Sable turned then. The fog had gotten into her eyelashes, and her eyes were the gray of the ice beyond the rail — not cold exactly, Lior thought. Old. Cold was a temperature; this was a patience.

"The census taker," she said.

"He spoke to me this morning." Lior's mouth was dry. "He knows about the coat."

Something moved behind Sable's face — quick, involuntary, the flinch of a woman who has just heard her own name in a crowd. "Come below," she said. "Not the cabin. The coal hold. Walls there are thick, and no one confesses anything standing up in the wind."

---

The coal hold smelled of tar and iron and the deep animal warmth of the ship's belly. Sable hung a lantern on a hook meant for a signal lamp, and its light made a small yellow country around them, maybe six feet across, with blackness pressing in on every border. They sat on an overturned crate, shoulder to shoulder, the way people sit when the alternative is facing each other.

"Start wherever it's easiest," Sable said. "They never are, but start somewhere."

Lior started with the coat.

"It was my mother's. My first mother — the one who died in the winter of the purges, the one whose face I don't remember. Maren kept it. Wool, gray, brass buttons. When I was old enough to travel alone, she had the lining opened and resewn, and she put things inside it, and she told me: *You carry this coat, you wear this coat, you let no one alter this coat, and if anyone asks what's in the lining, you look bored.*" Lior laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I've been looking bored for ten years."

"What's in the lining?"

"Cipher codes. The diplomatic keys of a republic that may already be dead. The last mission's whole legitimacy is sewn into a dead woman's coat, and I'm the envelope." Lior's hands were shaking; Lior watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to one of the twins, to the singer, to anyone but the person speaking. "There's more. You asked me once, at the rail, in the fog — you asked where I learned to watch doors. I lied to you. I said a border town. The truth is I learned it from Maren, who learned it from fear, because everything about our family was built to survive exactly one question: *who are you?*"

The lantern flame leaned. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the deck, paused, moved on.

"Say it," Sable said quietly. Not cruel. Almost gentle. "Whatever it is. Say it all the way through. I've spent twenty years hearing half of things."

Lior closed their eyes.

"Your name," Lior said. "The broker didn't retire a girl named Sable Voss. He gave her name to us. To me. My papers — the ones that say Lior Eastwick, the ones that got me berths and rations and a place on the verified list — they're built on your erased record. Maren bought them the night the archives burned. She took a baby out of a burning district and she took a dead girl's identity out of a burning office, and she raised me inside your name like a house." The words came faster now, tumbling, thirty years of a dam giving way. "Every form I ever signed, I signed with your life. Every time someone called me Eastwick, they were calling you. When the purser checked my papers for the berth list, he was checking *you*, and it passed, because it always passes, because it was made well, because a woman loved me enough to steal for me—"

"Stop."

Lior stopped. The word hadn't been loud. It had been the way ice sounds before it gives.

Sable was looking at her hands. She turned them over slowly, palms up, as if checking whether they still belonged to her.

"Do you know what I did," she said, "when the bureau hired me? They came to me because I was thorough and because I had no attachments they could leverage. They said: there are forged identities moving along the river lines, remnants of the old broker networks. Find the documents. Find the carriers. You of all people will recognize the workmanship." Her thumb traced a line across her palm, back and forth, back and forth. "I thought — good. Let me find them. Let me see the person who got to be real. I imagined hating them. I prepared to hate them. I had speeches ready."

"And instead?"

"Instead I met a courier who memorizes faces at docks because they're afraid of being recognized, and a mother who bribes pursers with shaking hands, and I thought: these people aren't thieves. These people are frightened. And I hated myself for noticing, because noticing was the first crack in twenty years of clean purpose." She looked up. Her eyes weren't cold anymore. They were wet, and furious about being wet. "I sat across from you at dinner, Lior. I watched you count the exits. I thought, *there but for a broker's ledger go I.* I didn't know how literally."

"I'm sorry." It was inadequate and they both knew it. Lior said it anyway, because some words are less about meaning than about refusing to leave a debt unnamed. "For the name. For the silence in the boiler room. For letting you tell me your own history without telling you — God, Sable, you were sitting next to your own grave and being kind to it."

"Don't." Sable held up a hand. Then, after a moment, lowered it. "No. Go on. Finish it. What else?"

"Maren." Lior's voice dropped. "She's spoken to the census taker. Or he's spoken to her — I don't know who approached whom. He offered her immunity. Berths. All three of us, safe passage, new papers, real ones, in exchange for the codes. That's why he hasn't taken me. That's why the judge was the one removed at Grayhaven and not me. He's not hunting anymore. He's negotiating. And Maren—" Lior's throat closed. "Maren would do it. She'd hand over the last diplomatic legacy of an entire republic to save me. She'd call it love. It might even be love. That's the worst part. Everything monstrous she's ever done, she did as love, and I am the shape her monstrosity made."

The lantern hissed. In the dark beyond it, the ship ticked and settled, ice grinding against the hull with a sound like slow applause.

Sable was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Lior began composing defenses, rebuttals, pleas — the whole armory a lifetime of secrecy builds. None of them were needed.

When Sable finally spoke, her voice was flat with a tiredness so complete it circled back around to calm.

"I'm not angry," she said.

"You should be."

"I probably will be. Anger takes energy, and I've been spending mine on the wrong thing for twenty years." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, a gesture so ordinary, so human, that it hurt worse than any blow. "Do you know what I wanted, all this time? Revenge sounded right, in the abstract. Justice. Restitution. Make them give it back. But sitting here, in a coal cellar on a frozen river, listening to you say it out loud—" She shook her head. "I don't want your name back. Your name is worn. It's got ten years of somebody else's living in it. It fits you the way my boots fit the last passenger who borrowed them. Taking it back wouldn't make me real again. Nothing makes you real again after that. You just — continue. As a question."

"Sable—"

"I just want to have been real."

The words landed in the little yellow country of lamplight and lay there between them, and Lior understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was the true confession — not Lior's theft, not Maren's bargain, but this: a woman asking not for restitution but for witness. Not *give it back* but *admit it happened*. Not justice but memory.

"You were real," Lior said. "You were real the whole time. The paper was fake. The girl wasn't. Maren kept a page — I think she kept a page, there's something else in the lining, I've never dared open it past the codes — proof of you. Of the original record. If it survived, then somewhere in the wreckage of every archive that burned, there's a document that says Sable Voss existed, born such a day, daughter of such a family, and no broker's pen ever touched it."

Sable went very still.

"A page," she repeated. "In the coat."

"In the coat."

They looked at each other across the crate, and something passed between them that had no name in either of their languages — not forgiveness, not yet, not trust, not yet, but recognition, the terrible relief of two people discovering they have been imprisoned in the same building from different cells.

"We should open it," Sable said. "Now. Tonight. Before he—"

A sound above them. Boots again — but heavier this time, deliberate, stopping directly overhead. Then three knocks on the deck plating, spaced evenly, patient as a man reading a form aloud.

Both of them knew the rhythm. Both of them had heard it at Vellmark, at the dock, in the corridor outside the cabins. The census taker did not knock on doors. He knocked on ceilings. He knocked on the world, politely, to announce he was already inside it.

Sable rose and pinched out the lantern without discussion, and the darkness folded over them like a page closing. In the black, her hand found Lior's sleeve — not gripping, just touching, the way you touch something to confirm it exists.

"If that page says what you think it says," she whispered, "then Maren Eastwick has been carrying my whole life in a coat pocket next to a republic's dying breath, and she chose which one to save."

"She saved both," Lior whispered back. "That's the thing about Maren. She never throws anything away. She just buries it."

Above them, the knocking came again — twice now, shorter, less polite — and beneath it, faintly, muffled by iron and ice, they both heard another sound that froze them harder than the river ever could:

Maren's voice, high and strained, calling Lior's name across the deck.

Calling it with the particular terror of a woman who has just made a bargain, and come to collect her child before the price comes due.
