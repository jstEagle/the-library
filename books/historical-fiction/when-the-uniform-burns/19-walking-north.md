## 19. Walking North

**LIOR**

The ice took our weight the way a creditor takes payment — without warmth, without comment, only the arithmetic of whether we were worth holding.

Maren went first. I insisted on it, though she argued, because if the river meant to swallow someone that morning I wanted it to have to work for the smaller of us. She walked with her arms out from her body like a woman crossing a beam in a dream, and every few steps she would stop and listen — not to the ice, but through it, down into the black water where the *Merrow* sat dying twelve miles behind us and twelve miles short of everything.

The shore lights were ahead. That was all we had: lights, and the rumor of a border, and the knowledge that somewhere behind us Sable Voss was being rowed south by strangers whose names were probably as false as everyone else's.

"Do you hear it?" Maren called back.

I listened. Under my boots the river made a sound I had never heard a river make — a long, slow groan, like a door opening in a house where no one is supposed to be awake.

"It's settling," I said. "That's what the captain said. Settling is good."

"The captain also said we'd make port by Thursday," Maren said, and kept walking.

We had left the ship at dawn, or what passes for dawn when the sky is the color of dishwater and the sun is a rumor behind fog. The last lifeboat had gone south an hour before, carrying Sable and three others whose berths had been bought with papers, bribes, or tears. Four berths. Nine claimants. And in the end the boat had gone light, because one berth stood empty and no amount of arithmetic could fill it with the two people who remained.

I had burned the codes. I kept coming back to that, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. In the boiler room, in front of the census taker's face, I had fed twenty years of a republic's last legitimacy into the fire, page by page, and watched him understand that he was watching a country die twice. He hadn't stopped me. That was the part I couldn't parse yet. He had stood there with his gloved hands folded and let the smoke rise, and then he had said, very quietly:

"Well. Now nobody has them."

And I had thought: *that wasn't mercy. That was something else.* But there was no time to name it, because the ice was cracking and Maren was pulling me toward the rail and the world had narrowed to the width of a gangplank laid flat on a frozen river.

Now the world was wider and worse. The pack ice stretched north in plates and ridges, gray-white under gray-white sky, and the wind came off it with nothing to slow it, finding every seam in my coat — the coat that was empty now, both secrets gone out of its lining, the codes to fire and the birth record to Sable's hands. The coat weighed nothing. It felt obscene, how little it weighed.

"Lior." Maren had stopped. She was looking back the way we'd come.

I turned. The *Merrow* sat in the ice like a fly in amber, her stacks cold, her rails crowded with the passengers who hadn't gotten berths and were waiting for the ice road to harden enough to walk. Small figures moved along her deck. Somewhere among them, I knew, the twins were arguing over who would go first across the river, and the singer was trying to sell the judge's denouncer a coat he didn't need, and the purser was probably already drafting a new manifest for the walk — four columns, nine names, priority by verified identity, God help us all.

But that wasn't what Maren was looking at.

At the stern rail, alone, stood a figure in a dark coat. Even at this distance I knew the posture — the stillness of a man who watches people the way accountants watch numbers. The census taker. He had not taken a berth. He had not joined the walk. He stood at the rail with his hands folded, and as I watched, he raised one hand — not waving, I decided. Saluting. Or counting. With that man it had always been hard to tell the difference.

"He's not following," Maren said.

"No."

"Then why do I feel followed?"

Because some men leave their eyes behind them when they go, I almost said. Because a question asked can't be unasked, and he had spent a whole winter asking questions. But I said nothing, because the wind was rising and the lights were far and my mother — my false mother, my true mother, the only mother the word had ever held for me — was standing on a frozen river waiting for me to move.

"We walk," I said. "North. One foot, then the other. We don't stop until the ice ends or we do."

**MAREN**

I had crossed this river once before, twenty years ago, in the spring, when it was water and not teeth. I had crossed it with a bundle inside my coat and a broker's receipt in my pocket and a name in my mouth that I practiced saying until it sounded like mine: *Eastwick. Maren Eastwick. Widow. One child.*

The child had been Lior. The name had been stolen. The widow had been a fiction assembled in a rented room above a print shop, and every year since, on the anniversary of the crossing, I had woken at dawn with my heart going like a trapped bird and lain still until it quieted, telling myself: *you did what was possible. You did what love could afford.*

Lying on a frozen river with your heart going like a trapped bird is very much the same feeling, I discovered. Except now there was nowhere to lie still.

The ice spoke constantly. That was the thing no one tells you — that a frozen river is not silent but conversational, muttering and cracking and clearing its throat, and every sound might be the small talk of cold stone or the opening argument of the water beneath deciding it wants its surface back. I learned within the first mile to sort the voices. The high ticks were safe. The long groans were safe. The sounds like a whip cracking somewhere behind you were — according to Lior, who had listened to the captain's briefing with the desperate attention of a scholar — mostly safe.

Mostly. The river was a bureaucrat too. Everything in *mostly*, nothing guaranteed, all terms subject to revision.

"Tell me about the lights," I said, because my legs were cramping and fear feeds on silence. "What did the purser say was over there?"

"Neutral territory. A customs house, a hostel, a consulate." Lior's breath tore away sideways in the wind. "He said they don't ask about papers."

"They always ask about papers."

"He said they ask *differently*. Not *who are you* but *what do you need*." A pause, boots scraping, the whip-crack of ice behind us making us both flinch and walk faster. "Sable will be there by tonight. If the boat holds."

"If the boat holds," I agreed, and did not say what I was thinking, which was: *if the boat holds, she will arrive at a consulate and give them a name, and for the first time in twenty years it will be hers, and the first thing that name will buy her is the power to decide what happens to us.* I had put the birth record in her hands myself — pressed it into her palm at the rail with both of mine wrapped around it, the way you press a hot coal into someone's grip so they can't give it back. *This is proof you existed,* I told her. *It was never mine to keep.* And she had looked at me with those gray eyes that held no forgiveness and no accusation either, just an exhaustion older than her years, and she had said:

"You should have given me this instead of a childhood."

"I know," I said.

"That's not enough."

"I know," I said again, and it was the truest sentence I had spoken in twenty years, and it cost more than the confession, and she took the paper and got in the boat, and that was the whole of it. There are debts you cannot pay. You can only stop pretending they aren't owed.

The lights grew by inches. Behind us the *Merrow* shrank to a toy, then to a smudge, then to nothing, and the river became the whole world — white below, white above, no horizon, just the two of us stitched onto the middle of it like a mistake in a ledger.

"Lior," I said, when we stopped to share the last of the bread. "If they arrest us over there—"

"They won't. Neutral ground."

"If they *question* us. If someone asks you who you are." I broke the bread and gave her the larger piece, out of habit, out of guilt, out of twenty years of overfeeding a child I had once starved of the truth. "Don't protect me. Whatever they ask, whatever it costs — don't lie for me anymore. Promise me. I've had my fill of being protected by lies. They're heavy. Heavier than the truth ever was."

Lior chewed slowly. My child — and I would not surrender that word, whatever the brokers and bureaus said; ten years of porridge and fevers and nightmares soothed makes a mother out of anything, even a thief — my child looked north at the lights and then at me, and there was frost in the lashes, and something in the expression I had never seen there before. Not the smoothness Lior wore like armor. Something underneath it, young and bare.

"You're the only family I've got," Lior said.

"No," I said. "You've got two. You just met the other one."

* * *

**LIOR**

We reached the ice road at midafternoon — a lane the locals had swept and marked with brush bundles, wide enough for sledges, running the last three miles to the customs house. Other walkers had used it before us. Their tracks were already filling with snow, and I found myself reading them the way I had learned to read faces on the *Merrow*: here a pair of boots walking fast, stride long, no hesitation — a man with papers. Here two sets of prints side by side, one dragging — someone helping someone. Here, near a marker post, a single set of prints that stopped, turned in a full circle, and continued north — someone who had changed their mind about something.

Everyone in this story was walking away from a name, I thought. Or toward one.

The customs house resolved out of the fog an hour before dusk: a low stone building with a lamp burning green in its window, and beside it the hostel, longer, with real smoke rising from a real chimney, and beyond both, strung along the shore like beads, the lights of the neutral town. Civilization, in miniature. Enough of it to matter.

A soldier met us at the edge of the ice road — young, wool-capped, a rifle slung with the casualness of a man who had never fired it at anything but targets. He looked at us the way the purser had looked at claimants: a quick inventory, clothes, faces, exhaustion.

"Papers?" he said.

Here it was. The question that had run under the whole winter like a current under ice. Maren's hand found mine. Her fingers were freezing and they squeezed anyway, and I understood the message: *don't lie for me.*

"We don't have any," I said. "Not real ones. What we had were forged, and I'll tell you the names on them if it helps, but they're not ours. We're refugees off the steamer *Merrow*. The ship's in the ice twelve miles south. There's a lifeboat that came through this morning — a woman named Voss, traveling alone. Ask her about us. She'll tell you the truth, and she doesn't owe us kindness, which means you can believe her."

The soldier blinked. Of all the answers he had collected at this post — I could see the collection in his face, the smugglers' bluster, the aristocrats' outrage, the forged documents offered with trembling hands — no one had ever simply confessed.

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "You're telling me your papers are false."

"All of them," Maren said, stepping up beside me, chin level, the old fierce dignity rising through the frost like heat through a grate. "Mine, the child's, the dead mother's whose coat we're wearing. Twenty years of forgery, officer, and I'd do it again, and I'll stand in whatever line you put me in while you decide what that's worth. But I'm done lying on ice."

The soldier looked at us for a long moment. Then he did something I did not expect. He laughed — a short, tired bark, the laugh of a man at the end of a shift.

"Ma'am," he said, "half the people who crossed today showed me papers signed by officials who died in the fall of the capital. One gentleman had a passport issued by a ministry that, as far as anyone knows, never existed. You're the first honest forgers we've had." He jerked his thumb toward the hostel. "There's soup. There's a register — write whatever name you want, we stopped checking against anything weeks ago. Half our own garrison's paperwork burned with the ferry office." He paused, and something flickered behind his eyes, some private amusement. "You want my advice? Pick names you can live with. It's easier on everybody."

*Pick names you can live with.* I carried the sentence into the customs house like contraband, like coal, like the warmest thing I owned.

Inside, the clerk at the desk was stamping forms by lamplight, and the room smelled of wet wool and cabbage, and it was the most beautiful room I had ever stood in. Maren wrote her name in the register — her real one, the first one, the girl she had been before she became a widow who never existed — and I watched her hand shake doing it, and then steady, and then finish.

Then the soldier who had met us came back inside, frowning at a clipboard, and crossed to the clerk, and their conversation drifted to us across the room in fragments the way conversations do when they concern you.

"—the bureau man. The one who came through yesterday, ahead of the steamer. Census section, said he'd be meeting a boat."

"Gone," the clerk said. "Checked out this morning. Before dawn."

"Did he say where?"

"Didn't sign the book properly, did he? Half a name and a smear." The clerk flipped a page, squinted. "Though the night man swears the signature wasn't the same as the one he arrived under. Says the arrival book says *Corven* and the departure says *Hale*, and the handwriting's identical, and neither name matches the travel warrant the bureau sent ahead."

The soldier grunted. "Bureau men. Half of them are using dead men's credentials since the capital fell. Probably stole the posting off a corpse."

"Or the corpse stole it first," the clerk said, and stamped a form, and the conversation moved on to coal rations.

I stood very still by the register, Maren's pen scratch still drying beside me, and let it settle over me the way the fog had settled over the river.

*Census section. Ahead of the steamer. Gone before dawn.*

He had known. He had known everything — the codes, the theft, Sable's true name, the shape of every lie aboard the *Merrow* — and he had stood at the stern rail in the gray morning and saluted our walking, and he had not followed, because he was already gone. He had checked out of the world the way he had checked into it: under a name that belonged to someone else, in handwriting that fit any hand, leaving half a name and a smear in the book. The man who had spent a winter demanding that everyone aboard prove who they were had never once, I suspected, been able to prove it himself. His warrant was borrowed. His authority was a costume. Perhaps his face was the only true thing he owned, and perhaps not even that.

*Everyone in this story,* I thought, *was someone else's forgery.* The judge fleeing under a cousin's title. The twins sharing one identity between them like a coat. The singer whose sheet music was seditious and whose name, I'd have bet the burned codes, was on a warrant under a different spelling. Sable, erased. Me, invented. Maren, assembled in a room above a print shop. And the census taker, the great examiner, the man with the form that contained the question that wasn't on any form — a ghost auditing ghosts, a counterfeit sent to count counterfeits, and when the country he claimed to serve collapsed, he had simply signed out under a new name and walked into the fog, and no one would ever come looking, because there was no one left with the authority to recognize him.

It should have made me angry. Instead it made me dizzy, the way the ice had made me dizzy — that vast indifferent surface, holding or not holding, asking nothing except that you keep moving.

Maren touched my arm. "You heard."

"I heard."

"He could come back."

"He won't." I said it with more certainty than I felt, and then found, examining it, that I actually felt it. "Men like him don't return to finished ledgers. We're written off. All of us. The codes are ash, the ship is ice, the republic is a memory with bad handwriting." I looked at the register, at our two names sitting honest on the page for the first time in twenty years. "We're nobody's case file now. We're just people in a room with soup."

Maren exhaled — a long breath, twenty years long, the breath of a woman setting down a sack she had forgotten she was carrying. Then her eyes went past me, to the window, to the darkening south where the river lay.

"The boat," she said quietly. "It should have arrived by now."

I had been refusing to think about it for six hours, the way you refuse to look at a crack in the ice you're standing on. The lifeboat had left before us, with the current's help and oars and lighter cargo. It should have beaten two women on foot by hours. The customs house had records of arrivals; I turned to the clerk before I could stop myself.

"A lifeboat this morning," I said. "From the steamer *Merrow*. Four passengers. Was there a woman named Voss?"

The clerk consulted his book with the maddening calm of a man whose country had not just ended. "Boat came in at midday, yes. Three passengers." He looked up. "You said four?"

My stomach dropped through the floor, through the ice, into the black talking water. "There were four. An older man, a woman in a gray coat, and—"

"The woman in gray registered," the clerk said. "Voss, she wrote. First time I've seen anyone smile writing their own name." He turned the book toward me, and there it was in careful letters: *Sable Voss*, and beside it, in the column for origin, she had written not a district or a city but a single word: *restored.*

"And the fourth?" Maren's voice had gone thin.

"Older gentleman, the third passenger said. He helped push the boat off the ice, saw them clear, and stayed behind." The clerk shrugged. "Said the boat rode better light. Said he'd walk it, or wait for the ice road." He returned to his stamping. "People choose strange things out there. It's the ice. Makes everyone a philosopher."

An older gentleman. The judge — the judge who had been denounced and put off at the midriver stop, whom I had assumed lost to the interior, to the purge, to the machinery. Unless he had found his way back to the river. Unless he had been waiting at the launch point, stateless and patient as winter itself, and had given his berth—

No. There was no berth to give. There had been one berth, and Sable was in it, and the arithmetic was closed.

But the older man had pushed the boat free and stayed on the ice, and the ice was talking tonight, and the wind was rising out of the south, and somewhere between here and the dying ship there was a man walking north through the dark with no light but the ones we were standing in.

Maren read my face the way she had read it over ten years of fevers and silences. "Lior. No. You can barely stand."

"There's soup here," I said, already reaching for my coat — the light, empty, obscene coat. "There's a lamp in the window. And there's a man on the ice who gave up the last seat in the boat, and I know exactly how that feels, and I am not letting the river have him."

Outside, the wind had turned. It came off the water carrying the smell of open leads and the sound of the ice working — groaning, settling, deciding — and far down the black ribbon of the swept road, past the reach of the lamplight, I thought I could see movement. A figure. Walking north. Slowly.

Behind me, in the warm doorway, Maren called my name — my true one, the one I was only learning to wear.

I started south onto the ice to meet him.
