## 4. What the Victors Keep

Mara did not sleep.

She told herself it was the clock — that a clockmaker's ear could not rest while a mechanism ran wrong somewhere in the city, and there was always some mechanism running wrong in this city now, some bell out of true, some escapement worn past its tolerance by four years of nobody's caring. But the shop was silent except for the tick of the regulator on the wall, and the regulator was perfect, and still she lay in the narrow bed above the workroom with her eyes open, watching the ceiling go from black to grey to the color of old bone.

The letter was under her pillow. She had put it there the way other people put knives there.

*Dear Mara,* it began, in a hand she had known since she was six years old and Anton had taught her to make her letters with a ruler underneath, because he said a hand without discipline was a hand that lied. *If you are reading this, then someone found my papers, which means I am not where I planned to be.*

She must have read it forty times. She knew its creases now, the way the paper had been folded twice and carried long enough for the folds to soften into furrows, the way one corner was stained with something brown and circular — tea, perhaps, or boot polish, or blood dried thin. She knew the sentence about the papers. She knew the way he crossed his t's low, like a man hurrying, though Anton had never hurried in his life until the war taught him to.

What she did not know — what she could not make herself know, lying in the dark — was whether it was true.

Forgers existed. The war had made a whole industry of them, whole workshops of men who could give you a dead man's signature for the price of two eggs, who could age paper with smoke and break seals with steam. She had heard the stories like everyone else. And grief, she knew, was the softest wax in the world; anything pressed into it left a mark.

But there was one thing no forger could counterfeit, because no forger would ever think of it.

At dawn she got up, dressed, and went down to the workroom, where the light came grey through the north window — the best light, the honest light, the light her father had insisted on when he built the bench thirty years ago. She unlocked the drawer at the back, the one with the false front, and took out the tin.

Inside were the family letters. Not many. When the second requisition came, they had burned most of what might incriminate, but Mara had kept a handful the way a miser keeps coins: three letters from Anton to their mother, written from the garrison in '41 and '42 before he went east; one from him to her, all business, about winding the tower clocks in his absence; and one half-finished draft he'd abandoned on this very bench in '43, mid-sentence, when the knock came at the door and everything after that belonged to the war instead of to them.

She laid them out on the felt with the care she gave to movements worth restoring. Then she set Tomas's letter beside them, weighted flat with a brass weight, and went to find her loupe.

The workroom smelled of oil and cold iron and, faintly, of the coffee substitute she'd drunk too much of yesterday. Outside, the street was waking — the milk cart, someone sweeping cinders off the step opposite, a child coughing in the building behind. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city pretending.

Mara bent over the letters and became, for an hour, nothing but an instrument herself.

It was the same discipline as examining a movement. You did not look at the whole; you looked at the parts, and you let the parts tell you whether the whole could be real. A forged watch had a beautiful dial and a train wheel cut slightly wrong, because the forger copied what could be seen and guessed at what could not. So with handwriting. Anyone could copy a signature. Almost no one could copy the unconscious things — the pressure, the hesitation, the corrections a hand makes without the mind's permission.

She started with the letter m. Anton's m had a particular sin in it: the first arch always taller than the second, a flaw from learning to write against a slanted school desk, and he had never corrected it because correcting it would have meant admitting the desk had won. In the garrison letters — first arch tall. In the note to her about the tower clocks — first arch tall. In the draft, abandoned mid-word, the m of *mother* unfinished, the first arch tall even in the stroke he never completed.

In Tomas's letter, the m of *mother* — and the word appeared twice — the first arch stood tall as a steeple.

Her chest hurt. She realized she had stopped breathing and made herself start again.

She checked the crossings of the t's: low, urgent, the crossbar beginning inside the stem rather than outside it, a habit so lazy it circled back around to being distinctive. Present in all four. She checked the y, which Anton looped below the line and then, oddly, hooked backward at the tip, like a fishhook, like something caught. Present. She checked the spacing between words, which widened toward the ends of lines when he was tired or frightened — she had teased him about it once, called it his coward's spacing, and he had thrown a pencil at her — and in Tomas's letter the last lines drifted apart like men losing formation in fog.

Then she did the thing she had been avoiding. She held the draft letter — the half-finished one, the last thing he had written in this room, in this light, at this bench — up beside Tomas's letter, edge to edge, and looked not at the words but at the ink.

Two different inks. Of course. Years apart, different pens, different everything. But the same *hand*, the same weight of pressure, the same slight drag on the downstrokes where he bore too hard, the same habit of pressing so firmly on the final letter of a word that it dented the page from behind. She turned the draft over. The dents were there, tiny craters in the reverse, exactly where the last letters fell.

Tomas's letter had the same craters. She had not noticed them last night. No forger working by lamplight in a ruined city thought about the back of the page.

Mara set down the loupe very carefully, as if it might go off.

The morning light had strengthened. It lay across the bench and across the letters, and for a long moment she simply looked at them — the living writing and the dead writing, side by side, identical — and let the truth arrive the way a heavy gear seats onto its arbor: slowly, and then all at once, with a click she felt in her teeth.

Anton had been alive. After the date on the death certificate. After the grave they had not been allowed to visit, the form letter from the records office, the two years of mourning she had performed like a duty roster. He had sat down somewhere — in a cellar, in a barracks, in a ditch, she would never know which — and folded this paper twice, and thought of her, and written *if you are reading this* in the hand their father's ruler had trained.

He had been alive, and the record said otherwise, and the record was the thing everyone believed.

She did not cry. That surprised her. She had expected, all night, to cry. Instead she felt something colder and more durable settle into place, the way a mainspring feels when it takes its tension — not comfort, nothing like comfort, but purpose. A mechanism, once wound, does not weep. It works.

* * *

Tomas came at ten, as he had said he would, and waited outside the door until she opened it, which she noted — a man who understood that a woman alone in a workshop needed to see him coming.

"You've been at it all night," he said. It wasn't a question. His eyes went past her shoulder to the bench, to the letters laid out in their careful row, to the loupe still sitting where she'd set it down.

"I've been at it all night," she agreed. She stepped back to let him in, then didn't move from the doorway quite yet. "Before you come in. I want you to answer plainly. Where did you get this?"

"From a man who carried it eleven hundred kilometers," Tomas said. "A courier who worked the eastern route. He died in March. Fever, not bullets. He made me swear I'd find the sister."

"The sister." Her voice came out flat. "You mean me."

"I mean the sister Anton described," Tomas said, "who checks every claim twice and forgives nothing. He said if anyone could tell true paper from false, it was the girl who took apart his watch at nine years old and put it back together running two seconds fast on purpose, so he'd be early everywhere."

Mara stared at him. She had forgotten that. She had forgotten it so completely that hearing it now was like hearing a voice from a drowned room.

"He never told anyone that," she said.

"He told the people he trusted," Tomas said. "There were fewer of those than you'd hope."

She stepped aside and let him in.

* * *

They sat at the bench because there was nowhere else to sit, and Tomas drank the coffee substitute without comment, which raised him in her estimation more than any speech could have. The letters lay between them like evidence at a trial, which, she was beginning to understand, was exactly what they were.

"It's his," she said. "You know that already. You watched me prove it."

"I wanted you to prove it to yourself," Tomas said. "I could have told you about the ink on the reverse. I didn't. If you'd found it wanting, no argument of mine should have kept you."

"And if I had found it wanting? What then? You'd have walked out and found another sister to hunt down?"

"There is no other sister," Tomas said quietly. "That was the whole point of the letter."

She looked at him across the bench. He was maybe thirty, hard to say — war aged people unevenly, like damp getting into wood. His hands, she noticed again, were steady in the way of hands that had learned steadiness the expensive way. There was a scar through his left eyebrow, old and white. He held his cup in both palms as if warming them, though the room was not cold.

"Tell me the rest," she said. "Not the part about Anton. The part you came here to tell me. The part about why a stranger spends a week studying the clock in the observatory tower before he dares knock on a door."

Tomas set down the cup. For a moment he seemed to weigh something, and then he reached inside his coat and brought out a folded newspaper — not today's; the paper was weeks old, cheap stock gone yellow at the folds — and smoothed it open on the bench between the letters.

She read the notice. It was printed small, buried on the fourth page among the livestock reports and the announcements of restored tram service, the kind of notice designed to be legally present and practically invisible:

*BY ORDER OF THE ALLIED COMMISSION FOR HISTORICAL RECORDS: All persons holding documents, testimony, or materials relevant to the period 1939–1945 are invited to submit such materials to the Municipal Archive for verification and inclusion in the Official Record. Submissions will be received until the first day of December. After that date, unregistered materials may not be entered into evidence, and the Official Record shall be deemed complete and authoritative in all proceedings.*

"Deemed complete," Mara read aloud. "Authoritative."

"The victors keep what the victors keep," Tomas said. "That's the phrase going around. They're writing the history of the war right now, in this city, floor by floor of the archive — and they're doing it properly, with clerks and indexes and certified copies, which is what makes it dangerous. A lie shouted is a lie people argue with. A lie filed is a lie that becomes furniture. In twenty years no one will remember it was ever contested."

"And Anton's record says he's dead."

"Anton's record says he's dead," Tomas agreed. "Signed, dated, filed. And whoever signed it — and I have reason to believe I know who — is currently employed helping compile the Official Record. Which means the man who wrote your brother's death gets to write the version of events everyone else will have to live inside."

The word *whoever* landed strangely. She caught it — the slight care in it, the door left deliberately ajar. She set that aside. One truth at a time; that was how you repaired a movement, and apparently how you repaired a world.

"So the deadline," she said. "December the first."

"Five weeks after that notice was printed. Less, now." Tomas tapped the newspaper. "Anything not registered by then doesn't just get ignored. It gets *overwritten*. The sealed record becomes the only record. Your brother stays dead officially forever, and whatever he did in those two missing years — whatever he protected, whoever he saved, whatever crime somebody committed to make him disappear — none of it ever happened. That's what an amnesty deadline is. It isn't forgiveness. It's a shredder with a calendar."

Mara looked down at the letters on the bench. Anton's hand, tall-arched and urgent, drifting apart at the ends of lines. *If you are reading this, then someone found my papers.*

"He knew," she said slowly. "In the letter. He wrote — wait." She pulled it toward her and found the passage, third page, the crease nearly worn through. "'The record will lie about me. Records always learn to lie when the shooting stops. Find the ones who keep the true copies.'" She looked up. "He meant this. The archive. The deadline. How could he have known about a commission that didn't exist yet?"

"Because he'd seen it happen before," Tomas said. "After the last border change. Men who falsify once learn that falsification pays, and they arrange to be the falsifiers next time too. Anton understood the machinery. He was—" Tomas stopped himself, and this time the pause was longer, deliberate, a man setting down a heavy tool. "He was involved in things I can't speak about yet. Not because I don't trust you. Because trust is a currency and I haven't earned enough of it, and because there are people watching the people who ask questions, and the less you know tonight, the safer you sleep tonight."

"That's patronizing."

"Yes," Tomas said, without apology. "It's also true. Ask me again in a week, when you've decided whether you're in."

"In." She turned the word over. "In what, exactly? What is it you want from me? I repair clocks. I'm good at it. It's the one thing I'm good at."

For the first time, Tomas smiled — a small, crooked thing, quickly gone, like a bird landing and leaving. "You proved a dead man alive in one night with a jeweler's loupe and four old letters. You read the back of the page. Mara — do you understand what the Official Record actually is? It's paper. Thousands upon thousands of pages, certificates and chime logs and transport manifests and death registers, all of it produced by clerks, stamped by machines, dated by systems. And every system has a seam. Every register has a gap. Every official timeline can be tested against something mechanical that couldn't be bribed — a clock, a logbook, a bell that rang or didn't ring at a certain hour." He leaned forward. "I need someone who knows how institutions keep time. Someone who can look at an official record and tell me, the way you told yourself this morning, whether the parts could possibly be real."

The workroom was quiet. The regulator ticked on the wall, patient, indifferent, keeping its honest seconds over their heads.

Mara thought about the grave they hadn't been allowed to visit. About the form letter. About two years of setting a place at the table and then learning not to. About the specific, engineered loneliness of mourning a man the state insisted had died, in a way the state refused to specify, on a date the state had chosen.

And underneath the grief, she felt it — unmistakable, alarming, alive. Purpose, kindling inside fear like a flame inside a lantern. Fear enough to shake her hands; purpose enough to hold them steady anyway. Her father had had a word for that balance. He'd called it *torque*. The force that turns.

"If I say yes," she said, "I do it my way. We verify everything. Nothing goes forward on faith — not a name, not a date, not a signature, not yours. If we're going to fight a filed lie, we file the truth so clean that no clerk alive can refuse it. Do you understand?"

"I understand," Tomas said. "That's why I came to you first."

"Not me first," Mara said. "Him first. I was just the address on the envelope." She gathered the letters together, squared their edges, and slid them back into the tin — all but Tomas's letter, which she left on the bench, open, in the honest northern light. "There's a municipal archive four streets from here. Somebody processed Anton's death certificate. Somebody stamped it, indexed it, shelved it. Clerks remember what officials forget — it's the only power they have, and they hoard it."

Something moved behind Tomas's eyes — caution, or respect, or both.

"I know a clerk," he said carefully. "Halina. Archive registry, fourteen years. She owes nothing to anyone, least of all to the likes of us, and she'll tell you so in language that would blister paint."

"Good," said Mara, and heard her own voice come out harder than she'd meant it to, and did not correct it. "I've spent two years being polite to a piece of paper that says my brother is dead. I'm finished being polite. Fetch your coat, Mr. Tomas. The archive opens at eight, and I want to be standing in front of her desk when it does."
