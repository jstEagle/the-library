## 9. The Officer's Signature

The photograph arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside a newspaper that Halina had bought for the obituaries and kept for everything else.

Mara spread it flat on Ferenc's worktable, between the tray of ten-point type and the cold teapot that had become as permanent a fixture of the print shop as the press itself. The paper was cheap newsprint, gray and soft, but the image printed upon it was crisp enough to wound: a row of officers on a reviewing stand, bemedaled, chins lifted against the winter light, and beneath them a caption in the victors' proud black lettering.

*Captain Emeric Voss receives the Order of Merit, Second Class, for services to civil administration.*

"That's him," Tomas said quietly.

He was standing at Mara's shoulder, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him through her coat, though neither of them had acknowledged it in words. She did not look up at him. She looked at the face.

She had expected — she realized only now how much she had expected it — a monster. Something coarsened, heavy-jawed, with cruelty written plainly across the features so that any child in the street might point and know. That was what the pamphlets promised, what the trials had taught the city to look for: evil as a visible thing, legible at a glance.

The man in the photograph was none of that. He was perhaps forty-five, narrow through the shoulders, with a long, thoughtful face and hair swept back from a high forehead. He wore his decorations the way a scholar wore reading glasses — as instruments, not ornaments. His mouth was set in something that was almost a smile, the smile of a man listening patiently to a joke he has already heard and found adequate. And his eyes, even reproduced in halftone dots on newsprint, even at this distance, even through the grain — his eyes were looking slightly off-camera, at something or someone just outside the frame, with an expression of mild, professional interest.

The expression of a man taking notes.

"He looks like a schoolmaster," Mara said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "He looks like the man who taught me fractions."

"He signed forty-one death certificates," Halina said from the doorway, where she stood with her coat still buttoned to the throat, as if the print shop were an office she might be summoned out of at any moment. "I processed thirty-eight of them myself before I stopped counting. So yes. Let us agree he looks like a schoolmaster. It will make it easier to remember that we are not dealing with a wolf."

* * *

They had come to the name by way of ink.

It was Halina who had made the connection, three nights earlier, working by lamplight at the far end of the table while Ferenc set pages and Mara cleaned the press rollers and Tomas read aloud from the stack of photostats they had accumulated like a slow snowfall. Halina had been a records clerk for nineteen years; she knew signatures the way Mara knew escapements, by their weight and their wear, by the small involuntary habits no forger thinks to copy.

"Look at the V," she had said, tapping one certificate and then another. "Everyone else makes the V in two strokes. He makes it in one, down and back up, like a checkmark. And see — after the final *s*, he always lifts the pen and adds a period. A period! As if the name were a sentence. *Voss.* Full stop. A man who ends things."

Forty-one certificates. All bearing the same single-stroke V, the same terminal period. All dated to nights when the tower above the city had recorded nothing — no raid, no alarm, no bells beyond the ordinary hours. Mara had verified it herself against the master clock's chime log, that falsified ledger she had found stitched into the mechanism's own history, and the silence of those nights rang in her now louder than any bell.

But a signature on a death certificate was a thread. They needed the cloth. And so Ferenc had spent two evenings at the public reading room, hat pulled low, copying by hand the columns of the official gazette — promotions, commendations, appointments — until he found the cloth entire.

Now he laid the gazette page beside the photograph, smoothing it with both palms like a man laying out a shroud.

"Read it," he said. "All of it. I want you to hear the whole architecture."

Mara read.

*Captain Emeric Voss, Order of Merit, Second Class, is appointed Deputy Director of the Historical Commission, with responsibility for the compilation of the definitive record of the occupation years. Captain Voss brings to this task eleven years of distinguished administrative service, including oversight of civilian registration and vital statistics for the eastern districts.*

She read it twice. The second time was not because the words were difficult. It was because her mind refused, on the first pass, to accept their meaning.

"The definitive record," she said.

"The official history," Ferenc said. "The one that will sit in every library, every school, every courtroom, for as long as anyone alive can read. The one against which all other accounts will be measured and found wanting." He took off his spectacles and pressed the bridge of his nose. "I sat on a bench for twenty-two years, Miss Ravensworth. I learned that history is not what happened. History is what survives, written by whoever is left holding the pen. And this man" — he tapped the photograph, gently, almost respectfully — "is being handed the pen. Formally. With a ribbon on it."

"And Anton's certificates," Mara said slowly, "the ones he signed — those are in the archive he now controls."

"They are in the archive he always controlled," Halina said. "He signed them as an officer of registration. Now he signs the history of the registration. Do you understand what that means? When the amnesty deadline passes and the sealed records are closed, there will be no contradiction anywhere in the official paper. The deaths will say what he said they said. The nights will have contained whatever he decides they contained. And your brother—" She stopped. For once, the bitter old clerk had run up against the edge of her own bluntness, and she looked away toward the dark window. "Your brother will have died on a night that never happened, killed by no one, in a raid that the clocks themselves do not remember. Because he will have written it so. Neatly. With a period after his own name."

* * *

Mara did not sleep that night. She lay in the narrow room behind the print shop — Ferenc had insisted, after the second visit to Halina's office, that they stop going home by predictable routes — and she listened to the building settle around her and thought about gears.

Her father had taught her that every machine tells the truth about its maker. A clock built by a careless hand runs carelessly; you can feel it in the fit of the pivots, hear it in the beat. But a clock built by a careful hand in service of a lie — that was the rarest and worst thing. That was a machine that ran true and pointed false. That was the chime log with its forged entries, stitched into the tower's own memory. That was, she understood now, staring at the ceiling beams, a man named Emeric Voss.

He was not careless. That was the thing she kept returning to, turning over like a watch glass. The single-stroke V, the terminal period, forty-one documents executed with perfect consistency — this was a craftsman. Somewhere in the machinery of the state there had been a place for men like him, men who understood that terror was inefficient and paperwork was eternal, and they had promoted him, decorated him, and now given him the pen.

And she had spent two years hating a shadow. Grief, she thought, is a kind of darkness, and in darkness anything can be imagined. But the photograph had done what photographs do. It had let light in. And the face that emerged was worse than any monster, because it was a face she might have passed on the street without a second glance — a face that would smile at her across a counter, sign her ration card, wish her good morning.

* * *

In the morning, Tomas was already at the table when she came down, and he had arranged the materials in a line: the photograph, the gazette page, the photostats of the certificates, and — apart from the rest, at the edge, as if it did not quite belong among them — the letter in Anton's handwriting.

"We should assume he knows about the letters," Tomas said, without preamble. He had the particular stillness he got when he had been awake all night, a stillness like deep water. "Voss. If he signed the death certificates, then either Anton escaped his custody or Anton was never in it. Either way, there are loose ends, and a man who writes histories for a living does not tolerate loose ends. He will have been looking for those pages since 1944."

"Then why haven't they surfaced?" Mara asked. "If he's had two years—"

"Because paper hides well, and because he didn't know what he was looking for until recently. Think about it. The commission is new. The deadline is new. Before the amnesty process began, the sealed files were just files — dangerous, but inert. Now they are evidence. Now every document in that archive is either going to confirm his version or contradict it, and he has five weeks to make sure of which." Tomas turned the photograph toward her. "We are not hiding from a policeman anymore, Mara. We are racing a historian. And he has staff."

Ferenc came in with bread and the morning's suspicion on his face. "There's more," he said. "I went past the commission offices on my way. There's a notice posted. Public submissions to the historical record are invited — invited, mind you, reviewed by the commission for accuracy and relevance." He smiled without any humor in it. "Do you see? He has built the trap into the front door. Anyone with evidence walks it directly into his hands, and it disappears into a review process, and the review finds it inaccurate. Or the person who submitted it becomes a question to be answered. Either way, the pen stays where it is."

"So we don't submit," Mara said.

"So we don't submit *through the front door*," Ferenc corrected. "Which means authentication, which means witnesses willing to stand behind every page, which means—" He gestured at the whole impossible arrangement of papers on the table, the whole impossible enterprise they had assembled around a dead man's handwriting. "Everything we discussed. Only now with a face on the other side of it."

Halina arrived last, breathless from the stairs, and she brought the morning's news with her like weather. "He's giving a lecture," she said. "Thursday week. The Historical Institute. Open to the public — 'Sources and Method in the Reconstruction of Civil Records.' My successor at the archive says the hall seats four hundred." She dropped into her chair and looked around the table, and Mara saw something in her face she had not seen before: not bitterness, not guilt, but a hard, bright anger, banked like a coal. "Four hundred people are going to sit in a room and listen to the man who murdered your brother explain how history is properly written. And every one of them will applaud."

* * *

That afternoon, Mara went alone to the observatory.

She told herself it was to check the master clock's strike train, which had been running a half-second heavy on the hour since the frost, and this was true, but it was not the whole truth. She needed to stand in the place where the truth was mechanical. Up here, in the smell of oil and cold stone, a lie had a weight you could measure. Down in the city, among proclamations and commissions and decorated captains, lies weighed nothing at all. They floated. They gathered honors.

She worked in silence for an hour, easing the strike governor, wiping frost condensation from the great wheels, and the familiar labor steadied her the way it always had. Then she climbed to the gallery window with the logbook under her arm and looked out over the rooftops, eastward, toward the district where the Historical Commission had taken over the old tax offices.

From here she could see its roofline. Somewhere behind those windows, a man with a schoolmaster's face was assembling the years of her life into an official shape. He had decided that her brother died on a night with no bells. He had written it down, neatly, with a period. And in five weeks' time, unless something intervened, that sentence would become the only sentence there was.

Mara opened the logbook to a fresh page and wrote, in her small precise hand, the date, the time, the state of the mechanism, and the correction applied. Honest entry, honest book. It was such a small thing. It was the only kind of thing she knew how to do.

When she came down, Tomas was waiting at the tower door, stamping the cold from his boots, and she saw at once from his face that something had happened.

"Halina sent me," he said. "A request came through the archive this morning. Official channel, commission letterhead." He held out a slip of paper, and she read it by the gray light of the doorway.

*By order of the Historical Commission: all chime logs and timekeeping registers for the municipal observatory, 1943–1946, are to be transferred to commission custody for review. Acknowledge receipt within seven days.*

Below the typed text, in blue-black ink, a signature. One stroke for the V. A period after the final *s*.

"He knows," Mara said. Her voice sounded very calm to her, the way the workshop went quiet just before something breaks. "He's pulling the logs. He's erasing the nights."

"He's starting with the clocks," Tomas said. "With your clocks, Mara. Which means he knows someone has been reading them."

She looked at the slip of paper for a long moment. Then she folded it along its crease and put it inside her coat, over her heart, where she kept the letters.

"Then we'd better decide quickly," she said, "what we're willing to do about Captain Voss — because Thursday week, he'll be standing in front of four hundred people, telling them how the story goes. And I think one of us should be sitting in that hall, watching his face when he says my brother's name."
