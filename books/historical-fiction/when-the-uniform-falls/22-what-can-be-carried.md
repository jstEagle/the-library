## 22. What Can Be Carried

The letter came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to anyone but Mara, and everything.

Tuesdays were the day she wound the master clock. She had kept the observatory's great mechanism for eleven years now — first as the apprentice who had been summoned that winter of 1946 to fix what the war had left broken, then as the woman who simply never left. The city had rebuilt itself around her in increments: the tram lines relaid, the burned quarter raised again in pale new stone that did not yet match the old, the archive hall re-roofed and reopened under a director who had been a junior clerk in Halina's reading room back when Halina still had a post to lose. Time had done what time does. It had not healed anything so much as it had worn the sharp edges off the grief, the way a gear tooth wears smooth — still turning, still bearing its load, but no longer cutting the hand that touched it.

She was at her bench on the ground floor of the tower when the postman's knock came, two soft raps against the doorframe because the door itself had swollen shut years ago and no one, least of all Mara, saw any reason to plane it down. Some things you let be crooked. They held their shape that way.

"Sign here," said the postman, though he never waited for the signature; he set the board on the bench among her files and screwdrivers and looked out the window at the gray morning while she wrote her name in the little box, and then he was gone, boots ringing on the cobbles, and the letter lay there among the tools like something dropped from another altitude entirely.

It was a thin envelope. Cheap paper, the kind sold in border towns where currency changed hands twice between breakfast and supper. The stamp was foreign — she did not recognize the crest at first, and then she did, and her hand went very still above it. A country three borders away. A country a man might reach if he walked out of custody in one direction for long enough and let the roads decide.

And the address. The address was in a steady hand.

Mara had spent eleven years learning every way a hand could tremble. She had watched widows sign death registers, watched old men press their seals into wax with fingers that shook like leaves, watched her own reflection write letters she never sent to an empty chair across the table. But this handwriting did not tremble. It never had. Even in the pages Tomas had carried through checkpoints and safehouses and the long corridor beneath the records hall, even in the testimony he had signed knowing what the signing would cost him — the letters had come out level, unhurried, each stroke placed as deliberately as a pallet in a escapement. *Steady hands*, she had thought the first time she saw them, up in the tower, watching him study the mechanism not as a thief studies a lock but as someone checks whether time itself is being kept honestly. Steady hands. That was how she had known him before she knew his name.

She did not open the letter at once. That was the discipline of her trade, and her trade had taught her everything else in her life too: do not force the mechanism. Let it open at its own pace. She made tea instead, in the dented pot on the spirit burner, and sat by the window where the light came through leaded glass in diamonds, and drank half a cup while the envelope lay on the bench and said nothing, the way envelopes do.

Then she opened it.

*Dear Mara.*

Two words, and her eyes blurred so completely she had to set the page down on her knee and look at the ceiling beams until the world resolved again. Eleven years. Eleven years since the bells rang over the records hall and the register sealed with his name inside it and hers outside it, since Voss's career cracked along the fault line they had cut into it and Ferenc walked free into a spring morning blinking like a man born twice, since Tomas disappeared — first into custody, then into the machinery of exile that moved men across borders the way water moves silt, slowly, invisibly, leaving no record anyone could follow. She had written to the addresses Ferenc passed along in his careful, lawyerly way. The letters had gone out and nothing had ever come back, and after some years she had stopped writing and started simply keeping the watch.

His watch. She wore it now, as she had worn it every day since the amnesty deadline passed and the truth entered the permanent register in ink that could not be lawfully erased. It ticked against her wrist even as she read, small and faithful, a second heartbeat she had agreed to keep alive on behalf of a man who might or might not still be alive himself. She had told herself for eleven years that keeping it running was duty. She had known for eleven years that it was something else.

*Dear Mara. I will not waste the page on apologies, because you would only say I should have spent it on news instead.*

Her breath caught — a laugh, actually, escaping sideways through the grief, because that was exactly what she would have said, and he knew it, and somewhere across three borders he had sat down with cheap paper and heard her voice saying it.

*I am well. That is the news, and I know you will want it plainly, so: I am well. I have work. There is a town here that smells of resin and rain, and a workshop that repairs the church clocks for forty kilometers around, and the old man who ran it needed someone with patience and died last winter leaving it to me, which I suspect was less affection than exhaustion, but I have not argued with the inheritance. The clocks are old and stubborn and lie about the hour the way all old things do, and I spend my days persuading them toward honesty. You would laugh at my methods. Or you would correct them. Both, probably.*

Mara pressed the page flat against her knee and read that paragraph three times. *Persuading them toward honesty.* Eleven years ago a man had stood in her tower and asked odd questions about the archive below, and she had wondered what kind of stranger checked time itself for lies, and the answer had turned out to be: this kind. This kind always.

*Ferenc writes to me. You know this, because I know he writes to you, because Ferenc has never met a piece of correspondence he did not immediately duplicate and distribute. He sends me your chime logs. Do not be alarmed — he sends them redacted, the way he does everything, out of habit more than necessity. I read them the way other men read scripture. I know the third bell runs four seconds slow on damp mornings and that you refuse to adjust it because the adjustment would be dishonest to the mechanism's true nature. I know you took an apprentice last autumn, a girl named Wren who breaks pivots and apologizes to the gears. I know the city keeps an honest record now. I know because we built it, and because you keep winding it.*

She looked up at the ceiling again. The beams were oak, black with age, and somewhere above them the great clock turned through its patient circles, and somewhere above that the bell frame waited, silent until noon, and she understood suddenly that she was crying not from sorrow but from the sheer unbearable weight of being known across that distance — known in particulars, in pivot-breaks and slow bells, known the way only a person who had shared a table of tea and fear in a print shop could know another person.

There was more.

*Halina's pension came through, Ferenc tells me. Backdated. The state that fired her has decided, in its leisurely way, that she was owed thirty years of dignity, and she has informed the ministry in writing that she will accept the money but not the apology, and that the apology may be filed with the other documents they once falsified. I laughed until the neighbors knocked. Tell her — no. I will tell her myself, when I write next, which will be soon, because this letter has reminded me that paper crosses borders faster than men do, and that I have been letting the faster road go unused.*

*One more thing, and then I will let you get back to your clocks.*

*I am coming home in the spring.*

Mara read the sentence the way she tested a hairspring: once for the obvious reading, once for the hidden flaw, once more just to be certain the thing was true. *I am coming home in the spring.* No conditions. No *if the permits come through*, no *if the amnesty holds*, no *if Voss's ghost has finally stopped rattling its chains in the ministries*. Just the sentence, plain as a chime log, signed with initials she had traced with her fingertip a thousand times in memory — the same initials that had signed the certification, the same hand that had taken the fall so the testimony could stand, the same man who had walked into custody on a Tuesday morning — she remembered it was a Tuesday, she had always remembered it was a Tuesday — and looked back once at the tower before the car door closed.

She folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, because precision was the only prayer she knew, and put it inside her shirt, against her sternum, where it rose and fell with her breathing. Then she stood, and took down her coat, and climbed the stairs.

* * *

The tower stairs were two hundred and six steps. She had counted them during the worst year, the year after the sealing, when counting was the only thing that kept her moving, and she had never stopped counting, though the need had long since drained out of the habit. Two hundred and six steps, and at the top the machine room opened around her like the interior of some vast and benevolent animal: brass and iron and oiled steel, the pendulum swinging in its long grave arc, the weights descending on their cables with the patience of glaciers, the whole apparatus breathing time into the city below through wires and rods and the great bronze throats of the bells.

She went first to the winding crank. It was ritual older than her tenure — the weights had to be raised every Tuesday, and though the city had offered, twice, to install an electric motor, she had refused both times with a courtesy that fooled no one. Some things should cost effort. Effort was how a thing stayed honest. She set her shoulder to the crank and began to turn, and the cable sang softly through its guides, and the great weight rose inch by inch out of the cellar dark, storing up hours the way the earth stores rain.

While she worked, she talked. She had talked to the clock for eleven years; it was the only conversation partner whose attention never wavered.

"He's coming home," she told it. "In the spring. Before the thaw finishes, probably, knowing him — he'll want to arrive while the roads are still bad enough that no one official bothers checking them." The crank turned. The weight rose. "He says the third bell runs slow on damp mornings. He reads Ferenc's redacted copies of my logs. Can you imagine — a man in a border town, reading chime logs like scripture." Her voice caught and steadied, caught and steadied, the way a governor regulates a runaway train. "I used to think I kept this clock for the city. Then I thought I kept it for Anton, because he asked whoever found his papers to finish the record, and the record lives in this tower as much as anywhere — the honest hour ringing out over the honest archive. But that's not true either, is it."

The weight reached the top of its travel. She locked the ratchet and stood breathing in the smell of oil and cold stone and bronze dust, her palms burning pleasantly from the crank.

"I keep it for him," she said. "I've always kept it for him. And now he's coming back, and I don't know what to do with a hope that size. It doesn't fit in a pocket. It doesn't wind down neatly. It just sits in the chest like a mainspring under too much tension, and I'm afraid of what happens when it lets go."

The pendulum swung. The escapement ticked its ancient arithmetic. The clock, being a clock, offered no comfort and no judgment, only continuity — and somehow that was the right answer, the only answer. Things endure. Things keep going. You put your shoulder to the crank on Tuesdays and you trust the mechanism, and the mechanism, if it is built well and maintained honestly, does not fail you.

She checked the train of wheels, as she always did, running her loupe along the teeth for wear. She oiled the pivot points with the fine needle bottle, three drops where three drops belonged and nowhere else. She adjusted the rate nut a quarter turn — the clock had been gaining two seconds a day since the cold snap, and honesty required correction, even when the error ran in the flattering direction. Then she climbed the last ladder to the bell chamber and stood beneath the twelve bronze shapes hanging in their frame, big as drowned gods, waiting.

Noon was nine minutes off. She sat on the maintenance ledge, the one Tomas had sat on eleven years ago while they planned the infiltration of the records hall, and she took out the letter and read it again, all of it, including the part at the bottom she had not yet let herself read properly — the postscript, four lines in that steady hand:

*P.S. I still have your brother's watch chain. I know you have the watch. When I come, we will decide together what belongs to whom, though I already know the answer, and I suspect you do too: it belongs to the record, and the record belongs to everyone, and we belong to each other. Forgive an old exile his sentiment. The clocks have made me soft.*

Below her, through the louvers, the city spread out in its patched and mended glory. The pale new stone of the rebuilt quarter had weathered now to something closer to the old color. Trams rang their small bells along streets named, some of them, for people on the register — she could see the corner of Halina Street from here, and the print shop that was now a museum with Ferenc's old press in the window, preserved under glass, still inked with the last true document it ever ran. Children crossed the square below without looking up, the way children do, taking for granted the honest hour, the open archive, the sealed register that any citizen could petition to read. They would never know what the truth had cost. That was the point. That had always been the point: to build a world where the truth was cheap, because someone had already paid for it.

Anton's chair was still empty. It would always be empty; that was a debt no spring could repay and no register could amend. But the second letter had said *finish the record*, and the record was finished, and filed, and read by schoolchildren now, and somewhere in its margins his name appeared not as a casualty of a raid that never happened but as a witness who had gone underground to protect others — which was the truth, and the truth, at last, was the only monument the city erected anymore.

The minute hand climbed. Mara wound the watch on her wrist — his watch, their watch, the watch that had outlasted custody and exile and eleven years of Tuesdays — and set it against the master clock's dial, and the two times agreed to the second, because she had seen to it that they always would.

Then noon came.

She did not pull the release lever herself; she never did. She let the mechanism do what it was built to do, the way she had learned to let everything do what it was built to do — grief, hope, love, time. The trip engaged. The hammer fell. And the first bell swung out over the city, deep and bronze and unmistakable, followed by the second, and the third — four seconds slow on the damp air, honestly slow, faithfully slow — and the fourth and fifth and sixth, until all twelve were rolling together across the rooftops and down into the streets, over the trams and the market stalls and the archive hall and the museum window and the schoolyard where children who had never known a falsified chime log paused, briefly, out of some instinct older than memory, to count the strokes.

Mara stood in the bell chamber with her hands over her ears and her heart wide open, and let the sound go through her the way it went through everything — leveling, unhurried, true.

Spring, he had written. She counted forward from this Tuesday: fourteen weeks, give or take, until the thaw. Fourteen weeks of winding and oiling and correcting the rate, of teaching Wren to apologize less to the gears and respect them more, of drinking tea by the diamond-paned window with a letter in her shirt and a hope too large for any pocket.

Fourteen weeks. She had waited eleven years. She could wait one spring.

But as the twelfth bell faded and the tower settled back into its ticking silence, Mara found she was already planning the descent — two hundred and six steps, and then the stationer's, and then good paper, the best in the city, because some answers deserved better than cheap stock. *Come by the north road,* she would write. *The third bell runs slow on damp mornings, but I'll be listening for you anyway. I've always been listening.*

She started down the stairs, and behind her the master clock kept the time, faithfully, for a city that had learned to keep it honestly — and for a woman climbing toward the light, carrying, at last, only what could be carried: a letter, a watch, a name, and the spring.
