## 9. Hesk Counts Twice

I have been accused of many things in my thirty-one years with the Meridian Trading Company, most of them by men who owed me money, and I have learned to wear accusations the way a dockhand wears tar—let them dry, let them harden, let them become part of the uniform. But there is one charge no one has ever laid against me that I would confess to freely, if anyone thought to ask: I count twice.

Once for the ledger. Once for the truth.

The first count is what they pay me for. The second is what keeps me employed, because a man who counts only once is a clerk, and a man who counts twice is a warehouse master, and a man who counts three times is either a director or a criminal, and in Vhalen the distinction is largely a matter of tailoring.

It was on the fourth of Deepwinter, during the pre-audit inventory, that my second count turned up something my first had missed. A fiber. Grey wool, coarse-spun, dyed with the cheap madder that laundresses use on company blankets—the kind issued to night watchmen, dock crews, and other persons whose comfort the company budgets at roughly nothing per annum. It had lodged in the hasp of the east door of Counting Room Three, wedged where the latch meets the frame, which is not a place a fiber arrives by accident. Fibers arrive there when a blanket drags across a gap. Blankets drag across gaps when someone carries something wrapped in one through a door that should have been shut.

I stood in that doorway for a long while, holding the fiber between thumb and forefinger like a relic, and I will admit—I felt something adjacent to pleasure. Not joy. Joy is for weddings and dividends. But the particular satisfaction of a man who has suspected the room was lying to him and has finally caught it at it.

Because here is what I knew already: on the third of Deepwinter, the pearl tallies were off. Off by nothing—a rounding error, a clerk's yawn, four carats of discrepancy across eleven thousand recorded. Any auditor in the city would wave it through. But I am not any auditor in the city. I am Brann Hesk, and I have spent nine years learning that the Meridian Company does not lose pearls. Pearls are small, portable, and worth more than the people who handle them, which means that every soul in this warehouse has a reason to steal them and every procedure exists because somebody once did. Four carats missing is not an error. Four carats missing is a test. Someone was checking whether we counted.

And now a blanket fiber in a door hasp, and the night watch roster from the third pinned to my board, and six names on it.

Six names. I read them the way I read everything—twice.

* * *

You must understand something about the night watch before you understand what I did next, and that something is this: the night watch is where the company puts people it has stopped looking at. The day shift gets the ambitious, the connected, the ones with relatives on the docks or debts to the right creditors. The night shift gets the rest—the old, the slow, the orphaned, the indentured. They walk the same corridors in the dark that the clerks walk in the light, and they are paid to see nothing, and mostly they honor the contract.

Which is precisely why anything interesting happens on their rounds. Smugglers do not fear the day clerks; they fear being *noticed*, and nobody notices the night. If someone moved a bundle through Counting Room Three at two in the morning, wrapped in a watch blanket, then either a watchman did it or a watchman let it happen, and either way the answer was in those six names.

I took the roster to my office and spread it flat under the lamp. Tam Vetch, fourteen, indentured, talkative, forgettable—no, not forgettable, I corrected myself, *remembered as forgettable*, which is a different thing and a useful one. Old Semy, sixty if a day, knees like rusted hinges, couldn't carry a bundle of blankets across a room without resting. The Durov twins, who I'd stake my pension stole candle-ends but never pearls, lacking the imagination. Pell's cousin's boy, temporary, gone in a week. And Lior Quintana.

Quintana. Sixteen. Pearl-diver's daughter, indentured since the drowning, which made her company property in the way a mooring post is company property—useful, load-bearing, and assumed to have no interior life. She tallied pearls better than any clerk on the day shift. She had been doing it since she was small enough that the counting stool required two books stacked beneath her. She never asked for anything. She never missed a shift. In nine years she had given me no cause whatsoever to think about her, and I want to be honest with you, since honesty costs me nothing here: I had filed her, mentally, under *furniture*.

But furniture does not get fibers in door hasps, and so I did what I always do when the furniture surprises me. I sat down, and I planned the interview the way a surgeon plans a cut—not where to strike, but where *not* to.

* * *

I conducted it in the small counting room off the east corridor, the one with the high window and the single chair, because architecture does half the work of interrogation and I see no reason to pay full price. I brought Quintana in at the end of her shift, when the tiredness had settled into her the way river damp settles into stone, and I set before her the roster page and, beside it, the fiber on a square of white paper.

She looked at the fiber for perhaps two seconds. I know because I counted—I told you, twice—and in those two seconds I watched her face do something I had never seen it do.

Nothing.

Now, you may think nothing is unremarkable. Nothing is the most remarkable thing a face can do. A guilty innocent flushes. An innocent innocent frowns, puzzled, maybe offended. A frightened person swallows, glances at the door, calculates distances. What Quintana did was go still—not frozen, not blank, but *still*, the way water goes still over something deep, and I thought: there it is. There is the interior life. It has been down there the whole time, under the lamp-light and the ledgers, and I have been counting pearls on top of it for nine years.

"Do you know what this is?" I asked her.

"A fiber," she said. Her voice was level. Leveler than mine would have been, and I resented that a little.

"It's a company blanket fiber. Madder-dyed, grey wool, night-issue. Found in the hasp of Counting Room Three, east door, which was found unlatched on the morning of the fourth."

"Was it." Not a question. A stone dropped down a well, waiting for the splash.

"You were on watch that night."

"I'm on watch most nights."

"And the door was latched on your rounds?"

Something moved behind her eyes then—fast, a fish under ice—and I want to tell you that I saw it because I'm clever, but the truth is I saw it because she wanted me to see something, and I have never entirely ruled out that what she showed me was itself the lie. That is the trouble with counting twice: eventually you suspect even your own arithmetic.

"Yes," she said. "Latched."

"Latched," I repeated. "And yet a blanket fiber from your issue—" I let it hang. Hanging things do more work than thrown things; a thrown accusation can be dodged, but a hanging one just swings there, getting heavier, until the accused reaches up and takes it down themselves out of sheer nerves.

She didn't reach. She looked at the fiber again, and then—and this is the part I have replayed perhaps forty times since—she said, quietly, "That could have come off any blanket. We all carry the same issue. Half the dock crew sleeps in theirs."

True. Completely true. Which is why it was such a good lie, or such a good truth, and I stood there in my own counting room unable to tell which I was holding, and I will confess to you that the sensation was not unpleasant. There is a sickness to deception, they say, a thrill and a shame. I wouldn't know; I've never lied well myself, which is why I built my career on paper, which lies beautifully and never trembles. But watching her lie—or not lie—I recognized the craft of it, the economy. No excess denial. No outrage. Just enough true fact deployed to make the false conclusion comfortable.

Where does a sixteen-year-old learn that?

From nowhere good, is where.

"The tallies were short on the third," I said. "Four carats."

"Rounding," she said. "Eleven thousand lots. It happens."

"It doesn't," I said. "Not here. Not to me."

She met my eyes then, and hers were the flat grey of the river in January, and she said, "Then perhaps you should count again, sir."

*Perhaps you should count again.* Said without insolence, without fear, with the mild helpfulness of a clerk suggesting a correction, and I dismissed her—what else could I do? I had a fiber and a hunch and a face that had done nothing, and none of it would survive five minutes in front of an auditor, let alone a magistrate. She walked out of that room with her back straight and her hands quiet, and I sat down in the single chair and put my head briefly in my hands, which is a thing I do perhaps once a year and regret each time.

Because here is what shames me, and I record it now so that some future reader of these papers understands the caliber of the person I was up against: for one moment—one—one half-second—I believed her. Completely. The way you believe in a floor when you step onto it. And then the floor shifted, and I understood that whatever was happening in my warehouse, Lior Quintana was not merely standing on it. She was *standing somewhere else*, somewhere I hadn't found yet, and she had just invited me to keep counting.

So I did. It's what I'm for.

* * *

By evening I had pulled every record attached to her name, which took eleven minutes, because the file was thin as winter ice. Indenture papers, renewal stamps, conduct marks—all clean, all routine, all stamped and countersigned in the tidy chain of custody that makes a document trustworthy, and I sat there holding the thin folder and feeling the specific unease of a man whose scales balance too perfectly. Nine years. No illness longer than two days. No requests. No letters in or out. No wages drawn beyond the mandated scrip. A girl who existed on paper exactly as much as the company required and not one line more.

People leave smudges, Quintana. Everyone does. The absence of smudges is its own kind of evidence, and I wrote that sentence in my private book—the one I keep behind the third shelf, the one that will matter someday, though I didn't know it then—and under it I wrote: *Watch the provenance.*

Provenance. Where a thing comes from. It is the first lesson of pearls and the last lesson of people, and I had applied it to merchandise for nine years without once thinking to apply it to the girl who graded my merchandise. Her papers said Quintana, orphaned, diver's stock, bound to the company by the standard instruments. Fine. But who drew the instruments? Whose seal? Through whose hands? A fiber tells you a blanket passed a door. A file tells you a story was written. And I had just spent an hour listening to a girl tell a story too, and both stories had the same quality: smooth, plausible, and suspiciously free of fingerprints.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark of my office, listening to the warehouse settle around me—the groan of ice against pilings, the tick of cooling iron, the thousand small sounds of a building full of secrets pretending to sleep. Somewhere above me, on the watch rounds, a lantern bobbed along the east corridor. Past Counting Room Three. Past the hasp. Past the place where a blanket had dragged through a gap carrying something wrapped and warm, because warm was the word my mind kept returning to, warm, and I disliked it intensely. Contraband is not warm. Stolen pearls are not warm. Whatever went through that door wrapped in grey wool had needed keeping alive, and I have been in this trade long enough to know that the only cargo worth that risk, the only cargo that turns a thief careful and a liar calm, is a person.

I took out my private book again and wrote one more line beneath the others:

*Count the nights. Something came in that shouldn't be here. Find it before the directors do, or it will find us all.*

Then I closed the book, and I began, methodically, to plan how I would search the loft rooms, the laundry, the ice-houses, and every shadowed corner of the Meridian warehouse—starting tomorrow, starting at dawn, starting with the night watch roster and six names and one girl who had looked at me with river-grey eyes and told me, in perfect calm, to count again.

I intended to take her advice.

I only wish, for both our sakes, I had known then what the counting would cost.
