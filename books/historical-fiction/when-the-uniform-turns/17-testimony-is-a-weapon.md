## 17. Testimony Is a Weapon

I have spent thirty-one years in the service of paper, and I will tell you what I know about paper: it is obedient. Paper does what you tell it to do. Paper says a house drowned in debt because you wrote *drowned in debt*, and it says a girl was born indentured because someone with a steady hand and no conscience entered her into a ledger before she could walk. Paper is the most loyal servant in Vhalen. It never asks whose hand holds the pen.

That is also its horror, if you think about it too long. I try not to think about it too long. I have a job to do.

The loft above the cooperage smelled of oak shavings and lamp oil, and Lior Quintana sat at a barrel-head table with a stub of pencil and a sheet of paper she was holding the way a diver holds a knife—useful, familiar, but not to be trusted near the throat. The baby slept in a laundry basket lined with two blankets, which was one blanket more than the company had ever issued me, and I had worked there since before her parents were married. I try not to think about that either.

"You're writing it wrong," I said.

She looked up. She has her mother's way of looking at people—straight on, unblinking, as though the person being looked at owes her an accounting. Her mother did it to me once, nine years and one lifetime ago, on the dock, with river water in her hair. I gave the daughter a different answer than I gave the mother. The mother got silence. The daughter gets the truth, in installments, like a debt being paid off.

"There's no wrong way to write the truth," Lior said.

"There are at least nine wrong ways, and I've watched the company use six of them." I took the pencil from her hand because some lessons require confiscation. "Sit. Listen. If we're going to do this, we do it properly, and properly means you understand what a deposition actually is before you swear one."

"It's testimony. Under oath. On the record."

"It's a story with teeth," I said, "and the teeth only bite in one direction, and you need to know which direction before you lean over the animal."

She sat. Good. She's quick, this girl—quicker than the company ever credited, which is precisely why they never credited it. You don't promote the people who frighten you. You indenture them.

* * *

"Here is what the winter court is," I told her, pacing the length of the loft, because I think better in motion and I have been accused of lecturing, accurately. "The magistrate's session sits while the river is closed. No barges, no circuit judges, no appeals going upriver to the capital. Whatever gets entered into the record between first freeze and ice break is entered *here*, in Vhalen, by *this* court, and it stays in this court's books. That's our advantage. That's the whole game."

"And the ice breaks when?"

"When it breaks. The rivermen say three weeks, maybe four. The old ones read the ice the way I read a ledger—by the sound of it. When it starts to sing at night, it's near done." I turned at the window. Below, the frozen river lay white and flat and treacherous, the road that would kill us or save us depending entirely on timing. "So we have three weeks, possibly four, to put a story into the official record that the Meridian Trading Company cannot buy, burn, or outlive."

"You make it sound like a siege."

"Everything is a siege. Most people just don't notice they're inside the walls." I pulled the second chair over and sat across from her, close, because what came next I did not want the cooperage walls to hear, though they were thick enough and the cooper long gone home. "Now. The mechanics. A deposition is sworn before the magistrate or his clerk, entered into the session record, and—this is the part that matters—*sealed copies* are struck. Three copies. One stays with the court, one goes to the parties named, and one may be lodged with a notary of the depositor's choosing. Understand? Once it's sworn and copied, it exists in three places at once. The company can lean on one clerk. It cannot lean on three institutions in three weeks."

"Odile." She said my name the way she'd say *the ice is thinning*. "You've done this before."

"I've *filed* things before," I said, which was true in a way I did not intend to elaborate on while a child slept in a laundry basket. "The point is this: the record is not a monument. It's not a statue in the square that stands because it stands. It's a fortress, and a fortress is only as good as its garrison. You swear your testimony, and then you *defend* it—with the seal, with the locket, with witnesses, with whatever corroborating paper exists. And there is corroborating paper. I know, because I filed some of it."

"With the falsifications."

"With everything," I said. "The originals. Do you understand what I'm telling you? When the company seized House Aurelian, the documents that went into the court record were copies—amended copies. The originals went to the company vault because the company wrote them and the company kept them, that's how these things work, the victor holds the pen. But a clerk with a guilty conscience and an hour alone in the archive can do a great deal with a copying press." I let that sit. Her eyes went wide and then narrow, working it through. She is not a sentimental girl. She understood immediately that my confession was also an arsenal.

"You kept the originals."

"I kept *evidence*, which is a different word for the same thing, depending on which side of the inquiry you're standing on."

* * *

She wanted to write it all down at once, of course. That night, that hour, everything—the seizure, the drowned barges, the baby, the forged indenture, her parents, the seal, all of it in one great pour. I had to take the pencil away from her a second time, and she did not like it, and I did not care.

"Slowly," I said. "A deposition is not a confession and it is not a diary. It is a *legal instrument*. Every claim in it must be either a matter within the depositor's own knowledge or supported by an exhibit or a sworn witness. Do you know what happens to testimony that's all fire and no kindling?"

"It gets struck."

"It gets struck, and worse—it gets *used*. The company's advocates will take every unsupported claim and hold it up like a rotten plank to suggest the whole floor is rotten. One exaggeration and they'll say the entire deposition is the fantasy of a disgruntled indentured girl who stole a baby. So we build it like a ledger. Debit, credit. Claim, proof."

She rubbed her eyes. The lamp was low; we'd been at it two hours. The baby slept on, one fist curled against her cheek, and I noticed—I always notice, it's the clerk in me—that Lior had positioned the basket between herself and the door. Instinct. The same instinct that had carried a dying woman through a whiteout to this warehouse door, I supposed. People deliver their precious things to whoever will not drop them. I try not to think about what that says about who I have been.

"Tell me about the rewriting," Lior said suddenly. "You said the victors rewrite records afterward. How?"

"Ah." I sat back down. This was the part I most needed her to understand, and the part that would cost me the most to say, because I was not describing the company to her. I was describing myself. "In the ninth year of my service, I was given a correction task. A shipment of grain had been logged at the wrong weight—clerical error, genuinely, nothing sinister. And I was taught how to correct it. Do you know the method?"

"Cross it out and initial it?"

"Heavens, no. That leaves a scar anyone can read. No. The proper method is a *replacement page*. You re-copy the entire ledger folio with the correction incorporated, as though the error never existed, and the old page is withdrawn and destroyed, and the new page is certified by a second clerk who has no idea there was ever a first. The record looks seamless. The record looks like the truth always was what it now says." I turned my hands over, palms up, the gesture of a woman showing she holds nothing. "The Meridian Company has been correcting its ledgers for forty years. Whole folios. Whole *years*. There are men in that building whose entire function is to be the second clerk—the innocent signature that certifies the seamless page. I was almost one of them once. I was nearly promoted into innocence."

"And the court record? Can they do that to the court record?"

"The court record is harder. It's held by the magistrate's office, not the company, and the session books are sealed at adjournment and sent to the capital archive. But—" I held up one finger, "—*before* adjournment, while the session is open, the parties may file amendments, corrections, and supplementary depositions. And the company is a party to more proceedings than you can imagine. If they learn what you've filed, they will file a *response*, and the response will be beautifully drafted, and it will contain three affidavits from company men swearing the seal is a forgery and the child is a foundling of no estate, and the magistrate—who is not corrupt, I want to be clear, he is merely *tired*—will have before him one girl's word against a trading concern that has paid the city's harbor fees since before he was born."

"So we lose."

"So we make sure the record holds *both* stories. That's the victory condition, Lior. Listen to me. You are not going to win this the way a duel is won, one party standing and one falling. You win by making the lie *expensive*. Right now, the company's version of House Aurelian costs them nothing. It sits in the record like a smooth stone, no edges, nothing to catch. Your deposition puts edges on it. It means that from the day you file forward, every historian, every lawyer, every clerk who goes looking finds *two* accounts, sworn and sealed, in direct contradiction, with exhibits attached. The company will spend years and money smothering it, and some of it will smother, and some of it will not. Paper is patient. Paper outlives the men who wrote it. I am living proof of what it outlives." I heard my own voice go strange on that last sentence and stopped.

Lior was quiet a moment. Then: "You're saying we're not trying to win. We're trying to be *unreadable*."

"I'm saying we're trying to be *unerasable*. Unreadable they can manage—they've done it before. Unerasable is harder. That requires the seal, the locket, the witnesses, the notarized copy, and above all, the *timing*. It must be entered while the session is open. After the ice breaks, the court adjourns, the books seal, and the next session sits in the capital in the autumn—by which time the company will have had eight months to prepare its response, purchase its affidavits, and correct its folios. We file in Vhalen, in winter, while their directors are warm in their houses and their advocates are scattered. We file while it hurts them."

* * *

We worked until the candle was a thumb's width from death. I taught her the form: name and estate of the depositor—and here we struck the first real problem, because "estate" meant her status, and her status on paper was *indentured to the Meridian Trading Company*, and a depositor in service to a named party must disclose it, and disclosure would ring every bell in the company's counting rooms within the day.

"There's a provision," I said, and I had spent two nights confirming it in the statute book I am not supposed to remove from the office, "for depositions concerning one's own condition of servitude. Section forty-one. A person bound in indenture may depose regarding the validity of the indenture itself without prior notice to the master, *where the depositor alleges the instrument is defective*. It goes straight to the magistrate, sealed, heard at session. The company gets notice when the record opens, not before."

"You memorized the section number."

"I memorized the whole book once, out of boredom. Boredom is the most underrated form of preparation." This was a lie dressed as a joke. I had not memorized it out of boredom. I had memorized it nine years ago, at three in the morning, sitting in the archive with the Aurelian seizure folio open in front of me and my heart going like a hammer, looking for any provision, any clause, any crack in the wall through which the truth might be re-admitted someday. I had found section forty-one and I had copied it out and I had waited. Nine years. That is the other thing I know about paper: it waits better than people do. People break. Paper just sits there, holding its breath.

"Say it back to me," I said. "The order of filing."

"Deposition first, under section forty-one, alleging defective instrument. Exhibits: the indenture papers, the seal, the locket portrait. Then the supplementary deposition on the seizure and the drowning—supported by witness testimony, to be sworn by the witnesses themselves, not by me. Then the notarized copy lodged with—" she hesitated.

"With Notary Casimir Brandt, on Tanner's Row," I said. "He's expensive, he's sour, and he has never once, in twenty years, lost a document or sold one. He doesn't like the company. He doesn't like anyone. It's his finest quality."

"And the witnesses." She said it carefully. "The divers."

"The divers," I agreed. "Your deposition can say what you know. It cannot say what Yorick saw on the water nine years ago—that must come from Yorick's own mouth, sworn. And the more of them who swear it, the harder it is to dismiss as one old man's grievance. A community's testimony is a wall. One man's testimony is a nail, and nails get pulled."

"They won't all agree. Some of them won't want to be seen."

"Then they don't sign, and we build the wall with the stones we're given. That's the whole craft, Lior. Nobody gets every stone." I blew out the candle, because the stub was done and because some conversations are easier in the dark. In the blackness, the river made its faint cold sound against the ice, and the baby sighed in her sleep, a small wet sound, entirely unconcerned with statutes.

"One more thing," I said into the dark. "When you file, the magistrate will ask your name for the record. And you will give it. Lior Quintana, in the book, in your own hand. Do you understand what that means? Once your name is attached to this, it is attached *forever*. The company will know within the hour. Hesk will know. Whatever happens after—whether we win, whether we lose, whether the ice takes the whole courthouse—you will never be invisible again. There is a kind of safety in being no one. You've lived in it sixteen years. You're about to leave it."

I heard her breathe. I heard the basket creak as she leaned over it, and I knew without seeing that she had put her hand on the child, the way you check a mooring line in bad weather.

"I've been no one my whole life," she said. "It didn't keep her safe. It didn't keep my parents' names in any book at all. Being no one is just being erased slowly, on a schedule." A pause. "I'd rather erase myself *on purpose*."

And there it was—the thing I had been afraid of and counting on in equal measure. She had turned the weapon around and looked down its length without flinching. Strategy, at last, where panic had been. I have watched many people decide to tell the truth, and it never looks like courage in the moment. It looks like arithmetic. It looks like a tired girl in a cold loft concluding that the numbers only work one way.

"Then we draft it properly," I said, standing, my knees announcing their objections. "Three days. You write, I correct, we cut every word that isn't load-bearing. And Lior—" I paused at the door, my hand on the latch, because the last thing needed saying and I wanted to say it looking at the river and not at her. "The child. The magistrate will ask who has custody of the infant depositor—because that is what she is, the moment you file, a *depositor*, an interested party, and the court will want her produced. You'll have to bring her. Into the hall. Past whatever eyes the company has there."

"I know."

"And you'll have to name her. On the record. She can't be 'the child' or 'the foundling.' The record requires a name."

Silence. Then, very quietly, in the voice of someone opening a box she has kept locked even from herself: "Nell. Her name is Nell."

Nell. I turned it over in my head as I went down the stairs into the cold. Eleanor, probably, underneath it—some respectable name she'd never chosen, dressed down into something small enough to hide. A hiding name. Well. In three weeks, God willing and the river permitting, it would stop being a hiding name and become a *recorded* one, and no replacement page in any company folio would ever get it out again.

I was halfway down Tanner's Row the next morning, statute book under my arm, rehearsing my speech to the sour notary, when I saw the child in the basket and knew at once that something was wrong. Nell's cheeks were too bright. Her breath came too fast. And when Lior touched her forehead, her face went the particular gray-white that I had last seen on a dying woman in a whiteout, and my heart, which has survived thirty-one years of ledgers, understood before my head did:

The fever had come, and the ice had not yet broken, and now everything had a deadline twice over.
