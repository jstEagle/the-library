## 19. The Doctor's Ledger

There is a particular kind of patient who arrives at a doctor's door in winter and lies to him before she has finished removing her scarf. I have practiced medicine in Vhalen for twenty-two years, and I can tell you that the lie almost always comes before the scarf, sometimes before the door itself, muffled through the wood: *it's nothing, Doctor, it's only a cough.* The people of this city treat illness the way they treat debt — as something shameful, best concealed until it has grown teeth.

So when the knock came at half past four on a Tuesday afternoon, with the light already going blue at the edges of my windows, I was prepared for a lie. What I was not prepared for was how young the liar would be.

"Doctor Pell?" A girl of perhaps sixteen, thin as a ledger's spine, holding a bundle against her chest the way one holds a stolen thing — too tightly, too privately, angled away from the street. "My — my cousin's baby. She's taken a fever."

*Cousin's baby.* Note the construction. Not *my sister's child*, not even *a neighbor's* — a cousin, that most elastic of relations, the word families reach for when the truth has no comfortable shape. I have heard *cousin* deployed to cover bastards, debts, deaths, and once, memorably, an entire second wife. It is the Swiss army knife of Vhalen kinship.

"Bring her in," I said, and stood aside.

The girl hesitated on my threshold exactly long enough to confirm every suspicion I had not yet formed. She glanced up the street — my street is quiet, mostly funeral parlors and a shuttered chandlery, chosen deliberately for its lack of traffic — then stepped inside as though crossing into cold water.

* * *

The bundle was a girl-child, perhaps seven months old, and she was burning. Not the dramatic furnace-heat of the plays — real fever is quieter than that, a heat that seems to come from somewhere deeper than skin, as if the body has begun to argue with itself. Her cheeks were two bright coins. Her breathing had a catch in it, a small hitch at the top of each inhale, like a stitch in sewing.

"When did it start?"

"Last night." The girl's voice was steady. Too steady. Steadiness in the very young is not composure; it is rehearsal. "She was fine at supper."

"And her mother? Where is her mother?"

A pause of precisely one heartbeat too long. "Away."

I set down my bag and looked at her properly. She met my eyes, which most liars cannot manage, and I will confess what went through my head, because a doctor's head is not a clean place and there is no use pretending otherwise: I thought, *runaway indentured girl, stolen infant, and you, Imre, are about to become an accessory to something.* And then I thought, *the child is sick, and the law does not know how to take a temperature.*

"Undress her to the shift," I said. "Slowly. Fevered children chill fast."

While she worked I lit the lamp and got out my instruments, and it was in the act of lighting the lamp that I saw the face.

You must understand something about me before we go further. I am not a romantic man. I do not believe in blood calling to blood, in faces rising like ghosts from the past, in any of the machinery by which novels arrange their coincidences. Faces are bone and fat and habit; they repeat themselves in families because families share bone and fat and habit, nothing more. In twenty-two years I have delivered some four hundred children in this city, and I could walk the fish market identifying grandmothers by their granddaughters' chins without any recourse to the supernatural whatsoever.

But I had attended Lady Serevan Aurelian.

Nine years ago, in another life, when I still took private patients among the merchant houses and imagined myself climbing, I had been called to the Aurelian townhouse on Cordwainer's Row — a difficult pregnancy, a husband anxious to the point of rudeness, a wife who was gracious to me in the way of someone being gracious to furniture. I attended the birth of a daughter. I held the child before the nurse did. And I remember thinking, with the mild vanity of a physician noting his own handiwork, *she has her mother's brow.*

The brow was standing in front of me now, seven months old and feverish, attached to a child who was supposed to be dead.

Everyone in Vhalen knew the story, or thought they did. House Aurelian, drowned in debt and scandal; the barges lost on the winter river with the family's remaining wealth and several members aboard; the infant heir presumed perished with them or dead shortly after of grief and circumstance. The company that absorbed the Aurelian contracts — Meridian, naturally, Meridian absorbs everything the way the river absorbs rain — had closed the estate with a thoroughness that impressed even the other companies, and thoroughness of that kind always makes me slightly ill. Grief is never that tidy. Estates are only ever that tidy when someone wants them closed.

I am telling you all of this now, in the order I understood it, but I want to be honest about the order in which I actually understood it, which was: first the brow, then a cold feeling in my stomach, then the memory arriving whole like a body surfacing, then a long moment in which I stood holding a lamp and staring at a baby while her caretaker watched me with increasing alarm.

"You recognize her," the girl said. Not a question. Her arms had come around the child protectively, and I noted — filed away, as we say — that she positioned herself between me and the door. Not between the child and me. Between herself and the door. As if the danger came from outside, and I were merely weather.

"I recognize a fever," I said, which was true, and also a lie, and also the correct answer, and I have rarely been so aware of a sentence doing three jobs at once.

* * *

The examination took twenty minutes. Otitis, I judged, left ear, with secondary inflammation — painful, dangerous in an infant if it travels, but treatable if treated now. I lanced nothing; there was nothing yet to lance. I gave instructions for warm compresses, for keeping her upright, for watching the breathing, and I measured out drops of tincture with the little glass pipette I reserve for infants, because infants cannot swallow powders and their mothers cannot afford apothecary mistakes.

"She'll need to be seen again in three days," I said. "If the fever climbs past a certain point before then, come at once. Any hour. Knock hard."

"How much do I owe you?"

"A crown."

She produced it from somewhere inside her clothing — produced it the way people produce money they cannot afford to spend, with a small grimace of loss — and set it on my table with a precision that told me she had counted her coins recently and often.

"That's not enough," she said suddenly. "For the visit and the medicine both. I know it isn't. I'll bring the rest when—"

"It's enough." I waved it off. "I charge the companies double and the poor nothing. You've landed neatly in the middle."

That earned me something I had not expected: a flicker of a smile, quickly suppressed, as if smiling were also against somebody's rules. Then she wrapped the child — carefully, expertly, better than most mothers I see — and stood there a moment, weighing something.

"Doctor," she said. "Did you — I mean. Do you treat many patients from the warehouses? The trading companies' people?"

And there it was. The question under the question. She wanted to know if I was safe, and more than that, she wanted to know if I knew I was being asked.

"I treat whoever knocks," I said. "My ledger records complaints, not addresses. That's how ledgers stay honest."

Another look passed over her face — relief and suspicion fighting for the same ground, neither winning — and then she thanked me, and lifted the bundle, and was gone into the blue evening with her feverish stolen-or-not-stolen child, leaving behind one crown, the smell of coal smoke, and a problem the size of a city.

* * *

Now. The ledger.

Every doctor keeps one. Mine is a plain bound book, ruled in columns: date, name, complaint, treatment, fee. Twenty-two years of them line my back room, forty-one volumes, and in those volumes is written the medical history of half of Vhalen — births, fevers, the slow erosion of dockworkers' lungs, the fashionable complaints of merchants' wives, everything. Doctors' ledgers outlive doctors. They get sold, inherited, subpoenaed. A wise physician writes his entries knowing that someday a stranger will read them.

A foolish physician writes his entries believing no one ever will.

I sat down that evening with the lamp turned low and my pen uncapped, and I considered the problem of Imre Pell's honesty.

Here is the entry as duty dictates it should read:

*17 Feb. Female infant, approx. 7 months, presented by unnamed girl (indentured?, warehouse district). Otitis media, L. Tincture prescribed. Fee 1 crown.*

Duty, however, is a word that gets used a great deal in this city by people who mean *convenience*. If I wrote *unnamed girl*, I was lying by omission, because I had a strong professional suspicion — approaching certainty — of whose child this was, and a certainty of whose household the girl served. If Hesk or any of Meridian's men ever came asking — and men like Hesk always come asking eventually, it is practically their profession — *unnamed girl* would tell them nothing, but it would also tell them I had nothing worth taking, and men like Hesk do not leave empty-handed. They take the ledger. They take the pen. They take the doctor's license and his reputation and, if the mood strikes them, his future, on the theory that a man who noticed once might notice again.

But here is the other side of the coin, the side I turned over all evening while my supper went cold: if I wrote *unnamed girl*, and the truth ever surfaced — the inquiry everyone whispers about, the magistrate's winter court, the ice that will break in weeks — then my ledger becomes worthless. Worse than worthless: complicit. A doctor who treated the last heir of House Aurelian and recorded her as *unnamed*, in the very weeks when the question of that house was before the court? No magistrate would believe I hadn't known. My neutrality would be read as cowardice after the fact, and cowardice after the fact is indistinguishable from guilt.

An entry, I realized, is a weapon that fires in both directions depending on who holds it later. That is the terrifying thing about records, about paper, about ink: they do not care about your intentions. They simply wait, patient as ice, for whoever comes with the power to read them aloud.

So I wrote this:

*17 Feb. Female infant, c. 7 months, presented by young woman (servant class), complaint of fever. Features of note. Otitis media, L ear. Tincture, dosing instructed. Follow-up required 20 Feb. Fee 1 crown, paid.*

*Features of note.*

Two words. Let the lawyers chew on those. To a company man reading casually, it means nothing — doctors note features constantly, birthmarks, deformities, the interesting case. But to a magistrate conducting an inquiry into House Aurelian, sitting across from a doctor who attended that house's last confinement nine years ago? *Features of note* is a door left unlocked. It says: *I saw. I said nothing then. Here is what I saw, written down while the memory was fresh, and you may draw your own conclusions about why I did not write the name.*

It is either cowardice or cunning, and I genuinely do not know which, and I suspect the answer depends entirely on which direction the river breaks.

* * *

Three days later she came back, as instructed, and the child was better — fever broken, ear draining cleanly, the small furious vitality of infancy reasserting itself. The girl looked as though she had not slept since Tuesday, which she probably had not, and she watched my hands during the examination with the vigilance of someone guarding treasure in transit.

"She'll keep," I said. "Keep her warm, keep her dry, and keep her away from drafty counting rooms."

Something moved behind her eyes at *counting rooms* — a flinch, quickly mastered. So. Warehouse girl confirmed, if I'd had any doubt, which I hadn't.

"Thank you, Doctor." The crown again, exact change, counted out. "Will you — if she needs anything further—"

"Knock hard," I said. "Any hour."

At the door she paused, and for a moment I thought she would ask the question — *do you know who she is, Doctor, do you know what you're looking at* — and I found myself, to my own surprise, hoping she wouldn't. Because if she asked, I would have to answer, and answering meant choosing, and I had spent three days constructing an entry specifically so that I would not have to choose. Neutrality, I have come to understand, is not the absence of a position. It is a position, held very carefully, at great cost, and it only works so long as nobody asks you to defend it out loud.

She didn't ask. She nodded once, pulled her scarf up, and went out into the gray afternoon.

I returned to my desk and made the follow-up entry:

*20 Feb. Same infant. Recovering well. Features of note persist.*

*Persist.* Another lawyer's word. Another unlocked door.

Then I sat for a long time looking at the two entries, and I thought about the girl — her steadiness, her counted coins, the way she'd placed herself between the child and the world — and I thought about Lady Serevan's brow, and I thought about the fact that in twenty-two years of practice I have learned exactly one thing with certainty: sooner or later, everybody's ledger gets read.

Mine included. Perhaps especially mine, now.

I closed the book, tied it with its cord, and shelved it among its forty brothers — one volume among forty-two, anonymous, waiting. And I went to bed telling myself I had done nothing, written nothing, chosen nothing.

Which was, of course, the biggest lie of the week. Even by Vhalen standards.

What I did not know — what I could not have known, standing at my window that night watching the river ice groan in the dark like some vast animal turning over in its sleep — was that within the fortnight, a broad-shouldered man with careful eyes and a company pin in his collar would climb my steps, knock politely, and ask to see my ledger.

And that I would have to decide, with him standing on my doorstep breathing my air, whether *features of note* was going to save that child — or bury us all.
