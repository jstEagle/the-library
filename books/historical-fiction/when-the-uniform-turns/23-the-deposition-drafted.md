## 23. The Deposition Drafted

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a warehouse loft above counting rooms, and I had spent sixteen years believing it was the sound of nothing. Now, with a candle guttering on an upturned crate and a sheet of company paper — company paper, stolen from my own employer, which felt less like theft and more like a refund — spread flat before me, I understood the silence had never been nothing at all. It was the sound of everything I had not said, stacked floor to ceiling like bales of unsold cotton, waiting all this time for someone to strike a light.

Nell slept in the blanket-lined drawer I'd rigged beside my cot. She slept the way only the very young and the very guilty sleep: completely, as though the world had already been settled in her favor. I envied her. I was about to commit the single most dangerous act of my life, and it required nothing but ink and honesty, which are, in Vhalen, the two most expensive commodities on the market.

Odile had told me the rules that afternoon, in the ledger room, with her voice pitched exactly as it always was — dry, precise, a woman reading aloud from a catalogue of disasters. "Sworn deposition," she said. "You swear before a magistrate's clerk that what you write is true. Once it enters the record, it cannot be quietly removed. It can be disputed, contradicted, buried under a hundred other documents — but it exists. It has weight. Paper is the only ghost that can testify."

"And after?" I'd asked. "You told me victors rewrite records."

"They do." She had not flinched. "Which is why we file before the ice breaks, and why we file copies. Many copies, in many hands. A truth kept in one place is a hostage. A truth kept in twenty places is a nuisance, and nuisances survive."

So now I sat in the loft with my stolen paper and my borrowed candle and my entire life arranged before me like a corpse on a slab, and I tried to write the first sentence.

I wrote: *My name is Lior Quintana.*

Then I sat back and stared at it, because it occurred to me — with the force of a dock rope snapping under load — that I had never written those words before. Not once. I could sign my name, of course; indentured girls learn to sign, mostly on documents that sign away pieces of them. But to write my name at the top of a page, as an assertion, as the opening move of a story where I was the one telling it — that was different. That was like standing on the frozen river and declaring the ice would hold.

*My name is Lior Quintana. I am sixteen years of age, indentured to the Meridian Trading Company since the age of six, and I swear the following to be true.*

I crossed out *swear* — I hadn't sworn yet, that came later, before a clerk, hand on whatever object they used for swearing in this city — and wrote *state*. Precision, Odile said. The law loves precision the way a diver loves a good line: it's the thing you hold onto when everything else goes dark.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about writing the truth: it does not come out in order.

I had imagined it would. I had imagined sitting down and marching through the years like a clerk through a column of figures, everything in its row, everything summing neatly. Instead my pen kept snagging. I started with the baby — the bundle at the door, the whiteout night, the note that said *forget the face* — and got three lines in before I realized I couldn't explain why I'd taken her in without explaining the note, and I couldn't explain the note without explaining the locket, and I couldn't explain the locket without explaining House Aurelian, and I couldn't explain House Aurelian without explaining my parents, and my parents were the bottom of the river, and the river was the beginning of everything.

So I began again. Further down, as it were.

*My parents were divers. Tavish and Sefa Quintana. They worked the pearl beds off the Vhalen shallows, and they died in the winter of 1902, when I was six, in a season when the company records say there were no drownings.*

That last clause — I wrote it and my hand shook. Because that was the sentence, wasn't it? That was the hairline crack running through the whole wall. Nine years of quiet, Yorick called it, and the quiet had a shape, and the shape was an absence in a ledger. Somebody had decided my parents' deaths were not worth a line. Somebody had decided that, and I had spent my whole life on the other side of that decision, believing history was something that happened to other people, when in fact history had happened to me so thoroughly and so early that I'd mistaken it for weather.

I wrote about the seizure. What I knew of it — which was fragments, gathered from Yorick's people and from Odile's careful, guilt-hoarded admissions. House Aurelian, the merchant dynasty whose barges had carried half the river's trade, drowned in debt and scandal in a single season. The Meridian Company had held their paper. The Meridian Company had called in that paper. And when the Aurelian barges went down in the ice — with cargo, with records, with, if Yorick was to be believed, men whose lines had been *cut* — the company had helped itself to the wreckage and called it recovery of assets.

*The drowning of the Aurelian barges was never investigated,* I wrote. *The drowning of my parents, who were Aurelian retainers, was never recorded at all.*

There. I had written it. The ink sat on the page looking very small and very black, and I felt absurdly like apologizing to it for how much weight I'd just asked it to carry.

Nell stirred in her drawer and made a small sound, the sound of a dream being negotiated with, and settled again. I got up and checked her anyway — checked her breathing, checked the blanket, checked the locket where it lay against her chest with the miniature portrait of a woman dead nine years, a woman whose cheekbones the child wore like a loan. Lady Serevan Aurelian's daughter. The heir everyone believed dead. The loose thread Hesk was hunting with the patience of a man counting the same inventory twice.

I went back to the candle. The wax was pooling unevenly; I straightened it out of pure nervous habit, then made myself stop, because I could see myself becoming one of those people who fusses with candles to avoid becoming a person who changes the world.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight, I hit the hard part.

Not the seizure. Not the drownings. Not even the part where I wrote — in careful, clerkly script that would have made Odile wince with pride — *my indenture papers bear the seal of House Aurelian, a seal that could not lawfully appear on any document executed after the house's dissolution, from which I conclude that my indenture was forged from the records of that house.*

That part had been terrible to write, but it was terrible the way cold water is terrible: a shock, and then a numbness, and then, strangely, a kind of clarity. My identity was never mine. Fine. Then I would build a new one out of the wreckage, the way the divers built new lines after every season's losses. You don't mourn the rope forever. You splice.

The hard part was Tam.

I hadn't planned to write about Tam. Tam was the boy two cots down in the indenture quarters, seventeen, quick with numbers, hopeless at cards, who had been selling information to Hesk — I was nearly certain, as certain as you can be about a person you've shared bread with for nine years. I'd seen him coming out of Hesk's office at an hour when no honest errand lives. I'd seen the way his eyes went to Nell's drawer when he thought no one was watching, the way a man eyes a purse he hasn't decided to lift yet.

And I sat there with my pen hovering and realized that if I wrote his name, and the deposition ever saw daylight, Tam would hang in the same wind that lifted me. Hesk would know who talked. Hesk's revenge wouldn't wait for a magistrate.

But if I left him out, and the truth came out another way, and it emerged that I had known and said nothing — then my testimony had a hole in it, and holes in testimony are what lawyers feed on. Odile had been merciless on this point. "The moment they find one thing you omitted, they will use it to suggest everything else is omitted too. A half-truth is not half as good as a truth. It is a truth with a handle attached, so they can carry it wherever they like."

I thought about Tam buying out his contract. That was the rumor, anyway — that Hesk had promised him his freedom, the way you'd promise a dog a bone for fetching. I thought about how I'd felt watching him, the sick arithmetic of it: *he gets out, I stay in, Nell gets found.* And I thought about what I was asking the record to do. I was asking it to say that the company's world ran on debts and betrayals and paper forged in the dark. It would be a strange kind of honesty that described that world and left out the only other person in it desperate enough to sell.

*There is a boy,* I wrote, and then stopped, and then made myself go on, *named Tam Corver, indentured like me, who I believe has been reporting to warehouse master Brann Hesk. I name him not to condemn him. He is what the company makes of us. I name him because the record should show that this machine does not only crush people; it teaches them to crush each other, and calls the teaching opportunity.*

I read that back and winced. *Calls the teaching opportunity* — too clever. Odile would strike it. But I left it in, because it was true, and I was learning that in a deposition, true and ugly beats polished and safe. The whole point was to stop being safe.

* * *

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time I reached the end, and the loft had gone from cold to the particular bitter cold that comes in the hour before dawn, when the river ice groans below like something turning over in its sleep.

I wrote the last of it in a hand that had gone stiff and cramped:

*I have kept this child alive for [I counted] thirty-eight days. She has a name, which I gave her, and a birthright, which others gave her and tried to take away. I am told that by speaking I may lose what freedom I have. I have considered this. I was six years old when this company took my name and my parents and my future and gave me in exchange a uniform and a tally-book. There is very little left for speaking to take. But there is a great deal for it to give back — to her, if not to me.*

*Let the record show that the unnamed child at the warehouse door is Nell, of House Aurelian, and that she is alive. Let it show that her house was destroyed by methods that will not survive daylight. Let it show that I knew all this and chose her anyway.*

*Forget the face, the note said.*

*I decline.*

I sat back. The stub of candle threw my shadow huge against the rafters, a giant hunched over a page no bigger than a spread hand, and I thought: there it is. There's the whole of me. Sixteen years, and it fits on eleven sheets of stolen paper.

It didn't fit, of course. That was the joke of it — the bitter, liberating joke. Eleven sheets couldn't hold the smell of the counting room in winter, or the way Marta had shown me to mix milk and water and a drop of honey, testing it on her own wrist like a woman testing bathwater for a queen. Eleven sheets couldn't hold Yorick's face when he talked about the cut lines, the way an old man's face goes when he describes something he's carried so long it has grown into him like a splinter the flesh has closed over. Eleven sheets couldn't hold the sound Nell made when she was falling asleep — a small, rising hum, like a kettle very far away — or the weight of her head against my collarbone, which was, I had come to believe, the actual unit of measurement for everything that mattered.

But the record didn't need everything. The record needed the bones. The rest — the smell and the hum and the weight — that was mine. That was the part no magistrate could file and no director could burn. I was only now understanding that this was what Odile had meant, all those weeks ago, when she'd said testimony on the record means something. It wasn't that the record was so powerful. It was that the act of testifying was. You walked in a nameless thing and you walked out an author. Whatever they did to the page afterward, they couldn't un-write the having-written.

I blotted the last page with the corner of my sleeve — proper blotting sand was downstairs, and I wasn't going downstairs — and stacked the sheets, and weighted them with the candlestick, and sat for a moment in the grey beginning of light with my hands flat on the paper like a woman warming them at a small fire.

Below me, through the floorboards, I heard the first sounds of the warehouse waking: bolts, boots, the cough of the counting-room stove. And under those sounds, another sound I had learned to dread the way sailors dread a particular note in the wind — the deliberate, unhurried tread of Brann Hesk making his morning rounds, stopping, I noticed, a beat too long beneath the loft ladder.

I gathered the sheets into my shirt, against my skin, where they lay warm as a second heartbeat.

Nell opened her eyes and looked at me — that solemn, ancient look infants keep folded away behind all the crying, as if they've read ahead in the book — and I lifted her from the drawer and held her and whispered, because even now I didn't quite dare say it aloud:

"Tomorrow, little one. Tomorrow we go make it official."

And then, from the yard below, cutting through the morning quiet like a blade through sailcloth, came the sound I had been both dreading and awaiting: the town crier, hoarse with cold, announcing the magistrate's winter session — and the words that turned my blood to river water.

*"—the court shall sit until the ice breaks. Three days remain."*
