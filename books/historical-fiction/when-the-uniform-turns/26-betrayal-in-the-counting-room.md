## 26. Betrayal in the Counting Room

There is a particular sound a counting room makes at dusk, when the clerks have gone and only the lamps are lit and the abacuses sit in their rows like sleeping animals. It is the sound of nothing being watched. I had come to love that sound the way other people love church bells.

I was there because of Marta's brother's boat license, which I had finally pried from Drawer 44 of the licensing cabinet—a whole saga involving a borrowed key, a feigned coughing fit, and an hour of my life I will never get back—and because Nell had developed opinions about being set down, all of them loud. She was asleep against my chest now, wrapped in the grey blanket Hesk had not yet managed to connect to me, her breath making small damp stars on my collarbone. The license itself was folded flat against my ribs, under the blanket, over my heart, where I kept everything I could not afford to lose.

Which is why I heard the door.

Not the door opening—doors open all the time in a warehouse; the building breathed through its doors like an old man through his nose. What I heard was the *quality* of the closing. A door closed by a clerk sounds apologetic. A door closed by someone who does not want to be heard sounds like this: a slow compression of air, a latch eased home with two fingers, the particular silence afterward that has weight to it, like snow on a roof.

I stepped backward into the shadow between the ledger shelves and stood very still, the way you do when you are sixteen and holding something illegal in every sense except the one that matters.

Tam came around the end of the shelf row.

He did not see me. He was walking fast, head down, arms crossed tight over his chest as if he were cold, though the counting room was warm enough that the ink pots hadn't skinned over in weeks. He went straight to Hesk's office door—the master's own, brass plate, the only door in the building anyone knocked on—and he stopped in front of it for a long moment, the way a man stops in front of a door when his hand and his conscience have gotten into an argument and his hand is winning.

Then he knocked, twice, soft, and went in, and the door shut behind him, and I stood in the dark with a sleeping baby and a stolen boat license and the sudden, sick understanding that I had just watched a boy sell something.

I didn't know what yet. That came later. But I knew the posture. You learn it young in an indenture house: the hunched shoulders of a person about to trade a piece of themselves they can't get back. Tam had been wearing it for a week, actually, if I was honest. Ever since the audit notices went up. He'd been smiling wrong—not the way you smile when something good happens but the way you smile when you've decided something good is going to happen and you're trying to make it true by force.

* * *

Let me tell you about Tam Verrin, because if I'm going to hate him properly I should do it accurately.

He was seventeen, two years my senior in age and roughly forty years my senior in bitterness, indentured since he was nine, same as me, and we had grown up in the same dormer loft with the same lice and the same thin soup. When I was eleven and got the fever, Tam was the one who smuggled me extra blankets off the drying lines, getting thrashed for it when Marta reported the shortage—Marta reports everything, it's how she survives, we'll come back to that. When I was thirteen and Odile caught me reading a shipping manifest upside down out of boredom, it was Tam who lied and said he'd asked me to check figures. He covered for me the way you cover for family: without thinking, without keeping score.

Or so I believed, right up until the winter I turned sixteen, which is the winter I learned that everyone keeps score. Some people just keep it in a book you're not allowed to read.

Here is what I know now, assembled later from pieces, the way you assemble anything in this city—from fragments, rumors, and things people said when they thought no one was counting. Here is what happened in that office while I stood frozen between the shelves with my heart going like a diver's hammer on stone.

Hesk offered him a buyout.

Three years early. Full discharge papers, signed, sealed, his name struck from the company rolls—which is to say, his freedom, the thing every soul in this building would commit any sin short of murder to hold in their hands. In exchange: information. Who had been in the vault. Who had taken the fibers. Where the child was hidden.

And here is the part that took me longest to understand, the part I still turn over at night like a stone looking for what's underneath: Tam didn't sell me because he was evil. He sold me because he was tired. There is a difference, and the difference matters, and I will spend the rest of this account pretending it doesn't hurt less than it does.

* * *

He came out of the office after perhaps ten minutes. I counted them by Nell's breathing. Ten minutes to price a childhood friendship—I kept thinking, absurdly, of the blanket, of him hauling blankets down from the drying line for a shivering eleven-year-old, and what those blankets cost him, and whether he'd done the arithmetic even then.

He walked back up the shelf row, and this time he saw me.

I want to be fair to that moment, because fairness is the one luxury the Meridian Company never extended to anyone and I refuse to adopt their accounting methods. He flinched. That's the first thing. He flinched like a man caught, which means some part of him had hoped I wouldn't be there, or had hoped not to care that I was. His face did several things in quick succession—surprise, guilt, and then a terrible smoothness sliding over both, the mask of a boy who had just made his decision and could not afford to reconsider it.

"Lior." Quiet. "You shouldn't be here."

"Neither should you." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "What did you tell him?"

"Nothing." Too fast. Everything said too fast is a confession wearing a coat. "Inventory questions. The audit's coming, everyone's getting asked—"

"Tam." Nell stirred against my chest and I shifted her, instinctively, turning my body so that my back was to him, which was stupid, because you don't turn your back on a man who has just sold you, but she was asleep and warm and some instincts run deeper than survival. "Whatever he offered you—it isn't real. He'll take what you give him and then he'll take your three years anyway. Men like Hesk don't pay debts. They *are* debts."

Something moved behind his eyes. For one moment—one long, lamp-lit moment—I thought I'd reached him. I thought about the blankets again. I thought, *say yes, say you'll walk away, say nothing to anyone, and I will never mention this night to a living soul.*

"I've got four years left," he said. Not to me, exactly. To the middle distance, to the ledger spines, to the version of himself that was nine years old and newly indentured. "Four years. Do you know what four more winters in the loft does to a person? You don't count anymore after a while. You get *counted.*"

"And what do you think happens to me?"

"You—" He stopped. And here is the thing I will carry to my grave, heavier than the boat license, heavier than the seal, heavier than any paper in that building: he looked at Nell. Just for a second. Just a flick of the eyes toward the bundle at my chest, and away, fast, like touching a hot stove. And then he said, in a voice I had never heard him use, flat and rehearsed, the voice of a boy reciting terms he'd agreed to in another room:

"I don't know anything about a child. That's what I told him. I don't know anything about a child."

It was almost mercy. It was mercy the way a loan is a gift. He had told Hesk about the vault—he must have, the fibers, the night watch, the shape of it all—but he had drawn a line at Nell, or told himself he had, and he wanted me to know it, wanted credit for it, wanted his small cruelty entered on the merciful side of the ledger.

"Tam," I said. "He already knows about her. Whatever you gave him, it points at me, and everything that points at me points at her. You didn't draw a line. You drew a map."

His jaw worked. He looked, for a moment, like he might cry, and I hated him for that too, because I was the one who should have been crying and I couldn't afford it. Tears are expensive when you're holding a baby in a room full of evidence.

"He says if I'm wrong about you," Tam said, barely audible, "if there's nothing to find, then nothing happens to you. That's what he said. Nothing happens."

"And you believed him." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Tam. We grew up in this building. You've seen what 'nothing happens' looks like. It looks like Yorick's cousin. It looks like my parents."

That landed. I watched it land, watched him go white around the mouth, because everyone in the river quarter knew the Quintana story, knew two divers went into the water for the company's salvage and the water kept them, and no one ever asked why. He opened his mouth. Closed it. And then he did the worst thing he could have done, worse than betrayal, worse than lying:

He walked past me. Close enough that our shoulders nearly touched, close enough that I smelled the tallow soap on him, the same soap I used, and he said, without stopping, without turning:

"The audit's Thursday. Don't be here Thursday."

And then he was gone, out the side door, into the whiteout, and I stood alone among the ledgers with my heart hammering and my arms full of everything he'd just put at risk.

* * *

I did not sleep that night. I sat in the loft above the counting rooms with Nell in my lap and I ran the evening back through my mind the way Odile runs a column of figures—twice, three times, looking for the error.

The error, of course, was me. I had trusted him. Worse: I had trusted him *because* of the blankets, because of the manifest, because of nine years of small kindnesses, and I had built my entire operation—Nell, the seal, the deposition draft folded inside my shirt lining—on the assumption that Tam Verrin's decency was structural. Load-bearing. And it turned out his decency was just another inventory item, subject to audit, available for purchase at the right price.

No. That's the anger talking, and I promised myself I'd write this true. Here is truer: his decency wasn't for sale. His *exhaustion* was. Hesk hadn't bought Tam's malice; he'd bought his way out. Four years of cold lofts and thin soup and being counted instead of counting—that was the price tag, and I have stood in front of that price tag myself, in the dark, many nights, and I cannot swear before any magistrate living or dead that I would not have paid it. If our positions had been reversed—if Hesk had offered me my freedom for Tam's secret—I would like to believe I'd have refused. I would like to believe it the way you'd like to believe the river won't rise. Hopeful. Unverified.

But belief doesn't matter now. What matters is Thursday.

Thursday the directors arrive. Thursday the audit begins, and Hesk will have his men turn the warehouse inside out with the patient thoroughness of men who are paid by the hour and hold grudges by the decade. They will search the loft. They will search the laundry. They will find the grey blanket, or the milk tin, or the candle stubs, or the deposition draft itself, folded against my ribs where I keep everything I cannot afford to lose—and then they will find Nell, and then it will be over, all of it, the record unwritten, the ice still solid, the truth still buried under nine years of quiet and one boy's buyout papers.

Unless I am gone before Thursday.

I looked down at Nell, asleep in my lap, her fist curled around my thumb with the absolute confidence of someone who believes the world is held together by the people who love her. She had a fever two weeks ago and lived. She had been born in a snowdrift to a dying woman and lived. She had survived the Meridian Company's entire apparatus of paperwork and menace for a month and a half, and she did not yet know her own name was contraband.

"Thursday," I whispered to her. "We won't be here Thursday."

She slept on. Outside, the river groaned under its ice, the long slow complaint of a thing holding more than it can bear, waiting for spring, waiting for permission to break open. Somewhere below us, in the counting room, Tam's discharge papers were already being drafted, I supposed—or maybe they weren't, maybe they'd never exist, maybe Hesk had promised him exactly what he'd promised everyone else in this building's history: everything, in writing, later.

It didn't matter which. Either way, the price had been paid, and I was the currency.

I folded the deposition draft smaller. I tucked the boat license deeper. And I began, in the dark, to plan a crossing of the frozen river.
