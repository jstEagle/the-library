## 1. The Weight of Lead

There are two ways to measure a life's work, my father used to say. By what it weighs, and by what it weighs on you.

He said this while carrying a full case of fourteen-point type up from the cellar, which weighed — I knew because we'd argued about it once, and I'd won by fetching the scale — a little over forty pounds. He said it the way he said most things, which is to say as though the observation had only just occurred to him, though I later found the same sentence written in the margin of a manual printed in 1809. My father was a man who recycled his own wisdom and called it thrift.

I was thinking about weight that morning because I was measuring the shop.

Not with the scale. With my eyes, and with a habit I didn't know I'd developed until I caught myself doing it: standing in the middle of the composing room, turning slowly, and asking of every object in sight the same question. *Can it be carried?*

The type cases could. Forty pounds each, and we had thirty-one of them, which was a problem for another day, or for no day at all, if the rumors stayed rumors.

The hand press could not. Old Columbine — Father had named her, because he believed a press deserved a name the way a ship did, and because he had a weakness for jokes that took a full minute to land — the press weighed as much as a horse and had opinions to match. Her iron frame had come from Brava in three pieces on a cart whose driver still, twenty years later, told the story of the axle at every tavern within walking distance of the water. She was not going anywhere. If the orders came, Columbine would stand in this room and be whatever they made her stand and be, and I would have to live with that, the way you live with leaving a dog behind.

The cutting tools, though. The gravers and scorers, the punches he'd ground himself from clockmaker's steel, the little brass gauge with the worn numbers that he'd held the way other men held a pipe — those fit in a satchel. I'd checked. Twice. The satchel lived under the floorboard by the wash-up sink, under a layer of ink rags that smelled so powerfully of lampblack that even I, who had spent my whole life breathing the stuff, wrinkled my nose when I lifted them.

I put the rags back and stood up and told myself I was being ridiculous.

Then I did it again the next morning.

* * *

The rumors came in with the fish, which is how most things came into the harbor district. Odile Marchetti brought them herself, two baskets of sardines balanced on her hip and her youngest, Pia, trailing behind her picking up anything Pia found, which was everything.

"They're drawing up the lists," Odile said, in the doorway, not coming in, which was unlike her. Odile came into places the way weather did. "At the customs house. Names of printers. Names of *shops*."

"Names of shops," I repeated. "Not men?"

"Shops." She set the baskets down on the step, which she never did — the fish went straight through to the kitchen, that was the arrangement, twelve years of it. "A shop can't argue. A shop can't run. A shop just stands there being a shop until someone comes for it."

Pia had found the composing stone I'd left out and was arranging sardine scales on it in what I chose to believe was an artistic pattern.

"Who told you this?"

"Who told me the tide turns? The same people who always know." Odile had a way of looking at you when she said things like that, half challenge and half affection, as if to say: *you know I'm right, and you know how I know, and we're both going to pretend the knowing is respectable.* "The customs men have stopped joking. That's how you can tell. When Berto stopped joking, it meant the tax was coming. When the harbor watch stopped joking, it meant the press gangs. Now nobody at the customs house will even look at you, and the one who does — the little one, Ferro — he looked at me like I was already a widow."

"Ferro looks at everyone like that. He looked at me like that when I bought a stamp."

"He looked at you like you were a *customer*." She gathered up Pia, who protested the loss of her scales, and hoisted the baskets again. "Put the good ones under the stone, Wren. And think about what I said."

I put the good ones under the stone. I thought about what she said. I thought about it while I distributed type, which is the work your hands do while your mind is elsewhere, and by the time the morning's job was set — five hundred handbills for the chandler Voss, announcing a sale on rope that had been announced every quarter since before my father died and would be announced every quarter until the sea gave up on commerce altogether — I had thought about it into a very specific shape.

The shape was this: the type cases could be carried. Columbine could not. And the question I could not stop asking, the question that had gotten under my skin like a sliver, was *why am I the one asking it?*

A printer with nothing to hide measures her shop by jobs and by coin. A printer with something to hide measures it by what can be carried.

I set the last line of Voss's handbills — *ALL ROPES REDUCED* — and looked at it, and thought: if they come for the shop, this is what they'll find. Rope. Sardine prices. The harmless sediment of a harbor town. And underneath, in the locked case in the cellar, in a drawer that had been my father's and was now, officially, nobody's — the other thing. The thing I had been printing twice a month since the spring, in a face I'd cut myself so it wouldn't match anything in the shop's inventory. The broadsheets. The ones signed with a dead man's name.

I washed my hands. I always washed my hands when I thought about the broadsheets, a little ritual, as if the ink on my fingers were evidence. It was, of course. Everything in that shop was evidence. The rag under the floorboard. The smell of the special ink I mixed from Father's old recipe, the one with the soot from the tallow lamp that gave the letters their particular gray. Even Columbine herself, if anyone ever thought to take her apart and read the impressions worn into her platen like rings in a tree.

*Can it be carried?* I asked the shop.

The shop, being a shop, said nothing.

* * *

In the afternoon I did the accounts, which is to say I sat at Father's desk and moved numbers around until they resembled honesty. The desk still smelled of him — or of the pipe he'd given up the year before he died and never quite given up, the way a man gives up a pipe. I kept his ledger open on the left, mine on the right, and I had stopped, some months ago, being able to tell where his handwriting ended and mine began. We'd learned our letters from the same cases. There's a thing that happens when you set type your whole life: your hand starts to look like the type. His a's and my a's had the same little tail.

The bell over the door went, and a customer came in wanting two hundred funeral invitations by Friday, and for a quarter of an hour the world was only the world: black borders, a name in roman caps, the widow's preference for doves over lilies. I quoted her a price. She haggled, because everyone haggles, because haggling is how the harbor district says hello. She left with a promise for Friday and a dove instead of a lily, and I stood in the doorway after her, and I looked up the street toward the customs house, and I saw what Odile had seen.

The customs men were standing in the doorway of their own building. Not working. Standing. Watching the street the way cats watch a hole in a wall.

One of them was Ferro.

He did not look at me. He looked at the shop. At the sign above the door — ASHGROVE & SON, PRINTERS, the letters my father had cut himself and gilded with his own hands, and which had needed a chisel and a ladder and a great deal of stubbornness to amend after his death, since there was no son and never had been. I'd kept the sign as it was. It was easier than explaining, and it was, in a way I didn't examine too closely, a kind of company. A shop with two names in it feels less empty than a shop with one.

*Can it be carried?* I asked the sign.

No. But it could come down in an hour, if it had to. The gilding would flake. Father would have minded. Father would also, I suspected, have understood.

* * *

That evening I locked up early and went down into the cellar, ostensibly to fetch a ream of the good paper, and actually to stand in front of the locked case and have the conversation I'd been not having with myself all day.

Here is what the case held, if you were me, and only if you were me:

The broadsheets. Eleven of the twelve editions, one copy each — the twelfth I'd sent out into the city folded inside fish deliveries, hymnals, and once, memorably, inside a consignment of Voss's rope handbills, so that somewhere in Vellamare a man had bought rope and received, gratis, eight hundred words on the seizure of the Tallow Street houses. I hoped he'd read it. I hoped he'd read it to his wife.

The matrix. A single line of type, locked in its little frame, that I'd cut for the signature line: *Printed for the people of Vellamare by T. Ashgrove.* My father's initial. My father's name. A dead man's byline, doing the work his daughter couldn't risk doing under her own.

And the letter. His last letter, the one that had come from upcountry three weeks before he died, four lines long, saying only that he'd taken a commission he couldn't speak of, that I wasn't to ask, and that if anything happened to him I should mind the shop and mind my own business, in that order. I'd read it perhaps four hundred times. I'd stopped hearing the words and started hearing the spaces between them, the way you can hear a lie in a pause.

*If anything happened to him.* What a thing to write. What a thing to have written, and then to fall from the harbor stairs on a night with no wind and no drink in him, a man who could walk the seawall blindfolded, a man who had climbed that staircase ten thousand times.

The coroner had called it an accident. The coroner had called it an accident very quickly, and had not asked to see the body twice, and had charged the family — had charged *me* — a fee for the certificate that was, I now realized, standing in the cellar with the lamp shaking slightly in my hand, exactly the sort of fee a man charges when he wants the transaction over with.

I had been so busy grieving that I had not done the arithmetic. That was the truth of it. Grief takes up the whole desk. But grief was twelve months old now, and the arithmetic had come in the night, the way arithmetic does, and it said: *the stairs, the fee, the letter, the lists at the customs house, the names of shops.*

I put the lamp down. I took out the matrix — just to hold it, just to feel the little brass frame warm in my palm, forty pounds of type in a case, and this, one line, weighing almost nothing, weighing on me like the sea.

"Can you be carried?" I asked it.

It was a line of type. Of course it could be carried. It could be carried anywhere. It could be carried to the ends of the earth, and it would still say what it said, and the man it named would still be dead, and the question of who had actually held the stick when those words were set would still hang in the air like the smell of lampblack, waiting for someone to answer it.

I put everything back. I locked the case. I went up the stairs — slowly, holding the rail, which I had never done before in my life and which I now understood my father had probably never done either, and which he had fallen from anyway, or not from, and the not knowing was a weight too, and it could not be carried anywhere, it could only be set down in type, someday, somewhere, in a face of my own cutting.

The bell over the door went. I froze on the stairs, one hand on the rail.

It was only the evening air, moving the door in its frame. Only the harbor, breathing.

But through the window, up the street, I could see a lantern stop outside the shop. Hold there. A shadow across the glass, the shape of a man standing very still, the way men stand when they are counting windows.

Then it moved on, toward the customs house, and I stood in the dark of the stairwell with my father's name over my head and my own name in my mouth, unspoken, and I began — that night, quietly, without ceremony, the way you begin anything that matters — to make the list of what could be carried.
