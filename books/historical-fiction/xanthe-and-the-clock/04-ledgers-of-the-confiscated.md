## 4. Ledgers of the Confiscated

The Bureau of Records and Returns occupied the ground floor of what had once been a merchant's palace, and the state had done its best to make the building forget what it used to be. The frescoes in the entry hall had been whitewashed. The marble floors had been covered in cheap pine planking, ostensibly to protect the marble, though everyone understood the real purpose: pine echoed differently under boots, and a man who could hear boots coming had time to look busy.

Xanthe had looked busy for eleven years.

They arrived each morning at the seventh bell, hung their coat on the same peg, and sat at the same desk — third from the window, chosen years ago for its light and kept out of habit, or perhaps out of a superstition Xanthe would never have admitted to: that a person who always sat in the same place could not be accused of scheming. Schemers moved around. Schemers had corners to whisper in. Xanthe had a desk, a ruler, and a system.

The system was this: every census return from the harbor district came to Xanthe's desk first, was checked against the previous year's return, was annotated in a small precise hand in the margin, and was filed. Households that had grown were marked with a small circle. Households that had diminished were marked with a small dash. Households that had lied — and there were always a few, the widow who had taken in an unregistered nephew, the cooper whose "brother" had appeared three years running with a different surname — were marked with a small, patient dot, to be investigated when the season allowed.

Xanthe did not enjoy catching liars. Xanthe enjoyed *completeness*. There was a difference, and Xanthe had tried once to explain it to a colleague over lunch and had been met with the particular blankness of a man who thought a ledger was a ledger was a ledger. The difference was that a completed record was a small act of justice. Every person counted, counted *properly*, was a person the state could not later pretend had never existed. Xanthe had never said this aloud — it sounded dangerously like a philosophy, and philosophies were the sort of thing that got clerks reassigned to the salt marshes — but it was true, and Xanthe knew it was true the way one knows one's own hands.

The Ashgrove return lay on the desk that morning, and Xanthe looked at it for a long moment before touching it.

One dash. That was all the form required. Household diminished by one: Aldous Ashgrove, printer, deceased, cause of death entered in a clerk's hurried hand as *misadventure*. The dash was small and black and final, and Xanthe had made it four days ago with the same pen that made all the other dashes, and it had felt no different from any other dash, because how could it? People died. The harbor district lost a dozen souls a month to the sea alone, and the sea did not fill out forms.

But the interview at the print shop had stayed with Xanthe in a way that interviews did not usually stay. Not the lie itself — Wren Ashgrove had lied about operating the press, and lied competently, and Xanthe had noted the lie with a dot in the margin of the interview record and moved on. It was something else. It was the way the young woman had stood with one hand resting on the type case, the way a person rests a hand on a shoulder. It was the smell of ink so deep in the walls of that shop that Xanthe's coat had carried it home. And it was the question Xanthe had asked — the one question too many, the one that had made the young woman's eyes go still and flat as a harbor at dawn — and the fact that Xanthe had asked it at all.

*Who operates the press since your father died?*

A census taker did not need to know who operated a press. A census taker needed to know how many persons slept under the roof, their ages, their trades, their relation to the head of household. Xanthe had asked about the press because the question had been on the supplementary form — a new form, issued six weeks ago, marked *Priority*, with questions that had nothing to do with census and everything to do with presses, pamphlets, and persons of interest. Xanthe had filled out forty of these supplementary forms already. They went to a different office. They did not come back.

Xanthe had told themselves this was normal. Governments asked new questions; it was what governments did, the way rivers flooded and cats knocked things off tables. One filed the answers and one did not ask what happened downstream.

One did not, as a rule, go looking for the downstream office.

Xanthe touched the Ashgrove return, aligned its edges with the desk's edge — a habit, purely a habit — and reached for the next form in the stack.

And that was when the trolley came through.

* * *

The registry's trolleys were wooden carts with iron-rimmed wheels, and they were the single greatest affliction of Xanthe's professional life. They screamed along the corridors at all hours, pushed by junior clerks who had been told, in writing, that speed was a virtue in the handling of documents. Xanthe had composed, in the privacy of their own head, a formal memorandum on the subject of document handling, arguing that a record dropped on a corner or splashed by a leaking roof was a record damaged, and a damaged record was a small death of the truth, and — well. The memorandum existed only in Xanthe's head, filed there under *Someday*, in the same drawer where Xanthe kept the plan to learn the viol and the intention of someday visiting the northern lakes.

This trolley, however, was not the affliction. This trolley was an opportunity, though Xanthe did not know it yet, and would not know it for another four seconds, which in Xanthe's internal accounting was practically a lifetime.

It came around the corner too fast, as they all did, and the junior clerk pushing it — a boy named Pell, all elbows and anxiety — took the turn badly. The trolley's wheel caught the seam in the pine planking. The stack of volumes on the top shelf lurched. And one of them — a thick, gray-backed ledger with a brass clasp — slid off the edge, turned over twice in the air with the leisurely grace of a dying fish, and landed at Xanthe's feet, open, spine up, pages fanned across the floor like a bird that had flown into a window.

"I'm sorry — sir — I mean — I'm sorry," said Pell, in the tone of a boy who had broken things before and expected the universe to make him pay for each of them personally.

"It's quite all right," Xanthe said, already kneeling. "The damage is to the binding, not the contents. See? The pages are intact. A book is its pages; the rest is carpentry."

Pell stared at them with the expression of a boy who had never heard a book described as its pages before and was not sure whether this was comfort or heresy.

Xanthe gathered the fanned pages together, and that was when they saw the heading at the top of the open leaf.

**Register of Seized Estates and Goods — Harbor District — Third Year of the Regency.**

Xanthe's hands went still on the paper.

This was not a census ledger. This was not anything that had any business on a trolley bound for the returns room. Xanthe knew the registry's geography the way Pell presumably knew the streets he'd grown up in: returns to the east wall, taxation to the west, vital records in the locked annex, and the seizure ledgers — Xanthe had always assumed — in the sealed office on the second floor, the one with the new lock and the clerk who wore a sword to work, which in a records office was rather like wearing a sword to a library and made roughly the same amount of sense.

"Where is this going?" Xanthe asked, and was proud of how ordinary their voice sounded.

"The — the second floor, sir. I mean — the returns room first, to be cross-checked, and then up." Pell was reassembling his courage along with his trolley load. "They're doing a reconciliation. The seizure office and the tax rolls. Master Corvane wants the valuations verified against the assessments."

Master Corvane. The name arrived in Xanthe's mind and sat down there, uninvited, the way a large dog sits down on one's foot. Everyone in the registry knew the name Corvane. He was the inspector who had reorganized the harbor district's records two years ago, who had introduced the supplementary census forms, who was spoken of by senior clerks in the lowered, careful voices people used for weather that might turn.

"Reconciliation," Xanthe repeated. "Of course. Accuracy matters."

"Yes, sir." Pell hesitated. "Could I — should I take it, sir?"

Xanthe looked down at the open ledger. The page under their thumb listed entries in a firm, unlovely hand: names, parishes, descriptions of property, valuations, and dates of seizure. The ink was the standard registry ink. The hand was the standard registry hand. There was nothing remarkable about it at all, except that Xanthe's eye had already found the *A* section, because Xanthe's eye always found the alphabetical section, because Xanthe was a person who read a page the way other people breathed — automatically, completely, and without any possibility of stopping halfway.

**Ashgrove, Aldous. Print shop and dwelling, Tanner's Lane. Press (hand, iron frame), type (approx. 400 lb., mixed faces), stock, tools. Valuation: 640 crowns. Seized by order of the Inspectorate, 14th of the Frost Month.**

Xanthe read the date twice.

Fourteenth of the Frost Month. Xanthe knew that date. Not the way one knows a date from a ledger — the way one knows a date from a *death record*, because Xanthe had processed the death record eleven days later, because it had come through Xanthe's desk like everything else from the harbor district came through Xanthe's desk, because that was the system and the system was Xanthe's whole life.

Aldous Ashgrove had died of *misadventure* on the twenty-fifth of the Frost Month. Eleven days after the state had taken everything he owned — his press, his type, his tools, his livelihood, the entire material substance of his trade — while he continued, presumably, to live above the shop that was no longer his.

Xanthe knelt on the pine planking of the Bureau of Records and Returns and felt something shift in their chest. It was not a dramatic feeling. It was not a crack of thunder. It was smaller and worse than that: it was the feeling of a figure that does not sum. Eleven years of columns, and for the first time, a column that would not balance.

"Sir?" said Pell.

"Here." Xanthe closed the ledger — gently; it was not the ledger's fault — and held it out. "Mind the corner on the turn by the annex. The binding is already loose."

Pell took it, wheeled the trolley away at only a slightly less reckless speed, and was gone. The corridor settled back into its daytime noises: pens, pages, the distant scream of some other trolley, a clerk somewhere arguing mildly with a filing cabinet.

Xanthe stood, returned to the desk, and sat down. The Ashgrove census return was still there, with its small black dash. *Household diminished by one.*

Diminished. As if the household had been a thing that shrank on its own, naturally, like wool in the wash. As if it had not been *diminished by the state*, systematically, in a specific order: first the property, then the man, and then — Xanthe's mind supplied this with the terrible efficiency of a well-trained clerk — then the record. Because what came next, after a seizure and a death? A file went quiet. A name stopped appearing. The returns room had a shelf for closed households, and closed households stayed closed.

Xanthe picked up the pen. The next form in the stack was the cooper's household, the one with the brother of shifting surnames, and Xanthe looked at the little patient dot in its margin and felt, for the first time in eleven years, a flicker of something adjacent to shame. The cooper had been hiding a person from the state. Xanthe had been hunting him for it. And upstairs — or across the hall, or wherever the seizure office kept itself — the state was hiding things from *everyone*, and doing it with the same ink, the same forms, the same pine-planked corridors. The same system. *Xanthe's* system.

"No," Xanthe said quietly, to the desk, and then, because precision mattered even in self-rebuke: "That is not a sound conclusion. One ledger is not a policy. One coincidence of dates is not a murder."

But eleven days. And the shop had been worth six hundred and forty crowns, and the death had been called *misadventure* by a hand Xanthe did not recognize, entered not in the district register where deaths were properly recorded but on a supplementary form — the new form, the *Priority* form, the one that went downstream and did not come back.

Xanthe had processed that form. Xanthe had filed it. Xanthe had drawn the dash.

The morning passed the way mornings passed: forms, checks, annotations, the cooper's dot patiently carried forward. Xanthe was aware of performing the work rather than doing it, the way a person is aware of their own breathing. At the midday bell, the other clerks rose and filed out toward the pie shops, and Xanthe did not rise. Xanthe sat at the third desk from the window with a cold meat roll they had no appetite for, and thought.

The thought, when it finally assembled itself into words, was this: *A reconciliation requires two sets of records. The seizure office has its ledgers. The tax rolls have their assessments. But the census returns — the returns I file — record who actually lived where, and when, and until when.*

And Xanthe, who had spent eleven years making the census complete, was the only person in the building who could say, with dates and signatures and the full weight of a meticulous hand, exactly how many households in the harbor district had been *diminished* shortly after their property was seized. Not just Ashgrove. If there was a pattern, Xanthe could find it. If there was a pattern, it would be arithmetic, and arithmetic did not lie, and arithmetic could be *proven*.

Xanthe did not like where the thought went next, and went there anyway.

Because a pattern was not something one could unsee. And a pattern, once found, would have to go somewhere — and every official channel for it led, eventually, to the office with the new lock and the clerk who wore a sword.

The afternoon bell rang. The clerks returned smelling of onions and pastry. Xanthe aligned the stack of forms, squared the pen to the desk's edge, and worked until the seventh bell, and the work was flawless, and none of it felt like anything at all.

At the eighth bell, coat on the same peg taken down, lamp out, Xanthe paused at the corridor where the trolleys were parked for the night. The gray-backed ledgers stood in their row on the return shelf, awaiting the morning's trip upstairs. Xanthe counted them — a habit, purely a habit — and noted that there were nine, and noted which shelf, and noted that the night watchman made his rounds at the half hour and was fond of his pipe, which took him a quarter hour per round, and noted the window at the corridor's end that did not quite latch, which Xanthe had reported twice, in writing, and which had never been repaired.

Xanthe stood in the darkening registry and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like falling, that they were composing a plan. That the plan had a shape. That the shape was: *come back after the watchman's first round, copy what can be copied in the time the pipe allows, and leave no trace — because a clerk who leaves no trace is a clerk who survives to file another day.*

It was not the behavior of a man who trusted his institution.

Xanthe pulled the door shut, heard the lock catch, and walked home through streets that smelled of the harbor — tar, fish, and under it all, faintly, from the direction of Tanner's Lane, ink — rehearsing, all the way, the exact angle at which a page must be held to a copying hand by lamplight so that the light does not throw a shadow across the line.

* * *

The copying took three nights.

The first night, Xanthe's hands shook so badly that the entries came out looking like the work of a frightened spider, and Xanthe tore up the page and began again, and tore that up too, and went home having accomplished nothing except the discovery that fear has terrible penmanship.

The second night was better. The third night, Xanthe found the pattern.

It was in the dates. Seizure, then death — seizure, then death — the interval shrinking as the Regency's third year turned into its fourth, as though the office had grown more efficient, as though someone had noticed that eleven days was untidy and had brought the figure down. A cooper. A rope-maker. A widow who had owned two boats. A chandler whose name Xanthe remembered from the returns, whose household dash Xanthe had drawn *personally*, and whose death had been recorded as a fall on the stairs, and who had owned, per the seizure ledger, a stock of lamp oil worth four hundred crowns.

Seven households. Seven dashes in Xanthe's own margins. Seven small black marks that Xanthe had made in good faith, believing they recorded the world, when in fact they had been recording the world *as someone wanted it written*.

On the third night, in the cold corridor, by the light of a shuttered lamp, Xanthe copied the Ashgrove entry one final time — the name, the press, the six hundred and forty crowns, the date — and then, beneath it, in a hand smaller than any Xanthe had ever used, wrote the date of the death, and the word *misadventure*, and set a single question mark after it.

The question mark looked very small on the page. It looked like nothing at all. It looked like the smallest, most harmless mark a pen could make.

Xanthe folded the page into the lining of their coat, latched the window behind them, and walked home through the waking harbor with their heart going like a trolley with a bad wheel, knowing two things with absolute certainty: that the page could not go through any channel in the registry without destroying them, and that it could not — *could not* — sit in a coat lining doing nothing, because Xanthe had spent eleven years believing that a completed record was a small act of justice, and this page was a record, and it was not yet complete, and somewhere on Tanner's Lane there was a young woman who had lied to Xanthe about a press, and who was owed, at the very least, the truth.

Xanthe reached their door as the harbor bells rang the dawn, and stood a moment in the gray light, and admitted it plainly, the way one admits a sum has failed to balance:

The institution was not going to correct the record.

So the record would have to find its own way out — and Xanthe knew exactly whose door it would have to come knocking at.
