## 8. The Registry Fire

Xanthe Quill had a habit that their superiors would have called thoroughness and that Xanthe privately called survival.

It was this: whenever Xanthe copied a document into the registry's official ledgers, they copied it twice — once in the fair hand required by the office, and once in a small, cramped script in a private notebook bound in grey calfskin, which lived in the inside pocket of their coat, over the left breast, where a less honest man might keep a locket.

The habit had begun years ago, when Xanthe was new to the census service and had misfiled a return for the tannery on Cooper's Lane. The correction had taken three days of apologizing to a supervisor with breath like pickled herring, and Xanthe had sworn, standing in the stairwell with their ears still burning, never again to trust a record to exist in only one place. Paper burned. Paper got wet. Paper got borrowed by clerks who forgot to return it, or borrowed deliberately by clerks who very much intended not to. A record in one place was a rumor. A record in two places was a fact.

So on the evening before the fire, Xanthe sat at their desk in the Hall of Returns — a long room on the second floor of the registry, lit by forty candles because the city had decided oil lamps were too great an expense for people whose whole business was paper — and copied, in small cramped script, a page from the seizure ledger marked *Confiscations, Third Quarter*.

The page concerned the estate of one Aldous Ashgrove, printer, of Rope Walk in the harbor district.

Xanthe had not gone looking for it. That was the thing Xanthe would remember afterward, turning it over like a coin that kept coming up wrong side out: they had not gone looking. They had been filing the quarter's census returns — household after household, names and ages and trades, the great dull river of Vellamare flowing past their pen — and the Ashgrove return had come up in its turn, and beside it, because the returns were cross-indexed against the confiscation rolls since the spring reorganization, the ledger entry had been lying open on the reference shelf like a door someone had forgotten to lock.

Xanthe had read it the way one reads anything at the end of a long day: without meaning to, all at once.

*Estate of Aldous Ashgrove, printer. Seized under provision 12, seditious output. Appraised value, 340 crowns. Disposition: press and stock to the Office of Public Information; premises to the harbor development fund; personal effects entered as residue.*

And beneath that, in the column every clerk knew and no clerk discussed, the date of seizure — followed, four lines down, by a date in the coroner's margin, in a different hand entirely:

*Deceased. Stairwell fall. Ruled accidental.*

The dates were eleven days apart.

Eleven days. Xanthe had sat with their pen lifted, ink gathering at the nib in a fat black bead, and thought about eleven days. Eleven days was time enough for a man to appeal a seizure. It was time enough for him to hire a lawyer, or write letters, or say things in public about the fairness of provision 12. It was, if one were the sort of person who noticed such things — and Xanthe was paid, modestly, to be exactly that sort of person — time enough for a man to become inconvenient.

Or it was nothing. People fell on stairwells. Vellamare's stairwells were steep, badly lit, and slick with harbor damp half the year. The coroner's men wrote *stairwell fall* forty times a season. There was probably a rubber stamp.

Xanthe told themselves all of this while copying the page into the grey notebook, which was the trouble with habits: they acted before the mind finished arguing. The pen moved. The facts went down in two places. And then Xanthe blotted the notebook, closed it, buttoned the coat over it, and went home through the drizzle to a cold supper and a night of sleeping badly, waking at intervals to think *eleven days* and then firmly thinking about something else, the way one steps over a loose paving stone one has decided not to report.

* * *

The fire started, according to the watch, at some hour of the morning when no honest witness was awake to have an opinion about it.

Xanthe arrived at the Hall of Returns at half past seven, as always, to find the street in front of the registry occupied by three fire engines, a crowd of perhaps two hundred spectators eating roasted chestnuts with the solemn enjoyment of people who had been given a free spectacle before breakfast, and a great deal of smoke — not the roaring black kind, but a low, greasy, businesslike smoke seeping from the second-floor windows as though the building itself were quietly embarrassed.

"Second floor's gone," said a chestnut seller near the steps, with the satisfaction of a man delivering excellent news. "Whole of it, they're saying. Started in the records room."

"The records room," Xanthe repeated.

"Aye. Where the old papers are." He held out a twist of paper containing six chestnuts, apparently as a condolence offering. Xanthe took one automatically and did not taste it.

The second floor. The Hall of Returns. The reference shelves. The cross-indexed rolls. The seizure ledgers, third quarter, fourth quarter, back through the two years since the reorganization — the entire paper history of everything the state had taken from everyone in Vellamare, stacked in oiled oak cabinets that had been installed, Xanthe now recalled with a strange floating clarity, only last autumn. New cabinets. New shelving. New locks on the doors of the records room, ordered specially, because the old ones had been deemed insufficient.

A junior clerk named Pellory came hurrying down the steps with soot on his collar and his eyes shining with the particular excitement of a young man who has survived something and intends to enjoy describing it.

"Quill! Thank goodness, we couldn't find you — the supervisor wants everyone accounted for. You heard? Started in the night, smoldering, they think — slow burn, no flames till nearly dawn. The watch says the flues must have drawn the heat straight along the records room ceiling." He shook his head mournfully, a mourning that did not quite reach the rest of his face. "Everything's water-logged or ash. The ledgers, the indexes, the old parish copies — all of it. Twenty years of returns, gone."

"All of it," Xanthe said.

"Well — the current quarter's safe, they'd already been moved downstairs for binding. But the archive—" Pellory spread his hands, a gesture of terrible, gleeful helplessness. "Gone. Isn't it extraordinary? Of all the rooms. If it had been the binding shop, or the washroom, or anywhere else—"

"Yes," Xanthe said. "Extraordinary."

There was a word for what Xanthe was feeling, and the word was not grief, and it was not shock, and it took Xanthe another full minute, standing on the registry steps holding a chestnut they could not eat, to find it.

The word was *recognition*.

Because here was the shape of it, laid out plain as a printed page: a fire that began in the one room where the state's debts were written down. A slow burn, chosen — if chosen — precisely because a slow burn leaves no dramatic evidence, no screaming neighbors, no midnight rescue. New cabinets that would burn evenly. New locks that would keep out any clerk working late. A date — Xanthe's stomach turned over slowly, like a boat coming about — a date that fell, how many days after Xanthe had stood at the reference shelf reading about a printer and a stairwell? Nine days. Ten. Xanthe had not mentioned the Ashgrove page to anyone. Had not filed anything unusual. Had done nothing but copy it into a private notebook and carry it home over the left breast.

But Xanthe had requested the cross-indexed roll. That request was logged. Requests were always logged; Xanthe had helped design the logging system two years ago, in a burst of professional pride that now curdled in memory like milk left in sun.

Someone could have seen which pages a census taker had pulled. Someone could have wondered why. Someone could have decided that whatever a meticulous little census taker found interesting was worth making unfindable — and while they were at it, why not make everything unfindable? Every seized shop, every vanished estate, every widow's petition, every appeal that had died in a drawer. Not just one page erased. The whole shelf. The whole idea of a shelf.

"Quill?" Pellory was peering at them. "You've gone gray as your coat. Do sit down."

"I'm quite well," Xanthe said, and discovered their voice worked, which was reassuring. "I'm going to walk around the block. The smoke."

The smoke, indeed. Xanthe walked around four blocks, ending nowhere in particular, in a light rain that smelled of char even at that distance, and conducted the following conversation with themselves, silently, in the manner of a careful person taking inventory after a burglary:

Item: fires happen. Registry buildings are old; flues are wicked; the watch's account is plausible. Plausible. Everything about it was plausible. That was precisely what made the skin crawl — that no single fact, examined alone, proved anything. It was a document set in perfect type, every letter correct, and the lie living somewhere in the spacing between the words.

Item: if it was an accident, then Xanthe was a clerk standing in the rain doing arithmetic about coincidences, and ought to go home and laugh at themselves over supper.

Item: if it was not an accident, then Xanthe was carrying, in a grey calfskin notebook, the only surviving copy of a page that somebody with power had wanted very much not to exist — and that somebody believed, with reasonable confidence, that it no longer did.

Xanthe stopped under the awning of a chandler's shop and looked at the rain coming off the eaves in a beaded curtain, and felt the full weight of the item settle onto their shoulders like a wet coat.

Erasure was policy.

Not a scandal, not an aberration, not one corrupt inspector cutting corners — but policy, practiced calmly, funded properly, executed with good cabinetry. The state did not merely take things, Xanthe understood now, watching the smoke rise over the rooftops like a flag nobody would admit to flying. The state took things and then took the memory of the things, and then, in time, would take the question itself, so that a child born in five years would grow up in a city where Aldous Ashgrove had never owned a press, and the stairwell had never broken anyone, and the word *confiscation* referred to something that happened in other countries, long ago, to people who had probably deserved it.

And the truly horrifying part — Xanthe pressed two fingers against the pocket where the notebook lay, feeling its small rectangular heartbeat — the truly horrifying part was how well it worked. How nearly it had worked on Xanthe. Because there was no page anymore. There was no ledger, no index, no second copy in any archive in Vellamare. If Xanthe's habit had failed them for one evening — if they had been tired, if they had skipped the copying, if they had trusted the system they served — then the entry would exist now only in Xanthe's memory, and memories, Xanthe knew with professional precision, degraded faster than any paper. In a year Xanthe would remember *a printer, I think, in the harbor district*. In five years, nothing certain at all.

One person's habit had stood between the truth and the smoke. One person, and a notebook bought for four crowns at a stationer's on Candle Street.

Xanthe laughed — a short, appalled sound that made the chandler's boy look up from sweeping — and then stood very still while the laughter drained away and left behind it something colder and more durable.

Because here was the next item on the inventory, the one Xanthe had been circling: what did one do with a fact that the state had just spent a considerable sum of money, and presumably some degree of risk, to destroy?

Filing it was impossible. There was no longer a file. Reporting it through channels meant handing it to the very people who had lit the match, assuming channels reached that high — and Xanthe had begun, reluctantly, to suspect that channels reached exactly that high, and no higher, and that the top of the channel was where the matches were kept.

Which left, by elimination, the page itself. The name on the page. The dead printer, and — Xanthe closed their eyes and retrieved it, that first afternoon in the print shop on Rope Walk, the smell of lead and ink, the young woman with ink-stained fingers who had lied about who ran the press with a steadiness Xanthe had admired even while noting it for the record — the daughter.

Wren Ashgrove had lied to a census officer. Xanthe had known it within four minutes and let it stand, and had told themselves at the time that it was procedural leniency, a minor irregularity not worth a follow-up visit. That excuse had worn thin on the second visit and was now, standing in the rain, threadbare beyond repair. Xanthe had let the lie stand because some part of them — the part that kept the grey notebook, the part that copied everything twice — had already suspected that the official version of Aldous Ashgrove was set in false type.

And now the official version had been fed to a fire, and the daughter was the only other person in Vellamare who might understand what that meant.

Xanthe turned up the collar of their coat and began walking toward the harbor district, at first without deciding to, and then with great deliberation, noting as they went — because the habit would not stop, the habit was the only reliable thing left — the number of patrols on the streets, the fresh proclamations pasted over older proclamations, the way the fish market seemed quieter than a Tuesday should be.

The rain thickened. Somewhere behind them, the registry smoke thinned and dispersed, and by evening there would be nothing above the rooftops to show where twenty years of records had been, and the chestnut sellers would move on to better locations, and the city would get on with its day.

In Xanthe's inside pocket, over the left breast, eleven lines of cramped grey-ink script rode against Xanthe's heart: a dead man's name, a date of seizure, a date of death, and eleven days between them.

*Xanthe thought:* I am either about to make a terrible mistake, or the only correct one available.

The harbor smelled of tar and low tide. Rope Walk began at the corner where the chandlery gave way to the net-menders' sheds, and Wren Ashgrove's shop stood a third of the way down, its windows dark, its door shut, a strip of paper across the glass announcing in neat printed letters that the establishment was TEMPORARILY CLOSED FOR REPAIRS.

Xanthe stood across the street for a long moment, getting the words ready, knowing none of them would do.

Then they crossed.
