## 14. The Inspector's Dinner

The invitation arrived at the registry office on a card of heavy cream stock, edged in gilt, smelling faintly of rosewater and menace.

*Inspector Corvane requests the pleasure of Clerk Second Class Xanthe Vey's company at supper, Thursday next, seven o'clock, at his residence on the Lantern Terrace.*

Clerk Second Class. Not even First. The card managed to be flattering and diminishing in the same breath, which Xanthe suspected was a skill of long practice rather than accident. They turned it over twice, looking for instructions — dress code, whether one brought one's own wine, whether one brought one's own confession — and found nothing but the inspector's seal pressed into red wax: a closed hand holding a key.

"Good heavens," said Pellam from the next desk, craning over. "You've been *summoned*. Nobody gets summoned to the Lantern Terrace unless they're about to be promoted or arrested."

"Perhaps both," Xanthe said. "I could be promoted directly into a cell. It would save paperwork."

Pellam laughed. Pellam always laughed. That was why he would die a Clerk Second Class, comfortable and unexamined, and Xanthe had privately resolved years ago never to tell him that his laughter was the most useful thing in the building, because it let everyone else stop laughing before it became suspicious.

Xanthe spent Wednesday doing what they always did with dread: organizing it. They laid out their second-best coat — the best would look like ambition, the third-best like insult — brushed their boots until they could have seen the harbor lights in them, and rehearsed answers to questions that had not yet been asked. This last habit was old. It went back to childhood, to a mother who asked questions like an auditor and a father who answered them like a man reading from a prepared statement, and Xanthe had learned early that the only safe position between those two people was to have already thought through everything.

They were thorough. Everyone said so. It was meant as praise. Lately, Xanthe had begun to hear it differently.

* * *

The Lantern Terrace was called that because of the row of bronze lanterns along its balustrade, each one burning behind glass against the sea wind, so that the whole street glowed like a string of held breaths. Below it, the harbor district lay dark and ordinary — the fish market shuttered, the taverns muttering, the print shops cold. From up here, you could almost believe the city was peaceful. You could believe anything, up here. That was presumably the point of the elevation.

Corvane's house was not large by the standards of the terrace, which meant it was enormous by any other measure. A servant took Xanthe's coat, another took their hat, and a third, apparently, took inventory of their face, because the man looked at them a moment too long before gesturing them toward the dining room.

Inspector Corvane stood at the window with his back to the door, hands clasped behind him, watching the harbor. He was smaller than his reputation. Xanthe had expected something monumental — the architect of the confiscations, the man whose signature sat at the bottom of more seizures than any other in Vellamare's history — and instead found a neat, silvering person in a plum-colored waistcoat, built rather like a well-kept ledger himself: everything entered, nothing wasted.

"Clerk Vey." He did not turn around. "Do you know what I'm looking at?"

"The harbor, sir."

"The harbor," Corvane agreed. "Forty-one vessels at anchor. Six permitted to sail this week. Do you know how I know that? Because I count. Counting is the whole of government, Clerk Vey. Everything else is embroidery." Now he turned, and his smile was warm the way a stove is warm — pleasant at a distance, dangerous to touch. "You count too. I've read your returns."

"I try to be accurate, sir."

"Accurate." He tasted the word. "Most people mean obedient when they say accurate. I don't think you do. Come, sit. The soup waits for no one, and my cook takes insults to her soup worse than insults to her inspector."

The table was set for two, which Xanthe had half expected and still found unsettling; dinners for two were interrogations with cutlery. The soup was a pale gold thing with saffron floating on it like little flags of surrender. It was, infuriatingly, delicious.

"You've been with the census five years," Corvane said, ladling with his own hands, refusing the servant who hovered. "Registry before that. Your error rate is — I made a note — one discrepancy in eleven thousand entries. Eleven thousand." He shook his head with genuine admiration. "I once had a clerk who averaged one error per page. He now runs a customs house in the southern provinces, where his errors are someone else's problem. But you. You are a different animal entirely."

"Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me yet. Accuracy is a virtue right up until it becomes a liability." He smiled again, and ate three spoonfuls with the unhurried precision of a man who had never once burned his mouth. "Tell me about the Ashgrove return."

The saffron turned to lead in Xanthe's mouth. They had known — had *known*, since the card arrived — that this question was coming, and had rehearsed four versions of the answer, and now all four evaporated like water on a hot press plate.

"A routine household survey, sir. Widow's household — no. Late printer's daughter, sole occupant. One dwelling above the premises, one press, one apprentice listed as—"

"As a resident laborer, yes, I've read it. What interests me isn't what's in it. It's what isn't." Corvane set down his spoon and folded his hands, and Xanthe understood suddenly that the whole dinner was this moment, and everything before it had been the soup. "A master printer dies — falls, tragically, from his own roof while repairing a gutter, in dry weather, a man of sixty with no history of dizziness — and within a month his estate is seized, his ledgers transferred, his press requisitioned for state work, and his daughter continues operating as if nothing has changed. And my census clerk files a return noting all of this with perfect accuracy and asks—" he paused, delicately, "—no questions at all."

Xanthe kept their eyes on the soup. "The scope of my office is household composition, sir. Cause of death belongs to the coroner. Estate matters belong to the seizure bureau."

"Yes. Yes, they do." Corvane leaned back, and for a moment the warmth in his face seemed real, or real enough, the way a painted fire is warm if you don't think about it. "That is precisely the correct answer. It is the answer a careful man gives. And I invited you here tonight, Clerk Vey, because I want to know whether you are a careful man or merely a frightened one. The two are often confused. Even by themselves."

The second course came — fish, naturally, in this city, done with some sauce that had probably been confiscated along with everything else — and Corvane talked pleasantly through it, about the weather, about the new paving on the Fishgate road, about a production of a comedy at the Grand Theatre that he had found insufficiently funny. Xanthe answered in monosyllables polished smooth. Somewhere beneath the conversation, beneath the clink of forks, ran the other conversation, the real one, conducted entirely in subtext:

*I know you noticed.*

*Notice what, sir?*

*The gutter. The dry weather. The girl printing alone.*

*I file what I am given, sir.*

*And what are you given, Clerk Vey? Who gives it to you?*

And Xanthe, smiling, passing the salt: *No one, sir. I am given nothing. I only count.*

"You know," Corvane said, over the cheese, "when I first came to Vellamare, eight years ago, this was a disorderly city. Printers printing whatever they liked. Rumor moving faster than the post. A lie could cross the harbor before the truth had found its boots." He sliced the cheese with small, exact movements. "Now every press in this district is either state-licensed or silent, and the ones that aren't licensed are learning silence very quickly. Do you know how we did it?"

"Thoroughness, sir?"

"Bookkeeping." He said it with the tenderness other men reserved for their mothers. "Every press registered. Every registration tied to a name. Every name tied to a family, a debt, a weakness. You cannot burn a rumor, Clerk Vey. But you can burn a ledger, and a ledger is where rumors go to become facts." He pointed the cheese knife, gently, like a tutor. "Which is why people like you matter. People who make the records true. Or true enough."

"And if the records are wrong, sir?"

The room went very quiet. The servants had, Xanthe realized, quietly withdrawn; the door to the kitchen stood shut; the only sound was the sea wind worrying at the lanterns outside.

"Wrong," Corvane repeated, softly, savoring it. "What an interesting word. Not *incomplete*. Not *outdated*. Wrong." He poured wine — two fingers each, no more, a man who never drank enough to loosen anything, including his tongue. "Let me tell you a story. Once there was a clerk — clever, meticulous, promoted young — who began to wonder about things outside his scope. Coroners' reports. Seizure schedules. Whether a certain fire in a certain records annex had really started, as the report says, from a carelessly set lamp." He watched Xanthe over the rim of his glass. "And this clerk wondered, and wondered, and one day he wondered his way into a conversation exactly like this one, and he understood — sitting across from a man who was being perfectly courteous to him — that courtesy was the message. That the dinner itself was the warning. That he was being told: *we see you counting.*"

"What happened to him?" Xanthe asked. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them; inside, something was running very fast in a small circle.

"He got a promotion," Corvane said. "To a desk with better light and less interesting papers. And he lived a long, comfortable life, because he understood the lesson." He set down the glass. "Vellamare is entering a new season, Clerk Vey. There are elements in this city — printers, dockworkers, the usual romantic debris — who believe the old disorder can come back. It cannot. When the new order settles, there will be two kinds of people in it: those who helped build the record, and those the record was built about. I would very much like you in the first category. You're far too good at your work for the second."

"Thank you, sir."

"And one more thing." Corvane rose, signaling the evening's end, and walked Xanthe personally to the door, where the servant waited with their coat like a man waiting to close a book. At the threshold, the inspector put a hand briefly on Xanthe's shoulder — light, paternal, terrible. "Your neighbor on Candle Lane. The widow Ferren. She mentioned she'd seen you walking in the harbor district after hours. Twice this month." His smile was kind. "Counting, I assume."

"Always, sir."

"Of course you are." He patted the shoulder once. "Mind the gutters, Clerk Vey. This city has a great many roofs, and the weather is not always dry."

* * *

Xanthe walked home the long way, which was to say the way that passed no one's window and offered no one a view. The lanterns of the terrace shrank behind them. Below, the harbor district breathed its low nighttime breathing — a dog, a drunk, a ship's bell marking the watch — and every sound of it sounded, tonight, like a door closing somewhere ahead of them.

They had gone in expecting to be recruited or destroyed. Instead they had been *measured*, which was worse, because measurement preceded both. Corvane knew about the harbor walks. Which meant someone had reported them — the widow Ferren, or someone wearing the widow Ferren, or simply a name invented to let Xanthe know that names were currency the inspector traded in daily. Which meant the net around Wren Ashgrove's shop was not closing. It had already closed, quietly, months ago, and everyone inside it had been living since then in the comfortable illusion of open air.

And Corvane had said *the records annex*. Casually. In passing. A fire Xanthe had copied pages out of the night before it burned — pages listing Ashgrove among estates taken *before* the owner's fall — and the inspector had mentioned it over cheese as though remarking on the weather.

He didn't know what Xanthe had copied. But he suspected that something had walked out of that building on two legs, and he was checking the legs.

At home, Xanthe lit no lamp. They sat in the dark at their own small desk, in their own small rooms, and took out the copied page — Ashgrove, dated nine days before the gutter, signed with a seal they had traced once and never dared trace again — and looked at it for a long time.

Filing it through channels would destroy it. Keeping it was a slow arrest. Burning it was the promotion Corvane had described: better light, duller papers, a long comfortable life.

Xanthe thought of the census return, the neat columns, the daughter listed as sole occupant, operating alone. *Who operates the press?* they had asked her, and she had lied, badly, and they had noted the lie with professional satisfaction and filed it away like a specimen.

The specimen was looking at them now from the copied page. Nine days. A dry night. A gutter.

There was one place the page could go where it might become something other than evidence of Xanthe's ruin. One person it named. One shop on Candle Lane with a press that, according to every record Xanthe had ever filed, belonged to a dead man.

In the morning, they would need a reason to walk the harbor district again. They found, to their mild horror, that they did not need to invent one.
