## 26. Sworn in a Cellar

The candle had been lit for an hour before anyone spoke about why they were there.

That was Odile's doing. She believed — and said so often enough that the rest of them had stopped arguing — that no oath should be sworn on an empty stomach, because a hungry man swears too quickly just to get to the food, and a full man swears slowly, knowing what he's committing to. So the cellar of her house on Salt Lane had been transformed, by degrees and with considerable clattering, into something between a chapel and a kitchen.

Bram sat on an upturned herring barrel with a bowl of barley soup in his hands, watching the candle flame bend every time the door above opened and shut. He was a large man who had spent eleven years learning to make himself small — first as a soldier, where bigness got you noticed by sergeants looking for volunteers, and then as a deserter, where it got you noticed by everyone. He had gotten good at folding himself into corners. But there was no corner in Odile's cellar that fit him, so he sat in the middle like a piece of furniture nobody had ordered, and he ate his soup.

"You're holding the spoon wrong," Odile told him.

"I've held a spoon since before you were born."

"Then your mother failed you." She took the bowl from him anyway, ladled more into it, and gave it back. This was how she loved people: by correcting them and feeding them in the same breath, so that you could never quite tell which was which, and eventually stopped trying.

There were seven of them below ground that night. Wren Ashgrove, ink still under her fingernails from whatever she'd been setting in type upstairs before dark. Xanthe, the census clerk, sitting very straight on a crate of pickled onions as though the onions might be called upon to testify later and needed to be addressed formally. Teo, the apprentice, fifteen years old and wearing the particular expression of a boy who has recently done something brave and cannot decide whether to be proud or terrified, so has settled on both at once. Old Father Anselm, defrocked and unbothered, who had brought his own candle stub because he distrusted other people's candles. A docker named Pell who smelled permanently of tar and rope. And Odile herself, presiding.

And Bram. Who had come to swear to what he'd seen, and who had been dreading this evening for nine days, and who was therefore extremely grateful for the soup.

* * *

It had been Xanthe's idea, originally — or rather, Xanthe's audit. Two nights ago, in the print shop, going through the disguised pages of the true account page by page with the patience of a person counting someone else's money, Xanthe had looked up and said, "Three signatures missing."

Wren had said, "Whose?"

"Bram's account of the raid. Father Anselm's record of the burials. And Pell's deposition on the cargo manifests." Xanthe had laid the pages flat, aligned their edges, and added, in the tone of someone reporting a fire in a neighboring building, "An unsigned testimony is worth nothing. Less than nothing. It's a rumor with good posture. If Corvane's historians get hold of this book and find blank lines where names should be, they'll say we invented everything, and they'll be half right."

So here they all were, underground, eating barley soup by candlelight, preparing to put their names to paper in a house that could be raided by morning. Nobody had said that last part aloud. Everybody was thinking it. You could hear it in the way Teo kept glancing at the ceiling.

* * *

Odile fed them in order of seniority, which she determined herself and which did not correspond to age. Bram went third, after Anselm and Pell, on the grounds that deserters were junior to priests but senior to clerks. Xanthe accepted this ruling without comment, which Bram found interesting. He had watched the clerk now for long enough to know that Xanthe catalogued everything — slights, favors, the exact number of times Odile refilled a bowl — and filed it all away somewhere behind those careful eyes. A man built like a ledger. Bram trusted ledgers. Ledgers, at least, told you honestly when they'd been cooked.

"So," Odile said at last, setting down the ladle with the finality of a judge taking up the gavel. "Names."

She had cleared a space on the washing table — scrubbed raw, covered with a cloth she'd clearly ironed for the occasion, because even treason deserved a clean linen — and laid out the pages Wren had printed. Three documents. The raid. The burials. The manifests. Beside them, an inkwell, four pens cut from goose quills, and a stick of sealing wax that Odile had produced from somewhere with the air of a woman revealing she'd been carrying a knife all along.

"We go oldest first," she said. "Father Anselm."

The old priest rose with the creak of a ship's rigging. He was seventy if he was a day, bald as an egg, with hands that shook until the moment they touched something holy or official, at which point they went steady as a mason's. He read his own account through twice, lips moving. It was two pages long and concerned the night in the autumn when the bodies of three men — dockers, seized in the first wave of arrests — had been left at the gate of the poorhouse, and how Anselm had buried them without licenses, without registers, without anything but a shovel and a memory, because the state had declared the men never to have existed.

"The names are all here," Anselm said. "Jorin Vale. Matti Coss. The boy, Aldric — he was nineteen." His voice didn't waver. His hands, holding the pen, didn't either. "I baptized Aldric. I can spell his name in my sleep."

He signed. Then he melted the wax, pressed his thumb into it beside the signature, and said, in Latin, something old and formal that made the candle flames seem to stand straighter.

"What's that mean?" Teo whispered.

"It means he's done this before," Odile said. "Next. Pell."

Pell's deposition was shorter — the manifests, the crates marked as salt that weighed like iron, the customs officer who'd looked the other way and been promoted for it. Pell signed with a laborious X, then insisted on adding his mark beneath it, a little anchor he'd been drawing since childhood, and then, when Xanthe gently suggested that an anchor was not a legal signature, said, "It's mine, isn't it? More than my name is. My name's on a dozen debt papers. The anchor's only ever been on honest work."

Xanthe considered this with the gravity of a magistrate and ruled, "Both. Name and mark." Pell beamed like a man knighted.

Then it was Bram's turn, and the soup in his stomach turned to lead.

* * *

He had written the account himself, three drafts of it, in a hand gone clumsy from years of carrying pikes instead of pens. Wren had set it in type without changing a word, which he understood was a kind of mercy. Other people would have softened it. She hadn't. It said what it said:

*On the night of the ninth of Frostmonth, I was one of twelve soldiers under Sergeant Hallow detailed to the premises of Ashgrove & Son, printers, Salt Lane. Our orders, given verbally by Inspector Corvane's aide, were to secure the press and the type and to detain the proprietor. The proprietor, Elias Ashgrove, offered no resistance. He asked only that his daughter's belongings be left untouched. Sergeant Hallow struck him with the butt of his halberd at the foot of the stairs. He struck him again when he was down. I counted five blows. Afterward the sergeant told us the prisoner had fallen. We carried the body out through the yard and the report was written as an accidental death. I was paid two silver marks for my silence, as were the others. I have kept the marks. They are sewn into this statement's backing, should any doubt me.*

They had argued about that last line. Xanthe had wanted the coins physically attached to the document, as evidence. Wren had pointed out that sewing coins into a page meant cutting the page open to prove it, and that Corvane's men would love an excuse to handle the original. In the end the coins stayed sewn in, and everyone pretended this was a reasonable decision, and Bram privately thought it was mad, and privately loved them all for it.

Now he stood, and the barrel creaked under the loss of him, and he picked up the pen.

His hand shook. Not from fear, exactly. From the strangeness of it — eleven years of being the man who kept quiet, who looked at his boots, who learned the soldier's first prayer (*let it be someone else's turn*) — and here he was, about to write his name under a sentence that would hang him if the wrong eyes ever saw it. *I counted five blows.* Five. He'd counted them the way you count anything you can't stop: helplessly, precisely, forever.

"Take your time," Wren said quietly. "The ink doesn't dry fast in a cold room."

That helped, oddly. That she knew about cold rooms and slow ink. That she wasn't rushing him toward the thing that had killed her father.

He wrote his name. Bram Hollis. It looked strange on the page, small and ordinary, like a sparrow sitting on a cannon. Then he pressed his thumb into the wax Odile held ready, and the wax took the print cleanly, whorls and all, and Odile said, "There. Now the state knows exactly whose thumb to break, and we know exactly whose word stands behind it. Fair trade."

"That's not comforting," Bram said.

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true. Sit down and finish your soup."

* * *

Teo went last, and that was deliberate too, though Bram suspected the boy didn't know it. Odile had arranged the order so that the youngest would sign onto a page already crowded with older, tougher names — so that when his turn came, he wouldn't be swearing alone into the dark, but joining a company. Whether Teo saw the kindness of it or simply the logistics, Bram couldn't tell. The boy read his statement — his confession, really, about the weeks he'd carried information to Corvane's men under threat to his mother — and his ears went red as brick, and his pen hovered.

"They'll call me a traitor," Teo said. "In the book. Forever."

"They'll call you a boy who was threatened and who came back and told the truth," Wren said. "Those are different words. We chose the second ones."

"And if the readers choose the first?"

"Then they can write their own book," Odile said, "and see how they like being fifteen with a sick mother and a sergeant asking questions." She put a hand on the back of his neck, briefly, the way you'd steady a fence post. "Sign, love. Your mother's name goes in the acknowledgments whether you like it or not. Wren's already set the type."

Teo signed. His letters were beautiful — the apprentice's hand, drilled into him by Elias Ashgrove himself — and seeing that handwriting, so like his father's, made Wren look away for a moment toward the candle, and Bram pretended not to notice, because some griefs prefer to be unobserved.

* * *

Afterward, Xanthe collected the pages, checked each signature against a list — of course there was a list — and pronounced the account complete. "Every testimony witnessed, every witness named, every name spelled correctly." A pause. "Except Pell's anchor, which is spelled however anchors are spelled."

"Correctly," said Pell.

Then Odile did the last thing, the thing Bram would remember longest. She took the bread that had been warming on a stone near the candle — a round loaf, cracked gold on top, absurdly domestic for a cellar full of conspirators — and broke it, and gave a piece to every one of them, starting with Anselm and ending with herself.

"In the old country," she said, "when fishermen swore a share agreement, they ate together after. My grandmother said a contract signed hungry gets broken hungry." She looked around the circle, at the priest and the deserter and the docker and the clerk and the boy and the printer. "We're not fishermen. What we've signed tonight won't divide any catch. Half of us may not live to see it read. So eat. Because if this is the last supper any of us get, I'll not have it remembered as barley soup and trembling."

"It was excellent barley soup," said Anselm solemnly.

"It was adequate barley soup," said Odile, pleased.

They ate. And somewhere in the eating, without anyone deciding it, the cellar stopped being a meeting and became something else — Pell telling a story about a mule that had once refused to board a customs barge and been right to; Anselm correcting his theology; Teo laughing for the first time in weeks, a startled bark of a laugh he seemed surprised to own; Xanthe actually smiling, small and crooked, like a lock turning. Bram sat among them with bread in his hand and thought: *this is what I deserted toward.* Not safety. He'd known there was no safety. This. A family made of oaths instead of blood, sworn over soup in a hole in the ground, stronger than any regiment he'd ever served in, because every man in it had chosen to be there and could leave and hadn't.

He almost said so. He was saved from the embarrassment of sincerity by the sound of boots overhead.

* * *

Everyone froze. Seven people, mid-chew, silent as the dead Anselm had buried.

The boots crossed the floor above — unhurried, two sets, maybe three — paused near the front room, and moved on. A neighbor's lodgers, most likely. Or a patrol walking its rounds. Nothing. Probably nothing.

But the warmth had gone out of the room the way heat leaves a stone, and when Odile finally exhaled and said, "Well. Someone fetch the rest of that loaf up before it stales," her voice was lighter than her eyes.

They climbed the ladder one at a time. Xanthe went first with the sealed pages wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, and Wren followed, and at the top of the ladder Wren stopped and looked back down at Bram, still on his barrel.

"Five blows," she said softly. "You counted them. You wrote it down. Do you know what that's worth? Every historian who ever writes about this will have to count them too, because you made them countable. That's not absolution, Bram. It's better. It's arithmetic."

She went up. Bram sat alone in the cooling cellar for a moment, next to the empty bowls and the guttering candle, and let the words settle into him like sediment into clear water.

Then he climbed up after them, into the dark of Salt Lane — and stopped dead on the bottom stair.

Through the window, across the lane, lanterns were moving along the harbor wall. Not patrol lanterns, strung loose and swinging. Naval lanterns, ranked and regular, a procession of them, and beneath them figures working with ropes and cranes at the quay where the *Merrow's Grace* — the last neutral ship, due to sail on tomorrow's tide — lay at her moorings.

Soldiers were loading her. Or unloading her. At midnight, it was impossible to tell which, and somehow it didn't matter, because either way it meant the same thing.

Somebody had reached the harbor first.
