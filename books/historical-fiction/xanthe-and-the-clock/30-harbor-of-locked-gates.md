## 30. Harbor of Locked Gates

The notice went up on the customs house door before the gulls had finished their breakfast, and by the time Odile Perrin had sold her first two baskets of sardines, the whole fish market knew what it said.

*By order of the Inspectorate of Ports and Harbors, all vessels in the roadstead of Vellamare are hereby requisitioned for state service. No ship of any registry shall clear the harbor until further notice. Further notice will not be forthcoming.*

Well. The last part wasn't written down, but everyone understood it anyway. Odile had lived her fifty-three years by the harbor's one great law, which was that nothing ever left Vellamare when the Inspectorate wanted it kept, and everything left when the Inspectorate wasn't looking, and the entire difference between those two conditions could be purchased for the right price from the right person.

The right person, in this case, was Deputy harbormaster Lucien Vasseur.

Odile stood at her stall with a sardine in one hand and her whole mind already at work, the way a netter stands at the rail reading the water before casting. Around her the market did what the market always did, which was to say it grieved loudly, schemed quietly, and sold fish through both.

"They've taken the *Meridian*," wailed Marta from the eel stall, who took every piece of bad news as a personal affront arranged specifically against her. "The *Meridian*! My cousin's husband works her rigging! Requisitioned! As if she were a wheelbarrow!"

"She's a neutral ship, is what she is," said old Perrin — no relation, though he'd claimed cousinhood whenever it suited him for thirty years. "Vellamare flag, foreign papers. They can't requisition a neutral."

"They just did," said Marta.

"They've *written down* that they did," Odile corrected. "There's a difference. Paper says a thing; the harbor decides what the thing is worth."

Three heads turned toward her with the slow, hopeful gravity of gulls eyeing a dropped loaf. Odile sighed inwardly. This was the trouble with being the one who got things done: people looked at you afterward like you owed them the getting-done as a personal service.

"I'm not saying I can do anything," she said, which in the grammar of the fish market meant *I can do something, but it will cost, and I will collect.*

* * *

The economy of favors in the harbor district was older than the Inspectorate, older than the customs house, older, some said, than the harbor itself, which had after all been dug by men who were paid in favors and salt fish. Odile had spent a lifetime building her credit in it the way other people built fortunes in coin, and she knew its rules the way she knew the tide tables.

Rule one: never ask for a favor the same week you grant one. It looked like trade. Favors had to look like love, or at least like habit.

Rule two: never go straight to the man you need. Go to the man who eats with him.

Rule three: whatever you're asking for, pretend it's smaller than it is. Nobody sells you a harbor gate; they'll sell you a door that happens to be open at a certain hour.

She spent the morning collecting. Not coin — coin was crude and left a trail — but obligations. She gave Marta the good position at the head of the lane for tomorrow, which cost her nothing and would be remembered for a month. She sent her boy Jules — not her boy by blood, but by the older arrangement of a dead sister and a promise — to carry a bucket of fresh-caught whiting to the widow of the lighthouse keeper, who had not asked for it and would therefore owe her. She stopped at the chandler's and paid, in cash, for a coil of rope she did not need, because the chandler's wife was Vasseur's sister-in-law and cash given freely to family was the purest currency in the district.

By noon she had a map of the deputy harbormaster's week drawn in favors, and it looked like this: Vasseur ate his midday meal at the Tête de Poisson on Tuesdays and Thursdays, in the corner booth, alone, with a carafe of the cheap white wine he pretended not to prefer. On Tuesdays he was sour. On Thursdays he was paid.

Today was Thursday.

* * *

The Tête de Poisson smelled of garlic, fry oil, and forty years of spilled wine soaking into floorboards that had given up caring. Odile arrived at the booth just as Vasseur was tearing his second piece of bread, and she did not ask to sit. She sat. In the economy of favors, hesitation was a tax.

"Deputy harbormaster." She set down a small parcel, wrapped in oilcloth, on the table between them. Inside it: two smoked eels, from Marta's stall, from the batch Marta had sworn were too good to sell. "From a friend who admires thoroughness in public servants."

Vasseur looked at the parcel the way a cat looks at a hand that has scratched it before and once, memorably, held a spray bottle. "Madame Perrin. I'm eating."

"You are. And it's a crime what they charge for fish in here, given we're sitting on top of the entire sea." She helped herself to the wine and poured two cups, which was either an act of friendship or an act of ownership, and Vasseur's face said he couldn't decide which, which was exactly where she wanted him.

"I know why you're here," he said.

"You don't, or you'd have finished the eels already."

He almost smiled. That was the thing about Vasseur that the Inspectorate had never understood: he was not a true believer, he was a tired one. He had spent twenty years stamping papers for men in clean coats who had never once smelled bilge water, and he had the particular corruption of the tired — he would do anything, but only for people who understood that he was doing them a favor and not the reverse.

"The requisition order," Odile said. "Terrible business. I hear the *Meridian*'s master is in a state. Crying at his moorings. Very embarrassing for the harbor, a grown man crying."

"The order came from Corvane's office."

"I know. I also know orders are written by clerks and un-written by deputies. It's the whole architecture of this city." She leaned in, and lowered her voice to the register she used for telling Jules the truth about his mother, gently, once, the only time. "Lucien. There's a ship that needs to clear on the morning tide. One ship. Quietly. Papers in order, cargo declared, nothing in it that would embarrass you even if you looked — and you won't look."

"And if Corvane asks me why a neutral vessel left a locked harbor?"

"Corvane asked you to requisition the *Meridian* because it was the only ship due to sail. He wants the harbor sealed and everything in it. What he does not want — what no man in a clean coat ever wants — is a record showing the seal leaked. You won't tell him, I won't tell him, and the ship's master will swear on his mother's grave the gate was open when he came through it, which it will have been. Gates are only locked, Lucien, when somebody's watching them."

Vasseur turned his cup slowly on the table, around and around, a man negotiating with himself and losing. "You're asking me to risk my post."

"I'm asking you to remember who kept your sister-in-law's husband out of the press gang in the winter of the bread riots. And who found the surgeon for your mother's leg when the charity ward had her waiting eleven weeks. And who — " Odile paused, delicately, the way one pauses before the final course — "knows what's in the second ledger. The one you keep under the floor tile in your office, because the first ledger, the honest one, would get you hanged."

The cup stopped turning.

"That," Vasseur said quietly, "is a very ugly thing to say in a public house."

"It's a very ugly thing to keep under a floor tile. We're both ugly today. It's Thursday."

For a long moment the only sound was the fry cook shouting at an apprentice and the gulls conducting their eternal litigation over the midden heap. Then Vasseur exhaled through his nose, the sigh of a man surrendering to a force older than the Inspectorate.

"Morning tide," he said. "One ship. And the gate fee — " he named a figure that made even Odile, who had buried two husbands and out-haggled a customs commissioner, blink — "in coin, delivered by someone who has never spoken to you in his life."

"Done."

"And Perrin — " his voice dropped further, and something in it changed, the tiredness sharpening into something almost like fear — "whatever's on that ship, whatever's in those crates your people have been hauling around the district like hymnals, which is what they say they are and which I am choosing, God help me, to believe — make sure it's worth what you're spending. Because if Corvane catches this, he won't stop at the ship. He'll take the whole street. You. The fishwife. The boy."

Odile stood, took her parcel back, and put the eels in his hands anyway, closing his fingers over them.

"They're hymnals," she said. "Sing something nice at my funeral."

* * *

The coin was the problem, of course. It was always the problem. Favors could buy a gate opened, but Vasseur wanted his gate fee in hard silver, and the harbor's economy of love did not extend to bribing its own deputy harbormaster.

She called the circle together that evening in her kitchen, over a stew that had been stretched with water and hope until it was more of a fish-flavored rumor than a meal. Wren sat with her hands wrapped around a cup she wasn't drinking, wearing the look of a woman doing arithmetic with her whole life. Xanthe sat beside her, upright as a filing cabinet, taking notes nobody had asked for. Bram hovered by the door out of soldier's habit, and Teo — young Teo, who had been fed and forgiven and folded back into them like a stitch into a seam — was washing bowls because washing bowls was what Teo did when the world frightened him, and the world had been frightening him continuously for a week.

"The gate fee is forty crowns," Odile said.

Silence. Forty crowns was a year of fish. Forty crowns was the press's worth in scrap lead. Forty crowns was the kind of sum that existed in this kitchen only in the way that dragons exist in stories.

"I have eleven," said Wren.

"I have six," said Bram, "and a knife I could sell, and a conscience I couldn't."

"The monastery copies are worth nothing until they're printed," Xanthe said, "and the printed sheets are worth nothing if they stay in this kitchen. The arithmetic is ugly but it only runs one direction. We need the ship."

"I'll sell the stall," Odile said, before she had fully decided to, and then heard herself say it and was surprised at how little it hurt. "The pitch, the awning, the scales. Marta will buy it, and Marta will let me work it Sundays, because Marta is a vulture but she's a vulture with a heart."

"You can't," said Wren. "That stall is — it's your whole — "

"It's fish," Odile said. "The stall is fish. What you're carrying on that ship is the first true account anyone will read of what was done to this city, and I have been selling things my whole life, and I am telling you as a professional: this is the only thing on the market worth forty crowns."

Teo set down the bowl he was washing. "I'll take it to him," he said. "The coin. To the deputy. You said someone who's never spoken to you — that's me. I've never spoken to you in my life, Madame Perrin. Ask anyone."

Everyone looked at him. He was nineteen and had the particular thinness of a boy who had been frightened for a long time and had decided, recently and permanently, to be brave instead, which is a decision the body pays for before the spirit does.

"It's a delivery," Odile said slowly. "Not a mission."

"It's forty crowns to a man at the harbor. I've run deliveries since I was twelve." He almost smiled. "And I owe this circle more than bowls."

Odile looked at Wren. Wren looked at the table. And Odile, who had buried two husbands, raised one orphan, fed a deserter in her cellar, and out-stubborned an Inspectorate, felt the old fishwife's instinct rise in her — the one that reads weather in a flat sky and knows when the water is too calm.

"Tomorrow," she said. "After the market opens, so the lanes are full and busy. And Teo — if anyone follows you, you drop the coin in the first drain and you walk away and you come home. The coin is replaceable. You are not. Are we clear?"

"Clear," said Teo.

He was smiling when he said it. That was the part she would remember afterward, in the bad hours: he was smiling, and the lanes would be full and busy, and the water was too calm, and she had let him go anyway, because the tide waited for no one and the truth does not keep.

She slept badly, which for Odile Perrin meant she slept three hours instead of five, and rose in the dark to salt the morning's catch for a stall she had already sold, and stood at her door watching the harbor lights through the last of the night — the *Meridian* at her moorings, the customs house dark, the gate that would be open at morning tide if a boy with forty crowns and a smile did what he'd promised.

The water was very calm. The gulls had gone quiet.

"Come home by noon," she said to the dark, to the boy, to no one, and the harbor, which remembered everything, said nothing back.
