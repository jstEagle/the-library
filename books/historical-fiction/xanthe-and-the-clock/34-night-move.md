## 34. Night Move

Bram had spent four years learning the drainage tunnels of Vellamare as a soldier, and he had hated every hour of it. The tunnels smelled of everything the city wanted to forget — tannery runoff, night soil, the brine that seeped up through the old Roman brick whenever the tide pressed hard against the harbor wall. His sergeant had called the patrols "character-building." Bram had built a great deal of character down there in the dark, mostly of the kind that made him desert.

Now he was back in the tunnels by choice, carrying a crate of seditious hymnals on his shoulder, and he found he didn't mind the smell at all. Fear, it turned out, was an excellent seasoning. It made even corruption taste like freedom.

"Left here," he whispered over his shoulder.

"I know," said Wren behind him. "You said left. I heard you say left."

"You're going right."

There was a pause in the dark, the particular pause of a proud woman conceding a small point. Then her boots scraped leftward, and the crate on Xanthe's shoulder behind her bumped against the tunnel wall with a hollow wooden knock that made all three of them freeze like conspirators in a bad play — which, Bram reflected, was more or less what they were.

The tunnel held its breath with them. Somewhere ahead, water dripped from the vaulted ceiling into a standing pool, each drop counting out the seconds they could not afford to waste. Corvane's raid was set for the morning after next. The ship — Odile's miracle, bought with fish-market favors and a bribe that had cost Teo his liberty — would sail on tomorrow's black tide, and no tide after that for anyone who valued their necks. Which meant tonight was the only night, and the crates had to move, and the man who knew the tunnels best was a deserter the army still wanted flogged at the masthead.

Bram adjusted the crate on his shoulder and kept walking.

* * *

They had gathered at dusk in Odile's cellar, which had become over the past weeks something between a refuge, a chapel, and the world's most dangerous supper club. The printed sheets were stacked there in sections, disguised as hymnals for the Chapel of Saint Ferren — a pious fiction so thorough that Odile had insisted they include actual hymns on the facing pages, "so God isn't embarrassed if anyone looks." The true account of the crackdown ran beneath them in type Wren had set word by word: the seizure ledgers, the monastery copies, the sworn testimony of eleven witnesses, every name signed in ink that would not wash out.

Eleven signatures. Teo's among them, sworn before his arrest, delivered to Xanthe through a bribed turnkey along with a note in a boyish hand: *Tell Wren I spelled my own name right this time.*

That note had done something to Bram that four years of soldiering never had. It had made him want to be worthy of company.

Odile fed them before they went — she always fed them, as though courage were a stew that needed topping up — and stood at the foot of the cellar stairs with her arms folded, surveying them the way she surveyed the morning catch.

"The deputy's man says the quay gate opens for the night cart at the second bell," she said. "After that, nothing moves until dawn except patrols. So you'll be nothing moving until dawn. Understood?"

"We understood the plan when we made it," said Xanthe mildly.

"Aye, but plans are like fish," Odile said. "Fresh for a day, then they start to stink. Go on. And Bram—" She caught his sleeve as he passed, and her voice dropped to something meant for him alone. "You come back too. You're not cargo."

He managed a grin. "I'm the map, Odile. Maps don't get lost."

"Maps get burned," she said, and let him go.

* * *

The entrance to the tunnels was behind the cooperage on Tanner's Lane, under a grate that Bram had once helped weld shut himself, in another life, with another name stitched inside his collar. He'd loosened those welds two years ago, quietly, on a night patrol, without quite knowing why. It seemed now that some part of him had been planning his defection since long before his feet agreed to it.

The grate came up with a groan he felt in his teeth. Below, the dark opened like a throat.

"Roman work," Xanthe said, peering down with the particular reverence of a clerk confronted by very old paperwork. "These channels predate the walls. There will be records—"

"There will be rats," Bram said. "Records later. Rats now."

Wren laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, but real. Bram had noticed that about her these past weeks: the laugh kept escaping, no matter how heavy the crates or how near the soldiers. It was, he suspected, how she survived. He'd known men who laughed the same way in the trenches outside Harrow Fen, digging in the frozen mud, and he had trusted every one of them.

He went first, lantern shuttered to a coin of light, and the dark took them.

* * *

The main channel ran wide enough for two abreast beneath the Fishmarket district, its floor a shallow stream of gray water that sucked at their boots. Bram navigated by touch and memory: the third arch where the brickwork bulged from a collapsed sewer line; the junction marked by a mason's chiseled fish, worn nearly smooth, that the old patrol maps called the Pike; the side gallery where the ceiling dropped so low that even stooping, your spine complained in a language of small hot pains.

"Here." He stopped at a rusted iron door set into the left-hand wall, half-hidden by silt. "Old customs inspection gallery. Runs under the quay warehouses. Comes up behind the salt sheds."

Xanthe lifted the lantern a fraction and studied the door with open suspicion. "It's locked."

"It's barred. From our side." Bram reached through the gap and hauled, and the bar clattered down inside. "We patrolled this route twice a week for three years. You learn which doors the garrison actually checks and which ones they just report checked. This one, Sergeant Voss reported checked for eighteen months without touching it. He had a woman in the Salt Quarter and better things to do with his evenings."

"That is," Xanthe said slowly, "the most honest audit I have ever heard described."

"I contain multitudes of wasted hours."

Wren shifted her crate to her other hip. In the coin of lamplight her face was pale and set, and Bram saw that she wasn't really hearing them. She was listening to the tunnel — or rather, through it, toward the shop above, toward the press that in forty hours would be torn apart by soldiers' hands. She had told them plainly, the night the orders came down: *the press cannot be saved, and I've stopped trying to save it.* But knowing a thing and walking away from it were different animals, and Bram recognized the animal in her face because he'd been feeding it for years.

"It's just lead and oak," he said quietly. "What's in the crates is the press now."

She looked at him. For a moment he thought she might snap at him — she had earned the right — but instead she nodded once, the way you nod at a truth you already owned and resent being handed.

"I know," she said. "Keep walking, deserter."

* * *

They moved in stretches of ten minutes and rests of two, Bram counting the intervals by the drip of the water, which he had learned years ago kept better time than any watch he'd ever been issued. The crates were not heavy individually — Wren had packed them like a woman packing eggs, each section wrapped, each crate balanced — but the tunnel made everything heavier, the way grief makes stairs longer.

At the Pike junction they heard the first patrol.

Boots, above. Not in the tunnel — on the street overhead, crossing the grate at the mouth of Cooper's Alley, close enough that grit sifted down through the iron bars and ticked against the crates. Four men, by the stride. Halberds, by the occasional ring of shaft against buckle.

Bram killed the lantern with his palm and put his back to the wall, and the other two did the same, three shapes dissolving into brick. The boots crossed. Paused. Crossed on.

Nobody breathed until the sound had gone entirely, and then Xanthe exhaled in a thin controlled stream, and Bram realized the clerk's hands were shaking — not from fear, exactly, or not only. Xanthe had spent a career in rooms where danger came wearing good manners. This was different. This was danger with boots on.

"First time under the city?" Bram asked softly.

"First time under anything," Xanthe said. "I have led a scandalously vertical life."

Wren made that escaped-laugh sound again, muffled against her sleeve, and Bram felt the strange warmth of it spread through his own chest. That was the thing nobody told you about terror: shared, it turned into something almost like joy. He'd felt it once before, crossing the border reeds at night with two other deserters, all of them laughing silently like idiots while the searchlights swept the marsh. Men laughing because they had finally chosen, and choosing felt like flying even while it felt like falling.

"Two hundred yards to the gallery door," he said. "Then the worst part's over."

"The worst part," Wren said, "is boarding a ship past a naval officer with Corvane breathing down the quay."

"Aye," Bram admitted. "Then the worst part is that. But this part is the wettest part, and I find it helps to rank your miseries."

* * *

The customs gallery was dry, mercifully, and tall enough to walk upright, and it smelled of salt and old rope instead of the channel's ripe history. They rested there while Bram relit the lantern and checked the route in his head one more time, the way he'd once checked ammunition.

It was Xanthe who broke the quiet, in the careful voice of someone opening a ledger they weren't sure they should open.

"Bram. The night you deserted. The Ashgrove raid was two nights before."

The words settled over the little room like silt. Wren had gone very still beside her crate.

"I know why you're asking," Bram said. "And yes. I was on the cordon that night. Outer ring, harbor side. I never went in the shop." He looked at Wren directly, because looking away would have been the unforgivable thing. "But I heard the order given. Not shouted. Given quietly, the way real orders are given. Lieutenant Hask to the entry team: *nothing leaves the premises.* And then, after — when your father was carried out — Hask said, *the account is closed,* and the men laughed, and I didn't, and that was the night I started keeping my own accounts."

Wren's hands were flat on the crate lid, pressing down as though the truth might buck underneath her. When she spoke, her voice was steady in the way of a beam bearing exactly its rated load.

"'Nothing leaves the premises.' Those words?"

"Word for word. I can swear them. I will swear them, on the ship, in ink."

She nodded slowly. "Then they go in the book. Word for word." She looked down at the crate under her hands — at the hundreds of pages inside, the true account, the record that would outlive every lie told above ground — and something in her shoulders eased by a degree. "You see what you are, Bram? You're not the map. You're a page."

Bram found he had to look at the lantern for a moment. Four years a soldier, months a fugitive, weeks a smuggler of truth, and no one had yet told him he was a page. It went through him like warm wine.

"Well," he managed. "Someone had better proofread me."

"I will," said Xanthe instantly, and it was impossible to tell whether the clerk was joking, and somehow that made it funnier, and for a moment the three of them stood in a smuggler's gallery under a sleeping city, laughing as quietly as people can laugh with death walking the streets above.

* * *

The last stretch ran beneath the quay itself, and here Bram slowed them to a crawl, because this was the stretch that had changed since his patrolling days. Corvane's men had installed new grates after the harbor lockdown — Bram had watched them do it from a rooftop, taking notes like the traitor he apparently was — and one of the new grates sat directly above the gallery's exit stair, behind the salt sheds.

But new grates were fitted by day laborers, and day laborers were paid by the piece, and pieces fitted in haste sit loose. Bram had come at low tide two nights ago, alone, and tested every bar of the new grate with a pry bar wrapped in cloth. Six bars solid. The seventh rattled like a loose tooth.

"The seventh bar," he murmured now, crouched at the foot of the stair with the others bunched behind him. "Lifts and swings. Don't force it, don't rush it, and whatever you do, don't let it clang. Above us is the yard behind the salt sheds, and the yard watch drinks, but he drinks lightly on requisition nights."

"And beyond the yard?" Xanthe whispered.

"Forty yards of shadow between the sheds and the quay gate. The night cart comes at second bell. Odile's cousin drives it. We're the night's delivery." He paused. "One more thing. If it goes wrong — if there's a whistle, if the yard lights come up — you two go back. Back to the Pike, back to Tanner's Lane, back to Odile. The crates matter more than the carriers. That's not bravery talking. That's arithmetic."

"No," Wren said.

"It's the sensible—"

"No," she said again, gentler this time, and in the dark her hand found his forearm and gripped it, callused fingers, printer's strength. "Nobody goes off the record, Bram. Nobody gets left in a tunnel to keep the arithmetic tidy. We go together or the whole book is a lie. Isn't that the entire point of everything we've done?"

He wanted to argue. He was, after all, the professional in matters of retreat; he had made a study of withdrawal, both tactical and moral. But her hand was on his arm and her voice was quiet and certain, and he understood suddenly that he was looking at the difference between them: he had deserted an army, and she had refused to desert anyone, ever, and only one of those things was going to be remembered kindly.

"Together," he said. "God help us."

"He's in the hymnals," Wren said. "Facing pages."

* * *

Second bell came across the water like a benediction.

The seventh bar swung free without a sound — Bram had oiled it with lamp grease two nights ago, and his own foresight nearly made him weep with gratitude for his past self. One by one they rose through the gap into the salt-yard dark, crates passing hand to hand, and crouched in the shadow of the sheds while the night cart rattled up to the quay gate and Odile's cousin argued cheerfully with the gate sentry about the weight of his manifest, loudly, at length, a performance of bureaucratic friction designed to hold every eye at the gate.

"Now," Bram breathed.

Forty yards of shadow. Three figures, six crates, two trips. The first trip went like a blessing: across the open ground in the cart's noisy cover, crates up onto the false bottom among the fish barrels, Wren and Xanthe flattening themselves behind the cart's tailboard while the sentry and the carter disputed tariffs. On the second trip, halfway across, the yard watch stumbled out of the salt shed to relieve himself not ten feet from Bram's hiding place, and Bram lay flat among the shadows with a crate under his chest and his heart hammering like a compositor's stick, and the man yawned, and watered the wall, and went back inside, having come within a breath of becoming the most famous fool in Vellamare.

Then the last crate was aboard, and the false bottom was nailed down by Odile's cousin's quick hammer in the noise of the argument, and the cart rolled through the gate toward the *Merrow's Grace* waiting at the black-tide berth, and Bram stood in the shadow of the last warehouse with Wren and Xanthe, empty-handed, alive, and shaking.

"Quay gate to gangway," Xanthe whispered, consulting some internal schedule. "Thirty paces of open stone. Two sentries. One naval officer."

"One naval officer," Bram agreed. He flexed his hands, willing the trembling out of them. "And somewhere on this quay, if the deputy's information holds, Inspector Corvane himself may walk the night rounds. Requisition night. He likes to watch his property."

Wren straightened beside him. In the starlight her face was unreadable, but her voice, when it came, was the voice of a woman reading her own typeset proof aloud, every letter accounted for.

"Then let's give him something to read," she said, and stepped out of the shadow onto the open quay, toward the gangway, toward the officer, toward the held breath of the tide — and Bram, the map, the page, the deserter, followed her into the light.
