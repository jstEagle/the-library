## 35. Boarding at Black Tide

The tide turned black an hour before dawn, which was the harbormaster's deputy's way of saying the moon had gone behind the clouds and the lamps along the quay had been allowed to burn low. Wren stood in the shadow of the customs shed with a hymnal crate on her shoulder and her heart going like a trip-hammer, and she thought, not for the first time, that her father would have hated this plan and then improved it.

"Steady," Odile murmured beside her. She smelled of fish and lamp oil and courage, which was to say she smelled like herself. "The mate is paid. The officer is not. Remember the difference."

"I remember the difference," Wren said. "I'm the one who counted the coin."

"Counting the coin is the easy part." Odile adjusted the shawl over her head and became, by some alchemy of posture, a fishwife delivering a late order to a hungry crew. "The hard part is looking bored. Boredom is the most expensive thing you can wear tonight. Put it on."

Wren put it on. It fit worse than the shawl.

* * *

The *Meridian's Grace* sat low in the water at the third berth, a fat-bellied neutral trader out of Sarnholt flying colors that belonged to no one at war with anyone — for now. Her first mate, a broad man named Hesk whose honesty had been purchased at a price Wren still considered insulting rather than extortionate, met them at the gangplank with a manifest and a lantern held so that its light fell everywhere except where they wanted it.

"Hymnals," Hesk said, loud enough for the sentry at the quay's end to hear. "Fourteen crates of hymnals for the congregation at Sarnholt. Devout lot, the Sarnholtians. Sing all day."

"They do love their singing," Wren said, matching his volume.

They did love their singing. That was the beauty of it, the part that had kept Wren awake with something adjacent to joy for three nights running: every crate really did contain hymnals. Real ones, printed on the government contract she'd signed in blood and self-disgust, page after page of approved psalms in approved type. The true account — the whole of it, thirty-six signatures and Bram's testimony and the copied ledger pages and Xanthe's formal protest folded in as front matter — lay printed on the same sheets, interleaved between the psalms in a rotation Xanthe had devised, so that no single crate contained more than a fraction, and no inspection of any single crate could reveal anything but piety.

"You'd need the whole shipment," Xanthe had said, auditing the final sheets by candlelight, "assembled in one place, to read what it says."

"And who's going to assemble fourteen crates of hymnals?" Odile had asked.

"No one," Xanthe said, "who isn't looking."

Hesk took the first crate from Wren's shoulder and swung it up the gangplank with the ease of a man handling feathers. "Third crate's got a loose slat," he said quietly, mouth barely moving. "Nail's backed out. I'll seat it before the officer pokes about."

"There's nothing in the third crate but psalms," Wren said.

"There's nothing in any crate but psalms," Hesk agreed solemnly, "and I'll defend every note of them."

* * *

The naval officer arrived at half past four, which was earlier than expected and therefore exactly on time for the kind of night this was shaping up to be. He came down the quay with two marines at his heels and a lantern of his own, and Wren knew him at once, or knew his type: young enough to be ambitious, old enough to be careful, with the particular neatness of a man who had never once been surprised and intended to keep his record clean.

He stopped at the gangplank and looked at the crates stacked on the cobbles, and then he looked at Wren, and Wren watched him arrive at the obvious conclusion — printer, delivery, dawn sailing, requisitioned ship — and then watch himself decline to draw it, because drawing conclusions was above his station and getting above your station was how careers ended in Vellamare these days.

"Manifest," he said.

Hesk handed it over. The officer read it the way Xanthe read things, line by line, twice.

"Hymnals," he said.

"For the Sarnholt congregations," Wren said. "There's been a revival. Very musical."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite amusement, not quite suspicion, but a cousin of both. "A revival. In wartime."

"Especially in wartime, sir. People sing when they're frightened. It's cheaper than drinking."

That earned her a longer look. She held it, bored, tired, a tradeswoman with a bad back and a long night ahead, and inside she was screaming in a register only dogs and dead fathers could hear.

The officer opened the nearest crate with a mariner's knife, cutting the twine with two economical strokes. He lifted out a hymnal. He opened it. He read a psalm. Wren stood very still and thought about type metal and her father's hands and the exact weight of a well-cut letter M, because thinking about those things kept her from thinking about the fact that the true account of a massacre was sitting eleven feet away in a crate with a loose slat.

"Approved edition," the officer said at last, checking the colophon against a list he produced from his coat. "Government press."

"Ashgrove and Daughters," Wren said. "We're honored to serve."

"Were you always Ashgrove and Daughters?"

The question landed soft as a feather and heavy as an anchor. Wren felt Odile go still beside her, felt the whole quay hold its breath — Hesk at the rail, the marines at parade rest, the black water slapping the pilings below.

"My father ran it as Ashgrove alone," Wren said. "Since his death, it's been me. The census office has the full record, if you'd like to verify. A Mr. Xanthe conducted the survey personally. Very thorough."

It was a gamble and she knew it — spending Xanthe's name like coin, trusting that the census office's own paperwork would vouch for itself. The officer consulted nothing. He simply nodded, the way men nod when a question has been answered correctly according to the answer key they carry in their heads.

"Load them," he said. "All fourteen. And nail that slat." He glanced at the third crate without knowing why. "Loose crates sink ships."

"So do loose lips," Odile said cheerfully, "but nobody's ever confiscated those."

The marines actually smiled. The officer did not, but he let the moment pass unexamined, which was its own small mercy, and walked on down the quay to inspect the next berth, his lantern bobbing away into the dark like a slow, punctilious star.

Wren exhaled for what felt like the first time since the moon went behind the clouds.

"Bored," Odile reminded her. "You lost the boredom somewhere around 'revival.'"

"It's back now."

"It had better be. Look."

* * *

Corvane came down the quay the way winter comes — no hurry in it, and no possibility of stopping him either.

Wren saw him from forty yards and had time for exactly one full breath, in through the nose, out through the teeth, the way her father had taught her to stand at the press when a sheet jammed and panic would ruin the whole forme. Corvane wore civilian wool tonight, dark and unadorned, and walked without escort, which was somehow worse than marines. Men who brought soldiers were performing power. Men who came alone already owned it.

He stopped at the foot of the gangplank and regarded the crates, and then the ship, and then Wren, with the unhurried attention of a man reading a document he had written himself.

"Mistress Ashgrove."

"Inspector." She kept her voice level and her hands visible and empty, which took more discipline than anything she had done in her life, including setting twelve thousand words of testimony in eight-point type by candlelight. "You're out late."

"The harbor doesn't sleep, and neither do I." He picked up a hymnal from the open crate — the officer had left it lying on the cobbles, spine cracked to the Twenty-Third Psalm — and turned it over in his hands as though weighing it. "Your work?"

"Our press. My father's design, my composition."

"He taught you well. Or someone did." He set the hymnal down with exaggerated care, aligning its spine with the edge of the crate, and Wren understood that the gesture was the message: *I notice disorder. I correct it.* "Fourteen crates. A large order for a city under curfew."

"Sarnholt's congregations have been patient. Piety keeps, but not forever."

"No. It doesn't." He smiled, and it was the smile from the dinner party, the one that never reached anywhere near his eyes. "You know, Mistress Ashgrove, I have admired your thoroughness from the beginning. Most printers would have closed shop after such a loss. You doubled your output. Grief, channeled into industry. It's the healthiest thing I've seen in this city all year."

Behind her, Wren heard Odile shift her weight, and heard, fainter, the creak of the gangplank as Hesk found a reason to be standing on it.

"My father built the shop to print," Wren said. "Stopping would have killed it too."

"Yes. Yes, I imagine it would feel that way." Corvane stepped closer, close enough that she could smell the ink on her own sleeves and, beneath it, the cold stone scent of the registry fire that clung to him like a second coat. "Tell me something, since we're both awake and honest at this hour. Do you believe history is written by the victors?"

"I believe it's *printed* by whoever pays for paper," Wren said. "History does the writing itself, eventually. It's stubborn that way."

"Eventually." He tasted the word. "A dangerous adverb. Everything inconvenient lives in *eventually*. Every debt, every promise, every account left unsettled." His eyes moved across the crates, unhurried, counting. She watched him count. Fourteen. She watched him arrive at fourteen and find nothing there, because there was nothing there, there were only psalms, there were only psalms, God help her, there were only psalms. "My warrant for this vessel arrives with the morning tide. Requisition papers. The *Grace* will sail under our flag, cargo and all."

Wren's stomach dropped through the quay, through the pilings, into the black water, and kept falling.

"That's a shame for the Sarnholt congregations," she said, and her voice did not shake, and she would remember that for the rest of her life, that her voice did not shake. "Though I suppose they'll sing to your flag as readily as anyone's. People sing when they're frightened."

Corvane studied her for a long moment. Somewhere behind him, far up the quay, a bell began to ring the half hour — five bells, the tide turning, time bleeding out of the night faster than any of them could bind it.

"You remind me of your father," he said at last. "He also believed the record would vindicate him. Right up until the end." He straightened his cuffs. "Good luck with your delivery, Mistress Ashgrove. I hope the singing goes beautifully."

He walked away up the quay, unhurried, and the dark took him the way it takes everything eventually, and Wren stood at the foot of the gangplank with her heart hammering and her face made of wood and understood, with total clarity, that he had known. Not proven. Not certain. But known the way a man knows weather coming in his bones — and that his warrant would come with the tide, and that everything now depended on minutes, and bribes, and whether a corrupt deputy could lose a piece of paper in the right drawer for one hour longer.

Odile's hand closed on her arm, hard.

"He's gone to the harbormaster's office," Odile breathed. "To fetch it early. I know that walk. That's a man walking to collect a paper."

"Then we load faster than he walks," Wren said. "Wake everyone. All hands. We sail on this tide or we don't sail at all."

She turned to the crates, and began to count them aboard.
