## 11. The Informer

Sera Vukovic had learned to count her fears the way other women counted coins—by touch, in the dark, without lighting a lamp. There was the fear for Tomas, who was seven and asked too many questions on the street. The fear for little Ana, whose cough had gone from dry to wet over the winter and who needed a doctor the family could no longer afford to see openly. The fear that lived in her husband's empty chair, four years vacant now, since the gendarmes had come for Branko one Tuesday morning and returned his coat but never him. And beneath all of these, like silt under harbor water, the newest fear: the list. Forty families. Her name among them, she was almost certain, because Ferro's face had gone careful when he'd read it aloud, and Ferro's face was never careful unless the news was worst.

She sat at the kitchen table with a cup of chicory going cold between her hands and listened to her children breathe in the next room. Tomas slept the way boys sleep, sprawled and boneless, as if the world were a safe place to abandon oneself to. Ana slept curled small, conserving breath. Sera counted their breathing the way she counted everything now—one, two, three—and told herself that a mother who could count could also calculate.

That was the word she used, later, when she tried to forgive herself. *Calculate.* Not betray. Not inform. Calculate.

The trouble with calculation is that it only works if you believe the numbers are honest. And Sera had spent four years learning that in Vellamare, every number was a lie except the ones written by men with guns.

* * *

The network met that Thursday in the back room of Nonna Lidia's tavern, and Sera watched Mara Marchetti the way she had begun watching everyone lately—with the exhausted suspicion of a woman who has realized that safety is a currency and someone at the table is spending it.

It wasn't anything Mara did. That was the terrible part. Mara did nothing at all. She sat with her hands folded, ink still ghosting the edge of her right cuff where she'd scrubbed imperfectly, and she listened while Ferro laid out the delivery run in the flat voice he used for fish prices and other things that were not really about fish. The manifest would move through the customs house inside a bale of silk. It would reach the Corvina before the morning tide on Sunday. Forty names would cross the water and become testimony instead of corpses.

"Questions?" Ferro said.

Sera had one, and it sat in her chest like a fishhook: *What happens to us while you're playing courier?* But she didn't ask it. She looked around the table—at old Ferro with his ruined knuckles and his eyes like a man reading weather; at Lidia, arms crossed, radiating the indignation of a woman who considered the security police a personal affront to her housekeeping; at young Paolo, who still believed courage was a kind of youth; at Mara, quiet as a closed ledger.

And Sera thought: *One of us is already talking.*

She couldn't have said why she was sure. Certainty of that kind doesn't arrive with reasons. It arrives the way damp arrives through stone—slowly, everywhere, undeniable. Four years since Branko. Four years of the regime tightening its fist around the harbor quarter one finger at a time, and always, somehow, the police arriving a step behind the network's movements, a half-step ahead of its plans. Someone was selling them. Someone had been selling them for longer than anyone wanted to say aloud.

Or—and this was the thought that came to her at three in the morning, standing at the window while Ana coughed in her sleep—or there was no informer at all, and the net was closing simply because nets close, because regimes do not need traitors when they have patience, and the only question left was which side of the wall her children would be standing on when it fell.

Tomas woke and padded into the kitchen, dragging his blanket. "Mama, why do you stand at the window?"

"Counting boats," she said.

"There aren't any boats."

"Not yet." She lifted him onto her hip even though he was too big for it, even though her back protested, because there were fewer and fewer years left when he would let her. He smelled of sleep and salt. Against his hair she whispered the thing she could not say in daylight: *I will keep you. Whatever it costs. Whoever it costs.*

She did not yet know how precisely the night would hold her to that vow.

* * *

The security police kept an office in the former shipping insurance building on the Piazza del Sale, and everyone in the harbor quarter walked past it the way you walk past a dog you know will bite—wide, quick, eyes forward. The man who worked the civilian complaints desk was called Ottesen, a Dane or something near enough, gray-faced and gray-voiced, famous for two things: he never raised his voice, and he never forgot a face.

Sera told herself she was only walking past. She told herself that on Monday, and again on Wednesday, and each time her feet slowed a little more in front of the tall windows, and each time she hated herself a little more for the slowing, because slowing was a decision the body made before the mind agreed to sign it.

On Friday it rained, a thin cold rain off the Adriatic that turned the cobbles the color of gunmetal, and Sera stood under the awning of the tobacco shop across from the insurance building with Ana's prescription folded in her pocket—a prescription she could not fill at the harbor pharmacy anymore because the pharmacist had started writing down names—and she watched the door of the office open and close, open and close, swallowing ordinary people and returning them looking slightly smaller.

Ana coughed in the night again. Twice she woke gasping, and Sera held her upright against her shoulder until the spasm passed, feeling the small ribs working like bellows with sand in them. And at some hour after midnight, in the dark, Sera Vukovic performed the calculation she had been circling for a week, and found that it had only ever had one answer.

If the list was real—if forty families were to be loaded onto trucks within the week—then warning the network was useless, because the network was already penetrated or already doomed. If the list was not real, then she had risked nothing. But if she went to the police first—if she gave them something, anything, a coin dropped in their palm—then perhaps they would look at her the way creditors look at a debtor who comes forward voluntarily. Perhaps her name would move. Perhaps not off the list entirely. Perhaps only down it, below the line, into the column marked *cooperative*, which was not survival but was adjacent to it, the way a ledge is adjacent to the ground.

She was not stupid. She knew what she was choosing. She simply believed, with the whole animal conviction of a mother, that the arithmetic permitted nothing else.

The enemy was not Ottesen. The enemy was not even the regime, though God knew the regime deserved the word. The enemy was the shape of the choice itself—the machine built so carefully, so deliberately, so that every gear inside it was a frightened person, and every frightened person turned another gear, and the machine ran on fear the way engines run on coal, endlessly, hungrily, needing only that no one refuse to shovel.

Sera shoveled.

* * *

She went on Saturday afternoon, when the light was failing and the piazza was nearly empty, wearing her plainest coat and carrying a market basket with two leeks in it, because a woman with leeks is a woman buying dinner, not a woman making history.

Ottesen received her in a room with green walls and a window painted over so that the daylight came through amber, like light through beer. He did not ask her name at first. He poured tea from a chipped pot—tea, not coffee, the drink of men who work long hours and want no stimulants clouding their judgment—and he waited. Waiting, Sera discovered, was his instrument. He could wait the way stone waits.

"I have children," she said finally, because she had rehearsed a dozen openings and every one of them collapsed, and only the truth underneath all of them held its shape.

"Yes," Ottesen said. "Two. A boy of seven and a girl of five. The girl is unwell."

The floor tilted. Sera gripped the arm of the chair. "How do you—"

"We know a great deal about the harbor quarter, signora. We know less than we would like about certain evening activities at a tavern on the Calle dei Pescatori." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, precise as a man setting a clock. "I am not a cruel man. I want you to understand that. Cruelty is inefficient. What I am is thorough, and thoroughness, in my experience, is kinder in the end. A person who talks to me today sleeps in her own bed tonight. A person who makes me find her information elsewhere—" he shrugged, a small economical gesture, "—sleeps somewhere worse, or nowhere."

"I don't know anything," Sera said, and heard how thin it sounded, how exactly like the thing a guilty woman says.

"No," Ottesen agreed mildly. "But you know someone."

And there it was, the whole trap laid out in six words, and Sera sitting inside it, and the door behind her a very long way away.

She thought of Ferro teaching the orphaned Marchetti girl fish-market codes twenty years ago, his hand on the child's shoulder. She thought of Lidia's brandy and her furious decency. She thought of Mara's ink-stained cuff and her silence, which might be loyalty or might be guilt, Sera could no longer tell the difference. She thought of Ana's ribs. Of Tomas asking why Mama stood at the window. Of Branko's coat coming home without Branko.

"The courier," she heard herself say, and her voice sounded like it belonged to a stranger, someone standing behind her, using her mouth. "There's a delivery moving before Sunday's tide. Through the customs house. Silk bales—I don't know which ship, I swear I don't, they keep that part dark even from—" Even from me, she almost said, even from us, and stopped herself at the lip of that word *us* the way you stop at the edge of a drop.

Ottesen wrote nothing. That frightened her more than writing would have. He simply looked at her with his gray patience and said, "Go on."

"It's a woman," Sera said. "Young. Dark-haired. She works—in an office. Government." Each fact fell out of her like teeth. "I don't know her name. I've seen her at the tavern, that's all, I've seen her and I've put it together, that's all I've done, I swear before God that's all—"

"You've done enough," Ottesen said, and for one vertiginous moment his voice was almost gentle, and Sera understood that this was the cruelest thing in the room: that he meant it. To him this was mercy. This was the system working as designed, the frightened gear turning smoothly, everyone in his proper place. "A census clerk, you think? In the harbor district?"

"I don't know. I said I don't—"

"You've been very helpful, signora." He rose, and the interview was over the way a wave is over—what it carried had already been taken. At the door he paused. "Your daughter should see Dr. Ambrosi on the Corso. Tell him Ottesen sent you. He'll write whatever prescription is needed, and no one will write down yours."

It was a kindness. It was a leash. It was both, and she took it, and she walked home through the rain with her leeks and her hollowed-out chest, and the strangest thing, the thing she would carry to her grave, was that her legs felt lighter. For one block, two blocks, she felt *safe*, and she despised the feeling and could not put it down, because fear had held her so long that its absence was its own kind of intoxication.

Then she reached her own street and saw the light in her own window and thought of her children waiting, and the lightness curdled into something else—not relief, not guilt, but the terrible neutral weight of a thing done that cannot be undone. She had not given Mara's name. She clung to that. She had given a description, a profession, a schedule, a route—but not a name, and surely there was distance in that, surely there was some country left between what she had done and what she feared she had done.

Surely.

* * *

Saturday night the network gathered again, and Sera sat among them with the leek-and-potato soup cooling in her stomach and could not meet anyone's eyes. Ferro talked logistics. Lidia refilled glasses. Paolo argued cheerfully about tides. And Mara sat quiet with her folded hands, and Sera looked at her—at the dark hair, the young face, the ink-shadowed cuff—and felt the confession rise in her throat like bile and swallowed it down, and swallowed it, and swallowed it.

Say it, she told herself. Stand up and say it. *I went to them. I'm sorry. Run, all of you, run tonight.*

But if she said it, they would run, and if they ran, the delivery would fail, and if the delivery failed, the forty families would go into the trucks with no testimony crossing the water—and her children would be among the forty either way, cooperative column or no, because Ottesen's promises were worth exactly what a promise from a security officer was worth, which was the paper it wasn't written on. Silence, then. Silence bought with silence. She was inside Ferro's creed now, the useful silence, and she understood at last that it was not a creed at all but a debt, compounding hourly, collected eventually, always.

Near midnight, as the meeting broke up, Mara paused beside her chair. Close, Sera saw, how tired the girl's eyes were. How much younger than her competence.

"You've been somewhere else all evening," Mara said quietly. Not accusing. Just seeing, the way she apparently saw everything—columns, discrepancies, the number that didn't match between one ledger and the next.

"Just tired," Sera said. "Children."

Mara nodded and squeezed her shoulder once, briefly, a comrade's touch, warm and utterly undeserved, and went out into the dark toward the customs house and the silk and the tide.

Sera sat alone in the emptied back room listening to the tavern sounds die away above her, and she pressed both hands flat against the table as if steadying herself on a deck in rough water, and she thought: *They will catch her. They will catch her because of me, and she will die, or Ferro will, or Lidia, and I will be at the funeral holding my children's hands and no one will ever know why the net closed so neatly, so quickly, so well.*

Outside, the rain had stopped. The sky over the harbor was clearing toward Sunday, toward the morning tide, toward the Corvina riding at anchor with her holds full of darkness—and somewhere in the green-walled room on the Piazza del Sale, a gray man was already writing down a description of a young woman with dark hair who worked in a government office, and drawing, with his precise clockmaker's hand, the circle smaller.
