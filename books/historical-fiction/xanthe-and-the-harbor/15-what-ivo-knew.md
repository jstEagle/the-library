## 15. What Ivo Knew

The summons came at seven in the morning, delivered by a boy in a gray cap who did not wait for a reply.

*Section Four. Ninth floor. Bring your district files.*

Ivo Brandt read the note twice, then folded it into quarters and put it in his breast pocket, where it lay against his heart like a small cold coin. He had been expecting this for three days—ever since the harbor patrol reported that their courier had slipped the net at the fish market, ever since the photograph was promised. Expecting it, and dreading it, and unable to say even to himself which he dreaded more: that the courier would be a stranger, or that she would not.

He dressed carefully. That was habit, not vanity—in Vellamare under the Directorate, a man's collar said what his mouth could not. He chose the dull gray suit, the one that made him look like what they believed him to be: a minor functionary, a demoted auditor with a limp reputation and no ambitions worth watching. Ten years ago he had worn better suits and carried worse secrets. The suits had gotten cheaper as the secrets got heavier, and some mornings, knotting his tie before the spotted mirror in his rented room, he wondered if the arithmetic of his life had been running backward all along.

The census bureau smelled of dust and cold tea and the particular staleness of paper that has been handled by too many afraid hands. Ivo gathered his district files into a leather satchel—the Marchetti ledgers among them, though he told himself that meant nothing—and walked out into a morning so bright and blue it felt like an insult.

Vellamare in October wore its beauty like a widow wears her wedding dress. The light came off the Adriatic in long gold sheets; the fishing boats knocked gently against the quay; somewhere above the customs house a gull screamed once and fell silent, as if corrected. And along the harbor road, past the shuttered offices of the shipping lines, past the garrison gate where sentries stood with their rifles sloped and their eyes moving, the city went about its business with the careful, over-polite rhythm of people who have learned that attention is dangerous.

Ivo walked through it all with the files against his hip and the note against his heart, and he thought: *I could still turn around. I could still be only what they think I am.*

He did not turn around. He was not sure, anymore, whether that was courage or surrender, and the not-knowing had become its own kind of wound.

* * *

Section Four occupied the ninth floor of the Ministry building on the Piazza del Sale, and it occupied it the way mold occupies bread. The elevators were reserved for directors; Ivo took the stairs, counting landings, giving himself time to arrange his face. By the eighth floor he had settled on attentive boredom, the expression of a man who has been summoned many times and found each summons tedious. It was a good mask. He had practiced it for years in audit rooms, across tables from men whose careers he was dismantling, back when dismantling careers had been his work and he had believed the work meant something.

The corridor on nine was carpeted in green so dark it swallowed footsteps. Doors stood at intervals, unmarked, and behind them Ivo knew there were rooms without windows where questions were asked in voices that never rose. Everyone in Vellamare knew. Knowing and not-knowing were the twin disciplines of the city, practiced daily, like prayer.

A secretary with a face like folded paper took his name and made him wait forty minutes on a bench beneath a portrait of the Director-General, rendered in oils at three-quarter life size, gazing toward a horizon that existed nowhere on any coast Ivo had ever walked. When the inner door finally opened, it was not the secretary who called him but a man Ivo had met once before, briefly, in another life: Captain Enzo Varzi, Section Four, with his neat mustache and his voice like oil poured over water.

"Brandt." Varzi did not offer a hand. "You've been busy."

"I do my work."

"So we're told." Varzi closed the door. The office beyond was spare—a desk, two chairs, a filing cabinet, a window with a view of the roof of the customs house and, beyond it, a sliver of harbor so blue it looked painted on. On the desk lay a single folder, and beside the folder, an ashtray shaped like an anchor, and beside the ashtray, a lamp with a green shade.

"Sit."

Ivo sat. Varzi remained standing, which was a choice, and Ivo noted it the way he noted everything now: automatically, uselessly, the habits of a man trained to see everything and permitted to act on nothing.

"You audited the harbor district census," Varzi said. "You flagged discrepancies in household counts. You requested a follow-up on a courier route through the garrison sector. You wrote—" he consulted a card he did not need to consult, "—a memorandum dated the ninth, recommending expanded surveillance of the fish market. Thorough work, Brandt. Almost too thorough, for a man in your position."

"My position being?"

"Auditor fourth class. Demoted 1941. Reassigned to census." Varzi smiled without warmth. "We know your file, Brandt. We know it better than you'd like. Which is why you're here."

He opened the folder and drew out a photograph.

Ivo had prepared himself for this moment for three days, and discovered in the instant it arrived that preparation was worthless. The photograph was small, four by five, grainy, taken from above—from a rooftop or a window—looking down on the colonnade by the fish market. A woman stood at the edge of the frame, half-turned, a market basket on her arm. Her face was in profile, caught mid-motion, sharp and clear despite the grain: the set of the jaw, the line of the neck, the dark hair pinned back with the practical severity of a woman who has no time for mirrors.

Mara Marchetti.

Clerk second class, census bureau. Orphan of the '38 flood year, raised—according to her file, which Ivo had read eleven times—in the keeping of one Ferro Baldassare, proprietor of a tavern on the Via delle Reti. Ink-stained cuffs. Neat figures. A deflection in her voice like a stone skipped across still water, and eyes that watched him leave with something he had spent two weeks refusing to name.

"The courier," Varzi said. "Photographed Tuesday, making contact near the silk warehouse. We believe she's carrying documents out of the harbor district—manifests, shipping papers, possibly lists. In four days the freighter *Corvina* sails on the morning tide, and whatever she carries goes with it, or she does, or both." He laid the photograph on the desk between them, face up, and turned it slightly, squaring it to the edge of the wood with one finger. "You know the district. You know the census rolls. Look at the face and give me a name."

The green lamp hummed. Somewhere below, a tram bell rang, bright and ordinary, a sound from a different country.

Ivo looked at the photograph.

He looked at it the way he had once looked at falsified ledgers—with total attention, with the professional patience to let the thing reveal itself, because a lie always reveals itself if you refuse to blink. And the photograph revealed itself: it revealed Mara Marchetti standing in the October light with a basket on her arm, doing whatever she did in the hours when the bureau was dark and the harbor breathed its other breath. It revealed her jaw set against something. It revealed, in the angle of her body, in the direction of her stride, a woman moving toward the water.

Give me a name, Varzi had said.

And here was the thing Ivo had understood for days without admitting it, the thing that had been growing in him since the garrison gate, since the awning in the rain, since the moment he wrote her name in his notebook and could not make himself deliver it: he had already identified her. He had identified her the first week, probably—some part of him had—when her numbers shifted between ledgers and she lied to him with such clean competence that he had walked out of the bureau feeling not deceived but *seen*. Every audit since had been a courtship conducted in the grammar of suspicion. Every re-reading of her file had been a letter he didn't send. He had told himself he was building a case. He had been memorizing a face.

"I can't identify her from this," Ivo said.

Varzi's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Look again."

"I am looking." Ivo kept his voice level, bored, the auditor's voice, the voice of a man discussing column totals. "The angle is poor. The lighting is poor. Half the women in the harbor district dress that way and carry baskets that size. If you want a name from me, give me a frontal image, or give me the original negative, or give me something better than a shadow with a basket."

Something moved behind Varzi's eyes—not anger, not yet, but the recalibration of a man revising his estimate of a tool. "Your memorandum suggested you had suspicions regarding certain census irregularities. Households whose counts changed between ledgers. A clerk whose district covered the silk warehouses."

"Suspicion is my profession, Captain. Confirmation is yours." Ivo leaned back, crossing one ankle over his knee, letting the pose do its slow insolent work. "If I confirm every hunch I file, I'm doing your job. If I guess wrong, I burn an innocent clerk and the real courier walks. You didn't summon me here for guesses. You summoned me because I'm thorough. So be thorough with me."

The silence stretched. Outside the window, the sliver of harbor glittered, indifferent, beautiful. Ivo thought of Mara's voice saying *the tide doesn't ask permission*, that night under the awning, rain hammering the canvas while the whole city listened to the storm instead of to itself. He thought: *she is moving toward the water right now, or she will be tomorrow, and every hour I sit in this chair buying her doubt is an hour she spends buying distance.*

"Very well," Varzi said at last, softly, which was worse than shouting. "Keep the photograph. Study it. Compare it against your rolls. You have until the *Corvina* sails." He slid the picture across the desk with two fingers, almost gently. "And Brandt—if you identify her and fail to report it, that is treason. If you fail to identify her convincingly, that is incompetence, and we have procedures for incompetence too. Your file gives you very little room, either way. I'd think about which failure you can survive."

"I'll think about it," Ivo said, and took the photograph, and shook the offered hand—dry, cool, brief—and walked out through the green silence of the ninth floor with his heartbeat loud in his own ears and his face arranged into attentive boredom, the best work he had ever done, performed for an audience of one dead-eyed captain and a Director-General painted staring at a sea that belonged to no one.

* * *

He did not go back to the bureau. He walked.

Down the ministry steps, across the Piazza del Sale where the old salt merchants' arcades stood converted to ration offices, down through the streets that spilled toward the water the way all streets in Vellamare eventually did, as if the city itself leaned seaward, straining after its own reflection. He walked past the customs house with its brass scales built into the façade, scales large enough to weigh cargo and rusted into decorative uselessness—he had always found that funny, in the years when he could find anything funny. He walked past the garrison gate where the sentry who had waved Mara's basket through without searching it stood his post with the blank diligence of a man who suspects nothing, which was either innocence or genius, and Ivo no longer believed he could tell the difference.

At the rail above the fish market he stopped. Below, the colonnade was emptying, the day's trade done, the stalls folding up like tired hands. Gulls worked the cobbles for scraps. The light was going amber, and the water beyond the mole turned the color of old brass, and somewhere out in the roadstead, riding at anchor with her lights not yet lit, the *Corvina* sat black against the evening, waiting for the tide the way Varzi waited for a name.

Ivo took out the photograph.

He held it in both hands and looked at it one last time, properly, without the audience, without the performance. Her profile against the pale stone. The basket. The stride aimed at the water. He thought about the ledger columns she had shifted, one family's count moved like a chess piece to keep a widow's sons off a list—Ivo had reconstructed that much from the files, and more, far more, a whole architecture of small mercies built into the census rolls, invisible to anyone who read numbers the way the Directorate read them, which was as ammunition. But Ivo had been trained to read numbers the way an auditor reads them: as testimony. And her testimony, ten years of it, written in her neat clerk's hand, said one thing without variation, without exception, without a single entry he could find that served herself:

*These people exist. Count them. They were here.*

He thought about his own testimony. Eleven years of it. The audits that had once toppled ministers—before he learned the ministers he toppled were replaced by worse ones, before he learned his precision was a blade someone else aimed, before the demotion that was really an exile and the exile that was really a message: *know your size.* He had spent six years knowing his size. Six years of census forms and household counts, of shrinking himself to fit a life the Directorate had measured out for him, telling himself that a man who could not fight could at least keep his hands clean.

His hands had never been clean. They had only been empty.

And now they held a photograph, and a name, and a choice, and the tide was coming in.

"Mara Marchetti," he said aloud, quietly, to the gulls and the emptying market and the black ship at anchor. Saying it felt like stepping off a ledge. Saying it felt like the first honest act of his adult life.

He walked to the end of the quay, where a brazier stood for the night watchmen, coals banked and glowing. He checked the street behind him—old habit, newly purposeful—and then he fed the photograph to the fire, corner first, watching the flame take it: the stone colonnade curling brown, then black; her profile softening, blistering, gone; the basket a ghost of ash that lifted on the harbor wind and scattered over the water he now understood she was trying to reach.

It should have felt like treason. It felt like arithmetic. For the first time in eleven years, the columns balanced.

He stood until the coals showed no trace, then turned up his collar and walked back through the blue dusk to the bureau, where he wrote, in his precise auditor's hand, a request for transfer off the harbor surveillance detail—citing conflict of interest, citing compromised objectivity, citing the demoted man's oldest shield, his own damaged credibility. *I cannot serve as a reliable witness in this matter,* he wrote, and signed it, and knew as the ink dried that Varzi would read the true sentence underneath, the one written in the space between the lines:

*I know exactly who she is. Come and prove it.*

He sealed the envelope. Through the bureau's tall window, the harbor lay dark now except for the riding lights of ships and the sweep of the patrol boat's lantern along the mole, patient as a lighthouse, hungry as an eye. Four days. Three, perhaps, if the wind favored the *Corvina* and the tide came early.

Ivo put on his hat and went down into the streets, walking not toward his rented room but toward the Via delle Reti, toward a tavern with a green door where an old man named Ferro poured brandy for the harbor's ghosts—because if the net was closing, and it was, then someone had to warn the ones inside it, and the man with the cleanest dirty hands in Vellamare had just decided whose side his hands were on.

Behind him, at the top of the hill, the lights of Section Four burned on, floor after floor, window after window, like a building that refused to sleep. Ahead of him, at the bottom, the sea waited, black and enormous, keeping the tide's own counsel.

He walked toward the water.
