## 8. Teyo's War

Teyo Brandt had been in Xanthe long enough to know the city by its smells, and lately the smells were wrong.

Bread and horse and river ice — those were the honest smells of a garrison town in winter. But under them now sat something sweeter and more rotten: lamp oil burning in rooms that should have been dark, wax from too many candles, the particular stink of officers who had started drinking before noon. A garrison preparing to hold smelled like whetstones and latrines. A garrison preparing to leave smelled like this.

He stood on the north bastion at dawn, watching the river do its slow work against the bridge pilings. The ice had come early this year. It always came early or late, never on time, like everything else in Vessany's last years.

"Brandt." Corporal Hesk came up the stairs two at a time, breathing hard, which was itself a report — Hesk was twenty-three and shouldn't have been winded by forty steps. "Maren's aide wants the muster rolls again. Third time this week."

"Tell him the rolls haven't changed since Tuesday."

"He says he wants them fresh."

Teyo looked out at the river rather than at the boy. "Fresh rolls for a fresh army," he said. "We've got neither. Tell him to use Tuesday's."

Hesk hesitated. Teyo could feel the question forming behind him, the one the younger men had started asking with their silence instead of their mouths: *Sergeant, what's happening? Are we holding? Are we being sold?* He didn't answer it. He'd spent nineteen years learning not to answer questions he couldn't survive answering.

"Go on," he said. "And eat something. You look like a fence post."

When the boy's boots faded down the stair, Teyo took out the stub of pencil he carried and, on the back of his glove, wrote nothing. That was the trick of it now. He kept his hands busy so they wouldn't shake.

---

Nineteen years. He turned the number over the way you turn a coin you're deciding whether to spend.

He'd come to Xanthe at sixteen, a farm boy off the Sarn lowlands with a rifle too big for him and a general's signature on his enlistment papers — Aurec Maren, then only a colonel, building the 4th Sarn Rifles out of boys nobody else wanted. Maren had walked the recruit line himself in those days. He'd stopped in front of Teyo, looked at the mud on his boots, and asked where he was from.

"Willow Bend, sir."

"Good barley country." And then, because Maren even then understood what men needed to hear: "You'll eat better here than you did there. I promise you that."

He'd kept the promise, too. That was the thing people forgot about Maren, the young Maren, the one who slept in the same frozen billets as his company and learned every man's name and paid out of his own pocket when the quartermaster came up short. Teyo had followed him across the Marish campaigns, up the Kessel heights, through the winter at Dornholt where they'd eaten boiled harness leather and Maren had eaten it first, visibly, standing where the men could see.

Teyo had killed his first man at Dornholt and cried about it after, and Maren had found him in the stable and sat down next to him on a feed crate and said nothing at all for ten minutes. Then: "It gets lighter. Not good. Lighter." Nineteen years later Teyo could still hear the exact weight of that sentence.

That was the man he'd given his life to. The one who ate leather first.

The other one — the one in the governor's residence now, with the aide-de-camp and the wine cellar and the coalition envoys arriving under flags of truce — Teyo had watched him get built piece by piece over six years, the way you watch a riverbank erode. Nothing dramatic. Just each flood taking a little more soil. A decoration accepted when he'd once refused them all. A transfer request denied for a captain who'd questioned orders. The day Maren stopped eating in the mess.

You couldn't point to the moment a man became someone else. You could only notice, one morning, that he already had.

---

The mess hall at midday smelled of barley soup and wet wool, and Teyo took his bowl to the corner table the way he always did, back to the wall, sightline to both doors. Old habits. The room was half full — half full meant a third gone, desertions and sickness and the twelve men transferred to the residence guard, which everyone understood was where Maren put soldiers he trusted and soldiers he suspected, in roughly equal measure.

Corporal Hesk slid in across from him without asking, which meant he had something.

"Sergeant. The courier woman. Delacroix."

"What about her?"

"She was asking questions in the archive wing yesterday. About dispatch protocols. Who sees what, how the secondary ciphers route." Hesk stirred his soup without eating it. "Voss's clerk got short with her. She didn't push. But she noticed, sir. You can tell when someone notices."

Teyo ate slowly. The envoy's courier. He'd marked her the day she came through the gate — small woman, road-worn, eyes that catalogued a room the way his own did, except hers went to papers and exits while his went to weapons and men. She'd saluted the garrison flag properly, which most diplomats' people didn't bother with anymore. Small thing. He'd noticed.

"You worried she'll find something?" he asked.

"I'm worried about what happens to whoever finds something." Hesk finally looked up. His face had the particular grayness of a man who'd done arithmetic he didn't like. "Sergeant. The coal shipments stopped last week. Did you know that?"

"I know."

"Not reduced. Stopped. And the granary tally — I did the count myself, I was on fatigue detail — we've got maybe five weeks at full ration, eight if they cut us to half. Nobody cuts a garrison to half rations in the first week of winter unless—"

"Unless they don't plan to feed it past thaw." Teyo set down his spoon. Around them the mess clattered on, oblivious or pretending. "I know the sums, Hesk. I've been doing them since autumn."

"Then what do we do?"

The question hung there. Nineteen years ago, the answer would have been simple: take it to the colonel. The colonel would fix it, because the colonel fixed things, because the colonel was one of us. Now the man the complaint would be taken to was the cause of it.

"We do our duty," Teyo said. "We stand our posts. We keep the men fed as long as the stores allow, and we keep them calm, and we don't repeat rumors." He held the corporal's eyes. "And we remember who we serve. Not the general. The garrison. The walls. The four thousand people inside them who aren't soldiers and can't leave. Whatever happens above us, that doesn't change."

Hesk nodded slowly, and Teyo saw the boy believe him, and hated how much of what he'd just said was the kind of speech a man gives when he has no plan. *Duty.* He'd seen what duty bought at Dornholt. A mass grave and a monument nobody visited.

---

He found her at dusk, of all places, in the archive corridor outside the embassy offices — the courier woman, Delacroix, standing with a lantern in her hand and an expression he recognized from funerals. She'd been reading something. Or trying to read something she wasn't supposed to read yet.

"Sergeant Brandt," she said, when she saw him. Careful voice. Diplomat's-adjacent careful.

"Ma'am." He stopped a polite distance off. "You'll want to be careful in this corridor after dark. It's on the aide's patrol route, and he counts doors."

"Does he?" Something moved behind her eyes — not fear, calculation. "Counting doors. That's a strange thing to patrol for."

"It's a strange winter." He glanced down the corridor both ways, then made the decision he'd been circling for three days. "Walk with me, ma'am. The east gallery has a stove. No reason a courier should freeze."

She fell into step beside him. She was shorter than he'd thought, and tired in the deep way, the way that doesn't wash out. They walked past the arrow slits where the last gray light was dying over the river.

"You served under General Maren a long time," she said. Not a question. She'd done her reading.

"Nineteen years."

"And you stay."

It wasn't flattery and it wasn't an accusation. It was the genuine question, the one he asked himself at three in the morning, delivered flat by a stranger who had no idea she was walking on the thin ice over the well he drowned in nightly.

"The garrison stays," he said. "So I stay."

"That's not the same as staying for him."

"No," Teyo said. "It isn't."

They reached the gallery. The stove ticked in the corner, doing its poor best. He gestured her to the bench near it and remained standing, because there were things easier to say upright.

"Ma'am, I'm going to say something I shouldn't, and you're going to forget I said it. Can you do that?"

Her chin came up slightly. "Sergeant, I carry sealed dispatches through a war zone for a republic that may not exist by spring. Forgetting is not among my skills."

Despite everything, almost despite himself, Teyo felt the corner of his mouth move. "No," he agreed. "I suppose it wouldn't be."

He looked out the arrow slit at the river, black between its banks of ice, running under the bridge pilings where the season's first floes ground and groaned.

"When the ice closes the bridge," he said, "most people think that means the war stops here too. Winter truce. Everybody sits in their warm rooms and waits for thaw." He turned back to her. "Thaw is coming, ma'am. Middle of March, maybe earlier this year, the Sarn breaks up fast and mean. And when it does, the coalition comes across that bridge, or what's left of it. Everyone in this city knows it. What they don't know—" he paused, chose the words with a sapper's care, "—is that some of the people in this city are counting on it."

She'd gone very still. "Counting on it."

"There are officers in this garrison who've stopped drilling the walls and started packing wagons. There are supply ledgers that don't add up unless you assume no one's here to eat the stores by spring. There's a general who dines with coalition envoys and calls it diplomacy." He let that sit. "I've followed Aurec Maren since I was sixteen years old. I've bled for him in places whose names would mean nothing to you. So understand me when I say this is the hardest sentence I've ever spoken: I think Xanthe is going to be traded away. The walls, the stores, the garrison, the civilians — handed over as part of terms, to buy whatever the buyer thinks he's buying. And the people who find out too loudly, too early—" he met her eyes, "—they tend to have accidents. Desertion, usually. Very tragic. Happens in every besieged city."

The lantern light caught her face. She wasn't shocked. That frightened him more than shock would have. She looked like a woman confirming a bill she'd already been shown.

"Why tell me?" she asked quietly. "You don't know me."

"I know you saluted the flag."

"That's all?"

"It's more than most." He straightened. "And I know you asked about the secondary ciphers, and your clerk got short with you, and you backed off smart instead of pushing. That's not a spy's discipline, ma'am. That's an archivist's. Spies push. Archivists wait." He picked up his cap from the bench. "Whatever you're waiting for — and I'm not asking — wait faster. Thaw doesn't negotiate."

He was at the door when she spoke again.

"Sergeant. Your general. At Dornholt — the histories say he held the pass with a third of his strength and lost none of his wounded. That he carried every man out who could walk." Her voice was even, but underneath it ran something rawer. "Was that true?"

Teyo stood with his hand on the latch a long moment. Outside, somewhere across the river, the ice boomed — the great cracking reports of the floes shifting, the sound the whole city had learned to sleep through and that Teyo had never once managed to.

"It was true," he said. "That man existed. I ate beside him." He opened the door onto the cold. "I keep waiting for him to come back and court-martial whoever's wearing his coat."

He heard her breath catch — almost a laugh, and not.

"Get some sleep, ma'am," he said. "And lock your papers."

---

That night Teyo climbed the north bastion alone, as he did most nights now, and stood his private watch over a river he could not defend.

The ice stretched bank to bank, white-gray in the starlight, groaning as the current worked beneath it. Somewhere out there, under his boots' worth of frozen water, lay the channel that had carried Vessany's grain barges and gunboats and, in his grandfather's day, the republic's first parliament steamer, flags flying. A whole country had moved on that river. Now it was a road the enemy hadn't used yet.

He counted, the way he always counted. Four thousand civilians. Two thousand one hundred effectives, and falling. Five weeks of grain. One bridge, closed. One general, dining upstairs by candlelight with the men who'd be coming through the breach.

Against all of it: one courier woman with a lantern and an archivist's patience, and one sergeant too old and too stubborn to stop believing that a record mattered, that somebody ought to be standing post over the truth the way he stood post over these walls — not because it could be saved, but because abandoning it was the one surrender he refused to sign.

Downriver, the ice boomed again, longer this time, a sound like a door opening very far away.

Three days later, Coral Delacroix would knock on his door after midnight, pale as the frost on his window, and ask him to guard a room while she copied papers that would hang them both.

But that night he didn't know it yet. That night he just watched the river break itself apart and rebuild, and wondered which of them it would spare.
