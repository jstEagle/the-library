## 9. Breaking the Cipher

The embassy kept its papers in a room with no windows, on the third floor of the chancery, behind a door that locked from the outside. Coral had catalogued it twice now, once by candle and once by the gray light that came through the transom over the door, and both times her hand had paused at the same box.

It sat on the second shelf, between the treaty precedents and the garrison correspondence. Plain pine, iron corners, no seal on the lid — which was itself strange, because everything else in that room was sealed, ribboned, waxed, witnessed. This box had been opened and closed so many times the lid's hinge had worn a bright line in the wood. Someone had read these papers often. Someone had wanted them close.

And they had never passed through her hands.

She was the courier. Everything that moved between Vessany and its last envoy moved through Coral Delacroix's satchel or under her eyes. That was not vanity; it was procedure, drilled into her since she was sixteen: *the courier sees all that travels, or the courier is a hole in the record.* Her mother had written that in the margin of the merchant codebook Coral still carried, in ink gone brown as old blood.

But this box had come to Xanthe by another road. She'd found the manifest discrepancy on her third night in the fortress — a crate listed as "stationery, assorted" that weighed forty pounds more than stationery should, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, this box, and inside the box, perhaps two hundred sheets of paper covered in cipher.

Not the republic's cipher. She knew that one; she could read it in her sleep, the rotating substitution Voss's office had used for a decade, elegant and brittle. These pages used something else. The letter frequencies were wrong. The groups were too short. And there were no nulls — no padding, no decorative nonsense to break up patterns. Whoever wrote this hadn't cared whether anyone broke it. They'd assumed no one would ever try.

That assumption was the first thing that frightened her.

* * *

She worked at night because the days belonged to performance. By day she was the dutiful courier, delivering Voss's sealed letters to their recipients, taking tea with the chancery clerks, smiling at Sergeant Teyo Brandt when he walked his rounds past the embassy gate and pretending she didn't notice how he always slowed there, how he always looked at the windows, counting what he saw. By day she asked nothing and noticed everything.

By night she laid the ciphered pages across the floor of her room, weighted at the corners with candlesticks, and fought them.

The trouble with a cipher you can't name is that you can't attack it directly. So she did what her mother had taught her to do with any locked thing: she stopped looking at the lock and started looking at what the lock was protecting. She made lists. Proper nouns, probably — names, places, sums. Repeated trigrams. The most common symbol in the whole corpus appeared 1,140 times; the next most common, 690. In Vessany's official cipher those ratios would have meant E and T. Here they meant something else, or the same things wearing different clothes.

On the ninth night she noticed the numbers.

Scattered through the text, unencrypted, were figures — prices, she thought at first. 14.2. 3.75. 61. She copied them all onto a single sheet and stared at them until the candles burned down, and then she laughed, once, without any humor in it at all, because she recognized them.

They weren't prices. They were page references.

Her mother's codebook — the one every Delacroix factor had memorized on the caravan routes, the one Coral had carried across three borders sewn into her coat lining — was built on a dictionary principle. A number sent you to a word. But the numbers only worked if both parties held the same edition, and the edition her family used was a merchant's ledger-almanac printed in Corvane thirty years ago, long out of print, worthless to anyone who wasn't born into the trade.

Someone in the embassy was using her mother's book.

She sat very still for a long time after that, listening to the wind worry the shutters and the far-off groan of ice on the Sarn, and let herself understand what it meant. It meant the writer of these pages was someone who had learned codes the way merchants learn them — informally, practically, in back rooms. Not a trained cryptographer from the foreign ministry. Someone older. Someone from before.

There were perhaps six people in Xanthe who fit that description, and four of them were dead.

* * *

Decoding went slowly, then all at once.

The almanac gave her the vocabulary; the surrounding cipher gave her the grammar. She worked out within two nights that the outer layer was a simple keyed alphabet, rotated by date — each page headed with a numeral that told her how many letters to shift. Sloppy. Arrogant. The work of a man who had never imagined an audience.

The first decoded page was accounts. Grain contracts, coal tonnage, the price of fodder. She almost stopped there, disappointed, telling herself she'd broken into a quartermaster's private ledger, that the mystery was embezzlement and nothing more.

Then she turned to the fourth sheet, and the dates on it stopped being routine, and the entries stopped being grain.

*Item: compensation schedules, officers of the Fourth Brigade, upon transfer of allegiance.*
*Item: pensions, civil administration, provisional authority.*
*Item: persons remanded to coalition custody per schedule annexed.*

Per schedule annexed. She flipped forward through the box, hunting. Most of the later pages were exactly that — annexes, lists, columns of figures beside columns of names rendered in the merchant numerals, which was why she hadn't seen them at first glance. Names weren't spelled out. Names were numbers. Page, line, word. A whole city's worth of people reduced to references in a book sold to caravan factors.

She pulled the almanac from her satchel with hands that would not quite stay steady and began to look them up.

The first twenty were officials. She knew some of the names — magistrates, the harbor commissioner, a senator's widow who had fled east ahead of the armies. Each entry had a notation beside it, a small circled letter. *A*, she worked out from context, meant detention. *B* meant removal — transport out of the city, destination unspecified. There were very few *A*s.

Then she reached entry sixty-one, and the circled letter was *C*, and there was no key anywhere in the box for what *C* meant, and somehow that was worse than any definition could have been.

She looked up the next name. Then the next. The room was very cold and she did not notice. The candle stubs burned down around her like a little congregation of witnesses, and she kept translating, because that was the only thing she knew how to do with horror — render it accurately, in full, without abbreviation.

Entry eighty-eight. She turned the pages of the almanac with numb fingers. Page 214, line 9, word 3.

*Delacroix.*

All of them. Her father, dead eleven years and listed anyway, as though the ledger of the living and the ledger of the owed could not be reconciled except by erasing both. Her aunt Meret in Corvane. Her cousins. The name repeated down the column seven times, seven branches of one family, each with its own figure beside it — not a pension, she understood suddenly, not a compensation. A bounty. A price per head, computed the way her mother had taught her to compute freight: by weight, by distance, by difficulty of handling.

*C* meant something you paid for.

She set down the almanac. She stood up. Her legs held, which surprised her. She walked to the window and looked out at the frozen river, white and silent under the moon, beautiful the way a blade is beautiful, and she thought: *they are going to sell us, and I am carrying the invoice.*

Because that was the final obscenity of it, the thing she couldn't stop turning over. The dispatch case strapped to her own shoulder, the sealed orders she had guarded across three hundred miles of winter road, the mission she had risked her life to complete — it was all one document with this box. The public treaty and the private annex. The surrender dressed as peace, and the peace priced per head, and her family's name entered in the column marked *C* in a cipher built from her own mother's trade-book.

Whoever wrote these pages had learned codes in the same school she had. She kept coming back to that. It was the detail her mind refused to release, worrying it like a loose thread. Six people alive who thought in merchant numerals. Four dead. Which left two, and one of them was herself.

* * *

She did not sleep. Near dawn she repacked the box exactly as she'd found it — hinge-line aligned, oilcloth folded to its original creases, the false manifest restored atop the crate — and stood in the windowless room for a moment with her palm flat against the pine lid, the way you'd rest your hand on a grave.

Then she went down through the sleeping chancery to Voss's study, meaning — she told herself — only to look at him, to confirm that the face she had loved since childhood could not be the face behind these pages.

He was awake. Of course he was awake; he was always awake now, sitting by his cold hearth in the gray hour with a single lamp, and on the desk before him lay a half-finished letter, and beside the letter, open to a dog-eared page, sat a merchant's almanac, Corvane printing, thirty years old.

Voss looked up. He saw her face. Whatever he read there, he didn't reach for the almanac, didn't close it, didn't move at all. He just watched her across the lamplight, an old man in a dying embassy, and said quietly:

"You always were your mother's daughter."

The words landed like a sentence pronounced. Coral stood in the doorway with the frozen river at her back and the decoded names burning in her memory, and understood that the conversation she had been dreading since the bridge had already begun — and that he had known, all along, exactly which page she would turn to first.
