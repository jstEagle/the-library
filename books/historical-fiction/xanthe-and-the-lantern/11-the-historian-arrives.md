## 11. The Historian Arrives

The flag of truce went up on the third morning after the river froze solid — a white banner with the coalition's grey chevron stitched beneath it, running up the eastern watchtower like a wound being bandaged.

I heard it before I saw it: the change in the garrison's sound. Xanthe had a particular noise, these past weeks — boots, hammering, the groan of ice against the bridge pilings, Teyo's men grumbling through their drills because Maren wanted them seen working even if there was nothing left to drill for. That morning the noise thinned. Men stopped at the walls. Even the wind seemed to pause to look.

From the archive window I watched the coalition party come down the east road: six riders under the white flag, and behind them a sledge drawn by two heavy horses, loaded with lacquered trunks that gleamed like beetle shells. Not ammunition. Not provisions. Furniture, I thought first. Then I understood what kind of furniture.

"Who is it?" I asked the clerk beside me — Pellam, one of Voss's junior men, who spent his days pretending not to count the days.

"Sereth Olland." He said the name the way you'd name a disease you'd only read about. "The historian."

"I know the name. Why would a historian come under flag of truce?"

Pellam gave me a look I was beginning to collect from everyone in this city — the look of a man deciding how much truth a courier could carry without breaking. "Because he's not coming to write about what happens here," he said. "He's coming to decide what it will have been."

* * *

Voss received the news in his quarters, standing at the window with his back to me, and for a long moment he said nothing at all. I had learned to read his silences the way other people read letters. This one said: *so it has begun.*

"They sent Olland," he said finally. "Not a general. Not a diplomat. A historian."

"That frightens you."

"It should frighten you more." He turned. The winter light made him look older than his sixty years, older than the man who had taught me my ciphers over a kitchen table while my mother laughed at us both. "A soldier takes a city and leaves ruins. A historian takes a city and leaves a story. Ruins can be rebuilt around. A story gets into everything. It gets into the children." He straightened his collar with a small, precise motion — the old ambassador's tic, preparing himself for a room. "Maren will receive him like visiting royalty. And we will attend, and smile, and be written down as reasonable men concluding a reasonable arrangement."

"And if we're not reasonable?"

"Then we will be written down anyway." He picked up his gloves. "Come. You should see this. Whatever else you are, Coral, you are a keeper of records, and you should see a record being manufactured while it's still warm."

* * *

They held the reception in the great hall of the citadel, the same hall where Maren had dined us three weeks earlier, but the room had been rearranged since then in ways that told their own story. The republican banners were gone from the walls. In their place hung the garrison's own colors, and above them, centered where no one could miss it, a single empty frame — large, gilded, waiting. Someone had already built the wall space for the portrait of the man who would deliver Xanthe to its future.

Maren stood beneath it in dress uniform, silver at the collar, looking comfortable in a way he never did among his own officers. Power fit him better than duty did. I had noticed it at the dinner; I noticed it now with the clarity of someone who had stopped hoping otherwise.

The doors opened and Sereth Olland came in, and I confess my first thought was disappointment. I had imagined the coalition's court historian as something monumental — tall, cold, a man carved out of verdicts. Instead he was small and round-shouldered, sixtyish, with ink-stained fingers and the apologetic stoop of a lifelong reader. He wore plain grey, no decorations, and he crossed the hall with his head slightly tilted, as if the room itself were a text he was skimming.

Then he reached Maren, took the general's hand in both of his own, and began to flatter — and I understood that the stoop was the weapon.

"General Maren." His voice was warm, unhurried, carrying without effort to every corner of the hall. "I have read your correspondence. All of it. Do you know what struck me? Not the strategy — though the strategy is elegant. What struck me was the mercy in it. Here is a man who could have made Xanthe a byword for resistance, who could have given the republic one last beautiful corpse to worship for a century — and chose instead to give it a future." He released the hand and stepped back, surveying Maren like a statue he approved of. "History remembers sieges, General. But it *loves* surrenders done well."

Somewhere near me an officer exhaled something that was almost a laugh. Maren smiled the smile of a man being told exactly what he had paid to hear.

"You flatter me, Master Olland."

"Historians don't flatter." Olland's eyes twinkled. "We merely quote selectively, and we intend to quote you extensively." He gestured, and his assistants began opening the lacquered trunks right there in the hall — no shame in it, no pretense. Out came ledgers, folios, a portable writing desk of polished walnut, an inkstand with three wells. "I've taken the liberty of beginning the official account. 'The Orderly Dissolution of the Republic of Vessany' — that's the title the chancellery prefers. I confess I argued for it. Dissolution suggests chemistry, doesn't it? Something natural. Something that was always going to happen." He said it lightly, a joke between reasonable men, and half the room laughed.

I did not laugh. I stood very still and felt the floor tilt a degree beneath me.

*Orderly dissolution.* Four words doing the work of ten thousand omitted ones. No starvation in them. No deserters turned away from the bridge to freeze in the reeds — I had heard them in the dark, that night, crying to men on the walls they had defended. No ciphered lists of names traded like currency. No massacre planned and filed under *security measures*. Just chemistry. Something that was always going to happen.

Olland moved through the hall like water finding its level, and everywhere he stopped, he stopped with purpose. To the quartermaster: "You'll want the provisioning figures precise — hunger makes poor history, all that rumor." To a captain whose company had held the east wall: "Your name, sir — spelling? Good. Names matter. A history without names is just weather." To each person a small gift: attention, importance, the promise of appearing in the record. He was collecting the city the way a leech feeds, painlessly, and every person he touched stood a little straighter afterward, already rehearsing their own paragraph.

And then he stopped in front of Ambassador Ilan Voss.

* * *

I was close enough to hear it. Close enough to see everything.

"Ambassador." Olland bowed — correctly, precisely, the bow owed to a representative of a state that still technically existed. "An honor. Your dispatches have been models of clarity. Future scholars will thank you."

"Future scholars," Voss said, "will read what you permit them to read."

Olland's smile didn't move a muscle. "Ambassador, may I be frank? We are both professionals of the document. You know as well as I that there has never been a history without a hand on the scale. The question is never whether the scale is held — it is who holds it, and how gently." He leaned in, confiding, almost kind. "I hold it gently. Ask anyone. I let the defeated keep their dignity. I give the dead good deaths. Is that so terrible a service?"

"It is a service," Voss said quietly. "I have performed versions of it myself for thirty years. That is why I know its price."

"And yet here you are," Olland said pleasantly, "at the signing."

The words dropped into the hall and lay there. Around us the conversation continued — wine poured, names spelled, the walnut desk set up by the fire — but I heard nothing else clearly. I watched my guardian's face do something I had never seen it do: nothing. Total stillness, the stillness of a man standing on ice and hearing it begin to speak underneath him.

Olland straightened, patted Voss once on the arm — a gesture of such gentle condescension it constituted an execution — and moved on to General Maren, already talking, already drafting aloud: "...and here, of course, we'll place the account of your negotiations with the embassy, emphasizing the months of patient diplomacy..."

Patient diplomacy. Months. I thought of the second cipher, the dispatches that bypassed me, the decoded line I had read by candlelight with my hands shaking: *surrender terms*, and beneath it, names. My family's names. There had been no months. There had been weeks, and lies, and a girl carrying sealed paper she was never meant to understand.

That was when Olland's gaze crossed mine.

It lasted perhaps two seconds. He was mid-sentence, mid-flattery, moving through the crowd — and then his eyes found me, a woman standing too still in a room full of performing people, and paused. Not recognition. Assessment. The look of a man cataloguing: *witness, unattached, intelligent, possibly useful, note her.*

He inclined his head slightly. I inclined mine. Two professionals of the document, acknowledging each other across a room where history was being fitted for clothes it hadn't earned.

* * *

I found Teyo Brandt afterward on the east bastion, where the wind came off the frozen river hard enough to make conversation a private thing.

"Well?" he said. He'd watched the reception from up here, he told me, rather than stand in a warm room and applaud. "What's he like?"

"He's worse than a monster."

Teyo snorted. "That's a new one."

"A monster you can fight. A monster knows it's hated." I pulled my coat tighter and looked down at the river — white now, featureless, the bridge closed, the far shore a grey suggestion through the haze. Somewhere beyond it, the coalition armies waited for spring and paperwork. "Olland is pleasant. He's careful. He'll give every dead man a dignified death and every survivor a survivable story, and in fifty years no one will be able to prove him wrong, because he'll have gotten to the proof first. He isn't writing lies, Teyo. He's writing the version that makes lying unnecessary."

Teyo was quiet a moment. Below us, on the ice, a squad of engineers walked the river's edge, driving measuring stakes — checking the thickness, marking the safe lanes. Preparing, I realized, for the thaw. Or for whatever Maren intended to happen when the thaw came.

"My wife used to say something," Teyo said at last. "She was a schoolteacher, before. She said: show me who writes the books and I'll tell you who won, and show me who reads them and I'll tell you who they're turning into." He spat over the parapet. "Never understood it till today."

"Teyo." I kept my voice level, though my pulse wasn't. "If someone wanted to get a true account off this river — past those stakes, past the guards, past everything — how thick is the ice really?"

He turned and looked at me for a long time. Wind. Ice groaning somewhere out in the dark like a ship changing its mind. I watched him decide, deliberately, not to ask what I meant.

"Thick enough to walk," he said. "Thin enough to break. Same as everything else in this city." He glanced back toward the citadel, toward the lit windows where Sereth Olland was dining with General Aurec Maren, two men agreeing on the past before it finished happening. "Ask me again when you're ready to hear the answer."

I wasn't ready. But that night, back in the archive, I unlocked the lantern my father had left me, and took out the papers hidden in its base, and began to plan as if I already were.
