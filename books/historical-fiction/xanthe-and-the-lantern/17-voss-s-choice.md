## 17. Voss's Choice

The summons came at the hour when even the guards stopped pretending to watch.

A boy in Maren's gray livery — not Voss's own household, those men had been reassigned two days ago, politely, permanently — stood in Coral's doorway with a folded slip of paper and would not meet her eyes. The message was five words long, written in a hand she had known since she was six years old, since the first time it had appeared at the top of her lessons: *Come now. Come alone. Please.*

The *please* frightened her more than anything Maren had done.

She went down through the embassy's back corridors, past the sealed archive door with its new wax and its new lock, past the clerk's station where the junior man who had betrayed her sat copying inventory lists with the diligence of someone who had chosen survival and could not look at what that choice cost. He did not look up. She did not slow.

Voss had been moved. Not to a cell — Maren was too careful for cells, too careful for anything that might later be called cruelty — but to the old map room on the embassy's ground floor, the one room in the building that had never been wired for listening, because Ilan Voss had insisted on it twenty years ago when the republic still had the standing to make such demands. The garrison had honored the insistence. Old courtesies die last.

He was standing at the great table when she entered, both hands flat on the surface, as if he were holding the room down against a wind only he could feel. He had shaved. That was the first thing she noticed, and it undid her more than the bruised hollows under his eyes or the way his coat hung off him now, a coat that had fit him like armor all her life. He had shaved because he intended to be seen clearly. She understood this without knowing how she understood it. Some preparations announce themselves.

"You came alone," he said.

"You wrote please."

"I wasn't sure you'd come at all." He managed something that was almost a smile. "You've become a very stubborn woman, Coral. I'd like to take credit for it."

"Take the blame instead." She stayed by the door. "Maren showed me your cipher traffic. Your letters to the capital. Every concession you offered before they asked. You didn't just carry the surrender terms, Ilan. You *drafted* them."

"Yes."

"That's all? Yes?"

"What would you have me say?" He straightened, and for a moment the old envoy stood there, the man who had faced down three coalition delegations and talked a fleet out of bombarding a neutral port, the man whose voice had taught her that words were load-bearing structures. "That I did it reluctantly? That I wept over each clause? You read the dispatches. You know the arithmetic better than I do. The war was lost before I ever boarded the boat. My task was to lose it *smaller*."

"And my family is part of what got smaller."

He flinched. It was real; she had spent a lifetime learning to tell his real flinches from his performed ones, and this one reached his hands. "Yes," he said again, quieter. "Your family. And four hundred other names. Officials, officers, archivists, teachers. Everyone the coalition's security commission flagged as a future problem. Maren agreed to hand them over as a condition of his own amnesty. I signed the schedule of names myself."

"With my mother's name on it."

"With your mother's name on it," he said, "and I told myself it was a list, not an execution order. I told myself names on paper are not deaths. I told myself a great many things, Coral, and every one of them was true, and every one of them was a lie, and I have not slept properly since the second week of autumn." He looked down at his hands. "You want me to beg your forgiveness. I won't insult you with it. What I have instead is worse and more useful."

* * *

He crossed to the cabinet in the corner — the one she had catalogued herself, weeks ago, in another life, and marked *empty, personal effects* — and took from it a leather folio, plain, unmarked, the kind envoys used for notes never meant to leave their own desks. He set it on the table between them but did not push it toward her.

"My testimony," he said. "Written over eleven nights, in my own hand, dated and signed. Everything. The original instructions from the Provisional Council — which were, whatever else you believe of me, genuinely a request for terms. But also the letters that followed. The private channel to Maren's aide. The amendments I proposed. The names I added to protect people I loved, which meant removing protections from people I merely owed duty to. The moment I understood what 'orderly dissolution' meant in the security commission's vocabulary, and the moment I decided to keep understanding it wrong." He tapped the folio once. "It is not a confession extracted under duress. It is not a forgery. It is the account of a man who chose, at every fork, the road that let him keep his position, and it ends where it ends."

Coral looked at the folio and did not touch it. "Why now?"

"Because Sereth Olland arrived." Voss said it simply, and the simplicity was the blow. "I've met his kind before. In the last war, in the one before. A historian attached to a victorious army is not a scholar, Coral, he is a mortician. He arrives to embalm events before they cool. I watched him at dinner tonight taking notes on Maren's jokes. In six months there will be a book. In ten years it will be the book. *The Peaceful Settlement of the Sarn Question.* Orderly. Inevitable. No massacre, because massacres require records, and records require witnesses, and the witnesses will be—" he gestured vaguely eastward, toward the river, toward everything— "elsewhere."

"So you're giving me the truth to spite them."

"I'm giving you the truth because it's the only thing I have left that isn't broken." For the first time his voice cracked, and he let it. "Listen to me. I raised you on stories about service. About the republic as a lantern — your father's word, not mine, though I borrowed it shamelessly. Light carried forward. I believed it when you were a child. Somewhere in the middle years I stopped believing it and kept saying it, because the saying was useful, because the saying kept young couriers carrying sealed cases through frozen passes for a state I already knew was being sold out from under them. That is my crime, Coral. Not treason. Worse than treason. I made loyalty into a tool and handed it to children."

"I'm not a child."

"No." He studied her face, and whatever he saw there seemed to settle something in him. "No, you're not. You cracked a cipher I helped design, copied documents under a traitor's roof, and refused a general's offer to his face. I've served with ambassadors who couldn't have done one of those things." He pushed the folio across the table at last. "So I'm asking you to do the one thing left. Take it across the ice. Take it with the lists, with the forged ledgers, with everything you've already gathered. Let my account stand next to theirs. If history must judge me, let it judge me on the whole record — mine included, against me."

"Why should I trust it?" The question came out harder than she intended, and she let it stay hard. "You lied to me for months. You let me courier surrender papers believing they were peace papers. Why is this document different from every other document you've ever sealed?"

He was quiet for a long time. Outside, wind moved along the walls of Xanthe, and somewhere above them a sentry's boots crossed a floor.

"Because every other document I ever sealed was designed to be opened by someone who needed to believe something," he said finally. "This one is designed to be opened by someone who needs to *know*. There's a difference. It's the difference I built my whole life on forgetting." He met her eyes. "And because I signed it. Whatever happens after tonight, that signature will cost me everything Maren has left me — which is comfort, and safety, and the fiction that I acted alone. Olland will burn it if he can. Maren will call it forged. The coalition will bury it. But it will exist, Coral. It will have existed. Someone will have held it in their hands and read it and known that one man inside the machine said: *this is what we did, and here is how, and here is my name.*"

* * *

She picked up the folio. It weighed almost nothing. Documents always weighed almost nothing; that was the whole terror of them.

"The lantern," she said. "It fits in the base. With the rest."

"I know. I chose the lantern for you, after your father died. Did you think that was sentiment?" Something flickered across his face that was almost the old mischief. "It was contingency. I've hidden papers in worse places. Though I confess I hoped you'd never need it."

"You hoped a lot of things."

"I did." He exhaled. "Coral. When you cross — and you will cross, Teyo Brandt is a better man than either of us and he'll get you to the water — when you're on the far bank and they detain you, and they will detain you, and some clever staff officer puts you in a room with Sereth Olland — don't argue with him. Don't plead. Historians are vain in a very particular way. Show him his own draft next to the truth and let him see which one he wants to be author of. Vanity is a door. Walk through it."

"You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else for eleven nights." He came around the table then, slowly, an old man's crossing of a short distance, and stopped in front of her. Up close she could see he had been crying earlier, or had made himself cry, working the grief loose the way you work a stiff lock. "I have one more thing to ask. Not forgiveness. I haven't earned the right to ask for that. Just — remember me accurately. Not kindly. Accurately. The record doesn't need me to be good. It needs me to be *true*, and I finally am."

She wanted to hate him cleanly and found she could not. That was the cruelest thing he had ever done to her: making himself legible too late.

"Ilan," she said. "If they take you—"

"They will take me tomorrow, or the day after. Maren can't leave me functional any longer than he has to; my existence embarrasses the treaty. It doesn't matter." He touched her shoulder once, lightly, the way he had on the dock at Vessany City when she was nine years old and terrified of the water. "What matters is that the river thaws eventually, and so does everything else. Go home, Coral. Go east. Write it down."

* * *

She was at the door when he spoke again, and she turned, and he was standing very straight in the lamplight with his hands behind his back like a man addressing a delegation, and for a moment he was entirely the envoy again, entire and unbowed.

"One correction to the record," he said. "For your file. The mission was never peace. But you — what you're doing now, tonight, with a sledge and a sergeant and a dead man's lantern — *that* is the mission. That was always the mission. Everything else was staging."

She went out into the cold. Behind her, through the closing door, she heard him begin, quietly, to dictate to himself the final paragraph of a document no one would read for years — and outside, along the river road, the ice groaned once, long and low, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Teyo was waiting in the shadow of the gatehouse. He read her face in the dark.

"He gave you everything?"

"He gave me everything."

Teyo nodded slowly and looked out at the black river. "Then we go tomorrow night," he said. "Before Maren finds out what he's given you. Before the thaw. Before any of it." He pulled his collar up against the wind. "Get some sleep, courier. Tomorrow we carry the republic across the ice."
