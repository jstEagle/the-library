## 3. One Question Too Many

Mr. Ambrose Pell believed in the sanctity of lists.

He had believed in them since boyhood, when his father, a customs clerk at Dover, had taught him that a thing unwritten was a thing that had never happened. *The Empire runs on paper,* his father used to say, tapping the ledger with one ink-stained finger. *Men run on blood. Paper outlasts both.* Pell was forty-one years old now, and he had made of this inheritance a creed. He carried his census satchel like a sacrament — leather worn to a soft brown shine, brass buckles polished, inside it the forms of His Majesty's 1917 register arranged in exact order: Schedule A, Schedule B, the supplementary returns for institutional households, and the thin blue carbon copies on which he recorded everything twice.

Saltmere was the eleventh stop on his circuit through the coastal parishes, and by all accounts the least important. A convalescent house, they called it — a straggling grey pile above the shingle with salt-scoured windows and a garden gone to seed and rosemary. Such places were multiplying along the coast like barnacles, full of men the war had finished with but not yet killed. Pell did not dislike them. He simply could not abide what they did to arithmetic.

He knocked at half past nine in the morning, precisely as scheduled, and was admitted by a housemaid who looked at his satchel the way one looks at a snake.

"Mrs. Aldery will see you in the morning room," she said.

Mrs. Aldery proved to be a tall woman of sixty-odd with iron-grey hair pinned so severely it seemed less styled than sentenced, and eyes the colour of a winter sea — pale, flat, and capable of great depth without revealing anything. She received him standing, which he noted. Most people sat when a man with official papers entered; sitting was surrender's posture.

"Mr. Pell," she said, reading his card without taking it. "The census man."

"The Registrar General's enumerator for the district, madam. I'm completing the institutional returns. Your house shelters wounded servicemen, I understand."

"It shelters men," Mrs. Aldery said. "Some of them are wounded. Some of them are merely tired. The distinction is not always ours to make."

Pell uncapped his pen. "I'll need names. Full names, ranks, regiments, dates of admission, and the nature of each man's condition. The form requires—"

"I know what the form requires." She said it without heat, which unsettled him more than anger would have. Anger was a thing a man could push against. "You'll have what the form requires, Mr. Pell. You'll have nothing else."

* * *

She gave him eleven names.

He wrote them down in his careful clerk's hand, and even as he wrote, something in him began to itch. It was not the names themselves — they were ordinary enough, a Wren, an Ives, a boy listed as T. Marsh whose age she gave as seventeen when the lad himself, glimpsed through the morning-room window carrying coals, could not have seen eighteen winters. No. The itch was the house itself.

Eleven names. But he had counted beds.

He had counted them before he ever knocked — a habit his father would have approved of — walking the gravel drive slowly while pretending to consult his route map. Windows told you everything if you knew how to read them. He had counted fourteen upper windows with curtains drawn against the sea light, and curtains drawn meant rooms in use, and rooms in use meant beds, and beds meant bodies, and bodies meant names.

Fourteen windows. Eleven names.

It might be nothing. Linen cupboards, stores, a room kept for the doctor. Pell had spent twenty-three years among forms and he knew better than most how easily the world refused to match its paperwork. But his father's voice was in his ear again, patient and terrible: *A discrepancy is never small, Ambrose. A discrepancy is a door, and behind every door there is either a mistake or a man, and it is your business to learn which.*

After luncheon — which Mrs. Aldery did not offer and he did not request — he asked to see the wards. She granted it with a graciousness that should have reassured him and instead rang like a struck bell: too clear, too deliberate. She wanted him to see the wards. Therefore the wards contained nothing. Therefore he must look elsewhere.

The first ward was exactly what a ward should be. Six iron bedsteads, six men, six faces turned toward him with the blank courtesy of the photographed. He checked names against his list. All present. All correct. The second ward held five men, and here his pen paused over the fifth bed, because the man in it was not on Mrs. Aldery's list at all.

"Name?" Pell asked.

The man looked at the nurse — a slender figure in a grey uniform, cap low over watchful eyes, hands stained with iodine — before answering, and the pause lasted one second too long.

"Barnes," the man said. "Private Barnes."

Pell wrote it down. He wrote it in the margin rather than on the line, because the line belonged to the register and the margin belonged to him.

* * *

It was the nurse who troubled him most, though he could not at first have said why.

Edmund Thackeray — the name was on the staff schedule Mrs. Aldery had provided, listed as assistant matron, though Thackeray moved through the wards with an authority no assistant possessed. There was a stillness about the nurse that Pell distrusted on principle. Stillness, in his experience, was what people practiced when they had something to hide; honest people fidgeted, apologized, filled silence with confession. This one watched him the way a lighthouse watches a ship — steadily, impersonally, and with a fixed intention of warning him off the rocks.

In the evening, when the household gathered the patients into the small parlour for tea, Pell requested permission to complete his door-to-door verification of the staff quarters. Mrs. Aldery, reading a letter by the window, said, "The staff rooms are up the back stair. Mind the fourth step; it's loose," and did not look up.

He minded the step. He also counted doors. Four staff rooms, three occupied — or so the arrangement of boots and basins suggested. The fourth door stood ajar on a narrow cell of a room: iron bed, washstand, a crucifix of driftwood nailed above the pillow, and a Bible. On the bed, folded back with military precision, lay a blanket, and beneath the edge of the pillow —

Pell was not a thief. He wished the record to show that he was not a thief. But a pillow askew is public property to an enumerator verifying occupancy, and he lifted the corner of it with two fingers the way a surgeon lifts a dressing, meaning only to confirm there was nothing beneath it.

There was something beneath it.

A book. Small, dark, cheaply bound — a stationer's ledger of the sort sold for sixpence in any town in Kent. It was not the Bible. It was not the ward's medicine book, which lived on its proper shelf downstairs. It was a private thing, hidden the way private things are hidden, and Pell felt his heart begin that slow, cold acceleration he had learned to recognize as the physical signature of discovery.

He did not open it. That was the discipline his father had drilled into him: *Never touch the evidence until you can name it.* He set the pillow back exactly as he'd found it, noted the room number in his pocket memorandum — E.T., north wing, fourth door — and withdrew.

But his hand remembered the weight of the corner of that book. Cheap paper. Many pages. And pages, in a house where names went missing, meant only one thing to a man like Ambrose Pell.

* * *

That night, in the rented room above the Anchor Inn, he built his case the way other men built fires: patiently, stick by stick.

The parish records lay open across the counterpane — he had collected them from the vicar that afternoon under the pretext of verifying baptisms, and the Reverend Mr. Soames, being a man who believed in cooperation between authorities, had handed over the registers with cheerful innocence. Pell worked by lamplight, cross-referencing, and the work was to him what prayer was to other men.

Eleven names from Saltmere. He took the first: Wren, P., private. Found him — a birth registered in Ashford, 1893, a family of hop-pickers, and here his pen stopped. Because beside the Wren entries, in the same cramped parental hand, was another name. *Wren, J.* Born 1889. And Private Wren had told the enquiry clerk at his admission — Pell had obtained a copy of the admission summary from the regional medical office, because obtaining copies was what he did — that he had no next of kin living.

No next of kin living. Then why did the parish register show a brother alive and enlisted in 1914, with no death record anywhere in Kent?

Perhaps the brother was dead in France. France swallowed records whole; Pell knew that better than anyone. Perhaps. He wrote *Wren, J.?* in the margin and moved on, and the moving-on was the most disciplined act of his life, because every instinct he owned was screaming at him to dig.

By midnight he had four question marks on the page. Four men whose stories bent slightly away from the shape of their paperwork. Not proof. Never proof — proof was a luxury the bureaucratic mind learned early to live without. What he had was pattern, and pattern was better than proof, because proof ended cases and patterns fed them.

He lit a fresh lamp and laid out his blue carbons. Eleven names given. Fourteen curtained windows. One name in the margin — Barnes — absent from the official return. One hidden book beneath a nurse's pillow. He drew a line connecting them, because his father had taught him that too: *When the facts won't speak, make them stand in a row, and they'll confess.*

Outside, the sea worked at the shingle with its patient, indifferent sound, and somewhere beyond it — thirty miles, perhaps forty — the German fleet lay at anchor in the dark, and men in that fleet had orders no census taker would ever see. Pell knew nothing of raid timetables or naval dispositions. He knew only that the coast was nervous, that church bells were muffled, that the lifeboat crew drilled at odd hours. War had taught the coastline to listen. Pell listened too, in his own key.

What he heard was Saltmere, counting wrong.

He thought of the nurse's face — Thackeray's face — calm and level and closed as a ledger's cover. He thought of the boy carrying coals who was sixteen at most and listed as seventeen. He thought of the hidden book, sixpenny boards and secret pages, and found that his hands had begun to tremble, not with fear but with the peculiar ecstasy of the man who has caught the scent of a trail after years of empty road.

His father had died believing the world was saved by accurate paper. Pell intended to die believing it too, and between now and then there was work.

He blew out the lamp, and in the dark he spoke aloud to the empty room, quietly, the way one speaks to a jury that isn't there yet:

"One question too many, Nurse Thackeray. Just one. And I mean to find it."

Below his window, the tide came in over the names of the drowned, and kept none of them.
