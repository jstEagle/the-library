## 1. The Weight of Rivers

The ink froze first.

Ulyana learned this in her third winter as an apprentice: that the river outside Master Dario's workshop was not the only thing that could be stopped by cold. The ink went sluggish at first, dragging behind the pen like a thing reluctant to speak, and then it thickened altogether, and she had to set the pot on the iron stove and warm it with her breath cupped around the rim, whispering into it as though it were a small animal she was coaxing awake. *Come now. Come now.* The words had become a prayer she did not know she was praying.

She warmed her hands over the same stove, then returned to the plate.

The workshop smelled of tallow and cold stone and the particular sweetness of aged paper — a smell like bread that had been forgotten in a drawer, or like the inside of an old violin. Frost ferned across the north window in patterns so intricate they might have been engraved by one of Dario's own burins, and Ulyana had caught herself more than once studying them instead of her work, thinking that if she were asked to draw frost from memory she would fail, because frost drew itself only once and never the same way twice. That was the difference between the world and the map. The map could be corrected. The world simply happened.

On the table before her lay Plate Forty-Four — the last plate, the delta country where the Vessa split into nine brown fingers and lost itself in the marshes that even the empire's surveyors had never properly walked. Dario had drawn the coastline before he died. He had drawn the soundings, the sandbars, the little villages marked with the circles that meant *inhabited, unfortified, poor*. What remained for Ulyana was the lettering, the hachures along the low bluffs, the final washes of blue — and the title cartouche, which she had left deliberately for last, the way one leaves the hardest word of a letter until the end so that everything before it can be written without trembling.

*Atlas of the River Provinces of the Empire,* it would say. *Compiled by Dario Marchetti, Master Cartographer. Completed by his apprentice.*

She had argued with herself about that last line for eleven days. *Completed by* sounded like an apology. But to leave it off would be a kind of theft, and worse than theft — a lie about how things are made. Nothing was made by one pair of hands. Even the rivers were made by rain that fell on mountains the rivers would never see.

She dipped the pen. The ink had warmed enough to flow. Outside, the light was doing what November light did in Veressk — arriving late, leaving early, spending the hours between in a gray indecision that made the snow on the rooftops look less like snow than like ash.

* * *

She measured everything now by what could be carried.

This was a habit she could not name the beginning of, though she suspected it had started the day Dario died — four months ago, coughing blood into a rag in the back room while she held the lamp steady so he could see the coastlines one last time. He had gripped her wrist with fingers thin as drafting tools and said, *Finish it. They will ask what a town is worth. Show them.* And ever since, some part of her mind had run its quiet arithmetic beneath every other thought: the loaf on the shelf, two pounds; the jar of goose fat, one pound; Grandmother Rosa's medicine, the brown glass bottle with the wax-sealed cork, half a pound but worth more than all the rest. The atlas itself, bound, would weigh perhaps nine pounds. She knew this because she had weighed it. She had stood in the empty workshop at midnight holding the loose plates against her chest like a sleeping child, and then she had put them on the scale, because knowing a thing's weight was the closest she could come to knowing whether she could save it.

Nine pounds. A man could carry nine pounds up any hill in the world.

Her grandmother's medicine was running low. There would be no more until the spring boats came, if the spring boats came, if there was anyone left to row them. Ulyana added this to the ledger she kept in her head, the one with no columns and no sums, only items and their weights and the growing suspicion that the total exceeded what two hands could hold.

Tomas came in just past noon, stamping snow off his boots in the doorway, bringing with him a gust of cold and the smell of the tannery where he worked hides six days a week. He was seventeen and had grown a hand's width taller since summer, as if his body were trying to outrun something. His knuckles were cracked from the lime vats. He set a wrapped parcel on the table — bread, still warm through the cloth — and looked at the plate she was inking.

"The delta," he said. "You're finally doing the delta."

"Finally."

"Does it look like home?"

"It looks like lines," Ulyana said. "It always looks like lines until you're standing in it."

He grunted, which from Tomas meant agreement, and pulled a stool to the stove, and for a while the only sounds were the scratch of her pen and the tick of the cooling iron and the wind working at the shutters like a patient creditor.

* * *

The crier came at the gray hour when afternoon gave up pretending to be day.

They heard him first as a bell — the garrison bell, which had hung silent for so long that its voice had gone rusty, so that it rang less like a summons than like a complaint. Then footsteps, many boots in rhythm, and then the voice, amplified by the brass cone the criers carried, flattening the words so that they arrived at every ear in the town with the same indifference, the same temperature, like snow falling equally on the church roof and the jail.

Ulyana put down her pen.

Through the frosted window she could see nothing but shapes — the dark mass of soldiers, the smaller shape of the crier on his crate, the lanterns they carried burning yellow through the murk. But the voice carried through the glass and the frost and the years of her life as if none of those things existed:

*"By order of the Provisional Command of the Eastern Marches: all male subjects of the Empire above the age of fourteen years are hereby conscripted to the Winter Levy. Report to the garrison square within three days, bearing your own provisions for seven days' march. Exemptions granted only upon presentation of certified papers. Failure to report is desertion. Desertion is death."*

The words repeated. The crier always repeated them; that was the craft of it, the way a mapmaker repeated a coastline until the reader believed it was real. *Desertion is death. Desertion is death.* Somewhere down the street a dog began to bark, and somewhere else a door slammed, and the ordinary sounds of Veressk continued exactly as before, which was the most frightening thing of all — that the world could receive a sentence like that and simply go on boiling its kettles.

In the workshop, Tomas had gone very still.

He was standing by the stove with his hands hanging at his sides, and Ulyana watched the color move in his face the way weather moves across a plain — first white, then red, then a settled, dangerous calm. Seventeen. Above fourteen. Three days.

"They can't," he said. It was not a question and not a statement. It was a man testing the floorboards of a sentence to see if it would bear his weight.

"Tomas—"

"They can't." Louder now. "There's no war. There's nothing out there but villages and old men and the marsh. Who are we fighting? Who is it *for*?" He struck the table with the flat of his hand, and the inkpot jumped, and a bead of black rolled toward the edge of Plate Forty-Four, and Ulyana lunged and caught it with the corner of her blotting cloth before it could reach the delta, before it could drown the little unfortified villages she had spent three days rendering true.

She looked up at her brother over the saved map, and something in her chest folded closed, quietly, like a compass shutting.

"We fight," she said, "because they have more boots than we do. That's the whole of the argument. It has always been the whole of the argument." She said it gently, which was the cruelest way to say it, and she saw him understand that she was right, and she saw him refuse to understand it, both at once, the way a boy refuses a thing precisely because understanding it would end his boyhood.

"I'm not going," Tomas said. "I'll burn my papers. I'll walk into the marsh. Let them chase me through nine rivers."

"You'd die in the first one. You swim like a sack of nails."

"Then I'll die on land like a man instead of inking coastlines like a coward—"

"Do not." Her voice came out lower than she intended, and harder. "Do not call this cowardice. You don't know what this is yet. Neither do I." She looked down at the plate, at the fine blue veins of the delta, at the title cartouche waiting blank as an open grave. Nine pounds, she thought. Two pounds of bread. Half a pound of medicine. One brother, eleven stone, who could not be weighed on any scale she owned.

Grandmother Rosa appeared in the doorway of the back room, wrapped in two shawls, her face the color of the paper on the table. She had heard. Of course she had heard; the woman missed nothing that happened within a mile of her, and much that happened beyond it. She looked at Tomas for a long moment, and then at Ulyana, and whatever passed behind her eyes was old and cold and prepared, the look of someone who had buried a husband and a son and had already begun, silently, to count what this house could afford to lose.

"Supper," Rosa said. "Both of you. Now. The arguing keeps."

But as Ulyana covered the plate with its linen cloth and banked the stove, she understood that the arguing would not keep — that it had already begun, in fact, in the silence of all three of them moving through the workshop gathering bread and shawls and the brown glass bottle, each measuring, each weighing, each performing the same secret arithmetic she had taught herself without meaning to.

What can be carried. What must be left. Who.

Outside, the garrison bell rang again, rusty and patient, and the snow began to fall on Veressk — evenly, indifferently, covering the roofs and the square and the road to the bridge that would close, though none of them yet knew how soon, with the season's last crossing.
