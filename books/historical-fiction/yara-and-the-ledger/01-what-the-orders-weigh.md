## 1. What the Orders Weigh

The orders arrived at dusk, which was typical. Nothing in this war came at a civilized hour — not the shelling, not the dysentery, and certainly not the paperwork that decided who lived long enough to complain about either.

I was elbow-deep in a basin of gray water, wringing out bandages for the fourth time because we'd run out of clean ones two days ago, when Corporal Vess stuck his head through the tent flap and held up an envelope as though it were something venomous.

"Dispatches, Dunmore."

"Put it with the others."

"There are no others. This is the first one in nine days."

That was true, and I hated him a little for saying it aloud. Nine days of silence from Division usually meant they'd forgotten we existed. One dispatch after nine days meant they remembered, and remembering was never good news. I dried my hands on my apron — a gesture of pure superstition, since the apron had not been dry since spring — and took the envelope.

It was thin. Thin envelopes meant short orders, and short orders meant someone senior had made a decision without consulting anyone who'd have to carry it out. I read it twice, standing there with wet hands, while Vess watched my face the way men watch a surgeon's face over an open wound.

*Evacuate within forty-eight hours. Transport capacity: twelve litters. Triage accordingly.*

Twelve litters. We had thirty-one patients who could not walk.

"Well," I said, folding the paper into quarters, then eighths, then small enough to disappear into my pocket, where it could pretend to be nothing. "That's arithmetic, isn't it."

Vess said nothing. He was twenty-two and still believed that if he stood quietly enough near me, the war might mistake him for furniture and leave him alone. It was a reasonable strategy. I'd have tried it myself if I weren't already furniture.

"Triage accordingly," I repeated, mostly to hear how ugly it sounded in my own voice. Accordingly. As though there were a manner of doing it that wouldn't sit in my chest like a swallowed stone for the rest of whatever life I had left.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about triage: it is not medicine. Medicine is a conversation between what's wrong with a body and what you know about bodies. Triage is a conversation between what's wrong with a body and what a wagon can hold. The body doesn't get to speak in the second conversation. Neither, really, do you.

I walked the ward that night with a slate and a stub of chalk, and I did the thing I have done so many times that my feet know the route better than my conscience does. Thirty-one patients. Thirty-one names, ages, wounds, fevers, and — the column nobody writes down but every nurse keeps anyway — *weight*. Not the weight of the man. The weight of the man *plus* his dressings, his blanket, his fever, his likelihood of screaming on a jolting road, his odds of arriving anywhere alive.

Private Ellery Hask, nineteen, gut wound, three days post-op, fever spiking. Carrying him would be carrying a death with extra steps. Leave him, and he dies here, warm, with morphine, with someone holding his hand. Take him, and he dies on the road, cold, jounced apart, alone among strangers. The mathematics were identical. The dying was not.

I marked him: *stay.* Then I stood there longer than the chalk required, because Ellery Hask had told me last Tuesday about his mother's plum trees, and because marking a man *stay* is just another way of writing a date on him, and I am, God help me, a person who writes things down.

Sergeant Odo Brannic, forty, thigh shattered above the knee, infection setting in but responding to the last of the carbolic. Twelve litters. He'd take one. He'd earned one by being too stubborn to die efficiently; the man had been hit in June and had spent the summer complaining about the quality of our coffee with such sustained artistry that half the ward credited him with their wills to live. You cannot put a number on that. I put a number on it anyway, because the litters don't care about artistry, and marked him: *carry.*

And so on, down the line, thirty-one times, until the slate held a ledger of its own — the carried and the stayed, the saved and the surrendered, sorted by tonnage. When I finished, my hand was steady and my stomach was not, and I went outside into the dark behind the supply tent to do the other accounting. The one no one ordered. The one no one would ever order.

* * *

The ledger lives in the bottom of my kit, wrapped in an oilcloth that has seen better decades. It began its life as a quartermaster's inventory book — you can still see *BOOTS, PAIRS* faintly ghosting through the first page where I pasted paper over it — and it has become, through no plan of mine, the only honest document in this entire theater of war.

Not that I set out to keep it. In the beginning it was spite, pure and simple. Three years ago, at Marrow Ford, we lost eleven men in a single afternoon and the official report recorded four. Four. As though seven soldiers had simply evaporated between the riverbank and the colonel's desk, as though shrapnel had opinions about which casualties deserved to exist. I copied the missing seven into the quartermaster's book that same night, sitting on an ammunition crate with a candle stub, feeling like both a criminal and a priest, which I have since learned are frequently the same occupation.

After that it became habit, and habit became compulsion, and compulsion became the closest thing I have to a religion. Every dead man the reports omit. Every civilian the army never counted in the first place. Every name that fell through the cracks between what happened and what got written down. I record them. Name, age, where they fell, what killed them, and — when I know it, which is more often than you'd think, because people talk to nurses even when they won't talk to officers — one line about who they were. *Laughed at his own jokes before anyone else could.* *Carried a lock of his sister's hair in his boot.* *Afraid of the dark and admitted it, which took more courage than the bayonet did.*

Four hundred and six entries now. I know the number the way other men know their own age.

Tonight I had three new ones.

* * *

The first was Corporal Fenwick, who died at four in the afternoon of a wound that should not have killed him — a flesh tear in the shoulder, clean, the kind of thing men write home about. But infection found him anyway, the way it does, and by noon his blood had turned traitor, and by four he was gone, and the young doctor — Dr. Pell, who is competent and kind and twenty-six and therefore exhausted — shook his head and said, "We did everything," and we had, and it didn't matter, and that is the whole of military medicine in one sentence.

Fenwick would go into the official log. He was regular army; his papers existed; some clerk in the rear would ink his name into a column with all the ceremony of inking anything. So technically I didn't need to record him. But the official log records *how* he died — *died of wounds received in action* — and that phrase is a locked door. It says nothing about how Fenwick, delirious in his last hour, kept asking whether his boots had been resoled yet, because he'd promised his brother he'd take care of them, and he wanted them in good order for the funeral, whenever somebody got around to having one. That detail would die with me unless I wrote it down. So I wrote it down.

The second was harder. A woman named Ada Sorn, who had come to us ten days ago from a farmstead east of the ridge, brought in by a neighbor's cart. Burns, bad ones, from a fire whose origin she never explained and I never pressed, because in the ninth year of a border war, a burned farmhouse explains itself. She was not a soldier. She was not, officially, anything — not a combatant, not a refugee with papers, not a resident of any village the army recognized. She was a woman lying in a bed in a field hospital, which made her, administratively speaking, a rounding error.

She died at midnight. Quietly, between one breath and the next, while I was changing the dressing on the man beside her. I sat with her afterward for a few minutes because there was no one else to do it, and I noticed her hands — work-ruined, knuckles like walnuts, a wedding band worn nearly to wire. Someone had loved those hands once, or been loved by them, or both. Her papers, if she'd ever had any, had burned with everything else. To the war office, Ada Sorn had never existed, and now she had stopped, and the sum total of her passage through the world would be a burn scar on a hillside and whatever I could fit into two lines of ink.

*Ada Sorn, age perhaps fifty. Farmer. Burned east of the ridge, tenth of the month. Held my hand on her third day and said the pain was nothing compared to losing the bees. Asked me to tell whoever came after that the orchard could be saved. I don't know if it can. I'm telling you anyway.*

The third was a boy. There is no kind way to say it and I've stopped looking for one. A boy of maybe fourteen, uniform cut down to fit him, no unit markings, no identification, found at the treeline by our forage party. Delirious from the start. Called for his mother in a language none of us spoke — not ours, not the enemy's standard tongue, something older, hill-country, the kind of speech that maps mark with blank space. He died at dawn without ever surfacing enough to give us a name.

No name. No unit. No country, possibly. The burial detail would dig a hole and the report would say *one unknown male, juvenile,* and that would be the end of it, the whole biography of a human being compressed into four words by a clerk who'd never see the hole.

I sat with the ledger open on my knees for a long time before I wrote anything. Behind the supply tent, the sentry coughed. Somewhere east, the guns were doing their slow nighttime arithmetic of their own. And I thought: somewhere there is a mother — there is always a mother, or there was — who taught this boy whatever language he died speaking, and she does not know yet, and when she stops knowing-not-knowing, there will be no record anywhere on this earth that he existed except the one I was about to make.

So I gave him a name. Not his name — I'm not arrogant enough to think I could guess it — but a placeholder, honestly labeled, because a blank line is a kind of erasure and I have sworn off erasure in all its forms.

*Unknown boy, approx. fourteen years. Hill-country speech, tongue unrecorded. Died at dawn, called for his mother. Buried under the elm behind the ward, third from the fence. Named here "Little Wren" for the sound he made in his sleep. If anyone ever comes asking: he was here. He was brave. He was fourteen, and it was not enough, and we are sorry.*

I blotted the page with sand, closed the book, wrapped it in its oilcloth, and pushed it back down into the kit beneath the spare socks I no longer wear because marching ruined my feet and sentiment keeps the socks company.

Then I went back inside and checked on Ellery Hask, who was awake, feverish, and asking about plum trees.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and smelling of rain that wouldn't fall. I had been awake for thirty hours, which is standard, and I spent it doing the second part of triage, the part that comes after the slate: preparing the stayed to stay.

This is the part they really don't teach you. Anyone can learn to sort the living from the dying. What takes practice is walking up to a man you've marked *stay* and finding something to say that isn't a lie and isn't the truth, because the truth — *you are being left behind to die because the wagon arithmetic doesn't favor you* — is a thing no one should have to hear, and a lie — *you'll be along directly* — is a thing almost no one believes from a nurse's mouth, because nurses are terrible liars. We spend our careers watching faces. It ruins the craft.

Ellery Hask saved me the trouble, in a way. I pulled the stool up beside him and hadn't gotten two words out before he said, "I'm staying, aren't I."

"I—"

"Don't." His eyes were bright with fever but perfectly level. "Don't do the speech. I've heard the speech. Odo told me what the numbers look like." He managed something that was almost a grin. "Just — if you're writing to anybody, my mother's address is on the card in my jacket. Tell her about the plums. Don't tell her the rest."

"The rest being?"

"That I cried. Nurses aren't supposed to notice that stuff."

"Nurses notice everything. It's the job."

"Then lie to her about that one thing."

"I'm a terrible liar," I said. "But for you I'll practice."

He laughed, which cost him, and gripped my wrist with a strength that surprised us both, and I stayed until the grip loosened and he slept, and then I went and did it again with the next one, and the next, thirty-one minus twelve conversations, each one a small surgery performed on living people with no anesthetic except time, and I had none.

By full light the evacuation wagons were audible on the north road, and Vess appeared at my elbow with the patient list and asked, in the careful voice of a man handling something live, "Who's going?"

"Read the slate."

He read it. His jaw worked. "Twelve litters," he said, as though I might have miscounted.

"Twelve litters."

"And the rest of us? Staff?"

"You're on the list. Second litter. You'll drive the point about coffee quality all the way to base hospital."

"And you?"

I looked at the ward — at the stayed, at the morphine doses measured out in careful rows, at the water basins filled and the bandages stacked, everything arranged so that whoever inherited this tent, friend or enemy, would find thirty-one minus however-many-we-couldn't-save human beings treated with the only dignity available, which is competence plus attention.

"I'm last litter," I said. "Somebody has to sign the place over."

Vess nodded and didn't argue, which is why I tolerate him. Then he hesitated, and lowered his voice, and asked the question that turned the whole gray morning sideways:

"Dunmore — the dispatch. It says we're falling back to Kell Bridge. Through the low country." He glanced toward the door, toward the east, toward the hills. "You know what villages are out that way?"

I knew exactly what villages were out that way. I knew one village in particular, though I hadn't let myself think its name in three years, and I felt it rise in me now like water finding a crack.

"Why?" I said.

"Because the census people are coming through," Vess said. "Government seal on the advance notice. Counting heads for rations and repatriation. They'll want to interview the evacuating staff." He shrugged. "They ask questions, apparently. Lots of them."

I thought of the ledger in the bottom of my kit, four hundred and six names deep, and of a promise I made in peacetime to a pair of healer's hands, and of a village that the war had been very carefully not mentioning.

"Then I suppose," I said, "we'd better count carefully."
