## 1. The Weight of Small Gears

The ferry into Verrance cost me my last silver coin and most of my dignity, because the crossing was rough enough that a fisherman's wife held my hair back while I contributed to the harbor's ecology. I mention this not for sympathy but for the record: I arrived in the city that was about to change my life smelling of herring and failure, carrying one toolbox, one carpetbag, and the particular pride of a person who has been summoned.

Summoned. That was the word on the letter, in ink so fine it might have been applied with a pin. *The Observatory requires the services of Mlle. Delacroix.* Not requested. Required. The Guild had sent three senior apprentices to Verrance over the past year — men with grey in their beards and certificates in walnut frames — and each had come home muttering that the master clock there was beyond anyone. Beyond anyone, apparently, except the invisible woman who repaired church tower clocks in towns so small they didn't appear on maps made by people who mattered.

I knew what it meant when someone finally sees you after years of looking straight through you. It never means anything good. Nobody summons an invisible person out of admiration. They summon you because the visible people have failed, and failures need somewhere to land. Still, I read the letter eleven times on the ferry, and eleven times my chest did the same small treacherous thing, which I believe is called hope.

The toolbox weighed more than the carpetbag. It always does. A toolbox is a promise you make to yourself about the future: that things can be fixed, that broken is a temporary condition, that your hands are worth something even when your name isn't. My father built it for me before I could reach its latch without standing on a stool. Ash wood, brass corners worn to the color of old honey, and inside, nested like eggs, forty years of accumulated certainty — files, pliers, pin vises, a loupe cracked down one side that I refused to replace because it still showed me everything I needed to see.

Verrance rose out the morning haze the way cities do when they want to impress you: white stone, green copper spires, the observatory dome sitting on its hill like a fat man at the head of a table. Gulls screamed their opinions. Soldiers stood at the quay in pairs, checking papers with the bored efficiency of men who have checked ten thousand papers and will check ten thousand more before anything interesting happens. War was coming. Everyone said so the way you say rain is coming — not as news but as weather, something to be endured rather than prevented. Crates were being stacked on the docks with stenciled numbers. A woman argued with a porter about the weight of a trunk that clearly contained her entire house.

None of it concerned me. I had a clock to fix and a reputation to build, and I confess that walking up from the harbor, I thought mostly about the second thing. Let them watch, I told myself. Let the Guild write letters about Mlle. Delacroix that don't begin with an apology for her sex or end with a request that she work quietly in the back room. Let my mother hear, wherever she was rotting, that the daughter she sold had become the kind of person who gets summoned.

Ah. You caught that. *Sold.* We'll come to it. Or we won't; some ledgers stay shut.

* * *

The road to the observatory climbed through streets that got quieter and better-dressed the higher I went, until the houses stopped being houses and became statements — villas with iron gates, gardens pruned into obedience. At the top, behind a fence whose gate stood open, the observatory squatted under its dome with the self-importance of all institutions. A brass plaque announced its dedication to *the exact measure of time for the benefit of navigation and the common good*, which is the sort of sentence institutions write when they want to be forgiven for something.

The door was oak, banded with iron, and bore a knocker shaped like a sun. I raised my hand to it.

And then I saw the child.

She sat on the step, tucked into the corner where the door met the wall, so still that at first I took her for a bundle of laundry someone had left. A girl, six years old perhaps, maybe seven — small for either. Dark hair cut short and unevenly, the way hair gets cut by someone in a hurry with scissors meant for cloth. She wore a coat too big for her, buttoned wrong, and shoes that had been good once, in another family's life. Her hands were folded in her lap around a book bound in grey leather, and she gripped it the way drowning people grip driftwood: not holding it so much as refusing to let go.

Her eyes came up to mine. Grey eyes, very wide, very calm, and underneath the calm something running like water under ice. She didn't speak. She didn't cry. She looked at me with the terrible patience of a child who has already learned that crying changes nothing, and I felt that look go through me like an awl through leather.

"Hello," I said, because one says something.

Nothing. Not mute, I decided — watching. There's a difference. A mute child looks away when spoken to; this one held my gaze with an attention that made me feel audited.

I looked up and down the street. Empty. The villa across the way had its shutters closed against whatever was coming. Somewhere below, the city went on with its packing and arguing, oblivious. No mother stepping out of a shop. No father rounding a corner. Just me, a child, and a door I'd been summoned to knock on.

There was a note pinned to her coat. I should say here that I considered not reading it. I want that on the record too, because later — when everything happened, when the names and the ships and the choices came due — people would ask why I involved myself, and the honest answer is that involvement began with a note pinned to a stranger's child, and I could have walked away. I nearly did. Twice, actually. Keep count.

The note was written in a hand I didn't recognize — quick, slanted, the writing of someone educated but hurried:

*To the clockmaker — Repair the master clock. Forget the face. Burn this.*

No signature. No explanation. Just instructions, as if I were a servant and the world my employer.

"Forget the face," I said aloud, and the child blinked slowly, once, like a cat deciding I was tolerable.

Here is what I know about abandonment, and I know it professionally, the way a surgeon knows anatomy: it doesn't look like drama. It looks like logistics. Someone sets you down somewhere sensible — a church step, a railway platform, an observatory door — somewhere with foot traffic, somewhere you'll be found. They choose your coat carefully, because coats are love you can leave behind. And then they walk away at an ordinary pace, because running attracts notice, and the whole art of leaving a child is to make it look like nothing is happening. I was set down outside a Guild chapter house in Brasey when I was four, wearing a good coat, holding a tin whistle. I remember the whistle. I do not remember the face. Whoever left me followed the instructions I now held in my hand, decades early and addressed to someone else.

So yes, I recognized the arrangement. Recognition is not the same as obligation. I told myself that standing there with the note going soft in my grip. I am a clockmaker, I told myself. I fix mechanisms. Children are not mechanisms. Whatever machinery had deposited this girl on this step belonged to other trades entirely — magistrates, priests, the kind of people who deal in fates — and interfering with other trades' machinery was how invisible women got made visible in the bad way, the way that ends with questions asked by soldiers.

I turned toward the gate. I got three steps. Maybe four.

* * *

Let me tell you about the second time I almost walked away, since we're keeping count. It was after I'd knocked — the knocker fell with a sound like a coin dropped in a well — and waited, and knocked again, and understood that no one was coming. The observatory was empty. Not merely unattended; empty, in the way a body is empty after the breath goes out of it. Dust sheets over the instruments visible through ground-floor windows. A caretaker's lodge with cold chimneys. The great dome silent above it all, and beneath the silence, if you pressed your ear to the oak, a silence of a different texture — the silence a building makes when its heart has stopped.

Because that's what a master clock is. I don't care what the astronomers call it — regulator, standard, the Great Timekeeper of Verrance — a master clock is a heart. Every clock in the city takes its beat from it: the harbor bells, the customs house, the train schedules, the courts, the ships' chronometers checked against it before long voyages. When it stops, the city doesn't know immediately. Time keeps seeming to pass. But every clock begins to disagree with every other clock, quietly, politely, the way people do when the authority they all pretended to respect turns out to be dead. And everyone keeps pretending, because the alternative is admitting that nobody is keeping the time anymore.

I tried the side door. Locked. I tried it again with a pick from my toolbox, because I had been summoned and summoning implies entry, and because the letter said *required*, and required is a word with teeth. Inside: dust, cold stone, the smell of old oil and older stone, and stairs winding up toward the clock room like the inside of a shell.

The master clock of Verrance stood three times my height, a black walnut case with a glass door, brass works visible within like the organs of some patient animal. And there it was, the impossible thing, the reason three senior apprentices had gone home defeated and a fourth had been summoned with a word like *required*:

The clock had stopped at 4:17 and 11 seconds.

Now, clocks stop at all hours; that's nothing. What's impossible is *where*. The minute hand stood at seventeen minutes past, the second hand at eleven seconds — and the hour hand, which should have lain between four and five, pointed instead precisely at twelve. Not near twelve. Precisely. As if the hour wheel had traveled alone, against the drag of the entire train, to arrive at an exact position no falling clock reaches by accident. Gears don't choose. Escapements don't deliberate. Something had stopped this clock deliberately, at a specific moment, and arranged the hands like a signature — or a message.

Three apprentices had looked at that and seen a mechanical fault. I looked at it and saw intent, and intent frightened me more than any fault, because faults yield to skill and intent yields only to understanding, and I did not yet understand.

I also saw, with the cold professional pleasure that cuts through fear like a file through soft brass, exactly what had been done. The escapement had been tampered with — not broken, *altered*, a modification so elegant that a lesser eye would call it damage. Someone had known this clock intimately. Someone had loved it, in the way that lets you hurt it well.

"Well," I said to the empty room, because talking to yourself is free and the acoustics were excellent. "Aren't you interesting."

Which was when I remembered the child on the step, and the note in my pocket, and the instruction to forget a face I hadn't yet managed to stop seeing.

* * *

I went back down intending, I told myself, only to move her somewhere defensible. The caretaker's lodge had a bench. Cities have authorities. There are, I assured myself, systems for children found on steps — I had been one such child, and a system had produced me, more or less functional, so the machine worked, didn't it? Hand her off, climb back upstairs, lose myself in gears where problems have solutions and solutions have torque specifications.

She hadn't moved. Same position, same grip on the grey book, same grey eyes lifting to mine with that awful patience. The afternoon had thickened toward evening; the light on the hill had gone the color of weak tea. Down in the city, lamps were being lit one by one, and I noticed — the way you notice a wrong note in music — that the lamplighter was working from a pocket watch he kept consulting with increasing irritation, as though it had contradicted him.

"You're hungry," I said. It wasn't a question. Children found on steps are always hungry; hunger is the first thing abandonment installs and the last thing it removes.

Nothing. But her stomach answered for her, a small treacherous sound, and something in her face flickered — shame, quickly mastered, mastered too well for a six-year-old, which tells you how much practice she'd had.

I had half a loaf and a hard-boiled egg wrapped in my carpetbag, ferry provisions, the egg slightly crushed. I sat down on the step beside her — not too close; abandoned children measure distance the way sailors measure depth — and unwrapped them. I ate half my half and set the rest on the stone between us, and looked at the observatory dome as if bread appearing next to strangers were the most ordinary event in Verrance.

After a long time — long enough that the lamplighter had moved two streets down and given up consulting his watch — a small hand took the bread.

She ate the way she did everything: precisely, completely, wasting nothing, including the crumbs, which she gathered from her palm with one finger. Then she looked at me again, and this time she spoke. Her voice was rusty, a hinge unused.

"It stopped," she said, nodding at the observatory, at the hill, at the whole silent weight of the building above us. "At night. I heard it."

"Heard what?"

"The clock." She frowned, searching for words, a child translating from some private language. "It makes a sound. All the time. Under everything. Like — " her small hands turned over, palms up, helpless before the size of what she meant. "Like the floor of everything. And that night it stopped. And then Mama put me here."

The word *mama* went through me sideways, under the ribs, where armor doesn't reach. I kept my voice level, which cost something.

"Your mama left you here?"

"She said to wait." The grey eyes held mine, steady as a plumb line. "She said someone would come who fixes things." A pause, and then, with the terrible literalism of children: "Are you the one who fixes things?"

"I'm a clockmaker," I said.

"No." She shook her head slowly, correcting me, and pulled the grey book tighter against her chest. "You're the one who was written down."

I should have asked what she meant. Any sensible person would have asked. Instead I sat on that cold step in the failing light, listening to a city tick unevenly below us like a watch running down, and I looked at this child who had heard the heartbeat of Verrance stop, and I felt the trap close around me with the smooth precision of a well-made mechanism — every part fitted, every tooth engaged, no play anywhere, and no way to back the whole assembly out again.

Above us, behind the oak door, the master clock stood at its impossible hour, waiting. In her arms, the grey book waited. And somewhere in the darkening streets below, whoever had written me down was counting on both.

"Come inside," I said, which was, though I didn't know it yet, the first entry in a ledger of my own.
