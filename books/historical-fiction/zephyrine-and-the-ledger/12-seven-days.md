## 12. Seven Days

I have been a sergeant of the Verrance harbor watch for nineteen years, and in that time I have learned that the sea keeps no records and men keep nothing else. Every plank of my job is paper. Manifests, berths, tonnage, names. When the war came down on us like weather, I assumed the paperwork would drown first and we would all be free.

I was wrong. The paperwork got worse. It always gets worse.

Seven days before the *Meridian* was scheduled to sail — seven days by the harbor bell, which was itself slaved to the observatory clock, which had been dead since before the proclamation — I was sent to the observatory to find out why the master clock was still stopped, and whether the man responsible could be shot.

The shooting part was a joke. Mostly. Undersecretary Vane made it with the particular flatness he reserved for things that were not jokes at all.

"Time is the schedule," Vane said, tapping the sailing order with one manicured finger. "The schedule is the ship. The ship is eleven hundred souls. If the clock is wrong, the manifests are wrong, and if the manifests are wrong, Sergeant, then everything you've stamped for three weeks is worth the ink it's printed in."

"And if the clockmaker can't fix it?"

"Then find out what's actually broken." He looked at me over his spectacles the way a man looks at a tool that has begun to rust. "Because something is always actually broken, Halloran, and it is rarely the mechanism."

That was the truest thing anyone said to me that week, though I didn't know it yet.

* * *

The observatory sat on the hill above the harbor like a stone eye that had decided, sometime in the past months, to close. Its dome was furred with gull droppings and its steps were swept clean — swept, I noted, not abandoned — and at the top of them stood a woman with a toolbox in one hand and an expression that suggested she had been expecting me for days and had prepared several disappointments in advance.

"Sergeant Halloran," I said. "Harbor office."

"I know who you are." She didn't move from the doorway. "You stamped the ration chits for the fishmongers' row. You denied old Perrot twice."

"He was claiming rations for a wife who died in '09."

"He was claiming them because he still sets two places at the table," she said, "and I'd have done the same. But you denied him politely, which is worse than denying him rudely, so I know your handwriting."

This was Ada Delacroix, then. The clockmaker summoned from God knew where over the heads of every senior apprentice in the guild. I had read her file, or what passed for one: a name, a trade license, a father struck from the guild rolls twenty years back, a mother listed under a different parish than either of them. A file like a locked room with the furniture missing.

"The clock," I said. "Undersecretary Vane wants to know when it runs."

"Do you want to know when it runs, Sergeant, or do you want to know why it stopped? Because those are different questions, and only one of them has an answer you can put in a report."

"I'm paid for reports."

"You're paid for stamps," she said. "Come inside anyway. It's cold on the step, and the child's asleep, and I'd rather argue where there's tea."

* * *

The interior of the observatory smelled of oil and cold brass and, faintly, of porridge. The great clock dominated the room like a cathedral organ built by someone with a grudge against cathedrals — wheels within wheels, weights hanging silent, the whole apparatus frozen at some impossible hour. I am not a mechanical man. I know a wheel from a spring and that is the full extent of my education. But even I could see that the thing was not broken the way things break. There was no rust, no snapped cord, no scattered wreckage. It looked less like a machine that had failed and more like a machine that had been *interrupted*.

A girl of six slept on a cot in the corner, curled around a folded blanket like a comma. Zephyrine, the woman called her — I'd heard the name from the harbor clerks, half rumor, half joke. The foundling of the observatory. The wind vane child.

"She talks now?" I asked quietly.

"When she wants to. Which is more than most people manage." Delacroix poured tea into two tin cups without asking whether I wanted any, which was its own kind of answer. "You've come about the manifests."

"I've come about the clock."

"No," she said, not unkindly. "You've come about the manifests. The clock is just how they're dressed."

I drank my tea and let her have the point, because she was right, and because I had spent three weeks watching the harbor office fill up with berth claims that stank of fresh ink and older money, and I was tired of pretending I hadn't noticed.

"Three weeks ago," I said, "the evacuation lists were honest. Bad news honestly recorded. Since then I've certified passage for a family named Aubert who own a candle shop and, according to their claim papers, also apparently own a shipping interest in Callais, a vineyard, and four children, two of whom I'm fairly sure are the same child photographed twice. I've certified a widow with six servants listed as dependents. I've certified a man whose signature matches three other men's signatures, all claiming to be him." I set down the cup. "Every one of those documents is timestamped against the observatory clock. Official time. And official time, right now, is whatever I say it is, because the clock is dead and the stamp is in my hand."

"So don't stamp them."

"If I don't stamp them, they get stamped upstairs. Vane's office has ink too. At least mine comes with a conscience attached, even if the conscience mostly files complaints."

Delacroix looked at me for a long moment. Then she did something I did not expect. She went to the workbench, lifted a false bottom out of a drawer — a clockmaker's trick, and a good one — and brought out a ledger.

It was small, bound in grey, sealed once with wax that had been broken carefully rather than torn. She laid it on the bench between us like a card played face-up.

"Read it," she said.

* * *

Three names. That was all. Three lines in a clerk's neat hand, each with a berth number aboard the *Meridian*, each with a date of entitlement that predated the war, predated the proclamation, predated every forged claim currently rotting in my inbox.

*Mireille Delacroix.* Her mother — I learned that later; at the time I only watched the woman's face do something complicated and painful and private while I read the name.

*Tomas Delacroix.* A name I did know. The guild clockmaker. The one who had struck his brother's name from the rolls two decades ago and never spoken of it again, according to everyone who spoke of it constantly.

And a third line, blank beside the name, waiting: the child.

"What is this?" I asked, though I already knew. You always already know. That's the dirty secret of being a decent man in a corrupt system — you're never surprised, you're only ever *confirmed*.

"It's the true ledger," Delacroix said. "Berths aboard the *Meridian*, assigned before the war, by people whose job it was to assign them honestly. The child's parents were clerks. Registry clerks. They kept this book, and books like it, and someone decided the book was worth more than the clerks." She said it flatly, the way you say a thing you've said to yourself many times until it wears smooth. "The child was left at my door with instructions to forget her face. Whoever wrote those instructions thought a clerk's daughter carrying a ledger could pass where a witness couldn't. They were right. Nobody looks twice at a child."

"And you've had this for how long?"

"Since the night I arrived."

"Three weeks. You've had proof of legitimate entitlements for three weeks and you haven't brought it forward."

"To whom?" She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only architecture. "To you? You'd stamp it, and the moment it entered the record it would sit in the same drawer as the Aubert forgeries, and any clerk with a reason to could declare it counterfeit. To Vane? Half the forged claims coming through your office trace back to people who dine at Vane's table. This ledger isn't evidence, Sergeant. Not yet. Evidence needs a clock that tells the truth and a man behind the stamp who does. I've spent three weeks finding out if you were that man."

"And?"

"And you denied old Perrot twice and felt bad about it both times," she said. "So. Possibly."

I should have been angry. A sergeant of nineteen years, sized up and tested by a clockwright with a toolbox and a foundling. Instead what I felt was relief, which frightened me more. Relief, because for three weeks I had been alone in a room full of lying paper, telling myself the system was merely strained, and here was a stranger handing me the truth: the system wasn't strained. It was bought. And she had known, and she had waited, and she had chosen me — me, Halloran, the polite denier — as the best available instrument.

God help Verrance, I was the best available instrument.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic that kept me awake that night, and I set it down plainly because it deserves plain setting:

If the master clock ran, then official time resumed, and every manifest I authenticated would bear a true timestamp — including the forgeries. The ring selling berths needed exactly that. A dead clock was, for them, an inconvenience; a running clock was a laundering machine. My stamp plus true time equaled clean papers for dirty money.

If the master clock stayed stopped, then the sailing schedule dissolved into argument, the *Meridian*'s departure became a matter of influence and queue-jumping, and the people with the least influence — a widow, a disgraced clockmaker's daughter, a six-year-old girl with a grey ledger — would be trampled by the queue itself.

Run the clock and certify the thieves. Stop the clock and surrender to the mob. Those were the choices the system offered, and the system had designed them carefully, because systems always design the choices so that the honest man breaks himself either way.

"There's a third option," Delacroix said, when I laid it out for her the next morning, standing among the silent wheels with the child watching us from her cot with eyes too old for their size.

"Name it."

"You run the clock," she said, "but you don't certify anything until the true ledger is in the record first. Entitlements precede applications. The three names go in before another forgery does. After that—" she shrugged— "stamp what you like. The thieves will still steal, but they'll be stealing second-class berths off honest people instead of first-class ones, and honest people will already be aboard."

"That requires the clock running *and* the ledger filed simultaneously. One doesn't happen without the other."

"Yes," she said. "Exactly. That's called an escapement, Sergeant. Two parts that only move together, each checking the other, so that neither runs away with the time." She put her hand flat on the dead clock's case, almost tenderly. "I've been building one for three weeks. I just needed a second wheel."

She meant me. I understood that. I stood there in the smell of oil and porridge, a man who had spent nineteen years being a cog and had never once been asked to be a wheel, and I heard myself say:

"Vane will want progress by tomorrow. I'll tell him the mainspring's cracked and a replacement must come up from the coast. That buys four days, maybe five."

"That's a lie to an undersecretary."

"It's a report with an optimistic forecast," I said. "There's a difference. It's thin, but it exists, and I intend to stand on it."

For the first time since I'd climbed those swept steps, Ada Delacroix smiled. It changed her face entirely, the way light changes a dial, and I understood suddenly why someone had summoned her above every senior apprentice in the guild — and suspected it had nothing to do with clocks.

* * *

We shook on it, which is a foolish way to seal a conspiracy and the only way available to us. No paper. Paper was the enemy's country now. Just a handshake between a sergeant and a clockmaker, witnessed by a sleeping child and eleven hundred pounds of silent brass.

At the door I paused. "The third name," I said. "The blank line. When the ledger goes into the record, the child goes with it. You understand that once it's official, she's evidence, and evidence gets handled by people who aren't gentle."

"I know what she is," Delacroix said quietly. Behind her, the girl had woken and was watching us, and I saw that she had heard, and that she understood too, in the terrible way children understand the shapes of things adults think they're hiding. "But thank you for saying it aloud. Someone should."

I walked down the hill toward the harbor, past houses with their doors propped open and their contents weighed in piles on the stoops — what could be carried, what could not — and at the bottom I looked back. The observatory stood closed-eyed against the sky, and somewhere inside it a dead clock waited for two conspirators to decide what hour it would tell.

Five days, I had promised Vane. Five days of cracked-spring fiction.

I gave us four. In my experience, everything takes longer than the honest forecast, and everything goes wrong sooner than the dishonest one.

I was, as usual, exactly right, and it helped not at all.
