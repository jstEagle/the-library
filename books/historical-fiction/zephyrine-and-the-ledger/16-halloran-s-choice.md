## 16. Halloran's Choice

There is a particular kind of order that arrives on good paper. You learn to recognize it after twenty years in the harbor office: the cheap requisitions come on the grey stock, the ones nobody will ever audit, but an order that matters — an order someone intends to hide behind later — arrives on cream paper with a wax seal and a signature that has been practiced in front of a mirror.

The one on my desk that morning was cream. It ordered me to certify the manifests of one Tomas Delacroix, broker, "as a matter of urgent civic priority," and it was signed by a deputy commissioner whose name I had seen on three other documents that week, each authorizing something slightly less legal than the last.

I read it twice. Then I did what any sensible man does with an impossible order. I made tea.

* * *

Let me tell you about my career, such as it is. Twenty-two years in the harbor office of Verrance. I have stamped things. That is, if you press me, the sum of my professional achievement: I have stamped things. Bills of lading, grain manifests, death certificates for sailors who drowned within sight of their own front doors, birth certificates for children born in the customs house because their mothers didn't quite make it to the midwife. My stamp has outlived four commissioners, two wars of the small unpleasant kind, and one scandal involving eels that I am legally prevented from discussing.

A small man. I know that. My wife used to say — before she took the girls east to her sister's, before the roads closed — that I was built like a man designed by committee. Useful, unremarkable, unlikely to be noticed. She meant it kindly. Most days I took it kindly.

But here is the thing about being a stamp: everything that passes through you bears your mark. Everything. The honest cargo and the dishonest, the true names and the bought ones, all of it leaves my desk wearing my approval like a borrowed coat. For twenty-two years I told myself that I was merely the instrument, that the hand holding me belonged to the city, and the city was mostly decent, and mostly was a word a man can live on if he doesn't look at it too closely in the lamplight.

This week I have been looking.

* * *

The forgeries started arriving nine days ago, or perhaps they arrived earlier and only got lazy nine days ago. Before the war announcement, the fakes were careful things — a date smudged here, a witness line left blank there, forgery as craft. Now? Now the wealthy of Verrance have stopped pretending. A shipping magnate presented me a manifest claiming his gardener's family as his own dependents, all six of them, including a boy of nineteen who was standing behind him at the counter wearing the magnate's second-best coat, which fit him like a tent on a fencepost.

"Family," the magnate said, not quite meeting my eye.

"Mm," I said, and stamped it, because the alternative was a scene, and scenes in the harbor office this week end with soldiers.

That is the confession I keep circling: I stamped it. And the one after it. And the one after that. Eleven manifests yesterday, and I would wager my pension — a joke, that; they'll void it anyway when the city falls — that eight were purchased rather than earned.

Because here is what nobody outside the office understands about certification: my stamp does not make a thing true. It makes a thing official. Truth and officialdom parted ways in Verrance some years ago and have been living apart ever since, seeing other people.

And now the master clock has stopped, and the sailing time of the Meridian hangs on an hour nobody can verify, and every forged manifest needs a timestamp to be worth anything, and the timestamp comes from me, and the time itself comes from a clockmaker's daughter up at the observatory who keeps telling me, with a straight face I am beginning to admire, that the escapement is "nearly sorted."

Nearly sorted. Nine days of nearly sorted. I am a bureaucrat, not a fool, though the distinction narrows daily.

* * *

She showed me the ledger three nights ago. I have been trying to stop reading it since.

Three names. Mireille Delacroix. Tomas Delacroix. And a blank line beside a child's name — Zephyrine, Ada calls her, after the wind vane on the observatory roof, because the girl wouldn't speak hers. A child of six, abandoned at a door with instructions to forget her face, carrying in her arms the only honest document left in this city.

Her parents were clerks. They kept the true ledger — the real entitlements, the ones issued before the money got involved — and they were silenced for it, and their daughter was sent out the back door of history carrying the evidence, because a clerk's daughter could pass where a witness could not.

I sat with that ledger in my hands for a long while after Ada went back to her clock. I thought about my stamp. I thought about the boy in the tent-coat. I thought about my wife's word for me — committee-built — and how committees, when they want to avoid responsibility, issue minutes. Records. They write down what was decided so that no single person has to have decided it.

Well. Here are my minutes.

* * *

The order came again this morning, hand-delivered by a runner who didn't wait for a receipt. Certify Delacroix's manifests. All of them. Urgent civic priority. The wax seal was fresh; the deputy commissioner signs perhaps forty documents a day now, and I'd bet half his signatures are applied by clerks, which means somewhere below him there is another small man applying his name to things, and so it goes down the chain until it reaches a stamp like me.

I laid the manifests out on my desk. Fourteen of them. I read every line, because if I was going to do what I suspected I was going to do, I wanted to know exactly what I was refusing.

They were beautiful work, I'll grant them that. Uncle Tomas — and isn't that a bitter little comedy, that the man buying Verrance's escape wholesale shares blood with the woman keeping its only honest book — Uncle Tomas had acquired families the way other men acquire wine: a widow here with two grown sons who died at sea years ago, resurrected for the crossing; a merchant house whose entire staff of eleven had been, per the manifest, "retained dependents"; a gentleman whose berth was justified by a naval commission dated four days from today. He had even forged the future. That takes a certain ambition.

And threaded through them, here and there, like wire through rotten fruit: names that might be real. A fisherman. A laundress. Two or three genuine entitlements buried among the bought ones, because the cleverest forgeries always carry a little truth — it gives the whole document a pulse.

If I certified these, the true names would sail wrapped in the false ones, laundered clean by association. And Zephyrine's blank line — the child's only claim — would stay blank, because the ledger she carried was the one document in Verrance that Tomas needed to disappear, and he had already taken it once. Ada told me that too, white-faced, five days ago. Stolen from under her own roof. The rules themselves stolen.

So. The choice, laid out on cream paper:

Certify, keep my post, keep my pension-that-won't-exist, keep my clean record of twenty-two years of stamping whatever came across the desk, and send fourteen lies across the water wearing my mark.

Or refuse, and be refused right back — overridden, replaced, possibly arrested, certainly ruined — and watch the same manifests get stamped by whoever sits down at this desk tomorrow, because the machine does not require me specifically. The machine requires a stamp. Any stamp.

There was a third option. It had been sitting in the back of my skull since the night Ada showed me the ledger, patient as a cat.

* * *

Here is what I know that the machine does not.

I have spent twenty-two years reading manifests, and a clerk reads them the way a priest reads scripture: daily, compulsively, until the patterns live in the body. The true ledger — the original entitlements, issued before the ring began selling berths — passed through this office in its day. I stamped its pages. I remember them.

Not perfectly. Let me be honest, since honesty is apparently the theme of my late career: I remember perhaps eighty percent, and of that, I am certain of sixty. Names, quarter-sections, the serial marks on the entitlement chits. The fisherman, Aldous Renne, berth 14, chit series K. The laundress, Odile Ferrant, berth 31. The widow Marchetti and her grandson, berths 22 and 23, issued jointly, witnessed by a customs officer named Brellier whom I drank with once and found tedious but honest. Page by page, entry by entry, the shape of the true arithmetic of who was owed passage on the Meridian.

It is not evidence. I know that too. Memory copied into an official record by the very officer accused of negligence — no court in the world would take it. But courts are for afterward, and afterward may never come, and meanwhile there is a ship, and a schedule, and a harbor full of people checking their papers against the clock tower, and if the true ledger exists anywhere in the official record — even as a marginal annotation, even as an appendix in a tired sergeant's handwriting — then it exists. It can be found. It can be checked against. When Halloran's record is finally opened, and records do get opened, eventually, even in burned cities — someone will find the true names sitting inside the official book like a coin baked into a loaf.

You cannot forge over a thing that is already written. Not cleanly. Not without leaving the scar visible.

That is the whole of my plan. It is not much of a plan. It is, if I'm truthful, barely a gesture. But I have watched gestures matter this week — a clockmaker's daughter withholding one wheel, a child carrying a book through a burning district — and I have concluded that the age of grand action is over and the age of small incorruptible acts has begun, and somebody has to open the batting.

* * *

I waited until the office emptied. The junior clerks left at dusk, hurrying home to pack what can be carried — everyone in Verrance is weighing objects against memories now; you see them in the street holding a lamp in one hand and a photograph in the other, doing arithmetic. Old Pellar stayed latest, as always, sweeping. I told him to go on. He looked at me a moment — Pellar has swept this floor longer than I've stamped it, and he misses nothing — and said, "Mind the ink, Sergeant. It's been behaving strangely all week." Which is either superstition or a blessing. With Pellar it's usually both.

Then I unlocked the drawer where we keep the annexation folios — the dead files, the ones nobody audits because nobody expects the city to need its past anymore — and I took out a fresh manifest register, the official kind, cream paper, pre-ruled, stamped with the harbor office crest on every page.

And I began to copy.

I wrote in my best hand, the one I use for anything that might outlive me: *Appendix to Manifest Certification, Meridian, final sailing. Recorded from memory by Sgt. E. Halloran, certifying officer, this date, under protest of the orders herein.* Protest noted. Good. Let the protest stand in the record beside the corruption; let future readers see that the machine ground on and that one gear inside it knew what it was grinding.

*Aldous Renne, fisherman, Canal District. Berth 14. Chit series K, issued 11th of the Flood Month, witnessed Brellier.*

My pen shook on the first name and steadied after. Isn't that always the way. The beginning is the cliff; after that you're just falling in formation.

*Odile Ferrant, laundress, Salt Lane. Berth 31.*

The lamp guttered once and I fed it oil with hands that smelled of ink and tea, and I kept going. The widow Marchetti. The cooper Havel, whose chit I remembered because he'd argued about it in this very office, loudly, for an hour, wanting to trade his berth for his brother's, and been refused, and gone away cursing me — hello, cooper, wherever you are; your berth is safe in the book now, curse me all you like. Entry after entry. Sixty-one names I could swear to. Another dozen I set down marked *probable,* because a record that claims more certainty than it owns is just another forgery, and I had come this far precisely to stop making those.

Somewhere past midnight I wrote the last entry, and beneath it, because the moment demanded it and no one was watching:

*The above constitutes the true ledger of entitlements aboard the Meridian as originally issued. Any manifest bearing names inconsistent with this appendix should be examined accordingly. The child who carried the original is owed the blank line beside her name. — E.H.*

Then I signed it, and stamped it — my stamp, my twenty-two-year-old stamp, pressed into cream paper one more time, harder than I have ever pressed it, hard enough that the impression bit through to the blotter — and closed the register, and locked it in the annexation drawer between a dead grain dispute and a drowned sailor's effects inventory, where it will sit looking exactly as boring as everything around it.

Boring. The great camouflage. Nobody audits boredom.

* * *

At dawn I certified fourteen forged manifests.

I want that understood. I want it in my minutes. I sat at my desk when the office opened, and I called the names, and I stamped every lie Tomas Delacroix put in front of me, and I wished each bearer a pleasant crossing, and the boy in the tent-coat grinned at me like I'd given him a present, and maybe I had — the present of another day in which the record still had room in it for the truth.

Because that is the trick of it, the small ugly genius of my third option: refusal changes nothing. The machine replaces you by lunchtime. But compliance with the letter and sabotage in the margins — that the machine cannot see. I have certified the forgeries into the light, and buried the truth in the archive, and when the reckoning comes — the soldiers, the tribunals, the historians, whoever inherits this mess — the two documents will meet, and the forgeries will crack against the true ledger like bad porcelain against stone.

It will cost me everything. I did the arithmetic while the ink dried. Career: ended the moment anyone reads the appendix. Pension: already a joke. Liberty: doubtful. There are men in this city this week who have been shot for less than falsifying harbor records, and men who have been decorated for more. The difference between decoration and bullet, as far as I can determine, is which side writes the history, and neither side has shown up yet.

My wife would say I should have kept my head down. My wife, God love her, is practical in the way of women who have carried families through lean years: head down, hands busy, survive. And if she were here I might have managed it another week. But she is east with the girls, behind closed roads, and a man alone in an emptying city discovers that his conscience has been waiting politely for the house to clear, the way debts do.

Twenty-two years of stamping things. One entry that stamps back.

* * *

I delivered the certified manifests to Delacroix's agent myself, before noon, because I wanted to look the operation in the face. The agent — a smooth young man with excellent boots — thanked me with a warmth that made my skin crawl, and mentioned, in passing, that Mr. Delacroix hoped to call on me personally before sailing. To express his gratitude.

"He needn't," I said.

"He insists." The smile widened a fraction. "He says men of your... reliability... are rare, and ought to be rewarded."

Reliability. Yes. I am reliable. Ask anyone. Ask the annexation drawer.

I walked back along the quay through a city packing itself into crates, past the queue for the ration office, past a wind vane turning lazily on some church spire — pointing seaward, as it happened, though I chose not to read omens into it — and I felt lighter than I have felt in twenty-two years, which is either grace or the specific giddiness of a man who has finally jumped and hasn't yet looked down.

One thing remains, and it is the thing that frightens me more than tribunals: the observatory. Because the appendix in my drawer is only words, and words need a time to attach themselves to, and the master clock still stands stopped at its impossible hour while its keeper tells me the escapement is nearly sorted. Nearly sorted, nine days running. Either she cannot finish it, or she will not, and I have begun to suspect — God help me, I have begun to hope — that Ada Delacroix is committing the same crime I am, in her own dialect, with gears instead of ink.

Two keepers of time, deciding which time matters.

Tonight I climb the hill and find out which side of it she stands on. And if she asks why a stamp like me has walked all this way in the dark, I intend to tell her the truth, plainly, for the first time in my career:

The record is ready. All it needs now is an hour to happen in.
