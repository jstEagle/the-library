## 18. The Master Clock Runs

*Ada*

I had not heard the master clock strike since I was a girl, and even then only from the far side of the harbor, where it arrived thinned by distance into something almost gentle. Up close it was not gentle at all. It was a sound that went through the floorboards, through the soles of my boots, through the bones of my feet, as though the observatory itself had cleared its throat after months of silence and found it still remembered how to speak.

I stood beneath the great dial with my hand on the case, feeling the strike travel up my arm like a pulse taken at the wrist. Twelve strokes. True ones. Each separated from its neighbor by exactly the interval Halloran's chronometer would have demanded, if Halloran had been there to demand anything. He was not. He was down at the harbor office doing whatever a decent man does on the morning he has decided to ruin himself, and I was here, in the tower, listening to my own masterpiece tell the city what time it was.

Zephyrine stood beside me, her hand tucked into mine without either of us having arranged it. She had stopped flinching from the strikes somewhere around the fourth one. Now she counted them under her breath — she could not help counting; she was her parents' daughter even if she did not yet know how much — and when the twelfth stroke faded into the rafters she looked up at me.

"It sounds different," she said. "Than before."

"Before it was lying," I said. "Now it isn't."

She considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Lying clocks still tick."

"Yes," I said. "That's the trouble with them. They're very convincing right up until they aren't."

Below us, Verrance began to move.

I will describe it as honestly as I can, because honesty is the only currency I have left that anyone might accept: it was beautiful, and I hated myself for finding it beautiful. The city moved to the clock's heartbeat the way water moves to a slope — carts first, then the long lines of families with their bundles, then the strange secondary traffic of people carrying other people's things for pay, then the soldiers directing all of it toward the quays with the weary courtesy of men who know they will be last aboard or not aboard at all. From the tower window the whole procession looked orderly. It looked like a city that had agreed on something.

It had agreed on nothing. It had agreed to obey a clock, and the clock was telling the truth now, but the truth was only a schedule, and the schedule was only a ship, and the ship had berths enough for perhaps one soul in five. Order is just arithmetic wearing its good coat. I had learned that from a ledger sealed with wax, and I had learned it again from a man who called himself my uncle, and I was about to learn it a third time at the foot of a gangway.

Zephyrine tugged my sleeve. "Are we going today?"

"We're going today."

"To the ship."

"To the ship."

"And you'll carry the toolbox?"

"I'll carry the toolbox." I crouched to her height. "And you'll carry nothing, because everything you need to carry, you've been carrying since the night your mother put the ledger in your hands, and you've carried it beautifully, and today you get to set it down."

She frowned. "You said we'd give it to Sergeant Halloran."

"I said we'd give it to someone who'd keep it safe. That's better than setting it down."

"That's not what you said."

"No," I admitted. "It isn't. But I've had two days to think about it, and thinking changes what people say. You'll learn that. Try to learn it slower than I did."

She took my hand again, tighter this time, and we went down out of the tower together, past the escapement ticking its honest tick, past the wheel I had traded away and the space where it used to live — a gap in the works that no one would ever see but me, and I would see it every day for the rest of my life, which I supposed was the price of craftsmanship: you live inside your own compromises the way a snail lives in its shell, and both are heavier than they look.

The streets were loud with departure. A woman argued with a porter over a trunk; a boy sold rope by the armful at triple the usual price and met no objection; an old man sat on his doorstep with his hands open in his lap, going nowhere, having decided it some time ago. Nobody looked at the observatory. Everybody looked at the harbor. The clock had struck, and the clock was law, and the law said eleven o'clock sailing, and it was half past nine, and half past nine meant *hurry*, and hurry forgives everything except being slow.

At the corner of Quay Street, Mireille Delacroix was waiting.

* * *

*Halloran*

I had certified forty-one manifests by ten o'clock, and every one of them was a small crime wearing my signature.

That is not quite fair. Some were honest. The Delacroix woman's papers were honest — I checked them twice, because checking them twice let me postpone the thing I actually intended to do with my morning, which was copy a dead ledger into a living record while my commanding officer was at breakfast. The widow Anselme and her three grandchildren: honest. The fishmongers Ollivier: honest, though their claim rested on a grandfather's naval service that the navy had never heard of, and I stamped it anyway, because the alternative was putting children off a ship, and I am many kinds of coward but not that one.

The rest — the silk merchant with his "clerkship in the harbor archive," the banker's cousin with his "medical exemption," the gentleman whose family name appeared on four separate manifests under four separate spellings — those were Tomas Delacroix's work, laundered through his niece's certification of the master clock, and every stamp of my stamp made me complicit in a scheme I had promised myself, two days ago, at a workbench in an observatory, that I would end.

Promises are easy at workbenches. Workbenches don't have Lieutenant Prévost standing behind you asking why the manifest queue is moving so slowly.

"Halloran." He dropped a folder on my desk. "Delacroix party, boarding priority. Uncle, niece, and—" he squinted "—a ward? Since when does a clockmaker have a ward?"

"Since paperwork," I said.

Prévost laughed, because it was a joke, and because everyone in Verrance had decided that jokes were the last free commodity and were spending them recklessly. "Get it processed. And Halloran — the harbor master wants confirmation the sailing time holds. The clock's running again?"

"The clock's running."

"Good. Nothing worse than a deadline nobody believes in." He clapped my shoulder and moved on down the row of desks, spreading cheer like a man salting a field.

I looked at the folder. *Delacroix party.* Three names. One of them was a lie — the ward line, where Zephyrine's name should have been, held instead the name of a child who had died of fever in the spring, a child whose berth Tomas had purchased from grieving parents too numb to haggle. I knew this because I had spent last night copying the true ledger from memory into the back pages of my own duty journal, in a hand disguised as my own worse handwriting, and the true ledger had a blank line beside a girl called Zephyrine, and blank lines do not board ships.

My plan, such as it was, lived in the bottom drawer of my desk under a false bottom I had built myself from scrap pine, in the hour before dawn, with tools borrowed from the observatory. Inside it: the copied ledger. Forty-one manifests' worth of corroborating dates. And a letter to the naval provost naming Tomas Delacroix as middleman to the document ring, timed — I hoped, I prayed — to arrive after the Meridian sailed, so that the arrests would happen on the pier and the innocent passengers would be gone and only the guilty would be left holding their beautiful paper.

It was a bad plan. It relied on the mail, on Prévost's laziness, on the provost's integrity, and on me not losing my nerve between now and noon. I have served in three postings and survived two wars' worth of boredom, and I can report that the most dangerous substance in any army is a plan that depends on several strangers being decent at the same moment.

But the clock was running, and the clock was true, and Ada Delacroix had told me — that night in the tower, her hand flat on the case as if taking the machine's confession — that a true clock doesn't fix anything. It just makes the lying visible. Everyone in Verrance now shared one honest hour, and against that hour, every forged document would eventually show its seams.

Eventually. The word of cowards and clerks. I stamped the Delacroix manifest, filed it, and went to find out whether *eventually* had decided to show up early.

* * *

*Ada*

"You came," Mireille said.

It was not a greeting. It was an accusation shaped like relief. She stood at the head of the quay with a single valise — my mother, who had once owned a house with a piano in it, traveling with one valise — and she looked at me the way creditors look at debtors, which is to say lovingly, in the way that money is loved.

"I came," I said.

"You repaired it. I heard it strike. The whole street heard it strike." Her chin lifted, and for a moment she was twenty years younger and telling the neighbors about her clever daughter. "They'll say a Delacroix kept the time of Verrance. Not that they'll say my name. They never say my name."

"They'll say yours now. It's on a manifest."

"On *my* manifest," she corrected, "which exists because I claimed what I was owed. Don't look at me like that, Ada. I fed you for six years on what was left after your father's family finished with us. I sold your apprenticeship because the alternative was selling the piano, and the piano was already sold, and after the piano there was nothing left to sell but you. I chose the version of ruin where you lived. Every mother in that queue made the same arithmetic. Mine simply showed its work."

There it was. Thirty years compressed into a speech she had clearly rehearsed on the walk down, polished like silver for the occasion. And the terrible thing — the thing I will not pretend I didn't feel — was that some cold clerkish part of me checked her figures and found them accurate. She had chosen. The choosing had been monstrous. The arithmetic had been sound. Both things were true, the way a clock can run and lie.

"Uncle Tomas is boarding," I said. "He'll want to be seen with you. Family looks legitimate."

"He'll want to be seen with the *name*. I am the name. He spent forty years pretending we weren't related, and now that a war has made names valuable, he's discovered his affection." She smiled, and it was the smile I remembered from ration queues, the smile that meant she had already won the argument in her head and was merely waiting for reality to file the paperwork. "Where is the child?"

The question landed like a dropped wrench.

"What child?" I said, and hated how easily the lie came, how much I sounded like everyone else in this city.

"The one the ledger named. The one you've been hiding in the observatory for two weeks like a cat you're not allowed to keep. Tomas talks, Ada. He talked to me across a dinner table for thirty years without ever once saying anything, and somehow I always knew everything anyway." She stepped closer. Her voice dropped to the register she had used on landlords and bailiffs, the one that cost her something every time she used it and she used it anyway. "Is she on the manifest?"

"She's on the ledger."

"The ledger is paper. The manifest is a berth. Which does she have?"

I looked at my mother — at the valise, at the queue forming behind her, at the gangway of the Meridian rising gray and patient against the morning fog — and I understood that she had asked me the same question her own mother must have answered about me, decades ago, in some office like this office, with some stranger's pen hovering over some line. *Which does she have?* And the answer, then, had been: neither. The answer had been: she has you, until she doesn't.

"She has me," I said.

Mireille's face did something complicated and brief, like weather over water, and then it closed. "Then you've made the same mistake I did. Loving something is not a legal instrument, Ada. When they call the names, love won't answer."

"They're calling the names now," I said, because they were — a naval lieutenant with a speaking trumpet, reading out boarding parties in a voice flattened by repetition — and I turned toward the gangway before she could see what the sound did to me.

* * *

*Halloran*

Tomas Delacroix boarded the Meridian at 10:42 by the master clock, and I watched him do it, and I let him.

He climbed the gangway like a man ascending a podium. He had dressed for history — good coat, good hat, a cane he did not need — and he paused at the top to look back at the city he had helped strip, and I swear on my mother's grave he smiled at it. At the crowd, at the smoke, at the whole doomed arrangement. As if to say: *you were all arithmetic, and I was the one holding the pen.*

Prévost stood beside me at the harbor office door. "That's the clockmaker's brother?"

"That's him."

"Heard he made a fortune in the evacuation trade."

"Heard that too," I said.

"From who?"

"Everyone," I said, which was true, and useless, and exactly the kind of answer a man gives when his real answer is sitting in a false-bottomed drawer waiting for the post.

The provost's letter had gone out with the nine o'clock rider. Nine o'clock plus the ride to the naval station plus the provost's breakfast plus however long it takes a bureaucracy to turn outrage into action: I estimated arrest on the pier by half past eleven, maybe noon. The Meridian sailed at eleven. The mathematics were tight and ugly and depended entirely on the clock above the observatory keeping perfect time, which it would, because a heartbroken woman had built it true and then given it away.

Ada Delacroix was coming down Quay Street now with the toolbox in one hand and the girl's hand in the other, and beside them walked the mother, and the four of them — no, the three of them and the ghost between them — made a strange procession toward the gangway. I had read the true ledger often enough by now to know its geometry by heart: Mireille Delacroix, entitled. Tomas Delacroix, entitled. Zephyrine, name entered, line otherwise blank. Entitled to what? the ledger never said. Entitled to passage, presumably. Entitled to survival, hopefully. Entitled, at minimum, to be counted — which is more than the document ring ever offered anyone, and less than a child deserves, and the entire distance between those two facts was the distance I had spent my career refusing to measure.

They reached the checkpoint. The lieutenant checked the manifest. Mireille stepped forward, valise first, certain as sunrise. Tomas's name was called and answered from the deck above, triumphant, waving his hat. And then the lieutenant looked up from his papers, and looked at Ada, and looked at the child, and said the words I had been dreading since dawn:

"This manifest lists three. I see four."

* * *

*Ada*

Of all the ways I had imagined this moment failing — Tomas intercepting us, Halloran arresting me, the lieutenant simply tearing the page — I had not imagined it failing through competence. The lieutenant was young and thorough and had clearly counted heads his whole life, the way I counted gear teeth.

"Three listed," he repeated. "Mother, uncle, ward." He glanced at the ward line, then at Zephyrine, and something flickered across his face — the particular discomfort of a man comparing a living child to a piece of paper and finding the paper wrong. "Name?"

Zephyrine opened her mouth. I squeezed her hand. Whatever happened next, she was not going to stand at a gangway and perform her own case like a beggar at a gate. I had promised myself that much, at least, and promises made to oneself at midnight in a clock tower are the only kind I have ever kept.

"The ward is with me," I said. "The third berth."

The lieutenant looked at the manifest again. The manifest said what Tomas had paid for it to say: a dead child's name, purchased in spring, resold in autumn. Somewhere in the harbor office was a duty journal in a policeman's disguised handwriting that told the truth about that line, and somewhere in the provost's saddlebag was a letter that would make the truth official, and none of it had arrived yet, and the gangway officer wanted a name *now*, because the clock above the observatory said 10:58 and the clock was true and the truth had a schedule.

Behind me, Mireille said, quietly, in the voice of a woman laying down her last card: "Tell him your name, child."

And Zephyrine — six years old, evidence in a stolen ledger, the daughter of silenced clerks, the girl I had named after a wind vane because she pointed at the weather before anyone else felt it — Zephyrine looked up at the lieutenant and said, clear as a struck bell:

"My name is Zephyrine. It's written in the real ledger. My mother wrote it."

The quay went quiet around us, that specific silence of a crowd smelling blood in the water. The lieutenant's pen hovered. Down the dock, I saw Halloran detach himself from the harbor office door and start toward us at a walk that was trying very hard to be a stroll. And high on the deck of the Meridian, leaning on his unnecessary cane, Tomas Delacroix saw the little knot of stillness at his gangway and began, unhurried, to come back down.

The clock struck eleven.
