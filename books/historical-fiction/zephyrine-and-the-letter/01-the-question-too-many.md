## 1. The Question Too Many

The fog came off the harbor at dawn the way it always did in Verrance, thick as wool and smelling of brine and coal smoke, and by seven o'clock it had swallowed the Halcyon Trading Company workshop whole. August Marchetti could have found her way to her desk blindfolded—had, in fact, done so once on a dare from a diver who no longer had a name in any ledger—and so she moved through the grey without hurry, past the drying racks hung with nets, past the tar vats breathing their sweet-rotten breath, up the narrow stair to the counting room where she had spent eleven of her twenty-six years.

The counting room smelled of ink and lamp oil and the particular staleness of paper that has been handled too much by too few hands. August lit the lamp at her desk, unlocked the drawer where the day's ledgers slept, and began, as she did every morning, with the tide tallies.

She was good at this work. She knew it without vanity, the way a diver knows his own lung capacity. Numbers did not lie, and more importantly, numbers did not look at you. In the counting room August Marchetti was nobody—a pair of hands attached to a pen attached to a column of figures—and nobody was the safest thing a person could be in Verrance. Her father had taught her that before he went into the water for the last time. *Be accurate, be quiet, be small.* The company rewarded all three.

By half past eight she had reconciled the previous day's salvage weights against the dock manifests and found a discrepancy of four pounds in the ambergris entry, which meant either a clerk upstream had transposed a digit or someone on Pier Six had pockets. She noted it in her private memorandum book—the one she kept in a cipher of her own devising, because accuracy without discretion was just another way of getting drowned—and moved on to the wage rolls.

That was when Corvane came down the stairs two at a time, which he never did, and stopped at her desk with the expression of a man delivering news he'd rehearsed and hated.

"Census man," he said. "Came early this year. He's upstairs with Master Wrenn, but he'll want the indenture rolls, and he'll want *you* to produce them."

"Me?"

"You're the keeper of records, aren't you?" Corvane's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. He was a broad, grey-templed man with forearms scarred from rope burn and a way of standing that suggested furniture built for a larger room. He had been August's supervisor for six years and her protector, in his quiet, deniable way, for longer than either of them acknowledged. "Wrenn's got the gout and no patience. You'll do it."

August closed her ledger. "How long does a census take?"

"An hour, if the taker's thorough." Corvane paused at the top of the stairs, and something crossed his face—not fear exactly; August had learned to read the difference between fear and its respectable cousin, caution—but something adjacent to it. "August. Answer what he asks. Nothing more. Census men are like divers. The dangerous ones are the ones who keep going down after everyone else has come up."

Then he was gone, and August sat for a moment with her hand flat on the closed ledger, feeling the first faint pressure of a question that had not yet been asked.

* * *

Mr. Oduya was not what she expected.

She had imagined a government man as a certain type: soft, bored, sweating through a coat too heavy for the season, wanting only to fill his columns and catch the noon ferry back across the strait. The man seated at Master Wrenn's desk—Wrenn himself having retreated to his chair by the window with his bandaged foot propped on a stool—was none of these things. He was perhaps fifty, lean, dark-skinned, with a close-cropped beard going silver at the jaw and spectacles that he wore low on his nose so that he looked at documents over them and at people through them. His coat was good quality and mended at both cuffs, which told August he had money once and had made peace with having less. A leather satchel sat open beside him, squared to the edge of the desk with a precision she recognized because it was her own precision, mirrored.

"August Marchetti," he said, reading from a form. Not asking. Confirming. "Indentured, Halcyon Trading Company, term commenced 1878. Occupation: ledger-keeper. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"And your age?"

"Twenty-six."

He wrote. His handwriting was small and exact, each figure placed on its line like a stone in a wall. August stood opposite him with the indenture rolls open on the lectern, ready to produce whatever he required, and waited for the interview to become routine.

It did, for a while. He asked the questions census men asked. Number of persons resident at the workshop: forty-one. Number employed in diving operations: nineteen. Number of apprentices: six. Births within the year: none. Deaths within the year: two, both divers, both recorded. He took down the names of the free clerks, the wages paid, the tonnage handled. His pen never hurried and never stalled. Twice he asked her to repeat a figure, and twice she repeated it identically, and the second time he glanced up at her over the spectacles with an expression she couldn't classify—something between approval and appetite.

"The workshop seems well kept," he said. "Your records especially. Most companies I visit, the rolls are a disgrace. Ink blotted, pages torn out, entries in three different hands. Yours—" he touched the indenture roll lightly, "—yours are beautiful."

"I keep them."

"So I see." He turned a page. "You've been here since you were fifteen. That's young for an indenture."

"My father signed the papers. After my mother died there were debts."

"He was a diver, I understand. Tomas Marchetti."

The name landed somewhere under her ribs, the way a dropped weight lands on a deck—with a sound you feel more than hear. "He was."

"Lost in 'eighty-one. The *Kestrel* wreck." Oduya said it gently, but he said it looking at his form, filling in the date, and August understood that he already knew everything he was saying. Census men worked from prior records; that was the entire architecture of a census, one year laid over the next like tracing paper. Which meant—

Which meant he had been reading her family's line in the old rolls. All morning, perhaps. While she stood there producing figures.

"Your sister," Mr. Oduya said.

The counting room was very quiet. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up. August heard her own pulse in her ears, steady, professional.

"What about her?"

"That's my question, Miss Marchetti." He set down his pen—a deliberate act, she noticed, the way a man sets down a tool when he means to use his hands—and folded his fingers together on the desk. "Zephyrine Marchetti. Born 1866, Verrance parish. She appears in the birth register, and she appears in the parish school roll through the winter of 'seventy-four. And then she appears nowhere. Not in the 'seventy-five district census. Not in any indenture roll, apprentice list, wage book, or mortality record in this port or, I would wager, any other. A girl of eight years old, and then—" he lifted his hands slightly, palms up, "—nothing. Twenty years of nothing."

"She died," August said.

The lie came out smooth. That was the terrible thing, the thing she would think about later, lying awake: how smooth it came out, how little it cost, as though it had been sitting behind her teeth for years waiting to be spent. "Fever. The winter of 'seventy-four. There was sickness in the harbor quarter—you'll find it in the parish burial notes if you look."

"I did look." He said it without accusation, without anything at all, which was worse. "There is no burial note. There is no death certificate. There is no doctor's record, no coroner's entry, no pauper's grave receipt. In Verrance, Miss Marchetti, even the poorest dead leave paper. Your sister left none at all. It's as though she stepped off the page of the world."

"Paper burns. Paper gets wet. This is a port city, sir. Half the parish records from that decade smell of bilge."

"All true." He picked his pen back up, and for a moment she thought—hoped—that was the end of it, that he would write *deceased, unrecorded* in his neat column and move on to the next name, the next life, the next nothing. He dipped the pen. He did not write. "And yet," he said, "when a child dies without record, there is usually a reason, and the reasons are usually one of three. Poverty, shame, or somebody being paid to lose the page. Which was it, in your family?"

The question went in clean, the way a fishhook goes in, and August felt it set.

She was aware, distantly, of Master Wrenn by the window, pretending to read a shipping notice, his gouty foot twitching. She was aware of the door, of the stairs, of the forty-one persons resident below. She was aware most of all of her own face, which she held very still, because she had learned young that a still face was a locked room.

"My father was grieving and poor," she said. "Grieving people don't file papers. Poor people can't afford to. If the parish lost the rest, I can't account for it. I was nine years old myself. I buried my mother that same year. I'm sorry the record isn't tidier for you, Mr. Oduya. Death rarely is."

Silence. Through the fogged window the harbor bell moaned, twice, marking some vessel's passage that August could not see.

Then Mr. Oduya nodded, slowly, and wrote in his column. She caught the word upside down before his hand covered it: *unverified*. Not *deceased*. *Unverified.*

"Of course," he said. "These things happen. They happen often enough, in fact, that a man in my position learns to notice when they happen *too* neatly." He finished the line, blotted it, and turned the form over to a fresh page with the crisp finality of a door closing. "Thank you, Miss Marchetti. You've been very helpful. Your records truly are beautiful. Whoever taught you to keep them understood something important."

"What's that?"

"That a page can hold a person or erase one, and the difference is only ever whether someone is watching." He closed his satchel, squared it again to the desk's edge, and stood. At the door he paused, half-turned, and looked back at her—not through her, as he had looked at the forms, but directly, the way one person looks at another. "I'll be in Verrance through the equinox. Completing the district. If you recall anything further about your sister—anything at all—I keep rooms above the chandler's on Rope Walk. I'm easy to find. Census men usually are. Nobody wants to talk to us until suddenly everybody does."

And then he was gone, down the stairs, out into the fog, his footsteps fading on wet cobbles, and August stood alone in the counting room with the indenture rolls open on the lectern and her heart doing something it had never done in eleven years of service: beating out of rhythm.

* * *

She told herself it was nothing. She was very good at telling herself things; she had had practice.

She told herself that census men asked strange questions every day, that a missing record was a missing record and meant nothing more than a careless priest or a damp cellar, that Zephyrine had been dead for fifteen years and the dead did not require paperwork to stay dead. She told herself this while she put away the rolls, while she ate her bread and cheese standing at the window, while the fog burned off and the harbor emerged piece by piece—the masts first, then the cranes, then the low brown hills beyond—until the world looked ordinary again.

But the question stayed. That was the trouble with it. It didn't argue with her lie; it simply lodged, the way a splinter lodges, too deep and too small to grip, working inward a hair's width at a time every time she moved.

*Unverified.*

Not *deceased*. He had written *unverified*, and the difference between those two words was a door left open, and August Marchetti had spent her whole life walking past doors left open because opening them was not the business of small, quiet, accurate people.

Except.

Except that at dusk, when the workshop emptied and the lamps were lit and the tally-men went home to their suppers, August found herself not at her own desk but at the cabinet where the older rolls were kept—the deep ones, the ones that went back past her father's time—and she found herself pulling the volume for 1874–75, and she told herself she was only checking a citation, only verifying a figure, only doing the diligence that was, after all, her trade.

She found the year of the fever. She found the harbor quarter's deaths listed in her father's era's cramped hand: the Kestrel widows, the children, the old men carried off by the lung-rot. She ran her finger down the column of names, and it trembled, just slightly, and she steadied it against the page.

No Zephyrine Marchetti. No burial. No fever entry at all for the Marchetti household beyond her mother, recorded, dated, witnessed.

Her sister had been eight years old. August remembered her—of course she remembered her, she remembered everything, that was her curse and her craft—remembered the gap in her teeth and the way she could hold her breath longer than any boy on the pier, remembered the night their father came home without the boat and without words, and the morning after, when Zephyrine's cot was stripped bare and August was told she had gone to kin up the coast, and then later, when August was old enough to understand what kind of stories adults tell children, she had been told the other version: fever, quick, no suffering, and never ask again.

She had never asked again. Fifteen years of never asking again. Because invisibility was safety, because small and quiet and accurate was how you survived Verrance, because the powerful wrote the history and people like her endured it, and enduring meant not looking at doors left open.

August closed the roll. The sound of it—leather on wood, soft as a held breath—seemed very loud in the empty counting room.

Above the chandler's on Rope Walk, a census taker was keeping rooms through the equinox.

She blew out her lamp, and in the dark she stood a moment with her hand flat on the closed volume, the way she had that morning, and admitted to herself, in the privacy of her own skull, the one thought she had refused all day:

If there was no record of Zephyrine's death, then there was no record of Zephyrine's death. And a girl who could vanish from every register in a port city without dying had not vanished at all.

Someone had erased her. And someone, somewhere, had been paid to hold the pen.
