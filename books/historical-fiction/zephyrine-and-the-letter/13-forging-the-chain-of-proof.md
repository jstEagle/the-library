## 13. Forging the Chain of Proof

The press room above the shuttered chandlery smelled of ink, lamp oil, and the particular despair of a man who had once owned three of everything and now owned one of nothing. Sabine Quill kept the single press because selling it would have meant admitting the game was over, and she had never in her life admitted anything a lawyer hadn't first made her admit under oath.

She heard August Marchetti on the stairs before she saw her—the careful tread of someone who had spent years being unnoticeable and could not quite shake it even when climbing to a hideout. The girl came through the door with a oilcloth bundle clutched to her chest like an infant, and behind her came Oduya, who closed the door and stood against it as though the whole city might follow them up.

"You brought it," Sabine said.

"I brought copies. The originals stay hidden." August set the bundle on the composing table with the reverence of a priest laying out relics. "You said proof that would survive a courtroom."

"I said bring me something worth surviving for. Whether it survives is another matter entirely." Sabine untied the cloth. Inside: pages copied in a small, disciplined hand—August's hand, she supposed—and a letter gone soft at the folds, its ink browned with age. She read the letter first, standing, reading fast the way she'd learned to read twenty years ago on the *Verrance Courier*, before the libel suit, before Hale's lawyers had taken her name off the masthead and her money out of her account and her reputation into the gutter where she now kept house.

When she finished, she looked up. "Who wrote this?"

"My father. I think. I can't prove that either."

"No," Sabine agreed. "You can't."

Oduya shifted by the door. "It's genuine. I knew him—Tomas Marchetti. He was a clerk in the shipping office before he went north."

"Wonderful. A dead man's handwriting, identified by a census taker with no standing, describing crimes he witnessed secondhand, addressed to an inspector who never arrived." Sabine laid the letter down as though it were something that might bite. "Do you know what Mr. Hale's counsel will do with this? He'll call it a forgery in under four minutes, and he'll be right to, because you cannot authenticate it. No witness to its writing. No chain of custody. It was found in a boot heel, for God's sake—a detail so picturesque it sounds invented, which means a jury will assume it was."

August's face did something complicated. Sabine watched pride and hope drain out of it and be replaced by the flat expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. Good. That was the right face. Hope was useless in a courtroom; arithmetic was not.

"Then what do we have?" August asked.

"Sit down. Both of you. This is going to take all night, and you're going to hate most of it."

* * *

They sat. Sabine cleared a space among the type trays and spread August's copies across the scarred wood, weighting the corners with lead slugs.

"Lesson one," she said. "Sentiment is not proof. You've spent your life inside ledgers, so you understand this better than most journalists ever do—but you'll forgive me if I test it anyway, because the last time I trusted my own conviction about what was true, it cost me eleven years and every friend I had."

"The libel suit," Oduya said quietly.

"The libel suit." She said it without bitterness, mostly. "I printed that Hale's grain contracts with the northern mills were padded. Every word true. I could prove none of it, because my source recanted, my notes were seized, and truth, Miss Marchetti, is not a defense—it is merely a claim until evidence makes it a fact. Hale understood that better than I did. So we are going to build this the way he would: link by link, each one load-bearing, no sentiment anywhere in the chain."

She pulled the first copied page toward her. "Your registry transcriptions. Tell me how they were made."

"Copied by hand, at night, from the original book in the records room."

"And the original?"

"In the workshop strongroom. Under Corvane's key."

"So what you have given me is your word that a book exists, containing entries you say you read, written down by you, unverifiable against the thing itself." Sabine tapped the page. "In court, this is paper. Nothing more. Hale's men will produce a registry—or produce nothing at all—and swear yours is fabricated. Where does that leave us?"

August was quiet a moment. "It leaves us needing the original."

"It leaves us needing more than the original. An original can burn. Your company has already scheduled a fire, I hear." She let that land; Oduya flinched slightly, which told her the equinox rumor had reached him too. "What we need is the one document Hale cannot disown, cannot duplicate, and cannot explain away. Tell me about the seal page."

August looked up sharply. "How did you—"

"You mentioned it on the stairs. Also, I spent six years covering the Halcyon Company, and I know how their instruments are drawn. Every transfer of indenture requires the director's private seal—not the company chop, mind you, the *private* one, the one Hale keeps personally because the transfers are personal. They're his inventory. If the registry lists names sold onward, then somewhere there is a master instrument bearing his seal that authorizes the whole trade. One page. Probably kept close, because a man like Hale trusts no vault but his own house."

"His townhouse," Oduya said slowly. "On Cordwainer Hill. I take the census rolls past it every season. There are always lamps burning."

"There are always lamps burning in houses with things to hide," Sabine said. "Or dinner parties. In Hale's case, both."

* * *

They worked until the lamp burned low. Sabine made August defend every line.

"This entry—'discharged to family' for a diver named Pellam. Do you know Pellam drowned?"

"I know his family never received him. His mother lives two streets from the workshop. She still sets out his coat on saints' days."

"Will she testify?"

August hesitated, and Sabine saw the answer before it was spoken. "She's frightened. And she's half-blind. And Hale's people have already been to see her once."

"Then she's worthless to us and dangerous to herself, and we leave her out." Sabine said it flatly, and watched August bristle. "Don't look at me like that. I'm not saying her grief doesn't matter. I'm saying a broken witness is worse than no witness, because opposing counsel will take her apart in an afternoon and use the wreckage to discredit everything else we bring. We protect people by keeping them out of the fire, not by feeding them into it."

"Then what good are any of us?" August asked. "Every person who knows anything is indentured or terrified or both."

"That," Sabine said, "is exactly why we need the seal page. Hear me. Testimony can be bought, frightened away, or buried. Documents can be forged—unless they carry a seal whose impression Hale himself must confirm or deny. If we put the seal page in front of a magistrate, Hale has only two moves: admit it's his, and hang himself, or deny it's his, and perjure himself against an instrument his own clerks drew. Either way, the registry stops being a rumor and becomes an exhibit. Everything else—your father's letter, your transcriptions, the divers' families—everything hangs off that one nail."

Oduya had been turning his hat in his hands, a clerk's habit he couldn't kill. "And if we can't get it?"

Sabine met his eyes. "Then we have a very moving story and no newspaper in Verrance will touch it, because every printer in this city owes Hale money or fear, usually both. I know. I *was* the story they wouldn't touch."

The silence stretched. Rain had started against the skylight, a fine commercial rain, the kind Verrance exported along with its fish and its lies.

August spoke first. "You want me to steal it."

"I want you to tell me whether it can be stolen. There's a difference, and the difference matters." Sabine leaned forward. "I'm a ruined journalist, not a burglar. But I've spent eleven years studying the man who ruined me, and I'll tell you what I know: Edmund Hale dines every Thursday. He dines publicly, lavishly, with the magistrate's brother and the harbor commissioner and whoever else needs to be seen agreeing with him. On Thursdays, his townhouse fills with servants running food and coats and carriages, and his attention is downstairs holding court. If that seal page lives in his study upstairs, Thursday is the night it is loneliest."

"You've thought about this," Oduya said.

"I've thought about revenge for eleven years. It's the only hobby I can afford." She let the joke sit, then took it back, because it wasn't entirely a joke and they all knew it. "But listen to me carefully, both of you, because here is lesson two, and it's the one that will keep you alive: whatever we do, we do it so that the proof doesn't depend on any one of us staying free. Not me, not you, not anyone. Copies in three places. Witnesses separated. If Hale takes one of us, the chain holds. That's how you fight a man with the law in his pocket—you make the truth bigger than any throat he can squeeze."

August was staring at the spread of pages, her finger resting lightly on one entry. Sabine followed her gaze. A name. *Zephyrine Marchetti — transferred.* The sister. The drowned-not-drowned girl at the center of all this quiet fury.

"You understand what you're asking for," August said, not looking up. "If we go into that house and fail, we don't get a trial. We disappear. Into the registry. Like everyone else."

"Yes," Sabine said simply. "That's the price of paper. I paid it once already, in the currency I had. You'll pay it in yours." She stood, went to the window, and looked down at the wet street where a lamplighter was making his rounds, flame by flame, pretending the fog wasn't winning. "So decide. Not tonight—Hale dines Thursdays, and today is Monday, which gives us three days to plan and one lifetime to regret. But decide honestly. If you want safety, take your copies and run north tonight, and I will not think less of you. I ran once myself. It didn't work, but I won't pretend the alternative was obviously better."

Behind her, the long pause of a woman weighing her whole life on a scale.

Then August said, "Tell me about the house."

* * *

They planned until nearly dawn. Sabine drew the townhouse from memory—she had covered a society wedding there once, back when editors returned her letters—and Oduya contributed the servant rotations he'd observed on his census rounds, because a man trained to notice households noticed everything about them. They argued about entrances. They argued about timing. They argued longest about who would go, and Sabine won that argument by losing it: she was too known, too watched; her face appeared in old engravings in three newspapers' morgue files. It would be August, and Oduya if he could stomach it, and no one else.

At the end, August gathered her copies back into the oilcloth, and paused at the door.

"Why help us?" she asked. "You don't know me. You don't owe these people anything."

Sabine considered lying, decided against it—lesson three, though she didn't say so: never lie to your allies about your motives, because motives are the one thing they'll eventually discover anyway.

"Because eleven years ago I had the truth and no proof, and I told myself the truth was enough," she said. "It wasn't. It cost me everything and changed nothing, and Hale has slept soundly every night since. I am fifty-three years old, Miss Marchetti, and I would like, before I die, to watch that man learn what sleeplessness costs." She turned back to the press, to the type, to the work. "Thursday. Come at dusk. Wear something that belongs in a house like that."

The stairs creaked twice, then fell silent. Oduya lingered a moment longer.

"You're testing her," he said. "All night. Tearing holes in everything she brought."

"Of course I am. Better I tear holes in it than Hale's counsel." Sabine sorted type without looking up. "Besides—if she'd cried, or quit, or argued from her heart instead of her head, I'd have sent her home. She did none of those things. She asked questions and took notes." She allowed herself the smallest smile. "She might actually be a journalist someday. God help her."

Oduya left. The rain thickened. Sabine stood alone in the lamplight with her one press and her eleven ruined years, and began setting type for a headline she did not yet have the story to fill—a headline she had composed in her head a thousand times over a decade:

*THE HALCYON REGISTRY: THE NAMES THEY SOLD.*

Three days, she thought. Three days to forge a chain strong enough to hang a director of the Halcyon Trading Company—or to bury four more names in his book.

She reached for the seal-red ink.
