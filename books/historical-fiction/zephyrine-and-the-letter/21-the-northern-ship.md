## 21. The Northern Ship

The ship was called the *Meridian Star*, and she smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular despair of third-class passage. August stood at the rail for the first two days out of Verrance, watching the coast unspool behind her like a thread being pulled from a hem, and she did not go below until the fog swallowed the harbor lights entirely.

She had never been on open water. She had spent nineteen years in a city built on it, had copied the manifests of a hundred ships, knew the freight rates for herring and coal and human beings to the farthing—and none of it had prepared her for the way the sea moved under her feet, patient and enormous, as if it were keeping time for something.

She kept the letter's copy sewn into her coat lining, against her ribs. She kept Sabine's last words folded in her memory: *Come back before the trial opens or your testimony dies unread.* She kept Zephyrine's name written on a scrap of paper in her pocket, though by now she could have recited it in her sleep—*Zephyrine Marchetti, transferred, northern route, mill registry of Corren Vale*—because she had copied that page four times over in the records room with hands that would not stop shaking, and some things a body learns the way it learns its own heartbeat.

Corren Vale was eleven days north by sea and two more by coach, if the coach came. The letter from the harbor diver—an old man named Tull who had wept when he told her—said only: *There is a woman at the Vale mills who signs her work with a bird drawn wrong. Ask her what she was called before.*

August had asked him why he hadn't said anything for six years.

He had looked at her the way people look at children who ask why the tide comes in. "Who would I say it to?" he'd said. "You?"

She had not had an answer then. She had one now, folded in a newspaper office in Verrance, drying in ink.

* * *

The north was colder than she had imagined cold could be. Not the damp gray chill of Verrance fog but a dry, mineral cold that got into the teeth, that made the mill town's brick smokestacks look like organ pipes in a church built for no god she recognized. Corren Vale existed, so far as August could tell, for exactly one purpose: the mills. They squatted along the river in rows, breathing smoke day and night, and the town clung to them the way barnacles cling to a hull—usefully, without illusion.

She arrived on a Tuesday with seven coins left and a story prepared. She was a clerk from the south, she told the boarding-house woman, come north on company business, an audit of mill labor accounts. The lie sat easily in her mouth; she had been telling versions of it her whole life. What unsettled her was how little she needed it. Nobody asked. In Verrance, invisibility had been imposed on her and she had made a craft of it. Here, she discovered, she was simply nobody, and nobody was a country anyone could enter freely.

It should have felt like freedom. It felt like standing in a field with no fences and realizing she had forgotten how to walk without walls.

She found the mills on Wednesday morning and stood outside the gate with the other women waiting for the shift change, listening to them talk about wages and a foreman named Brack and whose child had the croup. Ordinary talk. The talk of people who endured things and did not expect the enduring to be noticed. She knew this language fluently. It was her mother tongue.

At the bell, the gates opened and the workers came out like water finding a crack.

August watched faces. She had not seen her sister since she was nine years old—since the morning the company men came with papers saying there had been a drowning, since her mother had screamed once, briefly, like something tearing, and then gone quiet in the way that quiet became the rest of her life. Nineteen years. Whatever face came through that gate would be a stranger wearing a ghost's name.

And then a woman stepped out, small and square-shouldered, hair bound back in a kerchief the color of rust, and she was drawing something on the palm of her hand with a stub of chalk—a bird, quick and careless, wings too long—and August's whole body understood before her mind did.

She did not call out. Some instinct older than thought stopped her—the same instinct that had kept her alive through nineteen years of ledgers, the knowledge that you do not speak a dangerous thing in the open air. She followed the woman instead, at a distance, through streets that smelled of dye and river rot, to a narrow house with a blue door, and stood across the lane like a fool, like a thief, like a sister, until the door opened again and the woman looked directly at her.

"You've been behind me since the gate," the woman said. Her voice was flat, unhurried, the voice of someone who had learned that alarm costs energy. "If you're from Brack, I don't take corrections after hours. If you're from the company—" she paused, and something moved behind her eyes, something old and quick, "—then you're a long way south."

"I'm not from the company," August said. "I'm from Verrance."

Something flickered. Just at the corners of the mouth. "Everyone's from somewhere," the woman said. "That's not an answer." But she held the door wider. "You'd better come in where it's warm. You look like a letter that's been rained on."

* * *

The room inside was clean and spare: a stove, a table, two chairs, a bed, a shelf holding perhaps thirty books and a jar of chalk stubs. On the wall hung a cloth embroidered with the same wrong-winged bird, done carefully this time, in red thread.

The woman put water on to boil and did not speak until it did.

"Say whatever you came to say," she said, sitting down across the table, folding her hands. "I'll hear it once."

August had rehearsed this moment for eleven days on the deck of the *Meridian Star*. She had composed speeches. She had imagined falling into her sister's arms, imagined rage, imagined tears. All of it dissolved now, in this plain room, in front of this plain, watchful woman, and what came out instead was the truth in its plainest clothes:

"My name is August Marchetti. My mother was Sera Marchetti, of Tanner's Lane, Verrance. My father was Elias Marchetti, a diver for the Halcyon Company. And I had a sister who drowned—or didn't—in the spring of 1870, when I was nine years old."

The woman did not move. The stove ticked. Somewhere down the street a cart rattled past, and a dog barked twice and gave up.

"That's a story," the woman said at last. "People come up here with stories. Men from churches. Men from newspapers, once. A man three years ago who said he was my cousin and wanted me to sign a paper." Her eyes were brown, dark, level. "What do you want me to sign?"

"Nothing." August's voice cracked on it. "I don't want anything. That's the whole—I came eleven days on a ship because a dying diver remembered you, and I don't want anything except—"

"Except."

"To know if it's you." She made herself stop, made herself breathe. "Zephyrine."

The name landed in the room like a stone dropped in still water. The woman looked down at her own hands—at the chalk bird still ghosted on her palm—and for a long moment she was very still, the way water is still right before it freezes.

"I haven't heard that name in twenty years," she said quietly. "I buried it myself. Dug the hole and put it in and stamped the ground flat." She looked up. "Where did you get it?"

"The Halcyon registry. Your name is in it. Page forty-one. 'Transferred, northern route.' They sold you, Zeph. They sold you and they told Mama you drowned, and Papa found out and wrote a letter about it and they killed him for it, or near enough, and I have the letter, I have the pages, and in two weeks there's going to be a trial in Verrance that will tear the whole company open, and every name in that book will be read aloud in a courtroom—including yours."

She had not meant to say all of it at once. It came out the way floodwater comes through a broken door. When she finished, the kitchen was silent except for the stove, and Zephyrine—who had been Zephyrine for twenty years, or whatever she called herself here, and August realized with a lurch that she did not even know—sat looking at her with an expression August could not read.

Then she laughed. One short, hard syllable, with nothing in it of joy.

"A trial," she said. "In Verrance. Where I was cargo." She stood, went to the window, stood with her back turned. "Do you know what happens to a girl who's sold north? Do you know what the mills are? They're gentler than the ships, I'll give them that. Nobody drowns you here. They just count you. Hours in, hours out. I've been counted for twenty years, little sister. I'm very good at being counted." She turned around. "And now you want to uncount me. In public. With my name shouted in a courtroom full of strangers."

"I want them to answer for it."

"They won't answer to me. They'll answer to a magistrate in a fine coat, and he'll write it down in another ledger, and in fifty years some other girl will find that ledger and learn that justice is also just a place where names get moved around." She said it without heat. That was the terrible part. There was no bitterness in it at all. It was simply weather to her, the climate she lived in.

August felt something in her chest twist. "You're her," she said. "You're really her."

"I'm whoever survived," Zephyrine said. "That's the closest you'll get."

* * *

They talked until the light went out of the window and the stove needed feeding again, and it was not the conversation August had crossed a sea for. It was harder and plainer than that.

Zephyrine did not want to hear about the letter—not yet, not all at once. She asked questions instead, short and precise, like an auditor herself: Who else knows? How many copies? Where are they kept? Is the press safe? When August answered, she nodded slowly, filing each answer away, and August understood suddenly that this wariness was not coldness. It was scar tissue. This was what happened to a person when hope had been used against her too many times. Every question was a hand testing ice.

But between the questions, other things surfaced, small and sideways, like fish rising.

"You used to bite your nails," Zephyrine said once, apropos of nothing, watching August's hands on the table. "Mama used to paint them with alum so you'd stop. You cried for a week."

"She never told me that."

"There's a lot she never told you. She told me everything the night before they took me. She knew, August. She suspected. She made me memorize our street, our names, the color of our door. She said, *if anyone ever asks you who you are, say it fast, before they can make you forget.*" Zephyrine's voice stayed level. Her hands, on the table, did not. "I forgot anyway. Took me about four years. Then it took me another ten to remember, and by then remembering hurt more than forgetting, so I kept the bird instead. Drawn wrong on purpose. So it couldn't be anybody's emblem. Just mine."

August looked at the embroidery on the wall. "Why wrong?"

"Because the right one belonged to them. Everything right belongs to them. The ledgers, the laws, the truth with its proper seal on it." She almost smiled. "Wrong is the only thing they can't confiscate."

Later—much later, when the lamp was low and the tea had gone cold—Zephyrine asked the question August had been dreading.

"Why did you come? Truly. Not for the trial. The trial doesn't need me. A name read aloud is just a name; they don't need my body in the room." She leaned forward. "Why did you spend your money and cross the water?"

And August, who had lied smoothly to census takers and supervisors and directors, found she could not lie to this woman at all.

"Because I spent my whole life believing that people like us endure history, we don't make it. That the safe thing is to be invisible. And I believed it right up until being invisible started feeling like being complicit." She looked at her hands. "And because I lost you when I was nine and I have spent nineteen years being careful, and I am so tired, Zeph. I am tired all the way down. And I thought—if you were alive, then maybe the careful life wasn't the only one available. Maybe there was a version of me that got to have a sister."

The silence after that was long. Zephyrine rose, fed the stove, and stood with her hand on the iron door, watching the fire.

"When they took me," she said finally, "you hid under the stairs. I saw your feet. You were crying so hard your whole body shook, and I remember thinking, *don't come out, whatever you do, don't come out,* because if they took both of us, Mama would have nothing left to live for." She closed the stove. "You obeyed. You were nine years old and you obeyed, and it was the bravest thing anyone ever did for me, and I have thought about those feet under the stairs for twenty years."

August discovered she was crying. She hadn't decided to. It was simply happening, the way rain happens.

"So no," Zephyrine said, turning, and her own eyes were bright and hard, "I am not going to stand up in any courtroom. I am not going to be anybody's cause, or anybody's proof, or anybody's restored family in a newspaper. I've been property in public once already. Never again." She crossed the room, and then—awkwardly, stiffly, like someone performing an action her body had not rehearsed in two decades—she sat down beside August and put her arm around her shoulders.

"But you can stay the night," she said gruffly. "And tomorrow I'll show you the river. And you can tell me everything about Mama, everything, and don't leave anything out to spare me, because I'm done with being spared." A pause. "And when you sail back south—"

"I'm not sailing back alone," August said into her sister's shoulder. "I'm not asking you to testify. I'm asking you to exist where they can't erase you. Come to Verrance after. When it's over. Come see the rolls opened. Come watch them read the names."

Zephyrine was quiet for a long time. Outside, the mill whistle blew the late shift change, and neither of them moved.

"You always were stubborn," she said at last. "Mama said you got it from her. I think she was flattering herself."

* * *

August wrote to Sabine from the Corren Vale post office the next morning, her breath steaming, her fingers stiff with cold:

*Sister found. Alive. Whole enough. Will sail on the next packet and arrive no later than three days before the trial opens. Save me a column inch. I have one more thing to write.*

She sealed it, paid her last coin and a half, and handed it over the counter—and then stood a moment on the frozen street, looking north toward the mills, where the smoke rose straight into the white sky and the whistle blew and blew, counting everyone.

Three days' sail. Two weeks until the trial. And somewhere between here and there, Hale's lawyers were already arriving in Verrance, and Corvane was still in custody, and the city was holding its breath.

August turned toward the harbor and began to walk.
