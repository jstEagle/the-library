## 16. The Plan at the Orchard Wall

### Mirabel

There is a particular quality to a conspiracy assembled in an orchard in February: it smells of wet wood and old cider, and everyone involved keeps glancing over their shoulder as though the trees might report them.

We met at the Vasseau wall on the last afternoon before the tide — Odile's suggestion, naturally. If you are going to commit what the army would call treachery and I would call bookkeeping, you may as well do it standing among trees that have outlived every empire that ever marched past them.

Agnes arrived first, walking fast with her hands jammed in her pockets, which is how she walks when she has decided something and hates herself for it. Odile came from the house carrying a jug of cider as if it were communion wine. I came last, because I had spent twenty minutes in the linen room telling myself that I could still put the ledger back under the floorboard and be nobody again by supper.

I did not put it back. That is the whole of my courage, really: a series of small refusals to undo things.

"Sit," said Odile, patting the wall. "Standing people make speeches. Sitting people make plans."

So we sat, three women on a frost-cracked stone wall between bare apple rows, and planned the smuggling of forty-one names onto a ship full of historians.

"The *Alouette* sails at dawn," Agnes said. She had the tide table memorized; she has everything memorized, that woman, including, I suspect, every unkind thing I have ever said to her. "The crates go aboard tomorrow evening. Bandages, splints, the winter stores they're taking back for the garrison hospital. My crate is already packed."

"Your crate," I repeated.

"My crate." She looked at the orchard rather than at me. "Third shelf from the top, under the rolled compresses. There's a false bottom where we used to hide the brandy from the chaplain. Don't ask me why I know about the false bottom."

"Agnes—"

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me when the ship is out of sight and neither of us is in irons." She finally turned to face me, and her jaw was set in the way it sets when she is frightened and furious about being frightened. "You understand that if this goes wrong, it isn't the historians who suffer. It's us. A colonial nurse and a sister with no family to write letters home about her disappearance. We are exactly the sort of people who vanish without troubling anyone's conscience."

"I understand it better than you think," I said. "It's the same arithmetic Ashworth did. Some lives count in the ledger and some don't. He just chose the wrong column."

Odile snorted into her cider. "The English," she said fondly, "always think they invented cruelty. We had it here first, and we had better weather for it."

I loved her immediately and completely, in the way one loves people who have survived more than you and laugh anyway.

* * *

### Odile

They sat on my wall like two sparrows deciding whether the cat was asleep, and I thought: these children are going to get themselves drowned in paperwork.

I am sixty-three years old. I buried a husband, two sons, and one very stupid dog, and I have learned that grief does not frighten me but waste does. Waste frightens me. These two were proposing to carry the truth of my boys across the water in a box meant for bandages, and if they botched it, all that truth would rot in some officer's evidence locker while men in London wrote polite sentences about glory.

So I did what my mother did when the Prussians came through the first time, and her mother when the others came. I made tea — well, cider; we are not animals — and I organized.

"The cart leaves here at four o'clock tomorrow morning," I told them. "Before light. The quay guard changes at four-thirty and the new man is Gaspard's boy, who owes me for the winter apples and cannot read a manifest anyway. The fish barrels go down to the quay at five. The *Alouette*'s purser takes on provisions at six. Your Sister will hand over her medical crate at six-fifteen, official channels, proper signatures, nothing to look at twice."

Mirabel stared at me. "How do you know the purser's schedule?"

"How do you know your soldiers' names?" I said. "Because you wrote them down and paid attention. I have sold fish and apples to that quay for forty years. The purser takes on provisions at six. He always takes on provisions at six. Empires come and go; the purser takes on provisions at six."

The little nurse laughed — a startled sound, like something breaking open. I liked her for it. She has the look of a person who has been holding her breath since November.

"And the ledger itself?" asked the Sister, practical as a scalpel. "If it's found in the fish barrels—"

"It won't be found in the fish barrels," I said. "It will be found nowhere, because it will not travel in the fish barrels. The barrels are for looking at. The ledger travels in the crate, wrapped in oilcloth, inside the false bottom, under the compresses. The barrels hold nothing but herring and a decoy parcel — old farm accounts, boring enough to put a customs man to sleep. Let them search the fish. Fish have never lied to anyone."

"You've done this before," Mirabel said quietly.

I looked at my orchard, at the rows my sons pruned the last autumn before the war took them, at the ground where their names would grow no marker unless someone carried one across the sea.

"Everyone my age has done this before," I said. "That is what they never tell you about history. It is mostly old women moving things at night."

* * *

### Agnes

I want it recorded — somewhere, even if only in my own head, which is the only archive guaranteed to survive me — that I did not agree easily.

I lay awake half the night after the orchard wall doing sums. Not numbers; sums of a different kind. What the army has given me: a roof, a purpose, thirty years of being necessary instead of merely poor. What the army has taken: I began the list and stopped, because the list had no bottom, and a woman can drown making lists.

Here is what decided me, and it shames me a little, so I will say it plainly. It was not principle. Principle is a luxury I gave up years ago, along with sugar and Sunday afternoons. It was Mirabel's face when she said *he chose the wrong column.* She said it the way our patients say *am I going to die* — knowing the answer, asking anyway, hoping someone will lie to them kindly.

No one was going to lie to her kindly. So I would help her instead.

I told them my part at the wall and I have not slept properly since. The false bottom in the supply crate — yes, I built it myself, two winters ago, for brandy, and I am not proud, and God and I have an understanding about it that mostly holds. The same boards that hid contraband from a chaplain will now hide the dead from the historians. There is a sermon in there somewhere. I will never preach it.

At four o'clock tomorrow morning I will carry that crate down to the quay with my own hands, sign the manifest in my own name — *Sister A. Whitcombe, Zephyrine Field Hospital, 3 crates medical stores* — and watch it swung aboard by men who will never know they are lifting forty-one souls.

My name. On the manifest. Under my own signature.

I have spent thirty years being careful. It occurs to me now, sitting here in the dark with my boots still on, that carefulness was its own kind of dying. Mirabel talks about loyalty versus truth as though they are opposites. She is young. Loyalty and truth are the same animal; it is only fear that splits them apart.

I am afraid. I am going anyway.

* * *

### Mirabel

We settled the last details as the light went blue and then grey, and the frost crept up the wall and into our coats, and none of us moved, because moving would mean the meeting was over and the plan was real.

Roles, then. Agnes's crate — the vessel, the alibi, the signature. Odile's cart and her herring and her Gaspard's-boy guard — the noise and the misdirection, the ordinary commerce of an ordinary village doing what ordinary villages have always done under the noses of whoever claimed to own them. And mine —

"Nerve," said Odile.

"That's not a role," I protested. "That's a personality flaw."

"It is the most important role," she said serenely. "Tomorrow evening, Captain Reeve will be on that quay, because he is a careful man and careful men stand on quays. Someone must stand near him and be visible and dull. A nurse seeing off the supplies. You will smile. You will answer his questions. You will let him look at you while he is not looking at the cart."

"And if he searches the cart?"

"He will not search the cart, because he will be busy being charmed, and because men like that never suspect the cargo — they suspect the courier. Which is why the courier must be you, chérie, standing in plain sight, being suspected beautifully."

I laughed, and it came out wrong, half a sob. Forty-one names. Ashworth's confession in my handwriting. My whole future folded into oilcloth under some compresses, riding to sea in a box that smelled of carbolic.

And underneath the terror — I will admit it here, in the privacy of my own head, where honesty costs least — underneath it was relief. For months I have carried that ledger alone, under floorboards, under my apron, under my tongue. Tomorrow other hands would touch it. Other names would stand beside mine on the risk. The conspiracy of the overlooked: a nurse, a nun, a widow. The three categories of woman the empire trains never to be seen. Well. They had trained us too well in one direction and not at all in the other. Nobody watches for us because nobody believes we matter.

Let them keep believing it until dawn.

We stood to go. Odile pressed the cider jug into my hands — "for your nerve, since you claim to lack it" — and then she took my face in her rough apple-tree hands and kissed both my cheeks like I was family, which undid me entirely.

"For Étienne and Luc," she said. "And for all of them. Make the paper remember."

Agnes walked back toward the hospital with me through the darkening rows. Neither of us spoke for a long time. At the gate she stopped.

"If it goes wrong," she said, "you let me take it. All of it. You're the one with papers worth forging; I'm the one with nothing left to lose but habits."

"Agnes—"

"Don't argue with a senior officer, Nurse." But her eyes were wet, and she squeezed my arm once, hard, and went inside.

I climbed to the linen room and took out the ledger one last time — not to add to it, but to read it. Forty-one names. I said each one aloud, quietly, the way you'd wake sleepers gently. Tomorrow night they would begin the crossing I could not make for them.

Then, below the ward window, I saw lamplight moving where no lamplight should be — two figures by the dispensary door, and one of them tall, unhurried, turning his head with that particular courteous attention I had learned to dread.

Captain Reeve, walking the hospital corridors at night, three days early and counting something.
