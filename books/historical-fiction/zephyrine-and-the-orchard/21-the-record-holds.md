## 21. The Record Holds

The parcel arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Nothing that matters ever arrives on a Sunday, when one is prepared for miracles; it arrives on a Tuesday, between the mending and the inventory, smelling faintly of ship's hold and official indifference.

I was counting gauze — we were always counting gauze; the war had ended eight months prior and the empire still could not count gauze on our behalf — when Sister Berthe brought it up from the post room and set it on my desk with the particular delicacy of a woman who has decided a thing is important and wants you to know she has noticed it before you have.

"From London," she said. "Printed matter. Heavy."

"Everything from London is heavy," I said. "It's how they keep the colonies humble. The postage alone is a lesson in subordination."

Berthe did not laugh. Berthe had been sent to us in the autumn as a replacement for a nurse who had gone home to Marseille, and she had not yet learned that I made jokes the way other people made tea — constantly, and as a substitute for saying what I actually felt. She would learn. They always did, or they transferred to the coastal hospital and wrote letters about the strange colonial woman in the orchard village who talked to trees. Both outcomes were acceptable to me.

The parcel was addressed in a clerk's hand, the kind of handwriting that exists only in government offices: immaculate, anonymous, and slightly resentful of the effort. Inside, wrapped in brown paper and packed with wood shavings that smelled of some English forest I would never see, was a book.

*The Zephyrine Campaign: An Official History of the Coastal Operations, 1916–1918.* Revised edition. The word *revised* sat on the title page like a man who had been made to apologise at a dinner party and was doing it with as little grace as the law allowed.

I sat down. I am not ashamed to say my legs made that decision without consulting me.

I had waited for this book the way one waits for a verdict — with a rehearsed indifference that fools no one, least of all oneself. For months I had told Agnes, and Odile, and anyone who asked, that I did not care whether the correction ever came. *The truth does not require a printer,* I said. *The dead are not sitting by their letterboxes.* All very fine. All very false. I had checked the post every day since the thaw, and I had done it with the furtive hunger of a woman checking for a letter from a lover she insists she has forgotten.

Agnes found me an hour later still sitting with the book unopened in my lap, which tells you something about how busy we were in the winter of 1919, which is to say: busy enough to be interrupted, never so busy that grief could not find a chair.

"Well?" she said.

"I'm afraid of it," I said, because Agnes had earned the truth in ways I would still be repaying when we were both old women arguing about linens. "It's been so long that I've begun to hope they simply buried it, and a buried thing can't disappoint you."

"And if it has disappointed you?"

"Then I shall plant a tree over it and call the disappointment an orchard. It's the local custom. Everything here becomes a tree eventually."

Agnes pulled up the second chair — my chair, the intruder's chair, though by now she had squatter's rights — and nodded at the book. "Open it, Mirabel."

So I did.

* * *

I will not pretend I read it the way a scholar reads. I read it the way a woman reads a letter that might contain either a proposal or a dismissal — skipping, hunting, heart going like a drummer boy who has not been told the war is over.

The original edition — the one Captain Reeve's colleagues had been drafting in our ward while Ashworth died upstairs, glorifying the advance that had killed two hundred and eleven men whose names existed nowhere but my ledger — had been published the previous spring. I had seen a copy. Odile had bought one in the market town and we had read it together at her kitchen table, taking turns reading aloud in voices that grew flatter and flatter, until the account of the advance at the Vasseau orchard wall was finished and neither of us could speak. The book had given the advance four sentences. Four sentences for the mud, the orders that could not be countermanded, the boys who went over the wall into machine-gun fire because a colonel's report had already decided they were a reasonable expenditure. It had named three officers, quoted one dispatch, and used the phrase *acceptable losses* with the serene confidence of men who had never been anyone's loss.

This new edition was different in one way only, and that way was everything.

Appendix C. *Corrections and Additions to the Text.* Eleven pages. Eleven pages of names.

I read them the way you read a rosary. Aubert, Julien-Marie. Baker, Thomas E. Cissé, Amadou. The names of men I had washed and bandaged and failed to save, and men I had never met at all — the ones Ashworth had named in his last confession, dictated to me between dawn and dawn with his voice going out like a lamp running dry. There were names from other hospitals, other wards, names I did not know, which meant someone else had kept a ledger too, or Reeve had dug until his fingernails bled, or both. Two hundred and eleven names from the advance alone. Then others. Whole platoons restored to the record like furniture returned to a house after a burglary.

And at the head of the appendix, a note from the compilers, written in that same clerkly prose that manages to be both humble and insulting:

*The publishers acknowledge with regret the omissions of the first edition. The corrections herein were made possible by documents received after publication, the provenance of which cannot be fully established. The editors wish to record their debt to an unnamed nurse of the Zephyrine ward, whose records form the basis of Appendix C.*

An unnamed nurse of the Zephyrine ward.

I read the phrase four times. I read it a fifth time to be sure I had not invented it. I had spent a year of my life, and very nearly my career and my liberty, on those eleven pages, and the empire's historians had compressed me into seven words and a refusal to know my name — which, I will confess, was exactly what I had asked for, and exactly what stung.

"Say something," Agnes said.

"They misspelled Aubert," I said. "Julien-Marie Aubert. There's an accent missing off the first e. His mother corrected my spelling twice when I wrote to her, and I got it right, and some clerk in London has taken my right spelling and made it wrong again." I closed the book carefully, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "Two hundred and eleven names and they still couldn't be bothered to ask a Frenchman how to spell a French name."

Agnes was quiet a moment. Then: "Is that all you have to say?"

"No," I said. "But the rest is unsayable, so I'm leading with the accent."

* * *

I took the book to Odile that afternoon, because there was no question of taking it anywhere else. Some things are not yours to keep to yourself.

The Vasseau farm sat above the village the way it always had, patient as a grandmother, its orchards running down the slope in bare winter rows. The war had taken two of Odile's sons — Étienne and Luc, the advance at the orchard wall had taken Étienne and the fever ward had taken Luc, and if you think that distinction comforted her, you have never met a mother. She had given me their letters and a photograph the winter of the flood, evidence offered as freely as cider, and I had carried both into the historians' ship in a fish barrel, which is a sentence I still could not say aloud without laughing in the wrong place.

She was in the kitchen when I arrived, scalding jars for the spring preserving, and she read my face before I got my coat off. Odile had that talent. She could read a face the way I read a pulse.

"Sit," she said. "You have the look of a woman carrying news that weighs less than she expected and more than she wanted."

"Is that a country saying?"

"It's a mother saying. Mothers invent sayings because the real ones are too heavy to hand to children." She poured cider without asking. "So."

I put the book on the table between the jars. I turned to Appendix C and laid it open, and I watched her read.

I want to record what happened next precisely, because I have learned that precision is the only honest form of tenderness. Odile Vasseau did not weep. She read all eleven pages, running one scalded-red finger down the columns, and when she found the names — *Vasseau, Étienne. Vasseau, Luc* — she stopped, and her finger stopped, and the kitchen was so quiet I could hear the jars cooling on the rack, that small ticking of glass settling into its new shape.

"They're spelled correctly," she said at last.

"Yes."

"Someone asked a French person." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and bright and terrible. "That was you. You asked, or you wrote it, and they kept it."

"I wrote it."

"And they kept it." She closed the book, gently, the way she closed the lid of the chest where she kept her sons' letters. "Eight months, Mirabel. Eight months I have known that the first book said nothing. That Étienne was four sentences of *acceptable losses* in a language he didn't speak, written by men who never saw the wall. And now." She put her hand flat on the cover. "Now there are eleven pages."

"It's not enough," I said. "It's an appendix. They put the truth in the back of the book, behind the glory, where the light is bad. It's not enough, Odile. I want to be clear about that, because if you thank me I shall have to argue, and I haven't the strength today."

"I wasn't going to thank you." She stood, and went to the window, and looked out at the bare orchards. "I was going to show you something. Come."

* * *

What she showed me was a wheelbarrow, two saplings with their roots balled in sacking, a spade, and a morning that had decided, against all seasonal precedent, to behave like spring.

"The nursery man came through last week," she said, walking me down through the rows, the frozen mud giving under our boots in that reluctant way frozen mud has. "Pear trees. Late-bearing. I bought two." She said it the way you report the weather. "I've been waiting to plant them until I knew."

"Knew what?"

"Whether the world was going to say their names or not. I told myself: if the book comes and it says nothing, I plant the trees anyway, for me, because a woman cannot live in a house with two empty chairs and no trees. But if it comes and it says their names —" She stopped at a gap in the row where the light came down clean and long across the slope. "Then I plant them here, in the orchard, where they worked. Where Étienne could climb anything and Luc fell out of everything. Then it's not a grave marker. It's a place they'd have chosen."

She knelt then — a woman of sixty, in frozen ground, without a word of complaint, because kneeling in dirt was a language she had spoken longer than any of us had been alive — and began to work the roots loose from the sacking. I took the spade. We did not discuss the division of labour; it arranged itself, the way work does among women who have both buried people.

The ground fought us. March ground always does. I dug while she held the first sapling straight, and we did not talk, because there are holes that words fall into and never come out of, and the hole we were digging was quite literal enough.

It was only when the first tree was in — Étienne's, she said, because Étienne had been the elder and the elder goes first even in this — that she spoke again.

"Read me the note again. The one about the nurse."

"*The editors wish to record their debt to an unnamed nurse of the Zephyrine ward,*" I recited, driving the spade in, "*whose records form the basis of Appendix C.*"

"Hm." Odile patted the earth down around the young trunk with both hands, firm, ungentle, the way you handle anything you intend to survive. "Unnamed."

"That was the arrangement."

"It was a foolish arrangement."

"It was a *necessary* arrangement. Captain Reeve had already offered me protection in exchange for the ledger, which is how that man says *hand it over or I ruin you.* If I had put my name on it, I'd have been a colonial nurse who stole official documents and embarrassed the army, and there is no appendix long enough to save you from that. Unnamed was the whole point. The names were the point. My name was never —"

"I know what the point was." She looked up at me from the ground, mud to the wrists, and her expression was the one she used on grandchildren and on me, interchangeably. "I am saying it was a foolish arrangement *and* a necessary one. Both things are true. That is what nobody tells you about doing right: it costs you, and you pay, and the receipt is anonymous." She stood, brushing her skirts. "You gave them two hundred and eleven names and they gave you seven words. *An unnamed nurse.* As if you were a weather station."

I laughed. It came out of me sideways, surprised, and it turned into something else halfway through, the way laughter does when it has been waiting eight months, and Odile put her arm around my shoulders and let me do it against her shoulder, and neither of us called it anything.

"Plant the second one," she said finally. "Luc's. And then you're staying for lunch, and you're telling me everything the book got wrong, and there will be a great deal, because there always is."

* * *

We planted Luc's tree in the early afternoon, in ground that had softened enough to be kind, and I stayed for lunch, and I told her everything the book got wrong, and there was a great deal.

The dates of the supply delays were fudged. The account of the bridge closure made it sound like an act of God rather than a failure of the engineering corps. Ashworth appeared in three places, always as *a commander of firm convictions*, which is one way to describe a man who falsified casualty figures and then spent his last night on earth whispering corrections to his own confession while I wrote them down as fast as my hand could go. The book did not mention the falsification. Of course it did not. The appendix restored the dead, but it did not indict the living; the historians had performed the delicate operation of admitting everything and accusing no one, which is the national sport of every empire I have ever stood in.

And yet.

And yet there were eleven pages of names where before there had been four sentences of fog. Somewhere in London, in a library that smelled of dust and self-regard, a researcher in fifty years would look up the Zephyrine advance and find Julien-Marie Aubert, accent corrected or not, and Thomas Baker, and Amadou Cissé, and the Vasseau brothers, and two hundred and seven others, and none of them would be *acceptable losses* anymore. They would be men with names, in a book, forever, or at least for as long as books last, which given the state of the century was the only forever on offer.

Vindication, I decided, walking back down to the village in the late light, is a quiet and incomplete thing. It is not a parade. It is an appendix. It is a missing accent. It is an old woman patting mud around a sapling with hands too rough to be gentle and too gentle to be rough. It is seven words about a woman who will never be named, and the strange, stubborn comfort of those seven words, because *unnamed* is not the same as *unrecorded*, and I had spent two years learning the difference at the cost of nearly everything.

Agnes was waiting when I got back, sitting in my chair by the ward stove, and she looked up with the question already on her face.

"Well?" she said, again, the same word, the whole question.

"They're in," I said. "All of them. Eleven pages."

Agnes closed her eyes and let out a breath she had apparently also been holding for eight months. Then, because she was Agnes: "And you?"

"I've been demoted to a weather station," I said, "and promoted to a debt. I haven't decided which is worse." I sat down across from her and put the book on the table between us, open to the appendix, and for a while we just looked at the names, two women and a stove and a list of the dead, which is as good a portrait of that war as any the historians managed.

It was Agnes who broke the silence, and what she said I did not expect.

"There's a letter behind the appendix," she said. "Page two-forty. I saw it in the post room when the parcel came. I didn't read it." A pause, and then, with the careful neutrality she reserved for things that were not neutral at all: "But the envelope was from the Army Nursing Service, Mirabel. And it had your name on it. And it came in the same post as a book about setting the record straight."

I turned to page two hundred and forty.

The envelope was there, thin, official, my name in that same clerkly hand. I had been so busy with the book — with the names, with Odile, with the trees — that I had not even looked.

I broke the seal.

* * *

The letter was brief, which from the Army Nursing Service was either mercy or menace, and I read it twice before I let myself believe either.

They were offering me a transfer. Home. Passage on the spring convoy, a posting in the coastal hospital where I had trained, a return to the city and the sea lane and everything I had spent two years telling myself I wanted. *In recognition of your service,* the letter said, in the tone of an institution that believed service was its own reward and should be, and the date of the sailing was three weeks away.

Three weeks. The spring convoy. The same tide, more or less, that had once carried a fish barrel full of the truth out of Zephyrine harbour, and here it was coming back around again, offering to carry me.

I sat with the letter in my lap while the stove ticked and Agnes watched me and the light went amber in the ward windows, and I found that I could not make myself feel what the letter clearly expected me to feel. Relief, perhaps. Or homesickness, finally, after two years. Instead what I felt was the orchard slope in the late light, two new saplings standing crooked and stubborn in the cold, and Odile's voice saying *a place they'd have chosen,* and underneath all of it, quiet as a pulse, a thought I had been refusing to think since the thaw:

That the ward needed training. That the village girls who came to help were quick and fearless and had never been taught a thing. That there was a second ledger in my desk drawer, the new one, the one I had started in the autumn — not of the dead, but of the living: *Berthe, learns fast. Sylvie, steady hands, fourteen, too young, watch her. Odile's granddaughter, asks questions, good.* That I had been writing it for months without admitting what it was, which was a plan.

"Three weeks," Agnes said, reading the date over my shoulder, because she had never once respected the privacy of my post. "That's soon."

"It's the tide," I said. "It's always the tide."

"And will you go?"

I looked at the letter, and then out the window, where the last light was coming down the slope toward the orchard, catching on the bare rows, on the two small trees at the gap in the wall, standing up out of the frozen ground like a sentence someone had finally bothered to finish.

"I haven't decided," I said.

It was, of course, a lie. But it was a Tuesday, and some things you have to let arrive in their own time.
