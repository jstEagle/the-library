## 4. Names in the Margin

The army draftsmen arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for cleaning the pens. Master Ferran had held that Odile's Law — *never ink anything of consequence on a day meant for housekeeping* — and I had kept it for six years without once asking why, because her rules were like her maps: you trusted them until you'd walked the ground yourself and found she'd been right all along.

There were three of them. Two were boys in gray with ink-stained cuffs and the particular swagger of men who had never drawn a coastline but had been told, recently and by someone important, that they were now qualified to. The third was older, precise, and introduced himself to the room rather than to me, as though the room had rank and I did not.

"I'm Voss," he said. "Clerk-draftsman, second grade." He set his kit down on the long table without asking which end was mine, which was a courtesy, since by the new arithmetic of requisition none of it was mine. Not the table, not the presses, not the pens I'd cleaned that very morning in obedience to a dead woman's law.

"August," I said. "Apprentice."

"Apprentice," he repeated, writing it down, and I understood that I had just become a line item.

* * *

The first days were a study in being furniture. The gray boys — Pell and Dorn, by their muttered addresses to each other — worked the campaign sheets: road widths, bridge loadings, forage capacities. Numbers a map could carry without blushing. I was given the coastal plates to correct, which was a joke so fine I almost admired it. I had inked those plates. I had inked them at night, by shuttered lamplight, from Master Ferran's field notes, for an atlas that did not officially exist. Now the army wanted them corrected under its own seal, and I was the only person in the room who knew the corrections were a form of confession.

I corrected them beautifully.

This is the thing no one warns you about complicity: it's comfortable. You keep your hands busy, your eyes down, your mouth full of *yes, sir* and *as you say, sir*, and the days pass with a smoothness that feels almost like peace. Pell hummed while he ruled his lines. Dorn had a cough and a girl in some garrison town he described to anyone who'd listen. Voss said nothing at all for three days, which I mistook for stupidity, an error I would come to regret with the particular shame reserved for mistakes made about quiet men.

On the fourth day, they gave me the requisition files.

* * *

It was Voss who handed them over — a ledger-bound stack, string-tied, with the army's stamp bleeding through the cover like a bruise. "Cross-reference," he said. "The commander wants the coastal plates annotated against local holdings. Ferries, warehouses, granaries. You know the town."

I knew the town. That was rather the point of everything.

I untied the string expecting requisition schedules, and the first thing I saw was a name.

*Harrow & Sons, ferry rights, east landing. Debt outstanding to the garrison commissary, 41 crowns, 12 shillings. Loyal: probable. Note: eldest son absent from muster rolls — deserter?*

I knew the Harrows. Old Harrow had pulled me out of the shallows once when I was eleven and foolish about ice, and had never once mentioned it afterward, which in Vellamarch is the highest form of kindness. His eldest son hadn't deserted. He'd gone upriver to his aunt's the winter his mother died, because grief needs somewhere to sit, and I knew this because I'd carried his trunk to the ferry myself.

I turned the page.

It was the ledger. Not the ledger — not Master Ferran's ledger, the true one sleeping under the floorboards beneath my feet at that very moment — but its ghost, its echo, its *transcription*. Someone had copied her names into the army's files. Debts. Grudges. Who owed whom. Who had spoken against the occupation at the fish market. Who had fed the gray boys willingly and who had fed them with the particular silence that means *under protest*. Every entry in her careful shorthand, rendered into the army's plain hand, stripped of her purpose and fitted with a new column: *Action recommended.*

That column was new. That column was the army's.

* * *

I did not look up. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in my own head, which is the only court that will ever convene on the matter. I did not look up. I turned the pages with a clerk's flat efficiency, because Voss was three feet away ruling his lines, and because my face has always been the one part of me I could map accurately, and I was not going to surrender it now.

But inside, something was doing arithmetic it didn't want to finish.

*Widow Sarel, miller. Debt: none. Loyal: no. Note: harbors resentment re: grain levy. Action: monitor.*

Widow Sarel had given me bread every Sunday for four years and pretended it was stale so I'd take it without owing her.

*The Quill & Anchor, public house. Proprietor: E. Maren. Loyal: no. Note: permits seditious talk among boatmen. Action: monitor; consider closure.*

I had learned to read a current chart at the Quill & Anchor's back table, with Maren's bad coffee going cold beside me, while she pretended not to hear Master Ferran say things that would have hanged us both.

Page after page. The town I had spent six years learning to see — its debts and loyalties, its hidden hinges and quiet mercies — was all there, exactly as she'd recorded it, and beside every name now sat the army's cold annotation, turning knowledge into ammunition. Master Ferran had made this ledger to hold power accountable. To publish it. To say *these are the strings, and here is who pulls them, and look, all of you, look.* She had believed that a thing seen clearly could no longer be used in the dark.

She had not counted on the dark simply taking the lamp.

* * *

"Problem?" Voss asked.

I had stopped turning pages. I hadn't noticed I'd stopped.

"Handwriting," I said. "The copyist runs his entries together. Hard to cross-reference."

"Whose original?"

"A cartographer's. Field notes on local conditions." I heard my own voice and marveled at it — level, bored, slightly put upon. A voice for inventory. "Who compiled these for the files?"

"Requisition clerks at division." Voss took the stack from my hands — gently, I noticed; he handled paper the way some men handle birds — and turned to the first page himself. "The source material came with the workshop. Everything in the workshop came with the requisition." He said it without triumph, without apology. A fact, delivered like a fact. "You knew the previous master?"

"I swept her floors."

"Then you knew her work."

"I knew the ink was good."

He looked at me for a moment — a long moment, the kind that has a temperature — and then he handed the stack back and returned to his lines, and I went back to turning pages, and neither of us said another true thing for the rest of the afternoon.

* * *

That night I lifted the floorboard and took out the true ledger and held it under the lamplight, and I want to be honest, because honesty is the one luxury left in that workshop: I thought about burning it. Right there. One match, one lifetime of a dead woman's work gone to ash, and every name in Vellamarch suddenly illegible to the army, safe in the only place that can't be requisitioned — nowhere.

I even struck the match.

And then I thought of Master Ferran's marginalia, the note I'd found a hundred times in her field books and never understood until that winter: *A map that hides the truth is a weapon. A map that shows it is a witness. Weapons get captured. Witnesses get heard.*

She hadn't made the atlas to keep it under a floorboard. She'd made it to be *read*. And if I burned it, the army would still have its transcription — the ghost was already in their files, stamped and string-tied — and the town would have lost its own witness forever. I'd be burning the testimony and leaving the theft.

I pinched out the match.

What I did instead was worse, or braver, or possibly both: I read. All night, by shuttered lamplight, I read her ledger against their transcription, and I found the copyists' errors — a name misspelled, a debt doubled, a loyalty recorded as its opposite — and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that I was now the only living person who knew both versions. The truth and the army's version of it. And that whichever one survived the winter would be the one the town was remembered — and punished — by.

* * *

In the morning, Pell was humming again, and Dorn was coughing, and Voss was ruling lines with the patience of a man who believes the world is fundamentally a well-kept record. The gray light came through the north windows and lay across the campaign maps like a sheet over a face.

I sat down at my place at the long table, and I opened the requisition files to the first page, and I began to cross-reference, exactly as ordered, exactly as a good apprentice should.

And as I worked, I made a small decision, the size of a pen-stroke, which I did not let myself examine: every error I had found in the army's transcription — every misspelled name, every doubled debt, every loyalty inverted — I would correct. Quietly. In the files themselves. Because a clerk checks a copy against its source, and the source, as far as the army knew, was still sitting in the workshop.

They thought they had requisitioned Master Ferran's knowledge.

They had no idea they'd also requisitioned her apprentice — or that an apprentice, given six years of training in making false coastlines look true, could make a true town look false.

I dipped my pen. Outside, the river ice groaned once, long and low, like something turning over in its sleep.

I wrote the first corrected name.
