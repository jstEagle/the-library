## 7. The Clerk Who Reads Backwards

I have been told — by my mother, by three successive commanding officers, and once, memorably, by a woman in Osterlin who then declined to see me again — that I am a difficult man to like. I dispute this. I am a very easy man to *account* for. Ask me what I did on any given day of the past eleven years and I can tell you, with page references. It is not my fault that most people find their own lives so poorly documented.

My name is Imre Voss, and I am the requisition clerk to Commander Halvard Renn's occupation garrison at Vellamarch. I did not choose Vellamarch. Vellamarch was assigned to me, in the way that weather is assigned to a field. I arrived with two trunks, one portable writing desk with a locking drawer, and a conviction I have carried since boyhood: that the world is tolerable only insofar as it is recorded correctly.

You will want to know if I am a cruel man. I have considered the question. Cruelty requires appetite, and I have never had much of an appetite for anything except a well-kept margin. When the soldiers took the baker's boy last week, I entered his name, his trade, his father's address, and the source of the information into the detention register, and my hand did not shake. I have thought about that hand since. It did not shake because there was nothing in it. A man who trembles is a man holding something he should not; a clerk holds nothing. He holds the record, and the record is not his.

This is what I told myself, anyway, in the third week of the occupation, when I began to suspect the apprentice.

* * *

The apprentice's name was August Whitlock, though I learned later that this was rather like calling a river by the name painted on its bank. Master Odile Ferran had died in the autumn — officially of a fever, unofficially of whatever it is that kills people who refuse things — and the workshop had passed, in the army's arithmetic, from her estate to the requisition ledger, and from the requisition ledger to Commander Renn's ambition. The apprentice came with it, the way a hinge comes with a door.

I inventoried the workshop myself. This was proper procedure; the commander's other clerks were soldiers first and penmen second, and one of them had already catalogued a crate of brass instruments as "miscellaneous metal goods," which I corrected in red ink and which earned me a look I have received many times and never learned to fear.

The plates were the difficulty. There were forty-one of them when I counted, copper, each etched with a section of the province in Master Ferran's hand — roads rendered as fine as hair, bridges numbered, fords marked with depths in feet and inches. And in the margins, in a smaller script, annotations. Notations of ownership. Of debt. Of who owed whom, and how much, and since when, and who had vouched for whom before the provincial courts, going back decades. I read four plates before I understood what I was looking at, and when I understood it, I felt something I can only describe as professional vertigo.

Here was a document that claimed the province was legible. Not the province of roads and elevations — any competent surveyor could do that — but the province underneath, the province of obligations. The widow Harsany's mill held in trust against a loan from the Delvard family since 1811. The ferry rights at Kessel's Landing quietly purchased by a magistrate who adjudicated the sale himself. Three hundred small entries like teeth.

"This is the master's working atlas," I said to the apprentice, who stood by the window with the particular stillness of furniture.

"Yes, sir."

"It will need to be completed under army seal. The commander's instructions." I said this without inflection. Inflection is a form of error.

"Yes, sir."

I looked at the apprentice a moment longer than the answer required. I want to be honest about this, because honesty about oneself is the only inventory that cannot be falsified: I noticed nothing definite that day. What I noticed was an absence. The apprentice answered every question correctly and completely, and yet afterward I could not have told you a single thing August Whitlock wanted. In eleven years of reading men through their paperwork, I had learned that everyone wants something, and that the wanting leaks out somewhere — in a signature pressed too hard, in a claim filed a day early, in a request for leave that coincides too neatly with a market fair. The apprentice leaked nothing. Which meant either that there was nothing inside, or that the vessel was very well made.

I do not believe in vessels with nothing inside. I have audited too many empty crates.

* * *

Let me explain my method, since method is all I have ever had to offer anyone.

When I take custody of documents, I copy them. Not the whole — that would be theft, and I am not a thief — but the structure. An index. A concordance of names against entries, entries against dates. The army believes I do this for cross-reference purposes, and the army is correct, in the way that a stopped clock is correct twice a day. The deeper reason is older than my commission. When I was nine years old, the tax assessor in my home town misrecorded my father's parcel boundaries, and the error propagated — through the rolls, through the courts, through a foreclosure we did not understand until the bailiffs were already at the gate. My father spent six years trying to prove a mistake that no one had written down correctly at the moment it was made. He died still holding the wrong map of his own land.

So: accuracy is virtue. I hold to this. If the world had been recorded correctly, my father would have died a landowner instead of a litigant. Every error I prevent is a small restitution to a dead man who never knew me kindly. You may find this a cold creed. I find it the warmest thing I possess.

The trouble began, as trouble always does for me, with a discrepancy.

Plate thirty-seven covered the eastern valley — the Osterlin road, the river bends, the string of villages that smell of cabbage smoke and wet wool. The marginalia listed debts and allegiances in Master Ferran's compressed hand. But the plate itself was unfinished: the final inking incomplete, certain roads drawn in pencil awaiting the pen. And here was the discrepancy — the pencil lines did not match the marginalia. A road marked as belonging to the Corvess estate ran, in the pencil correction, through land the margin attributed to a tenant farmer named Aldous Petch. Someone had begun to amend the map against the notes. Someone had been checking the master's work.

Apprentices do not check masters. That is not what apprentices are for. Apprentices grind ink and keep their opinions in their pockets until they are masters themselves, and often long after.

I set the plate down. I picked it up again. I fetched my magnifying glass, which I keep in my breast pocket the way other men keep watches, and examined the pencil work under the workshop's gray light. The line weight varied. The pressure eased at curves and firmed on straights in a way that suggested a hand thinking about terrain, not copying it. These were not corrections being transcribed. They were corrections being *decided*.

I wrote in my private log — the locked drawer, the one I will burn if I am ever ordered to surrender it, though I confess I do not know if I would obey even then — *"A.W. possesses independent knowledge of the province exceeding apprenticeship. Source unknown. Observe."*

Observe. It is remarkable how much villainy can hide inside a word like observe.

* * *

Through February I observed. I will render my observations faithfully, because faithfulness is the whole of my religion and I see no reason to abandon it now, even in confession.

Observation the first: the apprentice worked slowly for the army and quickly alone. During supervised hours, the campaign maps progressed at a pace I timed against the garrison clock — deliberate, competent, unremarkable. But twice, arriving early to verify the overnight security of the plates, I found fresh work done in hours that should have taken days, done and then partially smudged or blotted, as though someone had raced the dawn and won and then tried to make the victory look like defeat.

Observation the second: the apprentice read backwards. I mean this literally. Reviewing the requisition files one evening — the files into which, by some clerk's efficiency in the provincial capital, pages of Master Ferran's private ledger had been interleaved among the standard inventories, names and debts and loyalties filed between invoices for lamp oil and boot nails — I looked up through the workshop's interior window and saw August Whitlock at the far table with a sheaf of papers, reading from the bottom of the page upward. I assumed at first it was some drafting technique. Then I saw the same habit three more times, always with the same class of document, and I understood: the apprentice was not reading the text. The apprentice was reading the *hand*. Bottom-up, you catch a forger. Top-down, you merely receive.

Who taught a mapmaker's apprentice to detect forgery? Or — a colder question, and I set it down here exactly as it occurred to me, because I have promised myself accuracy even where it indicts me — who taught the apprentice to fear it?

Observation the third, and this is the one that changed the shape of my days: the ledger pages in the requisition file were being handled. Pages carry evidence the way rivers carry silt. I had made folio marks on the Ferran materials when they were accessioned — small pencil ticks, my own system, top corner, invisible to anyone who did not know to look. By the middle of February, three of the ticks had shifted position. Someone was removing the pages, reading them, and returning them — carefully, correctly, but not perfectly. Nothing returns perfectly. This is the first law of my profession and the last consolation of my life.

So I did what any accurate man would do. I began to copy.

* * *

I want to be precise about my reasoning, because I suspect that someday — perhaps soon, perhaps in front of a tribunal, perhaps only in front of my own mirror, which is the stricter court — I will need it complete.

The ledger existed. It would be used. Commander Renn had already spoken of it in the language men use for tools: *the Ferran material gives us the province's nervous system, Voss; a commander who knows where every debt sits owns the province without firing a shot.* I transcribed his meaning accurately, as I transcribe everything. He intended reprisals dressed as administration. Debts called in. Loyalists rewarded on paper and punished in practice, since a man publicly favored becomes a target for everyone the favor costs. Names moved from margins to lists.

Given that the ledger would be used, my choices were these. One: refuse to participate. This would remove me from the process and change nothing about the outcome, and would also deprive the record of the only clerk in the garrison who could be relied upon to record it truly. Two: report my suspicions of the apprentice. To whom? To Renn, who would squeeze the apprentice for whatever the apprentice knew and add the results to the list. Three: copy the ledger myself, so that whatever happened — use, abuse, destruction, loss — a true copy would exist, held by a man whose entire life was arranged around the principle that true copies must exist.

I chose three. I tell you it was for the record. I believed it was for the record. I had built a whole architecture of self out of such beliefs, load-bearing and unexamined, and I lived in it comfortably, the way a man lives in a house he has never once stepped outside of at night to look at.

Copying took me eleven nights. I worked after hours in the records room, by my own lamp, with the door bolted and the key in my waistcoat. I copied in a compressed shorthand of my own devising — names reduced to consonant frames, sums to positional ciphers — so that the transcription, intercepted, would read as gibberish or as draft nonsense. I am not proud of the tradecraft. I am telling you about it because it was easy. That is the part that has kept me awake. Eleven nights, and not once did my hand shake.

On the fourth night, copying the entry for the baker's family — the boy Tomas, seventeen, who had swept Master Ferran's floors for three years and whose arrest warrant I had myself prepared from the ledger's testimony about his uncle's smuggling — I paused. Not from remorse. I want to be exact. From a sensation adjacent to remorse but colder: the recognition that I was producing, in my careful cipher, a document identical in function to the one I distrusted. The ledger did not kill the baker's boy. The ledger informed. I informed. Accuracy, I had always said, is virtue. But a knife is accurate too.

I finished the entry. My hand did not shake. I noted the time in my log: 11:40. Then, beneath it, for the first time in eleven years of logs, I wrote a sentence that was not a record of anything: *Ask yourself what the copy is for.*

I did not answer it. Answering questions is not my department. Recording them is.

* * *

Near the end, the apprentice caught me.

Not dramatically — nothing in that workshop was ever dramatic; it was a room where even catastrophe arrived smelling of ink. I had stayed late over the files, and when I came out to lock the plate cabinet, August Whitlock was standing in the doorway with a lamp, and we regarded each other across the dark like two instruments that had detected the same signal.

"You work late, Clerk Voss," the apprentice said.

"The work does not keep daylight hours, and neither, apparently, do you."

"No." A pause with weight in it. "I've seen your folio marks. On the Ferran pages. Top corner, pencil. You move them when you take the pages out."

I felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does when a building proves less solid than its facade. "You watch closely."

"Someone has to," said the apprentice, and there was nothing insolent in it. That was the worst part. It sounded like a statement of shared professional standards, a complaint about staffing.

"The pages are returned correctly," I said. "Every time. Whatever you are doing with them, you do it accurately."

"Thank you," said August Whitlock, and meant it, and that frightened me more than accusation would have. Accusation I could answer. Gratitude between two people circling the same forbidden document was a debt, and I did not have a column for it.

We stood there a moment longer. The lamp guttered. Somewhere outside, the river ice groaned — a long, deep sound, like a giant shifting in sleep, like the province clearing its throat before it says something irreversible.

"Good night, Clerk Voss," the apprentice said, and went.

I stood in the dark records room with my cipher-copy hidden in the locked drawer against my chest like a second heart, and I performed the operation I perform nightly: I reviewed the day, I verified the entries, I confirmed that everything was in order.

Everything was in order. That was precisely the problem, though I did not yet have the courage to write it down.

What I wrote instead, in the log, before blowing out the lamp, was this: *The apprentice knows I copy. The apprentice approves. Determine why.* And beneath it, after a hesitation I measured honestly at forty seconds, a second line, the first editorial remark I had ever allowed into a log:

*Determine also whether approval from that quarter is worth having.*

Outside, the ice groaned again, longer this time, and did not stop.
