## 1. The Callout Fee

The voicemail had come in at 6:47 in the morning, which told Freya Nystrom two things before he even played it: the caller was rural, and the caller was frightened. City people waited for business hours. Country people called when the thing in the walls had kept them awake all night and the coffee hadn't yet done its work on their nerve.

He listened to it twice while standing in his kitchen, a room he could have crossed in three steps if he'd bothered to hurry. The woman's voice was thin and careful, the voice of someone trying very hard to sound reasonable about something unreasonable.

"Mr. Nystrom? This is Astrid Halvorsen, out past Vester Road, the old white farmhouse with the—well, you'll see the house. We've got something under the floors. Scratching. And it's gotten into my flour, somehow, and I'd—I'd like you to come out today, if you can. Today would be good."

A pause, long enough that he almost deleted the message.

"It's not raccoons," she added, as though someone had accused her of saying so. "I know what raccoons sound like. This is—please just come."

Freya set the phone face-down on the counter beside his single clean mug and looked out the window at the parking lot of the Sundstrom Apartments, Units A through D, where his truck sat alone among the frost-silvered spaces because nobody else was up yet, or perhaps because nobody else lived here anymore, or perhaps because nobody else had ever quite lived here at all. He was forty-one years old and he had been the pest man of three counties for nineteen of them, and in that time he had learned the single most valuable trade secret there was, one no licensing board taught and no competitor had managed to copy:

Be forgettable.

It wasn't modesty. It was engineering. Freya Nystrom had a face like a closed door, a voice pitched to be nodded along to rather than remembered, and a habit of standing slightly off-center in any room, as though he were a piece of furniture someone had nudged out of true. Customers forgot him within a week. They remembered that the mice were gone, that the wasps' nest came down, that the bill had been fair. That was all they needed to remember, and all he wanted them to. He had built his whole life the way he built an exclusion barrier—tight seams, no gaps, nothing getting in, nothing getting out.

There were four voicemails on his phone besides Mrs. Halvorsen's. He didn't play them. He knew without listening that none of them required anything of him. One would be his sister, calling from her life in town, leaving the kind of message people leave when they've stopped expecting answers but can't quite stop dialing. *Hey, it's me. Nothing urgent. Call when you get a chance.* Two would be suppliers. The fourth he genuinely couldn't guess, and that small blank space in his own life felt, some mornings, like the truest thing about him.

He made coffee he drank standing up, ate toast he barely tasted, and pulled on the gray coveralls that were gray the way weather is gray—designed by God or by DuPont to pass unnoticed against barns, basements, and overcast skies. Then he took down the ledger book, wrote *Halvorsen, A. — farm callout — unknown vermin* in his small square hand, and went out into the cold.

* * *

The drive out past Vester Road took forty minutes through country that seemed to be holding its breath. October had come down hard overnight; the fields were the color of old bone, and the ditches held their first dead leaves like spilled tea. Freya drove with the window cracked because he liked the smell of this kind of morning—woodsmoke and wet gravel and the mineral cold coming off the stubble fields—and because the radio in the truck had been broken for two years and he had never once missed it.

The farmhouse announced itself before he saw it: a windbreak of bare elms, then the white clapboard rising out of a shallow rise, broad-roofed and patient, the kind of house that had been built to hold a family of ten and was now holding, if the voicemail was any measure, considerably fewer. There was a red barn with a sagging door, a pickup older than his own, and a dog—a big black-and-tan thing of indeterminate breeding—lying on the porch with its chin on its paws, watching the road with an expression Freya would later think of as *waiting*, though waiting for what, he couldn't have said.

He parked, gathered his kit, and had one boot on the bottom porch step when the front door opened.

"You're the pest man." Not a question. The woman in the doorway was sixty-odd, straight-backed, with white hair pinned up and hands that looked like they had wrung out a thousand dishcloths and buried two husbands' worth of trouble besides. Her eyes did a quick inventory of him—coveralls, clipboard, unremarkable face—and dismissed each item in turn. "Astrid Halvorsen. Come in, then. Mind the dog; he's been strange lately."

"Strange how?"

"He won't go in the cellar." She said it flatly, the way you'd report a fence post leaning. "Used to sleep down there summers, cool floor. Now he sits at the top of those stairs like they owe him money."

"I'll want to see the cellar, then."

"You'll want to see a lot of things." She stood back to let him pass, and as he moved by her she said, quieter, "I'll pay your callout fee whatever you find, Mr. Nystrom. I want that understood up front. But I'm telling you now so you don't stand in my kitchen later and explain raccoons to me like I'm simple. Whatever is in my house, it has *hands*."

Freya had heard versions of this sentence maybe six times in nineteen years. It always meant one of three things: rats dragging greasy paws across surfaces, a neighbor kid sneaking in, or a mind beginning to loosen its moorings. He gave her the nod he gave everyone—the nod that agreed with nothing and promised everything—and followed her inside.

* * *

The kitchen was warm and too clean, scrubbed the way kitchens get scrubbed when the person doing the scrubbing is scrubbing against something. A pot of coffee sat on the stove, untouched, going bitter. And on the table, laid out with the grim ceremony of evidence at an inquest, was a flour jar.

It was a big stoneware crock, the old kind, and someone had dusted its shoulders with fresh flour to make the prints show. Freya bent over it. Five-fingered impressions, pressed into the powder, small—a child's hand, or close to it. Four of them marched up the side of the crock, spaced like rungs, and the fifth was smeared, dragged, as though the hand had slipped on its way over the rim.

"When did these start?"

"Three weeks ago." Mrs. Halvorsen poured him coffee he hadn't asked for and set it down like a summons. "First just the one print, on the cellar doorframe, low down. I scrubbed it off. Thought maybe the pastor's grandkids had been through when I was at market. Then the flour. Then—" She stopped. Her jaw worked once. "Then the scratching started under the kitchen floor, every night between two and four. Not gnawing. Not running. *Scratching.* Slow. Like something writing its name over and over."

"Any entry points? Gaps in the foundation, vents?"

"I showed the last fellow who came out—he was from the county, two springs ago, for mice—all of it. You're welcome to walk the whole foundation. But Mr. Nystrom." She set both hands flat on the table, and he saw that they were not entirely steady. "Raccoons don't wipe their feet. Raccoons don't leave prints on the *inside* of a sealed crock lid. I found flour on the underside of that lid. On the *underside*. Explain that with your county training."

He couldn't, and he didn't try. Instead he did what he always did, which was to become slightly more boring than whatever was frightening her. He asked about feed storage, about the chimney, about whether anyone else lived in the house. She answered each question precisely, and it was only on the last one that her gaze flicked toward the stairs, upward, and softened into something complicated.

"My granddaughter's up in her room. Ingrid. Sixteen." A pause with weight in it. "She's been keeping notes on it. The scratching. Times, durations. She thinks I don't know about the notebook."

"Do you want me to talk to her?"

"I want you to fix my house," Mrs. Halvorsen said, and picked up the flour jar, and carried it away like a body.

* * *

The cellar door stood at the end of a short hallway, painted the same white as everything else, and it was shut. Freya stood in front of it a moment longer than he needed to, and he would have been hard put to say why. The hallway was warm. The kitchen was warm. But the air along the floorboards here had a stillness to it, a held quality, like the air in a room where someone has just stopped talking.

He opened the door. The smell came up to meet him—cold earth, coal dust gone sweet with age, and under it something faintly organic that his nineteen years catalogued automatically as *possible carcass* before amending itself to *possible nothing*. The stairs were wooden, steep, lit by a single bulb on a pull chain. At the top of them, pressed into the dust of the threshold plate, was a handprint.

Small. Five fingers. Pressed hard enough to have squeezed the dust into ridges at the edges, the way a living hand does, the way a dead hand doesn't.

Freya crouched over it for a long moment with his flashlight off, letting his eyes adjust, letting the professional machinery turn. Size suggested a child of eight to ten. Depth suggested real weight behind it. Position suggested someone—or something—had knelt here at the top of the stairs, braced one hand on the plate, and either descended or ascended. There was no corresponding print further down that he could see from this angle. There was no trail at all. Just the one, isolated, deliberate, like a signature at the bottom of a letter.

He clicked the flashlight on and went down.

The cellar was bigger than the house above it, the way old cellars are, rooms carved out over generations until the geometry stopped making sense. Shelves of preserves. A furnace that had been converted from coal sometime in the seventies and never loved since. Tool racks, mason jars, a bicycle with no tires hanging from a rafter like a fossil. Along the far wall ran the old coal chute, its steel door rusted to the color of dried blood, and beside it—his light caught it and slid past, caught it again—an area of wall that had been bricked in at some point, newer brick than the rest, mortared rough, roughly five feet wide.

His professional eye filed it under *old cold storage, sealed off, common enough* and moved on. His other eye—the one he didn't talk about, the one that had kept him alive in crawlspaces full of things with teeth—lingered on it a half-second longer than the first.

The cold down here was wrong. Not cellar-cold, which has texture and history and the smell of potatoes in it. This was a flat cold, a present-tense cold, and it pooled around the bricked alcove the way water pools around a drain. He stood in it and felt it through his coveralls and told himself, in the tone he used for clients, *pipes run behind that wall, that's all, thermal mass, old stone.*

He worked. That was the cure for everything; it always had been. He walked the perimeter with his light low, reading the dust the way other men read newspapers. Mouse runs along the baseboards, old and faint—nothing current. A spider population doing its work undisturbed. No droppings fresher than a season. No tracks in the fine silt near the chute except—

Except the prints.

They were in the condensation on the cold-water pipe, a line of small five-fingered marks following the pipe's curve, precise as a signature. They were in the dust on top of the preserve shelves, up where nothing under four feet tall should reach. And in one place, on the bare concrete floor in the middle of the room, they simply ended: a stride of prints walking from nowhere to nowhere, five steps, and then nothing, as though the walker had stepped off the edge of the world mid-stride.

Freya stood over the ending for a while. The bulb overhead buzzed. Somewhere above him, faintly, the dog began to whine.

"Okay," he said aloud, to the cellar, in his flattest voice. "Okay."

He set two snap traps along the baseboards, baited and flagged. He spread a ring of tracking powder around the bricked alcove, because whatever walked here walked *from* there or *to* there, and powder didn't lie. He checked the chute door—latched, rusted shut, undisturbed. Then he climbed the stairs, pulled the door shut behind him, and stood in the warm hallway breathing like a man who had been underwater.

Mrs. Halvorsen was waiting at the hall's end with her arms folded. "Well?"

"Traps are set. Powder's down. I'll come back day after tomorrow and we'll know what we're dealing with." He heard his own voice do its work—calm, competent, forgettable—and watched her hear it too, and watched her decide not to believe it.

"And the prints?"

"Something small gets in and out. I'll find the route." He was already packing his clipboard, already becoming furniture. "Keep the kitchen swept clean tonight. Bare floors. If it walks, it leaves a record."

At the door she stopped him with a hand on his sleeve, brief and hard. "My father-in-law built this house," she said. "My husband died in it. My son left it. And I will *not* be driven out of it by—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and finished differently. "Day after tomorrow, then."

The dog watched him go from the porch, chin on paws, eyes tracking him all the way to the truck with that patient, terrible attention. As Freya pulled onto the road he glanced in the rearview mirror and saw the farmhouse receding, white and blind-eyed in the morning light, and he saw—at least he thought he saw, at that distance, in that light—the upstairs window curtain fall back into place, as though someone had been watching him leave and had decided, now, to stop.

* * *

That night his apartment was exactly as empty as he'd left it. He ate standing at the counter. The four voicemails sat on his phone like unopened mail, and he let them sit. Outside, October rattled the parking lot with its first real wind, and the streetlight made one orange pool on the asphalt, and nothing anywhere required anything of him.

This was the arrangement. This had always been the arrangement. Nineteen years of being the man who arrives, fixes, invoices, and evaporates. He thought of it as cleanliness. Other people's problems came into his life the way pests came into houses—through gaps—and his job was to seal himself up tight after each one, caulk every seam, leave no trail. He was good at it. He was, if he was honest, the best at it he'd ever met.

So why, washing his single mug, did he keep seeing the prints ending mid-stride?

Why did he keep hearing *it has hands*, in that flat old voice?

And why—this was the one he circled longest, the one he finally set down the way you set down a hot pan—why had he written *Halvorsen, A.* in the ledger book, and beneath it, without deciding to, without noticing until later, the word *cellar*?

He went to bed early. The traps were set. The powder was down. Day after tomorrow he'd collect his data, name his animal, invoice his fee, and seal the whole business out of his life forever.

In the dark of the Sundstrom Apartments, Unit C, Freya Nystrom fell asleep to the memory of a cold that had pooled like water around a drain, and if his dreams that night were of small hands pressing flour, pressing dust, pressing condensation—five fingers, always five, patient as writing—he did not remember them in the morning, and so counted himself, as always, unaffected.

Day after tomorrow, the trap would be sprung.

It would not hold what it caught.
