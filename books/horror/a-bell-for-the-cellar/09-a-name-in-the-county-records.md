## 9. A Name in the County Records

The county archive occupied the basement of the old courthouse, which meant it occupied the one part of the building nobody had renovated since the Eisenhower administration. Freya found it by following the smell—damp paper, cold stone, the faint mineral tang of a place that had been underground long enough to forget the sun. A hand-lettered sign on the door read RECORDS & DEEDS, and beneath it, in smaller letters, someone had added HOURS: 9–4, MON–FRI, in a hand that had given up halfway through the F.

He had called ahead. He had not said why he was calling. He was good at that—saying just enough to be forgettable, a voice on a phone like a fly on a window, present and unremarkable. *Pest control, doing some background on a property. Old farm, out past the county line. Wondering about prior owners.* The clerk had hummed and said come on down, we keep everything, we're the county's attic.

The county's attic. He should have known, then, what he'd find. Attics keep what families won't.

The clerk was a woman in her sixties named Doris—her nameplate read DORIS, and beneath the name, in the same resigned hand as the sign, *I don't make the rules*. She wore a cardigan the color of weak tea and had the particular patience of someone who had spent forty years watching strangers discover how little the past cared about them.

"Property records first?" she asked. "Deeds, tax rolls, liens?"

"Deeds," Freya said. "Halvorsen farm, section fourteen. And then—" He paused. This was the part he'd rehearsed in the truck, and the rehearsal fell apart now, the way rehearsals did. "And then I'm looking for a person. Eirik Voss. Would have been in the county in the early sixties. Hired man, probably. Farm labor."

Doris looked at him over her glasses. Not suspiciously. Worse than that—knowingly. Forty years underground will teach a woman the difference between a man doing research and a man digging.

"Voss," she repeated. "There were Vosses up the valley. Lutheran family, big. Most of them moved to Minnesota after the war."

"This one wouldn't have moved."

"No," she said, and wrote the name on a slip of paper in pencil, pressing hard. "No, I don't suppose he would have."

* * *

The deed search took an hour and told him what he'd expected. The farm had passed from Halvorsen to Halvorsen like a stone passed hand to hand in a family that never opened its fist. 1911, 1946, 1979, 2003. Four transactions in a hundred years, each one between Halvorsens, each one clean, each one signed in the same slanted hand until the slant changed with the generations the way handwriting does, like a face aging.

It was the tax rolls that caught him. 1963. He ran his finger down the column of assessed improvements and found, in the clerk's spidery notation, an entry that had been struck through and re-entered: *barn roof, $180* corrected to *barn roof, $140*. And beside it, in the margin, a note in faded ink: *labor — E.V. — see ledger*.

See ledger. As if the ledger were the whole of a man. As if a life could be filed under *see*.

Freya sat with that for a long moment, in the greenish light of the fluorescent tube that buzzed like something trapped, and felt the cold from the Halvorsen cellar climb up through the memory of his hands. He had torn up those floorboards looking for a nest. He had found a bell with no rope. He had pocketed it and called it scrap iron, and his hands had shaken, and he had blamed the cold.

He was done blaming the cold.

"Missing persons," he said to Doris, when he could speak steadily. "1963, 1964. How are those filed?"

"By year reported, then alphabetically." She didn't ask why. She was already leading him down the aisle, her cardigan whispering against the boxes. "We kept them local back then. Sheriff's office has the originals, but we have copies. Most of them are nothing. Boys who ran off to the cities. Wives who walked out. One fellow in '68 who joined a commune and sent a postcard from California every Christmas for thirty years, just to spite his mother." She pulled a box, set it on the reading table, and looked at Freya with those forty-years-underground eyes. "Voss, you said."

"Voss."

The folder was thin. That was the first thing. He had expected—what? A thickness commensurate with a life? A man is a man is a man; even a short life should weigh something in paper. But the folder was thin as a hymnal, and when he opened it, the first page was a form, and the form was the kind of form that gets filled out when nobody expects to look for anyone.

*Name: Eirik Johan Voss. Age: 34. Last seen: [blank]. Reported by: [blank]. Circumstances: [blank].*

The blanks went on like that. Last known address: the Halvorsen farm, section fourteen. Employment: farmhand, Halvorsen. Date last seen: October 1963. Reported missing: March 1964—five months, and even then not by the family but by a sister, one Astrid Voss, of the valley Vosses, who had written a letter that was clipped to the form, its edges browned, its handwriting furious even through sixty years.

*He would not go without his wages. He was owed since June. He wrote to me in September that he would go to the farm and collect what was his and then come home. He never came home. Ask Halvorsen. Ask Halvorsen where my brother is.*

Ask Halvorsen.

The sheriff's notation beneath it, dated April 1964, in a hand so careless it was nearly a signature: *Interviewed A. Halvorsen re: above. States Voss left farm Oct. 12, 1963, destination unknown, owing no debts. No evidence of foul play. File closed.*

*Owing no debts.*

Freya read that line four times. The fluorescent tube buzzed. Somewhere above him, in the working courthouse, a door slammed, and the sound came down through the stone like a knock.

Owing no debts. A man who had written to his sister that he was owed since June. A man whose employer told the sheriff, with a straight face and a clean ledger, that he owed nothing and was owed nothing, and the sheriff—a man with a badge and a car and a whole county of other things to do—had written *no evidence of foul play* and closed the file, and that was that. That was sixty years of that. A man erased in a single afternoon by two sentences and a signature.

No death certificate. Doris confirmed it when he asked, her voice gone quieter. Vital records, 1963 through present: no Eirik Johan Voss. No grave, either—he had her check the cemetery registries, all of them, Lutheran and Methodist and the little county plot out past the fairgrounds. Nothing. A man who had never died because he had never been allowed to. A man who had simply, officially, ceased, the way a debt ceases when the ledger page is torn out.

The ledger. Ingrid's ledger. *Paid E. Voss, settled in full, cellar.*

He sat in the greenish light and understood, with the slow cold certainty of a man watching frost climb a window, that he was not looking at a missing persons file. He was looking at the paperwork of a murder that had never been called one. The law had come, had been fed two sentences, had swallowed them whole, and had gone away, and in going away had left the Halvorsen cellar to keep what it kept. Sixty years of flour and dust and condensation on pipes. Sixty years of fingerprints ending mid-stride. Sixty years of a bell with no rope, bricked in behind the coal chute with the man who should have heard it ring him to supper.

And the mirrors. He thought of the mirrors, lagging a half-second, then two, like the house itself was falling behind, like the whole farm was a clock winding down toward something. Toward a count. Toward a harvest under a rising moon. He didn't have a word for what he believed was happening, because he didn't believe in anything, not really—not God, not ghosts, not the persistence of anything past the last breath. But he believed in debts. Debts were real. He had built a whole life on them: callout fees, invoices, the clean arithmetic of what people owed each other for showing up.

Eirik Voss was owed.

* * *

He thanked Doris. She photocopied the file for him without being asked—three pages, thin as a hymnal—and slipped them into a manila folder, and as she handed it across the reading table she said, not looking at him, in the tone of a woman who has said this kind of thing before and been wrong:

"You'll find him?"

"I don't know what I'll find."

"Mm." She stamped the photocopies with a date stamp, the thunk of it loud in the basement quiet. "There was a Voss girl used to come in here, years back. Astrid. Came every spring for about ten years, asking the same question, and every spring we gave her the same folder, and every spring she left looking like she'd been hit." Doris closed the box. "Then she stopped coming. I always wondered if she'd found her answer or just got tired of the question." She looked up. "You're the first one since."

The first one since. Sixty years, and the first person to open the folder since the sister stopped asking. Freya stood there holding three photocopied pages and felt the weight of them all out of proportion—felt them the way you feel a key you've been handed for a door you're not sure you want to open. He had spent his whole life being the man who didn't get involved. That was the talent, the real one, the one under the pest control license: he could walk through other people's lives like a draft through a room, noticed at the door and forgotten by the hallway. Empty apartment, unanswered voicemails, a sister he hadn't called in a decade. Unremarkable. Untouched. Safe.

And now here he was in a basement, holding a dead man's name like something warm that had been pressed into his hands by a woman in a tea-colored cardigan, and he could not put it down. He tried, standing there. He genuinely tried. He thought: *photocopies, invoice, done. Tell the Halvorsens the property has a historical issue, recommend a priest, drive away.* The playbook. Seal, salt, denial. It had failed in the cellar and it failed here, in the archive, because the playbook was built for raccoons, and what he was holding had a sister who had written *ask Halvorsen* in a hand that shook with fury, and a grave that had never been dug, and a bell that had never rung.

Nobody else was going to say his name.

That was the thought that did it, the thought that broke something loose in him with an almost physical snap, like a joist giving way. The sheriff wouldn't—the file was closed, and sheriffs don't reopen files on the word of a pest controller with photocopied pages. The town wouldn't—the Vosses had scattered to Minnesota decades ago, and the Halvorsens had spent sixty years keeping the house quiet. Astrid Voss, if she still lived, was two counties away and old now, old as the crime. Ingrid was fifteen and had a notebook full of lag times and a grandmother going white at the mention of cellars. Mrs. Halvorsen would carry her father's sin to her own grave rather than speak it first.

The whole machinery of the living had conspired, across six decades, to keep one man's name out of every mouth. And the only voice left—the only one willing, the only one with a reason and a truck and a bad feeling in his gut that had stopped being professional and started being personal—was his.

Freya Nystrom, who had made a career out of not being anyone's witness.

He walked up out of the basement into the October light and it hit him like a hand—cold, clean, enormous after the greenish dark. The courthouse lawn needed raking. A flag snapped on its pole. Ordinary world, going about its business, and somewhere under section fourteen a man was waiting under the floorboards for someone to say his name out loud where the living could hear it.

Freya sat in his truck with the folder on the passenger seat and his phone in his hand, and for a long moment he did nothing but breathe.

Then he did the thing he never did. He dialed his sister. It rang twice and went to voicemail—of course it did; he was the man whose calls went unanswered, the man on the other end of the silence—and he almost hung up, the old reflex, the draft through the room.

"Margaret," he said, when the beep came. "It's Freya. I—" He stopped. Started over. "I need a favor. Your place across town. Is it still empty? I might need it for a few weeks. There's a family that—" A family that what. That had a dead man in the cellar counting toward the harvest moon. "There's a family that needs somewhere to stay. I'll explain later. Call me back."

He hung up and sat with his heart going hard, surprised at himself, surprised at the strange lightness under the dread, the way a man feels lighter after setting down something he didn't know he was carrying.

Then he turned the truck toward the Halvorsen farm, three photocopied pages riding on the seat beside him like passengers, and in the rearview mirror—just for a half-second, just long enough that he could have imagined it, and knew he hadn't—his own reflection looked back at him a beat too late, and nodded.
