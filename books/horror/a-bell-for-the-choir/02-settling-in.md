## 2. Settling In

The Harrow house took Isandro's measurements the way a stranger shakes a hand—firmly, and with something held back.

They had been at it three days before the hallway told them what it was. Isandro had walked it forty times by then, laser measure in hand, logging dimensions for the report: forty-one feet, six inches, east wing corridor, plaster walls, original pine boards. The number went into the tablet. The number did not change. But on the third morning, standing at the east end with the device warm in their palm, Isandro looked back down the corridor's length and felt, without any instrument to confirm it, that the far door was farther away than it had been yesterday.

They did what they always did. They measured again.

Forty-one feet, six inches. The same. Of course the same. Buildings did not grow. Isandro Dunmore had surveyed two hundred and eleven properties in nineteen years, and not one of them had gained so much as an inch, and this one wouldn't either, and if the light through the west windows hit the wainscoting at an angle that made the perspective lie, well, that was what old houses did. They lied with light. It was practically their profession.

Isandro wrote a note in the margin—*verify corridor length, possible settling*—and moved on, and did not think about it again until two in the morning, and then thought about it for a long time.

* * *

Moving in was a matter of procedure. Two duffel bags, a rolling case of instruments, a cooler of food, the folding cot that had slept them in a hundred empty properties. The Harrow house was vacant—had been vacant, the file said, for eleven years—and the estate's attorney had been almost too eager to grant occupancy for the assessment period. *Whatever gets it sold*, he'd said, with the particular relief of a man handing off a problem. Isandro had heard that relief before. It usually meant the house had a history the paperwork didn't capture.

They established camp in the second-floor bedroom with the best light, the one with a view over the back garden and the fence line to the Aldous place. The cot went against the wall opposite the door—Isandro always slept facing the door, a habit so old it had stopped being a choice. The recorder case went in the corner. The kettle went on the landing, where the one working outlet lived.

On the first evening they did the walk-through that would become the report's spine: every room, every wall, every window, photographed and logged. The house was a good house, structurally speaking. Sound bones under tired skin. Oak floors gone amber with age, original trim, the kind of craftsmanship that had stopped being affordable around 1920. Someone had loved this house once. The wallpaper in the parlor had been chosen, not merely applied. There were pale rectangles on the walls where pictures had hung for decades, and one set of marks, low on the kitchen doorframe, where someone had penciled heights.

Isandro crouched at the doorframe and read them. *Wren*, in a careful adult hand, and then dates climbing up the wood in half-year increments, the last one stopping at a height no taller than Isandro's own hip.

*Wren Harrow*, the file had said. Last Harrow. The house had passed through four families since, none of whom had stayed more than a handful of years, and then to the estate, and then to eleven years of silence.

Families left old houses all the time. Isandro logged the doorframe, photographed it, and told themselves the small cold feeling in their chest was the draft coming under the kitchen door.

* * *

The doors were the first real trouble.

Isandro had a system, and the system was this: every door in an assessed property gets closed at night. Open doors are how heat escapes, how drafts travel, how a two-week energy model gets skewed. So each evening, before bed, Isandro walked the house and shut every door—fourteen interior doors, three exterior, one cellar hatch. It took four minutes. They knew because they timed it, the way they timed everything, because a surveyor who doesn't time things is a surveyor whose numbers mean nothing.

On the fourth morning, three doors stood open.

The parlor. The linen closet on the landing. The door to the room Isandro had designated, in the report's draft, as *Bedroom 4—water damage, ceiling*.

They stood on the landing with their coffee going cold and looked at the three doors and felt the old familiar machinery start up, the machinery that had kept them alive and unremarkable for twenty years. Doors swell. Latches wear. A house that's been empty eleven years has doors that were never properly hung after the last renovation, and temperature differentials overnight will swing a loose door on its hinges like a slow hand pushing. It was physics. It was, Isandro told themselves, almost certainly physics.

*Almost certainly* was a phrase Isandro Dunmore had worn smooth as a river stone. Almost certainly the discrepancy in the safety reports was a clerical error. Almost certainly Mr. Okafor understood the risk he was taking when he signed. Almost certainly no one would trace the anonymous statements back to a junior surveyor who had learned to stand at the edge of every room, to speak only when spoken to, to leave no impression on any surface. Almost certainly.

They closed the doors again. That evening they closed them again. On the sixth morning, all fourteen were open, standing wide, and the cellar hatch was up too, and the cold coming up out of the cellar stairs was the cold of a held breath.

Isandro stood at the top of the cellar stairs for a long moment, coffee untouched, and then—because the report needed cellar documentation anyway, and because the alternative to going down was admitting there was a reason not to—they went down.

* * *

The cellar was brick and stone and colder than it had any right to be in September. Isandro's headlamp made a small bright world that moved with them: foundation walls sweating mineral bloom, the boiler dead in its corner like a fossilized animal, shelving with nothing on it. The floor was packed earth and old flagstone, and Isandro walked it slowly, measuring the perimeter by paces, logging cracks.

That was when the headlamp caught the chalk.

It was in the northeast corner, in the angle where two foundation walls met, and at first Isandro took it for a child's scrawl, some long-ago game, the kind of thing that survives in cellars the way fossils survive in shale. But it was too deliberate for scrawl. It was a hopscotch grid—ten squares, drawn in lines still white and sharp against the stone, numbered one through ten in a careful child's hand. The numbers were small and exact. Whoever had drawn them had cared about getting them right.

Beside the grid, in the same chalk, the same hand: *1891*.

Isandro crouched. The chalk lines were not faded. That was the thing their mind kept snagging on, the thing the machinery tried and failed to file away. Chalk on a cellar floor, a hundred and thirty years old, should have been ghost-ghost, should have been nothing, should have been worn to a smear by the first damp decade. These lines were bright. They looked drawn yesterday.

They looked, Isandro thought, and immediately wished they hadn't, like they were waiting to be used.

They photographed the grid. They measured it—the squares were child-sized, each one barely eight inches across, a game scaled for someone the height of the pencil marks on the kitchen doorframe. They logged the date, the location, the condition, and their hand was steady the whole time, because their hands were always steady, because steadiness was the first thing they'd taught themselves after the trial, after Okafor, after the day they understood that the safest person in any room is the one no one remembers is in it.

*Chalk markings, NE corner, cellar*, the log read. *Historic. Child's game. Dated 1891. Condition: unusually preserved.*

*Unusually preserved.* Isandro looked at the words and heard how they sounded. They deleted *unusually*. Then, after a moment, they put it back. The report was a report. It said what it said.

* * *

The cold spots came next, and the cold spots were almost a relief, because cold spots had explanations. Every surveyor knew them: air leaks, thermal bridging, uninsulated cavities where convection pulled warmth out of a room like a drain. Isandro mapped them with the infrared camera, professionally, and the camera gave them what the camera always gave them—blue blooms on the screen where the house leaked its heat into the night.

Except the blooms didn't correspond to anything. There was one at the head of the stairs that had no window, no vent, no exterior wall behind it. There was one in the parlor, dead center, four feet off the floor, hanging in the middle of the room like a hole cut in the air. Isandro walked the parlor with the camera up and the bloom stayed where it was, fixed in the empty space, and Isandro stood inside it once, deliberately, the way you touch a stove to check it's off.

The cold went through them like a hand through water. Not wind-cold. Still cold. The cold of a room where someone has just stopped speaking.

*Thermal anomaly, parlor center, no identified source*, the log said. Isandro had written stranger things in defect reports. They had once documented a house where every pipe in the west wall hummed a minor third. Houses were full of wrongness; that was the job. The trick—the survival trick, the twenty-year trick—was to keep the wrongness at arm's length, on paper, in numbers, in the safe grammar of *anomaly* and *unverified* and *requires further investigation*.

The trick was to never be the thing that was wrong. Only to notice it.

* * *

On the eighth night, Isandro lay on the cot listening to the house settle and made a list, the way they made lists of everything. Doors: open. Cold spots: unmapped to any cause. Corridor: possibly, impossibly, wrong. Chalk: too bright. And underneath the list, the thing they weren't writing down, which was that the house felt *attended*. Not haunted—that word was for other people, for tourists, for the kind of person who wanted a house to be more than lumber and plaster. Attended. The way a room feels different when someone is in it, even before you see them. The way a street feels different when footsteps behind you are keeping your pace.

Isandro had spent twenty years being the footsteps no one noticed. They knew what attention felt like from the receiving end, and the Harrow house was paying attention to them.

They lay in the dark and did the breathing exercise, four counts in, six counts out, and told themselves: two more weeks. Twelve days. The report gets filed, the check clears, and you are somewhere else, unsigned, unremarked, another name that passed through Vell's Crossing without leaving a mark. That was the whole architecture of their life. That was the load-bearing wall.

The house creaked. Somewhere below, in the direction of the cellar, a sound like a small foot set down carefully on flagstone.

Isandro lay still and did not log it, because there was no instrument running, because a sound without a recording is not data, because what you don't write down can't be counted.

The house was quiet the rest of the night. But in the morning, when Isandro went down to make coffee, the hopscotch grid in the cellar corner had changed. The chalk numbers were the same, the date was the same, the careful child's hand was the same. But there was a small stone sitting in the first square, placed dead center, the way a player places a marker before the throw.

And Isandro stood at the top of the cellar stairs with the cold rising past them like a question, and for the first time in twenty years, the machinery would not start. There was no explanation waiting. There was only the grid, and the stone, and the terrible professional certainty of a surveyor who has measured everything in this house except what is measuring them back.

That evening, they unpacked the audio recorders.
