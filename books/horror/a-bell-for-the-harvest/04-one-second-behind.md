## 4. One Second Behind

The house on Larkspur Lane had a way of holding sound the way a cupped hand holds water—everything pooled and slightly too close. Jonah noticed it her first night back from the hardware store, standing in the hallway with a paper bag of picture hooks and painter's tape, when she said "hello" to the empty rooms out of some half-ironic habit of hers, *hello, new house*, and heard the word come back to her a fraction too slowly, like an echo that had to think about it first.

She'd laughed at herself then. Old houses did that. Settling wood, plaster acting as a drumhead, all the ordinary acoustics of a structure that had been breathing for ninety years.

She was not laughing on Thursday night.

* * *

It started, or she noticed it starting, in the bathroom mirror while she brushed her teeth. The vanity light above the mirror was one of those old frosted globes that buzzed faintly, a sound like a trapped insect, and under that buzz Jonah watched a woman brush her teeth in a small yellow bathroom, and everything was fine, everything was normal, until she raised her left hand to test the water temperature and the woman in the glass raised hers a beat too late.

Not a full second. Less. The kind of delay you'd never catch unless you were looking for it—and Jonah hadn't been looking for it, that was the thing, she'd simply seen it, the way you see a typo in a paragraph you've read a hundred times. Her hand went up. The reflection's hand went up. Between the two motions there was a gap, thin as the space between film frames, and in that gap something in her hindbrain screamed *wrong* so loudly that she spat toothpaste into the sink and stood there gripping the porcelain with both hands, heart slamming, staring at her own face.

The face stared back. Perfectly synchronized now. Perfectly innocent.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud was how she handled herself; it was a trick she'd learned in graduate school, talking herself down from the ledges of her own imagination. "Okay. You're tired. It's been a long week. You've been reading about perceptual lag and saccadic masking and—" She stopped, because she hadn't been reading about those things, actually, but she knew they existed, knew the mind was a rumor mill that manufactured ghosts out of processing delays. The brain edited reality constantly. It filled in the blind spot. It backdated perceptions to make the world seem seamless. There were documented cases—she was almost sure there were documented cases—of people perceiving their own actions as delayed, of the famous experiments where a ticking clock seemed to slow—

She was reaching for the name of the experiment and couldn't find it, and that absence annoyed her more than anything else, and she went to bed telling herself firmly that tomorrow she would look it up, and that this was what skepticism looked like: not the absence of wonder but the discipline of it.

She lay in the dark listening to the house hold its breath around her, and she did not think about the mirror, except that she thought about nothing else.

* * *

Friday evening she came home with a stopwatch.

Not a real stopwatch—a cheap digital kitchen timer from the hardware store on Merton Street, four dollars and ninety-nine cents, with a big red button and a display that read hundredths of a second. She'd also bought a taper candle, white, twelve inches, and a box of wooden matches, and if the teenage clerk had wondered why the new schoolteacher was buying the equipment for a séance, he'd had the good manners not to say so.

She set up in the bathroom, because the vanity mirror was where she'd seen it, and because the bathroom had good flat lighting and a door she could shut against the rest of the house.

The plan was simple. Simple enough to be embarrassing, which was part of its virtue: if she couldn't explain the phenomenon, she could at least measure it, and measurement was the beginning of explanation, and explanation was the death of dread. That was the whole architecture of her life. That was the load-bearing wall.

Test one: the candle.

She lit the match, touched it to the wick, and watched the flame catch in the mirror. A flame is a good test object—it flickers, it stutters, it's never the same shape twice, and any lag between the flame and its image would show up as a kind of doubling, a ghost-flame trailing the real one the way a comet trails its tail. She held the candle up beside her head and stared.

The flame burned. Its twin burned. They moved together, stuttered together, leaned together when she breathed near them. If there was a lag, it was below the threshold of her eye, and she sat on the edge of the tub feeling both relieved and obscurely cheated, the way you feel when the monster doesn't show up for the photograph.

"All right," she told the mirror. "You get a pass."

Then she picked up the timer, and the mirror showed her picking up the timer, and she pressed the big red button, and—

There.

It was nothing. It was less than nothing. But she saw it, or she believed she saw it, which for the purposes of the exercise amounted to the same thing: the moment the display changed from 00.00 to 00.01, the reflected display changed a hair behind it, a sliver of time so thin she couldn't have sworn to it under oath, but thick enough that her stomach dropped through the floor of her certainty like a coin through ice.

She did it again. Press, release, press. Watched the numbers climb. And again she had the impression—the *impression*, she made herself use the word, she was rigorous with herself even alone in a yellow bathroom at seven p.m.—that the glass ran a little late. That somewhere between the world and its image, a very small amount of time went missing, or was held back, or was being *saved up* for something.

"Perceptual lag," she said to the mirror, and the mirror said it back to her, and she could not tell—could not *tell*, and that was the sentence that frightened her—if the echo arrived on time.

* * *

She spent Saturday doing what skeptics do, which is research, and what skeptics also do, which is build increasingly elaborate traps for their own imaginations.

The traps looked like this: a video camera borrowed from the school's media cart, propped on a stack of books, filming the vanity mirror while she performed a series of movements she'd choreographed on index cards. Raise right hand. Turn head left. Drop a pencil. Clap once. She filmed ten minutes of it, then another ten with the camera pointed at the hallway mirror, which was older than the vanity, beveled glass gone slightly gray at the edges like frost on a window.

Then she sat at her kitchen table with her laptop and watched the footage frame by frame, dragging the scrubber along in increments of a thirtieth of a second, and here is what she found:

Nothing.

The video showed her raising her hand and the mirror raising it simultaneously, to within the resolution of thirty frames per second, which is to say within a thirty-third of a second, which is to say well below anything a human eye could reliably detect. The pencil fell and its twin fell. The clap clapped. The hallway mirror was equally blameless. She watched the whole thing twice, then a third time at quarter speed, and by the end she was leaning so close to the screen that her breath fogged it, hunting for a discrepancy that refused to exist.

Which meant one of two things. Either the lag wasn't real—an artifact of her own perception, a trick of attention, the brain's editing suite showing her a rough cut instead of the final print—or the lag was real and lived only in mirrors, only in the direct line between her eyes and the glass, and vanished the moment a camera interposed itself, the way certain phenomena refuse to be photographed, the way—

The way nothing does, she told herself. Nothing refuses to be photographed. That's not physics, that's folklore.

But she wrote it down anyway, in the notebook she'd started keeping since moving to Hollow Creek, the notebook whose early pages held notes on her students' reading levels and whose later pages had begun, without her quite noticing the transition, to hold other things. *Vanity mirror: lag suspected, ~0.1–0.3s, unconfirmed. Hallway mirror: same. Video: no lag detected.* Underneath, in smaller writing, as if the pen had been ashamed of it: *Candle flame moved wrong at 11:40pm. Not lagged. Wrong.*

She didn't remember writing that last line. She must have written it—her handwriting, her pen, her notebook—but she had no memory of the candle behaving wrongly at 11:40, no memory of what *wrongly* would even mean for a candle, and she sat looking at the words for a long time with the particular cold feeling of finding a note in your own hand that reads *don't trust me.*

* * *

Sunday night she tried the stopwatch properly.

She'd thought about it all day, through laundry and lesson plans and a phone call with her sister that she'd cut short without meaning to, distracted, restless, her eyes drifting to the hallway mirror every few minutes like a tongue returning to a sore tooth. Her sister had asked if the town was treating her well and Jonah had said yes, everyone was very friendly, and it was true, everyone was friendly the way wax fruit is ripe. She hadn't mentioned the mirror. She couldn't have said why. Some part of her understood, with the wordless clarity of instinct, that mentioning it would be a mistake—not embarrassing, *dangerous*—and she filed that understanding away in the drawer where she kept things she'd examine later, and the drawer was getting full.

At nine o'clock she stood before the vanity mirror with the kitchen timer in her hand and gave herself instructions out loud, so there'd be a record, so there'd be a witness, even if the witness was only her own voice on her own phone's recorder, propped against the tissue box.

"Test protocol three," she said. "Randomized motor response. I will watch the timer's display in the mirror, not directly. When I see the display cross each whole second, I will raise my hand. If my perception of the mirror is lagged, my hand should rise *late* relative to the true display—which I can verify afterward against the audio recording, because the button click is audible."

She was aware, distantly, of how absurd she sounded, this woman narrating her own experiment to a bathroom, and she was aware of something else too, closer in, colder: that she wanted the lag to be real. That beneath the methodology, beneath the index cards and the protocols, some stubborn animal part of her *hoped*, because a measurable anomaly was survivable in a way an unmeasurable one was not. A lag could be fixed. A lag could be explained, diagnosed, dismissed. What could not be survived was the alternative—that the glass was fine and she was not.

She started the timer. She watched the mirrored display.

One. Her hand rose.

Two. Her hand rose.

Three. Four. Five, and her arm was tiring, and the numbers climbed in their small green digits, six, seven, and she was watching them with the flat focused stare of a marksman, eight, nine, and the bathroom was very quiet except for the buzz of the frosted globe overhead, ten, eleven—

And at thirteen seconds, the reflection raised its hand when she had not.

She stopped. Everything stopped. Her hand hung at her side; her breath hung in her chest; the timer kept counting, fourteen, fifteen, indifferent, and in the mirror the woman with the timer stood with her arm slack at her side exactly as Jonah stood, and yet for one instant—one frame, one sliver, one impossible heartbeat—Jonah had seen that woman's hand come up first. Before hers. Ahead of it.

A lag runs behind. That was the whole point of a lag. Things that trail cannot lead.

Her hand rose, belatedly, on its own, as if remembering its assignment, and the reflection's rose with it, matched, innocent, and Jonah stood in the yellow light with her heart going like a fist on a door and made herself say, in a voice that only shook a little:

"Protocol error. Perceptual artifact. Do it again."

She did it again. She did it five more times, arms burning, voice getting flatter and harder, and four of the five trials were clean, synchronized, boring. On the fifth, at twenty-two seconds, she saw it again—the leading motion, the hand that rose before hers rose, a gesture arriving from somewhere ahead of her, as though the woman in the glass were tired of waiting and had decided to go first.

Then she turned off the recorder and listened to it twice, and the recording captured her voice and the buzz of the light and the small clicks of the button, and at 0:41 she heard herself say "there," sharp, startled—and there was nothing on the recording to explain it. No anomaly she could point to. Just her own voice crying wolf in an empty room.

* * *

She took the candle into the bedroom at midnight, because the bedroom mirror was the oldest one, a tall cheval glass with a dark walnut frame that had come with the house, and because she was past caring about sensible places to be sensible in.

The wick caught. The flame stood up straight and threw her shadow long across the floorboards, and she held the candle at arm's length and watched the fire and its reflection burn side by side, twin flames, and she waited for the wrongness her own handwriting had warned her about.

For a long time, nothing. The flames breathed together. The wax wept together.

And then, as she watched, the reflected flame leaned.

Not with her breath—she was holding her breath, she realized, had been holding it for some time. Not with any draft; the window was latched, the door was shut. The real flame stood perfectly still, a bright teardrop on its black wick, and the flame in the cheval glass bent sideways, slowly, gracefully, the way grass bends under something walking through it, and held that bend for the length of one long exhale, and then stood back up, and the two flames were twins again, and the room smelled of smoke and cold dust.

Jonah set the candle down on the dresser with hands that were steadier than she had any right to expect. In the glass, her face floated pale above the flame, and she looked at it—at herself—and made herself ask the question out loud, quietly, the way you'd ask it of a person rather than a piece of furniture.

"What are you waiting for?"

The reflection mouthed the words with her. Of course it did. That was what reflections did.

But she had the sudden, unshakable sense—arriving whole, the way dread arrives, without argument or evidence—that she had asked the wrong question. That the thing in the glass was not waiting. That it was *counting*. That all week, in every silvered surface in the house, something had been keeping time with her, matching her move for move, second for second, patient as harvest, and that the lag she'd measured was not a defect in the glass or a glitch in her brain but a seam—the visible edge of something being carefully, deliberately withheld. Held back. Saved up.

The candle guttered. She blew it out, and the darkness rushed in, and in the cheval glass her own shape stood in the dark bedroom, and she stood there a long moment, watching it stand there, watching it watch her, and just before she turned away she could have sworn—could have *sworn*—that it turned away first.

She slept with the closet light on, which she told herself was about the unfamiliar house, the strange sounds, the ordinary machinery of a mind refusing to settle. In the morning she would call Edie Marsh, the custodian, who had told her to count chairs and not children, and who looked at Jonah sometimes with an expression she hadn't known how to read until now, and recognized suddenly, horribly, as pity.

In the morning she would ask Edie about the mirrors.

Because she had remembered, lying awake at two a.m. with the light buzzing, the thing she'd forgotten to remember: on her first day, unpacking boxes in the empty hallway, she had caught her reflection in the cheval glass and waved at it, stupidly, playfully, *hello, new house*—and the woman in the glass had already had her hand down.
