## 6. Milo

The boy in the third row never raised his hand.

Jonah noticed it the way she noticed most things—methodically, without urgency. She'd been at Hollow Creek Elementary for three weeks now, long enough to learn the rhythms of her classroom: the way Cara Whitfield narrated everything under her breath like a sports commentator; the way Deacon Ruiz could turn any worksheet into a paper airplane inside forty seconds; the way the afternoon light came through the east windows and turned the dust motes into a slow golden snowfall that made even her skeptical heart soften toward this strange, too-polite town.

And the way Milo Fenwick sat perfectly still through all of it, drawing.

She'd let it go at first. Art was a legitimate coping mechanism, and God knew some of these kids needed one. But by the fourth week she'd started keeping a private tally, and the tally said: Milo drew during reading, during math, during recess when the other children streamed out to the playground like water finding its level. He drew with a focus that wasn't daydreaming—it was transcription. Like he was copying something down before he forgot it.

His work was fine otherwise. Quietly brilliant, actually. His reading comprehension was two grades ahead. When she called on him, he answered in a soft, precise voice and then immediately looked back down at his desk, as if speaking had cost him something he needed to recover.

It was the drawing that kept snagging her attention. Because it was always the same thing.

* * *

On a gray Thursday in early October, she stayed after class to grade spelling tests and watched him through the window of the empty room. No—that was wrong. He was still there. That was the point. The buses had come and gone twenty minutes ago, and Milo Fenwick was still sitting at his desk in the emptying building, crayon moving in small, careful strokes.

She stepped back into the classroom. "Milo. Your bus left."

He didn't startle. That was the first odd thing. Most kids, caught alone after school by a teacher's voice out of nowhere, flinched like they'd been shot. Milo just looked up with calm gray eyes and said, "I know. My mom works late on Thursdays. I'm supposed to wait."

"Supposed to wait here? In the classroom?"

"The office has a bell." He said it matter-of-factly, but his hand slid over his paper—not hiding it exactly, more like shielding it. The way you'd shield a candle flame.

"Well, I can't leave you here unattended. Come sit up front where I can keep an eye on you while I grade." She softened it with a smile. "You can keep drawing. As long as you tell me what you're drawing someday."

He considered this with the gravity of a judge weighing a verdict. Then he gathered his crayons—he kept them in an ice cream pint container, colors worn down to stubs—and moved to the front row, directly beside her desk, and went back to work.

Jonah graded six tests before curiosity beat professionalism. She glanced sideways.

The schoolhouse. Of course. Every kid in Hollow Creek drew the schoolhouse eventually; it was the tallest building in town, a hundred and ten years old, all brick and tall windows, with the kind of storybook proportions that made adults nostalgic and children feel small. Milo's version was better than most—accurate, lovingly detailed, right down to the crack in the foundation on the north side that Jonah had tripped over her first week.

But there was a bell tower on his schoolhouse. A steeple, really, rising from the roof ridge, with an arched opening and a bell hanging inside it, drawn in careful crosshatched shadow so it looked heavy, look real, like you could hear it.

There was no bell tower on Hollow Creek Elementary. Jonah had checked the historical photos in the front office on her second day—the place had never had one, not even in the oldest picture from 1911. Flat roof. Chimney. No steeple.

"Milo," she said carefully, "that's beautiful. Is that our school?"

"Yes." He didn't look up.

"I don't remember our school having a bell."

"It doesn't." He shaded the arch above the bell, deepening the dark. "Not anymore. It's under the ground now."

Jonah set down her pen. "Under the ground."

"They buried it." He said it the way children say things that are simply true: the sky is blue, water is wet, the bell is buried. "A long time ago. But she can still ring it when it's time."

"She?"

Milo finally looked up at her, and something in his expression made the hair on Jonah's arms lift—a patience far too old for eight years old, a sadness that belonged to someone who had been waiting a very long time.

"You'll hear it," he said. "Everybody does. Right before."

"Right before what?"

The intercom on Principal Voss's desk crackled to life across the building, and Voss's voice, warm and smooth as always, announced that Milo's mother was in the pickup lane. Milo closed his crayon box, tucked his drawing into the front pocket of his backpack with the tenderness other kids reserved for phones, and stood.

"Thanks for waiting with me, Ms. Galloway," he said at the door. And then, without turning around: "Don't count the chairs."

He was gone before she could ask him what he meant.

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep. She told herself it was the coffee, then the season changing, then the house settling—her rented house with its too-many rooms and its mirrors she'd started avoiding without quite admitting why. Finally she got up, made tea she wouldn't drink, and sat at the kitchen table with a legal pad, because this was how she processed things: she wrote them down, and once they were written down, they became data, and data couldn't hurt her.

*Milo F., age 8. Draws the same image repeatedly: schoolhouse + nonexistent bell tower. Claims the bell is "buried" and that "she" rings it "when it's time." Knows about the chairs.*

She stopped. Crossed out the last line. Wrote it again. *Knows about the chairs.* Edie Marsh, the custodian, had told her that in week one, in that flat voice of hers, wiping down the art sink: *Count the chairs, honey, not the children.* Jonah had filed it as small-town eccentricity, the verbal equivalent of a horseshoe over a door.

Now a child was saying it back to her.

She thought about the roster. Twenty-seven names, twenty-six students, and the empty desk in the back row that never collected dust no matter how many weeks passed. She'd asked Voss about it in her second week and watched him perform a deflection so smooth it deserved its own TED talk—*oh, we keep the class size flexible, district paperwork, you know how it is*—and she'd accepted it because accepting it was easier, because she was new, because she wanted this job, because the whole town exhaled a warmth that made questions feel rude.

She was a skeptic. Skepticism was supposed to mean you didn't accept things. Somewhere along the way, she realized, she'd confused skepticism with comfort—assuming that because ghosts and vanishing students and lagging mirrors were improbable, they were therefore impossible, and therefore anything odd she saw must have a boring explanation. It was a comfortable faith. It required no evidence at all.

The tea went cold. Around two in the morning she heard, or thought she heard, a faint metallic tone from somewhere beneath the floor—so brief she might have dreamed it, a single soft note like a spoon tapped against a glass, felt more in her sternum than her ears.

She sat very still for a long time.

In the hallway mirror, her reflection sat very still too—one heartbeat behind, if she was honest, which she was trying harder to be lately.

* * *

Friday morning she did something she hadn't done since her own elementary days: she pulled a chair up next to a student's desk instead of standing over him.

"Milo. Can I see what you're working on?"

He hesitated, then turned his notebook around. The schoolhouse again—but different this time. This time the bell tower was cracked, the steeple split down the middle like a broken tooth, and the bell itself was falling, mid-drop, rendered in that same careful crosshatch. Below the school, in the earth, he'd drawn roots—or ropes, she couldn't tell which—descending into a darkness he'd colored so thoroughly the paper had gone soft and waxy under the pressure.

"That's new," she said.

"It changes." He shrugged, a small, tired motion. "Sometimes it's whole. Sometimes it's falling. Sometimes—" He flipped forward three pages, and she made herself look. The same schoolhouse, but the windows were filled with faces, dozens of them, small round faces pressed against the glass from the inside, their mouths open in shapes that could have been singing or screaming. One face in the back row was smudged, rubbed out with a thumb until the paper pilled.

Jonah's mouth had gone dry. "Milo, who are these kids?"

"Everybody." He said it gently, like she was the one who needed comforting. "Every year everybody sings. You have to sing so she knows you're here."

"*She* knows—"

"Ms. Galloway?" Cara Whitfield's hand shot up from across the room. "Can I go to the bathroom?"

"In a minute, Cara, I'm talking with Milo."

"But I gotta go *now*, it's an emergency—"

"Cara. One minute."

Cara rolled her eyes with theatrical suffering and slumped back. When Jonah turned around, Milo had closed the notebook. His hands lay flat on top of it, protective.

"Milo," she said, lowering her voice. "When you say *she*. Who is she? Is she a person? Someone you've seen?"

He thought about it honestly, head tilted, which she appreciated. Kids could smell a fake question at fifty yards, and she refused to ask any.

"She's under the school," he said finally. "Under the floor, under the boiler room, way way down. She's old. Older than the school. She's lonely." A pause. "That's why she invites people."

Jonah felt cold move up her spine, slow as syrup. "Invites people where?"

"To live with her. So she won't be lonely anymore." He picked up his orange crayon—the shortest one, worn to a nub—and held it without coloring. "My mom says I shouldn't talk about it. Everybody says that. But you asked, and you're the only one who counts the chairs, so."

The room tilted slightly. Jonah gripped the edge of his desk, casually, she hoped.

"Milo. Who told you I count the chairs?"

"Edie." He smiled then, the first full smile she'd ever gotten from him, and it transformed his whole face into ordinary childhood, and somehow that was worse. "Edie remembers stuff. She says somebody has to."

The bell rang for first period. Around them the classroom filled with noise and motion, twenty-six children flowing into their seats like water finding its level, and Milo turned forward and became, instantly, seamlessly, just a quiet boy in the third row.

But as Jonah walked back to the board, she heard him murmur something so softly she almost missed it, half to himself, half—to whom?

"*Please don't pick me this year.*"

* * *

She found Edie at lunch, mopping the cafeteria line with the grim economy of a woman who'd been doing it for thirty years. Jonah took the mop handle from her, and Edie let her, which was as close to intimacy as the custodian ever allowed.

"The boy in my third row," Jonah said quietly. "Milo Fenwick. He draws a bell tower on the schoolhouse. He says the bell is buried underneath. He says *she* rings it when it's time, and everyone sings so she knows they're here." She kept her voice level. "Edie, what is happening in this town?"

Edie's hands went still on the cart. For a moment her face did something complicated—grief and fear and a terrible weary recognition chasing each other like weather. Then it settled into the pleasant blankness Jonah had learned to distrust.

"Kids draw funny things," Edie said. "Always have."

"He knows about the chairs, Edie. He knows I count them. He said *please don't pick me this year*."

The mop bucket's wheels squeaked as someone's cart bumped it in the hall. Edie looked at Jonah for a long moment, and Jonah watched her decide something—watched the decision move through her like a stone dropping through dark water.

"Not here," Edie murmured. "Saturday. The laundromat on Kettle Street closes at six. After that, it's just us and the dryers." She resumed mopping, eyes forward, voice light as air. "Bring nothing. Tell nobody. And Ms. Galloway—" the first time she'd used her name, and it landed like a hand on Jonah's shoulder— "when the boy draws the bell falling? You come find me *that same day*. Not Saturday. That day."

Jonah's throat tightened. "Why?"

"Because that's how she tells us who's invited." Edie wrung the mop with steady, practiced hands, staring at the gray water circling the drain. "And that boy's drawings have been getting closer every week."

* * *

That evening Jonah sat at her kitchen table with Milo's file open in front of her—attendance record, emergency contacts, the thin folder of a child whose life fit in four pages. Mother: Dana Fenwick, night-shift nurse. Father: blank line. Allergies: penicillin. Reading level: exceptional. Notes from previous teachers, year after year, in different handwriting:

*Quiet. Keeps to himself.*
*Very imaginative. Drawings are unusual but harmless.*
*Sweet boy. Seems to carry a lot of worry for his age.*

Carry a lot of worry. Year after year after year, the same note in different hands, and not one of those teachers—herself included, until this week—had thought to ask what an eight-year-old had to worry about in a town with no crime, no poverty worth naming, no visible trouble at all.

She thought of his notebook. The bell falling. The faces in the windows. *Please don't pick me this year.*

Jonah had come to Hollow Creek telling herself a comfortable story: that she would teach her year, keep her head down, save money, move on. An observer. A tourist with a lesson plan. She'd built her whole adult life on the principle that other people's mysteries were not her problems—that detachment was wisdom, that caring less was surviving more.

But detachment, she understood now, looking at that thin folder, was just another word for letting it happen to someone else.

She got out a fresh sheet of paper and wrote at the top, in her neatest print: *MILO — WHAT WE KNOW.* Underneath: *The bell is real. The counting is real. Something under the school takes one child every harvest, and everyone in this town except maybe Edie Marsh has agreed to forget.*

Her pen hovered. Her hand, she noticed distantly, was shaking.

Outside, the October wind moved through the maples, and from somewhere far below the floorboards—below the foundation, below the frost line, below anything reasonable—came a sound so faint she could deny it if she wanted to.

A single, patient hum. Like a bell deciding whether to ring.
