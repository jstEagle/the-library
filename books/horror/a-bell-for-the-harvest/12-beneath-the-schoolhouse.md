## 12. Beneath the Schoolhouse

The boiler room smelled of rust and old heat, a smell Jonah had come to associate with the school's lower floor—the way the building seemed to hold its breath down here, the way the pipes ticked in the walls like something counting.

She hadn't meant to bring Milo. That was the thing she would turn over later, in the weeks when turning things over was still possible: she had meant to come alone, after the last bus, when the halls emptied and she could poke at the school's secrets without an audience. But Milo had been waiting by the stairwell door at three-fifteen, backpack hugged to his chest like a shield, and when she'd asked why he wasn't on the bus he'd said, simply, "You're going down there, aren't you. To the bell."

And she had looked at this small, serious boy—the one who drew the schoolhouse with a bell tower that didn't exist, who had told her *she rings it when it's time* in the flat, unbothered voice of a child stating the weather—and she had not been able to lie to him. Something in his face made lying feel like a cruelty. So instead she'd said, "You should be on that bus, Milo."

"I know," he said, and didn't move.

In the end she let him come, because the alternative was leaving him standing in an empty hallway with that look on his face, and because—she admitted it to herself as she unlocked the boiler room with the janitor's key Edie had pressed into her hand a week ago, saying only *you'll know when*—because she wanted a witness. She was tired of being the only person in Hollow Creek who saw the seams. A seven-year-old who drew phantom bells was, at least, a seam-seer.

The boiler room was a long low chamber of bare cinderblock, lit by two caged bulbs that buzzed at a frequency Jonah felt in her back teeth. The boiler itself squatted at the far end like a sleeping animal, its iron skin radiating a dry, patient warmth. Pipes ran along the ceiling in bundled arteries, wrapped in insulation that had grayed to the color of old hair. Somewhere water dripped in a slow, metronomic rhythm—*drip, drip, drip*—and Jonah caught herself matching her breathing to it before she made herself stop.

"Stay where I can see you," she said.

Milo nodded. He had gone very quiet—not frightened-quiet, she noticed, but the quiet of someone entering a room they've been in before. He stood near the door with his hands in his pockets, and his eyes went around the room once, unhurried, cataloguing, and settled on the far wall behind the boiler with the certainty of a compass needle finding north.

"It's warmer over there," he said.

"What is?"

"The wall." He pointed.

Jonah followed his finger. Behind the boiler, where the cinderblock ran to the ceiling in the ordinary way of basement walls, there was a section that was not cinderblock. It took her a moment to see it, and the seeing was unpleasant—the way an optical illusion is unpleasant, once your eye resolves the wrong image. It was brick. Old brick, laid in courses too fine and too regular for a boiler room, mortared with something that had gone the color of dried blood. Someone had built a wall across a doorway and then, apparently, hoped the boiler would grow over it like a scar over a wound. The boiler's pipes ran directly across the bricks, and the insulation had been cut and fitted around them with a care that struck her as almost reverent. Nobody had wanted this wall exposed. Nobody had wanted it hidden either. They had wanted it *forgotten*, and there was a difference, and the difference had a temperature.

Because Milo was right. The air near the wall was warmer—not boiler-warm, not dry heat, but a warmth with a pulse to it, faint but rhythmic, like standing near the open door of an oven, or like pressing your palm flat against the chest of something very large and very slow.

"Mr. Galloway's key opens the storage cage," Milo said. "I checked. But there's no key for that."

"No," Jonah said. "There wouldn't be."

She approached the wall slowly, her sneakers scuffing on the concrete, and the dripping behind her kept its time—*drip, drip*—and the caged bulbs buzzed, and the boiler ticked its iron ticks, and underneath all of it, so faint she might have invented it, was a hum.

She stopped a foot from the bricks.

The hum was not mechanical. She knew mechanical hums; she'd grown up above a laundromat and had slept her whole childhood inside the chord of dryers and compressors. Mechanical hums lived in surfaces—in the soles of your feet, in your fillings. This hum lived in the air itself, in the space in front of the wall, as though the air were a membrane that had been struck and was still, after decades, ringing. A bell note, she thought. A bell note below the range where bells are allowed to live, down where sound stops being heard and starts being felt, a pressure behind the sternum, a fullness in the ears like descending in an unpressurized plane.

"Milo," she said, and her voice came out strange to her, flattened, as if the room were absorbing its edges. "Come stand by the door. Just—stay by the door."

But he was already moving toward her, and she was too slow, and by the time she turned he was at her shoulder, small and warm, looking up at the bricked doorway with an expression she could not read and would remember forever.

"It's warm," he said. He reached out.

"Milo—"

His fingertips touched the brick.

And the hum changed.

It did not stop. It did not startle. It *answered*—that was the only word, and it was the wrong word, because answering implies a question, a courtesy, a turn-taking. What happened was that the note in the air bent, the way a struck glass bends its tone when you run a wet finger around its rim, and it bent *toward* Milo, gathered itself around him, and then—Jonah felt it in her molars, in the fluid of her spine, in the soft tissue of her throat—it rose. One note. A single tone, ascending, that matched nothing in the room and everything in the boy: the pitch of his voice, the frequency of *him*, caught and held and repeated back like an echo that had learned to want.

Milo said, "Oh," very softly. The way a child says it when they've found something they were looking for.

And the wall hummed his name.

Not the word. Not *Milo*, not letters, not language. But the shape of it—the exact acoustic signature of a small boy saying his own name, folded into the bell note, offered back through the brick with a patience so vast it made the boiler's patience look like a toddler's. It held the sound the way a cupped hand holds water. It did not ask. It did not call. It simply *kept* him, in sound, the way the yearbooks kept the smudged faces, the way the town kept its one empty chair.

Jonah grabbed Milo's arm and pulled him back and the note stretched after him, thinning, reluctant, and then—when she put her own body between the boy and the wall—it stopped. All at once, completely, the way a held breath is released. The room's ordinary sounds rushed back in: the drip, the buzz, the tick of cooling iron. Her own heartbeat, slamming.

She looked at the wall. The bricks looked back, dumb and mortared and warm, and the warmth was already fading, or seemed to be, or wanted her to think it was.

"Jonah," Milo said. He never called her Ms. Sandoval. He'd started with her first name the second week and she'd never corrected him, and right now she was grateful, because *Ms. Sandoval* belonged to the school and the school belonged to whatever was behind that wall, and she wanted, wildly, irrationally, to keep one name clean. "Jonah, it knows my voice."

"It doesn't know anything," she said, and heard how thin it was. "It's a draft. Old buildings make sounds. Air in the pipes, thermal expansion—"

"You heard it." He wasn't arguing. That was the worst part. He was looking at her with the gentle, sorry patience of a child consoling an adult who is about to cry, and his hand was still half-raised toward the wall, fingers curled, as if the brick had been warm as a sleeping animal and he missed it already. "It knew my voice before I touched it. It's been knowing it all year. That's why I draw the bell." He lowered his hand. "She rings it when it's time. I think—" He frowned, a small deep frown, an old man's frown on a boy's face. "I think it's almost time."

"We're leaving," Jonah said. "Now. Both of us. And tomorrow you're on that bus, Milo, and I mean it."

He went. He didn't argue, didn't dawdle, walked to the door with his backpack hugged tight, and at the threshold he stopped and looked back—not at her, she realized. At the wall.

"Goodnight," he said politely, to it.

The drip behind them stuttered. Just once. Just one beat of silence in the metronome, a skipped tick, a swallowed note—and then it resumed, and Jonah told herself she had imagined the stutter, and knew, with the new and unwelcome clarity that had been settling over her for weeks now like fine ash, that she had imagined nothing in this town yet.

* * *

She did not go home. She told herself she would—she got as far as the staff parking lot, her car cold under the sodium light, her keys in her hand—and then she turned around and went back inside with Edie's key burning a hole in her pocket like a coal.

The building at night was a different animal. The hallway lights ran on timers that clicked off in sequence behind her, section by section, as if the school were closing its eyes room by room, and her footsteps came back to her off the lockers with a half-beat of delay that she refused, absolutely refused, to think about. The mirror by the front office was covered with a sheet of butcher paper—had that been there before? She couldn't remember. She was no longer sure what she should be able to remember, which she suspected was the point.

In the boiler room she stood in front of the wall for a long time without touching it.

The hum was there, fainter now, or quieter, the way something can be quieter without being smaller. She put her palm against the brick—just her palm, flat, the way you'd test a child's forehead for fever—and the warmth came up through her skin and into the bones of her hand and she felt, distinctly, the rhythm under it. Not a pulse. A *ringing*. A vibration that had been going on so long it had worn itself smooth, the way water wears stone, the way a rope wears a wooden railing, the way a ritual wears a town.

"Who's in there?" she whispered.

Nothing. Of course nothing. The drip kept its time. The bulbs buzzed. And then, so softly she could have called it the building settling, the hum shifted—rose a half-step, held—and she recognized the pitch, and her whole body went cold, because it was not Milo's frequency this time.

It was hers.

Not her speaking voice. Something lower, older, the frequency of her at rest, of her asleep, of her at seven years old in a bedroom above a laundromat—except she had no memory of that, no memory of any seven-year-old self, her childhood a smooth gray blank she had always chalked up to an unremarkable life—and the wall hummed that blank back to her, filled it with a single sustained tone that fit the empty space of her like a key fits a lock it was cut for.

She snatched her hand back.

The note held for three more seconds. Four. Long enough for her to understand that it was not mimicking her. It was *confirming*. Running her the way a clerk runs a barcode. And then it faded, satisfied, or patient, or both, and the warmth in the bricks dimmed by one degree, and the room was only a room again.

Jonah stood in the dark with her hand pressed to her sternum, feeling her own heartbeat, counting it, needing the count to be hers.

*It knows my voice,* Milo had said.

It knew hers too. It had known hers longer.

She thought about the attendance roster with its twenty-seven names and twenty-six children. She thought about the dust-free desk. She thought about the yearbook faces smudged in the back row, decade after decade, and about Edie's face when she'd said *count the chairs, not the children*, and about the way the whole town smiled at her, warmly, uniformly, like a choir holding one note.

And she thought—standing alone in the dark under the school, with the warm wall at her back and the cold dripping at her ear—about the question she had been circling for weeks without letting it land: *why me?* Why did the mirrors lag for the new teacher? Why did the town's forgetting leak into her photos, her sleep, her sister's face? Skeptics didn't get haunted. Hauntings required belief; that was the folklore, anyway, the comfortable folklore. Unless the haunting wasn't a haunting. Unless it was a *recall*. A thing checking its inventory. A bell, humming the names of what it had been promised, and finding, among the town's twenty-six children, one name it hadn't expected to find still walking around outside.

The wall was warm. The wall had always been warm. She was suddenly, terribly sure that it had been warm since August, since before she arrived, since the day the rental agent handed her the keys to the house nobody else wanted and said, *you'll be perfect for it.*

Jonah backed away from the wall, one step, two, and the drip kept time behind her, and the caged bulbs buzzed, and she turned and walked up the stairs into the dark school, and behind her, faint and patient and unmistakable, the note began again.

Her frequency. Held. Kept.

Counting down to something.
