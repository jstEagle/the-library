## 13. Voss's Bargain

Dale Voss had been principal of Hollow Creek Elementary for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned that the job was mostly a matter of doors. Which ones you opened. Which ones you kept locked. Which ones you stood in front of, smiling, while someone on the other side knocked.

He was standing in front of one now.

The knock came again—soft, patient, the knock of a woman who intended to keep knocking until the building crumbled around her. Dale looked at his own reflection in the darkened window of his office: a heavyset man of fifty-three with thinning gray hair and reading glasses pushed up on his forehead where they did no good at all. A tired man. An ordinary man. The kind of man who coached Little League and grilled burgers on Sundays and had never, not once in his life, considered himself capable of evil.

*That's the trick of it,* he thought. *There's no moment where you become a monster. There's just the calendar, and your name coming up on it.*

He opened the door.

"Ms. Sandoval." He made his voice warm, administrative, the voice he used for angry parents and budget meetings. "Come in. Can I get you coffee? The machine in the lounge is terrible but it's hot."

"I don't want coffee, Principal Voss." Jonah Galloway came into his office like weather off the plains—not hostile, exactly, but carrying pressure ahead of her. She was maybe thirty, sharp-featured, with the particular stubbornness of a woman who had spent her whole adult life being told she was wrong by people who were wrong. She sat down across from his desk without being invited and put a folder on the blotter between them. "I want to know why my attendance roster has twenty-seven names and twenty-six students."

"Enrollment errors happen every year," Dale said. "Data entry. The district office—"

"The district office has never heard of Hollow Creek Elementary." She flipped the folder open. "I called them Tuesday. They have no record of this school at all. Not this building, not this staff, not these students. As far as the state is concerned, we're a ghost."

"We're an independent charter," Dale said, and was distantly amazed at how smoothly the lie came out, how many years of practice lived inside it. "Rural districts fall through the cracks. It's a paperwork problem, Ms. Galloway, not a—"

"A boy named Milo drew me a picture." Her voice changed. It dropped out of the register of professional grievance into something quieter and much more dangerous. "A picture of this schoolhouse with a bell tower. Except there's no bell tower. There's no bell. And when I asked him about it, he said, *she rings it when it's time.*"

Dale felt the floor tilt under him, very slightly, the way it always did when someone said *it* out loud. He glanced at the window. At the mirror in the corner—the small oval one, decorative, that had hung in this office since before his tenure. His reflection was looking at him. That was fine. Reflections looked at you. But for a half second he couldn't shake the feeling that it was also listening.

"Ms. Galloway," he said carefully, "Milo is a child with an active imagination. Children here grow up on harvest stories. Folklore. It's harmless."

"Is it?" She leaned forward. "Then tell me about 1961."

And there it was. The year, spoken plainly, in his office, in daylight. Dale Voss had rehearsed this conversation in his head for nineteen years without ever wanting to perform it. He had imagined shouting, tears, threats. He had not imagined this: a tired woman with kind eyes and a folder full of evidence, waiting for him to be honest the way you wait for a child to confess to breaking a window.

He could still lie. Nineteen years of practice said he could lie beautifully, send her back to her classroom with a smile and a pamphlet about charter school funding, and she would either drop it or dig until the town handled her the way the town handled things. Either way, the machinery ground on. The crops came up. The children—most of the children—grew up and grew old and forgot.

But his daughter was nine years old. And last night, over meatloaf, she had asked him why the mirrors in their house were slow.

"Close the door," Dale said.

Jonah closed it.

* * *

What surprised him most, once he started talking, was how ordinary it all sounded. That was the horror he'd never been able to explain to himself—that the worst thing in the world, spoken aloud in a fluorescent-lit office, sounded like a budget shortfall or a zoning dispute.

"There was a bell," he began. "Under the school. In the cellar behind the boiler room. The founders put it there in 1874, before the town above even had a name. You've seen the old photographs—the schoolhouse used to have a tower."

"I found the photos. In the archive room in the basement."

"Then you know the bell came down. 1931. The Depression hit, the town was starving, and the founding families made a decision." He rubbed his eyes under his glasses. "You have to understand what it was like. Crops dead three years running. Wells going brackish. People eating seed corn. And the thing beneath us—it doesn't eat crops, Ms. Galloway. It eats something else. Attention. Memory. *Children.* And in exchange, everything else grows."

"You're telling me this town made a deal with something under the school."

"I'm telling you the town made a deal and then spent ninety years pretending it hadn't." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "That's the genius of the arrangement. The forgetting isn't a side effect. It's the payment. Every adult in Hollow Creek carries a hole where the truth should be, and the hole is comfortable. It fits like an old shoe. You can live your whole life around it. I did. My father did. His mother helped dig the cellar."

"And the children."

Dale looked at the folder. At the attendance sheet with its twenty-seven names. At the photograph, visible at the top of the stack, of a class from decades ago—one face in the back row smudged to nothing, as though the paper itself had been rubbed raw by grief.

"One per class," he said. "One per graduating class, roughly speaking. Harvest to harvest. It's not exact. It doesn't need to be exact. It needs to be *enough.*"

"How?"

"It chooses. Or rather—" he searched for the words, words he had never actually assembled before, because no one in this town ever assembled them, "—it's offered. Someone speaks a child's name into the cellar door. That's the Invitation. The name goes down, and the child goes... where the name went. And everyone forgets the child was ever here. Except the parents, sometimes. Sometimes they remember in dreams and think it's just a dream, because that's what we're built to do with holes now. We call them dreams and we go on."

Jonah had gone very pale, but her voice held. "Cara Delgado. She was absent yesterday. Nobody in my class remembers her. The roster changed overnight. Twenty-six names."

"Yes."

"You knew her name was going to be spoken."

Dale's hands were flat on the desk. They were trembling. "It rotates," he said. "Among the founding families. The shame, I mean. Somebody has to say the name, and somebody has to choose which name gets said, and those jobs rotate so that no one family bears it alone and no one can refuse without—without the alternative."

"The alternative being what? What happens if nobody says a name?"

And here Dale Voss finally broke a little, because this was the part he had never said aloud, the part his father had told him drunk on Christmas Eve 1989, weeping into his eggnog, swearing him to a silence that didn't technically exist because no one else remembered enough to break it.

"1931," he said. "The year they took the bell down. The year the arrangement lapsed—because the man whose turn it was refused. Elias Marsh. He had lost two sons already and he said, *no more, let it starve.* And it starved. For eleven months." He swallowed. "The crops died in six weeks. Not failed—died. Turned black standing in the fields. The wells went sour. Livestock birthed dead young. And then people started forgetting other things. Their own names. How to read. Half the town died that winter, Ms. Galloway, and not of cold. Of *absence.* Of walking out into a field and forgetting why they'd come and lying down in the black corn and staying there. Forty-one people. We have their graves. We mow the grass over them every summer and nobody asks who they are, because asking is remembering, and remembering is the one thing this town cannot afford."

"So you feed it a child every year," Jonah said, "and it lets the rest of you live."

"In exchange for our memories of the price." Dale nodded slowly. "Yes. That's the bargain. That's the whole ugly shape of it. Prosperity and peace and a hole in your head where the cost should be. And every autumn, when the leaves turn, the town comes together at the schoolhouse and stands in the parking lot smiling at each other, and one of us walks down to the boiler room and says a name through the brick, and by morning nobody remembers anything except that the harvest was good this year. Good harvest. Good town. Good people." His voice cracked on the last word. "We're not good people. We're *convenient* people. There's a difference and I've spent nineteen years refusing to look at it."

Silence. Somewhere down the hall, a janitor's cart squeaked past. Ordinary sounds. The world insisting on its ordinariness.

"Why are you telling me this?" Jonah asked finally. "If the forgetting works—if everyone forgets—why break it for me?"

"Because it's starting to fail." He took off his glasses and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Your mirrors. You've noticed. Don't bother denying it—half the town has noticed, and noticing spreads like mold. Something about you, Ms. Galloway. Something about an outsider with no history here. The lag is worse near you. The holes are getting... restless. Edie Kowalski remembers her sister. Do you understand what that means? Edie Kowalski has remembered her sister for sixty years, alone, and if she can do it, others can, and if the town starts remembering all at once—" He shook his head. "Grief like that, all at once, unearned, unprepared. It would break us worse than 1931."

"So leave," Jonah said. "Take your daughter and go."

"Go where?" He smiled, and it was the saddest expression Jonah had ever seen on a living face. "The boundary holds until dawn, every day, every road. And even if we got out—even if we ran—we'd forget why we ran. Within a week we'd be driving back with a car full of groceries and no idea what brought us home. The forgetting follows its property lines, Ms. Galloway. It doesn't follow us. It waits for us."

"Then why tell me? If none of it matters—"

"Because you can still leave." He leaned forward, and now the principal was gone entirely, and what remained was just a father, wrung out, terrified, begging. "You have no roots here yet. No years of breathing that air. You can walk out tonight, drive until sunrise, and be free of it—and I am asking you, I am *begging* you, to do exactly that. Because there's another thing you should know. When the arrangement falters—when someone notices too much, digs too deep—the entity doesn't just take the chosen name anymore. It starts counting. It looks around the room and asks itself what else is available." His jaw worked. "You've been counted, Ms. Galloway. I don't know when it happened—I suspect the night you moved into that house, the one nobody rents—but the humming follows you now. Ask Edie. She'll hear it too. You're not a teacher here anymore. You're inventory."

Jonah stood. She picked up her folder and held it against her chest like a shield, and for a long moment she just looked at him—at the framed photos on his wall, the Little League team, the crayon drawing taped beside his diploma in a child's hand: *MY DAD IS THE PRINCIPAL.* 

"Your daughter," Jonah said quietly. "She asked about the mirrors."

Dale flinched. "Last night. Yes."

"What did you tell her?"

"That it was the light." His voice was ash. "I told her it was the light, and she believed me, because children believe their fathers, and that belief is the only reason any of this works. That's the whole engine, Ms. Galloway. Not the entity. Not the bell. *Belief.* Belief in the light, in the good harvest, in the smiling neighbors. Take away the belief and the whole town falls into the hole it's been standing on for ninety years."

Jonah walked to the door. Stopped with her hand on it.

"Milo," she said. "His name. Is it on a list somewhere?"

Dale said nothing. He said nothing for four seconds, five, six, and Jonah watched the silence answer her question more completely than any word could have.

"Leave tonight," he whispered. "Please. Before the harvest. Before the gathering. Leave, and don't look back, and in a month you won't even remember to hate us."

* * *

Jonah walked out through the emptying school. Three-forty-five, buses idling, children streaming toward the doors with backpacks and noise. She stood at the top of the front steps and watched them go, and every bright ordinary face made her chest hurt in a new way, because now she knew what the smiles were standing on.

Milo came out last, as always. He stopped on the bottom step and looked back up at her, and she saw it then—saw it clearly for the first time. The way he tilted his head, listening, when there was nothing to hear. The way his hand drifted to his sternum, pressing lightly, as if holding down something that hummed.

"Ms. Galloway?" he called up. "Are you okay?"

"Fine, Milo." She made her voice steady. "Go on home. Straight home, okay?"

He nodded and turned—and paused, one foot raised, and looked back over his shoulder at the school. At the blank wall where the bell tower used to be. His lips moved, shaping words she couldn't hear, and Jonah felt the hair rise on her arms, because she would have sworn, watching him, that whatever he was saying wasn't meant for her at all.

Down the street, Edie Kowalski stood by the flagpole with her mop bucket, watching Jonah with an expression that said: *Now you know. Now what?*

The afternoon sun lay warm and golden on the town of Hollow Creek, on its tidy lawns and its ripening fields, on forty-one unmarked graves and twenty-six small desks. Everything was beautiful. Everything was fed.

And in her pocket, Jonah's phone buzzed once—a text from an unknown number, five words long:

*He hears it too now.*
