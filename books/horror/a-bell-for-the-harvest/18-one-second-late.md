## 18. One Second Late

The town was screaming without sound.

Jonah felt it in her teeth as she ran—through the school's front doors, down the corridor where the lights buzzed like trapped flies, past the trophy case with its yellowed photographs of children whose faces had been worn smooth as river stones by something more patient than time. Behind her, in the gymnasium, the whole of Hollow Creek stood slack-faced and swaying, their borrowed peace draped over them like funeral shrouds, and Voss was weeping at the mouth of the cellar he'd opened with his own hands.

And through every mirror in the building—in the bathroom glass, in the display cases, in the dark windows that turned to mirrors when the sun went down—a child's voice had spoken in Jonah's own voice, from a childhood she could not remember living.

*Come in, Jonah. You were invited first.*

She didn't stop running. She couldn't afford to think about what that meant yet. Thinking was a luxury for people who still had time.

The boiler room door banged open under her palm. The heat hit her like a wall, and beneath it, that hum—the one she'd felt through the bricks behind the boiler, the one that answered Milo's voice like a mother answering a call it had no right to recognize. The bricked-up doorway glowed faintly now, mortar seams lit from within like veins of lava, and the warmth coming off it was not the warmth of any furnace. It was body-warmth. It was *inviting*.

Milo stood beside it.

She'd left him in her car with the doors locked and the engine running and instructions to drive if she wasn't back in ten minutes, and none of it had mattered, because of course it hadn't. He was eight years old and barefoot—he'd kicked his shoes off somewhere between the gymnasium and here, and she would learn later that he didn't remember doing it—and he was looking at the brick with an expression Jonah had seen on him only once before, the day he'd shown her his drawing of the schoolhouse with its impossible bell.

*She rings it when it's time,* he'd said.

"Milo." She kept her voice low and even, the voice she used for fire drills, for lockdowns, for all the terrible rehearsals schools made children practice because the world kept proving they were necessary. "Step away from the wall."

"It's not angry," he said. That was the thing about Milo; he said things plainly, the way other children reported the weather. "It's lonely. It's been lonely for so long, Ms. Sandoval. It just wants someone to come in."

"I know." She did know. That was the worst part. Through the brick she could feel it too—not words, exactly, but a pressure, a longing so vast and so old it had worn its own shape into the bedrock. A bell that hadn't rung in a hundred years. A throat that had swallowed every voice offered to it and grown hungrier for the offering than for anything it ate. "But we're not going to be the ones who fix its loneliness. Not tonight."

"It already picked me." He turned to look at her, and his eyes were calm in a way no eight-year-old's eyes should ever be calm. "Cara told me. Before she went in. She said it doesn't hurt after the first second."

"Cara is coming back," Jonah said, and heard her own voice crack on it, and hated herself for the crack, because Milo needed her to be certain and she was not certain of anything except that she would rather die in this hot dark room than watch him walk into that wall. "Do you hear me? Everyone is coming back. I don't know how yet. But I'm going to find out."

The hum deepened. The mortar seams brightened. And in the small, grimy window set high in the boiler room wall—the one that looked out onto the empty lot behind the school, the one that had gone mirror-dark with the sunset—Jonah caught movement.

She saw herself.

Not reflected. *Anticipated.*

In the glass, her own figure stood at the brick wall—not as she was now, three feet away, frozen—but pressed against it, both hands flat on the mortar, head bowed. And then the image moved: the Jonah in the window turned, crossed the room in four strides, seized something hanging from a rusted bracket near the old coal chute—a rope, a bell rope, frayed and gray with a century of dust—and pulled.

The window showed her nothing else. No bell. No sound. Just the pull, and the way her whole body leaned into it like a woman hauling a drowning man from deep water, and then the glass went black.

Her heart was slamming against her ribs. Because she knew what she'd just seen. She'd known since the night with the candle and the stopwatch, since the first lagging hand in her rented house's bathroom mirror, and she had spent weeks telling herself the obvious story: the reflection was slow. Broken. Delayed. A defect in the glass, a trick of the light, a phenomenon with a rational name she'd get around to looking up when she had time.

But the stopwatch hadn't lied. She'd measured it a dozen times in those early days, before she understood what she was measuring: her reflection raised its hand *before* she did. One second ahead. Every time. She'd written the numbers down in her lesson-plan notebook and then, like a good skeptic, she'd explained them away.

*The lag isn't a delay,* Edie had told her, the night of the confession, in a kitchen that smelled of burnt coffee and sixty years of grief. *Nothing in this town is what it looks like. Ask yourself why the glass shows you tomorrow instead of yesterday.*

Because it wasn't showing her tomorrow. It was showing her *herself*—the self that had already decided, the self one move further along than the woman standing here sweating and terrified in a boiler room. The entity had reached into her reflections to taunt her, to erode her, to show her ghosts of a stolen childhood, and it had made a mistake so beautiful she wanted to laugh out loud.

It had given her a preview.

Every mirror in Hollow Creek had been rehearsing her next second for weeks. Her unbelief—the skepticism Voss had begged her to leave town over, the detachment she'd guarded like a talisman—hadn't protected her from the thing beneath the school. It had made her the one person in Hollow Creek who'd been reading its mail.

"Milo," she said. "I need you to do something for me. Something brave."

He straightened, and God help her, he *straightened*, the way children do when an adult finally tells them the truth instead of a comfort.

"The rope by the coal chute. Do you see it?" He nodded. "When I tell you—only when I tell you—I want you to pull it as hard as you can. Can you do that?"

"What does it ring?"

"A bell," she said. "A bell that's been buried a long time. And I think—" and here was the leap, the faith, the thing she had sworn off believing in since she was small enough to have a childhood worth stealing, and she took it anyway, off the edge of everything, "—I think it's the only voice it's afraid of."

The brick wall exhaled.

There was no other word for it. The mortar seams flared white-hot and the hum resolved into something almost like speech, almost like a name being tried out in a hundred mouths at once, and in the blackened window Jonah saw flickers—her sister's face, or the place where her sister's face should have been; a kitchen she'd never cooked in; a small girl in a back-row desk raising her hand one second before anyone called on her. Everything it had taken from her, held up like coins.

*Stay,* said the pressure against her mind, gentle as a lullaby. *You can have all of it back. The sister. The mother. The girl you were before we kept her. Just stand still. Just let him come in. You've been so tired, Jonah. You've been tired your whole life.*

And the horrible thing—the thing she would carry, if she carried anything at all out of this night—was that it was right. She *was* tired. She had built her whole careful, skeptical, armored life out of the wreckage of something she couldn't even remember losing, and here was a warm wall offering to give her back the blueprint.

She looked at the window.

The window showed her a woman with both hands flat on the brick, head bowed—and then moving, turning, alive, reaching for the rope.

One second ahead. Her whole future, queued up in dirty glass, waiting for her to catch up to it.

"Okay," Jonah whispered. To the reflection. To herself. To whatever stubborn, doubting, impossible core of her had survived everything this town had done to her memory. "Okay. I'm coming."

She crossed the room.

The heat of the wall soaked through her palms like fever-heat, like the heat of a forehead before a seizure, and beneath her hands the bricks pulsed in slow rhythm—not a heartbeat, she realized. A pendulum. A bell swinging in some chamber deeper than any cellar, keeping time for a hunger older than the town laid on top of it. The mortar was soft now, giving slightly under her fingers, and she understood that it would let her touch it forever, would stay warm and yielding and full of promises until dawn, until the harvest came due, until Milo walked in on his own bare feet because children always eventually did what the loneliness asked.

She drew back her fist.

*You'll forget him,* the wall said, quick now, urgent, the lullaby curdling. *You'll forget all of them. You'll forget the boy and the bell and the way you were brave tonight. Is that a trade? Is that worth it?*

"That's the deal you never understood," Jonah said aloud, and her voice sounded strange and steady in the roaring heat, the voice of a woman who had finally stopped asking permission from the evidence. "I never believed in you. Why would I start believing you now?"

She swung.

The first brick cracked down the middle. Something beneath the school screamed—not through the air, but through the ground, through the soles of her shoes, through the fillings in her teeth—and every pane of glass in the building fogged white at once. She swung again. Mortar sprayed like shrapnel. Again. Her knuckles split and she barely felt it, because in the last unclouded sliver of the window she could see her reflection matching her blow for blow, one second ahead, pulling the rope, *pulling the rope*—

"Milo!" she screamed. "Now!"

He threw himself at the rope with his whole small weight, both hands wrapped in the frayed gray hemp, and for one endless heartbeat nothing happened—dust sifted down, the bracket groaned, the wall's heat surged up around her like a tide—

Then, somewhere far below the floor, deeper than the foundation, deeper than the bedrock, deeper than anything men had poured concrete over—a bell rang.

It was not loud. That was the miracle of it. It was small and clear and sweet, a single silver note rising through a hundred feet of earth like a bubble through dark water, and where it passed, the heat died. The hum stopped mid-breath. The pressure lifted off her mind so suddenly she staggered, hands still raised, punching at air.

The wall went cold. Ordinary. Brick and lime and nothing else.

And through the fogged windows of the boiler room, through every mirror in Hollow Creek Elementary, through the dark glass of a hundred houses where the town stood slack-jawed and swaying, the note kept ringing—one strike, and another, and another, each one smaller and clearer than the last, a bell tolling the hour of its own unburying, and Jonah Galloway stood in the cooling dark with bleeding fists and listened to the sound of a hundred years of silence breaking.

Milo let go of the rope. He was crying, great silent gulps of it, and she gathered him up off the floor and held him while the bell rang itself out below them, strike after strike, counting down toward something.

Toward sunrise, she thought. Toward dawn.

Toward everyone it had ever taken, walking home out of the mist.

Above them, through the ceiling, she heard footsteps—dozens of them, confused, waking, the whole town stirring out of its borrowed peace—and somewhere among them a woman's voice calling a name that wasn't Cara's, wasn't anyone's, a name sixty years dead, sobbing it into the dark like a prayer finally allowed.

The bell rang again.

And in the last mirror in the room, fogged and fading, Jonah watched her reflection lower its bleeding hands, smile at her—warm, exhausted, *grateful*—and mouth two words one second before she understood them:

*Thank you.*

Then the glass cleared, and showed her only the present, only now, only a woman holding a shaking boy in a cold room while the world outside began, at last, to remember.

The bell struck once more—a final, lingering note—and went silent.

Jonah felt something loosen behind her eyes, soft as snow sliding from a branch, and understood, with the last clarity she would ever have in this town, what the price was going to be.

She held on to Milo tighter.

"Tell me your name," she whispered into his hair. "Quickly. Before I forget to ask."
