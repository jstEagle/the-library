## 19. The Bell Beneath

The brick came away easier than it should have.

Jonah had expected mortar, dust, the stubborn resistance of a wall that had stood for a century. Instead the brick slid loose like a tooth from rotten gums, and behind it was not darkness but warmth—a breath of air that smelled of turned earth and candle smoke and something sweeter underneath, something like apples left too long in a cellar bin. The hum she'd felt through her palms for days now rose up through the floor itself, through the soles of her shoes, into the bones of her legs, as if the whole schoolhouse were a bell someone had struck and never allowed to finish ringing.

Behind her, Milo made a small sound.

She turned. He stood at the mouth of the boiler room corridor, lit by the single work lamp Edie had left burning, and his face was wrong—not frightened, exactly. Attentive. The way he looked when he was drawing, when the whole world narrowed to the line his pencil was making.

"It's louder," he whispered.

"I know."

"She's been waiting a long time."

Jonah's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the wall to steady them and felt the vibration travel up through her wrists, through her chest, settling somewhere behind her sternum like a second heartbeat slightly out of time with her own. One second out of time. Everything in this town was one second out of time.

"Milo." She crouched to his level, made herself look at him, really look—the cowlick he never quite smoothed down, the pencil smudge on his left cheekbone, the way his too-big flannel shirt hung off his shoulders because his mother bought everything a size up so he'd grow into it. *Remember this*, she told herself. *Whatever happens next, remember this.* "When I tell you to run, you run for the front doors. You don't wait. You don't look back. You find Edie. Can you do that?"

He didn't answer right away. His eyes went past her shoulder, to the hole in the wall, and something moved in them—recognition, or grief, or both.

"She doesn't want to hurt anyone," he said. "That's what nobody gets. She just wants to be remembered right."

"What do you mean, remembered right?"

But Milo only shook his head, and the hum deepened, and Jonah understood that the conversation—whatever conversation this had been—was over. The thing beneath the schoolhouse had heard its own name in the boy's mouth, even if no name had been spoken.

"Run when I say," she repeated.

Then she put her fingers back into the gap where the brick had been, and pulled.

* * *

The bricks came away one after another after another, faster than any wall had ever surrendered, until the opening was wide enough for her shoulders and the warm air poured out over her like water. Beyond it: stairs. Old wooden ones, sagging in the middle, descending into a darkness that was not empty. The darkness breathed. She could feel it against her face, slow and patient, the respiration of something very large sleeping very lightly.

A rope hung in the center of the stairwell.

She hadn't seen it at first—it blended with the dark—but now it resolved, thick and braided and pale as bone, running up through a pulley bolted into the joists above and vanishing into the black overhead where, she understood without knowing how she understood, the bell waited. The bell from the oldest photograph. The bell from Milo's drawings, drawn over and over, always in the cupola, always with its mouth open. They hadn't removed it. They'd only buried the way to reach it, and buried the memory of reaching it, and buried the memory of burying it, generation after generation, until the forgetting was load-bearing.

Jonah stepped onto the first stair.

It held.

She descended.

The stairs went down farther than the building had height. She counted thirty steps, then forty, then stopped counting because the numbers began to feel like a superstition, and the whole time the breathing darkness around her grew warmer and closer and more attentive, the way a room grows attentive when someone says your name in it. Somewhere above her, faintly, she heard the boiler room door bang open—footsteps, many footsteps, coming down through the school—and she knew the town had gathered, that Voss was weeping at the cellar door below, that midnight had come and the Invitation was being spoken, and that all of it was happening one second before or one second after she heard it, and she could no longer have said which.

Her foot found stone. Level ground.

The dark opened around her like a throat.

And the voice came—not from ahead of her, not from the walls, but from everywhere at once, from the air itself, and it was a child's voice, and it was her own.

*"You came down on your own."*

Jonah stood very still. Her eyes were adjusting, or the dark was deciding to let her see; either way, shapes emerged. A round chamber, earth-floored, its walls curving up beyond sight. The great rope descending through a hole in the ceiling and ending in a frayed tail a foot above her head. And all around the circular wall, at the height of a child's shoulder—

Desks. Dozens of them, ancient little wooden desks, arranged in rows that followed the curve of the room. Some were rotted to lace. Some looked freshly oiled. On each desktop, scratched into the wood in a child's uneven hand, was a name.

She found hers on the third row. *JONAH GALLOWAY*, gouged deep, the letters filled with something dark.

"You've been showing me," she said aloud, and her voice sounded small and enormous at once. "In the mirrors. All this time. It wasn't lag. It was—you were showing me what you took."

*"I don't take,"* the voice said gently, and it was so reasonable, so kind, that her knees nearly buckled. *"I keep. There is such a difference, Jonah. Your sister used to know the difference. Ask her."*

And it gave her Dana.

Not a description. Not a memory. Dana herself, whole and sudden, standing in their mother's kitchen with flour on her forearms, laughing at something Jonah had said, the specific laugh, the snort at the end she'd always hated and Jonah had always loved—Dana at seventeen, Dana teaching her to drive in the church parking lot, Dana's hand cool on her forehead the night of the fever, Dana on the phone last spring saying *you can't move to some town called Hollow Creek, Jo, promise me you'll call every Sunday*—

Jonah made a sound she didn't recognize as coming from herself.

*"You lost this,"* the voice said, sorrowful, intimate, closer now. *"Walking through my town, breathing my air, you lost her face by degrees and you blamed your own tired mind. I can give it back. All of it. Not just her. The house you grew up in. The dog. The summer you were nine. Your whole self, Jonah Galloway, every piece the forgetting has eaten—I will pour it all back into you, and you will walk out of here at sunrise complete, and the boy will stay, and the town will sleep, and no one will ever know there was a choice. That is my offer. That is my only offer."*

The rope hung in front of her.

Her hand was already rising toward it. She watched it rise as if it belonged to someone else—which, she thought distantly, was fitting, because it had been someone else's hand all along, hadn't it? A child's hand, scratching her name into a desk in a room beneath the world, a child who had been hollowed out and shipped away and handed a life she couldn't fully hold onto, a life that leaked, a life with gray rectangles where photographs should be.

"That's the trick," Jonah said. Her voice cracked. She let it. "That's always the trick. You give people back what they're missing, and they stop missing anything else. They stop asking. They ring no bells."

*"Is that what you think I am?"*

"No." She swallowed. "I think you're lonely. I think you were left down here with a bell and no one to ring it, and you learned to make children stay because staying was all anyone ever gave you. And I think—" her eyes burned, and Dana's laugh was still echoing in her skull, and letting go of it felt like tearing off her own arm— "I think that's the saddest thing I've ever known. And I'm going to ring the bell anyway."

Silence. The whole chamber held its breath.

Then, softly, almost tenderly:

*"You'll forget the boy."*

"I know."

*"You'll forget why you came. You'll forget you were brave. You'll stand in some other town someday and feel a shape missing from yourself and never learn its name."*

"I know."

*"And you'll do it anyway. Because you are your mother's daughter and your sister's sister, and they taught you the difference between keeping and taking."*

"Yes," Jonah said, and reached for the rope.

* * *

The moment her fingers closed around the braid, the entity screamed.

Not with sound—with everything. The chamber lurched. The desks rattled and skidded across the dirt floor. The dark itself seemed to convulse, and through it Jonah saw them, all of them, the kept ones, faces surfacing in the blackness like drowned things rising: children in sepia and silver and color, decades of them, back-row faces unsmudged at last and all of them looking at her, mouths open, and whether they were screaming with the entity or screaming at it she could not tell and had no time to learn.

She pulled.

The rope was heavier than anything had a right to be, as if she were hauling the whole buried weight of the town up hand over hand, as if the bell's tongue were made of every silence the town had ever agreed to. Her palms burned. The braid bit through skin. Above her, through the shaft, she heard the pulley groan, heard something vast begin to swing, begin to wake, and the entity's voice broke apart into a thousand voices, all of them hers, all of them begging—

*Stop. Please. I'll give her back. I'll give you Sunday phone calls and flour on her arms, I'll give you the fever night, I'll give you everything, please, please, no one has rung it in a hundred years, no one remembers how it sounds, please—*

"That's why," Jonah gasped, and pulled again. "That's exactly why."

The bell cleared the lip of the shaft.

It swung out over the chamber, huge and green-black with age, and it was beautiful, it was terrible, it was the shape of every drawing Milo had ever made, and hanging from its crown by a cord around its neck was a small brass hand-bell, a teacher's bell, the kind that calls children in from recess—and it rang first, all by itself, one bright clear note.

The entity shrieked.

Jonah hauled the rope the final foot and threw her whole body's weight into it, and the great bell swung, and its mouth opened over her—

and rang.

* * *

There are sounds that pass through the body and sounds that pass through the self, and the bell made the second kind. It went through Jonah's bones and her history and her name. It went through the chamber and the desks and the hundred scratched names, and each name flared white as the sound touched it, and the children in the dark did not scream anymore—they sighed, an exhalation a century long, and began, one by one, to come apart into light.

The entity screamed through all of it. It screamed in her childhood voice and in Voss's voice and in Edie's dead sister's voice and in twenty-six voices and twenty-seven, and Jonah rang the bell again, and again, tears streaming down her face, palms shredded and slick, ringing through the offers, through the bargains, through the final thin whisper—*I only wanted someone to stay*—ringing until the scream thinned to a wail, the wail to a hum, the hum to the note the town had been living inside all along, resolving at last, finishing the ring it had begun a hundred years ago and never been permitted to end.

On the last stroke, the chamber filled with dawn-colored light that came from nowhere, and every desk stood empty, and every name on every desktop faded to plain old wood.

The rope went slack in her hands.

Jonah slid down the wall to the dirt floor, and the light gathered around her, gentle as snowfall, and she thought—clearly, calmly, with the last coherent thought she would ever have about this place—*so this is what it costs. Fine. It's worth it. He gets to grow up.*

She thought: *Dana. If I can't keep you, at least I got to carry you down here one more time.*

She thought: *Milo, run now, run—*

And then the light took the thoughts, softly, one at a time, like a mother lifting sleeping children out of a car, and Jonah Galloway sat in the cold stone cellar of a schoolhouse in a town whose name was already dissolving, smiling at nothing, holding a length of frayed rope she could not explain, while far above her, through the broken bricks, through the boiler room, through the open front doors of Hollow Creek Elementary, the sun came up.

* * *

The bell's last note traveled outward through the ground like a ripple through still water—through the foundations of the school, under the streets, beneath the fields lying fallow past the town limit—and everywhere it passed, mirrors shivered and settled into honest glass. In the diner on Main Street, the pie case reflected the pie case and nothing else. In the Vosses' hallway, a woman catching sight of herself saw only herself, arriving exactly on time.

And at the edge of town, where the harvest fog had stood thick and unmoving since October began, the mist began, very slowly, to thin.
