## 5. 3:07 A.M.

Theo had told themselves, all evening, that they were not waiting for anything.

They had made a production of not waiting. They'd eaten dinner standing at the kitchen counter like a person with somewhere better to be — canned soup, eaten with the good spoon out of spite for the house's opinion of them. They'd carried their mother's journals up to the guest room and set them on the nightstand in a neat stack, spine-aligned, the way Theo aligned everything when their hands needed a job that wasn't shaking. They'd checked the cellar door twice. Locked. Checked it a third time anyway, because twelve years of pest control had taught them that the third check was where the real information lived: not *is it locked* but *do I need it to be.*

It was locked. The deadbolt was new; their mother had installed it herself, apparently, sometime in the last decade. There was a scratch of pencil on the doorframe beside it, low down, at the height of a kneeling woman: *checked.* And below that, in older ink, faded to the color of weak tea: *again.*

Their mother had been keeping a log. Of course she had. Forty years of journals downstairs and every one of them, so far as Theo had read tonight, circling the same small set of facts with the same terrible calm: *the bell rang at 3:07. I did not answer it. It rang four times and stopped. The well was quiet. I left it bread and milk, which is foolish, but it seemed rude not to.*

Bread and milk. For what? Theo had laughed when they first read that line, alone in the kitchen with the soup going cold, and the laugh had come out wrong, too loud, bouncing off the cabinets like something startled.

Now it was ten o'clock, and Theo lay in their childhood bed — narrower than they remembered, or they were wider, both true — staring at the ceiling where the old water stain had grown into a shape like a hand holding something small.

They were not waiting for 3:07. They had simply noticed, over the course of the evening, that they kept noticing the time.

Ten forty-seven. Eleven thirty-two. Twelve-fifteen. Each glance at the phone felt casual, incidental, the glance of a person who simply liked knowing things. A professional habit. In the field you learned to track time without thinking about it — how long since you last heard movement in a wall, how long until the bait stations needed checking, how long a trapped animal could go before panic turned to something worse. Time was just another thing you monitored.

Except that pest controllers did not lie awake monitoring the hour at which their mother's journals said a bell rang by itself.

*The bell rang at 3:07.*

Every entry. Not 3:06, not 3:09. Their mother had logged it the way Theo logged termite swarms — date, duration, conditions. Some entries were one line. Some ran pages. One from the winter Theo was seven simply said: *It knows when we have company. It waits until the house is ours again. I think it is polite. I think it is lonely. I think these are the same thing.*

Theo rolled onto their side. The mattress dipped in a way that suggested other bodies had lain exactly here, over decades, also not sleeping. Somewhere below, the house ticked and settled, doing its nightly work of being old.

And there was the other thing. The thing Theo had been circling all evening the way you circle a wasp nest before committing.

Wren.

Wren had sat on the porch swing at dusk like he'd been poured into place, hands folded, feet not reaching the floorboards — feet that had never once reached the floorboards, in all the years Theo had known him, because Wren had been nine forever and nine-year-olds dangled. He hadn't aged. That was the fact Theo kept picking up and setting back down, hoping it would be lighter each time. Twelve years since Theo had last seen him — no, longer, since Theo was seventeen and had made a conscious, embarrassed decision to stop looking at the corner of the yard where Wren waited — and Wren had not gained an inch or lost a tooth. His overalls still had the same grass stain on the knee. His smile still had the gap.

Imaginary friends didn't keep maintenance schedules. Imaginary friends didn't wait twenty-five years on a porch swing for you to come home, patient as furniture, and say, in a voice exactly as remembered: *"You came back. I kept your spot."*

Theo had gone inside after that. Had made soup. Had organized journals. Had done all the busy, sensible things a rational adult does when the impossible sits politely on their porch refusing to leave.

*"You'll want to be awake at 3:07,"* Wren had called after them, not rising from the swing. Not threatening. Almost apologetic. *"I'm sorry about that part. It's not my favorite either."*

Not my favorite either. As if the bell were weather. As if they were both just residents of a climate neither had chosen.

Theo looked at the clock. 1:58 a.m.

Fine. Fine. If they weren't sleeping anyway — and they weren't, they could admit that now, the fiction having worn through — then they might as well do what any professional would do with an unexplained phenomenon: document it. Get ahead of it. You couldn't solve a problem you wouldn't look at. That philosophy had served them through twelve years of raccoons in chimneys and things in walls that turned out, every single time, to have a boring name.

Every single time. Until this week.

They got up. Pulled on jeans over the T-shirt, socks against the cold floor — the house's floors had always been cold, even in July, a cold that came up from underneath, from the direction of the cellar, from the direction of —

Don't. Not yet.

They took the flashlight, their mother's journal — the current one, the last one, its cover soft as cloth from handling — and a kitchen chair, which they carried to the hallway where the bell hung.

The bell had been in the family longer than the family, or so the story went. Brass, hand-sized, with a wooden handle worn smooth and dark by generations of palms. It had hung on a nail in the hallway for as long as Theo could remember, high enough that children couldn't reach it, and their mother had never once taken it down to use it. When Theo was small and asked why they had a bell nobody rang, their mother had said, *Some bells are for calling and some are for answering,* and had declined to elaborate, and little Theo had accepted this the way children accept everything, filing it in the vast uncatalogued archive of parental strangeness.

Now Theo stood under it with a kitchen chair and a flashlight and the specific dread of a person about to confirm something they already know.

The nail was solid. The bell hung plumb, still, silent. Theo shone the light on it and saw — and here their stomach performed a slow, professional roll — that the brass was bright. Polished bright. Not tarnished, not dusty, though dust lay thick on the picture frames three feet away and on the nailhead itself. The bell gleamed like something tended. Like something someone touched often.

Or like something that touched itself often. Rang itself often. Whatever verb applied to a bell that rang at 3:07 a.m. for forty years without a hand.

Theo set the chair beneath it, climbed up, and did not touch the bell. They only looked close. Up close the brass showed faint concentric scratches inside the lip, fine as fingerprint whorls, the kind of wear that comes from a clapper striking the same arc ten thousand times. Ten thousand rings. At one per night, that was twenty-seven years. Their mother's journals went back further than that. Theo did some arithmetic they immediately wished they hadn't.

They climbed down. They arranged the chair facing the bell, sat, wrapped a blanket around their shoulders, and opened the journal across their knees.

2:40 a.m.

They read. Their mother's handwriting changed over the decades — looping and girlish at the start, cramped and efficient by the end — but the entries stayed level, stayed sane, and that sanity was somehow the worst part. This was not the diary of a frightened woman. This was the logbook of a professional. Theo recognized the tone because it was their own tone, the voice they used in reports: *observed activity consistent with prior visits; no structural damage; recommend continued monitoring.* Their mother had been running a twelve-year pest control operation on her own house, except the pest was a sound, and the treatment was attention, and the contract had no end date.

*March 11. Rang at 3:07. Four times. I counted with him through the wall, the way I used to count with Theo. It settles faster when I count aloud.*

*June 2. Humming again, under the floor. The song stops in the same place. Every time, the same place. Like a sentence that can't find its period.*

*October 19. Theo called today. First time in months. The bell rang early — 3:04 — as if it heard my mood. Or as if it wants me to know it hears everything. I sang to it instead of counting. Just nonsense, just lullaby syllables. It hummed along. God help me, it harmonized.*

Theo's throat had gone tight. They read on, faster, the blanket forgotten.

*December 24. Christmas Eve. I put out bread and milk in the cellar. Foolish. But it's been fifty years since anyone in this town was kind to those children, and I won't be the last generation of cruelty. It costs me nothing. It costs me nothing. (I write that twice because I don't believe it once.)*

2:59 a.m.

Theo closed the journal. The hallway was very quiet, the particular layered quiet of an old house at night — refrigerator hum from the kitchen, wind testing the eaves, the tick of cooling wood. Underneath it all, if you listened past listening, the faintest suggestion of damp, of depth, of the cellar breathing through the floorboards.

They had planned, insofar as they had planned anything, to watch the bell ring. To see it happen. To catch the mechanism — a draft, a truck on the county road setting up sympathetic vibration, a settling joist. There was always a boring name. There had always been a boring name.

At 3:06, the temperature in the hallway dropped. Not dramatically. The way a room cools when a door opens somewhere far off in a large building. Theo's breath appeared briefly in the flashlight beam, a small ghost, and vanished.

At 3:07, the bell rang.

Once. A clean, round note, warm as a struck memory, filling the hallway the way water fills a glass — completely, touching every surface. Theo felt it in their sternum. Felt it in their teeth. The clapper moved — Theo watched it, flashlight steady, hands steadier than they had any right to be — and there was no hand. No draft. No wire. The brass swung in its small perfect arc and the note rose and decayed and died, and in the silence after, Theo heard the house hold its breath.

Then it rang again. Twice. Three times. Four.

Four times. *Rang at 3:07. Four times.* Exactly as logged, year after year. Theo found themselves counting along, lips moving, and caught their mother's phrase rising unbidden: *it settles faster when I count aloud,* and heard their own voice, low, almost embarrassed:

"One. Two. Three. Four."

The fourth ring faded. The hallway settled. And Theo sat in the kitchen chair in their dead mother's house at three minutes past seven in the morning, heart slamming, mind doing what it always did — reaching for the boring name, the mundane explanation, the draft, the vibration, the trick of the dark —

— when the humming started.

It came up through the floorboards. Not from the cellar door, not from any direction a sound should travel. From everywhere below at once, the way cold comes from below, the way roots drink. Small voices. Several of them — Theo counted without wanting to, four, five, six, overlapping slightly, out of phase, the way children sing when no one has taught them to stay together. Girls and boys. None of them older than the gap-toothed age. None of them, Theo understood with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely, alive.

They were humming. Not singing — humming, wordless, a melody simple and old and round as a ring of children holding hands. Theo knew it. That was the horror under the horror: they *knew* it. It was a skipping song, a playground song, the kind sung in circles with a clapping pattern, and they had heard it before — where? When? Some afternoon of their childhood, coming up through these same floorboards while their mother stood in the hallway with her head tilted, listening, pretending to listen to nothing?

The melody climbed. Verse-shaped. It had the architecture of verses — repetition, variation, a turn at the end that promised resolution. Theo listened with their whole body, hunched forward on the kitchen chair, blanket fallen, flashlight beam trembling on the floorboards between their feet.

And then the song reached the place where the verse should end.

And stopped.

Stopped the way a step stops when there's no stair where the stair should be. The melody turned toward its resolution and found nothing there — a hole in the music, a missing tooth, a sentence with no period. The voices held at the edge of it, hovering, and the hovering was the worst sound Theo had ever heard: six small voices hanging on an unresolved note for one second, two seconds, three, waiting for something to carry them over, and nothing carrying them, and then the whole melody folding back on itself and beginning again, climbing again, turning again, stopping again at the same empty place.

Like a sentence that can't find its period. Their mother had written that. Decades of nights, she had lain awake hearing exactly this, and she had never once written down what it did to her, and she had fed it bread and milk and counted it to sleep, and she had never told Theo, never warned them, never —

The song cycled again. Stopped again. And this time, in the gap, in the hanging silence where the ending should live, Theo heard — or imagined, or was spoken to directly, there was no telling — a quality in the pause that was not frustration.

It was invitation.

The song wanted finishing. That was the whole shape of it, the whole century of it, the whole reason for the bell and the 3:07 and the patience of the thing on the porch swing: a circle of children had been interrupted mid-verse, and they had been holding that last note for a hundred years, and they were asking — politely, nightly, four rings at a time — for someone to complete it.

Terror was still in Theo's body, cold and total, a fist around the spine. But underneath the terror, rising through it like warmth through floorboards, something else had begun to move. Curiosity. The professional's disease. The itch that had made them good at their work: *find the source, understand the pattern, learn what it wants.* Because things that want something can be reasoned with. Things that want something have terms. And this — whatever this was — had been asking, in the most patient voice imaginable, for a hundred years.

Theo became aware, gradually, that they were humming.

Low, barely voiced, following the melody a half-beat behind the floor. Following it up, and around, and toward the turn — toward the hole — and some animal instinct yanked the sound out of their throat a half-second before they reached the empty place. They bit the note off. Sat shaking in the chair.

Below the floorboards, the humming paused.

All of it. All six voices. A silence with attention in it, the way a silence has weight when someone in the next room has just heard their name.

Then, softly — so softly it might have been the house settling, might have been water moving far underground, might have been a child on a porch swing smiling — the humming resumed. And Theo could have sworn it sounded, this time, pleased.

The bell hung above them, polished bright, waiting for tomorrow's 3:07. Theo sat in the dark with their mother's journal open on their knees, and wrote, in the margin of the March page, in their own cramped professional hand, the first entry of their tenure:

*Rang at 3:07. Four times. Counted aloud. Song incomplete — ends mid-verse, same place every cycle. Subject appears to want completion. Do NOT provide.*

They underlined the last three words twice. Then, because honesty was the whole point of a logbook, they added:

*But it asked so nicely.*

Upstairs, in the guest room where no one slept, dawn was still hours off. And down in the cellar, behind the new deadbolt, something shifted its weight — the small, patient shift of a listener settling in — and Theo, packing up the chair, decided that tomorrow they would drive to the county archive, because there was a song in this house one verse short of finished, and somebody, a hundred years ago, had cut that verse out of the world on purpose.

You couldn't solve a problem you wouldn't look at. And Theo Yarrow, sitting alone in the ringing dark, finally understood that they had inherited more than a house.

They had inherited the question.
