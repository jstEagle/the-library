## 11. Mother's Last Entry

The journals lived in the cedar chest at the foot of her bed, wrapped in a pillowcase printed with faded strawberries, which was how everything in Marion Yarrow's house was stored — precious things disguised as laundry.

There were eleven of them. Cloth-bound, the kind sold at church fundraisers for three dollars apiece, their spines cracked into soft spines like old dogs. The earliest was dated 1974, the year she turned twelve. The last one stopped in March, five weeks before her heart quit without asking anyone's permission.

Theo sat on the floor of her bedroom with the chest open in front of them and a mug of coffee going cold on the nightstand, and told themselves they were just tidying up. Estate work. Inventory. There was a whole professional vocabulary for this — *contents assessment, disposition of personal effects* — and Theo leaned on it the way they leaned on the word *infestation* when a customer's attic turned out to hold something with too many joints.

Read them or don't, they thought. But you're going to sit here all day either way.

They started with the first one, because that seemed fair. If you're going to read a dead woman's private thoughts, you should at least do it chronologically.

*June 3rd, 1974.* A girl's handwriting, round and careful, pressing hard enough to emboss the next page. *Got a B+ in arithmetic. Mama says I mope. I do NOT mope.*

*July 14th, 1974.* *The bell rang again last night. Papa says it's the wind in the chimney. The chimney isn't shaped like a bell.*

Theo turned pages slowly. The handwriting changed over the years — loosened, slanted, sprouted exclamation points during what appeared to have been a brief and passionate affair with calligraphy in 1981 — but the entries about the bell never changed at all. They arrived like weather reports. Regular. Unemotional. Almost bored.

*Oct. 2nd, 1986. Bell rang at 3:07. Counted to forty before it stopped. Fed it.*

*Fed it.* Theo frowned at the phrase. Fed it what?

*April 19th, 1990. Rang twice. Gave it the good long look. It settled.*

The good long look.

By 1998 the entries had acquired a tone Theo recognized from their own line of work — the flat competence of someone who has stopped expecting a problem to go away and started managing it instead. His mother had been running pest control on a haunting for thirty years, and she'd never once said a word.

Theo set the journal down and looked out the window. Wren was not on the porch swing today; the swing moved anyway, a small creaking rhythm, patient as furniture. Down the street, Mr. Halloran was watering his lawn at seven in the evening like a man who had made his peace with drought.

"Okay, Mom," Theo said aloud. "Okay."

They picked up the last journal.

* * *

The final book was thinner than the others, its cover stained with something tea-colored, and the handwriting inside had gone loose and large, the letters of a woman writing quickly before her hands got cold. Theo read standing up, then sitting down, then standing up again, because some sentences made staying still impossible.

*Jan. 9th. Doctor gave me the pamphlet. I told him I don't need a pamphlet, I need another twenty years. He laughed. Doctors laugh at everything they can't fix.*

*Jan. 22nd. Theo called. They sound tired. That job wears on a person — crawling under other people's houses all day, seeing what hides where it shouldn't. They think I don't know why they chose it. I know exactly why.*

Theo's throat tightened. They kept reading.

*Feb. 3rd. It rang early tonight. 3:07 still, but the humming came up through the kitchen floor after, and it sounded closer than usual. Wetter. I put my chair right over the spot and read aloud until dawn. It likes being listened to. That's the whole trick of it, if you can call a trick something you do every night for fifty years.*

*Feb. 11th. Found the strawberry pillowcase torn. Nothing else touched. It's getting impatient because I'm getting slow. Well. It can wait its turn. Everything waits its turn in this house.*

Then, on a page dated February 14th — Valentine's Day, of all the rotten jokes — the handwriting changed. Slowed. Got smaller, the way people write when they are telling the truth and want to fit it all on one page.

*I met him when I was nine.*

Theo's hands went cold around the journal.

*A boy on the porch swing. Same swing, same creak. He said his name was Wren and he asked me if I was lonely, and I was, so badly, after my sister died, and I said yes, and he smiled like the sun coming up over the well field. I played with him all summer. I taught him hopscotch. I told him things I never told anyone.*

*Mama caught me talking to him in August and went white as flour. She sat me down at this very table and told me about Adeline Crowe, and about the verse, and about what happened to the Hollis boy in 1931 who sang along. Then she told me the rule, the only rule: you never finish the song, and you never let it finish you. You keep it fed with attention instead. You listen. You sit with it. You give it your evenings so it doesn't take your children.*

*I asked her why it couldn't just be left alone. She said, "Because it's lonely, Marion. Loneliest thing in the county. And lonely things take."*

Theo sat down hard on the edge of their mother's bed.

Nine years old. The same age. The same porch. The same boy with the same smile, offered to whatever child in that house was lonely enough to see him.

*So I made the bargain,* the entry continued. *I gave him my summers. Every year, I came back and sat on that swing and talked to him and let him be real for me, and every year I watched him not change while I did. And when I grew up and got married and got pregnant, I was terrified — terrified, Theo, if anyone ever reads this — because now there was a NEW child in the house, a soft new lonely thing, and I knew what lonely things take.*

*So I fed it double. My attention and my nights. I sat over the cellar floor and hummed the safe verses — the first four, never the fifth — and I let it lean against me like a cat against a leg. And when Theo started talking about "their friend Wren," I didn't scream. God knows I wanted to. I made cocoa and asked sensible questions and waited for them to grow out of it, the way I grew out of it, the way you grow out of it by growing INTO the watching instead.*

*People will say I should have burned the house down. Maybe. But houses burn and wells refill and songs outlive paper. The only thing that ever held it was somebody paying attention. So I paid. Fifty years of installments on a debt I didn't make. And I'd pay it again. I'd pay it twice.*

The next entry was dated March 2nd, and it was short.

*Last night I dreamed about him. He was still nine. He took my hand — his hand was cold as well water, always was — and he said, "You're leaving, Marion." And I said, "Yes." And he said, "Who feeds it then?"*

*And I woke up and wrote this, because I know the answer, and I hate the answer, and I'm not strong enough to say it out loud yet.*

*It'll be Theo. It was always going to be Theo. Forgive me, sweetheart. I tried to buy you more time than I got.*

That was the last entry. Below it, a few blank lines, and then — pressed so lightly it was almost invisible, the pen barely kissing the paper — a single sentence in a different hand entirely. Round. Careful. Childlike. The letters of someone who had learned to write in 1898 and never needed to learn anything newer:

*thank you marion*

Theo closed the journal with hands that would not quite obey, and sat in their mother's bedroom while the light went blue and then gray and then dark, and somewhere below the floorboards, faint and wet and patient, something began to hum.

* * *

They found Wren on the porch at midnight, which was where Wren always was, sitting on the swing with his knees together and his hands folded, looking like a photograph of a child that had been left out in the sun for a hundred and twenty years — colors intact, warmth gone.

"You knew," Theo said.

Wren didn't flinch. He'd had decades of practice at not flinching. "Knew what?"

"Don't." Theo's voice came out rougher than they intended. "Don't do the innocent thing. Not tonight. You knew her. You knew her when she was nine, and you knew her every summer after, and you sat there and let me believe I invented you."

The swing creaked once, softly, under no weight at all.

"I didn't let you believe anything," Wren said. "Believing is what you did. I just stood still and let it happen. There's a difference." He looked out at the dark yard, at the well cap's outline beneath the grass. "Your mother used to bring lemonade out here. Two glasses. She'd drink both, eventually, but she always poured two."

"She paid you," Theo said. "Fifty years. She sat in that kitchen and hummed at the floor so you wouldn't—" Their voice cracked and they hated it. "So you wouldn't take me."

"Yes," Wren said simply.

"And you let her."

"I let her." He turned then, and his eyes in the porch light were the color of water that has been standing too long. "Theo, I need you to understand something, and I need you to understand it before you decide what I am. Lonely things take. Your grandmother said that, and her mother said it, and they were right. I am the loneliest thing in this county. I have been hungry since 1898. And every single time a child in this house was lonely enough to see me — your great-aunt, your grandmother, your mother, you — I could have taken them. Do you understand? I could have finished the song with any of you. One verse. One small voice singing along, and the chorus closes, and everyone goes home."

He folded his hands again.

"I didn't. Because your mother poured two glasses of lemonade. Because she sat with me and complained about arithmetic and taught me hopscotch rules I already knew, and never once, not in fifty years, treated me like a thing to be managed. She treated me like a friend who was having a very long bad day." His voice did something complicated on the word *friend*. "And it turns out that being treated kindly is the cruelest thing this town ever did to me, because it taught me I'd rather be visited than fed."

Theo stood on the porch boards and felt twelve years of rational explanations come apart quietly, like wet cardboard.

"She's gone," Theo said. "And her last entry says it's me now. The installments."

"The installments," Wren agreed gently.

"No." Theo shook their head. "No, I'm not doing fifty years of humming at the floorboards. I'm not inheriting her shift. I want to know how it ends, Wren. All of it. The song, the well, the verse everybody redacted. I want the whole thing."

Something moved behind Wren's face — hope, maybe, or fear, or the place where those two feelings share an apartment. He looked younger suddenly, if that was possible for a boy who hadn't aged since Grover Cleveland was president.

"You don't know what you're asking for."

"I know exactly what I'm asking for. I unclog drains for a living. I've seen what's at the bottom of things."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Out past the fence line, thunder muttered — the storm front that had parked itself over the valley three days ago, refusing to move, refusing to rain, just waiting, the way everything in Bellows waited.

"All right," Wren said finally. "But we start tomorrow, in daylight, and we start with the melody only. No words. Whatever happens, Theo — however curious you get, however close it feels, however much it seems like the song is *yours* — you do not sing the words. Promise me."

"Why?" Theo asked. "What happens if I sing the words?"

Wren looked at the porch swing, at the well field beyond it, at some point in 1898 that only he could see.

"Your reflection goes first," he said. "And it doesn't wait for you."
