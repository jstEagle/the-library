## 6. The Sister Who Stopped Calling

The voicemail came in at 2:47 a.m., which was the first wrong thing about it.

Naomi Halloran had kept her phone on silent for eleven years—the year Theo was born and she stopped being a woman who could be reached at all hours and became one who chose when to be found—but that night the phone buzzed against the nightstand like an insect trapped under glass, and she surfaced from a dream she wouldn't remember to look at it. The screen glowed in the dark bedroom. MARION HALLORAN, said the caller ID. HOME.

Her mother had been dead since April.

Naomi sat up in bed. Beside her, Daniel breathed the slow tide-breath of a man who could sleep through anything, and beyond the window the city did what the city always did at 2:47—a siren somewhere on Delancey, a truck downshifting on the bridge, the low electrical hum of ten thousand lives refusing to go quiet. She stared at the phone until the glow dimmed, then picked it up with both hands, the way you'd pick up something hot or holy.

Seventeen seconds of voicemail. She would know later that she'd memorized it by the third playing, and that she would play it perhaps four hundred times over the following week, in the shower, on the subway, standing at her kitchen counter at dusk with a glass of wine going warm in her hand, as if repetition were a form of translation—as if enough listenings might turn it into something that made sense.

*Naomi.* Her mother's voice, thin and papery but unmistakably hers, with that faint river-flat drawl she'd never lost in sixty-eight years of living two hundred miles inland from any river worth the name. *It's Mama. I need you to come home before the counting starts. Before the counting—* a pause, a breath that caught on something, *—before it's finished. Call your brother. He knows. He's always known. Come home, baby.*

Then static, soft as snowfall, and the line went dead.

---

She didn't sleep again that night. She sat in the kitchen with the overhead light off because the overhead light felt too honest, and she played the message over and over into the dark, and the dark played it back, and somewhere around the fifth hour she began doing what Hallorans do, which is to say she began building explanations.

A prank. Someone with a recording—Mama had been talkative near the end, chatty on the phone even when the cancer had thinned her voice to thread, and anyone could have spliced together something cruel. A scam; there were scams now that cloned voices from three seconds of audio, scams that called grandmothers pretending to be grandchildren in jail, and this was probably that, only inverted, some new cruelty aimed at the grieving. A wrong number. A glitch. A dream she hadn't quite woken from.

Each explanation held for about ninety seconds before it developed a leak.

Because the voice wasn't assembled. It breathed. It hesitated in the places Marion Halloran had always hesitated—before hard subjects, before apologies—and it used Naomi's childhood name, *baby*, drawn out to two syllables, *ba-a-by*, the way no scam algorithm would ever think to do. And it knew things. It knew Gideon. *He knows. He's always known.* Which was true, in the way that everything about her brother was true in a sideways fashion: Gideon knew things he never said, carried knowledge the way other men carried tools, close and quiet and slightly rusted.

By dawn she had stopped trying to explain it and started trying not to hear it, which lasted until 9:15, when she cracked and called the one person she'd sworn off calling.

---

Gideon answered on the fourth ring, and his voice came through the line sounding like a man speaking from inside a well.

"Naomi." Not a question. Just her name, set down between them like a chess piece.

"Gideon." She stood at her kitchen window. Forty stories below, the city crawled. "I got a voicemail last night."

"Mm."

"From Mama."

The silence that followed was so long and so specific that she pulled the phone away to check the call hadn't dropped. It hadn't. When he spoke again his voice had changed—not softer exactly, but lower, more careful, a man stepping onto ice and testing each footfall. "What did it say?"

"You're not surprised." She heard her own voice sharpen, the way it always sharpened around him, twenty years of small abandonments compressed into an edge. "That's the thing I want you to understand right now, Gideon. You are not surprised. Your mother—who has been dead for seven months—left me a voicemail at quarter to three in the morning, and your reaction is *what did it say*, like you're taking a statement."

"What did it say, Naomi."

"It said—" and here her throat closed, traitorous, grief ambushing her out of nowhere after seven months of successful avoidance, "—it said to come home before the counting. Before the counting is finished. It said to call you. It said *he knows, he's always known.*"

Another silence. This one had a texture she didn't like—a listening quality, as though the line itself were paying attention.

"I've been meaning to call you," Gideon said finally. "About coming home."

"You've been meaning to call me for seven months. You missed her funeral, Gideon. You sent flowers. Flowers, to a woman who taught you every hymn you play on that little radio show of yours, and you couldn't drive four hours to stand next to me while they put her in the ground." The words came out fast and old, worn smooth from years of internal rehearsal. "So forgive me if 'I've been meaning to call' doesn't land like a comfort."

"I couldn't be there," he said, and it wasn't an apology, it was a fact, delivered flat as a weather report, and that flatness—that infuriating, bottomless flatness—was the thing she had hated longest about him.

"Why not? One time. One honest answer, in forty years. Why weren't you at her funeral?"

Static rose on the line then, sudden and thick, a sound like wind through wires, and for half a second Naomi thought she heard—underneath it, beneath the hiss—something almost like a bell. Then it cleared.

"I'll tell you everything when you get here," Gideon said. "Book the flight. Bring Theo. And Naomi—" a pause, and in it she heard something she had never once heard in her brother's voice: fear, plain and unarmored, "—don't listen to that message after dark."

He hung up before she could ask what he meant.

---

She booked the trip that afternoon, sitting in her office with the door closed while spreadsheets bloomed and died on her monitor, and she told herself it was for practical reasons, which was the family religion, after money. Theo had fall break the following week. Flights into the regional airport outside Hymn's Ferry were cheap in October. Daniel couldn't get away from work, which suited them both—they had reached the stage of marriage where separations were a relief arranged jointly, like vacations. She told herself all of it, and all of it was even true, and none of it was the truth.

The truth was that her mother's voice had asked her to come home, and Naomi Halloran had stopped saying no to that voice sometime around the age of six.

But underneath the truth was the other thing, the sediment under the river water: she wanted to go back to fight. Twenty years ago she had left Hymn's Ferry the way you leave a burning building—quickly, without looking back, carrying nothing—and in twenty years she had returned maybe nine times, each visit shorter than the last, each departure feeling less like leaving a town and more like escaping a sentence. Because Hymn's Ferry was where their father had died, and where their mother had gone quiet in a way that never lifted, and where Gideon had stayed, growing stranger by degrees, a man who lived alone above a radio station in a dying town and spoke to strangers in the dark for a living. She had built a life in the city the way you build a seawall. She had thought it would hold.

Now her dead mother was calling through it.

---

Theo took the news the way Theo took everything—with his whole body. He was sixteen and built entirely of long bones and curiosity, a boy who dismantled alarm clocks for fun and had once, at eleven, wired the doorbell to play the first four bars of a hymn their grandmother loved, just to make her laugh. Marion had laughed until she cried. Naomi remembered that. She was remembering more lately; grief, she was learning, was less a wound than a weather system, pressure changes you could feel coming in your teeth.

"A voicemail," Theo said. They were in his room, which smelled of solder and the cedar blocks he used for his model boats. "From Grandma. Like—an actual voicemail. On your actual phone."

"On my actual phone."

"And Uncle Gideon says don't listen to it after dark." Theo turned this over with the expression he wore when a mechanism refused to behave. "That's a weirdly specific instruction. If it was a prank or a deepfake or whatever, why would he care when you listened? And how did he know it was real? You didn't even play it for him."

Naomi looked at her son—at the quick clever hands, the grandmother's gray eyes—and felt the old dread, the Halloran inheritance, settle over her like a coat. "No," she said. "I didn't."

"So he already knew something was going to call you." Theo said it simply, without drama, the way children state terrible things, and then he shrugged. "Cool. Weird, but cool. When do we leave?"

---

The week passed strangely. Naomi went to work and came home and cooked dinner and signed Theo's permission forms, and all the while the voicemail sat in her phone like a coal that wouldn't cool. She noticed things she hadn't noticed before. That she flinched now when the phone rang after sunset. That she'd begun sleeping with the hallway light on, a habit from childhood she'd thought long buried. That twice—twice—she woke at precisely 3:11 a.m. for no reason, heart hammering, certain she'd heard something just below the threshold of hearing, a tone, a tolling, a bell rung once far away and then silence, as if waiting.

On Thursday she broke her own rule. It was evening, full dark, rain ticking against the windows, and she told herself she was backing up the file, archiving it, preserving evidence—practical reasons, the family religion—and she played it one more time with Theo doing homework at the kitchen table behind her.

*Come home, baby.*

And then—she had never caught this before, not in four hundred listenings, or maybe it hadn't been there before, or maybe it needed the dark and the rain and her son's breathing in the room to surface—beneath her mother's voice, underneath the words, so faint it might have been imagination or interference or the memory of a sound rather than the sound itself:

Bells. Counting down.

She deleted nothing. She bought the tickets Tuesday morning instead.

---

They flew out Sunday, into a sky the color of old pewter, and the plane dropped through cloud cover an hour before landing and showed her the world remade: hills gone bronze and rust, fields stitched with fog, rivers running the color of hammered tin. Theo pressed his forehead to the window and narrated everything, delighted, and Naomi watched the landscape rise toward her and felt the strange double motion of homecoming—pull and push at once, love and its opposite wearing the same face.

Hymn's Ferry. Population shrinking every census. One stoplight, two churches—one of them dead these thirty years, its bell tower standing over the town like a hand raised mid-oath—and a radio station at the edge of the dark stretch of Route 9 where her brother had spent his entire adult life talking to the night.

The rental car smelled of pine air freshener and cold vinyl. Theo fiddled with the radio as they drove, scanning stations, catching fragments—a preacher, a country song, weather—and then, near the edge of town, a hymn. Low and slow, an organ and one voice, drifting in and out through the static like something surfacing to breathe.

"That's Uncle Gideon's station," Theo said. "Right? The overnight guy?"

"That's him."

"He plays music at two in the afternoon?"

Naomi glanced at the clock. 2:47. She hadn't noticed. Something turned over in her chest, slow and cold.

"No," she said. "He doesn't."

The hymn faded as they drove out of range, and neither of them spoke, and ahead the town of Hymn's Ferry gathered itself out of the fog—rooftops, water tower, the black finger of the old church's steeple pointing at a sky that seemed, to Naomi, to be listening.

She had come home to confront her brother. She had come home because her dead mother asked. She had come home with a suitcase full of twenty years of unsaid things, and a son beside her who had already heard too much, and a voicemail she could not stop hearing.

She did not yet know that she had also come home to be counted.
