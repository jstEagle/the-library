## 8. Family Arrives

The knock came at nine in the morning, which was wrong in two ways. Nobody knocked on Gideon Halloran's door at nine in the morning—Hymn's Ferry knew he slept until noon after the overnight shift—and nobody knocked at all anymore. The town had learned to call ahead or not come.

He opened the door with his hair still wet from a shower he'd taken to feel human, and Naomi stood on his porch holding a canvas bag and an expression he recognized from every family photograph where someone had just said something unforgivable.

"Hey, Gid."

Behind her, half-hidden by the doorframe like a boy who'd been coached to be polite and was already regretting it, stood Theo. Fifteen now. He had her dark hair and their father's jaw, and he was looking past Gideon into the house with the frank, inventorying stare of a kid who took things apart to see what they were made of.

"Naomi." His voice came out flat with sleep. "What—"

"You didn't answer my texts."

"I don't check texts before noon. You know that."

"I know you don't check anything before noon. That's why I drove through the night." She lifted the bag slightly, as if presenting evidence. "We're staying a week. Maybe longer. I need to talk to you about Mom, and I'd rather do it in person than over voicemail, and I figured if I gave you warning you'd find a reason to say no."

The word *voicemail* landed somewhere under his ribs and sat there, cold. He thought of the message he had not deleted, the one on his own machine, the one he played only when the house got too quiet: *Gideon, honey, come home before the counting.* Her voice, warm and slurred at the edges the way tape always made voices sound, recorded last spring, three weeks after they buried her.

He stepped aside. It was that or stand in the doorway forever, and his sister had never once lost a game of chicken.

* * *

The house had never been built for guests. He gave Naomi the spare room, which smelled of dust and old hymnals because he'd used it for storage, and Theo got the couch, which the boy accepted with a shrug that said he'd slept worse places, probably in pieces of machinery.

Gideon spent the first hour doing arithmetic. Not money—time. How long did it take to strip the basement? Could he carry two hundred cassettes up the back stairs and out to the shed without being seen? Could he pull the sleep-capture rig off the nightstand without explaining why a fifty-three-year-old man recorded himself sleeping?

The answer, he knew, was no. There were too many tapes. There were too many years. The archive wasn't a thing he owned; it was a thing he *was*, twenty years of silence compressed onto magnetic ribbon, and you couldn't hide a spine like that in a garden shed.

So he did what he could. He locked the basement door—the padlock had been there so long the paint had grown around its hasp—and he told himself Naomi wouldn't care, wouldn't ask, would be too busy loading her grievances to go poking around. And he unplugged the recorder on his nightstand and stuffed it in the sock drawer, beneath the socks, beneath everything, like a teenager hiding cigarettes.

It was absurd. He understood that even as he did it. But his hands needed something to do besides shake.

Naomi, meanwhile, moved through the kitchen like she still lived there, opening cabinets, frowning at what she found—or didn't find. "You're still eating like a bachelor," she said. "Canned chili and instant coffee. Gid, you're fifty-three."

"It keeps me honest."

"It keeps you *alone*, is what it does." She set down the mug she'd claimed and looked at him across the counter, and for a moment her anger thinned and he saw what was underneath, which was fear, which was love, which was the same thing in this family. "Mom's been gone eight months and you haven't called me once. Not once. I had to hear about Marcy Ellery's funeral from Carol Dunmore on Facebook."

"I sent flowers."

"You sent *flowers*." She laughed, but it wasn't a laugh. "You know what Mom would have said about flowers?"

He knew. He said nothing.

From the living room came the sound of Theo discovering the record player, then the sound of Theo abandoning the record player, then a long silence that Gideon would later understand had been the sound of a fifteen-year-old noticing the padlock on the basement door and filing it away.

* * *

They ate takeout Thai food at the kitchen table because Naomi refused to cook in a kitchen that hadn't been stocked since the Obama administration, and Theo asked questions between bites—not many, but precise ones, the way a surgeon asks where it hurts.

"So you do radio," Theo said. "Like, actual radio. Overnight."

"Two a.m. to five. Hymns, dedications, the request line. Twenty years."

"That's a long time to talk to nobody."

"The town listens. They just don't call much anymore."

Theo nodded slowly, chewing, eyes drifting toward the hallway, toward the basement door, and Gideon watched him do it and felt the padlock grow heavier with every second.

Naomi talked about the city, about her job, about the funeral—she circled back to the funeral twice, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. "You didn't come. Your own mother. Carol said you were on the air. Carol said you did your *show*."

"Somebody has to play the hymns."

"Gideon." She put her fork down. "I drove eleven hours. Don't make me spend the week being angry. Just tell me why."

And there it was, the question he'd spent twenty years building walls against, arriving now from inside the walls, served with pad thai. Because the truth was that he couldn't tell her why without telling her everything, and everything was two hundred cassettes behind a padlocked door and a voice in his bedroom saying *seven* in the dark.

"I'll tell you," he said. "This week. I promise. Just—give me a day or two to get my head around it."

She studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her look away first, which had never happened before, and that frightened him more than anything else that day.

* * *

He went to bed at noon like always, exhausted in the marrow-deep way that comes from lying all morning, and he lay in the dark listening to the house settle around his sleeping family and thinking: *not while they're here. Whatever happens, not while they're under this roof.*

The recorder stayed in the sock drawer. He fell asleep anyway, because the body takes its toll regardless of what the soul is hiding, and he dreamed of the church bell and woke at four-something to golden light going amber and the smell of solder.

Solder. In his house. He followed it down the hall.

Theo was sitting cross-legged on the living room floor surrounded by the guts of the sock drawer: the recorder, disassembled; the SD card, held up to the window light like a jeweler assessing a stone; a small battery-powered speaker Gideon didn't recognize, wired to something that had probably been a drone two days ago.

"Theo."

The boy didn't flinch. That was the worst part. He just looked up with his grandfather's jaw set at exactly the angle their father used to set it when he'd been caught and had decided not to pretend otherwise. "Your lock on the basement is junk, by the way. Painted shut, and the hasp screws are stripped. I didn't go in. I want you to know that. I almost did, but I didn't."

"Put it down."

"I will. I'm putting it down. But first—" Theo pressed a button on the little speaker, and the bedroom filled with Gideon's own breathing, slow and wet with sleep, recorded two nights ago, and then—

Then the other voice.

Low. Level. Patient as water wearing down stone. It came out of the small speaker and filled the room the way it filled the bedroom at 3 a.m., and Gideon felt the floor tilt under him.

*"How many are left?"*

*"Seven."*

A pause, his own breathing, and then his own voice, small and ruined with sleep: *"Seven what?"*

No answer. There was never an answer to that one.

Theo stopped the playback. The silence afterward had texture, weight, a pressure against the eardrums.

"Dad's old police scanner taught me how to spot modulated audio," Theo said quietly. "There's a second waveform under yours. It doesn't come from the room—it's not acoustic, it's on the recording itself, like it's being written directly onto the card. I ran it through a spectrogram app." He turned the phone around. On the screen, Gideon's breathing was a soft green wash, and beneath it ran a thin black line, steady, deliberate, shaped like speech. "That's it. That's the voice. It's not in the air, Uncle Gideon. It's in the machine."

Gideon sat down on the arm of the couch because his legs had made an executive decision without consulting him. "How much did you listen to?"

"All of it. Forty-one nights' worth." Theo's voice stayed level, but his hands, Gideon noticed, were wrapped around his knees, knuckles white. "Mostly it's just breathing. But some nights it asks. *How many are left. Who's next. Does the host remember.*" He swallowed. "Last Tuesday it said something else. I've listened maybe thirty times trying to decide whether to wake you up."

The afternoon light was dying outside the windows, going the color of old brass, and somewhere across town, though neither of them could have known it yet, the rope was hanging quiet in the tower of St. Ambrose.

"What did it say, Theo."

The boy looked at him, and for one second he was just a kid again, scared, wanting his mother, and then he made himself say it:

"It said, *'Tell him the count knows his name.'*"
