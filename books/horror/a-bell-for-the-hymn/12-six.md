## 12. Six

The bell woke me at 3:11, the way it always did now, as if it had learned the exact frequency of my sleeping heart and struck it like a tuning fork.

Six strokes.

I lay in the dark counting them before I was fully awake, the way you count the gaps between thunder to guess at the distance of a storm. One. The iron voice rolling down from St. Ambrose hill, through the pines, through the walls of the house my father built. Two. My bedroom window buzzing faintly in its frame. Three. Theo stirring in the spare room—I heard the creak of the cot, the small animal sound he made in his sleep when something woke him. Four. Somewhere down the street a dog began to howl and then thought better of it. Five. The silence between strokes had grown longer over the past week, as though the bell were savoring them, or as though the arm pulling the rope were tiring, or as though the thing on the other end of the rope were taking its time because it knew, now, that no one was coming to stop it.

Six.

Then nothing. The town exhaled. The dog quit. The window went still.

I lay there with my hands folded on my chest like a man in a casket, doing the arithmetic I had been doing for three nights, the arithmetic that got shorter every time, like a countdown, like a prayer said backward.

Eleven. Then nine. Then seven. Now six.

I got up because there was no point lying still. The house was cold in the way houses get in late October, cold from the inside out, and I stood at the window with the blanket around my shoulders and looked down at Hymn's Ferry laid out in its shallow bowl of valley, streetlights like drowned coins at the bottom of dark water. The church stood on its hill above everything, black against a sky the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges, and I could just make out the bell tower, the open arch where the bell hung, and I thought: it rang for someone. It has already rung for someone tonight. Somewhere down there a name has come off the list, and I won't know whose until morning, and morning was only two and a half hours away, and I knew I would not sleep, so I went downstairs and made coffee I wouldn't drink and sat in the studio with the board powered down and waited for the phone to tell me, or for the scanner, or for Ruth, or for the voice.

It was Ruth who called, at 5:40, her voice flat and careful in the way that I had learned meant the news was worse than her words.

"Harlan Peck," she said. "Found this morning by his daughter. In his bed. Natural causes, the medic says. Ninety-one years old, heart just—stopped." A pause, and I heard her turn something over, a piece of paper, the sound of it loud as a door. "Gideon. He was smiling. His daughter keeps saying it. He was smiling like he'd just heard good news."

I closed my eyes. On the desk in front of me the cassette deck sat with its red light off, patient as a held breath.

"Harlan Peck was one of the seven," I said.

"I know."

"Which means there are five names left."

"I know, Gideon."

"And one of them—"

"Don't." The word came out fast, and then slower, the way she does when she's pulling herself back inside some professional shape. "Don't say it on the phone. I'm coming over. I'm bringing the door photographs. Don't touch the recordings until I get there."

She hung up. I sat in the blue-gray dark of the studio with the coffee going cold beside me and looked at the cassette deck, and I did what she told me not to do, because I had been breaking my own rules for twenty years and I was, if nothing else, consistent.

I pressed play.

* * *

The tape was from the night before—the last thing the voice had said to me before I finally slept, if you could call it sleep, that shallow gray drifting I'd been living in since the count came down to seven. I had asked it, in the flat voice of a man reading his own obituary, whether the ones it took suffered. It was not a question I should have asked. It was not a question I wanted the answer to. But Harlan Peck was eighty-nine when his wife died and no one had ever asked him how he was doing after, not really, not once in two years, and I needed to know if what was coming for the five of them—the four of them—was mercy or something wearing mercy's coat.

The hiss first. Then the voice, low and unhurried, the way it always was, a voice like water moving under ice.

*"He will dream of the orchard,"* it said. *"He was eleven. His brother was alive. The apples were heavy that year. He will dream it the way it was, not the way it ended. He will not want to come back."*

I had stopped the tape then, last night. Tonight I let it run, because Harlan Peck was already dead and smiling, and the voice had been right, and there was a horrible comfort in that, a comfort I hated myself for feeling.

The hiss continued. And then, because the voice always knew when I was listening, it spoke again, and this time it sounded—there was no other word for it—*satisfied.*

*"Six,"* it said. *"You counted with me. I heard you. You are getting good at this, Gideon."*

I stopped the tape. My hands were not shaking. That was the worst part. Twenty years ago they would have shaken. Ten years ago they would have shaken. Now they lay on the deck quiet and steady, and I understood that the shaking had been a kind of innocence, and that I had spent it all.

* * *

Ruth arrived at six-fifteen with the photographs and a thermos of coffee she poured without asking, and Theo came down in his socks with his laptop under his arm and his hair standing up like he'd been electrocuted by his own dreams, and the three of us sat in the studio that had been mine alone for twenty years and looked at the evidence of a thing that had no evidence.

"Harlan Peck," Ruth said, laying the photographs out on the console between the faders like tarot cards. "Died between two and four a.m. Medic put it around three. Bell rang at 3:11. Six strokes." She tapped the photograph of the church door—that great slab of oak, older than the town's charter, with the names scratched into it in a hand that no one could identify because it wasn't any hand at all, the letters too regular, too patient, like something grown rather than written. One of the names had a line through it now. Fresh. The wood around the stroke was pale, raw, like the tree itself had flinched.

"Harlan Peck," she said again, and drew the line in the air with her finger. "Crossed off."

"Five left," Theo said. He'd pulled up the list on his laptop, the one we'd built together, the archive cross-referenced, the pattern he'd found glowing in it like a vein of ore: *the voice only counts souls owed.* Deaths wronged, covered up, forgotten. And the corollary we'd tested, the one that had given us three nights of something almost like hope: *a death acknowledged and mourned closes the account.* Marcy's name had come off the door the morning after we held her memorial, after her sister finally said out loud, in front of forty people in the VFW hall, that Marcy had died alone because the family let an old grudge do the talking for twenty years. We had said the true thing. The door had heard it. The line through her name had appeared like a scar healing.

"So we do it again," Theo said. "Five memorials. Five truths. We can do five in—" he looked at the window, at the light still not coming, "—we don't even know how much time we have. The bell's been ringing every night. Six last night, six tonight. It's not waiting for us anymore. It's on a schedule."

"Or it's counting down," Ruth said, "to something."

None of us said it. None of us had to. It hung in the studio air between the foam panels and the dead microphones, the thing we had all seen on the door and all refused to read aloud.

Because the names were not in the order they'd been scratched. We'd checked. The order on the door was the order of the deaths, going back decades, the oldest debts first. Except the last name. The last name had been scratched in a deeper hand, a later hand, and it sat at the bottom of the list like a signature on a letter.

*Halloran.*

My name. My father's name before me, and mine after him, because the debt had passed down the family like the house, like the chin, like the silence.

"The count is descending," I said, because someone had to, and it might as well be me; I had spent twenty years saying the unsayable into a microphone at two in the morning, and this was only more of the same, only with the whole town listening instead of the sleepless and the grieving. "Eleven, nine, seven, six. It's not random. It was never random. It's a countdown, and it's counting down to the last name on the door."

Theo closed his laptop slowly, the way you close a door on a room you don't want to look into.

"Dad—"

"It's my name, Theo." I said it plainly. I found that I could say it plainly, and that frightened me more than the name did. "It's been my name since the night the voice first said it. I just kept hoping the list would run out of road before it got to me."

* * *

After Ruth left—I made her leave; she'd been up thirty hours and arguing with her about it was the only normal thing left in my life—I sat with Theo at the kitchen table while the morning came up gray and thin over the hills, the kind of light that makes even a living town look like a photograph of itself.

He was seventeen. He had his mother's stubbornness and my father's hands, the long clever fingers that were always taking something apart, and he sat across from me turning his cold coffee mug in slow circles, and I watched him decide to ask the question, the way you watch weather come in.

"Does Mom know?" he said. "About any of it. The archive. The voice. The name on the door."

"She knows I don't sleep," I said. "She knows why we stopped being a family. She never asked for details. I never offered them. It was the deal we made without making it."

"That's a terrible deal."

"It's the only deal I knew how to make." I looked at my hands. They were still steady. "Theo, when this is over—whatever over looks like—I need you to take your mother and go. I mean it. If the count gets to one and the name at the bottom of the list is still mine, I need you both a long way from Hymn's Ferry."

He set the mug down. His jaw did something that was older than seventeen.

"You said the host who listens can be added or subtracted," he said. "That's what the voice told us. Your twenty years of listening made you part of the mechanism. But it works both ways, right? That's what it *said.* Added *or subtracted.*" He leaned forward, and his eyes had that brightness they got when he was inside a problem, the brightness I'd been watching since he was four and took apart the toaster. "So we subtract you. We close the account. We do for your name what we did for Marcy's. We say the true thing out loud."

"The true thing about my father is a confession," I said. "It's not a memorial. It's an indictment. And the man it indicts is still alive, Theo, and rich, and this town has been looking the other way for forty years, and I'd be asking it to stop."

"Then ask."

I almost laughed. Almost. It came out as something else, something between a breath and a wound.

But he was right, and I knew he was right, and the knowing sat in my chest like a coal. The count was descending. Five. Then four. Then three. I had watched it come down the stairs of the numbers one at a time, patient as winter, and I knew now what I had refused to know since the first night the second voice answered a question I never asked: it was not coming for the town. It was coming for *me.* It had been coming for me for twenty years, dialing my request line at two in the morning, filling my basement with two hundred voices, teaching me, training me, walking me down the long corridor of the dead one cassette at a time, until I arrived at the last door, and the last door had my name on it.

The voice had said once, in the early tapes, in 2005, on a night I'd never been able to forget no matter how I tried: *"Every listener is called home eventually, Gideon. Some just get more invitations than others."*

I had thought it was a joke. I had archived it under *miscellaneous.*

The phone rang.

Not the studio line. The house line, the old rotary in the hallway that I kept because my father had installed it, because some part of me had always known I'd want to be standing in his house, holding his phone, when the call finally came for me.

Theo and I looked at each other down the length of the kitchen table, and neither of us moved, and the phone rang a third time, a fourth, and outside, faint and impossible in the full light of morning, from the hill above the town, from the tower of the old hymn church where no hand had pulled a rope since 1987—

the bell began to ring.

Five strokes. In daylight. Ahead of schedule. As if it had heard us talking, as if it had heard Theo say *then ask,* and it was not willing to wait, and it was not willing to lose, and it wanted me to know, before I even lifted the receiver, that the number on the door was already changing.

Theo picked up the phone on the sixth ring and held it out to me without a word, and I took it, and put it to my ear, and the line was full of that hiss I knew better than my own breathing, and underneath the hiss, low and unhurried, satisfied, the voice said my name the way my mother used to say it when I was in trouble.

*"Gideon,"* it said. *"Come and count with me."*
