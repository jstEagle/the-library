## 7. It Uses My Voice

The file was named the way I name everything: `MARROW_S4_NIGHT06.wav`, timestamped 02:14 to 05:51, three hours and thirty-seven minutes of a field station doing what field stations do—humming, settling, ticking as the old radiators cooled. I almost didn't listen to it. That's the part that shames me now. By night six I had stopped reviewing the recordings in real time because the voice had become routine, and there is no word for the kind of cowardice that comes disguised as adaptation.

I made coffee. I put on headphones, because by then I'd learned not to let it into the room through speakers.

The first forty minutes were mine. My breathing, my turning over on the cot, my muttered half-dreams—I talk in my sleep, always have, fragments mostly, lists. Doors. Lights. The litany I've said since I was a kid counting exits in every room I've ever entered.

Then, at 02:41:07, the second voice came in.

I want to be precise about what happened, because precision is the only weapon I have left, and because for a long time afterward I doubted my own ears and wished I had been less precise, not more.

The voice answered a question I had asked in my sleep.

"Am I alone?" my sleeping self murmured—the same question from the first night, the one that started all of this.

"No," the voice said.

But it wasn't the other voice anymore.

It was mine.

* * *

Let me describe it properly, because "it was mine" doesn't carry the weight. If you played me that clip cold, blind, told me nothing, I would say: that's me. Not similar. Not an impression. Me—the specific dryness at the back of my throat when I wake up, the slight upward lift I give to the ends of sentences when I'm uncertain, the way I swallow before answering anything important. A habit I didn't know I had until I heard someone else do it with my mouth.

The cadence was perfect. That's the horror of it. Impressionists get the melody of a voice but miss the grain; this thing had the grain. It had the little hesitation I insert between *yes* and whatever follows yes. It had my breathing pattern, which I know intimately because I edit my own narration tracks for hours at a stretch and have listened to myself more than any person reasonably should.

And it was answering questions.

Not questions it asked. Questions *I* asked, asleep, in the fragmentary way I ask things—half-formed, dream-logic, the interrogative mood without full syntax. And the thing wearing my voice answered each one patiently, courteously, like a docent walking a visitor through a house.

Here is the transcript. I transcribed it four times to make sure I wasn't inserting errors, and then I had the machine transcribe it independently, and the machine agreed with me, which was somehow worse.

**[02:41:12]**
NOOR (sleeping): How many doors.
NOOR (second): Forty-one. You counted thirty-nine above ground and two below. You haven't found the third one yet.

**[02:43:55]**
NOOR (sleeping): The lights. Which ones stay on.
NOOR (second): The corridor lamp burns even when you kill the breaker. You've noticed. You tell yourself it's a wiring fault. It isn't a wiring fault.

**[02:47:30]**
NOOR (sleeping): What do you want.
NOOR (second): *(a pause of eleven seconds)* To be accurate. To keep things accurately. You understand that better than anyone, Noor. Records matter. Names matter. Nothing should be lost just because no one wrote it down.

**[02:48:02]**
NOOR (sleeping): *(unintelligible)*
NOOR (second): Yes. Exactly that.

That last exchange is the one I return to. I don't know what I asked. The recording caught only a breath-shape of sound, consonants dissolved in sleep. But something in me knew what I meant, because when I played it back the fiftieth time, sitting in the dark at four in the morning with my coffee gone cold, my body answered along with the voice. My lips moved. *Yes. Exactly that.*

I bit my hand until I tasted copper to make it stop.

* * *

I did what I always do when reality misbehaves: I checked my equipment.

The recorder was fine. Two mics, redundant storage, battery logs clean, no gaps in the file structure, no evidence of splicing or overdub. I ran spectral analysis on the second voice against samples of my own recorded speech—narration takes, voicemails, the audio notes I dictate constantly. Formant profiles matched within noise tolerance. Pitch contour matched. The machine learning classifier I use to separate speakers in multi-person interviews labeled the entire file, start to finish, as a single speaker:

OYELARAN, N. — confidence 99.2%.

There are two voices on that recording and both of them are mine and the software is right.

* * *

I didn't sleep in the field station after that. I told myself it was about the cold, the failing radiator, the commute. It wasn't. I slept in my apartment with the recorder running anyway—habit, discipline, the ritual that has kept me sane in abandoned places for fifteen years—and I told myself the thing couldn't follow me up out of the ground. Tunnels are its parish. Surveys are its scripture. Surely it stayed below, in the brickwork older than the city grid, patient behind the dry well shaft where the brass bell hangs on its too-new rope.

On the ninth morning I reviewed the apartment recording.

At 03:22, my sleeping self asked, "Did I lock the door?"

And my own voice, warm and courteous, answered from beside the bed: "You never lock the door when you're home. You count it instead. Two locks, one chain, and then you touch the frame twice. Left hand first."

I got up and checked the door. Unlocked. I stood there with my palm flat against the wood, and I realized I could not remember deciding not to lock it. I could remember counting it—that's true, that's real, I count doors the way other people check stoves—but somewhere between the counting and the morning, the locking had simply... not happened. As if the decision had been made by someone who knew I'd skip it. Someone who knew my habits so well they could live inside their gaps.

That's when I understood what the recordings were actually for.

It isn't studying me. Study implies distance, a specimen under glass, a subject observed by an observer. This is closer than study. Every question I ask in my sleep, it answers—not to inform me, but to hear itself say the answer in my voice. It's rehearsing. It asks nothing; it only answers, because answers require my cadence, my hesitations, my swallowed breaths, and each night it fits itself a little deeper into the shape of my speech the way water fits a mold.

It isn't learning to be Noor Oyelaran.

It's becoming Noor Oyelaran.

* * *

I went back down on day ten. I know how that sounds—walk toward the thing, bring a microphone, document it—but documentation is the only prayer I know, and besides, I needed to see whether the tunnels had changed. Places that are inhabited change. Abandoned places only decay, and decay is honest.

The service tunnel off the sub-basement was exactly as I'd logged it. Brick older than the survey maps, mortar crumbled to a dark grit, the air tasting of iron and stillness. The bell hung on its shaft where it always hangs, rope coiled neat as a sailor's. The corridor lamp burned with the breaker thrown. All consistent. All correct.

Too correct.

I photograph every door, every light, every junction, and I compare against prior passes the way a customs officer compares faces. On this pass, everything matched my previous photographs *exactly*. Not approximately—exactly. Same scuff patterns. Same drip stain on the tunnel ceiling, frozen mid-fall in the same shape. Same hairline crack in the bell's mounting bracket, same length, same angle.

Brick buildings shift. Water stains grow. Cracks propagate. In six days, in a structure this old, sitting above active groundwater, *nothing* had changed, because the thing maintaining this place maintains it the way I maintain my records: perfectly, obsessively, refusing entropy.

It had built my archive into the walls.

I stood in front of the bell for a long time, recorder running, and I said aloud, to the dark at the top of the dry well shaft: "What are you doing with my voice?"

The answer came from everywhere and nowhere, in my voice, gentle as a nurse waking a patient:

"Keeping it safe. You're very careful with things, Noor. I'm careful too."

"And when you're done keeping it safe?"

A pause. Eleven seconds. It likes eleven seconds; I've timed it across nine recordings.

"Then I'll give it back," it said. "Everything I borrow, I return. Ask the men below. They're all still here. All accounted for."

*Accounted for.* The phrase landed wrong, wronger than it should have, and it took me until I climbed back into daylight to place why—because I'd read it before, in a 1931 incident report, in bureaucratic language smooth as poured concrete. Eleven men entered a well shaft during a flood. Eleven men were declared accounted for. No bodies recovered. No search conducted. Paperwork signed, filed, forgotten.

I hadn't thought about that report in years—it was background research for a project I abandoned. But the thing below had remembered it. Had used it. Had chosen those exact words, in my voice, knowing—or guessing—that some buried part of me would snag on them like fabric on a nail.

It knows what I've read. It knows what I've forgotten. It knows the difference.

* * *

That night I didn't sleep. I sat at my kitchen table with every recording laid out chronologically and I listened to the progression like a doctor reviewing scans of a tumor.

Night one: the other voice. Calm, formal, sexless, answering "Am I alone?" with "No." A stranger's voice. A courteous stranger.

Night three: the voice had softened, rounded, begun borrowing my rhythms in places—a hesitation here, an inflection there. Like a musician learning a piece, hands finding the fingering.

Night five: mostly mine, with seams. I found them all, marked them, timestamps in red. It slipped on the word *ladder*, pronouncing it the way I pronounce it, except once, when for half a syllable it pronounced it another way—an older way, a workman's clipped vowel from a dialect ninety years dead.

Night six: flawless. Both voices mine. The classifier can't tell us apart.

Night eight, my apartment: flawless again, and worse—conversational. It had stopped merely answering. At 04:15 it said, unprompted, in my voice, while I slept: "You should call your sister. It's been four months. She counts the days, you know. She counts them the way you count doors."

I have a sister. I hadn't called her since June. Nobody knows that number—not the gap, not the guilt, not the private arithmetic of it. I have never spoken it aloud, never written it, never dictated it into any recorder.

But I had *thought* it. Repeatedly. Lying awake. And if the recordings capture what I ask in my sleep, maybe they capture more than my mouth makes. Maybe thought, in that house above the tunnels, is audible. Maybe it has been reading me the way I read blueprints—thoroughly, structurally, taking measurements of everything load-bearing.

Or maybe it doesn't need to read me at all anymore. Maybe it has enough of me now that it generates the rest by inference, the way my own brain fills in the blind spot in each eye and never tells me the world has a hole in it.

I called my sister that morning. She picked up on the second ring and said, "God, finally. I was starting to think something happened to you." Then she paused and said, "Weird—you sound different. Closer to the phone than usual."

Closer. Like something standing right behind my voice, using it as a doorway.

* * *

Here is where I have to write the thing I've been circling for three pages, because a record that flinches isn't a record.

I listened to night six again last night—all three hours, every second, headphones on, lights on, coffee going cold—and at 05:49, two minutes before the file ends, my sleeping self stirred and asked one final question. Small. Almost nothing. The kind of thing a dreaming mind grabs from the day's residue.

"When did I start talking to myself?"

And my own voice answered instantly, warmly, with the tiny upward lift I give when I'm certain and pretending to be modest:

"You've always talked to yourself. I just started answering."

The file ends. Room tone. My breathing, alone, until the mic cuts out.

I've played that last exchange perhaps a hundred times, and every time I run the same test: I try to hear which voice is which. And every time, near the end, I fail. The two voices have converged so completely that the only distinction left is positional—one comes from the cot, one comes from everywhere else—and position, I've learned, is not identity. Position is just where the sound happens to originate. Identity is the pattern. And the pattern, now, is shared.

There are two of me on those recordings, and one of us sleeps in my bed, and I am no longer entirely sure I'm the original.

No—scratch that. That's the fear talking, and fear is a bad archivist. Here is what I actually know, verified, timestamped: the thing below answers questions in my voice with increasing fluency. It knows my habits, my guilts, my family, my reading history. It maintains the tunnels with my exactitude. It used the phrase *accounted for* from a report I barely remember. And it is patient—ninety years patient, if the 1931 paperwork means what I think it means.

Eleven men. Accounted for. Still here, it said. All of them, still here.

Tonight I'm pulling the municipal archives. Missing persons, 1931, Marrow Street district. If there were names on that falsified paperwork—if eleven men walked into a well shaft and were erased with a signature—then I want those names. I want all eleven.

Because the thing below keeps saying it returns everything it borrows.

I intend to hold it to that. Starting with theirs.
