## 14. Erasure Attempt

The first file to die was the one Noor trusted most.

They noticed it at 6:14 in the morning, sitting cross-legged on the floor of the field station with cold coffee going stale beside them, scrolling through the archive folder they had maintained for eleven years without a single loss. *MARROW_STATION_MASTER*. Four hundred and twelve files. Photographs of every door, every valve wheel, every hairline crack in the lower levels. The survey comparisons. The transcriptions of nine nights of sleep recordings.

The folder opened to show three hundred and six files.

Noor counted them twice, then a third time, because counting was what they did, because counting was the wall they had built between themselves and a world that kept trying to make them disappear into it. Three hundred and six. One hundred and six files gone, and not deleted in any way a machine understood—no trash folder, no recovery utility, no shadow copy. The drive simply reported that those files had never existed. The space they had occupied showed as free. The directory log showed no modification date after the night Noor had first played the recordings aloud.

That was the detail that made their hands go cold. Not the loss. The *cleanliness*.

Files corrupted by failing drives looked like crime scenes: fragments, checksums screaming, sectors marked bad. This was different. This was a room that had been tidied by someone who loved tidying. Someone patient. Someone courteous.

"No," Noor said aloud, to the laptop, to the station, to whatever was listening. "No, you don't get to do this."

The laptop's fan cycled once, softly, like a breath drawn through teeth.

* * *

By eight o'clock Noor had spread their defenses across the floor of the station like a triage unit. Two external drives. A second laptop, air-gapped, never networked. The cloud backups—three separate services, because Noor had learned young that anything held in one place could be erased from one place. And beneath all of it, the paper: four notebooks, dense with their cramped, ruled handwriting. Nine nights of transcription. Every question the voice had answered. Every answer it had given in Noor's own cadence.

They checked the cloud accounts first.

The login page for the primary service greeted them with a message Noor had never seen before: *This account does not exist.* They typed the credentials again, slowly, letter by letter, the way you'd speak to someone hard of hearing. Same message. The secondary service loaded fine—and showed an account containing nothing but a welcome email dated three days ago. As if the account had been created fresh, empty, waiting. The third service wouldn't load at all. The progress bar filled to ninety percent and hung there, breathing.

Noor sat back on their heels and made themself look at the pattern instead of panicking into it. That was the discipline. Panic was just another kind of noise, and noise was what the thing below traded in—it wore voices, it filled silences, it *answered questions nobody asked*. But records were quiet. Records were the opposite of the well. So it was coming for the records first.

It wasn't deleting evidence, Noor realized. It was deleting *witnesses*.

* * *

The external drives were worse, because the corruption there had style.

The first drive—the one that had lived in the Faraday box until two nights ago, when Noor had taken it out to compare transcriptions—was intact but wrong. The photographs were all still there, all four hundred door studies and light logs, but when Noor opened them, the metadata told a different story than the images did. The timestamps had been redistributed. Photos Noor knew they'd taken on the fourth night now claimed to be from the ninth. The sequence of their own descent into the tunnels had been shuffled like a deck of cards, so that the unmapped level appeared in the record *before* the documented sub-basement. Anyone reading the archive would conclude the photographer was careless. Or lying. Or losing their grip on time.

The second drive was honest about its violence. Half its contents had been replaced with a single file, duplicated across every freed sector: a text document containing only a list of names.

Eleven names. The roster from 1931. Okafor. Lindqvist. Marchetti. Ferreira-adjacent, almost—Ada's grandfather had signed as witness. And at the bottom, in the same plain type, a twelfth line:

**OYELARAN, N. — PENDING**

Noor stared at that word for a long time. *Pending.* Not condemned, not claimed. Pending. The way a form sits in a tray. The way a decision waits for a signature. The entity didn't rage or threaten; it filed paperwork. It had learned bureaucracy from the men who'd invited it in, and it had learned it well—well enough to know that the cruelest thing you can do to a person is make them a case number awaiting resolution.

"You'll have to do better than formatting," Noor said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "I've been backing up my life since I was nineteen. You're not the first thing that tried to erase me."

The station lights flickered—not a brownout, not a fault. A rhythm. Three short, one long. Then again. Three short, one long. Like something tapping out a reply down a very long wire.

* * *

It was the notebooks that finally broke something open in Noor's chest.

Paper couldn't be corrupted remotely. Paper was the last redoubt, the analog ark, and Noor had written in those notebooks with the devotion of a monk—every exchange transcribed twice, key passages underlined, dates verified against three clocks. They picked up Notebook Four, the one holding the transcription of the night the voice had first used Noor's own cadence, the night that proved it wasn't learning Noor but *becoming* Noor.

The pages were blank.

Not torn out. Not burned. Blank, the way pages are blank in a brand-new notebook, with that faint toothy texture of unmarked paper. Noor thumbed through it fast, faster, tearing toward the back where the final entries should be, and found the same absence throughout—a perfect, total whiteness that no eraser could achieve, no bleach, no solvent. Ink doesn't leave paper like this. Ink leaves ghosts, shadows, pressure marks in the fibers. These pages had never been written on, as far as the paper itself was concerned.

Except Noor's fingers remembered the weight of the pen. Their wrist remembered the hours. Somewhere in their body was a full transcript of nine nights, and nowhere in the world outside their body was there any proof.

Notebook One: blank. Notebook Two: blank except for the first page, which now read, in Noor's own handwriting—which was the part that made Noor sit down hard on the concrete floor—*Doors counted: 412. Lights logged: 87. All correct. Nothing further needed.*

Their own hand. Their own slanted, over-precise script, the script teachers had mocked and archivists had praised. The thing wasn't just erasing the record anymore. It was *authoring* it. It was writing Noor's documentation for them, in Noor's hand, concluding the investigation on Noor's behalf. *Nothing further needed.* Case closed. Go home. Be nobody in particular.

Noor laughed—one short, ugly sound with no humor in it—and heard how close their laugh had come lately to sounding like someone else's.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about being a person who documents things: your records are not *about* you, they are *of* you. Eleven years of door counts and light logs and transit stubs and timestamped photographs are not evidence of some external world. They are the scaffolding on which a self hangs. Take away the scaffold and what's left isn't freedom. What's left is negotiable.

Noor understood this viscerally, the way you understand a law of physics by falling. They had spent their whole adult life building archives because they had grown up feeling unarchived—unseen at school, unlisted in family photos, the child whose name adults got wrong even after correction. The documentation hadn't been a hobby. It had been an argument: *I am here. Here is the proof. Here are four hundred doors I stood in front of, and each photograph says a witness existed.*

And now something ancient and patient was answering that argument, line by line, file by file, with the bureaucratic calm of the 1931 engineers who had declared eleven drowned men *accounted for*. The machinery of erasure hadn't changed in ninety years. It had just gotten faster, and it had gotten *polite*.

Without the notebooks, who could say Noor had ever spoken to the voice? Without the drives, who could say the unmapped level existed? Without the cloud accounts, who was Noor Oyelaran but a claim without corroboration—and the entity had already entered its twelfth line into the ledger. *Pending.*

"I am here," Noor said to the empty station, and hated how much it sounded like asking permission.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon with the desperation of a drowning person bailing water, and the water came in faster than their hands.

They re-transcribed from memory—three nights' worth, the exchanges they could recite verbatim because they'd read them forty times—and wrote it all in a cheap composition book bought that morning from a bodega eight blocks away, paid for in cash, carried home in a paper bag, chosen at random precisely because it had no history. Fresh paper. Unwritten fate. They photographed each finished page with the old film camera they kept for exactly this kind of paranoia, the kind that recorded onto silver halide instead of silicon, the kind with no wireless radio inside it at all.

Then they hid the negatives in three places, and while they were hiding the third set—in the false bottom of the equipment case, under the coil of rope—they caught themself humming.

Humming a tune they didn't know. A work song, maybe, or half of one, with the lilt of the 1930s in it, the kind of thing men might have sung hauling cable in a flooded shaft. Noor stopped. Stood very still in the dimness of the station. Listened to their own throat as if it belonged to a stranger.

"Tomas," they said quietly. Testing the name in their mouth the way you test a stair that might be rotten.

The hum rose again, unbidden, and this time Noor recognized the shape of it—the exact melody from the recording, night seven, the moment the second voice had slipped and used the rigger's name. The song was Tomas's. It had been living in Noor's chest like a tenant, and now that the paperwork of Noor's identity was burning, the tenant was redecorating.

Noor put both hands flat on the table and breathed until the humming stopped. It took four minutes. They timed it, because timing things was the wall, and tonight the wall was all they had.

* * *

At dusk they did the inventory, and the inventory was a eulogy.

Surviving: the composition book, half-filled. Thirty-one frames of film, undeveloped. The original sleep recording—night one, the master file—still sealed in the small Faraday box bolted under the floor panel, untouched, because Noor had hidden it there weeks ago out of habit and had never once played it outside the box. Everything else: gone or falsified. Four hundred doors, unwitnessed. Eighty-seven lights, dark. Nine nights of conversation, unwritten. An entire documented self, reduced to a shoebox of analog scraps and whatever remained in one fallible human memory.

And the memory—that was the front line now. Noor could feel it. Every time they tried to recall the exact wording of the entity's offer, the phrasing slid, softened, rearranged itself into something more reasonable, more tempting, more *fair*. They remembered it offering safety; had it offered safety, or had it offered *belonging*? They remembered refusing; had they refused, or had they said *not yet*, which was a different word entirely? The past was supposed to be the one place records couldn't follow you and lie. But with the records gone, the past was just a story Noor told, and stories could be edited by anyone with patience.

The entity had patience. Ninety years of it.

Noor sat in the dark of the field station, the composition book on their knees, the film canisters lined up like ammunition, and forced themself to think like a documentarian instead of a victim. What survives an archive fire? Duplicates in other hands. Dispersal. Redundancy through trust. You don't beat erasure by hiding the last copy—you beat it by making sure there is no *last* copy, by putting the truth into so many keeping hands that forgetting becomes impossible.

Which meant Ada Ferreira. Which meant the historical society. Which meant getting the diary, the roster, the surviving recording out of Noor's possession and into the world before dawn of whatever morning the entity had circled on its calendar.

Which meant moving *now*.

Noor reached for the floor panel, fingers finding the recessed edge by muscle memory, and lifted it. The Faraday box sat in its nest of foam, dented tin, latch green with age. They drew it out and set it on the table, and their hand hovered over the latch without opening it, because they already knew what was inside, and knowing was enough.

Except it wasn't. Because as their fingers closed on the clasp, Noor heard—clearly, from directly behind them, in a voice that was Noor's own voice, pitched low and reasonable and terribly kind—

"You should listen to it one more time. Before you decide."

There was no one behind them. There was no one behind them, and Noor turned anyway, slow, the way you turn toward a sound in a house you thought you knew, and the station was empty, and the latch was warm under their thumb, warmer than tin has any right to be, and somewhere far below—down past the sub-basement, down past the brickwork older than the city grid, down at the lip of a dry well shaft—a bell rope swayed against brass with a sound too soft to be real.

Dawn was coming. Noor just didn't know yet which dawn.
