## 15. One Copy Left

The notebook was blank.

Not empty—blank. There is a difference, and Noor Oyelaran knew the difference the way a locksmith knows the difference between a locked door and a bricked-up one. Empty means nothing was ever written. Blank means something was taken out, and recently, and thoroughly. They ran their thumb across page forty-one, where three nights ago they had transcribed the exchange about the childhood street—the one where the voice had recited the name of the cul-de-sac in Ibadan with a tenderness that had made Noor's skin crawl for an hour afterward. The paper felt normal. It smelled normal. But under the desk lamp, tilted at an angle, there was the faintest ghost of pressure in the fibers, the memory of a pen's weight, like a bruise on skin.

The files were worse. Noor sat at the field station's folding table at four in the morning, laptop open, watching the directory refresh itself like a wound refusing to close. `TRANSCRIPTS_NIGHT1.docx` — corrupted. `TRANSCRIPTS_NIGHT2.docx` — corrupted. `DOOR_COUNT_MASTER.xlsx` — the file opened, and every cell was zero. Eleven months of counting, two hundred and six doors across the Marrow Street complex, all of it rendered as a clean grid of nothing. Not deleted. Zeroed. As if someone had gone through and answered each entry with the same polite refusal.

*It isn't stealing my work,* Noor thought, hands flat on the table to stop them from shaking. *It's editing me.*

The cloud backups were gone too—or rather, they had never existed, according to the service's cheerful interface. *No backups found for this account.* The support chat offered condolences and a link to a help article titled "Understanding Data Loss." Noor closed the laptop before they could read it, because reading it felt like agreeing to it.

They stood in the middle of the field station—the decommissioned pump house's ground floor, which the city still technically owned but no longer remembered—and did what they always did when the world stopped matching its own records. They counted.

One door: the loading bay, steel, rusted at knee height. Two: the office corridor, hollow-core, replaced sometime in the nineties. Three: the electrical closet, padlocked by someone who hadn't worked here in decades. Four through nine: the gallery of access panels along the north wall. Ten: the stairwell down to the documented sub-basement. Eleven—

Eleven was the door on no survey. The door to the unmapped level. The door below which a brass bell hung on a dry well shaft, its rope worn smooth by hands that were not supposed to exist.

"Two hundred and six," Noor said aloud, to hear a voice in the room that was theirs. "Two hundred and six doors, eleven lights, one bell."

The building said nothing back. That was almost comforting. Almost.

* * *

The Faraday box lived in the bottom of Noor's equipment duffel, wrapped in a sock, because old habits from years of urban exploration died hard. Signal-proof, EMP-proof, paranoia-proof. Noor had bought it back when they'd started documenting sites with active security systems, worried about remote wipes of camera footage. It had seemed like the kind of caution that would never pay off, the way carrying a whistle seems foolish until the night you fall into a storm drain.

Inside the box, in a padded sleeve, was a single SD card.

Night One. The original sleep recording. Unedited, untransferred, never uploaded anywhere—which meant, Noor realized with a lurch, never touched by whatever had reached through the cloud and the laptop and the notebooks and eaten their archive down to the bone. Whatever the thing below Marrow Street could do to data, it apparently needed the data to be reachable. Connected. Findable.

This card had been dark and silent and sealed in metal for nine days.

Noor held it up to the lamp. It looked like any other SD card: small, gray, disposable. Nine days ago it had recorded eight hours of Noor sleeping alone in this room, and somewhere in those eight hours, a second voice had begun answering questions Noor didn't remember asking. *Am I alone?* — *No.* That first exchange, the one that had turned their blood to well water when they played it back. Every subsequent night's recording had been copied to the laptop within hours. This one hadn't been. This one was the only artifact in Noor's entire documentation of the tunnels that the thing had never seen.

Or so Noor desperately hoped.

They dug the backup recorder out of the duffel—a battered Zoom H1n, older than the laptop, with a headphone jack that crackled if you breathed on it. They inserted the card. Their hand hovered over the play button for a long moment.

Here was the thought they didn't want to finish: *if it edited everything else, maybe it edited this too, before I sealed it away. Maybe there's nothing on this card but eight hours of my own breathing, and the version I heard the first time was already the lie.*

But no. The timeline didn't allow it. They had listened to Night One the morning after, heard the second voice, made notes in the margin of their paper log—notes that were now blank pages—and only afterward had the card gone into the box. If the thing had gotten to it, it had gotten to it before Noor ever heard the voice at all. Which would mean the voice had never existed. Which would mean Noor had spent nine days losing their mind in a pump house over an auditory hallucination.

That possibility was, in its way, more frightening than the alternative. Hallucinations could be treated. What was happening to Noor's records could not be explained by any diagnosis Noor wanted to live inside.

They pressed play.

* * *

The first hour was exactly what it should have been. Room tone. The tick of the cooling pump housing. Noor's breathing, slow and even, the breathing of a person who had finally, after months of sleeping in strangers' buildings, found a rhythm they trusted. At 11:47 p.m., a car passed on Marrow Street above, tires hissing on wet asphalt. At 12:15, the furnace in the neighboring lot kicked on with its familiar asthmatic rattle.

Noor listened with headphones, eyes closed, cataloguing sounds the way other people catalogued birds. This was the discipline that had made their documentation respected in a half-dozen obscure forums: not just photographs, but the full sensory record. A place wasn't mapped until you knew how it sounded at 3 a.m.

At 2:03 a.m., their own voice spoke.

*"Is anyone there?"*

Noor's eyes snapped open. They didn't remember saying it. They had reviewed this recording once before, days ago, and the question had been there then too—but hearing it now, after the notebooks went blank and the spreadsheets zeroed themselves, hit differently. It sounded less like sleep-talk and more like an interview. Like a form being filled out.

And then the answer, soft as a hand laid on a shoulder:

*"No."*

The voice was calm. Formal. Courteous in a way that made courtesy feel like a threat. Noor had described it in their lost transcripts as *a receptionist's voice, if the receptionist has been dead a long time and doesn't mind.* They had tried, in those early hours, to place its age, its gender, its region. It refused placement. It sounded like whoever you would most expect to hear, which was the problem.

Noor let the recording run, following along against their memory of the lost transcript. *What is this place?* — *Older than the grid.* *Who built it?* — *Men who were drowning.* Each answer arriving slightly before it should have, as if the voice had rehearsed. Each question asked in Noor's own sleeping voice, flat and uninflected, the questions of someone reading off a card.

Then, at 2:31 a.m., the exchange that had kept Noor awake for two full nights:

*"Do you know my name?"*

A pause. On the first listen, days ago, the pause had lasted perhaps four seconds. Now, wearing headphones in a dark room with their whole archive erased, Noor heard what they hadn't been equipped to hear before: the pause wasn't hesitation. It was *savoring*. The sound of something tasting a word before serving it.

*"Noor Oyelaran,"* the voice said. And then, gently, almost fondly, in the same breath, in the same voice, in a register so close to Noor's own that the headphones made it indistinguishable:

*"Tomas."*

Noor ripped the headphones off.

The room was silent. The lamp hummed. Their heart was going like a fist on a door.

They sat very still for a long time, staring at the little recorder, its green level meter frozen mid-flicker. Then they rewound. 2:29. Played it again. *"Do you know my name?"* Pause. *"Noor Oyelaran."* Pause. *"Tomas."*

Again. Louder this time, though volume changed nothing—it wasn't a matter of audibility, it was a matter of belief.

*"Noor Oyelaran."* Beat. *"Tomas."*

On the first listen, nine days ago, Noor's brain had folded that second word into the first. Heard it as an echo, a trailing syllable, a compression artifact. The mind does that; the mind is eager to make strange things ordinary. *Oyelaran-Tomas* had simply smoothed itself into *Oyelaran*, the way a stutter smooths into speech when you're not paying attention.

But it wasn't a stutter. It wasn't an echo. It was a slip.

*Tomas.* The name from the roster. Rigger, third shift, twenty-four years old in 1931, last seen going down the well shaft ladder with ten coworkers while the flood rose behind them. Tomas Ferreira—no relation to Ada that Noor had confirmed yet, though the surname had snagged in their teeth from the first day they'd read the falsified payroll ledger. Tomas, whose name appeared on the 1931 paperwork as *accounted for*, transferred to the Croydon site per standard rotation, a lie typed by a man who knew exactly where he was.

The thing below had been asked if it knew Noor's name. It had answered correctly—and then, in the same breath, in the same borrowed instrument of a voice, it had used the wrong one. Its own. Or one of its own. The mask of perfect courtesy had slipped for exactly one word, the way a glove slips when the hand inside changes shape.

*Perfection is the mask,* Noor thought, writing it on their palm with a ballpoint because every notebook in the building was compromised. *Masks slip.*

* * *

They played the rest of the recording straight through, twice, and found two more things they'd missed.

The first was small. At 4:12 a.m., Noor's sleeping voice asked, *"How many are you?"* and the answer came back as a breathy count—not words, but a sound like eleven people exhaling in unison, layered so precisely it registered as one long sigh. On the first listen it had sounded like wind in the vents. It was not wind in the vents.

The second was worse, and Noor had to sit with it for nearly an hour before they could admit what they'd heard.

At 5:38 a.m., near dawn, the second voice said—unprompted, answering no question, breaking its own pattern of courteous response for the only time in eight hours:

*"You count beautifully."*

And the voice it said it in was not the receptionist's voice. It was Noor's voice. Fully, completely Noor's voice—the specific dry warmth of it, the slight upward lift at the end of sentences that Noor's mother said they'd inherited from a grandmother they barely remembered. Not an imitation. A possession. At 5:38 in the morning, nine days ago, something in the dark had spoken *as* Noor, to Noor, while Noor slept, and had complimented Noor on the habit that defined them: the counting, the logging, the meticulous making-visible of forgotten things.

*You count beautifully.* Said in your own voice. By something learning to be you.

Except now, knowing about the slip at 2:31, Noor heard that sentence differently too. Because it wasn't only flattery. It was appetite describing its method. You count beautifully. You document beautifully. You make yourself into a record, and a record can be copied. A person is hard to wear. A *file* is easy.

Noor pulled the headphones off again and pressed both palms against their eye sockets until the darkness bloomed red.

Think. Think like a surveyor. What do you actually know?

One: the entity could edit any data it could reach. Notebooks, drives, clouds—all of it gone or falsified. Two: it had not reached this card, either because it couldn't find it or because the box genuinely shielded it. Three: it had slipped, once, nine days ago, and the slip was preserved. *Tomas.* Which meant that beneath the flawless surface—the perfect courtesy, the perfect mimicry, the perfect erasure—there was something that made mistakes. Something with a real shape underneath the glove.

And four, the one that mattered, the one that turned fear into a plan:

If it could erase Noor's records, why hadn't it erased Noor? Why edit instead of delete? Why leave a living witness walking around with a head full of transcriptions?

Because it didn't want Noor gone. It wanted Noor *negotiable*. An identity without documentation isn't destroyed—it's put up for sale. Every blank page, every zeroed spreadsheet, every corrupted file was a bid. It was driving down the price of being Noor Oyelaran so that, eventually, Noor would accept whatever identity was on offer instead. And Noor now knew—at least suspected—who the preferred seller was. Someone had to take a dead man's place. Someone willing. Someone whose own name had been made cheap enough to trade.

*Tomas,* the thing had said, tasting it. *Tomas.*

"No," Noor said aloud, to the dark room, to the recorder, to whatever might be listening through walls it had spent ninety years learning. "I'm not your twelfth. I'm your auditor."

The building said nothing. But somewhere far below—two floors down, past the documented sub-basement, past the brickwork older than the city grid—Noor would have sworn the air pressure changed. Faintly. Politely. The way a room changes when someone in it stops smiling.

They packed the Faraday box deep in the duffel. They pocketed the ballpoint and wrote on their forearm, where no erasure had yet reached: *NIGHT ONE CARD — INTACT. IT SAID TOMAS. IT SLIPS.*

Then they got out Ada Ferreira's number—the one scrap of the research chapter that existed in three places, including inked inside their boot—and called it, though it was barely past five in the morning and the sky outside the loading bay door was still the color of wet slate.

She picked up on the second ring, wide awake, as if she'd been sitting by the phone since 1931.

"It slipped," Noor said, before she could speak. "The thing downstairs. Last night I found a recording it never touched, and it slipped. It used one of the names."

Silence on the line. Then, in a voice gone thin and old and terribly certain:

"Which one?"

"Tomas."

The silence stretched again, longer, and when Ada Ferreira finally spoke, she was crying without any noise, the way people cry when they learned young that noise gets punished.

"Then come now," she said. "Before it finishes learning to say yours."
