## 3. Ten Years of Explanations

The first call that mattered came in October, eleven years ago, and Felix had been in the business exactly four months.

They remembered it the way you remember a scar's origin story—vaguely, with details worn smooth from handling. A duplex on Corliss Street. The tenant was a night-shift nurse named Dolores Vann who kept odd hours and an immaculate kitchen, and she'd called the pest control company three times about the same thing: something moving inside her walls at 4 a.m., every morning, precise as a train schedule.

Felix's supervisor, a heavyset man named Gus with a smoker's laugh and a laminated card of company talking points he'd never once consulted, handed Felix the work order with a shrug. "Rodent activity, probably squirrels in the soffit. You're young, you can crawl around."

So Felix crawled around.

The duplex sat at the end of its street like a comma at the end of a sentence nobody wanted to finish. The yard was brown in the way October yards go brown—not dead, just waiting. Dolores met them at the door in scrubs, having clearly come off shift twenty minutes earlier, her eyes carrying that particular glassy exhaustion of people who work while the world sleeps and therefore see what it does when it thinks no one is watching.

"It's not scratching," she said before Felix could introduce themselves. "Scratching I could live with. Scratching is an animal being an animal. This is—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, restarted. "It's deliberate. It moves along the wall behind my headboard, top to bottom, slow, like it's checking. Like it's counting the rooms."

"Could be a raccoon," Felix said. "Raccoons are methodical."

Dolores looked at them for a long moment, and Felix would spend years returning to that look, turning it over, trying to decide what it had contained. Pity, maybe. Or recognition—the look of one person who knows something true watching another person rehearse a lie they don't yet know they'll need.

"Sure," she said finally. "A raccoon."

* * *

The attic was hot and smelled of blown insulation and old pine and, faintly, underneath everything, something sweetish and wrong, like fruit going bad in a drawer somewhere out of sight. Felix crawled the length of it on their belly with a flashlight clamped between shoulder and jaw, checking for droppings, chewed wiring, entry gaps. They found all the ordinary evidence of ordinary neglect: mouse droppings in the corner, a gap where a vent pipe penetrated the roofline, a desiccated bird.

What they did not find was anything that explained the sound.

Because there was a sound. Felix heard it at 3:58 a.m., lying flat in the insulation with their cheek against the joists because they'd decided—professionally, scientifically—to wait for the scheduled activity rather than guess at it. At 3:58, something began moving in the wall cavity below them. Not scurrying. Not the frantic skitter of a trapped animal or the busy industry of mice. It moved the way a hand moves when someone runs it slowly down a banister in the dark. Top to bottom. Deliberate. Pausing at intervals that felt, absurdly, like attention.

Felix lay there with their heart going hard and told themselves the things there were to tell themselves. Thermal expansion. Pipes. The house settling as the temperature dropped. A branch against the siding. The mind, sleep-deprived, pattern-matching random noise into intentionality—that was a documented phenomenon, apophenia, they'd read about it, anyone could experience it.

The sound reached the bottom of the wall and stopped.

And then—it knocked. Twice. Softly. The way a person knocks on a door they know will be answered.

Felix stayed in that attic until sunrise, motionless, running through the taxonomy of explanations the way a frightened child runs through the names of saints. By dawn they had assembled a serviceable theory involving a raccoon in the wall cavity itself, though they never found the entry point, and they wrote up an invoice for exclusion work they performed with more thoroughness than the job strictly required, sealing gaps that didn't need sealing, because work done well felt like control and control felt like safety.

Dolores paid without argument. At the door she said, "You didn't hear it, did you."

"I heard something," Felix said. "Animals are unpredictable."

"No," Dolores said, not unkindly. "They really aren't."

She moved out in November. Felix knew because the property management company sent the follow-up contract to the new address request and Felix saw the forwarding order with their own eyes. The duplex rented again by January. No further calls. Whatever lived in that wall either followed Dolores or grew bored, and Felix chose—consciously, deliberately, with the full weight of a decision they would repeat ten thousand times—to believe the second option.

* * *

Here is what ten years of explanations actually looks like, from the inside:

It looks like a filing cabinet. Every strange thing gets a folder. Sounds become thermal expansion, plumbing hydraulics, wind loads, wildlife. Smells become dead animals in walls—a real phenomenon, common enough, and always available. Cold spots become HVAC imbalances, drafty sills, single-pane windows. Feelings—the prickle on the neck, the sense of being observed—become carbon monoxide, low-level anxiety, the simple mammalian fact that dark rooms feel watched because for two million years the things that felt watched in dark rooms survived longer than the ones that didn't.

Felix built the cabinet drawer by drawer, folder by folder, and the beautiful thing about it—the thing that made it sustainable—was that every folder was *true*. That was the trick of it, the elegance. None of the explanations were lies. Raccoons really do move methodically. Houses really do knock as they cool. Dead animals really do smell sweet and wrong. The cabinet was stocked entirely with facts, and the facts were arranged into a shape whose only function was to keep the other shape—the other possibility—from assembling itself in the dark of Felix's attention.

Gus had a name for it. "The trade eats skeptics or makes liars," he said once, drinking bad coffee in the truck outside a job site. "You get guys who quit after their first weird house, and you get guys who stay. The ones who stay learn to write reports that say *unresolved, monitoring recommended.*" He'd laughed his smoker's laugh. "Monitoring recommended. Best three words in this business. Means: I saw something, I'm not writing it down, and neither should you."

Felix had laughed too. It was easier than asking him what he'd seen.

There were other houses over the years, of course. There was the farmhouse outside Merton where every clock ran seven minutes fast and the family had simply adapted, setting their watches accordingly, living their lives seven minutes ahead of the county around them; Felix replaced the clocks and the new ones ran fast too, and the explanation folder said *electromagnetic interference from nearby transformer*, which was even plausible. There was the apartment where a woman's dead mother's handwriting appeared in the condensation on the bathroom mirror—*HI HON*—and Felix cleaned the ductwork, tested the humidity, and wrote *condensation artifact, unresolved, monitoring recommended.* There was the child who drew the same tall thin figure in forty consecutive crayon drawings and then stopped drawing it the week after Felix serviced the property, and Felix chose to believe the treatment worked, whatever the treatment had actually been.

Each folder went in the cabinet. Each cabinet got heavier. And the weight changed Felix in ways they didn't examine, the way a pack mule doesn't examine its spine.

The change was this: Felix stopped sleeping well in unfamiliar houses. They started arriving early to jobs so they could leave before dusk. They developed a habit—they would have called it professionalism—of never being the last person in a building, and if they ever were, of narrating their own actions aloud, *okay, checking the basement now, just me, Felix, human person,* as if naming themselves could hold their place in the world the way a bookmark holds a page.

None of this felt like fear. That was the cabinet's masterstroke. It felt like caution. Caution was professional. Fear was for people without explanations.

* * *

The trouble with a filing cabinet, though—the flaw in the architecture, visible only in retrospect—is that it requires every new document to fit an existing folder. And some documents don't fit. Some documents arrive already bleeding through the manila.

That happened twice before Harrow.

The first time was a rental in Ashford, a ground-floor unit where the tenant complained of footprints. Wet footprints, small ones, appearing on the kitchen tile each morning, leading from the back door to the refrigerator and stopping there—as if something came in nightly, opened the fridge, and stood looking inside for a long time. Felix checked the door seal, the weep holes, the foundation. Dry as bone everywhere. The prints were unmistakably made by bare feet, a child's size, toes and all, and they evaporated within the hour.

Felix put it in the folder marked *plumbing condensation / subfloor moisture migration* and hated themselves a little for how smoothly the folder closed. The tenant looked at the invoice, looked at Felix, and said nothing, and the nothing between them was enormous and mutual. Two adults agreeing, without a word, to protect a shared fiction because the alternative was unlivable. Felix drove home that night with the radio off and sat in the parked truck in their own driveway for forty minutes doing nothing at all.

The second time was worse, and Felix almost never thought about it, which was itself a kind of achievement—an act of mental carpentry so load-bearing that the whole structure of their composure rested on it. A house in Bellin Falls. A woman named Ruth Okafor who called not about sounds but about her son, six years old, who had begun leaving food at the baseboard of his bedroom. Crackers, apple slices, a juice box arranged with terrible care, refreshed nightly, untouched by morning except—

Except bitten. Small bites. Human teeth marks in the apple slices, Felix confirmed with their own eyes, kneeling on a child's bedroom floor at nine in the morning examining fruit like a forensic technician.

The boy wouldn't say who the food was for. When his mother pressed, he cried, and finally said, "She's hungry, Mommy, and she can't get up." When asked who *she* was, the boy pointed at the floor. Just the floor. The boards. The space beneath.

Felix inspected the crawlspace. Found nothing. Found less than nothing—found a crawlspace so clean it seemed maintained, swept, though no one had been under there in years according to the owner. They sealed the vents, treated for termites that weren't present, invoiced for general exclusion, and left.

Ruth Okafor walked them to the car. In the driveway she caught Felix's sleeve, and her voice dropped to something raw and quiet. "You felt it too. Under the house. Don't tell me you didn't."

Felix had felt it. A pressure, a *regard*, the distinct sensation of being appraised by something patient and below. They had filed it under *confined-space anxiety, common*.

"Ma'am," Felix said, "I found no evidence of any animal or structural issue."

"That's not what I asked."

Felix looked at her. She was holding herself very still, the stillness of a person standing at a border crossing deciding whether to declare what they're carrying. And Felix understood—with a clarity that arrived like cold water, unwelcome and total—that whatever Felix said next, this woman would build her next year on it. Say *you're right, something's down there* and she might flee the house, upend her life, or worse, start feeding it herself, formally, ritually, making room for it. Say *there's nothing* and she'd have to live with the boy's apples, the small bites, the pointing finger, alone, with no professional vocabulary to hold it with.

So Felix said the sentence they had perfected across a decade, the sentence that was technically true and functionally a mercy and morally a coin flip:

"There's no evidence of anything dangerous. Sometimes houses make us feel things. Kids especially. Keep the food coming for now—it costs you nothing—and call me if anything changes."

Keep the food coming. Felix heard the words leave their own mouth and understood, distantly, that they had just given advice indistinguishable from what a priest of some older religion might give. Propitiation. Feed it and perhaps it stays quiet. They drove away with their hands at ten and two and their reflection in the rearview looking, for a moment, like a stranger wearing their face.

Ruth Okafor never called back. Felix thought about the boy sometimes. About the apples. About whether the feeding was kindness or surrender, and whether there was a difference, and whether the difference mattered to the thing below the floor.

Mostly, though, they didn't think about it. That was the skill. That was the ten years distilled: not ignorance, but *managed* knowledge. Knowing exactly enough to function and arranging the rest in folders that closed.

* * *

Which brings the account, reluctantly, to the present. To the week of the Harrow job.

Because here was the thing Felix had noticed on the drive home from the initial walkthrough, hands on the wheel, the house shrinking in the mirror behind them: the old reflexes hadn't fired. On Corliss Street, in Ashford, in Bellin Falls, the cabinet had snapped open instantly, folders flying, explanations offered up before the fear could finish forming. That was the system working. That was ten years of craft.

At the Harrow house, in the basement, standing before a section of fresh mortar behind a sealed wall with small handprints pressed into it—prints that faced *outward*, fingers spread, as if pressed from the inside by something pushing to be let through—Felix had stood there for a long time and felt the cabinet stand silent.

No folder fit. They had turned the problem over and over on the drive home and the drawers stayed shut, and beneath the silence of the cabinet was a worse silence, the silence of a mechanism recognizing, for the first time in a decade, a document it could not file.

Felix told themselves it was fatigue. They told themselves the mortar was soft and neighborhood kids had gotten in somehow, though the wall was interior, though the seals were intact, though the prints were small and perfect and wrong. They told themselves the job was money, rent was due, and a pest controller who started seeing things in mortar was a pest controller who stopped eating.

All true. All filed. And yet that night, unpacking their kit in their own apartment, Felix caught themselves doing something they hadn't done since Bellin Falls: narrating aloud to the empty room. *Just me. Felix. Going to bed now.*

The apartment answered with ordinary silence, and Felix lay awake listening to their own plumbing, cataloguing every tick and gurgle, assigning each one its proper folder, practicing, warming up, keeping the instrument tuned.

Tomorrow they went back to the Harrow house. Tomorrow they'd work late, because the daylight walkthrough had raised questions the daylight couldn't answer, and because some part of Felix—the part that had knelt on a child's floor examining teeth marks in an apple—needed to know what the house did after dark, needed it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

They fell asleep eventually. Around 3 a.m., in the wall behind their headboard, something moved—slowly, top to bottom, pausing at intervals that felt like attention—and Felix, half-rising from sleep, told themselves it was pipes.

The folder closed. But for the first time in ten years, it closed on empty air.
