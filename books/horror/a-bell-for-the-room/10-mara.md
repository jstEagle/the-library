## 10. Mara

The county records office smelled of dust and cold coffee and the particular melancholy of paper that no one had asked about in decades. Felix had come in on a Tuesday morning, hungover on bad sleep, intending to do nothing more ambitious than pull the property history of the Harrow house—deeds, permits, whatever paper trail a house that old was required by law to leave behind. They had not expected to be helped. Ten years in pest control had taught Felix that bureaucracy, like vermin, preferred to be left undisturbed.

But the woman behind the reference desk looked up before Felix had finished explaining what they wanted, and something in her face changed—not alarm, exactly. Recognition, maybe. Or the expression of someone who had been waiting a long time for this particular question and had rehearsed her answer to it.

"You're working at the Harrow house," she said. Not a question.

Felix set their contractor's clipboard on the counter like a shield. "I'm doing an inspection. Pest control. It's a job."

"Nobody does a job at the Harrow house." She said it without drama, the way a mechanic says a car has a cracked block. "They inherit it, or they buy it cheap, or they're sent. Which one are you?"

"Sent. Hired. There's a contract." Felix heard how thin that sounded and hated it. "Look, I just need the ownership history. Deed transfers, dates of sale. Anything about structural work—there's a wall in the basement that's been sealed, and I need to know when and by whom."

The woman was already moving, her chair rolling back, her cardigan traded for a canvas jacket that had seen more archives than weather. "Come with me. Not the public terminals. The actual files."

"I haven't told you my name."

"I know your name." She paused at the gate in the counter, one hand on the swing door, and for the first time she looked uncertain—caught between the rehearsed answer and something rawer. "Felix Almeida. The pest controller. The house called the county office twice last week to confirm your contract was legitimate. It doesn't usually bother confirming anything."

*The house called.* Felix let that pass the way they let a lot of things pass lately, filing it in the drawer that was getting fuller by the day, the one labeled *deal with later.* "And you are?"

"Mara Osei." She said it the way people say names they've had to defend. "I'm the archivist here. And my great-aunt disappeared at that house in 1961, so before you ask whether I have a personal interest—yes. An enormous one. Now come on, before I change my mind about helping you."

* * *

The back rooms of the records office were a maze of gray shelving under fluorescent hum, and Mara moved through them with the certainty of someone who had grown up in the stacks the way other children grew up in orchards. She pulled boxes without checking labels, her fingers knowing the geography. Felix followed, clipboard against their chest, feeling like an intruder in a church of a different denomination.

"Adeline Osei," Mara said, not turning around. "My great-aunt. My grandmother's older sister. In the summer of 1961 she took a job as a mother's helper at the Harrow house—watching the Harrow children, helping in the kitchen. She was nineteen." A box came off the shelf; dust rose in the fluorescent light like something startled. "She was there for eleven weeks. Then one morning in August she didn't come down for breakfast, and the Harrows said she'd left in the night, gone home without a word. Except she never arrived home. There's no bus record. No witness on the road. She walked out of that house and into nothing."

Felix kept their voice level, professional. "People do that. Leave. Especially at nineteen."

"She left her shoes." Mara finally turned, and her eyes were dry but very bright, the brightness of something held under pressure. "Her shoes were by the back door, lined up neatly, toes pointing out, like she expected to put them on again in a minute. Nobody walks eleven miles home barefoot in August and tells no one. Nobody leaves their shoes."

She set the box on a table and opened it, and Felix saw that it was not county records at all. It was a family archive—letters bundled with string, a photograph, a school notebook, all of it handled with the care of relics.

"Sixty years, my family has been pulling at this thread," Mara said. "My grandmother wrote letters. My mother filed requests. I got the degree and the job specifically so I could sit close enough to the paper to hear it if it ever whispered." She looked at Felix then, really looked, the way an examiner looks. "And then a pest controller walks in asking about a sealed wall, and I have to decide in about four seconds whether you're the answer or the next person this house eats."

"And what did you decide?"

"I decided I'd help you and watch you at the same time." No apology in it. "Cautious alliance. That's the term, isn't it? I read a lot of diplomatic history for a woman who works in a county basement."

* * *

What she showed Felix first was not the letters. It was the ledger—or a photocopy of pages from it, the same ledger Felix had already found in the estate paperwork, though Felix hadn't said so yet and Mara didn't know they'd seen it. She laid the copies side by side on the table and tapped one column with a blunt fingernail.

"Payments. Look at the pattern. Every few years, a payment out of the Harrow estate to individuals—last names only, mostly. And next to each payment, in this column—" her nail stopped, "—the word *verse.* Sometimes with a number after it. One, two, three. Never anything after three."

Felix stared at the column. The paper was old but the handwriting, across sixty years, was the same hand—patient, upright, unhurried, the handwriting of someone keeping accounts for an obligation rather than a business. "Verse of what?"

"That's what I've spent my life not knowing." Mara sat down across from Felix, and for the first time she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "There was a song. I know that much. My grandmother hummed it sometimes without realizing—caught herself doing it and went white and stopped. When I asked her about it, once, when I was small, she said: *that's the Harrow song, and we don't keep it.* Like it was a debt. Like humming it was spending money you owed to something worse than a bank." She folded her hands on the table. "Adeline wrote one letter from the house. Just one, that survived. It's the reason I believe she didn't run."

She untied the string with fingers that were not quite steady and unfolded a single page, soft as cloth with age. Felix read it over her shoulder.

*Dear Nell — The house is fine and the pay is fine and Mrs. Harrow is kind in the way of people who are afraid of something and overcompensating. The children are sweet but strange. They will not go near the well and they will not say why, and yesterday I caught them at the parlor mirror making a game of it, standing very still to see who would move first. They said the glass remembers you a moment after you've gone, and if you watch the place where you were, you can see who's standing in your old spot. I told them not to be silly. I have not looked in that mirror since.*

*There is singing at night. From the pipes, Nell. I know how that sounds. I am not writing it for you to believe. I am writing it so that somewhere outside this house, on paper, in ink, it is written down that I heard it, in case I am not here to say so.*

*If I don't write again, ask about the ledger. Ask who gets paid, and for what verse.*

Felix read it twice. The fluorescent light hummed. Somewhere in the stacks, a pipe knocked—once, twice—and both of them flinched, and both of them pretended they hadn't.

"She knew," Felix said quietly. "In 1961, she already knew."

"She knew, and she stayed, because the pay was good and she was nineteen and nineteen-year-olds are rich in exactly the currency this house trades in." Mara's voice had gone flat, controlled, the voice of someone who had cried about this already and refused to do it in front of a stranger. "She stayed, and the house kept her. And every few years since, the ledger shows a payment, and the column says *verse,* and nobody has ever once written down what the song is."

Felix thought of the small handprints in the fresh mortar behind the sealed wall. Thought of the mirror in the hallway that lagged, just slightly, just a breath behind their own movements. Thought of the melody that had come up through the pipes at three in the morning, patient and sweet, wanting to be learned.

"I've heard it," Felix said.

Mara went very still. "Say that again."

"The song. Through the plumbing. Three nights ago." Felix heard their own voice and didn't recognize the steadiness in it, the steadiness of a person narrating their own disappearance in advance. "I wrote it down. I don't know why. I couldn't not."

For a long moment Mara just looked at them, and Felix watched her weigh it—the hope and the horror of it, the thing she'd waited her whole life to hear arriving in the mouth of a stranger with a contractor's clipboard. When she spoke, her voice was careful as a hand reaching into a trap.

"You transcribed it. Where is it?"

"At the house. In my notebook."

"Then we need to get it, and you need to understand something first." She stood, and gathered the photocopies, and squared their edges against the table with small, precise movements, the movements of a person imposing order on a room that was trying to come apart. "Everyone in that ledger column got paid. Everyone who sang, presumably, got paid and lived. That's the deal the house makes—it pays you to be complicit. Adeline wouldn't take the deal. I don't know how I know that, but I know it the way you know your own family. She wouldn't have sung along at anything. And the house doesn't keep people who won't sing."

She looked up at Felix then, and the brightness in her eyes had sharpened into something that was almost a warning, almost a plea, and entirely both.

"So I'll help you, Felix Almeida. I'll help you with everything I have—sixty years of paper, every record, every thread. But you should know what you're walking into with me. I want to find my aunt. And I am not at all certain that what's left of her wants to be found."

* * *

They parted at the records office steps in the gray light of late afternoon, Mara with her canvas jacket and her box of relics, Felix with a photocopy of the ledger pages folded into their clipboard like a splinter. She had made Felix promise to bring the transcription tomorrow—*not tonight,* she'd said, *because I don't trust either of us alone with it after dark*—and Felix had promised, and had walked four blocks toward the bus stop before realizing they were humming.

Three notes. Four. The beginning of the melody, rising out of them unbidden, sweet and patient, the way water is patient with stone.

Felix stopped on the sidewalk. Closed their mouth. Stood very still among the passing strangers, heart hammering, and understood with total clarity what Mara had been warning them about without quite saying: the song did not wait to be invited. It was already in them, the way the house was already in the ledger, the way complicity was already in the payment.

That night, back in the Harrow house with the doors locked and the lights on, Felix opened the notebook to the transcription and read it without humming, without letting the shape of it into the throat. And it was there, in the margin of the third page, that they saw it for the first time—the thing they'd been too tired to notice when they'd written it down at 3 a.m. in a half-dream.

The melody stopped. It simply stopped, mid-phrase, at the end of the third line, hanging on an unresolved note like a door left open. Below it, in Felix's own handwriting, in the same exhausted scrawl, though Felix had no memory of writing it:

*the rest was torn out. ask the one who kept the ledger.*

Felix looked at the page for a long time. Then, from the hallway, faint and unhurried, came the sound of the mirror settling back into time—*tick*—like a clock remembering itself, like something patient turning a page.
