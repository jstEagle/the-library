## 20. The Lawyer's Visit

The car that pulled into the Harrow driveway on Thursday morning was the color of wet slate, low and silent, the kind of vehicle that didn't announce itself so much as *arrive*, like a verdict. Felix watched it from the kitchen window with a mug of coffee going cold in their hands and felt their stomach perform a small, practiced maneuver they recognized from a decade of bad news: the drop before the drop.

They had known someone would come. Mara had warned them—*the estate isn't dormant, Felix, it's administered*—and after the letter from Aldous Harrow's firm arrived two days ago, all cream paper and embossed letterhead, refusing to answer a single question while acknowledging receipt of Felix's "inquiries," it had only been a matter of time. Institutions like this did not send letters when they could send people. Letters were for the living who might still be reasonable.

Felix set down the mug and went to open the door before the man could knock. It seemed important, somehow, not to let him knock. Not to let the house hear him ask to be let in.

The attorney was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned, wearing a charcoal suit that fit like it had been grown rather than sewn. He carried a leather folio under one arm and nothing else—no briefcase, no boxes of documents. Just the folio. Just whatever he intended to put on the table.

"Ms. Almeida." He extended a hand. His grip was dry, calibrated, gone in exactly the right amount of time. "Daniel Voss. Voss, Merrick, and Hale. We represent the Harrow family interests."

"Felix is fine." They stepped back to let him in, and immediately regretted it—not because of him, but because of what happened next, which was nothing. He crossed the threshold without hesitation, without pause, without the faint hitch that every other visitor to the house had shown since Felix started paying attention to such things. The mail carrier hesitated. The meter reader had hesitated. Even Mara, brave as she was, had stood on the porch for three full seconds the first time, her eyes doing something involuntary and arithmetic, counting exits.

Voss walked in like a man entering a bank branch. Which meant either he'd never been told what this house was—

—or he knew exactly what it was, and had some arrangement with it.

That second possibility sat down across from Felix at the kitchen table and refused to leave.

"Can I get you coffee?" Felix asked, mostly to watch him.

"Thank you, no." He opened the folio. Inside were perhaps six sheets of paper, aligned with a precision that suggested he had squared them against each other more than once in the car. "I'll be direct, if I may. My client values his privacy, and the privacy of the property. You've been conducting an investigation—"

"I've been doing pest control."

"You've been corresponding with an archivist at the county historical society. You've requested records from the probate office. You made inquiries regarding a well permit dated 1931." He said it without accusation, without heat, the way a man reads coordinates off a map. "You've also, I'm told, been asking questions of the neighbors. Several of them have called us."

"Several of them," Felix repeated. "Which several?"

He almost smiled. Almost. There was a version of the expression underneath, filed flat. "I'm not here to litigate your curiosity, Ms.—Felix. I'm here to make you an offer."

*There it is.* Felix leaned back in the chair and folded their hands on the table, and it was only as they did it that they saw them—their own hands, palms up, bare in the morning light—and thought about the mortar in the basement, the small prints pressed into it from the inside, and kept their face still through sheer professional discipline. Ten years of walking into crawl spaces full of things that used to be animals taught you a certain stillness. Your face was a tool. You kept it sharp.

"An offer," Felix said.

"The Harrow family is prepared to compensate you for the remainder of your contract, plus a settlement amount we can discuss, in exchange for two things." Voss laid the first sheet on the table and turned it to face them. "First: cessation of work on the premises, effective immediately. Second—" the second sheet, slid alongside the first, "—a mutual non-disclosure agreement. Standard language. You make no statements, written or oral, regarding the property, its history, its previous occupants, or any conditions observed therein. No interviews. No archives. No correspondence with historical societies or, ah, archivists."

"No archivists," Felix said. "You specifically named Mara Osei."

"We name parties of concern."

"Of concern to whom?"

"To my client." He folded his hands now, mirroring Felix, and for a moment they sat there like a photograph of a negotiation, symmetrical and bloodless. "I understand you feel you've found something. People in your line of work sometimes do. Old houses generate anomalies—settling foundations, acoustic phenomena, groundwater movement. I've read the engineering reports. The well on the property has been dry since 1974."

"It's half full right now," Felix said. "And warm. And it smells like iron."

Something crossed Voss's face then. It was very fast and very small, and if Felix hadn't spent ten years watching clients' faces for the flicker that preceded *actually, while you're here, there's something else you should know*—the flicker that meant the house had been talking to them too—they would have missed it entirely. It wasn't surprise. Surprise would have been almost comforting.

It was confirmation.

"The water table in this region is notoriously variable," Voss said, and his voice hadn't changed at all, which was somehow worse. "My client's offer is generous. I'd encourage you to consider the practical realities of your situation. You are a contractor on a month-to-month engagement. You have no standing. Whatever you believe you've discovered, pursuing it will cost you considerably more than it can possibly return. Whereas this—" he tapped the settlement figure, which Felix deliberately did not look at, because looking at numbers was how they got in, "—is immediate. Cash. No conditions beyond your silence and your departure."

"And if I don't sign?"

"Then my client will pursue every available remedy regarding trespass, defamation, and interference with private matters. Litigation is unpleasant. It is also slow, public, and exhausting, and at the end of it you would have nothing but legal fees and a reputation as the pest controller who cried ghost." He said the word *ghost* the way a doctor says a symptom the patient has self-diagnosed from the internet: gently, dismissively, with a hidden flinch.

Felix looked at him for a long moment. Then they stood up, went to the counter, and poured themselves the cold coffee, because their hands wanted something to do and their mouth wanted time.

Here was the thing Felix had learned in ten years of rationalizations, and learned again in the last few weeks at the cost of every rationalization they owned: the money was real. That was the trap's genius. The money was real, rent was real, the debt on the van was real, the ache in their lower back from twenty years of crawl spaces was real. Every temptation worth fearing came dressed in true things. The devil's arithmetic—Aldous's arithmetic, though Felix didn't have the name attached to it yet, not quite—always balanced. That was how you knew it was arithmetic and not justice. Justice didn't balance. Justice left scars.

And beneath the money, underneath it like a stone under leaves, was the other thing Voss was actually offering: permission to stop knowing. A signature, and the mirror in the hallway was just a mirror again, lagging because old glass warped, because physics, because anything, because *anything*. A signature, and the children in the glass were fatigue, were grief, were the brother-shaped hole Felix had carried since they were nineteen and had never reported missing properly because reporting it would have made it a thing with paperwork, a thing with a file number, a thing that was finally, officially, irreversibly true. A signature, and the song that came up through the pipes at three in the morning was just the house settling, singing itself to sleep.

The NDA wasn't buying silence. It was buying amnesia, and it was offering a fair price for it, and that was why Felix's hand had trembled slightly around the coffee mug, and why they set it back down before Voss could see.

"You know what's in the walls," Felix said.

Voss regarded them. Outside, wind moved through the dead elms along the street, and somewhere far off—a neighbor's yard, maybe, or maybe not—the well cover shifted with a sound like a throat clearing.

"I know what my client pays me to know," Voss said at last. "I've represented the Harrow interests for eleven years. In that time I have processed four settlements identical to the one in front of you. Four prior contractors. Four NDAs. All honored. All quiet." He paused, and for the first time something almost human moved behind the calibration. "Do you know what I've never done, Ms. Almeida? I've never been inside this house before today. I drafted the first of those agreements in 2013 and I have never once set foot on this property until this morning. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That you're careful."

"That I'm *paid to be careful*. And yet here I am." He glanced, briefly, at the ceiling—at the floor of the room above them, where Felix had heard footsteps crossing back and forth over three nights of an empty second story. When he looked back, the humanity was gone, packed away like equipment. "So consider the possibility that I'm not here because my client sent me. Consider that I'm here because the four before you signed, and you're the fifth, and the pattern wants its fifth."

The kitchen was very quiet. The refrigerator hummed. Under the hum, faintly, from the direction of the basement door, came a sound Felix had learned to dread more than footsteps, more than knocking, more than the wet dragging in the yard at night: a single sustained note, low, patient, rising out of the plumbing like breath drawn through a flute. The beginning of the melody. The first verse, offering itself again, the way it offered itself every day now, growing bolder, growing *closer to words*.

Voss heard it. Felix watched him hear it. And Voss did not startle, did not frown, did not ask *what was that*—he simply waited it out, eyes fixed on the middle distance, jaw tight, like a man in a dentist's chair between drills.

When it faded, he straightened his cuffs.

"Five hundred thousand dollars," he said quietly. "And the agreement. And you walk away whole. That's the best terms anyone has ever received, and I'm authorized to say that explicitly, because my client believes you'll understand what it means that he said so."

"What does it mean?"

"It means he knows what you know." Voss closed the folio. "It means he's afraid of what you'll do with it."

*He's afraid,* Felix thought, and the word landed strangely, because everything else about the house ran on appetite and patience and hunger, and fear was new. Fear was a crack in the covenant. Fear meant the arrangement could be broken, which meant it could be broken by someone, which meant—

"I'm not signing," Felix said.

Voss exhaled through his nose, slow and controlled, a man releasing air rather than frustration. "May I ask why? Professionally. It helps me advise my client."

Because my brother never got a lawyer, Felix thought. Because nobody offered five hundred thousand dollars for his silence; nobody even offered a search party past the third day. Because I learned young that some silences aren't bought, they're just taken, and I swore I'd never be on the taking end again. Because there are children in the glass who have been waiting since 1931 for somebody to finish a sentence that was cut in half on purpose, and I am apparently the kind of fool who volunteers for sentences.

"Because I already know how the story ends if I take the money," Felix said aloud. "I've read the ledger. Everyone who signed your agreements—they're in it. Dates and payments and a column marked *verse*. Your settlements don't buy silence, Mr. Voss. They buy verses. I'm not becoming a line item."

For a long moment Voss said nothing. Then he gathered his six sheets of paper, squared them, slid them into the folio, and stood. At the doorway to the hall he stopped, and did not turn fully around, and spoke to the hallway rather than to Felix—to the long dim corridor where the mirror hung, the one that ran a second late, the one where the children stood in the space Felix had just vacated.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the fourth contractor didn't sign either. In 1998. Her settlement is still in my files. Unsigned." A pause. "There's no record of her invoice being paid, either. Or of her, really, after October of that year. I've always assumed she simply left the trade."

"She didn't leave the trade," Felix said. "She wouldn't sing."

Voss's shoulders rose and fell once. "Then I'd advise you," he said, "to be faster than she was."

He let himself out. The slate-gray car whispered backward down the drive and was gone, and the street resumed its shape, the neighbors' curtains falling closed one by one like eyelids.

Felix stood in the kitchen with their heart going hard and their hands open at their sides, palms up, and looked down at them in the gray light. For a moment—only a moment—they thought the lines in their palms looked deeper than usual. Like impressions. Like something practicing.

They picked up the phone and dialed Mara, and it rang twice before she answered, and Felix said, "He offered me half a million to disappear, and he told me about the one who refused in '98," and heard Mara's breath catch, and heard, behind her, the sound of archive boxes being opened, paper moving, hope with a splinter in it.

"Felix," she said. "I found her name. The fourth child. The one who wouldn't sing. And Felix—I found what she wrote on the wall."

From the basement, soft and unmistakable, the pipes began to hum the melody again—but this time, for the first time, they were not alone. Something below was humming *back*.
