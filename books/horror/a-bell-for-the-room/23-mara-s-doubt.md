## 23. Mara's Doubt

The rain came in off the harbor around four o'clock, a gray curtain that turned the Harrow house's windows into smears of silver, and Mara Osei sat at Felix's kitchen table with her coat still on and her hands wrapped around a mug of tea she had not once sipped. Felix had learned to read people the way they read houses—by the drafts, by the places where warmth failed—and everything about Mara right now read as a structure holding itself up through sheer habit. Spine straight. Shoulders squared. Eyes fixed on the middle distance where, presumably, she was doing arithmetic she didn't want anyone else to see.

Felix put the transcription on the table between them.

Three pages now. The first verse in Mara's careful archival hand, dated and cross-referenced against the ledger's payment column. The second verse—the one that had come back through the drain with something underneath it, something that harmonized—annotated in red with the intervals that didn't belong to any tradition Mara could name. And beneath both, a blank space where the third verse should have been, ruled off with a single line, like a grave marker waiting for its name.

"You've been staring at that space for an hour," Felix said.

"I've been staring at it for thirty years," Mara said. "You just gave me a piece of paper to do it on."

She finally drank her tea. It had gone cold; Felix watched her drink it anyway, watched her throat work, watched the way her hands trembled just slightly on the mug—not fear-trembling, Felix decided. Something older than fear. The kind of shaking that comes from carrying a weight so long your body has forgotten how to set it down.

"My great-aunt's name was Efua," Mara said. "Efua Osei. She was nineteen in 1961. She came out here from Accra when she was sixteen to live with cousins and work—there's a whole story about the passage, about what it cost my family to send even one person, but that's not the story I'm telling." She turned the mug a quarter-turn on the table, a small ritual gesture, aligning something invisible. "The story I'm telling is that in June of 1961 she took a bus out to this neighborhood to clean houses for the summer. Wealthy families. One of them was the Harrows."

Felix said nothing. In ten years of pest control they had learned that silence was the most useful tool they owned. People filled silence the way water fills a low place, and what they filled it with was always the truth they'd been hiding.

"She wrote letters home every week. My grandmother kept all of them. Forty-one letters, May through September." Mara's voice stayed level, archival, the voice of a woman reading from a finding aid. "In the last one she mentions a song. She says—" and here Mara closed her eyes, and recited, and Felix understood she had recited this before, many times, alone, probably in the dark: "*'There is a song the children sing here, and it is the sweetest thing I have ever heard, and I cannot stop humming it, and I think perhaps I should be frightened of that.'*"

The rain thickened against the kitchen window. Somewhere in the walls of Felix's own small house—a house with no well, no mirrors, no history worth mentioning—a pipe ticked as it cooled, and both of them flinched, and both of them pretended they hadn't.

"The search found nothing," Mara continued. "Of course it did. The Harrows were cooperative in the way wealthy people are cooperative—they paid for things. Investigators. A reward that was never claimed. My grandmother went out there herself, twice, and came back saying the neighbors were kind and useless, kind and useless, everyone offering casseroles and no one offering information. She died without knowing. My mother grew up without knowing. And I—" Mara opened her eyes, and for the first time since Felix had met her, three weeks ago, in the county archive, she looked directly at them without any professional buffer between them. "I spent twenty years as an archivist learning how to find anything. Anything. Lost deeds, unrecorded burials, families erased from census records on purpose. I can find a woman who changed her name in 1904 and never told a soul. I could not find Efua Osei."

"Because she never left the property," Felix said quietly.

"Because she never left the property," Mara agreed. "And because everyone who knew where she went agreed to say nothing, and their children agreed to say nothing, and their children's children glance at a well cover when they think no one is watching."

* * *

It was fully dark now. Felix got up and turned on the lamp over the table rather than the overhead light, and neither of them discussed why—the overhead light made shadows jump, and jumping shadows had become, over these weeks, a thing neither of them could afford. The lamp threw a warm circle across the table, across the transcription, across the ledger page Felix had photocopied before deciding whether to burn the original. The column labeled *verse* ran down the right margin in faded iron-gall ink: dates going back past 1900, past 1860, each entry paired with a sum of money and, in later years, initials. Someone had been paying for the singing. Someone had been keeping accounts of survival.

Mara pulled the photocopy toward her and smoothed it flat, and Felix saw her do the thing she did when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed: she aligned the paper's edge precisely with the table's edge.

"I need to tell you something," she said, "and I need you to let me finish it before you respond, because if you interrupt me I will lose my nerve, and I am fifty-eight years old and I do not lose my nerve often, but this—" she touched the blank space under the second verse, "—this has my nerve in its teeth."

"Okay," Felix said.

"I've been transcribing the song for six days. You know that. You've heard me working. And I want you to understand that I have been rigorous. I've checked the melody against hymnals, against lullaby collections, against field recordings from three different folklore archives. I've done everything correctly. Everything an archivist is supposed to do." Her finger traced the ruled line marking the missing verse. "And somewhere around day four I noticed that I had stopped taking notes in the margins. Around day five I noticed I could hum the first eight bars without looking at the page. And last night, Felix, last night I woke up at three in the morning—I don't need to tell you what time it was—with the second verse running through my head, complete, note-perfect, including the part that harmonizes with whatever is singing back through your drain. And I lay there and I realized that I wasn't afraid."

The word hung in the lamplight.

"I wasn't afraid," she repeated. "I was *comfortable*. That's the part that frightens me now, retroactively—that the song had made me comfortable, the way a house makes you comfortable, the way a routine makes you comfortable, and underneath the comfort there was this—" she searched for the word, rejected two or three, settled on it like choosing the least-bad apple from a barrel, "—this *invitation*. Like the song was a room with a chair in it, and my name was already on the chair, and all I had to do was sit down and keep singing and everything would be fine forever."

"And you're telling me this because?" Felix asked, though some cold part of them already knew.

"Because I have to be honest with you about what I am in this," Mara said. "You've been treating me like the expert. The ally. The one with the archive and the methodology and the clear head. And I am those things, most hours. But you need to know that there are other hours." She folded her hands on the table, deliberately, the way a witness settles themselves before testimony. "Part of me wants the third verse found, Felix. Not for your reasons. Not to break anything. Part of me wants it completed because the song is the last place my aunt's voice exists. She hummed it. She wrote that she couldn't stop humming it. If we finish the transcription—if we recover the ending that someone tore out—then somewhere in the completion of it, in the shape of the whole melody, I will hear her. Nineteen years old, singing in a house that ate her, and the only surviving recording of her voice is a tune that lives in the heads of dead children and a dry well and me."

Her voice did not break. Felix almost wished it would. There was something more terrible about the steadiness—the sound of grief that had been professionally managed for decades and was now, under the pressure of a ruled blank line on a piece of paper, beginning to seep through the seams.

"Do you understand what I'm confessing?" Mara said. "I'm confessing that I am compromised. I'm confessing that the enemy doesn't only live in wells. It lives in the wanting. Every person who ever sang in that house sang because they wanted something—to live, mostly, yes, but the ledger shows payments, Felix, which means some of them sang for money, which means some of them sang and then went home and slept well. The song lets you survive and calls it living. And I have wanted to hear my aunt's voice for fifty-eight years, and the song knows that, and it is using my wanting the way it uses everything."

The rain had stopped. In the new quiet, the house made its small honest sounds—settling wood, the refrigerator's hum—and Felix found themselves thinking of chapter thirteen of their own private ledger, the brother-shaped hole in it, the report never properly filed, the decade of explanations built like scaffolding around a load-bearing guilt. They understood complicity from the inside. They understood wanting a thing so badly you'd sign almost anything to get it.

"Mara," Felix said slowly, "when you hummed it last night. In bed. Alone. Did anything answer?"

Mara was quiet for a long moment.

"That's the thing," she said finally. "That's the thing I've been circling for an hour instead of drinking this tea. No. Nothing answered. And I don't know if that's because the answering thing is bound to the house—or because it doesn't *need* to answer me anymore. Because I already know the song. Because the hook is set." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and very bright. "An archivist knows what a forgery looks like, Felix. I've handled thousands of documents. And I'm telling you: the comfort I felt last night was forged. It was made to fit me specifically. Which means the thing in that well has been reading my mail."

* * *

They worked another hour after that, because work was the raft they both clung to—Mara cross-referencing the ledger's initials against probate records she'd photographed, Felix drafting and discarding plans for lowering a microphone into the refilling well. The water had risen another foot overnight, according to the string-and-weight gauge Felix checked each dawn, and the smell coming up from the yard had shifted from iron to something sweeter, something that made Felix think of flowers laid on closed caskets.

It was near eleven when Mara stopped writing and sat back, and Felix knew before she spoke that the confession wasn't finished. First confessions never are; they're just the entry hall.

"There's one more thing," she said. "And this is the ugly one."

Felix waited.

"When we find the torn half of the verse—when, not if, because the fourth child scratched it into a wall and walls in that house keep what's written on them—I will have to sing it. Or you will. Someone will have to carry it to the well and give it air." Mara's fingers pressed flat against the table, whitening at the tips. "And I have imagined it. Every night this week. I close my eyes and I imagine the moment the melody completes itself, and I imagine hearing it whole for the first time in sixty years, and I imagine—" her breath caught, caught, released, "—I imagine that in the last bar, in the resolution, I'll hear her. Not a memory of her. *Her.* The way the children are in the glass. The way the dead in that house aren't gone, they're just... held. On the wrong side of a second."

"You think finishing the song might bring her back," Felix said.

"I think finishing the song might let me *hear* her," Mara corrected, fierce and precise. "And I know the difference, and the difference doesn't help. Because the covenant works exactly like that, doesn't it? Aldous Harrow sang as a boy and he's lived eighty years on the trade—his soul for his skin, his silence for his safety. The house pays out in exactly the currency you're starving for. It paid the neighbors in normalcy. It paid the children in another day of life. It would pay me in a voice." She laughed, short and terrible, with no humor anywhere in it. "Do you know what the cruelest design feature of that house is? It isn't the mirror. It isn't the well. It's that everything it offers is something you actually want. It never tempts you with what you'd refuse."

Felix thought of the piano lid, of catching themselves humming on the stairs three nights ago, of slamming their own mouth shut like a drawer. The enemy internal. They had written that phrase in their notebook and underlined it twice, and now here was Mara, the ally, the expert, sitting in their kitchen admitting that the enemy had a desk in her office too.

"So what do we do?" Felix asked. "With you. With me. With the fact that both of us want something from that song?"

Mara was silent for a while. Outside, the wet street gleamed under the sodium lamp, and far off a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"We write it down," she said at last. "What we want. Both of us. We write it on paper and we seal it in an envelope and we give it to each other, and we agree that whichever one of us starts talking about the song like it's a gift instead of a weapon, the other one opens the envelope and reads it aloud." Her mouth twisted. "It's not methodology. It's not archival practice. It's two drowning people agreeing to grab each other's collars."

"It'll hold until it doesn't," Felix said.

"Everything holds until it doesn't. That's what an archive *is*—a record of things that held until they didn't." Mara began gathering her papers, squaring the edges, restoring order to the circle of lamplight. But at the door, coat buttoned, bag on her shoulder, she paused with her hand on the frame, and Felix saw her decide to say the last thing, the thing under the thing under the thing.

"Felix. When I said I want to hear her voice—I need you to know that I also know what she'd say if she could. Efua wrote letters home every week for five months. She documented everything. She noticed the song and she was frightened of liking it, in 1961, with no archive training, no framework, nothing but her own good sense." Mara's jaw tightened. "My aunt was not a fool. If she were standing in this kitchen she would take me by the shoulders and tell me that the voice I'm chasing isn't hers anymore, that whatever sings in that house wears the voices of the taken like coats, and that the woman I miss died the day she started humming along." She opened the door onto the wet night. "I know all of that. I know it the way you know the fire is hot while your hand is moving toward it anyway."

She stepped out into the dark, and Felix watched her go down the walk, upright and unhurried, a woman carrying fifty-eight years of wanting in a canvas archive bag.

Felix closed the door and stood in the quiet kitchen, and it was only then, in the silence after, that they heard it—faint, patient, rising not from the pipes this time but from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls themselves, from the wet ground under the foundation:

the second verse, sung softly, in a voice that sounded almost exactly like a nineteen-year-old girl from Accra,

waiting for Mara to get safely out of range before it began practicing.
