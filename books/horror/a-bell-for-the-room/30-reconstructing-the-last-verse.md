## 30. Reconstructing the Last Verse

The fourth child had a name, and her name was Edith Vane, and she had written it into the plaster of the Harrow house's east bedroom wall in letters that got smaller as they went, as though she had been running out of room, or out of time, or out of the belief that anyone would ever come looking.

Felix stood with the flashlight held close, close enough that their breath fogged the beam, and read the name again. Below it, in a child's uneven hand, ran lines of text—not a diary, not a plea, but something stranger: the lyrics of a song, transcribed the way a child transcribes anything, phonetically, imperfectly, with letters invented on the spot where the sounds resisted spelling. *Dunna falla, dunna falla, the watter knows yr naym.* And then, where the second verse should have ended and the third begun, a long gouge, deliberate, deep, the plaster dug away to the lath beneath—except that the gouge was not empty. Felix tilted the light. Scratched into the raw wood underneath, in strokes that must have taken hours, maybe days, with something sharper than fingernails, the song continued.

"She hid it," Mara said from the doorway. She hadn't come all the way in. She was holding the archivist's box against her chest like a shield, and her voice had the careful flatness she used when she was keeping herself from feeling something until the work was done. "The verse. She hid it inside the wall of the house that was going to take her, because she knew no one would ever look at a child's scribbles. And she knew—" Mara stopped. Started again. "She knew that whoever came after would be taught the song the *right* way. The cut way. So she left the wrong way. The whole way."

Felix crouched, bringing the light along the lath. The letters were shallow but legible, each one scored with a patience that made their chest ache. This was not a child scratching in a tantrum. This was a child working the way a mason works, knowing the wall was the only archive that could not be torn, because the house kept its own walls, fed on them, needed them standing.

"Get the camera," Felix said. "Every angle. Before we touch anything."

Mara set the box down and worked, and Felix watched her hands—steady, methodical, the hands of someone who had spent years handling other people's griefs in paper form—and thought about how strange it was that the two of them had become a unit, a two-person expedition into a house that ate people, without either of them ever saying aloud that this was what they were. It had happened the way weather happens. One day there was sky, and then there was storm, and no one had signed anything.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon and into the blue dark of early evening, and they did not turn on the house's lights, because neither of them wanted to see what the house's lights showed. Felix's phone flashlight. Mara's camera. A camping lantern with a warm, dumb, honest glow. The house could lag its mirrors and open its doors, but it could not yet reach into a battery pack from a hardware store, and there was a grim comfort in that, in working by tools that owed the place nothing.

The problem was not reading the wall. The problem was that Edith Vane had been nine years old, and nine-year-olds spell the way water runs—downhill, around obstacles, pooling where the ground lets them—and the verse she had scratched was a phonetic minefield. *Sor-rah* could be *sorrow* or *Sarah* or the name of something that had no spelling in any living language. *The watter knows yr naym* was clear enough. *Dunna tell it wot it wunts* was clear enough, and worse for being clear. But a full third of the text was ambiguous, and Mara refused—absolutely, flatly refused—to guess.

"We are not reconstructing a poem," she said, spreading her photographs across the nursery floor, weighting the corners with the ledger, with her aunt's letters, with a jar of pens. "We are reconstructing a weapon. Or a key. Or a fuse. If we get a word wrong, we won't know until we're standing at the well singing it, and by then—"

"By then the well will be full," Felix said.

Mara didn't answer. She didn't have to. They had both been down to look that morning. The water had risen another foot overnight, black and patient, and it did not reflect the flashlight the way water should. It reflected things slightly *elsewhere*—Felix's own face, but a half-second older, the way the hallway mirror used to be, as if the well had learned the trick and improved on it. Felix had made themselves look. Felix had made themselves look for a full ten seconds, and in the last of those seconds their reflection had smiled without them, small and encouraging, like a teacher watching a student almost get the answer right.

They had climbed out of the cellar and not spoken for an hour.

"Cross-reference," Felix said now, crouching at the edge of the paper field. "Edith's spelling against the ledger. The ledger has the verses the family sang—the cut version. Where Edith's phonetics match the ledger, we're on solid ground. Where they diverge—" They tapped the gouged lath, the hidden continuation. "—that's the half that was torn out. That's where we work."

Mara looked at them for a moment, and something in her expression shifted, some assessment revising itself. "You know," she said, "for a pest controller, you think like an archivist."

"For a pest controller," Felix said, "I think like a lot of things. Ten years of explaining away things that don't explain. You get good at evidence." They hesitated, then added, because it seemed owed, because the wall downstairs had a name on it too: "My brother. The one I told you about. I was the one who found his shoes by the water. And I was the one who said maybe he'd gone off on his own. I built the explanation everyone wanted, because the alternative was that I'd missed something, and if I'd missed something, then it was partly mine." They heard their own voice go flat and careful, Mara's voice, the voice of a person deferring a feeling. "So. Evidence. It's the penance I know how to do."

Mara was quiet a moment. Then she reached across the paper field and touched Felix's wrist, once, briefly, and said, "Then let's do the penance right," and turned back to the photographs, and that was as close as either of them came, that night, to saying the other things.

* * *

The work went the way real work goes, which is to say it went badly and then suddenly well. For two hours they stalled on a single line—*and the one wot kept the kitchin klen*—arguing over whether *klen* was *clean* or *clenched* or a name. It was Mara who broke it, in the end, not through brilliance but through the archivist's oldest trick: she stopped asking what the words meant and started asking who wrote them, and what a nine-year-old in 1931 would have heard at a Harrow dinner table. The ledger, she said, recorded payments to a cleaning woman every second Tuesday for sixty years. *The one who kept the kitchen clean.* It wasn't metaphor. It was a child's inventory of the household. The song wasn't mysticism at its bones—it was a list. A list of who did what for the thing in the well, and what the thing gave back, and—Mara's voice dropped as she read the next line aloud—*and wot it tuk in exchaynge.*

"What it took," Felix repeated.

"In exchange." Mara's finger hovered over the photograph without touching it, the way you hover over a burn. "Felix, it's accounting. The whole song is accounting. The first two verses are the lullaby—the feeding song, the payment schedule. Everyone always assumed the last verse was more of the same, a third payment, a final feeding. But Edith's version—" She turned the ledger around so Felix could see the column she'd built, three nights of cross-referencing collapsed into a single spread of ink. "Edith's version doesn't pay. It *itemizes*. Look. *Yoo fed it wot yoo promist, and it tuk wot it wunted, and the diffrense is the ded.*"

The lantern flickered—not the house, just the lantern, just propane doing what propane does—but they both went still until it steadied.

"The diffrense is the dead," Felix said slowly. "The gap between what was promised and what was taken. That's... that's the accusation. That's the *bill*."

"That's why it was cut." Mara was on her feet now, pacing the nursery's small perimeter, her shadow swinging with her. "You can't have the children singing a bill. You can't have the payment song contain its own fraud. Whoever cut the verse—whoever tore it out of every record—cut it because the last verse doesn't keep the thing fed. It *names* what the thing did. It's not a lullaby, it's an invoice, and an invoice sung aloud at the well is a demand for—" She stopped pacing. "For return. For restitution. For everything it took to be given back."

"Edith," Felix said. "Edith refused to sing the payment song. She wrote the real one instead. And the house took her for it."

"And the house *kept her writing*." Mara's voice cracked on the second word, and she pressed on through it, jaw tight. "The house could have painted over that wall a hundred times. It repainted everything else. But it kept her wall, because—" She laughed, short and awful. "Because the house is a ledger too, isn't it. It keeps its accounts. It just never expected anyone to *audit* them."

* * *

They finished near midnight, kneeling on the nursery floor over the assembled text, and when it was done it did not look like much—a page of Mara's careful handwriting, phonetic renderings resolved into words, question marks still clinging to two lines they'd had to leave provisional. Felix had expected, without admitting the expectation, that the completed verse would feel different in the room. That the air would change. That the house would react.

The house did nothing. The pipes were silent. The mirrors—Felix had made a circuit, compulsively, at half past eleven—reflected in real time, ordinary and dull. The silence had a texture to it that Felix had learned to distrust more than any noise: the held-breath quiet of chapter thirty-three of a house's long patience, the quiet of a thing that has decided to stop singing *at* you because it is about to do something else instead.

"It's done," Mara said. She said it the way you set down something heavy. "It's done, and it's not enough. Two provisional lines, Felix. *And the wun wot wached the dores*—we think *doors*, but it could be *dares*, could be a name, could be—" She pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. "And the last line. The actual last line. Edith didn't finish it. Look."

Felix looked. They had both known it was there for an hour and neither had said it, the way you don't say the thing in the room that is looking at you. The scratched text ran down the lath, line by line, and stopped. Not faded—stopped, mid-phrase, the final strokes shallower than the rest, dragging, the way handwriting goes when the hand is cold, or tired, or being pulled.

*And the watter gives back wot it—*

Nothing after *it*.

"She ran out of time," Mara whispered. "Or it got to her. Or—" She swallowed. "Or she stopped on purpose. Because the last line isn't a fact, Felix. Facts can be hidden in walls. But the last line of an accusation is a *choice*. You have to say it yourself. In your own voice. At the well. She could give us everything except the ending, because the ending was never hers to give."

Felix stared at the incomplete line until the letters swam. *Wot it—*. Took? Owed? Promised? Feared? Every completion changed everything, and the well did not care which one they chose, and the well was rising.

"We'll finish it," Felix said, and heard their own voice come out steadier than they felt, which was, they were beginning to understand, the only kind of courage they had ever actually possessed. "At the well. In the moment. Whoever sings it finishes it. That's the design. That was always the design."

Mara gathered the photographs into their stack, squared the edges, and slid the finished transcription into the archivist's box, on top of her great-aunt's letters, and closed the lid, and latched it, and for a moment they both just looked at the box—two latches, one hinge, a cardboard container holding the only complete accusation against a thing that had been eating a town's children for a hundred years, and it weighed nothing at all.

"We should copy it," Mara said. "Two copies. Separate places. In case—"

"In case of what?"

"In case of Aldous Harrow," Mara said quietly. "Felix. He sent a lawyer. He came back. He sang here as a boy. He has *more* to lose than anyone if this verse gets sung. And we just spent twelve hours building the one document he'd burn the house down to destroy." She looked at the box, then at the door, then at Felix, and her face did the thing faces do when a thought arrives fully formed and terrible. "Where is your copy of the ledger? The page you kept?"

"Downstairs. My kit bag. By the cellar door."

The two of them looked at each other.

Then they were both moving, down the dark stairs, past the mirrors that reflected them honestly and on time, along the hall toward the cellar door—and Felix knew before they reached it, knew from the way the house's silence had changed key, from the cold breathing up through the floorboards, from the door standing open exactly one inch more than they had left it.

The kit bag was there.

The ledger page was not.

* * *

Outside, at the edge of the yard where the well cover sat under its crust of frost, a car engine turned over—old, expensive, idling with the complacency of a thing that had never once been chased. Headlights swept the dead grass, caught the well cover, held there a moment longer than headlights needed to.

Felix stood in the doorway with the empty bag in their hands, and Mara came up beside them, and neither of them spoke, because the engine was already pulling away, and because the well, beneath its cover, beneath its rising black water, had begun—very softly, very patiently, in a register no throat could reach—to hum.
