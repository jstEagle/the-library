## 1. Dead Air

The red ON AIR light had been burning for two hours and forty-one minutes when the phone system lit up for the last time that night.

Emrys saw it from behind the glass of the studio — the little board with its six lines, all dark all night except for the usual trickle of insomniacs and conspiracy hobbyists, and then, at 2:53 a.m., a single green light blinking like a pulse. Line four. Emrys had a superstition about line four, the same way some people have a superstition about the thirteenth floor, and the superstition was this: nothing good ever came in on it after two.

Ten years of *The Night Line* had built up a lot of superstitions. That was the job. You sat in a padded room from midnight to four, five nights a week, talking to a county full of people who couldn't sleep, and after a while you learned which silences meant the caller had hung up and which silences meant they were deciding whether to tell you the thing they'd called to say. You learned that grief kept the same hours as madness, and that on the radio they sounded identical, and that your job was to treat them the same anyway: gently, and without flinching.

Emrys had a voice for it. Everyone said so. A voice like a hand on the shoulder, one listener had written in, back when the show still got letters. Emrys had laughed at that, once, alone in the studio at three in the morning, and then stopped laughing, because it was true, and because a voice like that is a tool, and tools wear down.

"Caller, you're on *The Night Line*. You've got me till four. What's keeping you up?"

The silence on line four was the deciding kind. Emrys let it run. The VU meter trembled — someone breathing, shallow and quick, the way you breathe when you're holding yourself very still.

"Hello?" A woman's voice. Young, maybe. It was hard to tell. Some voices sound young because they're scared.

"Hi there. You're live, but it's just us. Take your time."

"I don't—" A breath. "Is this the radio?"

"It's the radio."

"The one at night."

"Every night. What's your name?"

Another silence. Emrys watched the clock above the console, the old analog one the station had never replaced because it made the room feel less like a coffin. The second hand ticked through eleven seconds before the woman spoke again, and when she did, her voice had changed. It was steadier now. Calmer, in a way that Emrys would think about later, much later, and feel the floor tilt under the memory.

"My name is Marion. I live out past the reservoir."

"Hi, Marion. What's going on out past the reservoir?"

"I wanted to tell someone what happened to me."

"Okay. I'm listening."

"It was the bathtub," Marion said. "I want you to know that first. Everyone always gets that wrong. They think the lake. But it was the bathtub, in my own bathroom, on a Tuesday."

Emrys's hand drifted toward the fader, the old reflex — when a caller starts talking about their own death, you ease them toward the door, toward the hotline number, toward morning. But the fader never moved. Something in the voice had hooked them, some quality underneath the words, flat and precise, like a woman reading from a page only she could see.

"Tell me about Tuesday," Emrys said, which was the thing you said, the thing that kept people talking, the thing that meant *I'm not going anywhere*.

"It was cold," Marion said. "The water. I remember being surprised by that, because I'd run it hot. But by the time I got in it had gone cold, like the house had drunk the heat out of it. I remember thinking that was strange. I remember thinking a lot of things were strange, right up until the end, and then I didn't think anything at all for a while, and then I thought everything, all at once, forever."

"Marion—"

"I was alone in the house. My husband was at his brother's. I'd had wine. Two glasses, maybe three. I lay back and the water went over my ears and the sound stopped — you don't know what silence is until you've been under water, the way it presses on you, like the whole world has put its hands over your ears. And then I couldn't get up."

The VU meter was steady. No tremble. Emrys realized they were leaning into the microphone, elbows on the console, and had been for some time.

"Couldn't, or felt like you couldn't?"

"Couldn't." No hesitation. "My arms were heavy. Not tired — heavy, the way they'd be if someone were lying on them. And the water was in my nose, and I remember the specific feeling of it, the burn, and I remember thinking, *I will remember this feeling*, and I have. That's the joke. That's the whole joke of it. I remember everything."

"Marion, I want to make sure you're safe right now. Are you somewhere you can—"

"I slipped," she said, as if Emrys hadn't spoken. "That's what the report says. Slipped, in the tub, alone. And it's true, in a way. I did slip. But it's the *in a way* that I wanted to tell someone about. Because before I slipped, I heard something."

Emrys's mouth was dry. Outside the studio glass, the hallway of the station was dark, the way it always was at this hour, the way it had been for ten years, and Emrys had never once, in ten years, noticed how dark, how like the inside of a held breath.

"What did you hear?"

"A bell," Marion said. "A small bell, like off a shop door, or like a child's toy. It rang twice. And then the water was cold, and then I was under, and then—" She stopped, and for the first time her voice wavered, and the wavering was worse than the calm had been, because the calm had been wrong and this was right, this was the sound of a person standing at the edge of the worst thing that ever happened to them. "And then I was under for good."

"Marion." Emrys's voice did the thing it did, the hand-on-the-shoulder thing, and it cost more than it ever had. "I hear you. I want you to hear me say that. I hear you."

"I know," Marion said. "That's why I called. I called the other stations first. They didn't pick up. You picked up."

The clock said 3:07. Emrys would remember that too, the way you remember the numbers on a receipt for something you'll never buy again.

"Marion, I have to ask you something, and I need you to be honest with me. Are you safe right now? Are you hurt?"

A pause. When she answered, the calm was back, and it was worse the second time.

"I'm not anything right now," she said. "That's sort of the point. I just wanted someone to know it was the bathtub, and it was Tuesday, and the water went cold like something had drunk the heat, and the bell rang twice, and I was alone, and I didn't want to be. That's all. That's the whole message. Thank you for picking up."

"Marion, wait—"

"Goodnight," she said, gently, the way you say it to a child. "You have a nice voice. It's like a hand on your shoulder."

The line went to dead air. Not the soft hiss of a hang-up — the clean, absolute nothing of a line with no one on it at all, as if the call had never been routed through any phone network in the world.

Emrys sat with the fader under their hand for a long moment. Then, out of ten years of professional habit, they leaned to the mic and said, "We're going to take a short break," and cued the overnight automation, and the station's voice — a syndicated feed, canned and warm — filled the studio with an ad for a mattress store in Harwick.

Emrys sat in the blue light of the console and did what they had done after every strange call for ten years, which was to take the strange thing apart and lay the pieces out flat until none of them were strange anymore.

Piece one: the county had a reservoir, and people drowned, and when people drowned, the ones who loved them called late-night radio shows, because grief doesn't keep business hours. Piece two: callers said *I* when they meant a friend, a sister, a neighbor — everyone did it, everyone softened the story by holding it at arm's length, and Emrys had learned not to correct them, because correcting them ended the call. Piece three: the bell. Houses had bells. Shops had bells. A wind chime, a door chime, the little brass bell on a cat's collar. The mind under stress reaches for sounds and attaches them to the story it's building, and drowning is the oldest story there is.

Piece four, which Emrys did not lay out flat, which stayed curled up in the corner of the mind like something with too many legs: *the water went cold like something had drunk the heat.* Nobody made that up for effect. That was a detail, not a metaphor. Details were the one thing you couldn't fake at three in the morning.

The automation rolled into the news at the top of the hour. Emrys pulled up the call log on the studio terminal, because there was always a call log, because the station had lawyers, and because ten years ago, in their first week, a caller had made a threat against a school and the log had been the only reason anyone knew which school.

Line four. Inbound, 2:53 a.m. Duration: fourteen minutes.

No number.

The field where the number should be held a string of zeros, and beneath it a status flag Emrys had never seen in ten years of reading this log: *NO ORIGIN*.

Emrys stared at it. The mattress ad ended. The newsreader said something about a bridge.

"Okay," Emrys said aloud, to the empty studio, in the voice they used for callers, the gentle one. "Phone system glitch. Old board. It happens."

It didn't happen. Emrys knew it didn't happen the way you know the stove is off. But saying it aloud was part of the ritual, and the ritual had held for ten years, and rituals hold as long as you don't look too hard at what they're holding back.

* * *

The station kept an archive. Not a good one — a closet off the break room with a space heater that didn't work and a decade of local papers in bound stacks, plus a terminal that ran the county's public records index, which was really just a front end for a database last updated when the county still had money. Emrys had used it a hundred times for the show. Local color, lost landmarks, the occasional anniversary of something terrible. *The Night Line* ran on other people's terrible anniversaries. That was the honest way to put it, and Emrys had put it that way to themselves more than once, usually at four in the morning, driving home with the windows down in weather too cold for it.

Marion. Out past the reservoir. Drowned in her bathtub on a Tuesday.

Emrys typed *drowning* into the archive search and got back the whole county's appetite for water: the reservoir itself, which had taken three boys in 1988 and a fisherman in 2004; the creek behind the middle school; a man in Harwick who'd driven into a flooded culvert. Emrys narrowed it. Last seven days. The database coughed up a coroner's notice from the *County Courier*, six days old.

**MARION ELLERBY, 54, of the Reservoir Road area, died Tuesday at her home. Authorities report no signs of foul play. Services were held Saturday at Pike & Sons Funeral Home.**

Emrys read it twice. Three times. The space heater that didn't work ticked anyway, the way dead things sometimes do.

Six days. The call had come in six days after the death, which was strange, but strangeness has a shape, and this shape had edges Emrys could hold: a sister, a friend, someone who'd loved her, someone for whom six days was the exact distance between *unbearable* and *ready to talk*. People called the show at exactly that distance all the time. Emrys had a name for them, privately: the six-day callers. Grief has a schedule.

Except.

Emrys pulled up the full notice. There was a photograph, small and grainy, the kind the paper ran for anyone with a family willing to provide one. A woman in her fifties, gray-blond hair, a smile that looked like it had been rehearsed for the camera and then forgotten.

Emrys had never seen this woman before in their life. Emrys was sure of it, and then, in the space of one long breath, sure of the opposite — because the voice was still in the room, or in Emrys's head, or in the walls of the closet with the dead space heater, and the voice belonged to that face, the calm and the wavering, *I called the other stations first. They didn't pick up. You picked up.*

"Okay," Emrys said, and their voice in the little room sounded like a stranger's. "Okay. So a friend called. A friend who knew the details. A friend who—"

Who said *I*. Who said *my husband was at his brother's*. Who knew the water had gone cold.

Emrys looked up the obituary. It was there, on the funeral home's site, with a guestbook. Emrys read it the way you read a ransom note, hunting for the thing that isn't being said. *Beloved wife of Gerald. Stepmother to two. Preceded in death by—* Nothing. No children. No sister named. The services had been Saturday. Six days after the death, and the services were already *over*.

The six-day callers called before the funeral. Emrys knew that the way they knew the stove was off. Grief calls before the funeral, when the person is still lying in state somewhere and the phone is the only place the grief fits.

Emrys sat back on the archive's one folding chair and looked at the photograph for a long time.

Then, because the mind is a machine for making the strange flat, Emrys did the last thing on the list. They called the station's overnight engineer — Sal, who had been the overnight engineer since before Emrys had the show, who answered on the second ring at any hour and never asked why.

"Sal. It's Emrys. Weird question. Line four tonight, around three. Any chance the board logged something weird? Ghost call, crossed line, anything?"

A pause on the other end. Sal's pause had a texture — the pause of a man putting down his coffee and turning to look at the equipment in question.

"Line four," Sal said. "Tonight?"

"2:53."

Another pause. Longer. When Sal spoke again, he had the careful voice people use when they're deciding how much to tell you, and Emrys had heard that voice on the show a thousand times and never once from Sal.

"Board's been doing something funny this week," Sal said. "Line four lights up when nothing's calling. I keep clearing it and it comes back. I was going to mention it."

"When did it start?"

"Tuesday," Sal said.

Emrys looked at the photograph of Marion Ellerby smiling her rehearsed smile, six days dead, drowned in a cold bathtub on a Tuesday, and felt — for the first time in ten years of taking apart strange things and laying the pieces out flat — a piece that would not lie down.

"Sal," Emrys said. "What time Tuesday?"

"Doesn't say. It just — hold on." Keys clacking. "First log entry, Tuesday, 3:07 a.m."

The clock in the studio had said 3:07 when Marion said *goodnight*. Emrys hadn't told anyone that. There had been no one to tell.

"Sal, I'm going to ask you something else, and I want you to think before you answer. When line four lights up with nobody on it — does the board log a duration? Does it show how long the line stays open?"

"Yeah, that's the weird part." Sal's voice had gone quiet, the voice of a man standing in a dark hallway listening to a house. "It's always the same. Fourteen minutes."

Emrys hung up without saying goodbye, which Sal would understand, Sal had been doing overnights for thirty years, Sal knew that some conversations end because you can't be on the phone anymore, not because you're finished.

The archive closet was very quiet. The photograph looked up from the terminal's glow. Outside the one small window, the sky was doing the thing it does at four in the morning, the color of a held breath, and Emrys realized they were going to have to drive home in it, to the house they'd lived in for three years, alone, the way Marion Ellerby had been alone, and that for the first time in ten years of *The Night Line*, Emrys did not want to turn off the studio lights, because the studio lights were the only lights in the county with a witness.

At the door, Emrys stopped. There was no reason to stop. The reason arrived a moment later, the way the reason always does, one beat behind — like a reflection that takes an extra second to catch up.

Somewhere in the building, faint, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the edge, something small and bright rang twice.

Emrys stood in the doorway and did not turn around. Then they turned around. There was nothing there. There was the dark studio and the dead console and the analog clock, its second hand ticking, patient, precise, keeping time for a room with no one in it.

Emrys drove home with the radio on, loud, all four hours of the sign-off loop, and told themselves it was for the company.

It was for the company. It was only for the company.

At home, in the dark hallway, on the way to bed, Emrys passed the mirror in the entry hall and did not look at it, and then did, and their own face looked back — tired, forty-something, ordinary, ten years of nights written under the eyes.

Emrys blinked.

The reflection blinked too.

Emrys stood there for a long moment in the dark, telling themselves the hallway light had flickered, that they were exhausted, that grief has a schedule and so does exhaustion, and then they went to bed and did not sleep, and if the reflection lay awake too, staring at the ceiling one second behind them, neither of them said anything about it.
