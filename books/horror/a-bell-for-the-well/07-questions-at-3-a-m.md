## 7. Questions at 3 A.M.

Paloma had a system for everything. It was, she'd always assumed, the twin thing — when you share a face and a birthday and a childhood bedroom with another person, you learn to build walls out of routine or else lose track of which memories are yours. So when she decided to interrogate the voice on her recorder, she did it the way she did everything: with a legal pad, a ballpoint pen, and a color-coded schedule taped to the wall of her dead brother's room.

The schedule was the part that would have made Palen laugh. *Monday: formulate questions. Tuesday: baseline recording, no questions, control conditions. Wednesday: ask questions. Thursday: analyze. Friday: conclude that I am losing my mind, seek professional help, possibly move to a coastal town where the water is normal and aboveground.*

She'd written that last line at two in the morning, and in the daylight it still made her snort, which she counted as a small victory. Grief, she was learning, was not a wave. It was a houseguest who rearranged your furniture while you slept and occasionally left dead things in the pantry.

The baseline recording came first. Tuesday night she set the recorder on the nightstand — the same nightstand, she realized with a small chill, that had held the journal before she'd found it — pressed record, and said aloud, for the benefit of any future skeptic including herself: "October 14th. Control session. No questions will be asked. Subject is Paloma Galloway, age thirty-one, skeptic, tired, and wearing her brother's flannel because it still smells like him and I refuse to apologize for that."

She slept. She woke at dawn with drool on the pillow and the flat, hungover feeling of a night spent dreaming about the well — which she had, again, the black circle of it, the rope, the sound of water that wasn't there.

The tape held forty-one minutes of her breathing, a cough at 1:52, and at 3:11 a.m. — the same time, to the minute — a sound like a chair being drawn back from a table in an empty room. Then silence. No voice. As if whatever sat across from her at night had waited politely for her to open the conversation and, when she didn't, had gone away hungry.

"Okay," she said to the empty room, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You want questions. Let's do questions."

* * *

She spent Wednesday preparing, and this was where the gentle humor of her system curdled a little, because she caught herself doing something she hadn't done since she was nine: she talked to Palen out loud. Not to a memory. To the room. To the possibility.

"Ground rules," she told the nightstand that evening, recorder already running, the red light glowing like a small patient eye. "One: you don't get to ask me anything. I ask, you answer. Two: no riddles. I have a master's degree and a low tolerance for cryptic bullshit. Three—" and here her voice did wobble, and she let it, because she'd learned that pretending it wouldn't only delayed the wobble and made it worse, "—if you're him, you say something only he would know. If you're not him, say that too. I can work with either. What I can't work with is nothing."

She turned off the lamp. She lay down in her brother's bed, in her brother's flannel, and stared at the ceiling where they'd once stuck glow-in-the-dark stars in the shape of nothing in particular, because Palen had insisted constellations were "just someone else's connect-the-dots, and we can do better."

At 3:11 a.m., she was awake. She had set two alarms and drunk no wine and told herself she'd simply lie still and listen, and she had expected to lie there feeling foolish. Instead she lay there feeling like a witness waiting for a defendant to enter the courtroom.

The recorder's red light burned in the dark. The house ticked. Somewhere down the street a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Then, from the direction of the nightstand — not through the speaker, which was the detail she would fixate on later, lying awake in the gray hours; the voice came from the air beside the machine, as if the machine were merely a courtesy, a formality, a microphone held up to a room that was already full — came the voice.

"Ask."

It was Palen's voice. She had prepared for that, told herself she was ready, and she was not ready. It was his exact cadence, the slight upward lift at the end of short sentences, the way he clipped his t's. But she had listened to the first tape a dozen times now, and she had noticed what she'd noticed and hadn't let herself name: the voice was *right* the way a photograph is right. Accurate. Flat. A Palen with the dimension pressed out of it.

"Question one," she said, and her voice sounded younger than she wanted it to. "What did we bury in the tin box behind the garage, summer we were ten?"

A pause. Not a thinking pause — a *choosing* pause, she thought. Like a lawyer selecting which truth to offer.

"The tin box," the voice said, "held your tooth, the one Mr. Delgado's fence knocked out. A baseball card of a man whose name you never knew but whose face you called Uncle Grumpy for two years. And a note that said, in my handwriting, *Paloma was here first.*" A beat. "Because you were. Born four minutes before me. You never let me forget it, and I never wanted you to."

Her throat closed. That was true. That was so precisely, devastatingly true — the note, the smugness of it, the way Palen had signed it *Palen, the second-best twin* and she'd laughed until she cried — that she had to press the flannel sleeve against her mouth for a full ten seconds before she could speak.

"That's not public knowledge," she managed. "That's not in any photo album. Aunt Verity doesn't know about the note."

"No," the voice agreed. "She doesn't."

"Question two." She sat up. The dark was total now, the glow stars long dead, and she spoke into it the way you speak into a well. "The journal. My brother's journal. Half the pages are torn out. What was on them?"

This time the pause was longer. When the voice came, it had shifted — still Palen's timbre, but the rhythm was different, slower, older, like a recording played at the wrong speed by someone who wanted very much for her not to notice.

"You already know what it was about," it said. "You read the half that survived. The well. The families. The arithmetic."

"Say it anyway."

"The town pays," the voice said. "The town has always paid. Twelve families signed a ledger in 1863 because the creek was dying and their children were thirsty, and the well — the well was never a well, Paloma. It was a mouth. And they learned what it liked to eat."

Her skin crawled in a slow wave, scalp to heel. "And the torn pages?"

"Names." The word came out soft, almost tender, and that was somehow the worst part. "He was making a list. He'd found the ledger's pattern — he was smarter than they gave him credit for, your brother, smarter than *you* gave him credit for — and he understood that the debt isn't paid by giving. It's paid by *naming*. By saying out loud what was taken. He was writing down every name the well ever swallowed, so that someone with a bell and a spine could say them all at once and end it."

The room seemed to lean in. Paloma became aware that she was gripping the flannel at her sternum, wringing it like a washcloth.

"Where are the pages?"

Silence.

"You said no riddles," the voice said, and there — *there* — was the lift at the end of the sentence, Palen's lift, deployed like a key turning in her chest. "I'm not riddling. I'm not *allowed* to tell you where. There's a difference, and you, of all people, know the difference between can't and won't."

"Then tell me what you *can* tell me."

Another pause. When it spoke again, the voice had changed again — not in pitch, not in cadence, but in *temperature*. Palen's voice had always been warm, even teasing, even angry. This was Palen's warmth the way a heat lamp is the sun.

"Ask me about the drowned man," it said. "The one in the casket with my face. Ask me his name. That I can give you. That I've been *waiting* to give you."

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. She reached for the water glass on the nightstand and found her hand shaking too hard and put it back.

"Who is he?"

"Tobias Finch," the voice said, and the name landed in the dark room like a stone dropped down a shaft — she heard it fall and fall and never hit bottom. "Born March 3rd, 1957. Twin of Thomas Finch. Taken from the Finch line in the autumn of 1964, when the well was owed and the Finches had no more money to buy their way out and the Harrows offered to *help*." A sound that might, in a human throat, have been a breath. "He has been down there sixty years, Paloma. He has been down there so long he's forgotten his mother's face but not her voice. He has been wearing my shape because a drowned boy learns that the living will only listen to a face they're already grieving."

The recorder's red light seemed very bright. Paloma realized her eyes were wet and did not wipe them, because wiping them meant moving, and moving meant admitting her body was still hers.

"You're not Palen," she said. It was not a question, but she made it one anyway, because she needed to hear the answer in his stolen voice. "Are you."

The pause stretched. Somewhere in the walls, the house ticked like a cooling engine, like a clock, like a countdown.

"I'm the one who answered," the voice said finally. "I'm the one who's been answering since 1964, every time this town got hungry and quiet. I'm the one who remembers all the names, because remembering them is the only thing I'm permitted to do down there." The warmth flickered — or she imagined it flickered — into something rawer. "Your brother is here too. Or what the well kept of him. I'm not him. But I'm the closest thing to him you're going to get, and I am *hungry*, Paloma Galloway, and what I'm hungry for is the same thing your brother was hungry for, the same thing Tobias Finch has been hungry for across sixty years of dark water."

"Which is?"

"Justice," said the voice that was not her brother, in her brother's voice. "Spoken aloud. Every name. Starting with the ones on the pages he tore out and hid where they couldn't be torn again."

* * *

She did not sleep again. At dawn she sat at the kitchen table with the recorder and the legal pad, and she played the tape back, and this time the voice came only through the speaker, thin and tinny and unmistakably *recorded*, a thing on a machine, evidence, data. That helped. That helped a lot.

*3:11 a.m. — Subject (P.G.) asks re: tin box. Voice responds with verifiable private detail. Detail correct.*

*3:19 — Subject asks re: missing journal pages. Voice describes contents: names, ritual, intent to end debt. Cannot confirm — pages unrecovered.*

*3:26 — Voice identifies drowned man as TOBIAS FINCH, b. 1957, twin of Thomas Finch, taken 1964. CLAIM IS CHECKABLE.*

That last line she underlined three times. Because that was the seam, wasn't it? That was the clean bright line her whole skeptical architecture could stand on: if Tobias Finch existed, if there had been a Finch family with twins in 1964, if a boy had gone missing that autumn and the town had buried the fact — then the voice knew things it could not know, and she would have to believe something impossible. And if Tobias Finch did *not* exist, then the voice was a liar, and liars had reasons, and she'd want to know whose.

Either way, she had work to do. She almost laughed at how much better that made her feel. Give Paloma Galloway a checkable claim and a county archive and she was a functioning human being again; it was the unanswerable questions that turned her into a woman talking to a nightstand.

She showered, dressed, and was halfway through her second cup of coffee when Aunt Verity let herself in with a casserole dish and the particular brightness of a woman performing calm.

"There's my girl. You're up early."

"Couldn't sleep." Paloma kept her voice light, kept her eyes on the legal pad, which she had — with a speed she was quietly proud of — flipped to a page of grocery lists. "Aunt Verity, random question. Did the town ever have a Finch family? Finches. Like the bird."

The casserole dish made it to the counter. Verity's hands did not. Paloma watched her aunt's face do something she had never seen it do in thirty-one years: it went *still*, all at once, like a pond freezing top to bottom.

"Why on earth would you ask that?"

"Just something in Palen's journal. A name crossed out." Paloma sipped her coffee, heart hammering, lying with the smooth ease of a woman who'd spent a week being lied to and was a fast study. "Probably nothing."

"Probably," Verity said. Her back was turned now, fussing with the casserole lid that didn't need fussing with. "Hollow Creek's had all kinds of families come and go. It was a long time ago." A pause, and then, too casually: "You know, honey, I've been thinking. The air out here isn't agreeing with you. Your mother was the same — city girl, thin blood. Maybe when the estate's settled you ought to head home. I could drive you as far as the county line myself, tomorrow even, before the—"

"Before the harvest moon," Paloma said.

The fussing stopped. In the silence, the refrigerator hummed, and the coffee maker dripped its last, and Paloma understood, with the clarity that comes at the exact moment it is least welcome, that her aunt had been trying to get her out of town since the funeral, and that the funeral had been *six days ago*, and that the harvest moon was four days away, and that everyone in this town was counting.

"The Finches," Verity said at last, without turning around, "were a long time ago, and the less said about them the better, and I will thank you to leave that journal out of it. Some pages get torn out because they're better off torn out, Paloma. That's not a sin. That's *mercy*."

"Whose mercy?"

Verity set the casserole dish down with a small, precise click, and when she turned, her eyes were wet and furious and terrified in equal measure. "Eat your eggs," she said, and left without her dish.

* * *

That afternoon Paloma drove to the county archive in Corrick's Ferry, forty minutes of dry brown hills and a radio that only got gospel and static. The archivist was a retired schoolteacher named Miss Odum who remembered Palen — "the polite one, always said thank you, such a shame" — and who let Paloma into the genealogy stacks with the wave of a hand and a warning not to touch the 1910 census with her coffee.

Paloma didn't have coffee. She had a name.

The Finch family took her twenty minutes to find. Thomas and Alma Finch, married 1954. Sons: Thomas Jr., born 1957. And — in the 1960 census, in the careful hand of a census-taker with nothing left to prove — a second son, listed between the lines of the printed form, squeezed into the margin where the enumerator had run out of rows.

*Tobias.*

Born March 3rd, 1957. Four minutes after his brother.

Paloma stood in the stacks with her hand flat on the page and her breath coming carefully, in and out, the way you breathe when the floor has tilted and you are pretending it hasn't. The voice had known the birthday. Not the year — the *day*. A fact that appeared in no newspaper, because she'd already checked the microfilm on the drive over, and there was no article about a missing Finch boy in the autumn of 1964. No article at all. A twin, gone, and the paper printed a harvest festival schedule where the story should have been.

She walked out into the flat white afternoon light and sat in her car in the archive lot with the engine off, and she let herself say it out loud, because saying it aloud was the only ritual she trusted.

"Something in that room knew things. Something in that room is wearing my brother's voice and asking me for justice. And I sat in the dark and *negotiated with it*." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Dad always said I'd argue with a fence post. Congratulations, Paloma. You found a fence post that argues back."

But that wasn't the thought that stayed. The thought that stayed was smaller and colder and had teeth.

The voice had said: *he hid the pages where they couldn't be torn again.* Hidden from the town. Hidden from Verity. Hidden from whoever had torn the journal in the first place — and someone had, someone with hands and a reason, someone whose face went pond-still at the name Finch.

And the voice had said it couldn't tell her where. *Can't and won't.* But it had also said *starting with the ones on the pages he tore out* — which meant the voice knew the names. Which meant the voice didn't need her to find the pages at all, not really, not for the names.

Which meant the pages mattered for a different reason. Not the list. The *handwriting*. The proof. Palen's own words, in Palen's own ink, describing what the town did — the one thing no voice in the dark, however convincing, could ever be.

The voice wanted her to have them. Verity wanted her not to. And Paloma Galloway had spent thirty-one years noticing that when two people wanted opposite things, the truth was usually standing in the middle, waiting to be picked up.

She turned the key. The engine caught. And as she pulled out of the lot, she found herself remembering, unbidden, the one place in her childhood that had always been off-limits, the one place Verity's rules had circled like a fence: *no one goes to the well house, and no one speaks near the well after dark.*

The well house. Where a careful boy who'd learned the town's rules would hide the one thing that mattered — in the one place the town was too afraid to look.

Paloma drove home through the brown hills with the harvest moon's twin, the sun, sinking red behind her, and made a list on the legal pad, and at the top of the list, in letters she pressed hard enough to emboss three pages down, she wrote:

*Tomorrow. Daylight. The well house.*
