## 12. What Palen Owed

Paloma had read somewhere — in one of the pop-science books she'd bought by the pound during her debunking phase — that dreams were the brain's janitorial staff, sweeping up the day's clutter and hosing down the emotional floors. She had found this comforting once. Now, lying in her childhood bed with the recorder running on the nightstand and the red thread from the ledger coiled on her chest like a small sleeping snake, she thought the janitor had clearly gone on strike.

She did not so much fall asleep as get taken.

* * *

The dream began with water that wasn't wet.

She was standing in the creek bed behind the house, the one that had been dry since before she was born, except in the dream it ran shallow and clear over smooth gray stones, and when she knelt and put her hand in it, the water parted around her fingers like a rumor around a polite person. The sun was wrong. It hung low and enormous, the color of a peach left too long on the counter, and it did not move no matter how long she stood there.

"Okay," she said aloud, because she had decided some years ago that if she was going to have an unreasonable experience, she was at least going to narrate it like a reasonable woman. "Either I'm dreaming, or Hollow Creek has finally gotten interesting."

"You're dreaming," said Palen's voice, behind her. "Probably."

She turned. He was sitting on the flat rock they used to jump off of as children, the one Verity called the Deacon's Pulpit for reasons neither of them had ever learned. He looked — this was the part that made her chest ache in a way dreams shouldn't be allowed to do — he looked *good*. Healthy. His hair was doing the thing it always did, sticking up on the left side like it was trying to leave without him. He was wearing the flannel shirt with the burn hole on the cuff from the time he'd tried to make s'mores with a magnifying glass and set his own sleeve alight, age eleven, laughing so hard he couldn't put himself out.

"That shirt burned," Paloma said. "I watched you bury it in the yard. You gave it a funeral. You cried a little."

"I know." He looked down at the cuff, at the hole that wasn't there anymore, and something flickered across his face — not sadness exactly, more like a man checking his watch and finding the time less convenient than expected. "It's what you remember. That's mostly what I've got to work with."

The words landed cold, despite the peach-colored sun. "Mostly."

"Sit down, Polly." He patted the rock beside him. "There's something you need to see, and I'd rather show you than tell you, because if I tell you, you'll argue, and you're very good at arguing, and I'm dead, so my stamina isn't what it was."

The joke should have been funny. Some distant part of her noted that it was even structurally funny, well-constructed, his kind of joke. But the rest of her sat down on the rock, because when your dead twin asks you to sit, you sit.

* * *

The creek changed.

It happened between one blink and the next — the clear water darkening like tea steeping, then like coffee, then like something that had never been a beverage at all. The stones beneath it went soft-looking, indistinct. And the smell arrived: mineral and old, the smell of the well house, of coins held too long in a fist, of cellars.

"Don't fight it," Palen said quietly. "Fighting just makes it take longer, and there's a thing coming you need to see all the way through."

"I hate when you say things like that."

"I know. You always hated being told how a movie ended too. Even when you hadn't started it yet."

The water rose. Not violently — that would almost have been a mercy. It rose the way floodwater rises in news footage, patient and total, climbing the banks, climbing their legs, and Paloma felt it reach her knees and it was warm. Warm as bathwater. Warm as blood left out. She grabbed Palen's arm and he was solid, blessedly solid, and together they were lifted — no, not lifted, *carried*, the way the town itself might carry two small figures it had decided belonged to it —

— and then they were inside the well.

Not falling. Standing. That was the worst of it, later, when she woke and tried to reconstruct it: they were simply *standing* at the bottom of the well, on dry packed earth, looking up the long stone throat of it to a coin of sky that showed the harvest moon, three nights early and swollen fat. The walls were close enough to touch. Someone had touched them. The stones were covered in marks — small ones, hand-sized, scratched over and over, hundreds of them, thousands, and it took her dream-slow mind a moment to understand what she was looking at.

Tally marks.

Someone had counted days down here. Many someones. For many days.

"How many," she heard herself ask, and her voice echoed strangely, arriving back at her ears from the wrong direction.

"The ledger says twelve names struck through in red," Palen said. He wasn't looking at the walls. He was looking at her, and his face had the expression he used to wear when he'd broken something of hers and was deciding whether to confess now or let her discover it organically. "But ledgers are for the people who keep them. The walls are for the people who were kept."

"Palen." Her voice came out thin. "What is this?"

"This is what I owed," he said. "Or what they said I owed. Those aren't the same thing, but nobody down here ever got to file a complaint." He took a breath she didn't need him to take. "Watch the moon. And watch the wall. And, Polly — whatever you see me do — remember that I didn't believe either. Right up until the end. We matched that way."

The coin of sky began to brighten. Moonrise, in the dream, happened fast, the way it does in time-lapse videos, and as the light poured down the shaft of the well, the shadows on the walls moved. Not the tally marks. Other shadows. Shapes that stood away from the stone and stretched, and among them — she saw it and her whole body went rigid — a shape with her brother's shoulders.

"No," she said. "No, I'm not watching this. This isn't — you drowned, they pulled you out, I *saw* —"

"You saw a body," Palen said gently. "Look again. Look at what you actually saw."

And the dream, having no respect whatsoever for her boundaries, replayed it.

* * *

The funeral parlor again. The casket. The undertaker's hands folding back the white cloth. But this time the dream camera — dream-Paloma's eyes, dragged where they didn't want to go — went to the face, and stayed, and *looked*.

Her brother's face. Of course it was. She'd known it was, had raged at herself for doubting, had catalogued her own paranoia like a scientist logging anomalous data. But now, seeing it again with the well still warm around her ankles, she saw what grief had smoothed over: the face was right the way a photograph is right. Slightly too still. Slightly too symmetrical. The scar above his eyebrow — the one from the bicycle, age nine, the one she'd teased him about until the day she left for college — absent. The crooked finger from the door-slamming incident, tucked demurely out of sight.

And the hands. She had held those hands at the viewing, cold and waxy, and told herself the wrongness was the embalming fluid. But the calluses were wrong. Palen had worked construction summers; his palms had ridges like old leather. These palms were soft. Scholar's palms. Office palms.

*Tobias Finch,* said a voice that was not in the dream, or was entirely the dream, coming from the walls themselves, from the tally marks, from everywhere. *His name was Tobias Finch.*

"Stop," Paloma whispered. "Stop it, stop —"

"He was a twin," the walls said, patient as sediment. "Taken in 1964. Kept sixty years past his turn. They owed the well a Galloway, and the Harrows owed the well a payment, and your aunt owed the Harrows a favor, and debts roll downhill in this town same as water."

"STOP."

The dream obeyed her, the way dreams do — everything stopped at once, mid-motion, mid-word, the moon frozen in its coin of sky, and in the ringing silence Palen turned to her and said, very quietly:

"Now you know why I brought you here instead of telling you. Because you'd have argued. And you'd have been *right* to argue, and it wouldn't have mattered, and that's the part I couldn't figure out how to say while you were awake."

"What did she do?" Paloma asked. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Verity. What exactly did she do?"

Palen looked up the shaft at the frozen moon for a long moment.

"She traded me," he said.

* * *

The dream shifted again, and this time it was memory — or something wearing memory's clothes — and Paloma understood with a lurch that she was seeing it from outside herself, floating near the ceiling of Aunt Verity's kitchen, three months ago, in the ordinary yellow light of an ordinary evening.

Verity sat at the table with a cup of tea gone cold in front of her. Across from her sat a woman Paloma knew only from photographs and the occasional grim mention: Margaret Harrow, the deputy's mother, dead these ten years, rendered here in terrible detail — the pearl earrings, the cardigan buttoned to the throat, the smile that engaged the mouth and nothing above it.

"You're asking me to choose," Verity said, and her voice was smaller than Paloma had ever heard it, smaller than the woman who'd raised two children alone and never once asked anyone for anything.

"I'm offering you arithmetic," Margaret Harrow said. "The debt comes due at the harvest moon. Twelve families signed; twelve families pay. Nine lines are done — the well takes its interest where it can find it, and it has been *patient*, Verity, but patience is not forgiveness. Three lines remain. Yours. Ours. The Motts'. Ezra Mott is ninety-one years old and childless; his line ends naturally, and the well will accept that. Ours we will manage, as we have always managed it. Which leaves yours. Two children. Twins." She smiled her mouth-smile. "Twins are traditional. The founders preferred twins. Two of a kind, so the well can't tell which one it got. So *God* can't tell."

"They're twenty-six years old."

"And the founders' children were younger, yes, but the well is not particular about age. Only about blood." Margaret folded her hands. "One twin, Verity. One. And the other goes free, and the line continues, and the debt is paid for another generation. Or refuse, and the well comes for both, and then it comes for the town's water, and then it comes for the town. You've seen the creek. You know what's already happening."

"There has to be another way. There's always —"

"There is always another way," Margaret agreed pleasantly, "and every family for a hundred and sixty years has searched for it, and none has found it, and the ones who tried anyway are the names on the walls of the well house cellar. Choose."

From her vantage near the ceiling, Paloma watched her aunt's face do something she had never seen it do in thirty years of knowing her: break. Not crumble — break, along a fault line that must have been there since childhood, since the first time a Galloway was asked what their blood was worth.

"Which one?" Verity whispered.

"That," said Margaret Harrow, rising, collecting her coat, "is the only choice you get. I'd think carefully. The well likes faith. A believer goes down easier, they say. Less... noise."

The kitchen emptied. Verity sat alone with her cold tea for a long time. And then, in the dream, Paloma watched her aunt take out a pen and a piece of paper and write two names on it, side by side, and stare at them, and stare at them, and finally draw a slow circle around one.

*Paloma.*

She circled *Paloma*.

* * *

"No," Paloma said, in the well, in the dark, in the arms of the dream. "No, that's not — she circled mine. She picked *me*. So how am I here and you're —"

"Because I switched the papers."

Palen was standing beside her again, and up close, in the moonlight pouring down the shaft, she could see that he was becoming translucent at the edges, like paper held against a window.

"After she wrote them down, she hid the list in the flour tin — the blue one, top shelf, she's hidden everything important in that tin since before we were born. I found it. I don't know how I found it. I'd been hearing things at night by then, same as you, and I'd stopped telling you because you'd have wanted proof, and I couldn't give you proof, only a feeling." He smiled, and it was his real smile, crooked, tired. "So I swapped the names. Wrote yours out, wrote mine in, put it back. She never checked. Why would she check? She'd already made the hardest decision of her life. People don't re-open a door like that."

"You *idiot*," Paloma said, and her voice cracked clean through the middle of it. "You absolute — you thought not believing would protect you? Is that what she told you? That the well doesn't take skeptics?"

"She thought faithlessness would make me invisible to it." He shrugged, and the shrug was fading too, going soft at the edges. "Turns out the well isn't picky about belief. Turns out it's only picky about *blood*, and about whether somebody signs the paperwork." He reached out and took her hand, and his grip was cool and getting cooler. "Polly. Listen. There's not much time and I need you to hear this part sober, so to speak. What she did was monstrous. Be angry at her. You're entitled; anger looks good on you. But she did it believing she was saving one of us, and she spent sixty years' worth of guilt picking *wrong*, and when you wake up, she is going to be sitting in that kitchen waiting for you, because she's known since the funeral that you'd find out, and Verity Galloway has never once run from a reckoning. Make her tell you the rest. There's a part she hasn't told anyone. About whose name was supposed to be on that list before ours."

The moon moved. Just slightly — a degree, maybe less — but the whole well responded, the tally-marked walls shivering, the warm dark below them exhaling, and somewhere far overhead came a sound like a bell being tested. One soft note. Then silence, the way a predator is silent.

"That's your alarm clock," Palen said. "Go."

"Palen — wait — whose name? Whose name was supposed to —"

"Wake up, Polly."

"Whose NAME?"

He leaned close, and his lips were at her ear, and his voice was almost gone, worn through like fabric, and what he said was:

"Ask her about Mama."

* * *

Paloma came awake at 3:11 a.m., gasping, drenched, the recorder's red light steady on the nightstand like a patient eye.

She lay still for a full minute, doing inventory. Heart: hammering. Sheets: ruined. Red thread: still coiled on her chest, and — she sat up sharply — *warm*.

On the nightstand, the tape kept turning. She let it run. She'd listen later, or she wouldn't; she suspected she already knew what it would hold. Instead she swung her feet to the floor, pulled on the flannel robe hanging on the door — Palen's flannel, the spare, the one without the burn hole — and stood in the hallway of the old house, looking down toward the kitchen.

A light was on under the door.

Of course it was. There had been a light on under that door every night since she'd come home, she realized now; she'd noticed and filed it away and said nothing, the way this town taught you to notice and file and say nothing. Verity did not sleep. Verity sat in the kitchen in the small hours with cold tea, keeping a vigil she had never explained, and Paloma had assumed it was grief.

It wasn't grief. It was a sentry post. Sixty years of sentry duty, waiting for the debt to walk back through the door.

Paloma's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the hallway wall and breathed, and underneath the shaking she found the other thing, the bigger thing, the thing with teeth: rage. Clean and total and clarifying, rage like a struck match in a dark room. Her brother had died holding a door shut with his body so that his sister could walk through it. Her aunt had drawn a circle around her name. And somewhere in this town, the Harrows were still keeping books.

She walked down the hall. She opened the kitchen door.

Verity sat at the table, exactly as in the dream, cup of tea untouched, and she looked up at Paloma with an expression of such complete, exhausted recognition — the look of a woman who has been waiting at this table for sixty years and knows, finally, that the appointment has arrived — that Paloma's prepared speech died in her throat.

"Aunt Verity," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Tell me about Mama. Tell me whose name was on the list before ours."

The teacup rattled once against its saucer, and stopped.

Verity closed her eyes.

"Sit down, child," she said. "This is a long confession, and I've only got the strength to make it once."
