## 18. The Town Remembers

Paloma had expected to have to drag Hollow Creek to the well the way you drag a cat to a bath. She had imagined locked doors, drawn curtains, maybe Ruth standing on Main Street with a bullhorn shouting something official-sounding about a mandatory civic assembly.

What she got instead was Mrs. Delia Pruett arriving at Verity's porch at seven in the morning with a casserole dish full of deviled eggs and the announcement that she'd be coming to "whatever it is you're doing down there," because her grandmother had been a Finch and nobody in this town had ever once said that name out loud where Delia could hear it, and she was eighty-two years old and done waiting.

"You'll want more than eggs," Paloma said faintly.

"I'll want answers," said Mrs. Pruett. "The eggs are for after. Assuming there is an after."

By nine o'clock, word had moved through town the way word always did in Hollow Creek — not by phone, which half the town still didn't trust, but by a kind of osmosis involving church parking lots and the pharmacy counter. By ten, when Paloma and Ruth drove out to the well with the ledger wrapped in a flour sack on the seat between them like something that might bite, there were already trucks lining the fence road. People stood in loose clusters, arms crossed, wearing their Sunday clothes or their work clothes depending on which lie they'd told themselves about why they were coming.

"They think it's a memorial," Ruth said, cutting the engine. Her jaw was still bruised from whatever had happened at Harrow House; she hadn't given Paloma more than three sentences about it, and Paloma had learned not to push. "I let them think it. Easier to get them here for a memorial than for an exorcism."

"Is that what this is?"

Ruth looked at the crowd, then at the ledger, then at the well house squatting beyond the fence with its door hanging open like a slack mouth.

"It's a reckoning," she said. "Memorials are for things that are over."

* * *

Ezra Mott was already there, of course. He sat in a folding chair someone had brought him, positioned exactly eleven feet from the well's stone lip — close enough to supervise, far enough, Paloma suspected, out of some old arithmetic he carried in his bones. He couldn't speak anymore; the red thread had seen to that. But he had a slate hanging around his neck on a shoelace, and a piece of chalk in his fist, and when Paloma knelt beside him he wrote one word and turned it toward her without ceremony:

*NAMES.*

"I know," she said. "I've been up all night with the list."

He wiped the slate with his sleeve and wrote again, slower, the chalk squeaking: *NOT JUST LEDGER. ASK THEM. EVERY FAMILY BRINGS ITS OWN.*

Paloma looked out at the crowd. Farmers and shopkeepers and two of the Okafor boys from the feed store, who were passing a thermos back and forth and pretending very hard that they weren't scared. Old Mr. Harrow was nowhere in sight yet, but his presence was already a kind of weather.

"Ruth," Paloma called. "We need names. Not just the twelve. Everything since. The payments."

Ruth went pale in a way that had nothing to do with the morning chill. "You understand what you're asking. Half these people don't know what their families paid. Some of them are going to find out their grandfather traded away—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Their memories. Their children's names off the birth records. Things like that."

"That's why we ask instead of tell," Paloma said. "I read the ledger. It doesn't say what was paid. It just says *paid.* The town has to supply its own truth today." She managed something that was almost a smile. "I'm a novelist, Ruth. I know how this works. You can't put words in people's mouths. They'll only believe what comes out of them."

* * *

They set up at the well itself, because Paloma insisted. A card table borrowed from the Legion hall. The ledger on top, open to the first struck-through name. The bell sat beside it under a quilt, and even muffled, even sleeping, it made the air around the table feel thick, the way air feels before a storm decides.

Paloma stood up on the low stone wall so she could be seen, and the crowd quieted in stages, front rows first, then back, the way a congregation quiets.

"My name is Paloma Galloway," she began. "Most of you knew my brother Palen. Some of you came to his funeral. I want to thank the ones who did, and I want to apologize to all of you for what I'm about to do to your afternoon."

A nervous laugh ran through the crowd. Good. Let them laugh while they still could.

"This book," she said, laying her hand flat on the ledger, "is from 1863. Twelve founding families. And starting in 1863, every year until sixty years ago, this book records a payment made to the well. One name a year, struck through in red. Twins, mostly. Sometimes not. Sixty years ago the payments stopped — or rather, they went underground, into small things, animals, memories — because the full debt was never forgiven and never collected. And now it's come due. All of it. With interest."

She let that sit. Somewhere in the back, a baby started fussing and was hushed.

"The water's rising. You've seen the creek. You've watched your dogs refuse to drink. You've found your children standing at their bedroom windows at night, facing east, toward this place." Heads nodded, reluctantly, all across the crowd, like weeds bending in the same wind. "There is one way to end it, and it requires every name this town has ever taken to be spoken out loud, here, over this water, before moonrise. So." She opened the ledger. "We're going to start with the founders, and then I'm going to ask. And some of you are going to hate me. That's fine. Hate me at the well. At least you'll be here."

She read the first name.

"Gideon Harrow. Taken 1863."

Nothing happened. No thunder, no toll. But the water below — visible now through the old grate, black and slow — seemed to lean toward the sound.

"Ambrose Mott. Taken 1864."

Eleven feet away, Ezra closed his eyes.

She worked through the twelve, and the crowd grew quieter with each one, and by the time she reached Tobias Finch's entry — *taken 1964, payment suspended* — Mrs. Pruett was crying openly into a handkerchief that had clearly been pressed into service many times before this morning.

Then Paloma looked up. "Now I ask. Who owes this well? Speak it yourself, or it doesn't count."

Silence. Long enough that Paloma heard the wind move through the fence wire, a thin whistling note.

Then a man near the back — Hollis Vann, who ran the hardware store and had a face like a closed cash register — stepped forward two steps and stopped, as if his boots had made the decision ahead of his pride.

"Vann family," he said gruffly. "1971. My father paid a... it was supposed to be our dog. But the well took my sister's memory of him instead. She forgot the dog ever lived. Forgot she loved it. She cried for a week and didn't know why." His voice cracked on the last word. "Vann. Say it. Whatever it does."

Paloma spoke the name back to the water. Below the grate, the black surface shivered.

And then it was like opening a gate.

Mrs. Pruett confessed a Finch debt from 1958. The Okafors, hesitant, admitted their uncle had sold "some years of his childhood" to keep the feed store solvent during the drought of '79. A woman Paloma didn't recognize walked all the way from the back row and said, in a voice like paper, "Tuller. My mother gave the well her wedding day. All of it. Every anniversary, she woke up not knowing why she was sad." Confession after confession, each one landing on the water like a stone, each one making the surface tremble and rise — and it *was* rising, Paloma realized with a lurch, visibly rising, the black level climbing the inside of the stones like a tide answering a moon that wasn't due yet.

Some denied, too. Of course they did. Bud Kessler shouted that this was witch nonsense and grief madness and that his family had never owed anybody anything, and Paloma simply looked at him and said, "Kessler isn't a founding name, Bud. Nobody's asking you for anything." He sat down looking strangely deflated, cheated of both guilt and drama.

It took two hours. Paloma's voice wore raw. Ruth kept the list, writing names in the ledger's margins in pencil, and Ezra rang no bell but held his slate up twice with corrections — *WRONG YEAR*, once, and *SAY THE MOTHER'S NAME TOO*, the second time, which opened a whole vein of confessions about mothers that had several grown men blowing their noses.

And through all of it, parked on the rise above the fence road, sat old Mr. Harrow's truck.

He'd arrived sometime during the second hour, unannounced, and simply sat there behind the wheel with the engine off, watching. When Paloma finally paused for water and looked up at him, he smiled. It was not a threatening smile, which somehow made it worse. It was the smile of a man watching a horse he'd bet on finally run the race.

Ruth saw him too. Her hand went to her side, where Paloma knew she wasn't carrying her sidearm anymore — she'd left it at the station, she'd said, because "today I'm not the law, I'm a Harrow, and those are different jobs."

"He's smiling," Ruth said quietly.

"I noticed."

"Why would he smile? We're ending it. We're taking everything his family built."

Paloma watched the old man's mouth move, though no sound reached them. She thought about the payment records in Harrow House, sixty years of them, and her mother's signature, and the shape of a debt that wanted, above all things, to be *acknowledged*.

"Because we're saying the names," she said slowly. "All of them. Out loud. Over the water. Exactly like the ritual wants." A cold thread went down her spine. "Ruth — the rite needs the bell rung at moonrise. But the *reading* — maybe the reading was always part of it too. Maybe we're doing his work for him."

Below the grate, the water rose another inch.

* * *

By late afternoon the crowd had thinned to the stubborn ones — Mrs. Pruett, the Okafors, Hollis Vann, a dozen others who had confessed and now refused to leave, as if walking away might undo their own words. The light had gone long and gold. Someone had started a fire in a barrel. Somebody else had found Mrs. Pruett's deviled eggs, and there was something almost unbearably human about the whole scene: a town eating picnic food around a haunted well, having spent the day confessing to sixty years of buried bargains.

Grim solidarity, Paloma thought. It tasted like deviled eggs and woodsmoke.

Ezra tugged her sleeve. He'd written something new on the slate:

*COUNT.*

She counted the names in the margins. Counted again. Compared against the ledger, against the pages from Palen's journal, against Ruth's memory of the Harrow payment books.

"We're missing one," she said.

Ruth looked up sharply. "Which?"

Paloma ran her finger down the column. Twelve founders, all spoken. The later debts, all spoken. Every name the town could remember giving, accounted for — except the one entry that had started all of this, the one written in fresh ink in a dead boy's hand, at the bottom of a torn-out journal page:

"Palen," she said. "Nobody's said Palen. He was taken. He counts. He has to be spoken at the well like all the others, or the list fails — and if the list fails—"

"—it claims the speaker," Ruth finished. "You."

The barrel fire popped. Out on the rise, Mr. Harrow's headlights flicked on, once, deliberately, like a wink.

And from deep beneath the grate, soft and patient and horribly familiar, the water spoke her brother's cadence:

*"Say it tonight, Paloma. Say it right."*
