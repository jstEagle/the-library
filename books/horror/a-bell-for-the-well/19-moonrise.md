## 19. Moonrise

The bell weighed more than grief.

That was the thought Paloma kept circling as she and Ruth wrestled it up the last stretch of the hill — the founders' bronze, green-black with a century of silt, its clapper still bare where the red thread had been. It weighed more than grief and less than guilt, and between those two measurements there was apparently just enough strength in her arms to get it to the lip of the old well before the moon did.

"Set it on the plank," Ezra had written on his slate that afternoon, when they'd planned this in the yellow kitchen of Verity's house while Verity sat at the table like a woman attending her own trial. "The plank across the mouth. You'll want it steady. Your hands won't be steady."

He'd been right about that. He was right about most things now that he communicated exclusively in chalk dust and furious eyes, which was, Paloma reflected, an improvement over some men she'd known who could talk.

The plank went across. The bell went onto the plank. And then there was nothing left to do but wait for the moon, which was already climbing over the ridge east of town, swollen and orange, the color of something that had been underwater too long.

Below them, Hollow Creek had turned out.

Not all of them. Not even half. But more than Paloma had dared hope when Ruth stood up in the diner at noon and said, in her deputy's voice, flat as a summons: *Well. Moonrise. If you've got names to say for your people, say them tonight or don't say them.* Some had come out of fear. Some out of shame. Old Mrs. Adeyemi stood near the front holding a photograph of a grandmother nobody else remembered. The Pell twins — actual twins, eleven years old, their mother's hands clamped white-knuckled on both their collars — stared at the well with the frank appetite of children at an aquarium. Even a few Harrow farmhands had drifted up from the valley, hats in hand, not meeting anyone's eyes.

And at the back, parked crooked across the cattle guard so no one could leave without scraping him, sat Mr. Harrow's truck, engine off, windows down, the old man himself just a silhouette behind the wheel. Watching. Smiling — she couldn't see the smile, but she could feel it, the way you can feel weather coming through a bad tooth.

"He'll try something," Ruth murmured beside her. She'd come armed, formally against regulation, informally very much within family tradition. "When it starts working. When he sees what it costs."

"I know."

"You don't have to do this alone. I could—"

"You could ring it," Paloma said, "and it would take *your* memories instead of mine, and your father would win twice." She shook her head. "It has to be me. Galloway debt. Galloway payment. That's the whole ugly arithmetic of this town, isn't it? You pay with what's yours."

Ruth didn't answer. She put her hand briefly on Paloma's shoulder — a deputy's grip, brief and hard, meaning more than it said — and stepped back to stand with the crowd.

Paloma looked into the well.

The water had risen. Two days ago it had been forty feet down, a rumor of black. Now it breathed six feet below the rim, warm and slow, and it moved when there was no wind, and once — she made herself look, she owed it that much honesty — once she had seen a face in it, or the idea of a face, the way you see faces in wallpaper. It smelled of iron and pennies and, underneath, faintly, of the creek behind her childhood house in the summer they were nine.

"Moon's close," called Dale Pell, squinting east. "Maybe ten minutes."

Ten minutes. Paloma took the folded pages from her jacket pocket. Her list. Three nights of work with the ledger open under a lamp, cross-checking against church records and Ezra's slate and, in two cases, against the weeping testimony of people who had finally decided that sixty years of silence was heavier than confession.

Twelve founding names, struck through in red in 1863 and never forgiven. Then the debts since: Tobias Finch, 1964. A Mott infant whose grave marker said only *ours*. Two drifters the town had stopped asking about in 1971. A Harrow cousin everyone pretended had gone to California. And Palen.

She read the list until she could recite it with her eyes shut. Then she shut her eyes and recited it, and opened them, and the moon broke the ridge.

* * *

It came up fast, the way harvest moons do, huge and butter-colored, and the moment its lower edge cleared the ridge line every dog in Hollow Creek began to howl at once, one long chord, and then stopped together, as if someone had lifted a needle off a record.

The crowd made a sound like a held breath let go halfway.

Paloma gripped the bell rope — Ezra had insisted on rope, hemp, nothing synthetic, and had written *IT LISTENS THROUGH METAL* on the slate with such force he'd snapped the chalk — and rang the first toll.

The sound was nothing like she expected. Church bells announce. This bell *asked*. One round bronze note that fell outward across the hillside and then, impossibly, fell inward, down into the well, and the well answered with a sound like a great throat swallowing.

And something left her.

She felt it go — a specific, physical subtraction, like a stitch pulled from cloth. She grabbed the plank's edge. The memory arrived whole in the space between heartbeats and departed the same way: the smell of crayons, the green shag rug, Palen at four years old drawing their family as stick figures and giving her three arms because, he explained with four-year-old gravity, she needed one extra for carrying things. Gone. Not dimmed. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone, leaving a smooth place the tongue keeps returning to.

"Oh," she said aloud. Her voice sounded far away. "Oh, that's how it works."

The second name. "Elias Thorne. Founder. Taken 1863."

Toll.

Her tenth birthday — cake with blue frosting, her mother laughing so hard at something Palen said that frosting came out of her nose — pulled loose and poured away down the well like water finding a drain. Below, the black surface shivered, and a voice rose through it. Thin. Many. A congregation of whispers saying *thank you thank you thank you* in overlapping keys, the way rain says everything at once.

Someone in the crowd sobbed. Someone else said a name along with her, unbidden, and Paloma understood: the ledger wasn't hers alone anymore. It belonged to whoever had the courage to speak it.

Third name. Fourth. Toll, toll.

Her mother's face.

It happened at the fifth name — Abigail Galloway, 1958, a debt Paloma hadn't known existed until she found her mother's signature in the Harrow house, dated eleven years before Paloma was born. The bell asked its question, and the memory came up out of her like a hook coming up out of deep water, and for one full second she saw her mother's face again — really saw it, the exact architecture of it, the small scar through the left eyebrow, the way her eyes crinkled asymmetrically when she laughed — and then the bell took it, and Paloma stood on the hillside knowing her mother had loved her and being unable, for the first time since she was six years old, to picture the face that love had lived in.

She heard herself make a sound. An animal sound, small and shocked.

"Don't stop," said Ezra, though he couldn't say it; he wrote it, big, on the slate, and held it up, and his eyes were wet. "DON'T STOP. IT ONLY KEEPS WHAT YOU SURRENDER WILLINGLY."

Willingly. There was the cruelty of it, laid clean. The ritual didn't steal. It *accepted*. Every toll was a door she had to open herself.

"Mercy Gable," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Founder's wife. Taken 1863."

Toll. The summer she learned to ride a bike, gone. The well exhaled, and this time the voices were louder, closer to the surface, and one of them — just one, distinct now from the chorus — said, in a voice she knew better than her own:

"Pally."

Palen.

Relief hit her so hard her knees nearly went. She leaned over the rim. Six feet down, the water had begun to glow faintly, moonlight caught in it and held there, and shapes moved beneath the glow, patient, upright, waiting in rows like a choir.

"Palen," she whispered. "I'm doing it. I'm saying them all. Hold on."

"Hold on," the water agreed, warm and fond, and it was his cadence, exactly his cadence, the little upward lilt he put on repeated words —

But it was wrong. She couldn't have said how, not yet, not fully. Like hearing a recording of yourself: every note correct, the sum somehow false. His lilt was there but it sat on the words like a coat on a hanger.

Sixth name. Toll. Her first day of school, gone. Seventh—

Seventh was Tobias Finch.

"Tobias Finch," Paloma said, and the word *Finch* had barely left her mouth when the well *moved*.

Not rippled. Moved — the whole surface tilting toward her like a pupil dilating, the glow flaring, and every whisper in the chorus cut off at once except one, which rose and rose, no longer thin, no longer many, one voice with the full weight of sixty drowned years behind it, and it said:

"*Say it again.*"

The crowd backed up as one body. Dale Pell grabbed his sons. Somewhere behind her, Mr. Harrow's truck door clicked open.

"Say my name again," the voice said, and it was still wearing Palen's shape, still wearing his warmth, but underneath it now she could hear the other thing, the older thing, hungry and patient and *grateful*, terribly grateful, the way drowning men are grateful. "Sixty years, girl. Sixty years I have been polite. Say it again and mean it."

Paloma's hands shook on the rope. The moon stood directly overhead now, white as bone, and the water below glowed like an eye.

She looked down into it, at the shape rising slowly toward the surface, wearing her brother's face the way the body in the casket had worn it — correctly, completely, and with none of his scars.

"Who are you?" she said.

The water smiled with her brother's mouth.

"My name," it said, "is Tobias Finch. And I believe you have eight more names to say — if you want to reach *his*."

* * *

Behind her, boots on gravel, unhurried. Mr. Harrow's voice, mild as Sunday: "Now, Miss Galloway. Let's talk about stopping while stopping is still an option."
