## 27. Tom Breaks

Tom Ashdown had not slept properly in eleven days, and he had stopped pretending to himself that it was the cold.

He sat at his kitchen table at half past one in the morning with a mug of tea gone cold beside him and the farm ledger open in front of his elbows, not reading it, just needing the weight of something ordinary under his hands. Outside, the wind came down off the moor and rattled the barn doors the way it always did, and for the eleventh night running he told himself it was the wind, only the wind, and for the eleventh night running he did not believe it.

The bell was in the barn.

That was the fact he could not get around. He and Hartwell had poured it themselves, four nights ago now, out at the forge past midnight, and Tom had stood there holding the crucible with his hands wrapped in sacking while the bronze went liquid and *spoke* — seven voices, thin and high, like children singing through water — and he had told himself then, too, that it was the metal settling, the gases escaping, anything, anything at all, and he had not believed that either.

His grandfather had helped melt the old bell in 1911. That was the family sin, the one his father had drunk himself around and his grandmother had whispered about only when the men were out of the room: that it was an Ashdown who had carried the crowbars into the tower, an Ashdown who had swung the first blow, an Ashdown who had taken the broken pieces down on a cart and sold them — or said he'd sold them — and taken the founders' coin, and the village's luck had come off its hinge that winter and never gone back on.

*Every family that left prospered,* Tom's grandmother used to say, counting the names off her fingers like a woman counting losses. *Every family that stayed shrank.* And the Ashdowns had stayed. Six generations of them, each one a little smaller in the churchyard stone, a little earlier in the ground.

And now there was a new bell sitting in his barn, cast from the old fragments, with a surveyor's name-tag melted into the pour, and every night when the wind rattled the doors Tom got up and looked out the window and half expected to see it ringing on its own, swinging on nothing, calling the debt in.

He had told Hartwell he was committed. He had stood in the smoking ruin of his own resolve and said the words — *I'm with you, lad, we'll see it done* — and he had meant them, in the way a man means a prayer he doesn't expect to be answered. But meaning and keeping were different animals, and Tom had spent his whole life learning the distance between them, watching his father promise sobriety at every funeral and break it by every wedding.

Tonight the distance had closed. Tonight Tom was going to end it.

* * *

He told himself it was mercy.

That was the story he built as he pulled on his boots in the dark kitchen, as he took the paraffin can down from the shelf where it had sat since the tractor days: that the bell was a wound and fire was a clean way to close it. That Hartwell was a decent man gone half-hollow from living in that house, a man who taped his own name inside his jacket like a schoolboy, and that what he needed wasn't a rite but a rescue. That Edith Marlowe, ninety-one years old and stubborn as a gatepost, would grieve for a fortnight and then sleep better than she had in sixty years. That the village — what was left of it, forty-one souls in nineteen houses — would be free of a debt it had never agreed to.

That was the story. Tom knew it was a story because he had told better ones to worse purposes, and because underneath it, in the cellar of himself where he kept the things he didn't look at, there was a simpler truth with a simpler shape:

He was afraid.

He was afraid the way his grandfather must have been afraid in 1911, standing in the tower with a crowbar in his hands, looking up at that bronze throat that had spoken for the village for three hundred years. He was afraid of the bell, and of the ledger Hartwell kept now in the house, the old ledger with the seven names ending mid-sentence and the eighth name — *Vesper Hartwell*, written in a hand that grew less like itself every day — and of the fine print Hartwell said was in there, the escape clause, the strike-out, the price. He was afraid of solstice morning, two days off, when the whole village would walk to the churchyard without knowing why, drawn like cattle, and something would be done over their heads that would either unbind them or finish binding them forever.

Fire was simpler. Fire was what a farmer understood. You burned the infected straw, you burned the diseased stock, you burned the wasps' nest out of the eaves, and the ground grew back. Fire did not ask you to believe anything. Fire only asked you to stand back.

He carried the paraffin can across the yard under a sky the color of unwashed wool. Snow lay thin and dirty over the flagstones, and his breath went up in flags, and the barn stood black against the darker field with its doors shuddering in the wind — *rattle, rattle, rattle*, patient as a knock.

"Wind," Tom said aloud, to hear a human voice. "It's only wind."

The doors gave when he shouldered them, and the cold inside was the cold of a held breath.

The bell hung from the beam over the old cart bay, where they'd slung it on a chain to let it cool and then never taken it down, because neither of them had quite wanted to touch it again. It was a small thing — Hartwell had insisted on small, a hand-bell almost, a bell a man could carry up a tower under one arm — and in the dark it was only a shape, a rounded shoulder of dull bronze, and Tom stood under it with the can in his hands and made himself look at it properly, the way you look at a snake before you decide what to do about it.

It did not move. Of course it did not move. It was a bell. Bells did not move unless they were swung, and it was not swinging, and the wind was outside, and everything was ordinary, and he was a sixty-three-year-old man standing in his own barn at two in the morning talking himself into arson, and the shame of that — the plain, human shame of it — nearly turned him around and sent him back to bed.

Nearly.

He thought of his daughter, Sarah, in Australia, who wrote twice a year and whose children he had never met, because the Ashdowns did not leave, the Ashdowns *stayed*, staying was the family trade. He thought of the churchyard and the shrinking stones. He thought of the way the village had emptied over his lifetime, shop after shop going dark down the high street, and how everyone said *times change* and nobody said *we owe something*, and how he himself had said nothing, nothing, nothing, for sixty years.

He uncapped the can.

The paraffin went over the straw bales at the far end first, then in an arc across the floor, then up over the cart — he'd have to write off the cart, that was fine, that was nothing, insurance would — and last, standing on a bale with his arms aching, he poured it over the bell itself, letting it run down the bronze in silver ropes, into the mouth, into the throat, filling the thing that had sung with seven voices until it was drowning in fuel.

Then he climbed down, walked backward to the doors, and took out his matches.

His grandfather's matches, actually — the old tin of them, Swan Vestas, that Tom had kept on the mantelpiece for forty years without once striking, a relic, an heirloom, the only thing of the old man's that had come down to him. He had taken it off the mantelpiece tonight without deciding to. His hands had decided.

"Grandad," Tom said, and his voice cracked in the cold. "You started this. Let's finish it different."

He struck the match.

* * *

The flame stood up straight and did not go toward the paraffin.

Tom had lit fires all his life — stubble fires, bonfires, the forge itself — and he knew how flame moved, how it leaned and crawled and leapt, how it went where the fuel and the wind told it. This flame leaned against the wind, out of the barn, toward the doors, toward *him*, and it did not spread. It stood on the match-head like a small golden animal considering him, and the paraffin-soaked straw ten feet away lay dark and cold as a grave.

Tom struck another match off the box. It lit, leaned the same way, and went out.

A third. The same. A fourth, and the flame bent double, away from the bell, away from the ledger — because that was the other thing, he realized, his stomach dropping through the floor — away from everything they had touched. The rite's things. The bell, and the ledger in Hartwell's coat, and the mold fragment Edith had given him, and Tom's own hands, which had held the crucible, which had poured the metal, which had — he looked at them now, shaking in the matchlight — which had been *in* it, up to the wrists, in the dream he kept having and kept not telling anyone about.

"Right," Tom whispered. "Right. Fine."

He did the thing any sensible man would have done an hour ago and did not do now, because the hour for sense had gone by with the paraffin: he took the paraffin can, walked to the far corner of the barn, well away from the bell and the cart and the straw he'd soaked, and he threw the whole can onto the dry floor there, and he struck a fresh match and threw that after it.

The fire went up like a greeting. A soft, whumping, orange blossom of flame, spreading fast across the dry boards, up the corner post, into the hay loft — the hay loft, God, the *hay*, a whole winter's worth — and Tom stood in the doorway and watched his own barn begin to die and felt, underneath the horror, underneath everything, a terrible clarity.

The fire had chosen. He had asked it to take the rite, and it had taken everything else.

He was running before he'd decided to run, hauling the water barrow from the yard, but he knew as he ran that it was theater, that the barn was gone already, that hay fire in December was a verdict not a negotiation. The heat came out of the doors in a wall and took his eyebrows and the front of his coat, and he went in anyway, because the tack room was on the near side and his father's harness was in it, and his grandfather's tin of matches was still in his pocket, and there was a moment — one long, stupid, blazing moment — when Tom Ashdown stood inside his own burning barn trying to save a tin of matches from a fire that had refused to burn them, and the roof beam came down in a gout of sparks and took him across the shoulder and the back, and the world went white and orange and loud.

He remembered crawling. He remembered the cold air of the yard like a slap. He remembered lying on the flagstones with the snow melting under him and his coat smoking, watching his barn burn with a steadiness that no barn fire had any right to, the flames standing up straight and orderly as a choir, and not one spark — he checked, he made himself check, squinting through streaming eyes — not one spark went near the bell.

The bell hung in the doorway's glow, paraffin-wet, untouched, and as Tom lay there gasping it swung.

Once. A slow, small pendulum arc, no wind inside, no hand on it, and it did not ring — it had no clapper yet, Hartwell had the clapper wrapped in his coat pocket like a relic — it only swung, gently, the way a head turns to look at you.

And Tom, singed, weeping, sixty-three years old and heir to the oldest sin in the village, lay on the frozen ground of his own yard and laughed. It came out of him like something being born, ragged and awful, laughter and grief in the same breath, because he had finally been given an answer he could not argue with. He had spent his whole life hedging, half-committing, standing in doorways, and the fire had looked at him and chosen its side without him.

His family had broken the bell. The fire would not break it back. There was only one direction left, and he had been walking away from it for sixty years.

* * *

Hartwell came down the lane at a dead run with his coat over his pyjamas and the ledger clutched to his chest like a child, and behind him, unbelievably, came Edith Marlowe, wrapped in two shawls, moving with the quick stubborn trot of a woman who had decided that dying in a ditch was preferable to being left out. The glow had woken the lane. By the time the fire brigade's single engine came grinding over from Marcham — forty minutes, it would be forty minutes, it was always forty minutes — half the village was standing in Tom's yard in their nightclothes, watching the barn burn, and nobody said anything, because there was nothing to say that the flames weren't already saying better.

Tom sat on an upturned trough with a blanket over his shoulders and burns dressed in the kitchen towel Edith had insisted on, and he watched Hartwell watch the fire, and he saw the surveyor's face do something complicated — grief for the barn, yes, but underneath it something worse, something like recognition, like a man watching a door close and understanding exactly which door.

"It wouldn't take," Tom said. His voice was gravel. "The bell. The fire wouldn't touch it. I soaked it, lad. I soaked it in paraffin and it sat there like — like a christening." He laughed again, that awful born-thing. "It burned my barn instead. My father's harness. The lot. Everything but the things that matter to it."

Hartwell crouched in front of him. Up close the surveyor looked worse than the fire — grey, thinned, like a photograph left in a window — but his eyes were steady, and there was something in them Tom had never seen in a man from outside the village, something that looked like it had already made its peace and was just waiting for the date.

"I know," Hartwell said. "I tried paper, the first week. The ledger wouldn't burn either. I put it in the grate and it came out cold, and the fire went up the chimney and set the bathroom curtain alight instead." He said it without drama, the way a man reports a measurement. "The house doesn't protect the rite, Tom. The rite protects itself. There's a difference, and I've had a long time to think about what it means."

"What does it mean?"

"It means it wants to be finished. Not prevented. Finished." Hartwell looked at the burning barn, at the small dark shape of the bell in the doorway's glow, swinging its slow patient arc. "Everything we've done to stop it has only cleared the way. Your barn, my curtains — it's tidying up around us."

Tom looked at his own hands, blistered across the knuckles where the beam had caught him, hands that had held the crucible, that had poured the metal, that were — whatever they were now — *in* it. His grandmother's voice came to him across sixty years, counting names on her fingers, every family that left, every family that stayed, and for the first time in his life he heard the end of the sentence she had never said aloud: *and the family that broke it owes the fixing.*

"Solstice is day after tomorrow," Tom said.

"Yes."

"Then we'd best get on." He stood, swaying, and made himself say the rest of it, all of it, out loud, in front of the village, in front of the fire, so it would be on the record the way Hartwell liked to say things were on the record. "I tried to end it tonight and it told me no. I'm done trying to end it. Whatever you need — the forge, the cart, my back, my name if it comes to that — you have it. My family started this in 1911. I'm the last Ashdown, and I'm telling you, lad, we'll see it finished or we'll see me in the churchyard with the rest of the small stones."

Edith Marlowe made a sound — not quite a word, something older — and put her hand, light as a bird, on his burned knuckles.

Hartwell nodded slowly. Then he reached into his coat, and Tom's stomach clenched — but it was only the ledger, and the surveyor opened it on his knee there in the firelight, snow falling into it and not melting on the page, and turned to the last written line, where *Vesper Hartwell* stood in that increasingly foreign hand.

"There's a page in here," Hartwell said quietly, "that I haven't shown anyone. Not even Edith. The terms of the strike-out. What it costs." He looked up, and the firelight moved in his eyes like water. "Before you're all in, Tom — before you give me your name — you should hear what it's going to take. All of it. Because I've read it four times now, and every time I read it, I understand it differently, and tonight —" he glanced at the burning barn, at the bell swinging its slow arc in the doorway, and his voice dropped to almost nothing — "tonight I think the ledger has been reading me back."
