## 32. Ringing

The stairs went on longer than they should have.

Vesper counted them as he climbed, because counting was the last discipline he trusted. Thirty-one steps in the survey he'd taken his first morning. He was at forty-six now and still rising, the new bell heavy against his chest where he'd slung it in a harness of Tom's leather straps, its bronze skin cold through his coat even though the metal had been molten less than two days ago. Behind him, doors slammed in sequence—hall door, pantry door, the cellar hatch—the house closing itself off floor by floor beneath him like a heart squeezing blood upward, trying to keep him, trying to keep all of him.

He did not look back. Looking back was how Albert Crane had been taken; Crane's final entry said so, in a hand that deteriorated letter by letter down the page until the last line was only a smear of ink shaped like a man turning around.

*Forty-nine. Fifty.*

The stairwell window showed him the village below, small and white under solstice snow, chimney smoke standing straight up in the windless cold. They had come without being called. He could see them gathered in the churchyard, dozens of dark shapes among the leaning stones, faces tilted up toward the tower. Old Mrs. Fenwick from the post office. The Hollis brothers, who hadn't spoken to each other in nine years and stood shoulder to shoulder now. Children who should have been asleep. The whole village drawn out of their houses by something none of them would be able to name tomorrow, standing in the snow like congregants waiting for a service that had been cancelled in 1911.

And at the tower door, blocking it with a body that cast no shadow on the snow, stood Mr. Pell.

Vesper looked away from the window before Pell could look up. He wasn't sure whether that mattered. He suspected nothing about Pell mattered except what Pell wanted, and what Pell wanted was in the ledger strapped to Vesper's back beneath the bell, pressed flat against his spine like a second skeleton.

*Fifty-three. Fifty-four.*

The air changed at fifty-five. It was subtle—a thinning, a taste of iron and old paper—and Vesper recognized it because he had walked into it deliberately once, at three in the morning, with his mother's maiden name held in his mouth like a stone. The between-space. The corridor that existed between hours, wallpapered in handwritten names, freshly prepared for him. It had expanded since then. It was here now, bleeding through the stairwell walls, and when he turned the next landing he saw it: a doorway where no doorway had been in any of his surveys, opening onto a corridor lined with that pale, patient wallpaper, names flowing across it in ink-dark cursive like veins seen through skin.

They were standing in it. All seven.

Albert Crane nearest, in his 1923 overcoat, his face smooth as an egg where features used to be—not erased, Vesper understood now, but *offered*, given away so long ago there was nothing left to take. Behind him the others, spread down the corridor's impossible length: men in tweed and gabardine and one in a war-surplus greatcoat, each holding the ghost of an instrument—a chain, a theodolite, a folding rule—held not as tools but as relics. None of them moved toward him. That was the worst of it. They simply stood in their corridor with the terrible courtesy of hosts, and the one farthest back, the one whose initials matched the alcove prepared at the corridor's end, opened his mouth and said, without sound, without breath:

*It doesn't hurt after the name goes.*

"I know," Vesper said aloud, and kept climbing.

That was the whole temptation, distilled. He had felt it himself in the corridor, the seductive looseness of a self unfastened from its own weight. The founders had built this thing out of fear of ending—he'd read it in the diary, heard it in the stones—and the house offered permanence the way drowning offers rest. His name for theirs. His erosion for their endurance. A fair trade, if you never had to remember what you'd paid.

But Edith had remembered her sister's name for sixty years. Sixty years of penance, carried alone, and she had handed it to him two nights ago like something breakable wrapped in cloth.

*Constance Marlowe.* He held it now behind his teeth, warm.

*Sixty-one. Sixty-two.*

The belfry opened above him all at once, and the sky came with it.

He had expected the louvred gloom of the old photographs, the boarded frame where the bell had hung before the founders' crime. Instead the roof was gone—or open, or *unmade*—and the winter solstice dawn poured down into the tower like water into a cup. The sun hung low and swollen and red above the eastern hills, too large, too near, staining the snow-clouds the color of old brick. Frost furred every stone. And in the center of the belfry floor, exactly where the original bell had hung, the oak beam still bore its iron staples, empty, waiting, patient as everything else in Hollowmere House.

Vesper set down the bell.

It made a sound when it touched the boards—a single soft note, bronze on wood—and the note did not fade the way notes fade. It sank. He felt it go down through the tower, down through the foundations, down into whatever the house stood on besides earth, and somewhere below he heard the cellar archive answer: pages turning, hundreds of pages, the ledger's vast family shuffling themselves in anticipation.

His hands were shaking. He noticed them shaking with a strange detachment, the way he'd learned to notice everything about himself lately—as data, as observation, as documentation. *Subject's hands tremble. Subject's breath visible. Subject's name illegible on all documents but one.* He took out the notebook. On the inside cover, in his own handwriting, which had stopped feeling like his own sometime around chapter eleven of this ordeal, was written:

*Vesper James Hartwell. Son of Anne Hartwell, née Calloway. Born March 3rd. I am the man writing this.*

He read it twice. Some of the words had begun to look like words in a foreign language he'd once been fluent in. But the shape of it held. The shape of it was enough.

Below him, the house slammed its last door.

Then silence—total, pressure-bearing silence, the silence of a held breath sixty years long—and Vesper knelt beside the recast bell and put his hands on it and began to speak the founder's words in the old dialect Edith had taught him, syllable by syllable, over tea, her voice cracking on the vowels her grandmother had used.

"*Ic thisne bell ringe for þa covenante. Ic nemne min nama on þa bocan. Gehyr hit, huse. Gehyr hit, hirede.*"

*I ring this bell for the covenant. I name my name in the book. Hear it, house. Hear it, heirs.*

The dialect tasted like cold iron and older winters. As he spoke, frost bloomed up the walls in fern-patterns, and the red light thickened, and the seven surveyors in the corridor below came to the foot of the belfry stair and stood there, looking up, their hollow faces lifted like men listening for rain.

"*Ic nemne hire þe ær me þolode—Constance Marlowe—þe naman næfre forgietene.*"

*I name her who suffered before me—Constance Marlowe—her name never forgotten.*

The name went out of him like a bird leaving a cage.

For sixty years that name had lived in Edith alone, a coal she'd carried in her bare hands, and now it passed into the air of the belfry and the air *took* it—Vesper saw it happen, saw the syllables become briefly luminous in the red light, a thread of gold hanging in the frozen air—and the whole tower inhaled. The staples on the oak beam sang. The frost patterns crawled. Somewhere beneath the village, beneath the churchyard where the living stood unknowing, something that had been interrupted mid-word in 1911 leaned forward to hear its sentence finished.

Vesper took up the rope Tom had braided from the bell's casting—bronze wire threaded through hemp, still smelling of the forge—and looped it over the beam, and hauled the bell up until it hung where it had always meant to hang, turning slowly, catching the red sun along its new seam. In that seam, mixed into the pour, was the metal of his name-tag, the paper talisman he'd worn inside his jacket until the house taught him better and he'd cut the tag into strips and fed it to Tom's crucible. His name was in the bell. That had been the point. An heir must be named, and the naming must be *in* the instrument, and so he had signed himself into the bronze the way the founders had signed themselves into the covenant a century and a half ago.

The bell turned. The clapper—forged from the 1911 fragments' largest piece, the crown of the original, hidden all these years in the belfry wall—swung gently against the inner skin, tapping, tapping, like a finger testing a door.

"Constance Marlowe," Vesper said again, in plain English now, because some things needed saying plainly. "Your sister kept your place. Your house is ending its hunger today."

He took hold of the rope.

Far below, faint through the tower stones, he heard Pell's voice—smooth, reasonable, unhurried—saying something to the crowd at the door. He didn't listen. Listening to Pell was how contracts got signed. Instead he thought of Edith at her kitchen table, ninety-one years old, writing his life into her parallel record in her careful schoolteacher's hand: *Vesper Hartwell is afraid of dogs since childhood. Vesper Hartwell takes his tea too sweet. Vesper Hartwell cries without knowing why, and it is grief, and it has an object, and I will tell him what it is when this is done.* Someone was keeping him. Someone had refused to let the house be his only biographer. Whatever the toll cost him, there was a copy of him safe in a woman's ledger, and love, it turned out, was also a kind of documentation.

He pulled.

The first toll was not a sound. It was a *decision*.

The bronze spoke, and the note that came out was the note the village had been hearing at dusk since 1911—the phantom toll, the debt-toll—but complete now, embodied, wearing its true voice instead of haunting through the air on borrowed ones. The sound struck Vesper in the chest and kept going through him, out through the frost-furred walls, down through the village, and the snow on the churchyard roofs slid all at once in a hundred white sighs.

And the between-corridor tore open.

Not widened—*tore*, like fabric, like scar tissue, like a page ripped from a binding. Vesper saw it from the belfry as the second toll rolled out: the air above the village splitting along a seam only he seemed able to see, the pale corridor peeling up out of the world's ordinary space and hanging there against the red dawn, impossibly vertical, its wallpapered length exposed to the open sky, names streaming off its surface like embers off a burning book. The seven surveyors stood at its threshold. The toll reached them.

Albert Crane stepped into the sound first.

There was no fear in it—that was what undid Vesper, watching, hauling the rope for the third toll with his arms burning and tears freezing on his face. There was *relief*. Crane stepped forward into the bell-note the way a man steps into a bath he has waited decades for, and the sound closed over him, and he came apart—not violently, not gruesomely, but the way smoke comes apart when a window opens: unraveling, thinning, becoming movement rather than matter, his smooth face the last part of him to go, and in the instant before it went, Vesper would have sworn on any ledger in the world that the face smiled.

The others followed. One by one, toll by toll, the surveyors of Hollowmere walked into the ringing and let it carry them apart—1923, 1931, 1948, the war-surplus coat, the folding rule dropping last of all, tumbling end over end down through the torn air until it faded into the snow. Each dissolution loosened something in the house. Vesper felt it through the rope: each toll lighter than the last, the tower's stones giving up a tension they had held so long it had become architecture. Doors that had slammed for him now stood open on darkness that was only darkness. The wallpaper-names in the dying corridor were running, blurring, washing out like ink in rain.

*Gehyr hit, huse,* he rang. *Hear it, house.*

*Gehyr hit, hirede. Hear it, heirs.*

The corridor was nearly empty now, its far end dissolving into ordinary blue morning, its prepared alcove with his initials crumbling into plaster dust. Only the last surveyor remained—the oldest, the first, the one from 1911 who had watched the bell broken and written *not metal but covenant* in a diary that cost him everything—and he lingered at the threshold, and turned, and looked up at Vesper with the ruin of a human face, and mouthed something.

Vesper, hauling the ninth toll, read his lips.

*Thank you for finishing the sentence.*

Then the sound took him too, and the corridor folded shut like a fan, and the sky over Hollowmere was only sky—vast, cold, streaked with the red of the low sun—and the bell rang on in Vesper's hands, lighter now, almost joyful, a small bronze heart beating in an empty tower.

He kept ringing. He didn't know for how long. Long enough that his palms blistered. Long enough that the sun climbed clear of the hills and turned from red to white. Long enough that, far below, the villagers in the churchyard began—one by one, without deciding to—to sing, some old carol none of them had heard since childhood, their breath steaming in the cold, their voices ragged and true.

And it was in the middle of all that light and loosening, with his arms half-hallucinated with fatigue and the ledger warm against his spine, that Veper heard the stairwell door open behind him, and turned, and saw Mr. Pell climbing the last steps into the belfry with the sale contract held out before him like a communion plate, smiling his smooth unhurried smile, and saying:

"You've left one page unsigned, Mr. Hartwell. Let's not leave the estate in limbo."

The bell was still ringing. Vesper's hands were still on the rope. And the ledger, he realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the weather, was no longer against his spine.

Pell was holding it too.
