## 3. One Second Late

The first night shift at Hollowmere House began the way all my shifts begin: with a census.

Ten doors between my room and Edith Vane's. I counted them twice, once going, once coming back for the thermometer I'd forgotten, and got ten both times. I noted it in the margin of my chart — *doors: 10, verified* — because that's what I do. People think rituals are superstition. Mine are bookkeeping. The world keeps accounts whether you watch or not; I just prefer to be holding the ledger when it balances.

Cora had shown me the night routine that afternoon with the brisk efficiency of a woman who wanted the tour over with. Kitchen here, linen closet there, call bell by the bed — though Edith's was disconnected, Cora said, because "she pulls it in her sleep and it wakes the whole house." A small cruelty dressed as a convenience. I made a note to have it reconnected and then remembered whose house this was, and unmade the note. You learn early in hospice work which battles belong to you and which ones you're only borrowing grief from.

"Ring your own bell if anything's wrong," Cora said at the door of my room. She said it lightly, but her eyes did something complicated on the word *wrong*, like a hand slipping on a stair. "The landline's in the study. Mobile signal is — well. Hollowmere sits in a pocket."

"A pocket," I repeated.

"Of poor reception." She smiled. It reached her mouth and stopped there, like a guest who wouldn't come past the hall. "Goodnight, Miss Pemberton. Edith sleeps best if you don't hover. She'll tell you the same, less politely."

Then she was gone down the corridor, and I heard a door close somewhere below — one door, I counted, out of habit — and the house settled around me like an animal folding its legs.

I've worked enough big old houses to know their nighttime grammar. They creak in sequence, not at random: joists cooling, pipes contracting, the bones of the place doing their slow arithmetic. Hollowmere spoke this language fluently. What unsettled me wasn't the noise. It was the pauses. Between each sound there was a beat too long, as if the house were waiting to see whether anyone would answer before continuing.

I told myself it was the quality of the silence. Country silence has more texture than town silence. That's all.

At eleven I went in to check Edith.

She lay small under the covers, hands folded on her breastbone like a woman posing for a portrait she disapproved of. Her breathing was the particular rhythm I'd learned to read across twenty years of dying: not labored, not easy, just *spaced*, as though each breath required a brief negotiation. Her chart said congestive heart failure, mild dementia, episodes of nocturnal confusion. Her chart said nothing about the way her eyes had found me at intake that morning and held on, sharp and appalled, as if I were a bill arriving at the wrong address.

"Mrs. Vane," I said softly. "It's Lena. Just checking your pulse."

Her eyes opened. In the low lamplight they were pale as weak tea, and they went first to the window behind me, then to me, then back to the window, as though confirming something.

"You're the new one," she said.

"I'm the new one."

"They always send someone." Her fingers twitched under mine. "Counting, are you? You will be. You can't help it. It's in the wrists."

"I don't follow, Mrs. Vane."

"No," she agreed, and closed her eyes. "You wouldn't yet."

I sat with her until her breathing evened into sleep, which took eleven minutes, and I know it took eleven minutes because I timed it against the second hand of my watch, because that is also what I do. Then I made my rounds.

The hallway outside Edith's room runs the length of the east wing, panelled in dark oak gone amber with age, and hung with three mirrors. The largest stands opposite the landing — a tall Victorian thing in a frame of carved leaves, the glass slightly foxed at the edges, the kind of mirror that makes every reflection look like a photograph of someone who died disappointed. Housekeepers love these mirrors. Nurses learn to ignore them. You spend so many hours watching faces do their final work that your own face in a dark hallway becomes just another patient you can't help.

I noticed it on my third pass.

I blinked, and my reflection didn't.

Not immediately. There was a lag — a fraction, no more than the time it takes to say *and*. My lids came down, came up, and then the woman in the glass followed, a beat behind, like a singer who'd lost the tempo and was trying to find it again without admitting it.

I stopped walking.

The hallway was dim, one wall sconce burning low, and I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the only instrument I trust: I did what any competent professional does when equipment malfunctions. I ran diagnostics.

First hypothesis: fatigue. I'd driven four hours that afternoon, and the eye does strange things in long corridors at night — peripheral flicker, microsleeps, the brain editing footage it hasn't fully processed. Plausible. Probable, even. I stood still and blinked again, deliberately, five times, counting.

One. My reflection blinked with me.
Two. With me.
Three. With me.
Four — late. A hair's width late, the kind of delay you could blame on a trick of the sconce light.
Five. Late. Clearly, measurably late. I raised my right hand.

The woman in the mirror kept hers down.

I held my hand up and watched her, and the sensation that moved through me wasn't fear, not yet. It was the specific vertigo of a nurse who takes a pulse and gets a number that cannot be right — 38 in a warm, conscious patient — and looks at the monitor, and looks at the patient, and knows that one of the two machines is lying, and dreads finding out which.

I lowered my hand. She raised hers.

I walked two steps forward. She walked two steps forward, arriving after I'd already arrived, so that we were briefly out of phase, two women occupying the same corridor on a slight offset, like film run a frame behind its soundtrack.

"All right," I said aloud, because saying things aloud is how I put them back in their boxes. "All right. Old glass. Warped glass. Light refraction. There's a physics explanation and I'm tired and I will find it in the morning."

My reflection said it after me. Her mouth moved on the delay now, visibly, and I watched my own face speak words I'd already finished saying, and something in my chest — some load-bearing beam of common sense — shifted a quarter inch.

I am not a fanciful woman. I want that on the record. I count doors and time pulses and chart everything because the world is full of people who feel their way to conclusions, and feelings are weather, and weather kills patients. Patterns keep people alive. I have built my entire self on the load-bearing truth that things can be measured, and measured things can be managed.

So I measured it.

I used my watch. I faced the mirror, waited for the second hand to reach twelve, and blinked hard, once, watching both the dial and the glass in the same field of view. The second hand swept. My lids fell and rose. And the woman in the mirror — my face, my tired eyes, my mouth — blinked while the hand traveled from the twelve to somewhere just past the one.

A second. Give or take. One full second of lag, in a mirror hanging flat on a wall, reflecting a hallway six feet wide.

There is no physics explanation for that. I knew it standing there, and I stood there anyway for a long moment, telling myself the sconce was failing, the glass was warped, my watch was slow, my brain was editing — running through the whole apologetics of exhaustion — while underneath it all a quieter voice, the one that never raises itself, said: *you know exactly what you saw.*

Here is what I have never told anyone, and what I told the mirror, uselessly, in a whisper:

Every patient I have ever nursed through the final hours has counted backwards. Not all of them aloud. Some move their lips. Some tap it out with a finger against the blanket, thirty, twenty-nine, twenty-eight, and die at zero with the regularity of a clock striking. Twenty-two years. Forty-one deaths. Every single one. I mentioned it once, early, to a supervisor, in careful clinical language, and watched her face perform the small polite arithmetic of deciding I was unstable, and I never mentioned it again. I built my life on top of that silence the way you build a house on a filled-in well. Solid, if you never dig.

And standing in that hallway, watching my reflection arrive late to its own body, I felt the well cover shift under my feet.

Because the dying count backwards. And the glass was falling behind. And both of those facts felt, in the wordless way of knowledge that arrives ahead of understanding, like the same fact.

"Miss Pemberton?"

I turned. Edith stood in her doorway in her nightgown, one hand braced on the frame, and she was looking not at me but past me, at the mirror, with an expression I recognized from forty-one deathbeds: the look of someone greeting an acquaintance they'd hoped never to see again.

"You should be asleep," I said, crossing to her. Her arm under my hand was cold as cellar stone. "Come back to bed, Mrs. Vane. You'll catch a chill."

"The glass is catching up," she said.

I guided her gently around, away from the mirror. She let herself be turned, but her head came back to it, the way a sunflower comes back to the light.

"It's been behind since the day they buried it," she said. "A hundred years of being late. It hates it. It's catching up, Miss Pemberton. Second by second. And when it catches up—" Her breath caught, that spaced negotiation faltering, and I steered her to the bed and sat her down and pressed the pulse oximeter onto her finger because instruments are how I pray.

Ninety-four percent. Heart rate fifty-eight, steady. Textbook. The machine lied serenely.

"When it catches up," I prompted, keeping my voice level, "what happens?"

Edith looked at me then, and her tea-pale eyes were perfectly clear, clearer than they'd been all day, and she smiled with terrible kindness.

"You'll hear it finish counting," she said. "We all will."

She lay back. Within minutes she was asleep, and within minutes after that — I timed it, I always time it — her lips began to move.

I leaned close. I told myself I was checking airway, checking breathing, performing assessments. But I heard it, faint as frost forming, under her breath, in the rhythm I have heard at forty-one deathbeds and never once spoken of aloud:

"...thirty... twenty-nine... twenty-eight..."

Not from a number too large tonight. From thirty. Like everyone. Like the beginning of the pattern, the first bead on the string.

I checked my watch against the hallway mirror on my way back to my room. Raised my hand. Counted the sweep.

The lag was still one second.

But as I stood there, hand up, heart hammering its own small countdown against my ribs, the woman in the glass — my face, my tired eyes — looked at me a beat after I'd already looked away.

And smiled.

I hadn't smiled.

I got back to my room, counted the doors twice — ten, still ten, thank God, still ten — and wrote in the chart, in my neatest clinical hand: *Patient ambulatory during night, mildly confused. No acute distress.* Below it, where no supervisor would ever read it, I added the only true sentence of the night:

*The mirror is one second late.*

Then I sat up with the lamp on until dawn, listening to Edith count in her sleep, and telling myself the thing I have told myself for twenty-two years, the sentence my whole life is balanced on:

*Patterns can be managed.*

Somewhere below me, in the dark of the sealed cellar, I thought I heard — very faintly, very patiently, like something turning over in its sleep — a bell begin to ring.

* * *

Morning came grey and thin. I made my report to Cora over coffee she didn't drink, and she listened with her hands folded and her face arranged into concern, and when I mentioned — carefully, clinically, a nurse raising a routine observation — that Mrs. Vane had been restless, speaking in her sleep, counting, Cora's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

"Counting," she said.

"Backwards. It's common in end-stage patients. Disorientation of time—"

"What number?" The question came out fast and flat, stripped of all its manners.

I hesitated. "Thirty. Downward."

Cora set the cup down without drinking. For a moment something moved behind her face — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Recognition, wearing fear's coat. "Old family habit," she said. "The Vanes count. Edith's done it all her life. Nothing to worry yourself over, Miss Pemberton. You're here for her heart, not her hobbies."

She left the room quickly after that, and I heard her pause in the hallway — I counted her footsteps, out of habit — and I heard her stop, dead, in front of the tall mirror by the landing.

Through the doorway I saw her raise her hand toward the glass.

And I saw her stand there a long moment, hand raised, staring, before she snatched it back and walked on fast, heels cracking against the boards like small gunshots.

She hadn't waited for the reflection.

She already knew how long it took.
