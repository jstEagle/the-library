## 5. What the Frost Draws

I have worked enough night shifts to know the exact quality of a house at four in the morning, when the heating gives up its argument and the dark stops pretending to be empty. Hollowmere at four in the morning had its own particular flavor, and the flavor was *watched*.

I'd slept badly, which was fine, because sleeping badly was a skill I'd been honing since nursing school. I made tea in the kitchen — the kettle took its sweet time, everything in this house took its sweet time, the house moved at the pace of a funeral procession it was enjoying — and I carried the mug upstairs to check on Edith before dawn.

That's when I saw the windows.

Frost had come in the night. Not the usual kind, not the lazy fern-work that creeps up the corners of panes in old houses. This was deliberate. Every window on the landing — all six of them — wore the same pattern, and I mean *the same pattern*, identical down to the last feathered branch, like six copies made from one negative. Frost doesn't do that. Frost is a drunk artist; it never repeats itself. It certainly never repeats itself *exactly*, on six panes of glass of different sizes, at different exposures, on different sides of the house. The east windows should have been thicker with it than the west ones. They weren't. They were twins. Sextuplets.

I stood there with my tea going cold and looked at the pattern properly.

It looked like a feather. Or a fern. Or — and this was the thought that arrived with the pleasant, weightless sensation of a stair missing where my foot expected one — it looked like the thing Edith had been drawing.

* * *

Let me back up. Two nights ago, Edith had spiked a fever that came and went like a guest who couldn't decide whether to stay. I'd charted it, medicated it, done everything the care plan said, and through all of it she'd kept trying to draw. I'd given her the sketchpad from her bedside drawer mostly to keep her hands busy, and she'd filled four pages with the same shape over and over — a branching, feathered thing, drawn with a shaking hand but drawn *confidently*, the way you draw something you've known your whole life rather than something you're looking at.

I'd assumed dementia. I'd assumed the repetition was the repetition you see in every advanced case, the mind circling the same drain. I'd filed it under *symptoms* and moved on, because filing things under symptoms is what I do instead of feeling things.

Now the frost was drawing it too, on every window, and frost doesn't have dementia.

I went and got the sketchpad. I held it up next to the landing window, and I'm not going to pretend my hand was steady, because it wasn't, and because lying to myself about the state of my hands seemed suddenly like a luxury I couldn't afford.

The match wasn't approximate. It wasn't *similar*. It was the same drawing, branch for branch, barb for barb, down to a small asymmetry in the lower-left frond where Edith's pen had skipped. The frost had even reproduced the skip. The frost had reproduced a *pen skip*, which is the kind of detail that should be impossible, and was impossible, and was nevertheless right there in the ice, rendered in crystals fine as salt.

"Okay," I said, to the window, because I'm the sort of person who talks to windows now apparently. "Okay."

I did what I always do when the world stops making sense. I made a chart.

* * *

By seven I had a system. I photographed every pane with my phone — the photos came out strangely, the pattern overexposed against the glass like it was giving off a faint light of its own, but the shapes were clear. I measured three of the patterns with a ruler I found in the kitchen drawer, and they were identical to within a millimetre. I wrote it all down in my notebook, in my tidy clinical handwriting, because that's my whole personality: when the abyss gazes back, the abyss gets a chart.

Here is what I know about myself, and it took Hollowmere about four days to teach me: I trust patterns over people. People lie, people deflect, people say *she's fine, she's just tired* in a voice that means *stop asking*. But a pattern — a pattern is honest. A pattern is something that will still be true tomorrow, that will hold still long enough to be understood. My whole career is patterns. Pulse, pressure, respiratory rate. The dying count backwards from thirty and die at zero, and I have never told a living soul, because telling someone would make it a story, and stories can be doubted, and I cannot survive doubt. But a chart — a chart just *is*.

So: chart. The frost pattern appeared on six panes, identical. Edith's fever sketches, four pages, same pattern, same asymmetry. Correlation noted. Cause: unknown. Recommended action: unknown. Prognosis: I genuinely did not want to think about it.

I checked on Edith. She was sleeping, breath rasping but even, and her hands were curled loosely on the coverlet like they were still holding the pen. On the pad, a fifth page had appeared. I want to be careful with that sentence, because I've turned it over all morning looking for a reasonable explanation and I can't find one. I had put the pad on the dresser, closed. The pen was in the drawer. At some point between seven and seven-forty, the pad had opened itself to a new page and acquired a fifth drawing, in Edith's shaky hand, though Edith had not left her bed.

The fifth drawing was different from the first four. The first four had been the feather alone. The fifth showed the feather, and beneath it, a horizontal line. And beneath the line, small and careful, a word: *two.*

I looked at the word for a long time.

Then I went and stood in front of the hallway mirror, because I already knew what I was going to find, and I needed to find it on purpose. There's a difference between dread that ambushes you and dread you schedule. The scheduled kind you can survive.

I raised my right hand. My reflection raised its right hand.

One.

Two.

Two full seconds. I counted it out loud, *one-Mississippi, two-Mississippi*, the way you time contractions or CPR compressions, the way you time anything that matters. Last night the lag had been one second. The night before, it had been a blink's worth, small enough to blame on tiredness, on the light, on my own eyes. I had blamed it very hard and very sincerely. Now it was two seconds, and the mirror held my raised hand in the air after I'd lowered mine, a ghost of a gesture, a photograph developing out of silvered glass.

I tried it three more times. Two seconds, every time. Consistent. Reproducible. *Measurable.*

And that — I want to explain this properly, because it matters — that was the worst part. Not the lag itself. The *consistency*. If the mirror had been erratic, if the reflection had danced and gibbered, I could have called it madness and been scared in the ordinary human way, the way you're scared of a dog off its lead. But this was behaving like a vital sign. It was changing at a *rate*. One second, then two. Something in this house was counting, and it was counting *up* while everything else in my life counted down, and it was doing so at a pace steady enough to plot on graph paper.

You cannot chart your way out of a thing that is itself charting.

* * *

Cora found me in the hallway at half seven, dressed already in her riding clothes, looking like a woman who had slept eight hours on a bed of money. She had that effect. Cora Vane's composure was the kind you could store grain in.

"You're up early," she said.

"The frost," I said. I'd decided on the walk from the mirror to be direct, or as direct as I ever am, which is direct the way a scalpel is direct — precise, but held at a careful angle. "It's the same on every window. Identical. That doesn't happen naturally."

Something crossed her face. Not surprise. *Recognition*, quickly folded away, like a letter slipped back into its envelope. "Old houses have strange frost," she said. "The glazing's uneven. It makes patterns."

"The same pattern on six panes. Including one that matches a drawing my patient made in a fever."

"Edith has always drawn." Cora's voice stayed level, pleasant, and that was the tell. Level is effort. Level is a hand pressed flat on a lid. "She was an art teacher, before she married in. Birds, mostly. Feathers and such. It's an old habit."

"She drew it in her sleep, Mrs. Vane. On a page that was closed. With a pen that was in a drawer."

For a moment — half a moment, less — Cora looked at me with an expression I couldn't file under anything. Not anger. Something closer to *measurement*. The way I look at a chart when the numbers have started moving in a direction I don't like. As if I were the symptom.

"Mrs. Pemberton," she said, and her voice was kind, which was worse, "you were hired to keep my mother-in-law comfortable. You're doing that beautifully. The house is old and the house is strange, and I know it takes some getting used to. But I'd ask you not to trouble Edith with talk of drawings and frost. She frightens easily now, under the confusion. It's not kind to her."

And there it was again, that sharpness dressed as solicitude, the same too-smooth deflection from the day she'd hired me. I'd noticed it before and let it go, because employers are allowed their evasions and I am not a detective, I'm a nurse. But it was accumulating now, Cora's smoothness, along with the sealed cellar hatch and the bell tower with no rope and the mirrors running two seconds behind the world, and I was starting to understand that I wasn't looking at separate oddities. I was looking at a pattern. And patterns, as established, are the only things I trust.

"Of course," I said. "For Edith's comfort."

"Thank you." She smiled, and it reached her eyes the way frost reaches a window — from the outside in. She turned toward the stairs, then paused. "The mirrors in the east corridor stick, by the way. Old glass. The silvering's failing. If they seem slow, that's all it is."

I hadn't mentioned the mirrors.

I want to be very clear about that, because my memory is good — it has to be, it's the only tool I've got that's never lied to me. I had not said one word about mirrors. I had said *frost* and *drawings*. And Cora Vane, unprompted, in the middle of a conversation about frost, had told me that if the mirrors seemed *slow*, it was only the silvering.

*Slow.* Not warped. Not spotted. *Slow.*

I stood in the hallway until I heard the front door close and the car go, and then I went back to my notebook and wrote it down, because if I didn't write it down I'd start telling myself I'd imagined the emphasis, and I am done telling myself things.

* * *

The rest of the morning, I did what nurses do, which is work. Edith woke at ten, lucid in the thin, wintry way she got for an hour or so each day, and I fed her porridge and gave her her pills and took her blood pressure, and all of it was normal, blessedly, boringly normal, and I clung to the boring like a drowning woman clings to a door.

"Your windows are frosted," she said, apropos of nothing, in her papery voice. "All the same. Like a flock."

"You can see them from here?"

"I don't need to see them." She looked at me then, and her eyes were clear, terribly clear, the clarity you get in the dying when the fog parts and something underneath looks out. "You've noticed the glass is catching up. Haven't you."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it. "It's two seconds now."

"I know." She said it the way you'd say *I know it's raining*. "It was catching up when I was a girl, too. It caught up all through my life, slow as a clock no one winds. And now it's catching up again, and I'm too old to run, and you—" she stopped. Her hand found mine on the blanket, cold as the windows. "You're not old at all. That's the trouble."

"Edith, what is it catching up *to*?"

She closed her eyes. For a moment I thought she'd gone back under, back to the fog, and I confess I felt relief, ugly and immediate. But then she said, barely a breath: "Don't count with it. Whatever you do, however it asks — don't count along."

"Count along with what?"

But she was gone again, back into the murk, her hand slack in mine, and when she woke properly at noon she looked at the sketchpad on the dresser and asked me, in genuine puzzlement, who had been drawing feathers.

* * *

I spent the afternoon being a professional. Medications, notes, a call to her GP that went to a receptionist who promised someone would ring back. Lunch I forgot and ate standing up at four. And all the while the house ticked along around me, and I ticked with it, and every so often I caught myself standing in front of a mirror without having decided to.

At dusk the frost came back.

It came back thicker, and it came back *changed*. The feather was still there on every pane — same pattern, same skip in the lower-left frond, faithful as a signature — but beneath it now, drawn small in the corner of each window, was a new mark. A horizontal line. And beneath the line, in crystals so fine I had to breathe on the glass to make them bloom:

*two.*

The same as the page. The same word, the same figure, in Edith's hand on paper and in frost on glass, and I stood in Edith's room with my breath fogging and clearing, fogging and clearing, and I did the thing I do. I thought about it like a chart.

One second, two nights ago. Two seconds last night. The word *two* appearing under the line. If the lag was a measurement, and the measurements were increasing, and the frost was reporting the measurement —

Then the frost wasn't haunting the house. The frost was *charting* it. Something in this house was keeping notes, the same as me, and its notes were better than mine, and its handwriting was on every window in the building, and it had chosen the one number I already knew how to read.

The dying count backwards. I have sat with forty-one of them, and every one of them counted, down from thirty, to zero, and I have never told a soul. I know a countdown when I'm standing in one.

The difference — the thing that made my hands shake as I wrote it in the notebook, in my tidy clinical hand, underlining it twice — is that countdowns end. They end at zero, and then my patients die, and that has always been the whole of it, a private arithmetic between me and the dying. But this count was going *up*. Two seconds, and growing. And a thing that counts up toward something is not counting down to an end.

It's counting up to an *answer*.

I looked at the window. My breath bloomed against the cold glass, and faded, and for a moment — I'd swear this in any court, though I'd never be fool enough to testify — the frost pattern seemed to shift, the feathered branches flexing like something breathing under cloth.

Behind me, in the darkening room, Edith began to murmur in her sleep. Numbers. Too low to catch.

I turned, and caught the hallway mirror through the open door — my own reflection standing there, watching me, hand already raised.

Mine wasn't. Not yet.

Two seconds later, it was.
