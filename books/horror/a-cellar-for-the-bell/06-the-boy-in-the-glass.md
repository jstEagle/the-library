## 6. The Boy in the Glass

I want to say the third night was when I stopped sleeping, but that would be a lie of the flattering kind. I'd stopped sleeping properly back at Alderwood, somewhere around patient number forty and the realization that my rituals weren't quirks anymore, they were load-bearing walls. You don't stop sleeping because of a haunted house. You stop sleeping because you've always been the kind of person who lies awake doing arithmetic on the dark.

But the third night was when I saw him.

* * *

It was two in the morning, give or take a minute I didn't trust anymore, and I was doing my rounds with the flashlight off because Hollowmere's night-lights had a way of dimming when I looked at them directly, like shy animals. The corridor outside Edith's room runs the length of the east wing — thirty feet of dark wood floor, wood-panelled walls, and that tall Victorian mirror halfway down that some previous Vane had clearly bought to make the hallway feel longer and had instead made it feel like a throat.

I'd been giving the mirror a wide berth for two nights. Professional courtesy. But there's only so long you can avoid a piece of furniture in your own workplace before it starts winning, so that night I planted myself in front of it and did what I do best: I took its vital signs.

The lag was still there. Two seconds now, steady as a pulse. I raised my hand; the woman in the glass raised hers a beat behind, like someone dancing half a step off the music. I blinked; she blinked late. I said, out loud, "You're fired," and watched her mouth move through the words after mine had finished, a silent delayed broadcast of my own voice coming back wrong.

Here's the thing about me: I have never once been comforted by being right. Being right is just the moment the dread stops being hypothetical and starts being scheduled.

So I stood there, cataloguing. And that's when I noticed that the reflection of the corridor behind me had a boy in it.

He stood maybe ten feet back, near Edith's door. Small — six, seven years old, in clothes that belonged in a photograph rather than a hallway: a collar buttoned to the chin, short trousers, boots polished by somebody who loved him. Dark hair, cut neat. He wasn't doing anything. He was just standing, hands at his sides, looking at the back of my reflected head with an expression I recognized from work — the expression of a child waiting outside a door while adults decide something about him.

I turned around.

Empty corridor. Night-light humming. Edith's door shut, exactly as I'd left it. The air held nothing but dust and lavender polish and my own heartbeat doing something unprofessional.

I turned back to the mirror.

He was still there. Still ten feet behind my reflection. Still waiting. And here is the detail that rearranged something in my chest: he hadn't moved either. When I turned, he should have turned with the room — the whole image should have swung. Instead it was as if he existed only from the mirror's point of view, a resident of the glass who had simply never bothered to learn about the world on this side of it.

"Okay," I said to him, because I talk to patients who can't answer all the time, and it seemed rude to make an exception just because this one was dead. Probably dead. Statistically dead. "You're going to have to speak up."

He didn't. He tilted his head — slowly, the way children do when they're deciding whether you're safe — and then he did the worst thing available to him, which was nothing at all. He just kept standing there, patient as furniture, while my reflection lagged two seconds behind me and the whole arrangement settled into something horribly domestic. Me, the boy, the glass. A family portrait nobody had commissioned.

I checked Edith. She was asleep, breathing shallow but even, her lips moving through numbers too soft to catch. I charted it. *2:14 a.m., patient murmuring, count indeterminate.* There is no box on a nursing form for *hallucination shared with architecture*, so I left that part out, which is its own kind of confession, if you're the sort of person who believes silence is a symptom. I am that sort of person. I've just never treated myself.

* * *

Over the next day and a half, I conducted the most rigorous study of my own sanity ever performed by one exhausted woman.

Rule one: he appeared only in reflections. Not the big mirror alone — any reflective surface. The hall mirror, obviously. The long window over the stairwell when the light hit it right. The dark screen of the television in the morning room, which showed the room behind me with its usual fidelity plus one small boy standing where the armchair wasn't. Once, horrifyingly, the polished lid of the grand piano, which gave me his face upside down, close, looking up out of the black lacquer like a diver under ice. I closed the piano. I don't play. It felt like shutting a coffin, and I thought about that comparison for a long time afterward and liked it less each time.

Rule two: he never appeared directly. I tried. I walked the corridors with my eyes forward and my attention split, the way you check a blind spot without turning your head, and there was never anyone there. I set up my phone to record the hallway overnight — because if I was cracking up, I wanted evidence, and if I wasn't, I wanted evidence more — and got nine hours of empty corridor and a battery that drained from full to zero in four. The phone didn't lie. It just refused to participate.

Rule three, and this is the one that undid me: he followed *me*. Cora walked past the hall mirror six times a day and cast a single reflection. Mrs. Halloway, the twice-weekly cleaner, dusted the glass without comment and without company. But whenever I passed, there he was, ten feet back, keeping my pace, stopping when I stopped. Not menacing. Attentive. Like a nurse assigned to my case.

I know how that sounds. I know what it means that I found it almost — not comforting, I'm not gone — but *familiar*. All those years at Alderwood, sitting with the dying while their families paced the halls and their machines counted down, and the one thing everyone wanted was witness. Somebody to see them out. Maybe that's all he wanted. Maybe the dead are just patients nobody charted.

Or maybe I was romanticizing a haunting because the alternative was admitting I was trapped in a house that was studying me back. Both things were true. That's the trouble with patterns: they're honest, and honesty rarely comes with just one explanation attached.

* * *

On the fifth evening, Edith woke screaming.

I was at her bedside before the sound finished leaving her, penlight in hand, running the checklist — airway, color, pupils — while her thin arms thrashed against the coverlet. "Edith. Edith, you're safe, you're in your room, it's Lena—"

Her eyes came open and fixed on me, and they were the worst thing I'd seen in that house, worse than the boy, worse than the frost, because they were *lucid*. Fully, terribly present, the fog burned off her mind like morning mist, and what was underneath was grief so old it had fossilized into terror.

"Tobias," she said. "Tobias, Tobias—"

"Who's Tobias?" I asked, and my voice did the thing voices do when you already know the answer — went gentle and stupid at the same time.

She grabbed my wrist. Ninety-one years old and her grip could have cracked walnuts. "Was he at the glass? Was he at the glass again?"

The question went through me like a needle finding a vein. She knew. Not in the vague, feverish way she'd known the glass was *catching up* — she knew specifically, precisely, the way you know a face you've spent decades avoiding.

"Edith," I said carefully, "tell me what you're seeing."

"He waits." Her breath was ragged. "He waits in the glass and he doesn't grow. He's never grown. Seventy years and he stands there in his good coat and he *waits*, and every year I think perhaps this year he'll be angry, because anger I could bear, anger has an end to it — but he isn't angry. He's only waiting. He's only ever waiting, and it's so much worse."

"Who is he, Edith?"

She looked at me then with an expression I'd seen on faces at the very end of life a hundred times — the look of someone checking whether the person holding their hand is strong enough to be told the truth. Apparently I passed, because she said:

"My brother."

The word dropped into the room and sat there.

"He was seven." Her voice had gone flat, recitation-flat, the tone of a woman reading from a ledger inside herself. "Nineteen hundred and eleven. He was ill and then he was worse and nobody — " Her jaw worked. "Nobody came. The doctor was 'engaged.' Father said boys die, it's what they're for, we'd bury two already. Mother rang the chapel bell until they took the rope away." Her grip tightened on my wrist. "They took the rope away, Miss Pemberton. From a grieving woman. They let her stand in the tower ringing with her bare hands until her palms bled, and then they boarded the bell up, and three days later Tobias died in the room below while the village went about its business, and I was eight years old, and I heard him counting."

My skin did something involuntary. "Counting?"

"Backwards." She stared past me at the ceiling. "All night. Down and down. And when he reached the end he stopped, and the house has never once, in seventy years, stopped counting with him."

I didn't say anything. What was there? *Ma'am, your dead brother and my dying patients appear to share a physician.*

"They buried the bell," she whispered. "In the cellar. Face-down in the earth, like a body. And the family paid, and paid, and paid, and told themselves it was settled." Her eyes came back to mine, and there was something in them I recognized from the other side of a hundred deathbeds: the desperate arithmetic of a person trying to hand off a weight before their arms give out. "It isn't settled. Nothing is settled. He's shown himself to you."

"How do you know that?"

"Because you flinched at the mirror yesterday." Almost a smile, terrible and dry. "And because he only shows himself to the ones he wants. He wants a witness, Miss Pemberton. He's had none for seventy years, and neither, I think—" her gaze sharpened, "—have you."

I opened my mouth to deny it and found, to my professional disgust, that I couldn't. Seventy years of watching people die alone while I kept my omen locked behind my teeth, and a dead child in a good coat had taken one look at me across a century and known exactly what I was.

Edith's lucidity was already draining, the fog rolling back in like a tide. Her grip loosened. "Don't tell Cora," she murmured, sinking. "Cora pays. Cora thinks paying is the same as answering. It isn't. It was never—" Her eyes fluttered. "He counts because no one counted *for* him. No one stayed until zero."

Then she was asleep, or somewhere beneath sleep, her lips moving through numbers again — and this time, in the quiet, I caught enough of them to feel the floor tilt. Not thirty. Not hundreds. Something vast, worn smooth by repetition, a number that had been falling for seventy years and was still falling.

I sat with her until my shift ended, because that's the job, because that's the vow, because a woman had just told me her brother died unattended and I would sooner have sawn off my own hands than leave her bedside. But all the while, in the black rectangle of the window beside her bed, I could see the reflection of the room behind me —

— and the boy was there, standing at the foot of the bed, looking at Edith with an expression I finally had a name for.

Not malice. Not hunger. Longing. The specific, bottomless longing of someone watching a family member sleep, wanting only to be counted, and knowing the count will run out before anyone turns around.

This wasn't a haunting. Hauntings are random, aimless, weather. This was a grievance with a shape and a name and seventy years of patience, and it had decided, out of everyone in the world, to show itself to me.

I looked at his small reflected face and understood, with the calm certainty that precedes all my worst decisions, that before this was over, someone was going to have to stay with him until zero.

The question — the one that followed me down the dark corridor, past the mirror where he waited, into my cold little room where I lay awake doing arithmetic on the dark — was whether that someone was going to be Edith, or Cora,

or me.
