## 9. Tobias, 1911

The parish archive of Lower Aldercombe occupied the back room of St. Cuthbert's, which was to say it occupied a damp annex with one window, one radiator that had given up sometime during the previous century, and a smell I can only describe as *old paper having opinions*. The archivist was a retired schoolteacher named Mr. Pell who wore fingerless gloves indoors and regarded me with the mild suspicion of a man who has spent forty years learning that everyone who asks for records wants something.

"Vane," he repeated, when I gave him the name. "Hollowmere Vanes."

"That's right. Family history. Medical context." I'd learned by then that "medical context" made people stop asking questions faster than any lie. It sounded official. It sounded like paperwork existed somewhere that would back me up.

"Mm." He wrote my name in a visitors' book — *Lena Pemberton*, in his careful hand, and I thought, absurdly, *there's another record of me now, another place where I exist on paper* — and led me into the annex. "Ecclesiastical registers go back to 1840. Civil records are at the county office. If you want the interesting years, though—" he paused, and something flickered across his face, the look of a man deciding whether to feed an animal through the bars, "—you'll want 1911."

"Why would I want 1911?"

"Everyone wants 1911 eventually." He set three boxes on the table and left me alone with them, which was either trust or a statement about how cold the room was.

I started with the baptismal register because you always start with births. Births are cheerful. Births don't know what's coming.

* * *

> **Baptisms, St. Cuthbert's, Lower Aldercombe**
>
> *Tobias Edmund Vane, son of Edmund Vane (gentleman farmer) and Margaret Vane, born 14th March 1903, baptised 5th April 1903.*
>
> *Note: father did not attend. Godparents: Rev. H. Ashworth and Mrs. Corrick.*

A boy born in March of 1903. Which would have made him eight years old in 1911.

Eight. Edith had been born in 1919, so Tobias was her uncle — an uncle she'd never met, dead before she could form a memory. And yet she'd screamed his name like it had been waiting behind her teeth for eighty years.

I turned pages. The register was routine, ink fading from confident black to apologetic brown as the decades passed, and I let myself do what I always do with documents: build a timeline, find the pattern, make the chaos behave. It's the same instinct that makes me count doors twice. The world is full of things that won't hold still. Paper holds still. Paper lets you win, a little.

* * *

> **Burials, St. Cuthbert's, Lower Aldercombe**
>
> *Tobias Edmund Vane, aged 8 years, buried 21st December 1911.*
>
> *Cause of death (per coroner's certificate): exposure and wasting illness.*
>
> *Note in different hand, later ink:* No service was held. See vestry minutes, Dec. 1911. Rector refused rites pending inquiry. Inquiry never convened. Buried without office by sexton alone.

I read that note four times.

*No service was held.* For an eight-year-old. In a village where they held services for drowned lambs, if the mood took them.

*Inquiry never convened.* Four words doing the work of a whole confession. Somebody asked questions. Somebody powerful said no. And then everybody went home and kept their mouths shut for a hundred years, which is the other thing people do besides counting doors.

My hands were cold. I told myself it was the radiator.

* * *

The vestry minutes were in the second box, bound in a ledger so water-stained it looked like it had wept. December 1911 ran to exactly two entries:

> *11th Dec. — Mrs. M. Vane attended evening prayer and remained after congregation departed. Rector reports she rang the chapel bell unbidden, some forty strokes, and could not be persuaded to cease until Mr. E. Vane came for her. She was heard to say the bell was not for the living. Bell rope removed following day by order of the churchwardens.*

> *19th Dec. — Special meeting re: the Vane child. Present: churchwardens Ashworth, Corrick, Tull; sexton Grieves. Resolved: matter closed. Resolved further: no further meetings on this subject. Carried unanimously.*

*Matter closed.* Two words. A boy dies of neglect in a house full of people paid to notice him, the village notices anyway, and the village's answer is to close the matter like a drawer.

And there it was — the missing bell rope. Hollowmere's tower had a bell but no rope, and here was why: the rope hadn't rotted away or been lost. It had been *removed by order*, deliberately, the way you'd remove a loaded gun from a room where someone unstable was pacing. Except they'd removed the rope and left the bell, which tells you everything about how well these people understood what they were dealing with. You don't take the bullets out of the gun. You just hide the trigger and hope nobody finds the gun.

*Forty strokes,* I thought. *She rang it forty times and couldn't be persuaded to stop.* And Edith, half-lucid in her bed, counting backwards from numbers too large to be days. Counting down toward something. Toward zero. Toward whatever number Margaret Vane had started when she pulled that rope.

* * *

Mr. Pell appeared at some point with tea in a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST GRANDAD, which I chose to interpret as self-aware rather than aspirational.

"Find your 1911?" he asked.

"I found a burial with no service and a vestry meeting that resolved never to meet again."

"Ah." He sat down across from me, unhurried, warming his gloved fingers on the mug. "You've got the nurse's manner. Very efficient. Very *moving along*." He sipped. "My grandmother was eleven in 1911. Down in the village, I mean. She wouldn't talk about the Vane boy either — except once, when she was dying, and I'll tell you what she said, since you've come all this way and the radiator's broken and you deserve something for it."

I waited. Waiting is most of my job.

"She said: *We all heard him crying, and we all decided it wasn't our business, and then his mother rang the bell, and we learned what our business was.*" He set the mug down. "Then she said the rest of it so quiet I nearly missed it. She said, *It came. Whatever she called, it came, and it started asking its questions, and we buried the questions instead of answering them, and God forgive us, we buried them shallow.*"

The room got colder. Or I did.

"Buried what, exactly?" I asked. "Where?"

"Well." He shrugged, a small, dry movement. "Shallow, she said. And there's only one cellar in this parish anyone's ever been frightened of."

* * *

I should have driven home. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them anymore. I found what I'd come for — a name, a date, a shape for the thing in the mirrors — and every competent instinct I owned said *go back, chart it, think it over in daylight.* But daylight was four hours off, and I had learned over the past week that Hollowmere doesn't operate in daylight, and neither, apparently, did I.

Because there was one more box. The third one, the one Mr. Pell hadn't mentioned, labeled in faded pencil: *MISCELLANEOUS PAPERS — DEPOSITED 1952 — VANE.*

Inside: correspondence, mostly dull. Rent receipts. A letter about drainage. And near the bottom, wrapped in a sheet of newspaper dated November 1952, a bundle of loose pages in a woman's hand — cramped, urgent, the handwriting of someone writing fast because stopping means thinking.

Margaret Vane. It had to be. The dates fit; the ink was 1911 or 1912; and no one else alive that winter would have written what these pages wrote.

I copied them out longhand, because the archivist's rules said no removals, and because copying them made me feel like I was doing something clinical instead of something that was going to follow me home. Here is what I copied:

* * *

> *[undated, early December 1911]*
>
> They have taken the rope. As if the rope were the sin. Edmund stood in the churchyard with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral, and it was HIS funeral they were pretending not to hold.
>
> Toby cried for six weeks where they could hear him in the lane. The Corricks heard him. The Tulls heard him. The rector heard him and sent a note about the DRAINAGE. Everyone heard him and everyone arranged their hearing so that it was not hearing, and now he is in the ground without a psalm, and they have resolved never to meet again, as though a meeting is what summons it.
>
> It was already summoned. I summoned it. Forty strokes and I would have rung forty thousand. I did not call it to punish Edmund. I called it because there is no court in England that will touch a man of property for letting a child die slowly in his own house, and SOMETHING must keep the books that men will not keep.

> *[later]*
>
> It does not shout. That is the worst of it. I expected thunder. Instead the house has begun to count. Small things at first — the clock striking wrong, Edmund counting his spoons and finding one too many, one too few. Then last night I woke and heard it under the floor, patient as winter, going down through numbers like a man descending stairs. It is not angry. Anger would be bearable. It is ARITHMETIC. It is keeping accounts, and the account is not finished, and it will not be finished while any of us live who heard that child and did nothing.

> *[December, final page]*
>
> The men came tonight with the sexton. They mean to bury the bell itself — dig it out, turn it face-down in the earth below the house, put the floor back over it. Ashworth says a bell laid mouth-down cannot speak. Tull says the earth will muffle it. Grieves says nothing; Grieves digs; Grieves has always known which side of the spade he stands on.
>
> I told them it will not work. I told them you cannot bury a question. They have resolved to try. They have resolved very hard.
>
> If anyone reads this: it was not madness. I knew what I was calling and I would call it again. Let it finish. Let it ask its questions of every soul in this parish who heard a child cry for six weeks and called it weather. We were not afraid of the thing in the bell. We were afraid of being ANSWERED.
>
> The count goes down. When it reaches the bottom, someone must stand at the bell and give it what it came for. A name. A debt. An answer owed.
>
> I am dying — no, that is not true, I am only tired, which is worse — and Edmund will outlive me and smile at the vicar and die warm in his bed, because the world protects men like Edmund the way a coat protects a shoulder.
>
> But the count goes down. Slowly. Through the years. Through the children. It is patient because justice, real justice, has never once been in a hurry.

* * *

I sat in that freezing annex for a long time after the last line, listening to the radiator tick like something counting.

Here is what I understood, sitting there, and I understood it the way you understand a diagnosis — all at once, and with the particular nausea of having seen it too late to be surprised:

The dying count backwards. All of them. Every patient I have ever sat with, thirty seconds out, ticking down to zero, and I never told a soul because who would believe me, and because telling felt like breaking something, and because — God help me — the pattern was *mine*, the one reliable companionship in a career full of endings.

It was never mine. It was never private. It was a summons, running down through a century of dying rooms, using every deathbed in earshot as a clock face. Margaret Vane pulled a rope and started a countdown, and the village buried the bell instead of answering it, and the count didn't stop. It just went looking for ears. And it found mine — found them young, found them willing, found a lonely girl who preferred patterns to people and would keep its secret faithfully for twenty years without ever knowing she was keeping anything at all.

I wasn't haunted. I was *employed.*

And the numbers Edith counted in her sleep — the vast ones, the ones too large to be days — those weren't Edith's arithmetic. Those were the master figure. The original sum, still descending, one deathbed at a time, one backward count at a time, a century of thirties subtracted from a debt that had never once been paid.

*Someone must stand at the bell and give it what it came for.*

I folded my handwritten copies into my coat, inside the pocket over my heart, which is where you carry things you're not ready to have read on your body.

Mr. Pell walked me out. At the door he stopped, and looked at me with those forty-years-of-asking-people-what-they-really-want eyes, and said, quietly: "You're staying at Hollowmere, aren't you."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, the way you nod at a diagnosis. "My grandmother said the house counts. Said it counts best at night, and loudest near the cellar, and that if you ever hear it reach a small number—" he opened the door for me, into the grey afternoon, "—you shouldn't be standing still."

I drove back through lanes the frost had drawn on, feathered white on every hedge and field, the same pattern repeating, repeating, repeating — and I caught myself, at the last bend before the gates, checking the rearview mirror to see whether the road behind me arrived on time.

It did.

For now, it did.

But the house was waiting with its door already open, and upstairs I could see Edith's window, and beneath my ribs — faint, patient, arithmetic — something had begun, very softly, to count.
