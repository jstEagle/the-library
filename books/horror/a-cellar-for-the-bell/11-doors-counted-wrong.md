## 11. Doors Counted Wrong

I have driven away from bad situations before. I'd like to say I do it often enough to be good at it, but the truth is that in eleven years of nursing I've only ever fled twice — once from a care home that smelled of lies and bleach in equal measure, and once from a wedding that smelled of nothing at all, which was worse. Both times the road had been kind. Both times it simply went where roads go: forward, away, toward somewhere with fewer secrets per square mile.

The road out of Hollowmere was not kind.

It was 4:17 in the morning when I pulled out of the stable yard, headlights carving a tunnel through fog so thick it seemed less like weather and more like a decision the house had made about what I was allowed to see. Edith had been dead for four hours. The counting had moved into my chest like a lodger who doesn't need furniture — *nine, eight, seven* — patient as a metronome, and I had done the arithmetic every nurse eventually does when a situation stops being medical: this is not my problem, this is not survivable, this is not mine.

Except it was mine now. It lived under my sternum and counted.

Still. A person can drive with something counting in her chest. People drive through worse. Grief, mostly. So I drove.

The lane unspooled ahead of me, hedgerows looming black on either side, the gravel popping under the tyres like slow applause. I passed the gate lodge — or a gate lodge; stone, ivy, one lit window — and told myself the lit window meant someone was awake in there, some normal human being with a kettle and a radio and no mirrors at all.

Then I passed the gate lodge again.

Same stone. Same ivy. Same single lit window, and this time I could see through it, because my headlights swept across it at exactly the same angle as before, and inside there was a chair, and on the chair there was folded clothing, and the clothing was grey, and I knew that grey. It was the cardigan I'd left draped over the chair in my room at Hollowmere, the one I'd decided not to pack because packing felt too much like admitting I was running.

I did what any rational person does. I kept driving. Rational people are famous for it.

The lane bent left, then right, then left again, and each bend had the quality of a sentence repeated by someone hoping you won't notice. The fog thickened until the hedges were just suggestions, then theories, then nothing, and my headlights illuminated a cone of gravel and mist and absolutely no evidence that the world continued past thirty feet. Somewhere behind me — though "behind" had stopped meaning anything — I heard the chapel bell tower creak in the wind. There was no bell in it to creak. There hadn't been since 1911. But houses lie, and their towers creak anyway.

At 4:41 by the dashboard clock, the road delivered me back to the stable yard.

Not approximately. Not a similar yard. The same yard, down to the oil stain shaped like a closed eye beneath where I'd parked, down to the frost writing its feathered pattern across the windscreen of Cora's car — identical feathers, identical spacing, the same design that climbed every window of Hollowmere and filled every page of Edith's fever sketches. The house stood beyond it, all its windows dark except one, and I knew without looking which one. Mine. With my grey cardigan visible in the glass, folded over a chair, waiting for me like a dog that knows you'll come back because you always come back.

I sat in the idling car for a long moment. In my chest, the count went on. *Four. Three.* It didn't gloat. That was somehow the worst part. It didn't even seem to notice I'd tried to leave.

"Fine," I said aloud, to the fog, to the house, to whatever kept the books here. "Fine."

I turned off the engine. The silence that followed had texture, like wool pressed against both ears, and underneath it the count resumed from wherever counts resume. I got out. My breath hung in front of me and did not dissipate so much as get filed away somewhere.

The front door opened before I touched it.

Not flung open. Not dramatically. It simply swung inward with the unhurried courtesy of a butler who has been informed of my arrival, and I understood that Hollowmere had known I was coming back before I did — possibly before I left — and had adjusted its schedule accordingly.

* * *

Here is a thing about me: I count doors. I count them the way other people check their phones, automatically, compulsively, a habit installed during my first year of nursing when a confused resident wandered into a supply cupboard and I found her an hour later because I'd noticed the door count was wrong. Ten doors on the ground floor of Hollowmere. I had counted them my first night, and my third, and every night since, because patterns are the only promises I've ever trusted. Ten doors: front, back, cellar hatch (sealed), three parlours, dining room, study, kitchen, scullery stairs. Ten. Reliable as a pulse.

I stood in the entrance hall, dripping fog, and counted.

Front door. One. Back door, two. Cellar hatch, three — still sealed, still wearing its century of dust like a disguise that fools nobody. Parlour, four. Morning room, five. Drawing room, six. Dining room, seven. Study, eight. Kitchen, nine. Scullery stairs, ten.

Eleven.

I stood very still and counted again, slower, touching each frame the way Edith used to touch the bedrail when she wanted proof the world was solid. Front door. Back door. Hatch. Parlour. Morning room. Drawing room. Dining room. Study. Kitchen. Scullery stairs.

And between the drawing room and the dining room, where the wall had been continuous plaster for as long as I'd worked here — I had leaned against that wall, I had hung Edith's IV bag on a hook screwed into that wall — there stood a door.

It was oak. It was old. Its iron hinges were furred with frost, and its handle was worn bright in the middle, polished by hands, many hands, over many years, which meant it had always been used, which meant it had never been anywhere else, which meant my ten doors had been the lie all along and the house had simply, generously, waited until now to correct my arithmetic.

I did not open it. I want that on the record. Whatever else I did that night, whatever I chose at dawn, I did not open the eleventh door. Some part of me — the part that has sat with two hundred dying people and learned exactly how much silence a room can hold — understood that the door wasn't locked. It was offered. And I am done accepting offers from things that don't explain themselves.

Instead I went to the hall mirror.

* * *

The mirror over the console table had been lagging for days. First a blink arriving late, then a raised hand answered a beat behind, then five seconds, then more, until looking into it was like watching a rehearsal of your own life performed by a diligent understudy. This morning — this endless, pre-dawn morning — the understudy had stopped following the script entirely.

In the mirror, the hall was lit.

Not dimly, not with the grey suggestion of light I'd left behind me. It was lit warm and gold, lamps burning on the console table, on the hall stand, light pooling on the floorboards like spilled honey. And the reflected version of me — the one who had apparently arrived several minutes ahead of my body — was standing at the eleventh door with her hand flat against the oak, head bowed, listening.

I watched myself listen. That is a sentence I will be unpacking in therapy for the rest of my life, if I live to have therapy, which at 4:50 in the morning seemed optimistic.

The reflected Lena straightened. Turned. Looked directly out of the glass at the actual me standing six feet away in the unlit hall, and her face was doing something mine wasn't: she was crying. Quietly, steadily, the way you cry when you've already agreed to something and are only waiting for your body to catch up to your decision.

"No," I said. My voice came out flat and professional, the voice I use to tell families things they don't want to hear. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

She mouthed something. No sound crossed the gap between us — sound, at least, still respected the border of the glass — but I read her lips easily enough, because I've spent years reading the lips of the dying through morphine haze, and the words were simple, and they were:

*He's waiting.*

And then she stepped aside, and the mirror showed me the rest of the hall — the real hall, minutes from now, or hours, or however far ahead the glass had gotten — and Tobias was there.

He stood at the foot of the cellar stairs. He was small, maybe seven, in clothes that belonged to another century, a sailor collar gone soft with age, and he was not translucent, thank God, he was not glowing, he was not any of the theatrics the word *ghost* drags behind it. He was just a boy, standing very straight the way children stand when they've been told to wait nicely, his hands folded, his chin up, his eyes fixed on the cellar hatch with the patience of someone who has been waiting a hundred and fourteen years and has gotten extremely good at it.

He turned his head and looked at the reflected me. Then, slowly, deliberately, he looked at the hatch. Then back at me. Then at the hatch.

I have received clearer instructions from unconscious patients.

"You're not getting me down there," I told him. My breath fogged the mirror, and when it cleared, the boy was closer to the glass — close enough that I could see his eyes properly for the first time. They weren't frightening. That was the trap of him, I think, if trap is even the right word for a child. They were tired. They were the eyes of a kid who has waited past bedtime, past midnight, past everything, in a house where everyone looked away, and all he wants — all he has ever wanted — is for somebody to look.

"I'm not the one who looked away," I said, and my voice cracked on it, because the count in my chest chose that moment to tick down — *two, one* — and start over, and I felt it like a hand tightening gently around something of mine.

Tobias tilted his head. And in the glass, behind him, the reflected hall changed: the lamps guttered, the gold light thinned to grey, and the reflected Lena was no longer at the eleventh door. She was descending the cellar stairs, hand over hand on the wall, and the frost on the walls around her was forming letters — not feathered patterns now, not decorations, but actual letters, actual words, written in ice on the plaster as she passed, and I couldn't read them yet. They were too far ahead. They were waiting for me to catch up.

The reflection held for three seconds longer. Long enough for me to see that in it, my sleeves were pushed up. Long enough to see dirt under my fingernails. Long enough to understand that whatever happened down those stairs, I would arrive there digging.

Then the mirror gave out — not shattered, not cracked, just emptied, going dark and depthless as a well, and my own reflection returned to it, ordinary, exhausted, forty-one years old, standing in a cold hall with a dead woman upstairs and a countdown under her ribs.

* * *

I found Cora in the kitchen, sitting at the table with the ledger open in front of her and a bottle of something amber beside it, untouched. She looked up when I came in, and I saw that she'd been crying, and that she'd stopped some time ago, and that stopping had cost her more than the crying had.

"The road brought you back," she said. Not a question.

"The road brought me back." I sat down across from her because my legs had unionized and voted. "Cora. There are eleven doors downstairs."

"I know." She turned the ledger toward me. Her finger rested on a line of faded ink, and in the lamplight I read it again, the line I'd found once before and told myself was a figure of speech: *It is invited. It must be answered.*

"There were always eleven," she said. "My grandmother wrote about it. You just have to need to leave before it shows you the extra one." She laughed, a small broken sound with no humour in it whatsoever. "That's the test, I think. Or the trap. I've never worked out which. Every Vane who ever tried to run got shown the door, and every one of them stayed, and every one of them fed it — a sacking here, a wage stolen there, small cruelties, little injustices, just enough to keep the count slow. Grandmother called it *tithing*. Like it was piety." Her finger trembled against the page. "Edith refused to tithe. Did you know that? Sixty years, and she wouldn't give it a single cruelty, not one. She thought starving it was mercy. She thought if the family just stopped, it would finish counting and go."

"And instead it sped up."

"Instead it got hungry." Cora finally poured herself a drink and didn't drink it, just held the glass like a handrail. "Lena. When the sun comes up, the count ends. One way or the other. Either someone answers the summons at the bell, or it answers itself, and I have read enough of that ledger to know what *answering itself* means. It collects the whole debt. Everyone in this house. Everything owed since 1911, all at once, with interest." She looked at me then, and her eyes were terrible and honest, the eyes of a woman setting down something heavy after carrying it so long her spine has grown around it. "You tried to leave. I'm sorry. I'm sorry — the road does that, it's not personal, it's just —"

"Just the house keeping its books balanced."

"Yes."

Upstairs, faintly, through floors and plaster and a hundred years of paint, I heard the cellar bell ring once. Buried face-down in frozen earth, ringing with each number, keeping time with the thing in my chest. *Nine,* it said. *Eight.*

And in the dark window above the sink — the one pane in the kitchen, the last working glass in a house full of dying mirrors — the frost began, very slowly, to write.

Letters this time. Whole lines of them, feathered and white, blooming across the pane like breath on cold glass, spelling themselves out in a hand that was careful and old and terribly patient. Cora and I read it together, neither of us moving, neither of us breathing, while the count ticked down beneath my ribs and the buried bell rang on.

The frost said: *ONE OF YOU ALREADY KNOWS THE WAY DOWN.*

And below that, smaller, almost gentle, in the way a hand is gentle when it closes over yours:

*SHE HAS BEEN PRACTISING HER WHOLE LIFE.*
