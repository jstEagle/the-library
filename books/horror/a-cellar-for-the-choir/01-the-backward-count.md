## 1. The Backward Count

The woman in room 4 counted backwards from ninety-one, and Marguerite Reyes had heard the numbers before.

She knew this the way she knew a hymn's first note — not by thinking it, but by the body's small recognition, the spine straightening slightly in the plastic chair, the breath going shallow and careful. *Ninety-one. Ninety.* Edith Warriner's lips barely moved. Her voice had the papery thinness of all last voices, but beneath it ran something else, something with grain and structure, like a thread pulled taut through cloth. *Eighty-nine. Eighty-eight.*

Marguerite had been a hospice nurse for eleven years. She had sat with the dying through two hundred and some deaths — she had stopped counting at two hundred because the number began to feel like a debt — and she knew the shapes of their departures the way sailors know weather. There were the ones who bargained, murmuring to wives long dead and fathers they'd disappointed. There were the ones who rehearsed, running through old arguments, old apologies, the unfinished business of a life sorting itself into piles. There were the ones who sang, who prayed, who cursed, who asked for the window to be opened in February because they could smell their mother's bread.

And there were the ones who counted.

She had noticed it first with Mr. Okafor, fourteen months ago. A retired machinist with hands like knotted oak, he had lain in the last week of his dying and whispered numbers, descending, unhurried, with the calm of a man reading off an inventory he'd known by heart for years. *Forty. Thirty-nine.* Marguerite had assumed it was the morphine, some loop in the dying brain, an arithmetic of dissolution. She had noted it in his chart — *patient exhibits repetitive vocalization, non-distressing* — and thought no more of it.

Then Mrs. Halloran, in the spring, counting down from a number Marguerite hadn't caught the start of, and the numbers were the same. Not similar. The same. The same descending sequence, the same unhurried cadence, as though both patients were walking down the same staircase, each at their own pace, each seeing the same steps. *Twenty-two. Twenty-one.* And when Mrs. Halloran reached the bottom — when she breathed the last number like a woman setting down something heavy — there had been a sound.

Marguerite had never been able to describe it in the chart. A sound like a struck bell, but wrong — not the bright bronze of a church bell but something deeper, duller, as though the bell were wrapped in wool, or buried, or made of a metal that had forgotten how to ring. It lasted perhaps two seconds. It came from nowhere. The monitors showed nothing. The other nurses, down the hall, heard nothing. Mrs. Halloran's daughter, asleep in the recliner, heard nothing. Only Marguerite, sitting two feet away, felt it pass through her sternum like a finger drawn down a wet glass.

And then Mr. Alvarez, in the summer, counting from sixty-four — *sixty-four, sixty-three* — and Marguerite, standing at his bedside with her hand on his thin shoulder, had felt the floor tilt under her professional composure, because she knew those numbers. She had heard them all before. Three patients, three separate deaths, three months apart, all walking down the identical staircase.

Now Edith Warriner, and ninety-one.

Marguerite had started her shift at seven. It was nearly eleven now, and the room held the particular stillness of the actively dying — not silence, never silence, the ventilator of the world still breathing somewhere, the radiator ticking, the ivy of sound that grows on all occupied rooms — but a stillness with weight, the stillness of held breath. Edith lay against the raised bed, her face turned toward the window, where November was doing its gray, patient work. Ninety-one years old. No family left. A niece in Spokane who called on Sundays. And the numbers, coming soft and steady, like water finding its level.

*Ninety. Eighty-nine.*

Marguerite sat in the plastic chair and did what she always did: she watched, she charted, she kept her hands busy. She moistened Edith's lips with a sponge swab. She checked the morphine drip — modest, appropriate, not enough to account for this. She wrote the time and the vitals in her neat, slanted hand, and then, in the margin, where no one would look, she wrote: *counting again.*

The words looked small and foolish on the page. She had been writing that phrase, or versions of it, for fourteen months, and it had begun to feel less like documentation and more like the record of a haunting she couldn't prove.

*Ninety-one,* Edith had said, at nine-forty in the morning, waking from a doze to say it clearly, once, like a woman announcing a departure time. And then the descent had begun.

Marguerite had gone to the nurses' station and pulled the old charts. She shouldn't have — it wasn't her patient yet, Edith had only been admitted Tuesday — but she'd needed to know where the number came from, whether ninety-one meant anything, whether the starting number was itself a kind of message. Mr. Okafor had started at forty. Mrs. Halloran, from what the night nurse could recall, somewhere in the twenties. Mr. Alvarez at sixty-four. No pattern in the starting numbers. No pattern in the ages, the diagnoses, the faiths — Okafor had been a lapsed Baptist, Halloran a devout Catholic who'd wanted last rites twice, Alvarez had died insisting he was an atheist and then, in the last hour, whispering numbers like a man reciting scripture he'd never learned.

The only pattern was the staircase itself. The same steps, down and down, and at the bottom, the buried bell.

*Eighty-seven. Eighty-six.*

Edith's eyes were open now, half-lidded, fixed on the middle distance where the dying do their deepest looking. Marguerite leaned in. "Edith," she said gently. "You're counting, honey. What are you counting?"

The old woman's lips curved. Not a smile, exactly. The ghost of a smile, the way a pressed flower is the ghost of a flower. "You can hear it," she said. Not a question. Her voice was dry leaves and wind. "Most of them can't hear it. The young ones. But you can hear it."

"Hear what, Edith?"

But Edith only went on, *eighty-five, eighty-four*, and her eyes had gone back to the middle distance, and Marguerite understood she would get nothing more. The dying never explained. That was the thing no one told you about hospice work — you spent your career at the border of the country where explanations lived, and no one crossing back ever brought so much as a postcard.

Marguerite sat back. Her hands, she noticed, were not entirely steady, and she folded them in her lap and looked at them with clinical detachment, the way she'd look at a patient's tremor. *Tremor, intermittent, bilateral, anxiety-related.* She was forty-three years old. She had held the hands of the dying through two hundred deaths. Her composure was a professional instrument, maintained like a surgical kit, and it was not supposed to rattle in a room with a sweet old woman counting to herself.

But it was rattling. Because she had done the thing she told her students never to do: she had looked for the pattern, and she had found it, and the pattern had no bottom.

She thought about the sound. The buried-bell sound. Twice she had heard it, at the end of two counts, and both times it had felt less like hearing and more like being *addressed*. That was the word her mind kept returning to, and she disliked the word, because words like that belonged to her grandmother's church, to incense and dread and the God of the small country chapel, and Marguerite had left that country deliberately, at nineteen, trading mystery for method. Numbers you could chart. Grief you could hold. But a sound that addressed you — that was a door she had nailed shut a long time ago, and something on the other side was now knocking.

*Eighty. Seventy-nine.*

The afternoon light shifted. Edith dozed between numbers, sometimes losing an hour, sometimes only a minute, and always resuming at the exact number where she'd left off, as though the count were kept somewhere safe for her, a coat checked at a door. Marguerite took her breaks. She ate half a sandwich in the break room without tasting it. She checked on her other patients — Mr. Deel, stable, complaining; Mrs. Okonkwo, sleeping — and returned to room 4 each time with the reluctance of a woman returning to a cold swim.

At four o'clock, Edith's niece called, and Marguerite gave the family update in her warm, level voice — *comfortable, peaceful, no pain to speak of, these things move at their own pace* — and hung up the phone and stood a moment in the corridor with her hand against the wall, because her voice had sounded like her mother's voice, and her mother had been dead six years, and grief did not schedule itself.

Then she went back in, and Edith was at seventy-one.

* * *

It was near midnight, on the recliner she'd pulled to the window, that Marguerite began the ledger.

Not the chart. The chart was done, the chart said what charts say. This was a different book — a small, hardbound notebook she'd bought that evening at the drugstore across from the hospice, choosing it with an embarrassment she hadn't examined, the way you choose a vessel for something you're not ready to name. She had told herself, walking back through the cold, that this was documentation. That a pattern observed across three patients — four now — was data, and data unrecorded was data lost, and a good nurse records. That was all. That was the whole of it.

She knew, in the private room of herself where lies are kept dusted and fed, that it was also a prayer. That writing the numbers down in her careful hand was a way of saying *I see this, I witness this, let it mean something, let someone after me find the meaning I can't.* Her grandmother had prayed with a rosary, bead by bead, and Marguerite prayed with a pen, line by line, and both of them were talking into the dark hoping the dark was listening.

The room was dim. The hallway light made a pale trapezoid on the floor. Edith slept between numbers, and the machines made their faithful small noises, and Marguerite opened the notebook to the first page and wrote, in her slanted, careful hand:

*Patient: Edith Warriner, 91. Admitted Tuesday. Began counting 11/14, approx. 0940. Starting number: ninety-one.*

She turned back a page — she had planned this, she realized, she had been planning this for weeks without admitting it — and wrote the others beneath, in the order she'd heard them:

*Patient: Daniel Alvarez, 67. Began counting 8/2. Starting number: sixty-four. Count ended 8/9, 0312. Sound at end: bell-like, low, unrecorded by monitor. Duration approx. 2 sec.*

*Patient: Ruth Halloran, 84. Began counting 5/17 (per night staff). Starting number unknown, est. low 20s. Count ended 5/19, 2210. Same sound. I heard it. No one else did.*

*Patient: Samuel Okafor, 73. Began counting 9/28, prior year. Starting number: forty. Count ended 10/2, 0540. Same sound.*

She sat looking at the four entries. Four staircases, identical. Four bells, buried, rung once each, heard by one person each — and the one person was always her.

The thought arrived then, the one she'd been keeping in the farthest room of her mind, and it walked out into the open and stood there: *the counts are choosing.* Not the patients choosing to count. The count choosing the patients. As though somewhere there was a list, and the list was being read aloud, one name at a time, in numbers, and the dying were simply the ones who could still hear the reading.

She wrote that down too. *The counting is not random. The counting is a selection.* And then, because the sentence frightened her, she wrote beneath it, smaller: *Documentation is a kind of prayer.* It was the truest thing she'd written all night.

Across the room, Edith stirred. Her lips moved.

Marguerite set down the pen and listened. *Sixty. Fifty-nine.* The numbers came slow now, spaced like footsteps on a long stair, and Edith's breathing had changed, gone lighter, the tide going out. Marguerite rose and came to the bedside and took the old woman's hand. It was cool and light, like holding a bird that had stopped struggling.

"Edith," she whispered. "I'm here. You're not alone."

Edith's eyes opened a slit. And in them Marguerite saw something she had seen twice before and never written down — a kind of *attention*, as though the dying woman were listening to something Marguerite could not quite hear, something just below the floor of sound, and finding it familiar, even kind. The dying did not look frightened. That was the worst of it. They looked like travelers hearing their stop announced.

*Fifty. Forty-nine.*

Marguerite stayed. She would stay all night; she had already decided, had already rearranged the morning in her head. The numbers descended through the small hours, patient as erosion. At two she charted. At three she wrote in the ledger, adding the times between numbers, building a table no one had asked for: the count was slowing, or perhaps deepening, each number given more room, like a bell swinging in a wider and wider arc.

At a quarter to five, Edith Warriner whispered *nine.*

Marguerite leaned close. "I'm here, Edith."

*Eight. Seven.* A long pause, a whole minute of breath, the tide nearly gone. *Six.*

And then Edith turned her head — with effort, with the last of her strength, deliberately, the way a person turns to look at someone they love — and looked directly at Marguerite, and said, in a voice suddenly, impossibly clear:

"You'll hear it too, nurse. Sooner than you think. It's already got your boy's number."

*Five.*

The word *boy* went through Marguerite like a needle finding wire. Theo. Twelve years old, asleep at this hour in the house on Delmore Street, sleeping badly since his father died in the spring, sleeping with the light on and his father's flannel shirt under his pillow. Marguerite felt the room tilt, felt her professional composure — that instrument maintained like a surgical kit — crack straight down the middle, and through the crack came not panic but something older and colder, the dread of a woman standing on ice and hearing, from far below, the sound of water moving.

"Edith," she said, and her voice was steady, God help her, her voice was a nurse's voice, level and warm, "what do you mean? Whose number? Edith, look at me — whose number?"

But Edith's eyes had gone back to the middle distance, to the listening, and the numbers went on, *four, three*, spaced now like a heart slowing, and Marguerite sat holding the cool bird's weight of the old woman's hand and did not know what she was begging for — for the count to stop, or only to slow.

At 5:14 in the morning, Edith Warriner whispered *one*, and then *zero*, the zero barely a shape of the mouth, an exhale, a door closing.

And the bell rang.

It came up through the floor this time, or through the walls, or through the marrow of the building itself — the low, wool-wrapped toll, deeper than she'd ever heard it, two seconds, three, and every machine in the room silent, and down the hall no one stirred, and Marguerite sat alone in the gray light with the sound still humming in her sternum, holding the hand of a woman who was no longer in it.

She sat for a long time. Then she opened the ledger, and her hand, when she looked at it, was perfectly steady, which frightened her more than trembling would have, and she wrote:

*Patient: Edith Warriner. Count ended 11/15, 0514. Same sound. Loudest yet.*

And beneath it, because she could not stop herself, because the pen seemed to move on some current of its own, she wrote the words Edith had said, verbatim, the way you chart a symptom:

*"It's already got your boy's number."*

She closed the book. Outside the window, November was turning toward dawn, the gray light coming up over the parking lot like water rising in a cellar, and somewhere in the building a machine alarm sounded, ordinary and human and welcome, and Marguerite Reyes sat in the silence after the bell and understood, with the calm of deep shock, that she had just begun keeping a ledger for a reason she would not admit until it was far too late:

She was not documenting a pattern.

She was answering a summons.
