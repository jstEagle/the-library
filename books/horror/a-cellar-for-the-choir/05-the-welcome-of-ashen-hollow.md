## 5. The Welcome of Ashen Hollow

The fog did not lift so much as it was pulled aside, like a curtain drawn by a hand that wanted to see who had come.

One moment the road was a gray ribbon unwinding through gray hills, and the next the trees stood back and Ashen Hollow opened below them in its shallow bowl of valley — slate roofs gone green with moss, chimneys standing straight as candles, smoke rising from every one of them in threads so orderly they might have been combed. Marguerite slowed the car at the crest of the hill and looked down at it, and something in her chest turned over, slow and heavy, like a body shifting in sleep.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing. It was beautiful the way a photograph is beautiful, everything composed, nothing accidental, no rusted truck abandoned at an angle, no sagging porch, no dead hedge. A century of weather had touched this place and left it tidied.

"Is that it?" Theo asked from the passenger seat. He had his forehead against the window, his breath blooming on the glass. "It looks like — I don't know. Like somewhere people go when they're tired."

"Tired of what?"

He thought about it with the seriousness he'd carried since his father died, that grave, careful way of considering questions as though grief had made him older than eleven. "Tired of leaving," he said finally.

She drove down into the village.

* * *

The first person to meet them was a woman carrying a basket of eggs, and she was already smiling before the car had fully stopped, as if she'd been standing on the cobbles waiting for exactly this arrival, at exactly this hour, for some time.

"You'll be Mrs. Reyes." Not a question. She set the basket on a wall with the care of someone setting down a sleeping child and came forward with both hands outstretched. Her skin was smooth as a peeled apple. Her eyes were brown and warm and utterly without surprise. "Maren Coleridge. We've been hoping you'd come. The roads are wicked in the fog — Anselm said you'd find your way anyway. He said you were the finding kind."

Marguerite took her hands. They were soft, uncalloused, warm as bread. "You knew I was coming?"

"The whole village knows." Maren said it lightly, the way one says *the whole village knows about the festival*, or *the whole village knows your name is on the war memorial* — ordinary knowledge, shared knowledge, the kind that binds rather than frightens. "A letter only travels so fast, but news of the letter — well. That flies."

Behind her, doors were opening along the lane. Not all at once — that would have been too much, too obvious — but in ones and twos, the way a congregation rises after a hymn. Faces appeared in doorways. A man paused in the act of stacking firewood. Two old women on a bench turned their heads together, like birds.

And here was the thing Marguerite noticed second, after the beauty, and it lodged in her professional mind like a splinter: there were no old faces among the ones that gathered. There were middle-aged faces, smooth and settled. There were young faces. There were children everywhere, dozens of them, spilling out of alleys and over walls with the loose-limbed energy of healthy childhood. But the two women on the bench — she would have called them sixty in the city, seventy in a hospice — sat with the upright spines and unlined hands of women who should have been forty-five.

"Your son," Maren said, and her voice went softer, sweeter, the way voices do around children and around the dying, and Marguerite felt the similarity of those two registers like a cold draft under a door. "What's his name?"

"Theo."

"Theo." Maren tried it out, tasting it. Down the lane, a boy of perhaps twelve broke away from a cluster of others and came running, and behind him came more, and Marguerite watched Theo open the car door before she could tell him to wait, because he was lonely — God, he had been so lonely since his father died, since before that, really, since the hospital year when every adult in his life had learned to speak in lowered voices — and here was a village full of children calling his name as though they'd rehearsed it.

"Theo! Theo Reyes!" The first boy reached him and seized his hand and pumped it solemnly, formally, like a small ambassador. "We saved you a place in the choir. We've been saving it a long time."

"We have places for everyone," said a girl with braids the color of dark honey, appearing at Theo's elbow. "But yours has been empty longest."

"Longest since when?" Marguerite asked.

The children looked at her. All of them, at once, the way a flock turns. Their faces were pleasant, flushed, healthy. None of them answered.

Then the boy laughed — a bright, ordinary boy's laugh — and said, "Come on, we'll show you the church," and Theo let himself be pulled away into the knot of them, glancing back once at his mother with an expression she couldn't read, half apology and half apology's opposite, and then he was swallowed into the group and carried up the lane toward the square, and the sound of the children's voices rose and braided together until she couldn't pick his thread out of it anymore.

"They're good with him," Maren said beside her. "Children need other children. It's cruel, keeping them solitary through a grief."

Marguerite looked at her. "I didn't say he was grieving."

"No," Maren agreed pleasantly, and picked up her basket of eggs. "You didn't."

* * *

The church stood at the head of the square, and it was wrong in a way that took her a full minute to name.

It was a fine building — old stone gone gold-gray, a squat tower, arched windows with glass that threw colored light onto the cobbles even in fog. It had the settled, much-loved look of a church that had held its congregation for centuries. And yet the tower was silent. Not quiet — silent, in the way a mouth is silent, a deliberate, maintained silence, the silence of something holding its tongue.

She understood when she stepped inside.

There was a bell rope. It hung in the tower arch where bell ropes hang, a long plaited line of it, splayed and tasseled at the bottom, worn pale at the grip by generations of hands. Above it the tower rose through the frame of the ringing chamber, the wheel mounts still bolted to their beams, the pit where the bell should swing open and shadowed and swept clean.

No bell.

Not a removed bell, not a bell awaiting recasting — the fittings were bare, the bolts rusted, the space where the great metal weight should have hung simply empty, and empty for so long that a bird had nested in the headstock and the nest itself had gone gray with age. The rope hung down into nothing. Someone had kept it plaited. Someone had kept it *swept*. For a hundred years, by the look of the dust-free beams, someone had tended the absence the way other parishes tend a relic.

"It's lovely," Theo said behind her, and she started, not having heard him come in — she, who heard everything, who had built a career on hearing the things families missed. He stood in the aisle with the honey-braided girl and three others, his face tipped up toward the vaulting, and he looked, for the first time in a year, like a child in a church instead of a mourner in one. "Does it ring?"

"Every Sunday," the girl said.

"But there's no—"

"You'll hear it," the girl said, with the gentle certainty of a nurse telling a patient the injection won't hurt, and Marguerite's spine went cold from tailbone to nape.

* * *

Anselm found her at the rope.

She heard his footsteps on the stone — measured, unhurried, the steps of a man arriving precisely when he intended to arrive — and turned to see a tall, spare man in a gray coat coming down the aisle, silver-haired but not stooped, with a face like a folded letter: creased, legible, closed. His eyes were pale gray, nearly colorless, and they rested on her with an attention so complete it felt physical, like being set down carefully on a scale.

"Mrs. Reyes." He took her hand in both of his. His grip was dry and cool and exact. "Welcome to Ashen Hollow. I am Anselm Voss. I keep the church." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "And I thank you for coming ahead of the count."

The words went through her like ice water through cloth.

"Ahead of the count," she repeated. Her voice came out level. Twenty years of deathbeds had taught her that; her voice never betrayed her, whatever her hands did, and she folded her hands now to be sure of them. "I'm afraid I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you don't." He smiled, and it was a genuinely kind smile, which was somehow worse. "Or rather — you know exactly what I mean, and you don't know that you know, and both of these things are true, and neither of them will matter until they matter. Forgive me. I've been verger a long time. I've forgotten how to say frightening things frighteningly." He gestured toward the vestry door. "You've driven a long way. You and the boy must eat. Maren will be insulted if you refuse, and she makes a pie that has converted more skeptics than my sermons ever did."

"My son—" She looked toward the aisle, where Theo had been standing. The aisle was empty. Through the south door she could see the churchyard, and in the churchyard the children had formed a ring around the oldest grave slab, and they were singing — a round, wordless, descending, each voice entering as another fell away, a sound like water going backward down a drain — and Theo stood at the edge of the ring, listening, his lips moving faintly, learning it.

"He's safe," Anselm said quietly. "I want you to hear me say that plainly, Mrs. Reyes, because I know what you're feeling right now, and I want you to know that I know, and that I am not your enemy. Your son is safe today. Every child in this village is safe today. That is not a small thing. That is the whole of the thing." He held the vestry door for her. "Come and eat. Ask me anything you like over the pie. I promise you straight answers, or as straight as the truth allows, which is sometimes a crooked line indeed."

"And if I decide to take my son and drive home tonight?"

He considered her with that same total, weighing attention. Outside, the round descended another turn, sweet and cold.

"Then I hope," he said, "that you decide it before Sunday."

* * *

The vestry had been made into a dining room, and the table was laid for twenty, and twenty came, and every one of them knew her name.

That was the texture of the evening, more than any single detail: the names. Maren Coleridge, yes, but also the schoolmaster Pell, and the doctor's widow Serafine, and a farmer named Brandt whose handshake was warm and whose eyes flicked once, quickly, toward Theo in the doorway — a flick Marguerite caught because she had spent two decades watching people look at the dying and the soon-to-die, cataloguing the exact quality of that glance, the arithmetic of it, the terrible tenderness-with-a-remainder. Brandt looked at her son the way families look at a patient on the last day of a good response. With love. With relief. With the count already running.

The food was abundant — roast fowl, dark bread, butter thick as clotted cream, the famous pie, apples from an orchard that should not, by any calendar she knew, still be bearing in this season. Everyone ate well. No one ate hurriedly. There was plenty, and there had clearly always been plenty, and Marguerite thought of the ledger in her bag upstairs in the room they'd been given — the hospice ledger, her private book of numbers — and thought: *no one goes hungry here. No one dies here. One pays, and everyone else eats.*

She caught herself thinking it in the tone of a woman reading a menu, and set down her fork.

"You're wondering about our health," Serafine said across the table. She was perhaps fifty by dress, thirty-five by face, and she said it kindly, without defensiveness, as though Marguerite had remarked on the weather. "Everyone wonders. We eat well, we walk, we don't fret. Fretting ages a person faster than anything. We gave up fretting a long time ago."

"What did you give it up *for*?" Marguerite asked.

Serafine smiled and passed her the apples.

Theo, at the far end of the table, was laughing. Actually laughing — head back, the sound bright and unfamiliar in her ears, a sound she realized with a lurch she hadn't heard since before the funeral. The choir children had annexed him completely. The honey-braided girl was showing him something on her wrist, a plaited cord with knots in it, counting knots, and the boy who'd run to meet the car was loading Theo's plate with a second helping of pie, and the whole end of the table bent toward him like stems toward sun.

He belongs there, said a voice in her that sounded dangerously like peace. Look at him. When has he ever belonged anywhere since his father—

And underneath it, colder, the nurse's voice, the one that had written ninety-one and eighty-four and seventy-six in a private book at 3 a.m.: *Bait is offered warmly. Bait is always offered warmly. That is how bait works.*

"Maren tells me you're a hospice nurse," said Anselm, beside her, having arrived without sound. Up close his agelessness was stranger — his hair truly silver, his hands truly old, and yet the face above them unlined, as if time had worked on him selectively, patiently, taking the hands and sparing the mask. "That must be hard work. Sitting with the counting."

Her blood stopped, restarted.

"I'm sorry?"

"The sitting," he said mildly. "The vigil. Holding the hand while the breath gets shorter. Most people can't bear it. Most people look away, talk louder, turn on the television. But you watch. You listen. You write it down." He turned his pale eyes to her, and there was no accusation in them, only a vast, patient approval. "Documentation is a kind of prayer. I've always thought so. We are great believers in prayer here, Mrs. Reyes. Very great believers in writing things down."

Across the room, the children had begun to sing again — softly, under the clink of plates, the descending round threading through the dinner like a ribbon through fabric — and Theo's lips moved with theirs, and he did not seem to notice that they were moving.

Anselm followed her gaze, and sighed, and for a moment the kindness in his face looked almost like sorrow.

"He has a lovely ear," he said. "They all say so. Come — you must be shown your rooms. You'll want to rest. Tomorrow I'll show you the church properly." He rose, and offered his arm, courteous as another century. "Including," he added gently, at the door, as the round descended below hearing and the candles guttered in a draft from under the cellar door, "the part of it that rings."
