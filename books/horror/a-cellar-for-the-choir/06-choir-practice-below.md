## 6. Choir Practice Below

The children came for him on the second morning, while his mother was still upstairs with the verger, learning the shape of the house.

Theo heard them before he saw them — a sound like rain deciding to be music, drifting up the rectory stairs and through the gap under his door. He had been lying on the bed in the room they'd given him, the one with the sloped ceiling and the window that looked out on the churchyard, pretending to unpack a suitcase he'd already emptied. Grief made him good at pretending. He'd had four months of practice since his father died, four months of telling teachers he was fine, of telling his mother he was sleeping, of telling himself that the hollow feeling in his chest was just hunger or tiredness or the weather.

The sound from below was none of those things.

He followed it down the stairs the way water follows a slope, without deciding to. The rectory's back hall opened onto a stone passage — he hadn't noticed it the night before, arriving in the dark with the fog pressing against the car windows like something trying to read them — and the passage sloped gently downward, its walls sweating cool mineral breath, lamplight pooling at intervals in little golden islands. His bare feet were cold on the flags. He should have gone back for shoes. He didn't go back for shoes.

At the end of the passage stood an arched door, oak gone black with age, and through it came the singing.

* * *

There were nine of them, maybe ten — children between eight and fourteen, arranged in two curved rows on risers of dark wood, in a low-vaulted room beneath what Theo guessed must be the choir loft above. The ceiling was ribbed stone, old as anything, and the lamps along the walls threw shadows upward so that the vaults seemed to breathe. At the front stood a girl of perhaps thirteen with wheat-pale hair braided tight against her skull, conducting with one hand held flat and level, as if she were smoothing the surface of invisible water.

They weren't singing words.

That was the first thing he understood, standing in the doorway with his heart going oddly light. It wasn't Latin, wasn't English, wasn't anything. The sounds they made were numbers — he knew this the way you know a face in a dream, without knowing how — sung descending, each phrase a step lower than the last, each voice entering a beat after the one before it, so that the melody folded over itself again and again like a ribbon falling down a stairwell. A round. But no round he'd ever sung in school, nothing jolly about it. This was a round the way a spiral staircase is stairs: technically true, and built to take you somewhere you couldn't see from the bottom.

*Eighty-eight,* sang the lowest row, and the sound was warm as bread.

*Eighty-seven,* answered the middle row, softer.

*Eighty-six,* said the top row, barely more than breath.

And then all together, layered, the numbers tumbling over one another down and down, and Theo felt the sound go through his sternum and settle somewhere behind it, in the place where his father used to be, filling it not up but over — the way snow fills a footprint, keeping the shape of what stood there while making it something else entirely.

The girl with the braid saw him. She didn't stop conducting. She smiled — a quick, unguarded smile, the kind kids use when they've caught another kid doing something forbidden and approve of it — and tilted her head toward the empty space at the end of the second row.

"Come on, then," she said, in the pause between phrases, and her voice was ordinary, friendly, a voice that belonged to someone who might sit next to him in maths. "You're the nurse's son. We saved you a spot."

He should have asked how she knew who he was. He should have asked what they were singing. He should have gone back up the passage for shoes, for his mother, for the sensible part of himself that had kept him upright at the funeral, that had carried the tray of sandwiches nobody ate.

Instead he crossed the cold floor and took the spot they'd saved, and when the round came around again, he opened his mouth and went down the stairs with them.

* * *

Here is what Theo would remember afterward, in the nights when remembering was the only thing left to do: that the numbers did not feel like counting. Counting was arithmetic, was waiting out a timeout in the corner of the gym, was the slow drip of a hospital clock. This was different. Each number was a place. Eighty-four was the smell of cut grass and the weight of a bat in his hands the summer his father taught him to swing level. Seventy-nine was the blue of the mug his mother drank tea from, the chip on its rim she refused to throw away. Sixty-one was his father's laugh, the real one, the ugly honking one he only let out at his own jokes.

The other children sang their own places into the numbers too — he could hear it, could hear that eighty-four meant something different coming from the red-haired boy on his left than it meant in his own throat, and somehow both meanings fit inside the note at once, the way a bell holds every echo it has ever made. That was the trick of the round, he began to understand. It wasn't a song. It was a container. You poured your dead into it and the dead got to keep singing.

Nobody told him any of this. Nobody explained. The girl with the braid — her name was Liesel, he learned later, though at the time she was only the flat hand smoothing the air — simply let the round come around to him again and again, and each time it arrived he was a little further down, a little deeper in, until he lost track of whether he was singing the numbers or the numbers were singing him.

His voice cracked once, on fifty-five, and nobody laughed. The red-haired boy reached over without breaking phrase and squeezed his wrist, twice, quick, like a pulse being taken, like a promise being made. *We heard that,* the squeeze said. *We all crack here. It's what the room is for.*

Theo had not cried at the funeral. He had not cried in the four months since, not once, because crying would have meant saying the thing out loud and saying it out loud would have meant his mother hearing it and his mother was already carrying enough — he could see her carrying it, see it in the way she stirred her coffee without drinking it, in the ledger she wrote in at midnight when she thought he was asleep. But somewhere around forty, in the warm dark under the choir loft, with nine voices holding him up like water holds a swimmer, Theo felt the tears come anyway, sliding hot down his face while his voice stayed steady on the count, and it was the easiest crying he had ever done, silent and painless, grief leaving him the way heat leaves a stone at evening: gradually, and then all at once, and then the stone is just cool, just a stone, just part of the garden.

When they finished — they never seemed to finish, exactly; the round simply grew quiet the way a fire does, settling into embers, into glow, into the memory of warmth — the children turned and looked at him with an expression he couldn't name yet. He would name it later. It was the look of people watching a guest drink from a well they all drank from, and hoping, genuinely hoping, that the water agreed with him.

"You've got a good low register," Liesel said. "Your grandfather had the same. We have records."

"I never met my grandfather."

"No," she said, and smiled again, and this time the smile had something underneath it, some patient sediment. "But the round met him. It keeps what it's given." She clapped her hands once, lightly, a teacher ending a lesson. "Same time tomorrow. Bring your voice. Leave everything else."

* * *

He came back the next day. And the next.

He didn't tell his mother, at first, not because it was secret exactly — the whole village seemed to know where he went, nodded at him in the lanes with small approving smiles, pressed extra bread into his hands at the bakery as if feeding a singer were a civic duty — but because he couldn't find the words. How do you tell your mother, who has spent four months watching you for signs of drowning, that you've found a room where the water is warm? How do you explain that the singing isn't sad even though it's all about going down, down, down, that descent can feel like being carried if enough voices are under you?

So he said "I made friends" and left it there, and watched relief move across her face like weather, and hated himself a little, and went down the stone passage anyway.

On the fourth day they taught him the harmony beneath the harmony — a drone, a single sustained tone that sat under the descending numbers like a floor under a staircase. "You hold the ground," Liesel told him, putting him at the center of the room, alone on his riser, the others arrayed around him in their crescents. "Everyone comes down to meet you. Whatever happens up in the count, you don't move. Can you do that? Can you be the thing that doesn't move?"

He thought of his mother, who had not moved, who had gotten up every morning for four months and made breakfast and driven him to school and written in her ledger and not moved, not once, not even when he knew — the way you know things about your parents without wanting to — that she wept in the shower with the fan running.

"Yeah," Theo said. "I can do that."

And he did. He held the drone while the numbers fell around him like leaves, like snow, like everything gentle that falls, and the room filled up with a sound so large it stopped being sound and became a kind of weather, a climate, a place you could live, and Theo understood — in the wordless way of the body, in the marrow — why the village looked the way it did. Why the people in the lanes had smooth faces and unhurried hands and eyes like still ponds. They had all, at some point in their long unaging lives, been the thing that doesn't move. They had all held the ground while the count came down to meet them.

It was the most beautiful thing he had ever been part of. That was the trap of it, though he didn't see the trap. Beautiful things don't announce their teeth.

* * *

His mother found him that evening flushed and glowing, coming up the passage with the other children, all of them talking over each other, and she stood at the head of the stairs with the lamplight behind her and something moved through her face so fast he almost missed it — a flicker, a flinch, a door opening onto a cold room and shutting again — before it settled into a smile.

"You sang today," she said.

"How'd you know?"

"You have the look. Your father had it after rehearsal." She touched his cheek with the backs of her fingers, checking the fever of happiness. "Was it good?"

"It was—" He searched and failed. "It was like being held by a lot of hands at once."

Her eyes went bright. "Then I'm glad," she said, and her voice was warm and steady and proud, and Theo believed her, because he wanted to, because she was his mother and mothers don't lie about being glad.

Except that later — much later, when knowing had changed the taste of everything — he would remember that her hand, checking his cheek for fever, had trembled. Just slightly. Just the way a hand trembles when it is gripping something very hard so that it will not reach out and snatch you back.

* * *

That night he slept the deep sleep of the truly spent, the sleep of a body that has given everything it has to a single sustained note. And in that sleep he dreamed — or half-dreamed, or drifted in the shallows between dreaming and something else — that he was still singing. The round went on without the room, without the risers, without Liesel's smoothing hand. The numbers descended through a darkness that was not frightening, that was in fact the most comfortable darkness imaginable, warm as a coat, and his voice went down them step by step, and the other voices went with him, dozens now, hundreds, generations of them, all descending together through the warm dark like a family walking downstairs to supper.

In the dream he was smiling.

He did not hear his mother come in. He did not feel her stand over his bed in the small hours, or see what her face did in the light of the lamp she held low so as not to wake him. He did not know that she bent close, listening, and went pale, and stood there a long time with her hand pressed flat against her own mouth.

Because he was humming in his sleep. Softly. Steadily. Descending.

Numbers, going down.

Fifty-two. Fifty-one. Fifty.

And in the morning, when he came down to breakfast pink-cheeked and hungry and happier than he had been in four months, his mother looked at him across the table with love in her eyes and something else underneath the love, something with teeth, and she said, "Theo, sweetheart. What numbers were you singing?"

And he opened his mouth to say *none, I don't remember, it was just a tune* — and found, to his surprise, that he knew exactly which numbers, all of them, every step of the staircase, right down to the one he'd woken on without waking.

"Fifty," he said. "I ended on fifty."

His mother set down her cup. Outside, far off across the green, in a church tower with a rope and no bell, something that was not a bell made a sound like a held breath finally being released.

"Mrs. Okafor ended on ninety-one," Marguerite Reyes said quietly, mostly to herself, mostly to the ledger in her eyes. "Mr. Brandt ended on sixty-seven. My patients ended wherever they ended, and then they—"

She stopped. She smiled at him, the beautiful lie of a smile, pride laid over fear like fresh paint over rot.

"Eat your eggs," she said. "You need your strength for rehearsal."

And Theo ate his eggs, warm and happy and loved, and did not ask her what happened after the patients ended, because some questions you save for the dark, and the dark was already, patiently, kindly, beginning to count.
