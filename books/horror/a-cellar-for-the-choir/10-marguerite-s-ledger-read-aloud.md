## 10. Marguerite's Ledger, Read Aloud

The cellar ledger was kept in a room off the sacristy that Anselm called, without irony, the archive. It was a narrow space with one high window, its glass so old and warped that the morning light came through it bent and honeyed, falling in a slanted column across a long oak table. Shelves lined the walls, and on the shelves sat ledger after ledger, bound in calf and canvas and, for the earliest years, in something Marguerite chose not to examine too closely. The spines were dated. 1911. 1912. 1913. They ran without a gap to the present, to the ledger that lay open on the table, its pages soft as cloth from a century of turning.

Anselm had left her alone with them.

"Read whatever you need to read," he had said, and there had been no reluctance in it, no guarding of the door. That was the thing she kept returning to, the thing that made her hands unsteady now as she drew her own ledger from her bag: he *wanted* her to see. He believed that what the pages held would look, to a nurse, like sense.

She laid her hospice ledger beside the village's open book. Her own was a cheap spiral-bound thing from a drugstore in her old life, its cover soft with handling, its edges furred. She had filled it over eleven months. She had told herself it was documentation, that a pattern observed and recorded was a pattern understood, that naming a thing drained it of its power to frighten. She had told herself it was a kind of prayer.

Now, with the cellar's ledger open under the honeyed light, she understood that she had been wrong about what she was praying to.

* * *

She started with her first patient. Mrs. Adele Brandt, eighty-seven, ovarian cancer, the woman who had taught Marguerite the shape of the thing without either of them knowing it. Marguerite had written the entry in her careful, upright hand, the hand she used for charting because charting had to survive being read by strangers:

*Patient counts aloud at irregular intervals. Numbers descending. Begins at 91. No distress associated with the counting; indeed the patient seems soothed by it, the way others are soothed by music. Ends at zero. At zero, patient reports "the bell." No bell audible on the ward. No bell on any recording. Patient died 14 March, 3:12 a.m., quietly, mid-exhale.*

She turned the pages of the cellar ledger back, back past the neat twentieth-century script, past the wartime entries where the ink went thin and the paper changed, back to 1911 itself, to the first book, the one whose cover was not leather and which she still refused to look at too long. The first entries were in a different hand — a verger's hand, older than Anselm, older than Anselm's verger before him — and the format was not the same. It was not charting. It was something between a parish register and a shipping manifest.

But the numbers were the same.

*Elias Wren, sexton. Count began at 91 on the third Sunday of Lent. Count concluded at zero on the eve of Lady Day. The bell rang at zero, below, where the voice was kept. He went down singing. The village was spared the fever that took the next town entire.*

Marguerite sat very still. The high window let its bent light down onto the two open books, hers and the village's, and for a moment she could not tell which pages were which.

She checked the dates. That was the professional in her, the part that had survived eleven years of hospice work by refusing to be moved until the facts were moved first. Elias Wren's count had begun in February 1911 and ended in March. Adele Brandt's count had begun in January of last year and ended on the fourteenth of March. One hundred and thirteen years apart. The same span. The same sixty-one days. The same descent from ninety-one to zero, number by number, at intervals that — she had charted this too, she had made little timelines in the margins like a woman plotting a fever — followed no medical logic whatsoever. Not the disease's progress. Not the medication schedule. Not the moon, the tides, the season. The intervals were irregular the way a melody is irregular, which is to say they were not irregular at all. They were composed.

She turned forward through the cellar ledger, decade by decade, and wrote the names in her own book as she found them, because her hands needed to do something or they would shake, and because — she admitted it now, in the privacy of the archive, where no one could hear her think — because writing it down had become the only way she knew to hold a thing.

*1943. Greta Ilsa Vogel. Count began at 91 in September. Refused the cellar. Count transferred to her sister, Anna Vogel. Anna concluded at zero on the Feast of All Souls. The village was spared the influenza.*

Ida. The old woman with the hands like folded paper, who had held Marguerite's face in the churchyard and said, *You're the one who writes it down.* Greta Ilsa Vogel. Ida had a sister once. Ida had refused, and the count had not stopped; it had simply changed its address.

Marguerite's pen hovered. Then she wrote, beneath the entry, in the margin where she kept her fever-plots: *It doesn't end. It moves.*

* * *

The morning went on without her. She was dimly aware of the village beyond the high window — the sound of the festival being prepared, hammering on the bandstand, children's voices running through the green, the smell of bread from the bakery that reached even here, into the archive, sweet and warm and utterly at odds with the room it had entered. Once, distantly, she heard the choir children singing their round, the wordless descending numbers, and she put her hands flat on the table until it stopped.

She worked through the ledger the way she had once worked through a difficult charting audit: methodically, patient by patient, name by name. And the pattern, once she saw it, was so simple and so total that she actually laughed — a single syllable of sound that the archive swallowed whole, that the bent glass and the old wood took in and gave back nothing of.

The counting was not local.

She had assumed — she saw now how completely she had assumed it — that the count was a thing of Ashen Hollow, a village superstition, a covenant with a radius. The bell had been melted in 1911, but its voice had been kept below, and the voice rang for the village's own, and the named went down into the cellar, and the arithmetic of grief stayed balanced within these hills. A closed system. A terrible little machine, but contained.

Except that Adele Brandt had never been to Ashen Hollow. Adele Brandt had been born in Sacramento and died in a hospice ward four hundred miles from these hills, and she had counted down from ninety-one in March of last year exactly as Elias Wren had counted down from ninety-one in March of 1911.

Marguerite turned the pages faster now. The village ledger recorded its own tithed, its own named, its own concluded counts — but between them, in the margins, in later hands, there were annotations. The verger's hand again, a later verger, 1958:

*The voice travels. It rang this year for a woman in the city where the Wren family settled. The count was heard there, not here. We learned of it by letter. The bell does not know distance. The bell knows names.*

And again, 1976, a different ink:

*The Hollow is not the bell's parish. The named are its parish. Wherever they lie, the count is heard. We keep the ledger because the ledger is the only map of where the voice has gone.*

Marguerite closed her eyes. She saw her hospice ward: the soft overhead lights, the machines that recorded everything and had never once recorded the bell. She saw Mrs. Brandt's lips moving in the green hour before dawn, shaping numbers no one had taught her. She saw the other three — because there had been four, there had been four before Theo, and she had written them all down — Mr. Okafor counting from sixty-four, the young man with the glioblastoma counting from thirty-eight, old Tomás counting from seventy-seven, each of them descending at their own composed tempo, each of them ending at zero with a sound like a struck bell that existed nowhere in the building and everywhere in the room.

They were not from here. None of them were from here. But somewhere in each of their pasts — she was certain of it now, certain the way you are certain of a diagnosis you can't yet prove — somewhere there was Ashen Hollow. A grandmother. A grandfather who left. A family name that had once been on one of these pages. The bell had been melted, but its voice had not been buried; it had been *disbursed*, scattered through a century of departures, and it rang for its own wherever they had gone, and the count followed them like a debt follows a name.

The village had not survived by hiding the bell below. The village had survived because the bell's parish was scattered so thin that the tithe came here only rarely — and the ledger, the century of ledgers, was the record of a debt being collected across a whole country, a whole century, one name at a time.

* * *

It was when she went back to her own ledger — to check a date, to check the interval between Mr. Okafor's sixty-four and his zero — that she saw it.

Her own handwriting. Page after page of it. Dates, numbers, intervals, margins full of her little fever-plots. She had written down when each count began. She had written down every number she had heard, in order, with timestamps. She had written down the sound at zero, describing it again and again, trying to get it right — *like a struck bell, like a struck bell heard through water, like a struck bell inside the ear* — refining her description the way you refine anything you care about, the way you polish a prayer.

And she had carried it here. She had carried it across the hills, through the fog, into the archive, and laid it on the table beside the cellar's book, under the bent honeyed light, and opened it, and read from it, aloud, in her own voice, the names and the numbers.

The archive was very quiet. The high window let down its column of light. Dust moved in it, slowly, the way dust moves when the air itself is paying attention.

Marguerite thought: *a ledger is a map.*

She thought: *I have spent eleven months drawing a map.*

Every timestamp, every interval, every number she had faithfully recorded — she had not been documenting a pattern. She had been *completing* one. The cellar's ledger knew where the voice had been. Hers knew where it was, and how fast it was moving, and — she turned the pages with hands that no longer shook, that had gone past shaking into something colder — and where it was going. Four patients. Four counts. And then the letter had come, the letter asking her to come for *the one who counts*, and she had assumed the village knew about Theo because the bell knew about Theo, because the bell had rung for him here.

But what if the bell had rung for him in Sacramento first? What if it had been ringing for him for weeks — Theo, who had been sleeping badly since his father died, Theo, who had been humming under his breath at breakfast without knowing he was doing it — and what if the letter had come not because the village heard the bell, but because the village had *read her ledger*? No. That was madness. No one had read her ledger. She had kept it in the nightstand drawer, in the house, alone.

Alone except for Theo, who had seen her writing at night. Who had asked her once, in the gentle voice he had developed this year, the voice of a child taking care of his mother, *What are you writing, Mama?* And she had said, *A prayer,* because she had thought it was a joke, because she had thought it was true in the safe way.

The ledger lay open between the two older books. Her handwriting, upright and careful, made to survive being read by strangers. Made to be read. She had told herself documentation was a kind of prayer, and she had been right, and she had been wrong in the way that mattered: prayers are addressed to someone. Every entry, every polished description of the bell, every faithful timestamp — she had written them *to* something, and the something had read every word, and the something had used them, and the map she had drawn with the best part of herself, the part that refused to let a patient count alone, that part had been the finger moving across the map, and it had stopped, at the end, on a small town in the hills, and on a boy.

The dust turned in the column of light. Far off, muffled by the archive walls, the village went on preparing its festival, and the children sang, and the bread smelled sweet.

Marguerite picked up her pen. Her hand was steady now, the terrible steady of a nurse with a diagnosis. She opened her ledger to the first page — to Adele Brandt, to ninety-one, to the beginning — and beneath the entry she wrote, in the same upright hand, the hand made to survive being read:

*This book was not a record. This book was an invitation. I am the one who read it aloud.*

She looked at what she had written, and she understood that she could not unwrite it, and that she did not want to. Because there was one thing the cellar ledger had shown her, one thing buried in the margins of 1943, in the thin wartime ink beside the name of Greta Ilsa Vogel, a line she had read and not yet understood:

*The count obeys the voice that carries it. It has never once been carried by love.*

The ink was old. The words were certain. And Marguerite sat in the bent light with her map spread open on the table, and began, very quietly, to wonder what would happen if it were.

Outside, the festival hammering stopped. And in the silence that followed, from the church above the archive, from the loft where no bell hung, came a single sound — soft, expectant, patient as a page waiting to be turned — like something being struck, once, far away, through water, inside the ear.
