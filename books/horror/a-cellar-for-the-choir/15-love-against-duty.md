## 15. Love Against Duty

The rectory room they had given Theo smelled of beeswax and dried lavender, as if someone had tried very hard to make a sickroom out of a place that was not sick at all. Marguerite sat in the chair beside his bed with her hands folded in her lap and her ledger closed on her knees, and she watched the late afternoon light come through the small window in bars of amber, laying itself across the quilt like something being measured out.

Theo was not dying. That was the thing she kept returning to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. She had spent eleven years sitting with the dying, and she knew the geography of it — the way the body begins to speak a different language, the skin going translucent at the ears, the breath developing its own tide. Theo had none of that. He had color in his cheeks. He had an appetite; he had eaten two of Ida's seed cakes at lunch and asked for a third. He was, by every measure she owned, a healthy boy of twelve who happened to be hearing numbers in the dark.

And yet the room had been prepared like a sickroom anyway. There was a carafe of water with a cloth draped over it. There was a basin on the washstand. Someone — Anselm, she supposed, or one of the verger's quiet helpers — had set a sprig of rosemary in a glass by the bed, and rosemary was for remembrance, and remembrance was the last thing this village needed more of.

"Mum." Theo's voice came from somewhere inside the pillow. "You're doing the staring thing."

"I'm allowed to do the staring thing. I'm your mother."

"You're doing the *worried* staring thing. It's different. Dad used to do it when you drove."

Marguerite smiled because he had offered her the smile, the way children do — handing their parents a small ordinary moment to hold so that everyone can pretend the larger thing is not in the room. "Your father did not worry about my driving. Your father worried about everything else and left driving entirely to God."

"Yeah," Theo said, and then, quieter, into the pillow: "Yeah, he did."

The light moved. Somewhere below them, muffled through floorboards and stone, the choir children were practicing, but today they were not singing the round. Today it was an ordinary hymn, something with words about green pastures, and the ordinariness of it was worse than the numbers had been. It meant they could turn it off. It meant the numbers were a choice they made, the way you choose which clothes to wear.

"Mum."

"Hm?"

"Promise me you won't do anything stupid."

She looked at him. He had turned his face toward her now, cheek creased from the pillow, eyes clear and gray-green like his father's, and there was no childishness in the look at all. He had been watching her the way she watched patients. He had learned it from her, she realized — the professional stillness, the reading of hands and breathing. Her son had been studying hospice nursing at her elbow for years without either of them naming it, and now he was using it on her, and he was good at it.

"What makes you think I'd do anything stupid?" she asked.

"Because you've got your thinking face on. The one where you've already decided and you're just working out how to tell people after." He sat up against the headboard, dragging the quilt with him, and faced her squarely. "Ida talked to you yesterday. You were in the churchyard for over an hour. And since then you keep going somewhere else in the middle of sentences, and you keep humming."

"I don't hum."

"You do. Numbers. Under your breath. Sixty-something, sixty-something." His voice didn't shake, and she understood suddenly what it cost him, that steadiness — a twelve-year-old holding himself level the way she held herself level over the dying, because one of them had to and he had decided it would be him. "I know what Ida told you. I found my page in the ledger, Mum. My name's already inked. There's just a blank where the number goes." He swallowed. "So whatever you're planning — and you're planning, don't tell me you're not — promise me you won't do anything stupid."

And here was the moment. Here was the narrow gate of it, and Marguerite Reyes, who had never once lied to her son about anything that mattered — who had told him the truth about his father's diagnosis on the second day, who had let him see the body because she believed children deserved the truth about death — heard herself open her mouth and lie.

"I promise," she said, "that I will not do anything stupid."

It came out steady. It came out warm. She even smiled, and the smile was true in every way except the one that counted, because what she intended was not stupid. What she intended was the single most careful thing she had ever done in her life. Stupidity was walking into the cellar and shouting. Stupidity was running at the road again until it looped them home. What she intended required precision, and rehearsal, and a mother's oldest skill, which is the performance of calm while the interior of you burns like a paper lantern.

Theo studied her for a long moment. Then he lay back down, apparently satisfied, and that was the worst part — that he believed her, that her lie had landed clean, that eleven years of honesty had built exactly enough credit to spend on this.

*Forgive me,* she thought, not to God particularly, but to the boy. *I'll ask forgiveness after. I'll have time. One of us will.*

* * *

That night she did not sleep, and she did not pretend to try.

She waited until the house settled — Anselm's footsteps crossing below, the last lamp going out, the village folding itself into that particular silence that was not silence at all but a held breath — and then she took her ledger to the window seat and opened it to the pages she had copied in secret over three nights, her handwriting disguised as her own shorthand so that even if Anselm read it, it would look like notes on hymnals.

Sixty-four.

That was where Theo's count stood. Sixty-four, begun nine nights ago at midnight, descending one number per night with the terrible patience of all things in Ashen Hollow. Fifty-five tomorrow. Fifty-four the night after. She had done the arithmetic four times and it always came out the same: zero fell on the eve of the feast, the night the whole village would come singing down into the cellar with candles and folded robes and faces full of love.

Unless the count changed hands.

Ida's voice came back to her across the churchyard grass, thin and dry as a pressed flower: *The covenant has one flaw, girl. It was written by frightened people, and frightened people write rules that love can slip through. The count can be sung aloud by someone who loves the named. Sung as their own. The bell doesn't care whose mouth the number comes out of. It only cares that it's sung.* And then Ida had looked at her with eighty years of guilt stacked behind her eyes and said the rest: *But understand what you'd be taking. The bell keeps what it's given. It doesn't give back.*

So she rehearsed.

She began under her breath, barely voicing it, the way she had once learned hymns as a girl in her grandmother's church — syllable by syllable, line by line, until the music lived in the body instead of the mind and could be called up in the dark without thinking. That was the trick of it, she understood now. The choir children hadn't been teaching Theo a song. They'd been installing something in him, note by note, so that when the bell rang he would sing whether he chose to or not. The count wanted to be sung. It had been trying to be sung through her patients for years — those whispering countdowns in the hospice dark, ninety-one, ninety, eighty-nine — and she had knelt beside it night after night and written it down, and her writing had been a kind of listening, and her listening had been a kind of consent.

Never again. If the count must be sung, it would be sung by her, on purpose, in defiance, as an act of theft.

"Sixty-four," she breathed. "Sixty-three. Sixty-two."

The numbers fit strangely in her mouth at first, the way any new prayer does, foreign and angular. But she had been learning descending patterns all her life. Vespers. Compline. The evening offices that walk the soul down into sleep one step at a time. This was only another office, a black one, a psalter with no mercy in it, and she was its most devoted student.

"Sixty-one. Sixty. Fifty-nine."

Outside the window, the fog had come up the hill from the hollow, and the streetlamps of the village burned inside it like coins at the bottom of milk. She could see the church tower, or the idea of it — the tower that held a rope and no bell, the rope that swayed sometimes in windless air, that Anselm waxed and polished with the tenderness other men gave to relics. Somewhere under the choir loft, sixty niches held sixty robes, and each robe held a name, and each name held a final number, and the whole architecture of it was nothing but a machine for making grief orderly. One grief instead of many. Anselm believed it was mercy. She had seen the belief in him, clean and shining, and she understood that she was not fighting a wicked man. She was fighting a reasonable one, and reasonableness was the most durable horror she knew.

"Fifty-eight," she whispered. "Fifty-seven."

Her voice was finding the tune now. That was the frightening part — how quickly it found it, how naturally the numbers wanted to fall, how her breath began to arrange itself around them without instruction. She made herself stop at fifty-six and sit with her hands flat on her knees, feeling her pulse, counting that instead. Forty-eight beats to the minute. A resting heart. A nurse's heart, trained slow.

She thought about Daniel. Not the polished grief of him, not the photograph on the mantel, but the man — his cold feet in bed, his habit of reading aloud whatever sentence struck him, his hand on the back of Theo's neck the way you steady a young tree. He had come from this place. His blood was in the boy asleep ten feet away, and maybe that was why the count had reached across a hundred miles and a hundred years to find Theo in his bed in the city, whispering numbers into his ear weeks before any letter came. The bell had been ringing for him all along. The letter hadn't summoned them. The letter had simply been the village catching up to its own hunger.

*I brought him here,* she thought, and let the guilt have its full weight, because she refused to flinch from it. *I put him in the car myself.* And then, because guilt was a luxury she could not carry into tomorrow: *And I will take him out again. Whatever it costs. Whoever it costs.*

She knew what whoever meant. She had known since the churchyard. The exchange rate Ida had described was simple and ruinous: one name for one name, a loving voice for a beloved life. Sing the count as your own and the bell takes you instead — or perhaps not instead. Perhaps additionally. Ida hadn't known. No one had ever tried it, in a hundred years of frightened, reasonable people. She might sing all sixty-four numbers and watch Theo walk free and then find the bell indifferent, greedy, taking both of them. Or she might sing and find the ledger opening a fresh page with her name already inked, the final number blank, waiting.

Either way, Theo lived. That was the fixed point she had chosen, the star she was steering by. Everything else was weather.

* * *

Near three in the morning she went to his bedside, because she could not help it, because she was weak in exactly the way she had decided not to be and strong in exactly the way that didn't matter.

He slept the way children sleep in strange houses — deeply but defensively, one arm curled near his chest, the rosemary sprig knocked sideways in its glass. In the bluish dark he looked younger than twelve, younger than the boy who had stolen glances at the ledger, and her chest did the thing it did, the old ache, the one that had started the day he was born and had never fully resolved.

She touched his hair. He stirred but didn't wake.

And then, softly, so softly it was hardly sound at all, she sang. Not the count — not yet, not here, though the numbers sat ready behind her teeth like coins held under the tongue. She sang the hymn the children had practiced that afternoon, the one about green pastures, the ordinary one, and she sang it as a lullaby, the way she had sung to him in the fever year, the way mothers have sung to children since before anyone thought to melt bells down for wars.

"He maketh me to lie down in green pastures," she breathed. "He leadeth me beside the still waters."

Theo sighed in his sleep and turned toward her voice, following it the way he had followed it as an infant, and for one suspended moment the room was only a room, the village only a village, the numbers only numbers, and a mother was singing over her sleeping child in the dark because that was the oldest covenant there was, older than 1911, older than any bell, and it had never once required a ledger.

Then, below the floorboards, faint as a memory of a memory, something shifted. Not a toll. Not yet. Just the rope in the tower stirring in windless air, a soft creak of old wood remembering its purpose, and Marguerite stopped singing with the last word still warm in her throat.

Tomorrow the village would begin preparing the feast. She had seen it in Ida's telling and in Anselm's gentle, sorrowful eyes: the tables carried out to the green, the loaves and the honey, the children given ribbons, the whole village pouring its terrible joy over the named boy like oil. They would dress him in a choir robe. They would love him openly, generously, with the genuine love of people who had survived a century by loving exactly this way — and she would have to stand among them and smile and eat bread with hands that already knew what they intended to do.

She bent and kissed Theo's forehead. His skin was warm. His breath came slow and even, forty-eight beats to the minute, a resting heart, a living boy.

"Fifty-six," she whispered against his hair — not the count, not quite, only the number, only a coin held safe — "and I will learn every one of them before they can take you."

Below the floorboards, the rope creaked once more, patient as bookkeeping, and waited for morning.
