## 2. Documentation Is a Kind of Prayer

The morning after the tavern, Yara Voss woke in the Kessler house with the particular clarity of a man who has found his thesis. It lay in him like a swallowed stone: *the refusal to repeat*. He had spent eleven years collecting songs across three countries — lullabies in Karelia, work chants on the Baltic coast, a funeral lament from an old woman who had wept while she sang and then thanked him for asking — and never once had he encountered a tradition built on prohibition rather than performance. Folklore was repetition by its very nature; a song existed only insofar as it could be sung again. A song that could not be sung twice was not a song at all. It was something else. Something closer to a wound.

He set up his equipment at the kitchen table of the Kessler house: the Nagra recorder with its reels, the shotgun microphone wrapped in wool against damp, his notebooks arranged in a fan. The house around him creaked and settled like an old animal finding its feet. Mrs. Kessler — Greta, she had said, though she pronounced it as though apologizing for it — had left bread and hard cheese on the counter and a note in careful, schoolroom script: *The cellar light is temperamental. Best not to go down.* He had read the note twice without thinking about it, filed it under landlordly eccentricity, and turned to his work.

By nine he was walking the village.

Hessel revealed itself slowly in the morning light, the way a face reveals itself when you stop trying to read it. It was not picturesque, exactly. It was *composed* — every crooked lane and leaning fence seeming to have been placed by someone with strong opinions about where things should stand. The fields rose around the village in low green terraces, and even to his untrained eye they were wrong. Too green for late September. Too thick-stalked, too heavy-headed. The grain bent under its own abundance as if ashamed of it.

His first interview was with a beekeeper named Tomas Reil, sixty or so, hands scarred into leather by fifty years of stings. Yara had chosen him deliberately: beekeepers sang to their hives in most of the old traditions, and a man who already talked to insects might be less guarded about talking to a stranger.

Tomas received him in his apiary yard, among the white boxes, and listened to Yara's explanation — folklorist, university, harvest songs, documentation — with the flat patience of a man waiting for a dog to finish barking.

"I don't sing," he said.

"Everyone sings something," Yara said, smiling. "Even if it's only to the bees."

"The bees get words," Tomas said. "Not songs." And then, because Yara did not leave, because Yara stood there with his notebook open like a small hungry mouth, the old man relented in the way that men do — grudgingly, looking away, giving less than he pretended. He recited, in a voice like gravel poured slow, four lines of a field blessing. Not sung. Spoken, deliberately flattened, the melody amputated before it could begin.

Yara wrote them down phonetically, asked if he could hear them again.

Tomas looked at him then — really looked, the first time — and there was something in his face that Yara would spend the rest of the day trying to name. Not anger. Not superstition's dull fear. It was closer to the look of a man watching a child reach toward a stove, calculating how much time remained before the touch.

"You heard them once," Tomas said. "That's what hearing is for."

He went back to his hives. The interview was over.

* * *

By noon Yara had collected refusals from seven people and fragments from four. He learned the shape of the pattern quickly, the way one learns the grammar of a new language: some villagers would speak a verse but never sing it; some would sing but stop at arbitrary points, mid-phrase, as though the song were a rope they dared not follow to its end; two refused everything, one woman pulling her door shut with such quiet firmness that Yara felt the click of the latch in his chest for minutes afterward.

And always, everywhere, the same rule surfaced without anyone stating it outright. When he asked a man named Peder Ilves — no relation to Marta, he insisted too quickly — to repeat a threshing verse so the microphone could catch it cleanly, the whole room changed temperature. Peder's wife stopped knitting. His son, maybe twelve, looked at his father with wide, appalled eyes.

"We don't do it twice," Peder said. His voice was steady. His hands were not.

"It's just for the recording," Yara said. "For accuracy. So I can be sure I've got it right."

"You've got it right," the boy said, and then flinched as though the words had been taken out of his mouth by someone else.

Yara walked back through the village center with his recorder bag heavy on his shoulder and his mind working the problem like a tongue working a loose tooth. Prohibitions in folklore were common enough — you didn't whistle in a mine, you didn't sing certain songs after dark, you didn't name the dead on certain days. But those prohibitions governed *context*. This was different. In Hessel the prohibition governed the act itself. Repetition was not unlucky here. Repetition was, apparently, impossible to permit.

Which meant the song was not being preserved. It was being *spent*. Each singing a coin removed from circulation, and no one willing to mint another.

At the tavern that evening he tried Marta Ilves again. She was wiping down the bar, and when she saw him come in with his notebook she did not pretend not to see it.

"One question," Yara said. "Just one. When you stopped last night — mid-line — what comes next?"

Marta set down her cloth. She was a handsome woman running to gray, with the kind of face that had decided long ago exactly how much it would show. She studied him for a long moment.

"You're staying at the Kessler house," she said. It wasn't a question either.

"Greta and Aldo have been very hospitable."

"Yes," Marta said. "They would be." She picked up her cloth again, wrung it, laid it flat. "Go home, Mr. Voss. Write your book about somewhere else. There are forty villages within a day's walk that will sing you their whole repertoire twice over and ask for more."

"That's not an answer to my question."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

She turned away, and Yara understood that pressing further, here, now, in front of the three silent drinkers at the corner table, would cost him more than it bought. He ordered a beer he didn't want and drank half of it, and as he left he felt the eyes of the room on his back like weather.

* * *

The transcription took him deep into the night.

This was the part of the work he loved best — loved with an intensity he'd never confessed to colleagues, who found the archival labor tedious. Sitting alone with the reel-to-reel, headphones on, playing a fragment of song over and over, coaxing the notation out of the sound line by line. It was, he had written once in a grant application he was mildly ashamed of, *a kind of prayer*: the patient, repeated attention to something fragile and vanishing, the belief that writing it down was a way of keeping it alive. Documentation as devotion. The ethnographer as a kind of secular priest, tending the flame of what the modern world wanted to let gutter out.

He worked at the kitchen table under a single bulb. Outside, Hessel lay dark and windless. Inside, the house ticked and murmured its old-house murmurs, and Yara transcribed.

The fragments came together strangely. That was the first thing he noticed, somewhere past midnight, pencil hovering over the staff paper. Individually the verses were unremarkable — harvest blessings, threshing rhythms, a lullaby-adjacent tune about the grain going home. But they shared melodic material in a way that suggested they were not separate songs at all. They were *movements*. Fragments of one long composition, distributed across the village like pieces of a letter divided among people who each believed they held only their own page.

He began laying them side by side. The beekeeper's spoken blessing fit, tonally, against a scrap he'd gotten from a woman at the well — sung, she'd claimed, by her grandmother, and only ever indoors. A child's skipping rhyme, collected almost apologetically from a girl of eight whose mother appeared within ninety seconds and took the girl inside without a word to Yara at all — that rhyme carried the same cadence, transposed up a fourth.

Someone had built a song too large to sing in any one house. Or — the thought arrived cold and unwelcome — someone had *broken* one, deliberately, and scattered the pieces.

It was near two in the morning when he noticed the other thing.

He had filled six pages. He flipped back to review the evening's work, checking his phonetics, and stopped. At the bottom of the third page, in his own handwriting, in his own pencil, there was a line he had no memory of writing.

It was not a lyric. It was not notation. It was a single sentence, in English, in his hand:

*who is reading this*

Yara sat very still. The bulb hummed. The house ticked.

He examined the page under the lamp, tilting it, hunting for erasure marks, for evidence of a joke, of exhaustion, of some fugue state of overwork. The handwriting was his — the slight rightward slant, the way he crossed his t's low. But the pressure was wrong. Lighter. As though the pencil had been held by someone imitating his grip, or as though he himself had been somewhere else when the words came out.

He was not a fanciful man. He told himself this the way one tells oneself anything at two in the morning: firmly, and without complete conviction. Fatigue. Automatism. Ethnographers' folklore was full of such stories — collectors who dreamed their material, who found verses in their notebooks they couldn't remember gathering. The mind, saturated, leaked.

He closed the notebook. Then, after a moment, opened it again, because closing it felt like conceding something.

The sentence was still there. Of course it was. Where else would it go?

* * *

Sleep came late and thin. He dreamed of water in a stone channel, of counting — endless tallying, one mark and another mark, the scratch of it — and woke at dawn with the numbers still moving behind his eyes.

But it was the ledger that occupied the daylight hours.

Greta Kessler had mentioned it offhandedly the previous evening, when Yara asked whether the village kept records — parish registers, harvest accounts, anything of the sort. "There are old books in the front room," she'd said, in the tone of a woman pointing out a cupboard she wished didn't exist. "Aldo keeps the key."

The key, it turned out, was in the lock. Whether Aldo had forgotten it or simply decided that a folklorist poking through parish ledgers was beneath concern, Yara never determined. He let himself into the front room — a parlor frozen sometime in the previous century, furniture under dust sheets like a room holding its breath — and found the shelf of record books behind the escritoire.

Most of it was mundane and beautiful in the way archives are beautiful: baptismal rolls in faded iron-gall ink, tithe accounts, a shepherd's wage book from the 1930s with marginal drawings of sheep that grew progressively more cartoonish as the winters passed. Yara worked through them with cotton gloves and mounting excitement, photographing pages, building the skeleton of Hessel's demographic history. Founding families: Kessler, Ilves, Reil, Brandt, Oja, Voss—

He stopped.

Voss.

There it was in the 1950s register, in a list of communicants. *Voss*. His own surname, rare enough in the world that seeing it here landed in him like a dropped stone hitting water. An emigrant family, perhaps. A coincidence of spelling. He noted the page number and made himself move on, though his pulse had picked up a beat he chose not to examine.

Then he reached the ledger for 1974.

It was a standard parish account book, cloth-bound, its spine cracked. The harvest year ran through it in the usual columns — yields, tithes, disbursements — and the handwriting was the same careful clerical hand as every other year. Except.

Except that the entries for autumn 1974 were wrong. Not missing — *wrong*. The yields recorded were impossible. Wheat returns triple the ten-year average. Rye figures that would have shamed the rich plains a hundred miles south. The clerk had written the numbers in the same even hand, but Yara, who had spent years learning to read the hesitations in old documents, could see the tremor underneath: letters formed slightly too carefully, the pen pressed harder than it needed to be, a man writing what he was told to write and pressing down as if the extra force could make it true.

And between the October and November pages, the binding told its own story. Six leaves had been cut out. Not torn — razored, close to the spine, by someone patient and unhurried. The stubs remained, a pale band of absent paper, and on the stub facing November's first entry, a ghost of transfer: the mirror-image impression of a few lines of text, ink pressed through from the page that had once lain against it.

Yara photographed the stubs from four angles. Then he fetched the lamp closer and read the ghost backwards, decoding the reversed impressions letter by letter, lips moving, heart climbing.

He got perhaps thirty words. Most were accounting terms — *received*, *agreed*, *in perpetuity* — and one date fragment that might have been the twenty-ninth of September. Equinox season. And one phrase, legible entire, that stopped his breath:

*—as was covenanted by the six houses, the debt being—*

The debt being what? The rest was lost, sliced away with the page.

Yara sat back in the dusty parlor, gloves on, camera warm in his hands, and felt the archivist's thrill curdle into something colder. Documents lied all the time; that was half of what studying them meant. But documents *showed* their lying — the erasure, the forgery, the awkward patch. This was different. Someone had cut these pages out fifty years ago and the book had sat on this shelf ever since, in this house, and everyone in the village knew — the way they knew not to sing twice — and no one had burned the ledger. Burning it would have been easier. Burning it would have ended the argument. They had left the wound open and bound, a book with a hole in it, because apparently in Hessel even the destruction of records followed a rule he didn't yet understand.

Or because whatever the pages had contained could not be destroyed by cutting them out. Only postponed.

He packed up his materials carefully, returned the key to the lock precisely as he'd found it, and stood a moment in the shuttered parlor listening to the house. The dust sheets stirred faintly in a draft that had no source he could find.

* * *

That night the transcription resumed, and the feeling he had been outrunning since the tavern finally caught him.

He worked through the fragments again, cross-referencing, and the composite song grew on his pages — verse fitted to verse, the distributed composition assembling itself under his pencil like a body being stitched together. And as it grew, the sensation strengthened: that he was not alone with the work. That the silence in the kitchen had texture and attention. That the words were arriving on the page slightly ahead of his deciding them, the way a reflection arrives —

No. The way a reflection arrives *late*. The thought surfaced whole and unwelcome: *the way a reflection arrives late*, and he did not know why he thought it, because nothing this morning had been late, the mirrors had been ordinary mirrors, and yet the phrase sat in his mind like a splinter, pre-loaded, waiting for a meaning he hadn't earned yet.

He wrote until three. When he finally capped the pen and stacked the pages, he counted them out of habit — an archivist's tic, inventory as comfort.

Seven pages. He had written six.

The seventh lay beneath the stack, face down. He turned it over. Across the top, in that lighter version of his own hand, in English, the same sentence as before — but longer now, extended, patient, like someone who had all the time in the world and wanted him to know it:

*who is reading this*

*you are*

*good*

Yara Voss sat in the pool of lamplight in the dead hour of the Kessler house, and did not sleep again. Somewhere below him — below the kitchen, below the floorboards, below the cellar Greta had warned him about in her schoolroom script — he thought he heard, very faintly, a sound like a fingernail drawing a line. One mark. Another. Another.

Counting.
