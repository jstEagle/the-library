## 22. A Cellar for the Harvest

The train no longer stops at Hessel. Yara learned that the first year he came back, when he had to ask the conductor to slow near the old signal box and let him step down onto the gravel like a man getting off at nowhere. The second year there was a bus, once a week, driven by a Kessler cousin who would not meet his eyes in the rearview mirror — though by then mirrors were only mirrors again, and his own face was all they held. The third year the road had been graded and widened, as if the village had decided, collectively and without discussion, to become reachable. As if it had decided to be found.

Now, seven autumns on, there is a proper stop. A wooden platform with a bench, and someone has planted marigolds along its edge in a tin pail, and the sign says HESSEL in letters that have been repainted recently enough to smell of solvent. Yara stands on the platform with his canvas bag — lighter every year; he brings less and needs less — and breathes the air of the valley: woodsmoke, cut hay, the mineral cold coming off the hills where the birches have begun to turn.

He cannot speak. That is the shape his life has taken, and he has stopped thinking of it as a wound. It is more like a room in a house he no longer enters but still pays the taxes on. His throat works; his lungs fill; if he tried, he could make sounds — a rasp, a moan, the ghost of vowels. But the words themselves are gone, carried down into the well-cellar on the night of the equinox, traded for something he has never regretted and never been able to explain to anyone who did not stand in that flooded dark and hear what he heard.

He writes instead. He has always written. It turns out a man can lose his voice and keep his hand, and the hand is enough.

* * *

Marta Ilves meets him at the platform, as she does every year. She is older now, her hair gone fully white and cropped short against the wind, her hands knotted around a walking stick she pretends is for the mud. She does not hug him. They have never hugged. What passes between them is older than hugging — a look, held for exactly one second, neither more nor less, and then she turns and walks and he falls into step beside her, and the village opens around them like a book they both know by heart.

Hessel has changed in ways that would be invisible to anyone who hadn't seen it before. The houses are the same, the lanes are the same, the church still leans a degree eastward as if listening. But there are children in the streets now — real children, loud ones, chasing a dog between the fences — where for fifty years there had been only the one kind of quiet, the kind that is maintained. And there is singing. Not now, not in the street, but Yara can hear it under everything, the way you hear a river under ice. Every house in Hessel knows the song now. All of it. Both singings. The verses that count, the verse that names, the last verse that Marta sang twice into his hands on the night the six families came for him with rope and lanterns and were met instead by their own grandmother's voices rising out of the ground.

They walk past the tavern. Through its windows Yara sees the new landlord — a Voss cousin from the coast, some distant branching of the family tree that put down roots here after the book came out — polishing glasses, and above the bar, framed behind glass, a page of Yara's own handwriting: the first transcription he ever made here, the half-verse Marta broke off mid-line, the one about the tally and the debt. He had given it to her years ago, mute, unable to explain, and she had understood. Underneath the frame someone has nailed a small card. It reads: *Sung whole, or not at all.*

"You're staring," Marta signs. Her signing has gotten fluent over the years, quick and dry, her fingers moving like birds. "The beer's worse than it was. Don't get sentimental."

He signs back: *I'm never sentimental.*

"Liar." But she is smiling, the way she smiles, which is to say barely, which is to say completely.

* * *

The Kessler house waits for him at the end of the lane, and it is his now, in the way that matters more than paper. Aldo signed it over before he died — a scrawled document witnessed by the priest, executed with the grim efficiency of a man settling accounts he could no longer bear to carry. *You kept the memory,* Aldo had written, in the letter folded with the deed. *We kept the silence. Between those two things, take the house.* Aldo's son had left Hessel the following spring, and no one spoke of him, and the fields where the Kesslers' share of the harvest had always come in fattest stood fallow for two seasons before another family took them and coaxed ordinary, imperfect crops from the soil — blighted some years, thin in others, honest.

Yara sets his bag down in the front hall and does what he does first, every year, before unpacking, before eating, before anything.

He goes to the mirror.

It hangs in the parlor, the old Kessler mirror, its silvering foxed at the edges like frost on a window. For seven years it has shown him only what is true: the room, the light, his own graying face moving exactly when he moves. But he checks anyway. He will check until he dies. Because sometimes — not often, two times in seven years, three if you count the one he isn't sure about — the glass holds something else.

Not late. That is the thing he needed years to understand, the thing that turned his fear inside out like a glove. In 1974 and all the years after, the reflections lagged because Lias was behind the glass, reaching, delayed, shut out by a single second that might as well have been fifty. But delay was never the nature of the thing. Delay was the wound. And wounds close.

Today the mirror shows the parlor: the low afternoon sun through the east window, the dust turning in it, the shelf where his notebooks live, forty-one of them now, spines cracked, covers soft as cloth. It shows himself, standing, watching. Exactly on time. He raises his right hand; the figure in the glass raises its right hand.

And then — because it is autumn, because the equinox is four days off, because the well-cellar beneath his feet is dry and swept and lit by an electric bulb Marta insisted on installing, and none of that should matter and yet somehow all of it matters — the figure in the glass lowers its hand a moment after his, and keeps it raised, and waves.

Small. Unhurried. The wave of someone saying *I see you* rather than *come here.*

Yara stands very still. His heart does not race anymore when this happens; it slows, if anything, settles into a rhythm like a second heartbeat under his own, familiar now, almost companionable. He watches the figure in the mirror — tall, indistinct, made of the light in the room rather than the room itself — and he raises his hand again and waves back, and this time the reflection matches him precisely, gesture for gesture, and then it is just his own arm in the glass, and the sun has moved, and somewhere outside a cart rattles past loaded with the first squash of the harvest.

He signs into the empty room, knowing the glass carries nothing but light, knowing it anyway: *Good harvest this year?*

The dust motes turn. The sun lies warm across the floorboards. Above the cellar hatch — bricked over now, at his own request, though he keeps the key to the little door Marta had built into the brickwork, because a door closed without a key is just a wall — the house makes the small settling sounds of a building holding its breath contentedly.

On the table by the window, his notebook has fallen open. He doesn't remember leaving it open. He doesn't remember taking it out of the bag at all.

* * *

That evening the whole village gathers in the churchyard, as it does every equinox eve, and Yara stands at the edge of the crowd where he always stands, among the outsiders and the shy ones and the ones who come only to watch, and Marta Ilves climbs the steps of the church porch with her stick and her white hair and her voice, which at seventy-nine is still the truest instrument in the valley.

She sings the harvest song.

All of it. First singing, then second, back to back, no pause between, the way she has taught them, the way they have sung it for seven years. The villagers' voices rise raggedly around hers — some too high, some dragging, the blacksmith's boy flat on every third note, the two sisters from the mill farm harmonizing a beat apart so that it sounds like an argument and a reconciliation at once. It is, objectively, badly sung. Yara has recorded it six times and transcribed it six times and every transcription is littered with his marginal notes: *dragging*, *pitch drift*, *entrance missed*. It is the worst performance of the song that has ever existed, and it is the only correct one, because it is sung whole, and together, and by people who know what each verse cost.

When they reach the naming verse — *Lias, Lias, the well remembers* — nobody flinches. That took years. The first time, grown men had wept openly in the churchyard; the second time, they wept privately; by the fourth year they sang the name the way you say any name, with love in it and grief in it and no fear at all, and the name went up over the fields and down into the ground, and the ground, Yara is certain, listened.

Afterward there is cider and bread and the children run shrieking between the gravestones, and on the parish memorial — the plain granite slab the village raised the year after the book was published — someone has left marigolds under the carved name that was not there for fifty years and will be there forever now:

**LIAS VOSS — TAKEN 1974 — REMEMBERED ALWAYS**

His own family name. His own blood. The relative he never knew he had, restored to the stone the way a bone is set. Yara touches the letters every year. This year he finds that someone has polished them — waxed, maybe, cared for — and beside the carved name, small and new, someone has added a second line in a different, shakier hand, cut shallow, recent:

*he waved.*

Marta appears at his elbow. She follows his eyes to the stone, and her mouth does the thing that is almost a smile.

"The children," she signs. "They leave messages. I've stopped trying to police it." A pause. Her hands hesitate, which they almost never do. "Do you mind?"

He shakes his head. Then, because she deserves the truth and he can give it to no one else: *I saw him today. In the mirror. On time.*

Marta goes still. Around them the churchyard noise continues — laughter, a fiddle being tuned badly, the dog from the platform barking at its own joy — but around the two of them the air draws in, the way it does.

"On time," she repeats aloud, though he signed it, though she knows, though they have had this conversation in fragments for seven years and it never gets easier and never gets harder either. Her eyes go bright and fierce. "Then it's finished. Whatever it was — the owing, the waiting — it's finished." She grips his arm with her knotted hand, hard, the grip of a woman who once sang a curse onto herself to save him and has never once mentioned it since. "You did that."

*We did,* he signs. *You sang it twice.*

"I sang it wrong," she says, and laughs, short and cracked, and wipes her eyes with the heel of her hand like a girl. "God, I sang it so wrong. Off-key the whole way through. He must have hated it."

*No,* Yara signs. *It was the first song anyone ever sang for him on purpose.*

Marta looks at him for a long moment. Then she takes his notebook out of his coat pocket — she has never asked permission; she has never needed it — and flips to the final page, and holds it up to the lantern light, and shows him.

There is writing on it. There was no writing on it that morning; he checked, the way he checks the mirror, the way he checks the well-cellar door, the small devotions of a man who lives with an open door in his life and has made his peace with the draft.

The handwriting is not his. It is round and careful and slightly uncertain, the letters of someone who learned to write a long time ago and has had fifty years of darkness to practice, and it says only:

*thank you*

Below it, smaller, newer, pressed so lightly the pencil barely kissed the paper, as if written by a hand that was already leaving:

*see you next autumn*

Marta closes the notebook gently and gives it back. Neither of them speaks — he cannot, and she, for once, chooses not to. Above the churchyard the equinox moon is rising, full and low and the color of the marigolds, and the last voices of the village are drifting home through the lanes, carrying snatches of the song, sung badly, sung whole, sung twice.

Yara Voss stands in the churchyard of Hessel with his notebook against his chest and his lost voice resting easy at the bottom of a dry well, and he is not afraid, and he is not alone, and the door — the one second wide, the one that was a wound and became a window and became, at last, simply a door — stands open behind him, exactly on time.

He waves at the moon.

Somewhere, he is certain, something waves back.
