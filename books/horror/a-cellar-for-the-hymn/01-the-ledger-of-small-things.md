## 1. The Ledger of Small Things

The coach let Mirabel Thackeray off at the bottom of a hill that did not appear on any map she owned, which was not surprising, because the map she owned was of Yorkshire and Hollowmere, according to her letter of appointment, was somewhere considerably further from anywhere at all.

"You'll want to mind the third step," the coachman said, handing down her trunk as though this were the most important thing he would say all day. "Schoolhouse steps. They've a way of giving."

"Thank you," said Mirabel, who intended to write this down.

She had kept a ledger since she was nine years old — a habit begun with a confiscated account book from her father's shop and never abandoned. In it she recorded everything: the price of bread in every town she'd passed through, the names of dogs, the exact wording of insults (her own and others'), the dates of first frosts, the number of stairs in every lodging she'd ever climbed. Eleven years of ledgers sat in trunks in three different cities, none of them cities she had stayed in. The ledger was the one constant companion of a woman who had been, by turns, a governess, an assistant instructress, a temporary mistress of infants, and once, gloriously for six weeks, the entire faculty of a school in Cornwall before it burned down.

*Third step gives,* she wrote, standing at the foot of the schoolhouse steps with her trunk beside her and her ledger open against her forearm. *Coachman's advice. Verify.*

The schoolhouse itself sat at the crest of the hill like a hen settling over eggs: broad, grey-stoned, comfortably squat, with a bell tower that seemed more ornamental than ambitious. Beyond it the village of Hollowmere folded itself into a shallow valley — forty-odd roofs, a church spire, a mill pond catching the late afternoon sun so that the whole scene looked briefly gilded, like a photograph of a place rather than the place itself.

Mirabel counted the chimneys. Thirty-one. She could not have said why; she simply always counted things, and the counting made her feel that the world was a thing that could be known, if only one were patient enough to write it all down.

"Miss Thackeray?"

A woman was coming up the hill toward her at a pace that suggested she had been watching from a window and had timed her approach to seem casual. She was tall and narrow as a lych-gate, dressed in dove-grey, with hair pinned so severely it might have been structural. Her face was pleasant in the way of a well-kept garden — everything in order, nothing wild.

"Mrs. Corvel," Mirabel said, extending a hand. "Headmistress. Thank you for meeting me — I know the coach was late."

"The coach is always late." Mrs. Corvel's handshake was brief and dry. "It's the hill. Horses object to Hollowmere. Not to worry — you walked up it easily enough." Something flickered across her face then, gone too quickly to name. "Most people do."

"I'm very fond of hills," said Mirabel, who was not especially, but felt that a woman beginning a new life ought to be agreeable about topography.

"Come and see your school, then."

*Your school.* Mirabel turned the phrase over as they climbed. She had worked in eleven schools and been wanted in precisely none of them — wanted in the sense of *needed*, yes, schools were always desperate for someone cheap and competent, but never wanted in the sense of *yours*. At thirty-four she had begun to suspect that belonging to a place was something that happened to other people, the way some people could grow roses and she could only ever grow cress.

The third step gave. She noted it.

* * *

Inside, the schoolhouse smelled of chalk dust, coal smoke, and floor polish, which Mirabel privately considered the correct smell for all buildings everywhere. Two classrooms, a cloakroom lined with pegs each bearing a child's name on a paper tag, a little office with a stove, and stairs down to a cellar door — heavy, iron-banded, painted the same grey as everything else.

"That's cellar storage," Mrs. Corvel said, before Mirabel had asked anything. "Coal and old desks. Nothing of interest."

"I wasn't—"

"No," agreed Mrs. Corvel. "You weren't." And she smiled, and the smile was warm enough that Mirabel decided she had imagined the oddness.

Eleven desks in the main classroom, arranged in neat rows. Eleven name tags in the cloakroom. Mirabel ran her finger along them: Wren Hallow. Ivo Merrick. Sable Dunn. Tansy Pike. And seven more. She copied all eleven names into her ledger before Mrs. Corvel returned, because names were the beginning of knowing children, and knowing children was the whole of teaching, in her experience, or ought to be.

"They're good children," Mrs. Corvel said from the doorway. Mirabel started slightly; the woman moved very quietly for someone so severe-looking. "Better than they have any right to be, some say. You'll find them attentive."

"All children are attentive when they're interested."

"Hm." Mrs. Corvel considered her for a moment. "Yes. I think you'll do, Miss Thackeray. Your references were excellent. Unusual, in a teacher who has moved about so much."

There was no kind way to answer that, so Mirabel answered honestly. "I've never found a place that wanted keeping, Mrs. Corvel. Only places that wanted staffing."

"And you think Hollowmere wants keeping?"

"I think every place does," said Mirabel. "Some are just better at pretending otherwise."

For the first time, Mrs. Corvel's composure shifted into something almost like pleasure. "Well," she said. "Supper at the Lamb and Lantern at seven. The innkeeper will feed you whether you can pay or not; he insists it's tradition. Good evening, Miss Thackeray. Rest well. Lessons begin Monday."

She left, and Mirabel stood alone in her classroom in the last of the light, and allowed herself — carefully, the way one tests ice — to feel hopeful.

* * *

The Lamb and Lantern was low-beamed and loud with the particular hush of a village pub absorbing a stranger. Conversation didn't stop when Mirabel entered, but it changed key, the way weather changes before rain. Forty faces, give or take. She counted. She always counted.

"You'll be the new miss, then." The innkeeper was a barrel of a man with forearms like hams and an expression of aggressive cheer. "Tam Hallow. Sit you down. Mutton? It's mutton. It's always mutton on Thursdays, that's tradition, don't fight it."

"Mutton," agreed Mirabel, sitting.

The room resumed its noise around her, but she noticed — because noticing was what she did — that the noise now had channels cut through it, little eddies where conversation bent away from certain topics as she passed near. A woman by the fire stopped mid-sentence about a flooded lane. Two men at the bar discussed the price of wool with the fervor of men discussing nothing whatsoever.

And yet. When a child — a small girl with ink-stained fingers and an expression of open assessment — appeared at Mirabel's elbow and asked, "Are you going to be our teacher proper, or another one of the leaving sort?" the table nearest them went quiet in a way that was not hostile. It was attentive. The whole room, Mirabel realized, was waiting for her answer with its breath half-held.

"That depends," Mirabel said gravely, "on whether you lot intend to be worth staying for."

The child considered this. "We're worth it. It's the teachers that aren't."

"Wren!" hissed a woman two tables away — the same woman who'd stopped talking about the flood.

"What? It's true. We've had four."

"Three," corrected Tam Hallow, setting down Mirabel's plate. "Mrs. Ellery only went to her sister's in Ganton."

"She never came back," said Wren.

"People move, child."

"Not from here they don't." And Wren looked directly at Mirabel when she said it, with the flat certainty children deploy when stating facts adults have agreed not to notice, and then she was gone, called away by her mother, and the room exhaled, and the ordinary noise of a Thursday evening closed back over the moment like water.

Mirabel ate her mutton. It was, she noted, genuinely excellent mutton. She wrote down the recipe as best she could reconstruct it from taste. Then, under the heading *Hollowmere — First Impressions*, she wrote:

*Village: 31 chimneys, 1 inn, 1 church, 1 school (mine). People: watchful but welcoming, the way geese are welcoming. One child (Wren Hallow) who says teachers leave and do not return. Four teachers, or three, depending whom you ask. Note: ask everyone. Discrepancies are where the truth keeps its coat.*

She read it back, frowned slightly at the last sentence — it was more poetic than her usual entries — and decided to keep it anyway. New places did that to her. Made her lyrical.

* * *

Her lodgings were the upper floor of the schoolhouse itself, two rooms with sloped ceilings and a window that looked out over the mill pond. By lamplight she unpacked: clothes, books, a tin of pens, and the ledger, which lived on her nightstand the way other people kept Bibles.

Before bed she made her ritual entry, the one she'd made in every lodging since she was nine:

*Ledger of Small Things, being a true account of Mirabel Thackeray's observations, kept so that nothing need be forgotten and nothing need be feared, for fear lives mostly in the unrecorded.*

Then the day's items:

*Candles: 6 in my rooms, 2 stubs. Stairs to my door: 14, creaking on 9 and 12. View: mill pond, willow, hills. Sounds: water, wind, a dog two lanes off, and — at intervals — a sound I cannot place. Low. Rhythmic. Perhaps the mill machinery. Perhaps singing. Will investigate tomorrow.*

She paused over that last line. She had heard the sound twice while unpacking: a faint, rising-and-falling hum beneath the ordinary noises of the night, too regular for wind, too shapeless for a hymn. The mill, surely. Mills hummed. Ponds reflected. Villages watched strangers and children said unsettling true things, and all of it was just the texture of a new place, the grain of it, which smoothed out once you lived there long enough to stop noticing.

That was the theory, anyway. Mirabel had moved often enough to know the theory was mostly true.

She blew out her candle, and in the dark she heard the humming again, fainter now, or perhaps she was simply falling asleep. Tomorrow she would count the schoolhouse windows from outside, verify the third step, and begin learning eleven names attached to eleven faces.

Monday, lessons began. She was, she told herself firmly, going to stay. She had decided it the way one decides to plant a tree: knowing it would take years, intending to see them.

*Note to self,* she wrote drowsily in the margin, pen wobbling. *Find out what the children mean by 'the leaving sort.' Find out why nobody leaves. Find out what hums.*

She underlined *hums*, blew out the lamp properly, and slept — not badly, for a first night in a strange bed in a strange village at the far end of nowhere.

In the morning, the entry would still be there, underlined, patient, the first thread of a pattern she could not yet see: that in Hollowmere, everything worth knowing was already written down somewhere — and everything written down had a way of disappearing.
