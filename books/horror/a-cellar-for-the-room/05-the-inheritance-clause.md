## 5. The Inheritance Clause

Halbrook's office smelled of dust and old decisions. It was the kind of office where the filing cabinets predated the telephone and the telephone predated optimism — a tall room above a shuttered tobacconist in Ashcombe, with a window so grimy it turned the afternoon into a rumor of light.

"You're the surveyor," he said, before I'd finished sitting down. Not a question. He said it the way a man says *you're the weather*, meaning: I have been expecting you for some time, and I am not pleased about it.

"Petra Eastwick. Marlowe & Finch sent me to value Hollowmere House. The bank wants—"

"I know what the bank wants." He lowered himself into a chair behind a desk that had been cleared with great care except for one leather folder, which he placed in front of him but did not open. His hands rested on either side of it like a man guarding a small animal. "I've been the solicitor of record for that property for thirty-one years. Before me, my father. Before him, his father. We are rather a family business when it comes to Hollowmere."

"That's reassuring," I said. "Continuity of records."

Something moved across his face — not quite a flinch, more the memory of one. "Yes," he said. "You could call it that."

I had come for paperwork. That was all. The appraisal was stalled on a title question — the bank's underwriters wanted confirmation of clear conveyance before they'd lend against a derelict, and the deeds Halbrook had faxed were, in his word, "complicated," which in solicitor dialect means *there is something here I would prefer you never read*. So I'd driven two hours through rain to read it anyway, because that is the job. You don't appraise a building by admiring its facade. You look in the cellar.

I want that on the record. Whatever happened after, whatever I became — I looked in the cellar because looking is the job.

* * *

The folder contained copies going back further than I expected. Deeds, conveyances, letters of administration, each one older than the last, the ink migrating from iron gall to typewriter ribbon to laser toner like sediment layers in a core sample.

"Walk me through it," I said.

Halbrook opened the folder at last, and I noticed his hands weren't quite steady. A solicitor of thirty-one years' standing, and his hands weren't steady over a set of land documents. I filed that away next to everything else I'd been filing away since Hollowmere, in the drawer marked *coincidences, pending*.

"The house was built 1811 by Josiah Crane, wool merchant. Ordinary man, ordinary greed. He dies 1841; the estate passes to his son Edmund Crane." He turned a page. "Edmund dies in 1847."

"How?"

"The parish register says a fall. The coroner's file says nothing at all, which is itself unusual." He said it flatly, the way you report a fact you've decided not to have feelings about. "After Edmund, the property passes to his daughter Constance Crane-Hale. She marries, moves to Bath, attempts to sell. No buyer. She dies intestate in 1863 — no will, no executor, the estate administered by the court. Her son takes it, flees to Canada within the year, dies there without issue. And from there—" he turned pages faster now, a deck of misfortune, "—intestate. Intestate. Abandoned. Sold at auction to a Mr. Pell, who lived in it four months and left in the night owing rent. Auction again. A family named Sorrell, 1902, gone by 1904. A Reverend Ames, who took it as a retreat and lasted eleven weeks. Intestate. Intestate. And in 1974, the last conveyance of record — a Mrs. Ada Voss, who died in 1989 without a will, leaving the house to nobody, whereupon it reverted to a state of legal limbo from which the bank now wishes to extract it."

"Nobody's held it more than a generation," I said. "In a hundred and seventy years."

"No one has held it *willingly*," Halbrook corrected, "for a hundred and seventy years."

I turned a page myself then, because he seemed reluctant to, and that's when I saw it — an insertion in the 1847 conveyance, written into the deed itself in a clerk's careful hand, the kind of clause that gets added late at night by someone who believes paper can hold back water.

I read it twice. Then a third time, aloud, because some things need to be said in your own voice to be believed:

"'...saving and excepting always the true inheritance of the dwelling known as Hollowmere House, which shall not pass by purchase, gift, or devise, but shall descend only to the issue of the blood of Maren Ash, should such issue exist; and if none exist, then to stand empty until such time as they do.'"

The office was very quiet. Somewhere below us, the shuttered tobacconist's sign creaked in the wind.

"That's not a standard clause," I said.

"It is not a standard anything," Halbrook said. "It is, strictly speaking, unenforceable nonsense. You cannot deed a house to a dead servant girl's descendants in perpetuity; the courts would laugh it out in an afternoon. Every solicitor who has touched this file has said so, including me, including my father." He paused. "And every owner who has tried to sell or will the house regardless has come to grief in ways I have chosen not to document. There is a difference between what holds in court and what holds, Miss Eastwick. Hollowmere has never confused the two."

"Maren Ash." I said the name carefully, testing its weight. From the parish records I'd pulled — because yes, by then I'd started pulling parish records, which tells you how far gone I already was — I knew her. Housemaid. Hanged in 1847 for stealing silver that turned up twenty years later sewn into the lining of the mistress's mattress, discovered by a mattress-maker with an honest eye and no sense of timing. Twenty years too late to matter. The song in the cellar plaster knew about the mattress. I hadn't told anyone that part yet.

"What happened to her family?" I asked. "Did she have any?"

Halbrook looked at me for a long moment. In that moment I understood he had been waiting for this question the way you wait at a bedside — dreading it, rehearsing it, knowing the answer changes everything and changes nothing.

"She had a brother," he said. "Thomas Ash. He left the parish after the hanging. There's a baptismal record from a village forty miles east — a daughter born to a Thomas Ash, 1851. And from there the name scatters, as names do. Marries out. Moves. Becomes other names." He drew a folded sheet from the folder — not typed, hand-drawn, a genealogy in faded pencil, generations of little descending lines like roots growing down through the page. "I have spent a certain amount of my career tracing it. My father did before me. It is, I'm afraid, part of the family business."

"Why would a solicitor trace the descendants of a hanged maid?"

"Because the clause requires it," he said. "Someone must know who the heirs are. Someone must always know." He unfolded the sheet fully and turned it toward me, and his finger came down on the third line from the bottom, and stayed there.

I looked. The name meant nothing at first. *Eastwick*. A marriage entry, 1911, Hollowmere parish church. An *Eastwick* born 1888. Another, 1920. The lines descending, thinning, until—

My grandmother's name. Edith Eastwick, born 1920, Hollowmere parish. Who left at nineteen and never once went back, and never said why, and whose silence I had always filed under *the war*, the way families file things.

The drawer marked *coincidences, pending* opened all the way and hit the back wall.

"No," I said.

"It's a common enough surname," Halbrook said gently, which was the first unkind thing he did, because gentleness in the face of evidence is just cruelty wearing a cardigan.

"My grandmother was from London."

"She was from Hollowmere. She left in 1939 and did not return. Her mother was an Ash before she was an Eastwick — see the second line. Thomas Ash's granddaughter." He tapped the page again. "You are the issue of the blood of Maren Ash, Miss Eastwick. As far as my research extends, you are the only living issue. Which means that legally speaking — well. Legally speaking, nothing. But the house does not concern itself with *legally speaking*."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to; my body simply concluded the meeting without me. "This is a prank. Or a scam. Or you're—"

"Sitting in a dusty office at my own expense, showing a stranger a document my family has kept quiet for six generations?" He didn't raise his voice. "Miss Eastwick, I take no pleasure in this. But you went into that house. You measured its rooms. You heard—" he stopped himself, and I watched him decide how much he knew, and land somewhere deliberate. "Whatever you found there, it found you back. It always does. The clause isn't a trap. It's a *signpost*. Someone wrote it into the deed so that someday, someone like you would sit in a chair exactly like that one and learn whose house they've been walking through."

"I'm a surveyor," I said. "I put numbers on rooms. That's all I do. That's all I've ever agreed to do."

"Yes," Halbrook said. "That is precisely the tragedy of it. Duty is never agreed to. It's only ever discovered." He began gathering the papers with those unsteady hands, squaring them, closing the folder over a hundred and seventy years of flight and failure. "There's one more thing. About the song."

"There are three verses," I said. "Carved in the plaster. I transcribed them."

His head came up sharply. "Three? You're certain? Only three?"

"Counted twice."

He exhaled — relief, unmistakable, and therefore more frightening than fear would have been. "Then it hasn't finished yet," he said, mostly to himself. He handed me the folder. "Take these. Read them. And Miss Eastwick — do not go back to the house. Sell your interest, assign it, abandon it, I'll draft whatever you like. But don't go back, and don't go down to that cellar again, and whatever you do—"

He stopped. Outside, the wind worried the old sign below, creaking it against brick, a slow rhythmic knocking like knuckles on wood, like fingers on a lid.

"—don't sing along," he finished quietly.

I carried the folder out into the rain. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Inside, under the deeds and the deaths, lay a penciled family tree with my grandmother's name on it, and beneath hers, following the descending line, mine — added recently, the graphite still bright, dated three days before the bank ever called my firm.

He had known I was coming. The house had known longer.

And in the margin beside my name, in Halbrook's small careful hand, a single note I didn't understand until later, much later, when understanding was the only thing I had left:

*She asks questions. Let her ask.*
