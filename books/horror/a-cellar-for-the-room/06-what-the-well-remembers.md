## 6. What the Well Remembers

There is a particular kind of lie that surveyors tell themselves, and it goes like this: *the house cannot hurt me, because the house is a depreciating asset.*

I had been telling myself that lie for eleven days when I rented the breaker hammer.

Not a sledgehammer — I want to be clear about that, because the distinction matters to me now in a way it didn't then. A sledgehammer is a decision you make with your arms. A breaker hammer is a decision you make with a credit card and a signed liability waiver at a hire shop in Ashcombe where a man named Terry looked at my ID, looked at my face, and said, "Hollowmere, is it?" in the tone of a man who has been asked to rent equipment to that address before and has opinions about it.

"Who else has hired from you for Hollowmere?" I asked.

"Nobody," said Terry. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The brickwork over the well was not good masonry. That was the first thing I noticed, standing in the cellar with the work light throwing my shadow huge against the far wall like a woman twice my size who had better posture. The bricks were mismatched — salvaged, some of them, older than the wall they'd been set into, one still bearing a smear of whitewash from some room upstairs that hadn't existed for a century. Whoever had built this wall had built it in a hurry, or in fear, or both, and mortar applied by frightened hands sets crooked. It had cracked along its courses like old skin.

It had also been repaired. Twice, by the look of it. Someone had come down here after the original bricking and pointed up the joints again, and then someone else had done it a third time, and each repointing used a different mix of lime, which meant different decades, which meant this wasn't a wall. It was a habit. Generations of owners had walked down these stairs, looked at the wall, felt whatever I was currently feeling, and mixed mortar about it.

I thought about that while I put on the ear defenders. I thought: *every one of those people stood exactly here and decided the same thing I'm deciding.* And then I thought: *and every one of them decided to keep it bricked.*

Which should have told me something. It did tell me something. I started the hammer anyway.

The noise in the cellar was total — a sound you feel in your teeth and your sternum, a sound that makes thinking impossible, which I suspect is why so many bad decisions in human history have been made with power tools. Brick came away in chunks. Behind the first course there was a second course, laid neater, and behind that, darkness and a smell.

I want to describe the smell properly, because everything afterward came out of it. Not damp — I know damp, damp is my professional colleague of twenty years, damp smells like cold pennies and rot. This smelled warm. Bread-warm. Yeasty and animal, like the breath of something sleeping in a small room, and underneath it, faintly, water that isn't water — mineral and metallic, the smell of a spring deep under limestone that has never seen daylight.

I shut off the hammer. The silence rang.

The last brick I took out by hand, because it was already loose, because it wanted to be loose, and I am choosing to believe that's an engineering observation and nothing more.

* * *

The well was smaller than I expected. Wells usually are, in my experience, which is limited to exactly one well, but still — I'd imagined something grand and architectural, a throat wide enough to swallow a cart. This was barely three feet across, ringed in stone worn smooth and slightly concave on the lip, the way stone goes when hands rest on it over and over across centuries. Leaning over it, I saw dryness. Dry stone going down into black, no gleam of water anywhere, no smell of standing stagnation.

Dry. And yet the air coming up out of it moved. Gently. In and out. In and out.

A shaft that deep and narrow will breathe with temperature changes; I know the physics; I could have written half a page of the report explaining it. But physics doesn't account for rhythm, and this had rhythm, slow and patient, the way a sleeper breathes, and it did not correspond to any temperature change, because the cellar held steady at eleven degrees and had all week.

And it was humming.

I don't mean vibrating. I mean humming. The tune — the tune from the plaster, the tune from the recordings, the tune I had caught myself whistling on Tuesday while making coffee and stopped mid-phrase with a jolt like touching a live wire. It rose out of the well at the threshold of hearing, the note your ears produce when the world goes quiet, except it wasn't my ears, because I hummed three notes back at it, experimentally, the way you might tap a glass to see if it's cracked.

The well changed key to match me.

I sat down on the cellar floor with my back against the far wall and my knees up and I did what I always do when reality misbehaves, which is take measurements. Air temperature at the well mouth: nineteen degrees, eight degrees warmer than the room, in a cellar with no heating, in October. Surface temperature of the stone rim: twenty-one. I wrote these numbers in my notebook in my tidy surveyor's hand and then I wrote underneath them, because I am apparently the kind of person I am now:

*It's warm because it's alive. Don't be stupid. It's warm because —*

I couldn't finish the sentence. Both halves were stupid. Only one of them was true.

* * *

Here is what I did next, and I've turned it over enough times since to know I can't pretend it was accidental. I knelt at the well. I put both palms flat on the rim stone, the way you'd steady yourself getting up from a prayer, and I told myself I was checking for vibration frequency, which is a sentence no one has ever said honestly in the history of forensic surveying.

The stone was warm as skin.

And Maren's last night came up through my hands like water through chalk.

* * *

I need to be careful here, because memory that isn't yours doesn't file itself properly. It doesn't come with edges. What I got was not a film; it was weather. Cold first — the cold of a servant's attic room in February 1847, cold that lives in the bedclothes, cold that makes you dress in shifts so no part of you touches air longer than necessary. Her feet on bare boards. Her breath visible. Seventeen years old — I knew she was seventeen the way you know your own birthday, not learned, just known, the fact sitting in me like a swallowed stone.

Then the kitchen. Then the mistress.

I have spent my career around death and I have developed a professional immunity to photographs of the dead, but I was not prepared for a living face, and Mrs. Harrow — I knew her name the way I knew the age, it arrived pre-installed — Mrs. Harrow's face was beautiful. That's the thing I can't get past. I had built her in my head as a hag, some gothic illustration, and instead she was thirty-two and lovely, with a soft voice and kind eyes and a way of tilting her head when she listened that made you feel like the only person in the world, and she used that face the way other people use tools.

*"Maren." Softly. Kindly. "The silver candlesticks are missing from the drawing room."*

And Maren's whole body did the thing bodies do when the ground opens — that drop, that freefall in the stomach — because she had dusted those candlesticks yesterday, she had polished them until her thumbs ached, and she understood, in one vertical plummet of understanding, that the missing silver had nothing to do with silver.

They found it in her pallet. Sewn into the straw tick of her mattress, wrapped in a cloth, six pieces of Harrow silver in a servant girl's bed, and I stood inside Maren's memory and watched the constable pull them out one by one while the household watched from the doorway with faces arranged into sorrow, and I knew — I knew with her knowledge, which is a knowledge deeper than evidence, the knowledge of the person the trap was built for — that Mrs. Harrow herself had sewn them there. Two nights before. In this room. With this thread. While Maren slept downstairs by the banked fire, trusting her, warming her hands on the trust.

Why? Because Maren had seen something. Because Maren had said something once, laughing, in the scullery, that a girl in service shouldn't say about the master's brother. Because Mrs. Harrow had needed a thief and Maren was there, and convenient, and had no family to make noise, and pretty enough that the accusation would stick with a satisfying ugliness. I got the reason the way you get a taste in your mouth — whole, wordless, true.

And the worst of it, the part I keep returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth: the mistress's face at the trial. Not cruel. Not gloating. *Composed.* Slightly sad. The face of a woman doing an unpleasant duty, and behind it, so faint only someone wearing Maren's eyes could see it, the smallest ease. Relief, almost. The relief of a problem solved.

They hanged her nine days later. I didn't get the hanging. The memory ended at the cell door, at Maren's hand flat against cold oak, and I think the well kept that part for itself, or maybe it gave me everything there was and the rest is silence, and the silence is worse.

* * *

I came back to myself on the cellar floor. I don't remember kneeling down. My palms were still on the rim and I snatched them away like the stone had bitten me, and my face was soaked, and I was making a sound — low, continuous, ugly — that took me a moment to identify as grief, because it wasn't shaped like any grief I own. It was too old. It was seventeen years old and a hundred and seventy-eight years old simultaneously, and none of it belonged to me, and all of it was mine now, non-refundable.

I sat against the wall for a long time. The work light buzzed. The well breathed.

Here is the accounting I finally made, because accounting is what I do, it's the only liturgy I have. I have appraised four hundred and eleven properties. I have walked through rooms where murders happened and felt nothing but draft calculations. I do not weep at crime scenes. I do not weep at funerals, including my father's, a fact my sister raised with me exactly once. I am, professionally speaking, a sealed unit.

And I sat on the floor of a derelict cellar crying for a girl dead since 1847, crying with a fury so specific and so enormous that it had nowhere to go, no defendant, no court, no restitution possible — everyone guilty was dust, everyone complicit was dust, the whole apparatus of justice had closed over the case a century and three-quarters ago and filed it under *nothing to be done* —

Except.

Except the well was still warm. Except the song was still unfinished. Except somebody, somewhere down the chain of blood and deed, was still the heir to this, and heirs inherit debts as well as houses, and I had spent eleven days establishing, with documents, with my own tidy hand, that the Eastwick line ran unbroken from Hollowmere parish to me.

"Was it you?" I asked the well. My voice came out wrecked. "In the recordings. Was that you?"

The breathing paused. Resumed. Slower. Which I chose to read as *yes*, and then immediately hated myself for reading anything into it, and then, sitting in the warm dark with salt drying on my face, stopped hating myself, because self-hatred requires believing you're wrong, and I no longer believed I was wrong. That was the crack going all the way through. Not the voice on the tape, not the doors measuring wrong. This. Grief without jurisdiction. You can explain away audio anomalies. You cannot explain away weeping for a stranger who is not a stranger.

I got up. My knees cracked. I packed the tools without looking at the well, the way you don't look at a dog you've kicked.

At the top of the stairs I stopped, because there was one more thing, and it had been waiting politely all evening for me to be ready.

The song. The rhyme on the plaster. Three verses gouged into that wall upstairs, three verses matching the diary, three verses recounting accusation and theft and rope. Every ballad I'd ever heard ended in a fourth verse — the moral, the turn, the ghost's demand or the sinner's ruin. This one just stopped. Cut off. And songs don't stop, songs get stopped, and the question that had been nagging at me for days finally assembled itself into words as I stood in the doorway with the well breathing softly below:

What was the last verse? And who cut it out?

I drove back to Ashcombe in the dark with the heater full on and the tune, for the first time, staying mercifully out of my head — and parked outside my lodgings to find the lights on in Halbrook's office above the chandler's, burning late, and the solicitor himself at the window, watching my car arrive, with the expression of a man whose calculation has just come due early.

He'd left his office door unlocked. He was holding a hymnal, and it was very old, and one page had been razored out so cleanly that only the stub of it remained, like a tongue.
