## 5. The Verse That Isn't Sung

The invitation came on a card, hand-lettered, slipped under the door of Maren's house the way bills and condolences are slipped under doors. *Harvest Gathering, the Tithe Barn, Saturday after dark. All are welcome. The heir most of all.*

Emrys read the last line three times. *The heir most of all.* Not *you* — never a name, never a pronoun. Just the word, dropped into the sentence like a stone into a well, and the village went on speaking around it.

They should have declined. They knew that even then, knew it the way you know a floor is rotten before your foot goes through. But the folklorist in them — the part that had spent eleven years chasing songs across counties and into the mouths of people who mostly wanted to be left alone — the folklorist won. A harvest gathering in Hollowmere. A village that wouldn't sing a song twice. Whatever was happening here, it was happening in the music, and music was the one country where Emrys had never been lost.

They packed the recorder carefully. Two spare batteries. The good microphone with the windscreen. They told themselves it was for the archive.

* * *

The Tithe Barn had been built for grain and now held only light and noise. Lanterns hung from the beams on iron hooks gone green with age, and their flames moved in a draft Emrys couldn't feel, all leaning the same direction, like grass before weather. Long trestle tables. Pumpkins with their stems still wet. A fiddler tuning in the corner with the absent patience of a man who had done it ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more.

The villagers were warm. That was the thing Emrys kept snagging on — they were *warm*. Hands pressed their arm. Cider appeared in their hand without anyone visibly carrying it. A woman with flour still on her knuckles from the morning's baking pressed a plate of apple cake on them and said, "You'll want your strength, I expect," and smiled, and the smile was perfectly ordinary, and Emrys spent the next ten minutes trying to decide whether that had been an odd thing to say and concluding that it hadn't, probably, that people said things like that all the time, that they were being paranoid, that the frost on the windows of the house had been a coincidence of early autumn and the chair in the photographs a trick of light and shadow, a developing flaw, a —

"Drink your cider," said a man beside them, kindly, and Emrys drank.

The fiddler struck up. Dancers came out onto the floorboards worn pale as bone. Emrys stood at the edge with the recorder in their coat pocket, running, thumb on the switch, and told themselves they weren't going to use it, that you didn't record people without their knowledge, that there were ethics, that there were *rules* —

The dancing went on for an hour. Emrys ate, drank, laughed in the right places, and all the while felt the barn's attention settle on them by degrees, the way a cat's attention settles on a bird. Not hostility. *Expectancy.* They caught the innkeeper looking at them, then away. They caught the old woman from the photographs — no, not from the photographs, from the lane, the small bent woman with the shawl — watching them over the rim of her cup with an expression Emrys could not read and did not want to.

It was near eleven when the fiddler set down his bow and the room, without being asked, grew quiet. Not the quiet of an audience. The quiet of a congregation.

The old woman rose.

* * *

She moved to the head of the room slowly, one hand on the back of a chair, and when she reached it she did not stand so much as settle, folding down into the space like water finding its level. She did not ask for quiet. She had never in her life needed to.

"This one's for the little ones," she said. "Though it's not a little song."

A few of the younger children were already being gathered by their mothers, moved to the far end of the barn, and Emrys noted that without understanding it. The children didn't protest. They went the way children go when they've been taught a thing so early it doesn't feel like a rule anymore — it feels like the shape of the world.

The old woman closed her eyes.

The lullaby was in no language Emrys knew, or half in one — old words worn smooth as river stones, and beneath them a melody so simple it seemed it must always have existed, three notes rising, two falling, the way a mother's voice moves when she is rocking. Emrys felt their chest ache with it. That was the craft of it, the terrible craft: it was *beautiful*. It was the most beautiful thing they had recorded in eleven years, and they had their hand in their pocket on the switch before they had decided anything at all.

*Click.* The red light was hidden by the coat's lining. The microphone drank.

Verse one. The room swayed slightly, unconsciously, a field of wheat. Verse two. Someone's baby slept against a shoulder. Verse three, and Emrys noticed that the villagers' faces had changed — not frightened, not sad, but *attentive* in a way that had nothing comfortable in it, the attention of people watching a door.

The old woman drew breath for the fourth verse.

And the room went silent.

Not hushed. *Silent.* The fiddler's hand went flat over his strings. A man mid-drink lowered his glass without a sound. The draft that had been leaning the lanterns all evening simply stopped, and the flames stood up straight as candles in a sealed room, and the silence came down on the barn like a lid.

The old woman's mouth was still moving.

That was the horror of it, the thing Emrys would carry afterward into sleepless hours: her lips shaped the words, her throat worked, her hand kept the rhythm on the chair-back — but no sound came out. Not a whisper. Not breath. The fourth verse was being sung into a silence so complete it had texture, like wool, like deep water, and every soul in that barn sat inside it and let her, and no one looked at her, and no one looked at the floor, and no one looked at Emrys, and the lantern flames stood perfectly still.

It lasted perhaps twenty seconds. It lasted a year.

Then the old woman finished — finished *something*, her mouth closing on a final shape — and opened her eyes, and the sound came back into the world all at once, the fiddler's chair creak, the baby's small snuffle, the crackle of the fire, so ordinary and so loud that Emrys's ears rang. The room exhaled. Someone laughed too brightly. Cider was poured. The gathering resumed as if a window had been shut against a gust.

The old woman looked directly at Emrys across the room. She didn't smile. She touched two fingers briefly to her own lips, and then to her heart, and sat down.

* * *

Emrys walked home the long way, along the lane, because the direct way passed the churchyard and they did not want to pass the churchyard tonight, though they could not have said why. The frost had come back. It lay in the grass at the field edges like spilled salt, and their breath hung before them, and the house when they reached it stood dark and waiting with the particular patience of houses that have outlived their owners.

Inside, they did not light the main lamps. They sat at the kitchen table with the recorder between their hands like something caught — a bird, a live coal — and they did not want to play it back, and they had to play it back, and these two facts sat down together inside them and refused to be reconciled.

They pressed play.

The lullaby came out of the little speaker warm and whole. The room of the Tithe Barn was in it: the fiddler's tuning, the shuffle of feet, the baby. Verse one. Verse two. Verse three. Emrys's own breath, shallow, close to the microphone. And then —

The silence.

On the recording, the silence was not silent.

Beneath the old woman's moving lips, beneath the wool-thick nothing the room had heard, the microphone — indifferent, faithful, designed to catch exactly what was there — had caught a second voice.

It was low. It was underneath the melody like a stone underneath moss, following the same tune a half-breath behind, the way a child learns from a parent, the way an echo learns from a voice. It was not the old woman's voice. It was not any voice. It was the sound a voice would make if it had been singing alone in the dark for a very, very long time and had forgotten everything about singing except the song.

Emrys's hand found the table edge. The kitchen around them was very cold, and the window above the sink was feathering over with frost even now, in early autumn, even with the fire lit — and the frost was not feathering. It was *drawing*. Lines, deliberate, patient: the long verticals of a doorframe. The rungs of a ladder. A staircase, descending.

They made themselves look away. They made themselves look back at the recorder.

The second voice reached the end of the fourth verse and stopped — but not cleanly. It stopped the way a person stops when they realize they are being listened to. A fraction of a beat too late. A held breath. And then, beneath the returning noise of the barn — laughter, cider, chairs — the voice said something. Two syllables. It was not in any language Emrys knew, but their body understood it the way the body understands a fall before the mind catches up.

It sounded like *almost*.

The recording ran on thirty more seconds and ended. Emrys sat in the kitchen of their grandmother's house with the frost climbing the window in the shape of a staircase going down, and understood, with the flat and total clarity that arrives only once or twice in a life, that they had just heard proof. Not folklore. Not atmosphere, not tradition, not the charming strangeness of a dying village. Proof. Something in Hollowmere sang the fourth verse, and had sung it for longer than the village had been refusing to, and tonight, for the first time, it had known it was heard.

The frost finished the staircase and began, at the window's edge, on something new.

Emrys did not stay to see what. They took the recorder upstairs, put it in the drawer with Maren's ledgers, and lay down on top of the blankets in their clothes, and did not sleep, and toward morning heard — or dreamed they heard, and would never be sure which was worse — a voice on the cellar stairs, humming three notes rising and two falling, stopping, always, just short of the top.

In the drawer below, the recorder's red light was off.

But the tape had moved.
