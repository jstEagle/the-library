## 9. I Don't Believe

I did not run so much as I fled with my dignity arranged around me like a coat I'd forgotten to button.

The walk from Maren's house to the Lamb and Lantern took eleven minutes in daylight, along a lane where the hedges had gone bronze overnight and the air smelled of iron and coming frost. I counted the minutes because counting is what rational people do when their hands won't stop shaking. Eleven minutes. A measured distance. A distance that could be walked back, walked away from, walked out of entirely.

Behind me, the house sat in its hollow of dead grass with its windows dark, and I did not look at them. I had spent three nights in that house—three nights of humming rising through the floorboards like water finding its level—and on the third morning I had found the cellar door standing open again, though I had locked it, though I had wedged a chair beneath the handle, though I had told myself *locks are locks* the way you tell yourself anything when the alternative is unspeakable.

So: the inn. Public rooms. Other people. The warm stupid noise of a village pub at half past four, when the farmers come in and the fire is lit and everything is exactly as ordinary as it has always been.

*I don't believe,* I said to myself as I walked, under my breath, a rosary of four small words. *I don't believe in chairs that move. I don't believe in frost that draws doorways. I don't believe in songs with voices underneath them.* Each sentence a stone laid down on the path ahead of me. Each one sinking slightly into the mud.

The Lamb and Lantern was low-beamed and amber-lit and smelled of woodsmoke and spilled cider, and for the first hour it worked. It worked the way a bandage works over a wound you're afraid to look at. I sat in the corner by the fire with a bowl of barley soup going cold and my recorder bag between my feet like a dog I couldn't let off the lead, and I listened to the room be ordinary. Darts. A radio murmuring the shipping forecast to an audience of no sailors. Two men arguing amiably about a fence.

I recited facts to myself the way my grandmother—no. The way *Maren Dunmore*, deceased, unrelated, a woman whose ledgers I had read, might have. Facts: frost forms patterns; pareidolia makes meaning of randomness; old houses settle and shift and their doors open; grief makes strangers of familiar rooms. I was tired. I was grieving, if not her then something adjacent to her—the version of her I'd invented before I came, the grandmother-shaped warmth I'd been carrying across two counties like a hot water bottle against my chest. Tired, grieving people misperceive. This is documented. This is fine.

The innkeeper, a broad, ruddy man called Tam Haskin who wiped the same clean glass over and over, nodded to me when I ordered and asked no questions, which I appreciated, because the questions were there anyway, stacked behind his teeth. *How's the house? Sleeping well? Heard anything?*

At seven the child came down.

Tam's daughter—I'd seen her once before, a small solemn girl of six or seven named Wren who sat on the stairs doing homework while the adults drank. Tonight she carried paper and a box of crayons and installed herself at the table nearest the fire, which happened to be near mine, and began to draw with the fierce concentration of children, tongue-tip between her teeth.

I watched her sidelong because watching her straight-on felt intrusive. Children have a way of looking back that strips you.

She drew a house first—all children draw houses—and then she drew figures, stick-thin, and then she drew something else, and I looked away, and when I looked back she was looking at me.

Not at my face. At the space just above my head, as if measuring something.

"Are you the lady from the picture house?" she asked.

"The—" I blinked. "Do you mean the big house? On the lane?"

She nodded. "Da says you live there now."

"I'm staying there a while," I said carefully. "It belonged to my family."

"Did you sit in the chair yet?"

The fire popped. Somewhere behind the bar a glass stopped being wiped.

"What chair?" I said, and heard my own voice come out flat and careful, a hand placed gently on a lid.

Wren shrugged, the magnificent indifference of childhood, and went back to her drawing. I watched her crayon travel. Brown for the floor. Grey for a wall. And then black—a chair, drawn in heavy black strokes, plain-backed, four-legged, set against the grey wall. She drew a figure sitting in it. She gave the figure my hair, or near enough. She gave it my jacket, the green one I was wearing at that moment.

Then she bent close over the face, working with a yellow crayon, and drew the smile.

It was too wide. That was the first thing. Not monstrous—nothing a child draws is ever truly monstrous—but too wide for the head it sat on, curving up past where cheekbones would go, and it had been drawn slowly, deliberately, each tooth a separate yellow mark, and the eyes above it were simple dots, and somehow the dots were worse than the mouth.

"Wren." Tam's voice came from behind the bar, quiet in a way that made the whole room quieter. "Love. Come here a minute."

She didn't come. She held up the drawing instead, turning to show me, and said, with a child's terrible pride: "That's you. You look happy."

I want to record accurately what happened inside me in that moment, because later I will need to know whether the fear arrived all at once or had been arriving for days and only then finished. The truth is it had been arriving for days. This was simply the moment it landed.

Because I knew that smile. I had seen it that morning, faint and wrong, in the bathroom mirror while I brushed my teeth—a softness at the corners of my mouth that I had blamed on the light, on tiredness, on the strange convexity of old mirrors. I had seen it in the photograph I'd taken of the frost-etched kitchen window, where the camera had shown me instead a wooden chair against a blank wall, and I had told myself *lens flare, condensation, a trick of exposure*. I had been assembling explanations the way a drowning person assembles driftwood, and every piece had sunk, and here was a child holding up the thing itself, rendered in crayon: me, seated, smiling, happy.

"That's very good," I heard myself say. My voice sounded like someone else's voice played through a bad speaker. "Why did you draw me in a chair?"

"Because that's where you are," she said, puzzled that I should ask, and returned to her colours.

*

Tam took the drawing from the table when he thought I wasn't looking. He held it the way you hold something recently dead—not with tenderness but with the care owed to things that mustn't be dropped. He showed it to his wife behind the bar. Her face did something complicated and brief, like weather crossing a field, and then she took it, folded it once, and put it in the pocket of her apron, and neither of them looked at me for the rest of the evening.

But the room had changed. I could feel it the way you feel a draft you can't locate—a coolness moving through the ordinary noise, touching each table in turn. The men arguing about the fence had stopped arguing. One of them, a heavyset fellow I vaguely recognised from the harvest gathering, kept glancing at me and then away, and each time he glanced away his jaw worked slightly, as if chewing down words.

By nine the pub had emptied with unusual efficiency. Farmers who would normally have stayed until closing gathered coats and said their goodnights in clusters, and several of them said them to Tam rather than to the room, and more than one pair of eyes slid across me without landing, the way you look at a road accident from the far lane—quickly, guiltily, grateful for the speed of your own passing.

I stayed. Stubbornness is the last organ to freeze.

"I'll take a room," I said to Tam, when the fire had burned down to a red argument among the logs. "Just for tonight. I can't— the house is cold."

Something moved behind his eyes. Pity, maybe. Or apology. "Course," he said. "Number three. Top of the stairs." He hesitated, wiping the glass that needed no wiping. "Miss Dunmore."

"It's Emrys, actually. Just—Emrys."

"Emrys." He tried the name out and didn't seem to like its weight. "You'll want to keep the window shut tonight. Frost's coming down hard off the moor. Gets in the glass, sometimes. Draws on it."

"Draws on it," I repeated.

"Patterns," he said. "Like ferns. Like—" and then he stopped himself, visibly, the way a man steps back from a stair edge in the dark, and said, "Like nothing. Goodnight."

He left the drawing on the bar. Unfolded. Face up. As if he wanted it where he could see it, or as if putting it away had begun to feel like hiding evidence.

I looked at it properly for the first time then, under the lamp. There were details I'd missed. Behind the chair-figure, filling the rest of the page, Wren had coloured the entire background grey—except for one patch, low in the corner, which she had left white, and in the white patch she had drawn, in brown crayon, a staircase. Descending. Nine steps. I counted them twice. Nine steps down into a whiteness that was either light or the absence of everything, and at the bottom of the stairs, barely visible, a smudge of black that might have been a doorway or might have been nothing at all, because children smudge, children scribble, children make marks that mean nothing—

Nine steps. I had counted the cellar stairs at Maren's house on my second day, idly, the way you count things. Nine.

I picked up the drawing. The paper was cold. Not cool—cold, cold as a windowpane in January, cold as something that had been outside a long time, and I stood in the empty pub holding a child's crayon drawing that chilled my fingers through the paper, and I said aloud, to the empty room, to the fire, to myself:

"I don't believe this."

My breath fogged. In a warm room, by a fire, my breath fogged, a small ghost leaving my mouth and unravelling in the lamplight.

Upstairs, number three was clean and narrow and smelled of lavender polish, and I locked the door and put the chair under the handle and then laughed—one short, cracked sound—because I had just done the thing with the chair again, the same futile ritual, the same superstition dressed as practicality. I sat on the bed with my recorder on my knees and played back the night's ambient recording, hunting for anything, some evidence, some thread of the objective world to hold.

Wind. Fire. The creak of the building settling. And underneath, so faint I had to press the headphones hard against my ears and turn the gain until the hiss swelled: a hum. Low. Patient. Rising and falling like breathing. Coming, according to the microphone's geometry, from everywhere at once.

I pulled the headphones off. The room was silent. The silence had texture, though—the silence of a held breath.

*I don't believe.* I lay back on the bed and said it to the ceiling. *I don't believe in the chair. I don't believe in the frost. I don't believe in the fourth verse, the ledger, the debt, the heir, any of it. I am a folklorist. I collect songs. Songs are just songs.*

And then, from somewhere below the floor—from below the floor of a building that had no cellar that anyone had mentioned, though of course I hadn't asked, why hadn't I asked—came the humming. Faint. Familiar. The melody from the harvest gathering, the lullaby that had died in the room at verse four, the tune that lived under songs the way roots live under grass.

It stopped. Politely. As if it had only meant to say goodnight.

I did not sleep. Toward dawn I drifted at the edge of it, and in that grey country between waking and dreaming I saw the drawing clearly, unbidden: the too-wide smile, the nine descending steps, the small black doorway. And I understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only to the exhausted, that the picture was not a warning aimed at me.

It was a record. The way my photographs were records. The way Maren's ledgers were records. The child had drawn what was already true, what was already in progress, what the cameras and the frost and the mirrors had all been patiently documenting for weeks: that somewhere, on some page of the world's accounting, I was already seated. Already smiling. Already home.

In the morning I would find the old woman—Edith Marlowe, the one who had stopped the song, the one whose eyes had followed me all through the gathering with something that hadn't been hostility but had been worse, something like pity. In the morning I would knock on her door and I would not recite my little creed of disbelief. I would ask her to tell me about the chair.

But that was morning. For now the frost crept up the outside of window number three, feathering the glass, and even half-dreaming I could see that these patterns were not ferns, not flowers, not any accident of temperature and moisture. They were letters. Small, deliberate, patient, spelling themselves outward from the corners toward the centre of the pane, and they spelled a name, and the name was mine.

* * *

I woke to sunlight and the sound of Wren crying downstairs, and Tam's voice, low and shaking, saying over and over: *Who taught you that? Who taught you to draw that?*

I dressed quickly and came down. The bar was empty except for the three of them. Wren stood by the fire clutching a fresh sheet of paper, tears cutting through the dusting of sleep still on her cheeks, and Tam held the new drawing at arm's length as though it burned.

He didn't offer it to me. But I saw it.

A chair. Empty this time. Drawn in careful black against a grey wall—and beside it, standing, a second figure with my hair and my jacket, arms crossed, frowning. Awake. Refusing.

And below it, in Wren's uneven six-year-old letters, copied out syllable by syllable from somewhere, from some voice only she could hear, five words that turned the blood in my wrists to ice water:

*SHE DIDN'T SIT EITHER. ONCE.*

Maren. It meant Maren. Forty years of *held until sunrise, paid*, forty years of entries in a ledger written in a steady hand—and somewhere before all that, before the first entry, there had been a night when Maren Dunmore had stood where I was standing, and refused, and survived it.

Or hadn't.

The old woman would know. I put on my coat while Tam tried to comfort his daughter and Wren wept and would not say who had taught her, and I stepped out into a morning so bright and cold it hurt, and the frost on the inn's windows was already melting in the sun, running down the glass in long transparent lines like the tracks of something that had wept there all night and was glad to go.

Edith Marlowe's cottage was at the far end of Church Lane. I walked fast. Behind me, faint through the inn's open door, I could still hear Wren crying—and beneath it, so low that perhaps only I caught it, the humming had started again, gentle as a lullaby, keeping time with my footsteps all the way down the lane.
