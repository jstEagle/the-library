## 13. Maren's Last Recording

The ledgers had a smell I came to know better than my own hands by the third night: dust and tallow and something faintly sweet underneath, like apples rotting in a locked room. Forty-one of them, one for nearly every year of Maren Dunmore's adult life, stacked in the cellar office in date order with a precision that would have shamed any archivist I'd worked with at the university. Each page ruled by hand. Each entry in the same small, upright script that never wavered, never hurried, as if she'd written all of it in a single sitting stretched across four decades.

*Held until sunrise. Paid.*

I'd stopped flinching at the phrase somewhere around ledger nineteen. That was its own kind of horror—the way dread wears smooth with handling, becomes furniture. I sat in her office chair with the lamp turned up as bright as it would go and read entries like a man reading tide tables, and outside the frost was drawing its slow geometry on every pane of glass in the house, and I told myself this was research. Documentation. The folklorist's oldest defense: *I am only observing.*

The last ledger ended in March of the previous year. After *Paid*, after her name in the column where names went—*M.D., heir*—there were three blank pages, and then, tucked into the binding at the back, a flat cardboard sleeve I might have missed entirely if the lamp hadn't caught its edge.

A reel-to-reel tape. Five inches across. Handwritten on the label in that same upright script:

*For whoever comes after.*

My hands were not steady. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the only currency I have left. My hands shook, and I carried the tape upstairs anyway, because whatever my grandmother had left me, I would not let it sit alone in the dark below the floorboards, an arm's length from that warm brick.

* * *

Maren's machine still worked. Of course it did; everything in that house worked, everything waited. It sat on the shelf above the study desk beneath a canvas dust cover, oiled and threaded, as though she'd cleaned it the morning of the day she died—as though she'd known someone would need it soon. I found blank reels in the drawer beneath, labeled in her hand: *field recordings, 1974–1981*, and beside them a single unmarked spool already half-erased, ready for use.

I threaded the tape with fingers that wouldn't quite obey. Pressed play.

First there was the hiss, the long oceanic hiss of old magnetic stock, and then a click, and then my grandmother's voice.

I had heard recordings of her before—or thought I had. There was a cassette from 1989 in the university archive, part of a collection on West Country harvest songs, eleven seconds of Maren Dunmore singing a ploughing tune before the interviewer's questions crowded her out. In that recording she sounded like what she was: an old woman doing a favor for a visiting academic, patient, slightly amused, holding something back the way you hold back a story you've decided isn't for strangers.

This was not that voice.

This voice was close to the microphone—so close I could hear the wet click of her swallowing—and it was low, and it was steady in the way a rope bridge is steady, which is to say not at all, which is to say someone has chosen to be steady and is paying for the choice with every second.

"I'm going to sing it," the voice said. "All four verses. If you're hearing this, you'll understand why no one else ever would."

Then she sang.

The lullaby. The one from the harvest gathering, the one that had died in the old woman's throat at verse four while forty villagers studied their drinks and the fire cracked and nobody breathed. I had it on my recorder still—I'd played it back a dozen times, hunting the second voice beneath the melody, the one that hummed along a half-step under, patient as water finding a level.

Maren sang verses one through three the way they were meant to be sung, soft and round, a mother's voice, a hearth voice. And then she came to the fourth verse, and the room around me changed.

I don't mean the temperature. I'd stopped trusting temperature days ago; the house ran cold at the edges like a body with bad circulation, and I'd learned to ignore it. I mean the quality of the air itself, the way sound moved through it. Verse four was not shaped like the other verses. The melody bent where it should have risen. The words were in no language I knew and yet I understood them completely, the way you understand a dream, the way you understand being watched. They were words about a door. About keeping a door. About how a guest who is never fed will eat the walls instead, and how the walls are warm, and how the warmth is borrowed, always borrowed, always owed—

And beneath her voice, that second voice again. Closer now. Not humming. Singing with her. A breath behind each syllable, like a shadow cast by sound.

On the tape, Maren faltered—not in the melody but in her breath, one ragged intake—and pushed through to the end. The last note hung. The hiss resumed.

Then she spoke, and this was the worst part, because she spoke the way people speak when they know exactly who is listening and when.

"If you're hearing this, you're the heir. I'm sorry. I tried to end it twice—once in '76, once in '99. You'll find the dates in the ledgers. Both times I got as far as the chair." A pause, long enough that I checked the machine to see if the tape had run out. "It doesn't want the song finished. Understand that. Three verses keep it. Four release it. That's why we never sing the fourth. That's why your great-great-grandmother cut it out of the hymnal with scissors, why the village would rather die than repeat a song—repetition wears the lock loose. Every time a song is sung twice, the door remembers it's a door."

The tape hissed. Somewhere in the hiss, very faintly, I thought I heard breathing that wasn't hers.

"But here's what they never told you, because telling it makes you responsible for it." Her voice dropped lower, and I leaned toward the speaker like a fool leaning toward a fire. "Three verses keep it. But a song sung *to* it—named, finished, given back—that's different. It hates being named. It spent two hundred years making sure no one would learn its name, and it nearly succeeded. It's in the parish records. It's in the fourth verse, if you listen past the melody, if you listen to what's underneath. The name is the erased syllable. It's been the erased syllable all along."

"Don't sit," she said. "That's what it wants. The chair isn't punishment—it's invitation. Sit down, hold out until sunrise, smile, and the debt renews for another generation, and another Dunmore grows up in this house learning to be afraid of songs. Forty years I sat. Forty years, and every sunrise I paid, and every payment taught it our shape a little better. Don't sit."

"Sing."

The word came out cracked, and behind it—under it—I heard the other voice stop. Not fade. Stop, mid-breath, the way a listener goes quiet.

"It hates being named," Maren said. "So name it."

And then the tape began to degrade.

I noticed it first as a thinning in her voice, a silvery corrosion at the edges of each word, as though the sound were being eaten from the rim inward. Then whole phrases began to drop out—not silence where words had been, but a kind of absence, a hole in the audio shaped exactly like her voice, so that I could feel the contours of what was missing. I grabbed the reel counter and wrote down the numbers as sections vanished, some archivist reflex firing even as my skin crawled: *0:41 lost, 1:17 lost, 2:03 lost.* The degradation moved through the tape in order, forward, deliberate, like something reading over her shoulder and deleting as it went.

Her final instructions survived longest. I transcribed them onto the back of a ledger page, my handwriting degrading alongside the tape into something barely legible:

*The true name is in the fourth verse. Listen underneath. The old woman knows the half of it—Edith Marsh, born 1931, ask her about the Year Without a Summer, ask her what the first Dunmore promised. She'll be afraid. Ask anyway.*

*The brick in the cellar alcove is warm because it isn't a wall. It's a held breath. Two hundred years of held breath.*

*When you sing the fourth verse, put the name where the hole is. Don't hesitate. Hesitation is a kind of sitting.*

*And Emrys—*

There the tape did something I have no framework for. Her voice said my name—my name, which she could not have known, which I had not yet been born when this reel was recorded, or so I told myself about the date penciled on the spine—and then the next word was gone, eaten, and the word after that, and the word after that, a whole sentence hollowed out, and I sat in the lamplight with my pen hovering over the paper trying to catch the ghost-shape of what she'd said. Something with an *l* in it. Something gentle. Something that made my eyes sting without my permission, grief arriving ahead of its reason.

The tape ran on. Hiss, and holes, and at the very end, in the last ten seconds before the leader slapped empty against the machine, her voice surfaced one more time from the corrosion, thin as light through ice:

"—love is also a debt, and I paid mine, and I'm sorry, and the frost is coming up the lane now, so I'll say it plainly: *it is only a guest. Guests can be released.*"

Click. The reel spun out. The lamp buzzed.

* * *

I sat for a long time afterward. Long enough that the frost on the study window advanced visibly, its crystalline doorway gaining a lintel, a threshold, a handle. I had stopped photographing the patterns; the photographs showed only the chair, and I no longer needed documentation to believe my own eyes. Belief had become the cheapest thing I owned.

What I did instead was take out my field recorder, the good one, the one I'd carried to eleven countries, and I played Maren's tape into it, copying what remained. And then I did the thing I should have done at the harvest gathering, the thing fear had stopped me from doing with forty witnesses in the room: I isolated the fourth verse. Stripped the melody away with filters and gain, dug down beneath my grandmother's voice into the layer where the second voice lived.

It took two hours. The house hummed around me the whole time, patient, and I hummed back at it, tunelessly, defiantly, because Maren had said hesitation was a kind of sitting and I intended to stand.

At half past three in the morning, buried under the melody like a stone under moss, I found it.

Not a word. A name-shaped absence—a place in the song where a syllable had been cut, and the cut was old, older than the recording, older than the hymnal with its scissored page, a wound in the music itself that every singer since had stepped around without knowing why. And around the wound, the remaining sounds arranged themselves like scar tissue forming letters. Consonants I could almost parse. A vowel that opened like a door.

I won't write the name here. Not because writing it frightens me anymore—though it does—but because Maren was specific, and she was right: the name belongs in the song, in the hole, at the right moment, spoken aloud in the right room. Written anywhere else it is just a sound, and worse than useless. It needs to be *given*. Sung home.

But I will say this: once I heard it, I understood the pity that had been growing in me all week, the pity I kept trying to drown in rationalism. The name was old. Older than English. Older than the parish. It was a name for something that waits, and keeps, and holds warmth it was promised and never given, and it sounded—God help me—it sounded *lonely*. Two hundred years beneath a bricked alcove, listening to a family sing three verses of a lullaby and stop, every generation, every time, like a knock that never gets answered.

The village called it a debt. The ledgers called it a bargain. But sitting there in the blue hour before dawn, listening to the scar tissue of a stolen name, I understood that we had done something crueler than owe it. We had invited it, seated it, and then spent two centuries refusing to learn what to call it, because a guest with a name can be spoken to, and spoken to can be answered, and answered can be released—and released means the warmth stops coming.

We hadn't kept it prisoner. We'd kept it *unacknowledged*, which is the only imprisonment that lasts forever.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin. The frost on the windows retreated to the corners but did not leave; it had learned economy. I slept two hours in the chair by the desk, upright, having quietly removed every seat I could carry from the bedroom, and dreamed of nothing, which felt like mercy.

When I woke, Edith Marsh was standing at the front gate.

I saw her through the frosted pane of the landing window—an old woman in a brown coat, motionless, looking up at the house with the expression of someone attending a funeral she disapproves of. Behind her, at the edge of the lane, two other figures stood back among the bare trees, watching, not approaching. Word had traveled, then. Word always traveled in Hollowmere; it was the one crop that never failed.

She didn't knock. She stood at the gate the way you stand at a grave, and after a while she took something from her coat pocket and set it on the gatepost: a sprig of rowan, and beneath it, folded small, a square of paper gone soft with age at the creases.

Then she turned and walked away, fast for her age, not looking back, and the two figures in the trees melted off after her, and the lane was empty.

I went down and retrieved the paper. Her handwriting, shaky, pressed hard enough to tear:

*You've been asking. I'll come at dusk, when the frost thins. Bring the tape. Bring the verse. And child—whatever she told you on that reel, know that we knew. All of us, every generation, we knew what Maren carried alone, and we let her carry it, because the alternative was sharing it. When you meet me tonight, you'll be meeting forty years of cowardice wearing my face.*

*I know half the name. Your grandmother knew the other half. Between us, maybe, the whole.*

Below that, added later, the ink a different pen, the hand shakier:

*They nailed rowan to your gate this morning. Do you understand what that means? Rowan is protection for the household inside. They weren't protecting you.*

*They were marking the boundary of what they're willing to lose.*

I stood in the cold lane holding the note, and behind me the house waited with its open cellar door and its warm brick and its patient humming, and ahead of me the village lay under a sky the color of dishwater, every window I could see furred white at the corners, every chimney smoking, every door shut.

Dusk was nine hours away. I went inside to wait, and to practice.
