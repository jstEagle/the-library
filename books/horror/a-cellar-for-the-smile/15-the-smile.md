## 15. The Smile

I woke on the second morning with frost inside the glass.

Not on it—inside it, between the panes, as though the cold had learned to live in the house's breath. The patterns had changed overnight. Where before they had drawn doorways and descending stairs, now they drew something smaller, more intimate: a hand, open, palm up. Waiting to be held.

I did not touch the window. I had learned that much. Instead I made tea with water boiled on the camping stove because I no longer trusted the taps—the pipes sang at night, a low harmonic note that rose when I listened too closely—and I carried the mug from room to room like a man carrying a candle through a house he suspects is not his own.

It was in the hall mirror that I saw it first.

The mirror is old, silvered glass gone soft at the edges, hung in Maren's house since before my mother was born. I passed it the way I had passed it a hundred times in the week since the doors sealed themselves, and something in my peripheral vision snagged—a wrongness so slight the mind tries to file it under *tiredness*, under *grief*, under any label kinder than the truth.

I stopped. I turned back.

The woman in the mirror was smiling.

I was not smiling.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last tool I have left. My mouth was closed, my jaw set in the flat line of a person who has slept badly and expects worse. But the reflection's mouth curved—gently, warmly, the way a host smiles at a guest arriving out of the rain. The smile reached its eyes. It was a good smile. It was a *welcoming* smile. And it sat on my face like a hand laid over mine on a table: patient, proprietary, certain of its right to be there.

I raised my hand. The reflection raised its hand a half-second late—not lagging, exactly. *Deciding* to follow.

I did what any rational person does. I told myself the glass was warped. Old mirrors lie; the silvering pools and shifts; light does strange things at seven in the morning in a house full of frost. I leaned close. My breath fogged the mirror, and when the fog cleared, the reflection was doing something worse than smiling.

It was looking past me. Down the hall. Toward the cellar door, which stood at the end of the corridor, shut, locked, and—as I confirmed with my own eyes an hour later, every time I checked—open.

* * *

The photographs were worse.

I have kept a camera beside my bed since the fourth night, after the frost wrote my name across the kitchen window in letters of white fern. Documentation is a folklorist's instinct: if the evidence refuses to travel, record it anyway, timestamp it, make it real by making it repeatable. Every morning since, I had photographed the windows, the cellar door, the chair—if it had moved again in the night—and finally myself, propped against the headboard, hollow-eyed, a study for whoever would write the account of my dying.

That morning's photograph showed me sitting in the chair.

Not standing against the headboard. Not holding the camera. *Sitting*—in the plain wooden chair, facing the camera, hands folded in my lap, wearing the smile from the mirror. The bedroom around me was correct: the same quilt, the same crack in the plaster shaped like a river delta, the same grey light through curtained glass. Only I was wrong. Only I was seated where I had never sat, composed as though for a portrait, patient as furniture.

I checked the metadata. 7:14 a.m.—four minutes after I woke. Four minutes during which, as far as my memory served, I had been boiling water, watching frost-hands form on the kitchen window, passing the hall mirror.

Four minutes I could not account for.

I went back through the previous mornings. The one before: me in bed, correct, exhausted, real. The one before *that*: me in the chair. Again. The smile again. And the morning before that, the very first photograph I'd taken after the doors sealed—I had assumed it showed me asleep, a blur of quilt and hair—but blown up large, sharpened until the grain screamed, it was the chair. Empty. Positioned at the foot of the bed. Facing me.

It had been facing me all along. I simply hadn't been ready to see.

I sat down on the floor among the scattered prints and understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, what was happening. This was not haunting. Haunting is a thing done *to* a place. This was editing. Somewhere in the margins of the morning, in the four-minute gaps my memory papered over, I was being moved—set down in the chair, arranged, smiled into place—and then set back, and the hours stitched up around the excision so neatly that only the photographs bled.

Time was being edited around me. Or I was being edited out of time, one missing minute at a time, and replaced with the version of me that belonged in the chair.

* * *

I tried an experiment. I am a scholar; experiment is my native prayer.

At noon I set three cameras in the bedroom—one digital, one film, one my phone recording video on a shelf aimed at the empty chair—and I left the room, locked the door behind me, and stood in the corridor counting my own heartbeats for one hour. When I unlocked the door, all three devices had recorded the same thing: an hour of empty chair, dust motes, shifting light.

Except the final frame of each. On every device, the last image—captured in the second before I re-entered, according to the timestamps—showed the chair occupied. Me, seated, smiling, hands folded, looking directly into each lens as though it had called my name and I had answered.

Three cameras. Three formats. One lie, told unanimously.

I burned nothing. I smashed nothing. I sat on the bedroom floor and laughed, once, a sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a person who has just realized the joke has always been about them. Because here was the thing I could not say aloud, the thing that made the laughter die in my throat:

In the photographs, I looked *happy*. Not drugged, not possessed, not grimacing behind a mask of compliance. Happy the way you are happy at the end of a long journey, setting down your bags in a house that knows you. The smile was not a mask. It was a *homecoming*. Whatever sat in that chair wearing my face was not enduring the sitting.

It was enjoying it.

And some part of me—the part that had hummed along without noticing to the lullaby's fourth verse, the part that had felt the cellar's warmth through the floorboards at night like a hearth through a wall—that part had not objected. That part had *gone willingly* into the four missing minutes, and come back smoothed and settled, and said nothing, because there was nothing to complain about. That is the horror I cannot shake, writing this by lamplight with the frost climbing the inside of the glass: not that the thing below can take me.

That it can make sitting look like my idea.

* * *

In the afternoon I walked to the village, because walls were becoming untrustworthy and I needed witnesses, even hostile ones. The frost line ended at Maren's gate; beyond it the lanes lay brown and ordinary, hedges heavy with hips, smoke rising straight from chimneys into a sky the color of old pewter. Normalcy, performed beautifully. I might have believed in it, a week ago.

Mrs. Tregale was sweeping her step. She saw me coming and her broom slowed, and she looked—at my boots, at the gate, at the middle distance—everywhere but at my face. I had meant to ask about provisions, about the road, about whether anyone in Hollowmere remembered that a living person was being walled up alive at the village edge with a wooden chair for a cellmate.

What came out of my mouth instead was: "Do I seem different to you?"

The broom stopped.

She looked up then, finally, and whatever she saw made her take half a step backward onto her own threshold. "You've got your grandmother's way of standing," she said, which was not an answer, and then, softer, almost kindly, almost to herself: "She used to smile like that. Near the end. Standing perfectly still and smiling at nothing, at the wall, at the garden. Forty years, and near the end she couldn't stop it. It leaked." Her knuckles whitened on the broom handle. "Go home, Emrys. Go home while your face is still yours."

"I'm not smiling," I said.

"No," she agreed, and resumed sweeping, and would not look at me again. "Not yet."

* * *

I went home. There was nowhere else; the roads looped; the frost held the perimeter; and besides, some traitorous gravity in my chest pulled toward the house now, toward the warm dark under the floorboards, and I no longer trusted my feet to carry me anywhere else even if somewhere else existed.

The light was failing. I lit lamps in every room, a childish abundance of flame, and made myself eat, and made myself check the cameras, and made myself not check the mirror. For two hours I worked on Maren's ledgers by lamplight, transcribing dates, building the timeline: forty years of entries in her small upright hand. *Held until sunrise. Paid.* Over and over, decade after decade, the same sentence, the same quiet bookkeeping of survival. Once, in the winter of 1987, a single deviation, pressed so hard the nib nearly tore the page:

*It sang to me tonight. I almost sang back.*

Below it, later, in ink gone brown: *Never sing back. Never answer. Never let it finish learning your voice.*

I read that line six times. Then I heard the humming.

It came up through the floor the way heat comes up through floorboards in a house with a good cellar fire—diffuse at first, sourceless, more vibration than sound. Then it gathered itself, found a melody, and I knew the melody. Everyone in Hollowmere knew the melody. It was the harvest lullaby, the one the whole room had gone silent around, the one whose fourth verse no throat in the village would complete.

But this version was sung in my voice.

Not an imitation. Not a mimicry with the seams showing. It was *my* voice, my particular grain and placement, the slight hoarseness I get in cold rooms, recorded somehow from the inside of my own skull and played back through the bones of the house. It hummed the first verse. The second. The third. And then it paused, delicately, at the edge of the fourth, the way a guest pauses at a doorway, and waited.

And I realized my lips had parted. That my throat had begun, without instruction, to shape the first syllable of the answer—to *sing back*, to complete the call-and-response older than the parish records, older than the Year Without a Summer. To give it the last piece of my voice it didn't yet own.

I clamped my hand over my mouth. I bit down until I tasted copper. The humming below swelled—not angry, never angry; *pleased*, the way a teacher is pleased when the pupil almost raises her hand—and then subsided, sinking back through the floorboards, down the stairs I would not look at, into the warm dark behind the brick.

The house went quiet. The frost on the windowpanes had drawn, in every room at once, the same new pattern: a face, seen front-on, smiling.

I did not sleep. I sat in the center of the bedroom with every lamp burning and Maren's ledger open on my knees, and at some hour past three I took the morning's ritual photograph—proof of self, proof of continuity, *I am here, I am still here*—and looked at the screen before I could stop myself.

The photo showed the bedroom. The lamp. The quilt. The chair, dragged sometime in the night to sit beside the bed, close enough that its occupant need only lean.

Empty.

For the first time since this began, the chair in the photograph was empty—and I understood why with a clarity that arrived like ice water. It was empty because I hadn't needed to be placed in it tonight. Tonight I had stayed on the floor, awake, biting my own hand. Tonight there had been no four missing minutes to fill.

Which meant the photographs had never been a haunting at all.

They were a *record*. A ledger, kept in silvered glass and silicon, of every sitting already completed—of all the time I had already given it without knowing, stitched away into the gaps of my memory. The chair in the pictures wasn't a prophecy.

It was an accounting. And the account, entry by entry, minute by minute, was approaching a total.

On the back page of Maren's ledger, beneath forty years of *held until sunrise, paid*, her final entry waited in ink so fresh it seemed still wet. I had read it before and read it as threat. Reading it now, with my own smile leaking at the corners of my mouth, I read it as arithmetic:

*The heir will hold out until sunrise. The heir will hold out until sunrise. The heir will hold out until*

The sentence does not end. It runs to the bottom of the page, off the edge, and continues—my stomach turns cold as I write this—on the inside of my forearm, in fine white lines, like frost fern, like scars, in handwriting I know is mine because it is also hers.

Outside, the sky is going grey at the edges. The humming has started again, and this time it is not asking.

This time, it is singing the fourth verse—and waiting for me to remember that I already know the words.
