## 19. Sunrise Vigil

The chair weighed less than it should have.

That was the first thing Emrys noticed as they lifted it from the landing where it had dragged itself in the night—the lightness of it, the way the wood gave under their hands like something that had been waiting so long to be carried that it had made itself easy to carry. It was a plain thing, ash-coloured and unadorned, its seat worn smooth by generations of sitting. Forty years of Maren Dunmore's weight were polished into it. Perhaps two hundred years of Dunmores before her. The grain of the wood seemed to shift when Emrys wasn't looking directly at it, the way frost shifts on glass, rearranging itself into patterns that almost resolve and then don't.

They did not let themselves think about how it had climbed the stairs. They did not let themselves think about the moment—two hours past midnight, their body wrung out like a rag, their knees folding toward that smooth-worn seat with a smile already assembling itself on their face—when they had caught their own hands on the armrests and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that they were three seconds from becoming the photograph.

Three seconds. That was all the margin the thing below allowed. And they had spent them.

Now the house was silent in the particular way houses are silent before dawn—not asleep but holding its breath. Frost sealed every window from the outside, thick as a second pane, and through it the coming daylight came down grey and diffuse, like light through gauze over a wound. The kitchen window bore the completed doorway now, drawn in feathered white: a doorframe, a lintel, and beneath it, descending into the lower half of the glass where no staircase could logically be, steps going down and down until the frost ran out of window to draw on.

Emrys stood at the top of the cellar stairs with the chair in their arms and the reel-to-reel tape's last words repeating behind their eyes like a bell that wouldn't finish fading.

*Don't sit. Sing. It hates being named.*

And beneath that, half-erased by the old woman's guilt-trembling voice, delivered in the dark of the frost line like contraband: the name. Or most of it. *Hollow* was the part everyone knew, because Hollowmere had been named for it, or it for the village, or both for each other, the way a married couple grows to share one face. But there had been a second syllable once, worn away by two centuries of careful not-saying, and the old woman had given Emrys what she had of it—a sound like wind finding the mouth of a cave—and told them: *You'll know the rest when you need it. It was always yours. You're a Dunmore.*

The humming came up through the floorboards then, faint and patient, the same note it had held all night. Not a threat. An invitation. It had been making invitations for two hundred years, and every Dunmore heir had answered by sitting down and smiling and saying nothing, keeping it company until sunrise, feeding it warmth and presence and silence, year after year, generation after generation, a guest kept forever in a room without a door.

Emrys thought of the bricked-up alcove. Of the warmth of the brick under their palm, warm as a sleeping animal, warm as a held breath. Of the parish record they'd found, the ink brown with age: *In the yeere of the Great Cold, when the wells froze to the gravel and the cattle died standing, Edith Dunmore did singe up that which lay beneath, and bargained.* Promised it warmth. Company. Heirs. And then, when the frost broke and spring came and the village lived, the village had looked at what they'd invited in and reached for the bricks.

It wasn't evil. Emrys had turned that over all night, turning it like a stone in the hand until the sharp edges wore familiar. It was *kept*. A guest who was never allowed to leave. A singer sung up into the light and then walled in with the singing still in its throat. Two hundred years of heirs sitting in front of its wall, smiling, humming along to everything except the fourth verse—the verse with its name in it—because naming it might mean acknowledging it, and acknowledging it might mean asking it what it wanted, and asking it what it wanted might mean having to answer.

The humming climbed another note. Patient. So patient. It had waited out forty years of Maren; it could wait out this dawn too, if Emrys sat, if Emrys smiled, if Emrys kept the family's oldest promise: company, but never conversation.

"No," Emrys said aloud, to the house, to the frost, to themselves. Their breath hung in the cold air like a small ghost. "Not tonight."

They carried the chair down the cellar stairs.

* * *

The cellar was colder than the rest of the house, which should not have been possible, since the rest of the house was sealed in frost, but the cold down here was different—it had texture, intention, a direction. It flowed out of the alcove wall like water out of a cracked jug, pooling around Emrys's ankles as they descended, and the humming was louder here, no longer through the floorboards but through the stone itself, rising out of the earth the way heat rises off summer tarmac, except inverted, a cold radiance, a song made of winter.

The alcove waited at the far end of the cellar, exactly where it had always been, exactly where Emrys had fled from it ten days ago with their skepticism in ruins. The bricks glowed faintly in the dark—or didn't glow, precisely, but held the darkness differently than the walls around them, the way ice holds light differently than water. Warm air shimmered above them, barely visible, a heat-haze in a place where nothing should be warm.

Emrys set the chair down facing the alcove. The legs touched the flagstones with four small clicks that sounded, in the silence, like a lock turning.

Then they sat down.

Not surrender. Positioning. They made themselves say the word inside their own head, made themselves hold the distinction the way you hold a rope over a drop: *I am not sitting because it called me. I am sitting because I choose where I meet it.* The seat was cold under them, colder than wood had any right to be, and yet some treacherous part of their body wanted to relax into it, wanted it the way a drowning thing wants the water closing overhead. The smile began at the corners of their mouth—they felt it arrive, felt it as an invasion, a tenant moving into muscles they hadn't offered for rent—and Emrys pressed their tongue hard against the back of their teeth and breathed through it, and the smile receded like a tide going out, grudging, taking sand with it.

The humming stopped.

In its absence the silence was enormous, total, a held breath two hundred years long. Emrys became aware of their own heartbeat, loud and mortal and absurd, and of the frost on the high cellar window beginning, very slowly, to change—to stop being pattern and start being script, letters forming in the white feathers of it, and though Emrys couldn't read them they knew without knowing how they knew that the letters spelled their name. *Emrys. Emrys. Emrys.* Written over and over in the frost like a child practicing, or a lover, or a debt collector.

"Edith sang you up," Emrys said. Their voice sounded very small in the cellar, and very human, and they took a strange comfort in that. "During the Year Without a Summer. She promised you warmth, and company, and heirs. And she got her spring. And the village got its harvests back. And you got—" they gestured at the wall, at the bricks, at the two hundred years of masonry between the creature and the sky "—this."

The frost letters on the window stilled.

"I'm the last heir," Emrys said. "There won't be another after me. I've broken the inheritance—I'm the only Dunmore left, and I'm not having children in this house, and I'm not passing this ledger to anyone. So whatever happens tonight is the end of the bargain either way. The only question left is whether it ends with me sitting here smiling until sunrise, one more time, forever, or whether it ends with someone finally saying your name."

The brick did not crack. The cold did not surge. Nothing dramatic happened at all, and somehow that was worse—that vast attention settling over Emrys like snowfall, weighing them, considering them, the patience of a thing that had learned that all visitors eventually sat down.

So Emrys began to sing.

* * *

The lullaby was the first song every Hollowmere child learned and the first one they were taught to abandon. Three verses, sweet and simple, a cradle-rocking tune about a boat on a dark lake and a lantern at the shore. Everyone knew the melody of the fourth verse existed because the third verse ended on a chord that leaned into it, hungry, unfinished—but no one sang it. At the harvest gathering, old Sera Wren had opened her mouth on the fourth verse and the whole room had gone silent as a held funeral, and Emrys's covert recording had caught what the room's silence had been hiding: a second voice beneath the melody, low and cracked and achingly human, singing words that no one in the village had sung aloud in living memory.

Maren's tape had degraded as it played, the fourth verse dissolving into hiss halfway through, but Emrys had listened to it eleven times, and grief is an excellent teacher, and they had it now. All of it. Except the name.

Except the name, which was half-erased, a hole in the middle of the song shaped like everything the village had spent two centuries refusing to say.

Emrys sang the first verse, and the frost on the window crept inward from the edges.

They sang the second verse, and the warm haze above the bricks thickened, and somewhere inside the wall something shifted its weight, the way a person shifts in a chair when a story they know is being told.

They sang the third verse, and the candle they'd brought guttered flat though there was no draught, and the cold gathered around them like a congregation leaning forward, and Emrys felt the fourth verse rise in their throat like water rising in a well, inevitable, and felt the smile begin again at the corners of their mouth—not the creature's smile this time but their own, terrified and fierce—and they came to the erased syllable, the hollow place in the song, and—

—and they knew it.

The old woman had been right. It rose in them whole, the missing sound, the worn-away syllable, and it fit their mouth like their own name, because it *was* their name, in the sense that everything the Dunmores had ever been was braided through it. It was a sound like ice cracking on a pond in spring. It was a sound like a door opening after two hundred years.

Emrys sang the fourth verse, and put the name in it.

* * *

The brick split with a sound like a rifle shot.

A seam of light opened down the centre of the alcove—not firelight, not lamplight, but the pale gold of a dawn that had no business existing underground, spilling through the crack in thin blades. More cracks spread from the first, branching like frost in reverse, like veins, like the branches of the rowan someone had nailed to Emrys's gate, and through them came warmth, actual warmth, rolling into the cellar in waves that made the candle stand up straight and tall for the first time all night.

And through the breaking wall, as the bricks sloughed and crumbled and fell away like old scales, the figure came forward.

Gaunt. Tall. Made of rime and patience, of hoarfrost grown into the shape of a person the way a fence-post grows into the shape of the tree that leans on it. Its face was the face from the photographs' extra chair—no, not the chair's face; the face the chair had always been facing. Its eyes were the deep blue of ice over deep water, and they were fixed on Emrys with an expression that Emrys had spent ten days learning to read in frost-patterns and ledger-margins and the pauses where songs refused to repeat:

Hope. Terrible, exhausted, two-hundred-years-long hope.

The humming started again, but different now—no longer patient, no longer an invitation repeated into emptiness. It was the melody of the lullaby, the fourth verse, sung back. Sung *with*. The creature's voice was cracked and low and it harmonized with Emrys's own, which had not stopped, which was still going, verse after verse, the name threaded through it like a silver wire, and together they sang the song complete for the first time since Edith Dunmore had sung it up out of the ground in the Year Without a Summer.

The frost on the cellar window began to melt. Emrys watched it happen between verses—watched the letters of their name lose their edges, run, become droplets, become ordinary water sliding down ordinary glass. Behind the glass, the sky was doing something impossible. It was lightening. Not slowly, not according to any schedule the season permitted, but quickly, urgently, the grey lifting into gold, and the first ray of sunrise came down the cellar stairs like a returned traveller, crossed the flagstones, and settled over the chair, and over Emrys, and over the gaunt figure standing in the ruin of its wall with dawn on its face.

It had never seen the sun. Emrys understood that suddenly, completely, the way you understand things in dreams. Two hundred years of dawns, and it had spent every one of them underground, counting them out in the company of smiling heirs who would not speak to it. It stood in the light now the way a man stands in rain after drought—still, disbelieving, letting it land.

The song ended. Emrys's voice gave out on the final note, ragged, and in the quiet that followed the creature looked at them, and inclined its head—an old courtesy, a traveller's thanks—and Emrys, hoarse and shaking and weeping without entirely knowing why, inclined theirs in return.

The chair, beneath them, sighed. When Emrys looked down, the smooth-worn seat was crumbling, the ash-coloured wood greying further, softening, sifting away at the edges like snow in sun. By the time they stood up from it, it was a mound of fine grey ash shaped briefly like a chair, and then not even that, just ash, just dust, just two hundred years of sitting finally finished.

The figure of rime turned toward the broken wall, toward the open dark beyond it where the earth went down and down, and paused there, at the threshold, looking back once more.

And Emrys, who had spent ten days learning every rule of this house, understood that this was the moment the whole inheritance had been building toward—not the sitting, not the smiling, not the vigil, but this: the open door, and the choice, and whether anyone would finally say the words that should have been said two hundred years ago, by Edith, by all of them, by every heir who sat and smiled and kept their cowardice polished smooth as the chair's seat.

"You were invited," Emrys said. Their voice was wrecked and it didn't matter. "You were welcomed. And you were wronged. I'm sorry. You're free."

The creature's frost-bright eyes closed. When they opened again, something in them had loosened, the way ice loosens, the way a fist loosens, the way everything loosens when spring finally comes.

It stepped backward into the dark, and the dark accepted it gently, the way a bed accepts the tired, and the humming faded downward, and outward, and away—not cut off, not silenced, but diminishing, a song being carried into distance rather than ended, until the cellar held only the drip of melting frost and the gold of full morning pouring down the stairs.

Emrys stood alone in the wreckage of the wall, in the water and the light, laughing and crying and unable to do either properly, alive, unsitting, unnamed by anything but themselves.

It was only when they climbed the stairs, stiff and salt-stung and blinking in the impossible brightness of the kitchen, that they saw the frost on the window had melted entirely—every pattern gone, every letter, every drawn doorway dissolved into clean running water—and that the village below was stirring awake under a clear sky for the first time in weeks.

And it was only when they got to the ledgers, needing—needing desperately—to see Maren's handwriting, to check the final page, to confirm what had been paid and what remained, that Emrys found the pages blank.

All of them. Every date, every entry, every *held until sunrise. Paid*, gone, the ink lifted from the paper as if it had never dried there, leaving only the ghost-creases of writing in empty cream-coloured leaves.

The price, whispered a voice in Emrys's memory that sounded terribly like the old woman. *Everything it was given, it takes when it goes. Even the giver.*

"Maren," Emrys said aloud, to the empty kitchen, and the name felt suddenly heavy in their mouth—heavy the way a thing feels heavy when it's the last of its kind.

Outside, down the lane, the first neighbour of the morning was walking past the gate, and Emrys threw open the door and called out to her—called her name, asked if she'd seen Maren, asked if she remembered—and the woman turned, squinting politely against the sun, and said:

"Seen who, dear?"
