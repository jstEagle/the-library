## 8. Edvard's Last Entry

I did not sleep so much as I negotiated with sleep, and the negotiations collapsed somewhere around two in the morning. The bed in the guest room was comfortable in the way of furniture that has been waiting a long time for someone to be comfortable in it, which is to say it felt less like rest and more like acceptance. I lay on top of the covers with my clothes on and my shoes by the door, because I am not an idiot, whatever else I may be.

The house breathed around me. That is the only word for it. Old houses settle, creak, tick as the pipes cool — I have heard them all, catalogued them all, filed them under *unmaintained*. Hollowmere did none of that. Hollowmere exhaled. Long, slow, patient breaths, like something lying beside me in the dark, counting my breathing to learn the rhythm of it.

I got up, took the lamp from the bedside table — an actual oil lamp, because of course there were no working outlets on that side of the house — and went back to the study.

The Edvard Lune report was where I'd left it, in the drawer of the desk, under a brass letter opener shaped like a pheasant. I had told myself I was finished with it. I had told myself a great many things since Tuesday.

* * *

Here is what I knew by lamplight, sitting in a dead man's chair in a dead man's study at two-forty in the morning:

Edvard Lune had surveyed this house in October 1974. His commission letter — the one I'd received, the one that had hired *me* — was written in his handwriting. Not similar. Identical. The same forward-leaning *h*, the same way the tail of his *y* hooked back like it was reluctant to let go of the word. I had compared them for an hour before dinner, letter to report, and the conclusion was inescapable and insane: the firm that hired me was corresponding in the hand of a man who had vanished from all records fifty years ago.

His report was unfinished. That was the first strange thing, before the mirrors, before the cellar door cleared its throat at me. A professional appraisal, forty pages of meticulous measurement — and then it simply stopped, mid-sentence, on page forty-one, in the section for *Outbuildings and Subterranean Structures*.

*The cellar requires further —*

That was it. *The cellar requires further.* Further what? Inspection, presumably. Time. Courage. Pick one.

But here is what I had noticed at dinner, between the heirs' identical smiles and the extra guests in the mirrors who did not touch their food: Lune's margins were full of pencil. Faint, hurried, written in what looked like a different mood entirely from the confident surveyor's hand of the body text. The heirs had set the report on the sideboard as a *courtesy*, they said, for reference. They had watched me take it upstairs with an expression I would describe as satisfaction, if satisfaction didn't usually come with teeth.

They wanted me to read it. That should have warned me off. It didn't. Curiosity is the one invoice I've never learned to refuse.

I flattened the pages under the lamp and began.

* * *

The marginalia started innocently enough. A measurement correction here. A note about moisture in the east foundation. Edvard Lune, apparently, was the kind of surveyor who talked to himself on paper, and I recognized the habit the way you recognize a relative's laugh.

Then, on page nine, beside a note about the hallway mirrors:

*They do not reflect. They remember. There is a difference and the difference is one second.*

I sat very still.

On page twelve, beside a sketch of the floor plan:

*The rooms are not fixed. Count them at your peril. The house counts back.*

Page seventeen, beside a routine note about the staircase:

*I have been here eleven days. The commission said three. I cannot account for the discrepancy and neither, it seems, can the house. It keeps trying to account for me instead.*

My lamp guttered. I trimmed the wick and kept reading, because stopping was no longer an option and I think we both know it stopped being an option around the time I caught my reflection waving at me a full second after I'd waved.

Page twenty-three. The handwriting had changed. Not the letters — the *pressure*. The pencil pressed so hard it had embossed the next three pages. I could read the ghost of it from the back, like braille:

*The song. The cellar door sings. I made the mistake of humming. It settled. God help me, it settled like a dog that has finally been acknowledged, and I felt — I felt glad. That is the trap. Not the door. The gladness.*

I looked up at the study mirror.

My reflection looked back at me, one second late, holding up its lamp one second after I held up mine. We regarded each other across the gap like two men on opposite banks of a river, both pretending the water wasn't there.

"Edvard," I said quietly. "You absolute fool. You came back the next day, didn't you."

The mirror said nothing. Mirrors are terrible conversationalists. But the reflection's mouth moved a half-second after mine closed, and I could have sworn — *could have sworn* — it was forming the word *yes*.

I turned back to the pages.

* * *

The marginalia on pages thirty through thirty-eight were a descent. I could track Edvard's deterioration not by his handwriting, which stayed maddeningly neat, but by his punctuation, which fell apart like a man's composure. Commas abandoned. Periods replaced by dashes — the typographical equivalent of someone talking faster and faster because the silence between sentences has started to answer back.

*The cellar sings verses one through three. I have transcribed them. They are a harvest song, older than the parish, older than the name Hollowmere. A working song. A counting song. You sing it at the gathering-in, you sing it to call the hands home —*

*— and there is a fourth verse.*

*There is always a fourth verse.*

*I heard it through the door last night. It was not sung TO me. It was sung AT the house. Or — no. No, I have it backwards. It was sung by the house, to itself, and the door is only the place where it leaks.*

I found myself holding the page very close to the lamp, the way you lean toward a fire, and I made myself sit back. My hands were not entirely steady. I noted this in the margin of my own notebook, because if I was going to come apart, I was going to come apart *professionally*, with documentation.

*The fourth verse is a binding. I am certain of it now. The first three verses gather. The fourth verse KEEPS. Whoever sings it aloud — not hums, not mouths, SINGS, with voice and breath and willingness behind it — is added to the count. The house counts its occupants the way a farmer counts his herd at the gathering-in. And the fourth verse is how you volunteer for the herd.*

*I have not sung it. I will not sing it. But I have caught myself, twice now, with my lips shaping the words. The house teaches it to you. It leaves the fourth verse hanging the way a host leaves a door open. It wants you to complete it. Completion is consent. Consent is belonging. Belonging is —*

The entry broke off. On the next page, in smaller writing, pressed lighter, as if written in a whisper:

*I saw them tonight. In the mirrors. People. Standing behind me in every reflection, in every room, and when I turn around the rooms are empty but the mirrors keep them. A woman in a green dress. A man with a surveyor's case like mine. They do not speak. They watch. I think they are trying to tell me something and I think the something is: do not finish the report.*

*The report is the contract. I understand that now. Every measurement I take, the house takes back. Every room I enter, the house enters me. The appraisal does not describe the property. The appraisal DESCRIBES ME INTO IT. Occupant: one. That is what the final page is for. That is what the final page has always been for.*

I set the pages down.

I did this carefully, the way you set down something that might be sleeping, and then I sat in Edvard Lune's chair in Edvard Lune's study and thought about the report the heirs had shown me at dinner. My report. Nearly complete. Forty pages of my own measurements, my own handwriting, needing only a signature on the final page.

*Occupant: one.*

They had shown it to me like a gift. Like a courtesy. Like a contract of employment, when it was a contract of *conveyance*, and the property being conveyed was me.

* * *

I am not a man who frightens easily, and I want to be precise about what I felt, because precision is the last tool I had left. It was not fear. Fear is for the moment the dog lunges. This was the earlier thing, the worse thing — the understanding, arriving cold and total, that the dog has been watching you for days and choosing the moment.

I stood up. The chair complained. The house exhaled.

And that is when I saw it.

In the study mirror, the room behind my reflection was occupied. Not by the faded figures from dinner — by me. There were two of me in the glass. One standing at the desk, lamp in hand, one second behind the world. And one seated in the chair I had just vacated, head bowed over the pages, exactly where I had been, and it was not leaving.

I turned around. The chair was empty. Of course it was empty. Chairs are honest that way; it's mirrors that lie by telling the truth too slowly.

I looked back. The seated reflection was still there. Still bent over the report. And as I watched, it lifted its head — slowly, the way you move when you don't want to wake something — and looked at me. Not with my face. With a face that had been my face for long enough to start developing its own opinions.

It raised one hand. Not a wave. A gesture of *stay*.

I did what any rational professional does when confronted with evidence that contradicts his entire worldview. I tested the hypothesis. I walked out of the study, into the corridor, and looked back through the doorway.

In the corridor mirror, the study behind my reflected shoulder was lit and occupied. Two figures at the desk. Both wearing my coat. One of them was writing.

I walked to the end of the corridor. There was a mirror there too, because this house has the mirror-to-room ratio of a funfair, and in it the study was still lit, still occupied, and now the corridor in the reflection held a third figure, walking away from a door I had already walked away from.

I went room to room. I will spare you the full catalogue, because I made one — fourteen entries, timestamped, in my notebook, because documentation is the last clean shirt I own — but the pattern held with the consistency of physics. Every mirror in Hollowmere now kept a copy of me in every room I had been. The study-Yara, writing. The bedroom-Yara, lying on top of the covers, not sleeping. The hallway-Yara, testing the mirrors. They did not dissipate. They did not fade. They *remained*, one second behind, patient as filed paperwork.

The house was not haunting me.

The house was *filing* me.

* * *

I found the last of Edvard's marginalia on page forty-one, beside the unfinished sentence. *The cellar requires further —*

The pencil note beside it was different from all the others. Written slowly. Deliberately. The handwriting of a man who knew it was the last thing he would write and wanted it legible for whoever came next, which is to say, for me:

*If you are reading this, you have been commissioned, which means you have been counted, which means you are already on the final page. They will show it to you. They will be kind about it. Kindness is the mechanism — I cannot stress this enough — the trap is upholstered.*

*The fourth verse is a binding. The first three gather, the fourth keeps. Do not sing it. Do not hum it. Do not let your mouth learn its shape, because the house counts mouths, not songs. If you must learn it — and you will need to learn it, that is the cruelty of this place, the only way out runs straight through the thing that holds you — then learn it the way a witness learns testimony. Silently. With your eyes open. Never with your voice.*

*A voice is a signature. A signature is a yes.*

*The kept ones know. The ones in the mirrors. They cannot tell you — I think their voices went into the song, all of them, that is what the shelves in the cellar are, that is what the basin is — but they can show you. Watch their mouths. Trust the ones who were visitors once. They remember doors.*

*My name was Edvard Lune. If you get out, say it somewhere I am not. That is all a ghost wants, in the end. To be mentioned from the outside.*

*The cellar requires further —*

And there it ended. Not because he was interrupted. Because he had run out of *outside*, and the sentence knew it.

* * *

I sat back down in the chair, because my legs had filed their own report and the finding was: unreliable.

The lamp burned. The house breathed. And in the mirror across the room, study-Yara sat writing at the desk, and hallway-Yara stood in the corridor behind him, and bedroom-Yara was, I presumed, still lying on that bed upstairs, one second behind the whole world, patient as an entry in a ledger.

Forty pages of my measurements. A signature line. *Occupant: one.*

The harvest moon was two nights away. The heirs had said so at dinner, warmly, the way you mention a family birthday. *Everything must be witnessed by the moon, Mr. Nystrom. It's tradition. You'll want to be settled in by then.*

Settled in. Inventoried. Counted at the gathering-in, along with everything else the house had gathered and kept.

I looked at my reflection — the live one, the one that at least had the decency to lag — and it looked back at me, and this time I could have sworn the lag was shorter. Half a second, maybe less. Closing the gap. Catching up. Becoming less a reflection and more a *resident*.

"Stay invisible," I told it. "That's the rule. Stay invisible or be inventoried."

My reflection mouthed the words back at me, and for the first time, the timing was exact.

Which meant one of two things. Either the mirror was learning to be honest —

— or the thing in the glass had finished learning to be me.

Upstairs, faint and muffled through two floors of old timber, the cellar door began to hum the harvest song. Verses one, two, three. And then, for the first time, it did not stop at the silence where the fourth verse should be.

It hummed the fourth verse.

Softly. Slowly. The way you sing a lullaby to something you are trying to put to sleep.

And every reflection of me in every mirror in Hollowmere House turned its head, all at once, to listen.
