## 6. The Founders' Ledger

The cellar door was where the floor plan said it would be, which by Casper's third day at Hollowmere had become the exception rather than the rule.

They had learned to distrust the house's geometry the way a sailor learns to distrust a horizon. The nursery measured four inches longer after dark. The east hallway's wallpaper repeated a face every ninth panel—a woman, mouth open, as if caught mid-word for two hundred years. And the doors. Casper had stopped counting doors twice a day because the numbers kept disagreeing with each other, and a surveyor whose instruments contradicted themselves was a surveyor who needed sleep.

But the cellar door was exactly where Aldercott's original plans placed it, at the end of the kitchen corridor, behind a coat rack that smelled of cedar and dust. Oak. Iron hinges. A lock so old it had gone soft, surrendering to Casper's multi-tool with a sound like a held breath released.

Casper stood at the top of the stairs for a long moment, headlamp on, listening.

The house made sounds. Casper had catalogued them the way they catalogued everything: settling timbers at dusk, a ticking in the walls that wasn't plumbing, and—only twice, only at the edge of hearing—something that might have been humming. They had told themselves it was wind in the flues. They had written *auditory anomaly, probable ventilation* in their notes, and then sat looking at the words for a long time, because Casper Zhou did not write *probable*. Casper wrote what was measured.

The stairs went down farther than the plans indicated. That was the first thing. The second was the cold—not cellar cold, not the even chill of buried stone, but a cold with texture to it, a cold that felt *attentive*, like walking into a room where a conversation has just stopped.

"Structural assessment," Casper said aloud, because saying it made it true, made it a task with edges, made them a professional and not a trespasser in someone's grave. "Footings, joists, damp, drainage. In and out in an hour."

Their voice fell into the dark and did not echo. Casper noticed that the way they noticed everything now—automatically, exhaustively, the way a body notices a new pain—and added it to the list of things they were not going to think about.

* * *

The archive room was at the bottom, and it was beautiful, and Casper hated it immediately.

Not the shabby beauty of the upper floors, with their dust-sheeted furniture and their mirrors hung in every room like portraits of absent relatives. This was deliberate beauty. The room was octagonal, walled in shelving from floor to ceiling, and the shelves were full—not of books, or not only books, but of glass. Jars, bottles, panes of window glass stacked like slate, lenses, a case of hand mirrors with their faces turned to the wall. Everything wrapped or boxed or sleeved in acid-free paper with the fussy precision of a collection maintained by someone who expected it to matter.

And it was all labeled. That was what made the cold crawl up Casper's spine and settle between their shoulders. Every shelf bore a card in faded copperplate: dates. *March 1804 – September 1804. November 1831 – January 1832. The winter the lake froze.* Ranges of time, shelved like produce.

Casper lifted one of the wrapped panes, held it to the headlamp. Plain glass, slightly green at the edges, pre-industrial. It caught their light and gave back a smear of glow and, beneath it—

They put it down. They were not ready to check whether it lagged. They had spent two days establishing that the mirrors lagged, one second, everywhere, uniformly, and they had built a small fortress of explanations around that fact: residual magnetism, some quirk of the old silvering compound, a trick of the light, a trick of the *mind*. The fortress held as long as they stayed in the upper rooms, where the mirrors were furniture.

Down here the mirrors were an *archive*.

At the center of the octagon stood a reading table, and on the reading table, centered with the same fussy precision, lay a ledger.

It was enormous—calfskin over boards, brass corners gone green, a clasp like a mouth. The cover was tooled with a design Casper's eye kept trying to resolve into ornament and kept failing to: interlocking circles, or cells, or panes. When they looked at it directly the pattern seemed to shift, the way the wallpaper face shifted when you weren't quite looking. Casper looked at the table instead. At the dust around the ledger, which was undisturbed. At the dust on the table, which was also undisturbed, which meant the ledger had been placed here recently or the room kept its own housekeeping, and Casper declined to decide which was worse.

They opened it.

* * *

The first pages were architectural. Casper knew architectural. Here was a language they could live in: spans, loads, courses of ashlar, the specification of lime mortar against the frost. Two hands had written the early entries, alternating—one cramped and mathematical, one flowing and given to marginalia. Casper reconstructed them without trying: the engineer and the dreamer. A married pair, or siblings, or two people who had agreed to build something and then disagreed, forever, in the margins.

The dreamer wrote in the margins things like *the glass must be hungry before it is full* and *we are not building a house, we are building an ear.*

The engineer wrote things like *load-bearing wall, east range, see plate 9.*

Casper turned pages. The house rose in the ledger the way it had risen in fact—foundations in 1798, the mirror glass ordered from a firm in Bohemia that did not, as far as Casper knew, exist. And then, somewhere around the entries for the roof, the two voices stopped alternating and started converging, and the ledger stopped being about construction and started being about *use*, and this was where Casper's professional detachment—already cracked, already patched—finally gave way.

Because the ledger described what the house was for.

Not in the language of madness. That was the horror of it, the thing Casper would later try and fail to explain to anyone: the ledger was sane. It was the sanest document they had ever read. It stated its premises, defined its terms, showed its work. It said: *memory is a property of place. It persists in materials as charge persists in a Leyden jar. Glass, being amorphous, holds it best; mortar, being laid wet by hands that are feeling something, holds it next. A room in which a thing is felt strongly will retain the feeling. A room designed to feel strongly will retain everything.*

It said: *we propose, therefore, a house built as an instrument. Every mirror a cell. Every wall a choir. The house will not remember for us—it will remember instead of us. What is kept here need not be carried. Grief, laid down in glass, may be set on a shelf and labeled, and the griever may walk out lighter.*

*A choir of kept moments,* the dreamer had written in the margin, and someone—later, in different ink—had underlined it twice.

Casper stood in the octagonal room surrounded by two hundred years of shelved time and understood, with the particular nausea of a professional recognizing a colleague's work, that this was not a haunted house.

Haunted implied accident. Haunted implied residue, echo, something trapped. This was *engineering*. This was a machine that worked. Every anomaly Casper had measured—the lag, the doors, the nursery's four inches, the humming—was not a symptom of haunting. It was a *function*. The house was doing what it was built to do, competently, continuously, the way a furnace makes heat.

The mirrors didn't lag because they were broken.

They lagged because they were *full*.

* * *

Casper sat down at the table without deciding to. Their legs made the decision. They read for what the headlamp's battery meter told them was forty minutes, and it might have been four hours; the cellar kept its own time, and Casper had stopped trusting clocks in this house on the first morning, when the kitchen clock had run a second slow and then, an hour later, a second fast, as if correcting itself.

The ledger's middle years were a record of operation. Entries in a third hand—successors, then. Curators. *Deposited: the last words of Thomas A., spoken at his bedside, kept in the north parlor glass, his widow present.* *Cleared: the east corridor, 1849, at the family's request; the moments released were described as "a low weeping" and took three days to disperse.* *Note: the house prefers grief. Joy keeps poorly. Anger keeps not at all—it burns the silvering.* And once, in the cramped engineer's hand though the engineer had been dead forty years by the dating: *the house is learning to keep without being asked. This was not the design. Or it was the design, and we did not have the courage to write it.*

Casper read that line four times.

The house is learning to keep without being asked.

They thought of the nursery, four inches longer at night. Rooms grew when they were full. That was in the ledger too—*expansion is a sign of satiety; a full cell swells*—and Casper had measured that growth with a laser level and logged it as an anomaly, and the house had *let* them measure it, had held still for the instrument the way a patient holds still for a stethoscope.

The house knew what they were. It had known since they walked in with a case of instruments. It was being surveyed and it was *cooperating*.

Casper's hands were not quite steady when they turned to the final section. The last fifty pages of the ledger were different—denser, the two original hands returning, older now if the tremor in the letters meant anything, writing in a compressed and urgent shorthand that took Casper a page to crack and then, once cracked, was perfectly clear, because it was not shorthand at all. It was ritual notation. A procedure.

*The Keeping,* they had called it. A rite to be performed once a century, or when the house grew too full, or when the family line required renewal of the bond. It required components Casper's eye slid past—blood, glass, a night with no moon—and then, listed among them, plain as a line item in a specification:

*and the hand of an office: a surveyor, sworn to measure truly, who shall sign the instrument of the house, whereby the Keeping is sealed and the choir is bound for its term.*

Casper read it again.

*A surveyor, sworn to measure truly, who shall sign.*

The page after that was a diagram. Casper knew it immediately, knew it the way you know your own signature: it was a floor plan of Hollowmere, but not the floor plan in the sale particulars. This one had thirty-two rooms. The thirty-second was circular, at the heart of the cellar, and it was drawn with a door—drawn with a door that the modern plans did not show, that the modern plans *could not* show, because Casper had walked every corridor of this house twice and there was no circular room, there was no door, there was only the archive and the stairs and the dark.

The diagram labeled the circular room in the dreamer's flowing hand: *the choir stalls.*

And beneath the diagram, the final entry in the ledger, written in ink so fresh it still had a faint sheen under the headlamp, in handwriting Casper had not seen before and recognized anyway, because it was *neat*, because it was *precise*, because it was the handwriting of someone who wrote what was measured:

*The office remains open. The office is always open. We are always building toward the day a surveyor comes to Hollowmere with instruments and a duty and a signature in their pocket, and we are always nearly ready.*

*The last page is for them.*

Casper turned the last page.

It was blank. Not empty—blank the way a form is blank. Faint ruled lines. A space for a name. A space for a date. And at the bottom, printed in that same fresh, neat, patient ink, a line that Casper read aloud without meaning to, their voice flat and professional in the octagonal dark, the voice they used for readings on site, and heard it fall into the room without echo and be *kept*:

*"Signed in true measure, the house is bound; the choir is sealed for its term; and the surveyor shall be remembered."*

*Remembered.* The word sat on the page like bait on a hook, and Casper sat looking at it, in a room full of shelved time, in a house that had been waiting two hundred years for someone with their exact job description, and understood that the cold on their shoulders was not the cold of buried stone.

It was the cold of attention.

Somewhere above them, faint through two floors of mortar and glass, the house began—very softly, in a voice Casper had last heard in their own childhood bedroom—to hum.
