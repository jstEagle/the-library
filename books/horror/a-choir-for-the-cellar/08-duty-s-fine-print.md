## 8. Duty's Fine Print

The call came at 6:47 in the morning, which meant it wasn't a courtesy.

Casper knew the arithmetic of their firm the way they knew load-bearing walls. Devi Raghunathan did not call before seven unless something had gone wrong upstream of her, and nothing went wrong upstream of Devi that didn't eventually come downhill with her name attached to it as the person who would fix it or be fixed by it. Casper watched the screen glow on the nightstand of the Hollowmere guest room—the house had insisted, in its wordless way, that they stay; doors had simply declined to open toward the exit until the third evening—and let it ring twice before answering.

"Zhou."

"You're still on site." Not a question. Devi's voice had the flat brightness of someone reading from a dashboard. "Good. Listen, I'll be quick because I know you hate mornings and I hate them more."

Casper sat up. The curtains were the color of weak tea and the light coming through them made the room look like a photograph of itself. Somewhere below, in the walls, the house made the sound it always made just before dawn—a settling, a long exhalation through the joists, like an organist testing a pedal.

"The Aldercott closing moved up," Devi said. "Buyer's counsel wants to exchange by Friday. That means your appraisal needs to be in our system by Thursday noon, signed, sealed, no exceptions flagged."

Casper looked at the door of the guest room. It was closed. They had counted the doors of this corridor last night—seven—and this morning there were six, and they had not yet decided whether the house was eating them or offering them.

"Devi." They kept their voice level, professional, the voice of a person for whom buildings were only buildings. "There are anomalies."

A pause. On the line, Casper could hear the office waking up around her: a kettle, a printer warming, someone laughing two desks away in a world where mirrors told the truth.

"What kind of anomalies?"

"Dimensional inconsistencies. A nursery that measures differently at different hours. Door counts that don't reconcile between surveys. Material fatigue in the cellar masonry that doesn't match the age of the structure." Casper heard themselves reciting it and realized how sane it sounded, how small, how thoroughly stripped of the thing underneath—which was that the wallpaper repeated a face, and the face was patient, and last night it had been Casper's face.

Another pause, longer. When Devi spoke again she had dropped the dashboard voice and picked up the other one, the quiet one, the one she used when she was about to make a problem into Casper's problem.

"Casper. You know what an anomaly flag does to a sale at this stage. Buyer's counsel sees 'dimensional inconsistency' and they order a structural engineer, and the engineer orders a geotech survey, and we lose the quarter, and—" She stopped herself. Started again, softer. "How inconsistent? Are we talking settlement? Subsidence? Anything that becomes *our* problem after close?"

"No," Casper said. And then, because lying is a skill like any other and theirs had always been precision: "Nothing that transfers. It's... measurement noise. Old house, old additions, probably unpermitted work from the forties. I can write it up clean."

The silence on the line had a texture. Casper had spent eleven years learning to hear the textures of silences—the silence of a client deciding not to ask, the silence of a contractor padding an invoice, the silence of a colleague who has just been handed deniability and is deciding whether to take it. Devi's silence now was the third kind.

"Write it up clean," Devi said. "You're the surveyor of record. Your signature, your judgment. If you say the numbers reconcile, the numbers reconcile." A beat. "And Casper—it's a good commission. Finish strong and the Merrow Street portfolio is yours next quarter. You've earned a building of your own to run."

She hung up before Casper could decide what to say, which was, Casper suspected, the point of calling at 6:47.

They sat on the edge of the guest bed for a long time, holding the phone, watching the light move across the floor. In the wardrobe's mirrored panel—the house had mirrors the way other houses had baseboards, everywhere, unavoidable—their reflection sat on the edge of the bed one second behind them, and in the gap between the two versions of Casper Zhou there was room, exactly one second's worth, for something else to stand.

* * *

The report took all day.

Not because the writing was hard. Because honesty kept trying to get in.

Casper worked at the library table in the east study, laptop open, laser measure and notebook squared beside them like instruments of a defense. The template was familiar muscle memory: Executive Summary. Scope of Inspection. Structural Observations. Recommendations. Eleven years of reports lived in their hands, and the hands wanted to type *anomalous dimensional variance observed in northwest wing, recommend engineering review*, and Casper kept deleting it, and typing it again, and deleting it.

By ten they had found the trick, and the trick was to tell the truth sideways.

*Northwest wing exhibits minor dimensional irregularities consistent with historical modifications and undocumented alterations.* True. The nursery gained four inches at night. That was certainly an undocumented alteration.

*Masonry in cellar archive shows surface spalling consistent with age and damp exposure; no structural deficiency noted.* True, if you ignored what the spalling spelled out when the light hit it at dusk, which was a column of names, and the most recent name was still wet.

*All interior glazing intact and functional.* Casper stared at that sentence for four minutes. Then typed: *All interior glazing intact and functional.* Thirteen mirrors down the main stair alone, every one of them running a second late, every one of them showing rooms with an extra person standing in them—but intact. Functional. The words held. Words were load-bearing too.

It was astonishing, really, how much of the truth could survive inside a lie if you built the sentence carefully enough. Casper had spent their whole career believing that measurement was a moral act—that to measure a thing honestly was to honor it, that a surveyor's signature was a small secular sacrament, *I was here, I saw, I attest*. Now they sat in a dead woman's study typing sacraments in reverse, attesting to a house that did not exist, while the actual house stood around them listening.

Because it was listening. Casper was sure of that now. All week the house had responded to attention—doors opening ahead of their hand, dimensions shifting under the tape like a cat arching into a stroke. And today, as the lies accumulated on the screen, the house grew... pleased. There was no other word. The radiators ticked in rhythms almost like contentment. The draft through the hall moved warm instead of cold. At noon, when Casper went down to the kitchen, the tap ran clear instead of rust, and the light through the window fell in a long welcome rectangle across the floor, pointing at the table, at the chair, as if the house had set a place for them.

*Good instrument,* the warmth said. *Keep playing.*

Casper ate standing up, facing away from the window, and did not sit in the offered chair.

* * *

At three o'clock, Wren came.

Casper heard the car before they saw it—an engine laboring up the mile of gravel, hesitating at the bends the way every engine hesitated, as though the drive itself were reluctant. Through the study window Casper watched Wren get out: dark coat, hair pulled back, a document case under one arm, moving up the front steps with the specific posture of someone approaching a house the way you approach a hospital room. Quickly, so the dread doesn't have time to organize.

They met in the entrance hall, under the great mirror that hung above the fireplace—one second late, both of them, always one second late, so that the hall was briefly populated by four people whenever they stood together, and Casper had learned not to look up, and could see by the set of Wren's shoulders that Wren had learned the same lesson decades ago.

"Papers," Wren said, lifting the case. "My solicitor couriered the final drafts. I thought—I don't know. It felt wrong to sign them somewhere else. Like RSVPing to a funeral by text." They glanced around the hall, at the staircase, at the doors. "Where do you even work in this place? Did you find a room that stays put?"

"The east study. Mostly."

"Mostly," Wren repeated, and something passed over their face—not surprise, not quite. Recognition. "Mostly is fair. This house doesn't do *entirely*."

For a moment they stood there in the lagging light, and Casper felt the pull again, the one they'd been feeling since the day Wren arrived to sign and stayed to talk: the pull of another person who flinched at reflections. It would be so easy. *Wren, the nursery measures four inches longer at night. Wren, there's a thirty-second door. Wren, the ledger in the cellar needs a surveyor's signature and I think the house has been waiting two hundred years for mine.*

But Devi's voice was in their ear: *your signature, your judgment*. And beneath Devi's voice, older and colder, was the clause Casper had read in the founders' ledger with their own eyes: *the appraisal concluded, the sale executed, the surveyor attesting.* The ritual had steps. Casper was a step. And everything they'd learned about the house suggested it rewarded cooperation and remembered refusal.

"I should show you the summary," Casper said instead. "Before you sign anything. So you know what you're buying into. Selling into."

Something flickered in Wren's expression—disappointment? relief?—and they followed Casper to the study without another word.

* * *

Wren read the whole report. That was the worst part. Casper had expected a signature hunt—most clients skimmed to the number and the date. But Wren Aldercott read every page, sitting across the library table with the afternoon dying behind them, and Casper watched their eyes slow down on the sentences Casper was proudest of, the true-lies, the load-bearing words.

"*Minor dimensional irregularities,*" Wren read aloud, finally. "Consistent with historical modifications." They looked up. "Which historical modifications?"

"Unpermitted work, probably. Forties, fifties. Hard to date without opening walls."

"Mm." Wren turned a page. Turned another. The house was very quiet around them—the particular quiet Casper had begun to think of as held breath. "You know, my grandmother used to say the house had opinions about paper. She burned my grandfather's blueprints in the yard. Said the house hated being drawn."

"That's—" Casper started, and found they had nothing to finish the sentence with.

Wren reached the final page. The certification. *I hereby attest that the foregoing accurately reflects the condition of the premises.* Below it, the signature line, and below that, printed in the boilerplate no one ever read, the assignment clause: *Surveyor of record acknowledges execution of sale documents as conclusive evidence of completion of engagement.*

Casper watched Wren read that clause. Watched them stop on it. Watched their thumb move once across the printed words, back and forth, like a person checking a scar.

"It's strange," Wren said quietly. "Two hundred years of Aldercotts signing things in this house. Deeds, wills, contracts. My mother used to say we didn't own Hollowmere, we *staffed* it." They looked up, and their eyes were steady and sad and far too perceptive. "You've written it clean, haven't you. Whatever you found. You've written it the way the paperwork needed it written."

Casper's mouth was dry. "The numbers reconcile within tolerance."

"That's not what I asked."

The held breath of the house seemed to tighten. In the mirror above the study door, Casper caught the reflection of the room one second late: Wren leaning forward, Casper leaning forward, both of them closer than they actually were, and in the gap-second between the real pair and the reflected pair, something in the glass leaned closer still.

"Wren." Casper heard their own voice go low, careful, the voice they used for bad news in good buildings. "When Friday comes. When you sign. Read every clause. Especially the ones nobody reads."

Wren held their gaze for a long moment. Outside, the light failed all at once, the way it did here—not a sunset but a withdrawal, the house pulling the day in after it like a blanket.

"All right," Wren said at last, and stood, and gathered the papers but left the report on the table, facedown, like something they didn't want looking at them. At the door they paused, hand on the frame. "Casper. Whatever you're carrying in that report that isn't on the page—you're not the first person to carry it through these halls. But the others all put it down eventually. The house collects what we set down."

They left. Their footsteps climbed the stairs, unhurried, and faded, and the door of the guest room closed with a click that sounded almost gentle.

Casper sat alone in the study with the doctored report glowing on the screen. The cursor blinked in the signature field. Eleven years of sacrament, and here was the desecration, and it had taken only one phone call and forty minutes of careful sentences, and the terrible thing—the thing Casper would carry out of this house if they carried out anything—was how *easy* it had been. How natural. Power, it turned out, wasn't a wall you broke down. It was a door that opened ahead of your hand.

They typed their name. The letters appeared in the field, weightless, deniable, damning.

On the screen, in the margin of the digital document, for half a blink, a watermark surfaced that Casper had never placed and no template contained: a column of names, fine as engraving, and at the bottom, still wet, a new one forming letter by letter—

*C-a-s-p-e-r*

They slammed the laptop shut. In the darkened study, thirteen mirrors down the main stair began, very softly, to hum—in a voice that was almost, but not quite, one second late.
