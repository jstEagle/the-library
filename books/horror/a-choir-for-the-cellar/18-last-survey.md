## 18. Last Survey

The countersign had to be true.

That was the sentence Casper woke with at four in the morning, lying on the study floor under Wren's coat, the ledger open across their chest like a second ribcage. *The flaw requires a surveyor to sign the papers and strike the clause.* And beneath it, in the founders' cramped, confident hand, a condition they'd almost missed: *the instrument must be read honestly, or the reading is void.*

Honest. The word sat in Casper's stomach like a swallowed stone. They had spent their whole career being honest about other people's buildings and dishonest about themselves — measuring every joist and settlement crack to the millimetre while their own interior went unrecorded, unvisited, unclaimed. A childhood promise made over a grave: *I'll never be remembered, I promise, I'll be so careful no one ever has to carry me.* The house had found that promise weeks ago and had been playing it back ever since, tuning them with it.

If the reading had to be true, then it had to be all of it. Every door. Every mirror. Every memory the house had pried up out of them like floorboards.

Casper got up quietly. Wren slept on the chaise, one hand hanging down, fingers still curled around the twin pen. Casper didn't wake them. This part — the walking through, the naming of things — had to be done alone, or it wasn't a survey at all. It was just company.

They took the tape, the clipboard, the torch. They wrote at the top of a fresh page, in block capitals, the way they'd been taught on their first job at nineteen:

**FINAL SURVEY — HOLLOWMERE HOUSE — CONDUCTED IN GOOD FAITH.**

Then they opened the front door and began.

* * *

Doors first. Always doors first; that was the discipline.

Casper counted them aloud, touching each frame, because the house respected touch the way a liar respects an oath — technically. "One." The front door, oak, iron hinges, cold as a held breath. "Two." The parlour. "Three." The parlour's false door, panelled into the wall, which opened onto nothing but had never once failed to open when Casper's hand came near.

By door nine the house started arguing.

It did it the way it always did now — not with sound but with *lag*. The hallway mirror at the end of the east corridor ran three seconds behind, and in those three seconds Casper watched themselves walk toward a door they hadn't reached yet, and beside that reflected self stood a small figure in a hospital gown, holding a paper boat.

"No," Casper said, and kept counting. "Ten."

The figure was Milo. Had been Milo. Their brother, dead at eleven, drowned in the reservoir while Casper — twelve years old, fifty metres away, deliberately not watching, because watching felt like hovering and hovering felt like being seen caring — had been looking at the water and choosing not to look. That was the memory the house loved best. It had shown it to them in the nursery, in the cellar, in the bathroom glass with the tap running. It showed it now, three seconds late, in every mirror down the corridor, and this time it added sound: a thin humming, in Casper's own voice, the tune they used to hum to Milo when he couldn't sleep.

Casper stopped at door fourteen. Stood in front of the largest mirror, the one with the tarnished silver frame, and let the lag play out. Watched the small figure lift the paper boat. Watched their own reflection — twelve years old, wet to the elbows, mouth open on a scream that had taken twenty years to arrive.

"I know," Casper said to the glass. "I was there."

The humming faltered. Just slightly. Like a choir losing its place when the soloist goes off-script.

"You keep showing me this like it's a secret. It isn't a secret. It's the worst thing I own and I've owned it since I was twelve." Casper's voice stayed level, surveyor-flat, and that was the trick of it — not courage, just procedure. "I chose not to watch him. I've chosen not to watch it every day since. So here's my note for the record: I looked away. Deliberately. Once, and then forever."

They wrote it down. Actually wrote it, on the clipboard, in the column marked DEFECTS: *Corridor E — occupant grief, structural, load-bearing. Owner aware.*

The mirror shivered. The figure in the gown put down the boat.

"Eleven," Casper said, and moved on.

* * *

The house fought harder on the upper floors.

Door seventeen wouldn't stay counted — it slid along the wall like a puck on ice, presenting itself at different intervals, and Casper chased it twice before understanding that chasing it was the game. Instead they stood still, marked its last position, measured from the window to that position, and wrote: *Door 17 — mobile. Position at time of survey: 2.4m north of window. Does not wish to be counted. Counted anyway.*

Something in the walls made a sound like a struck glass.

Door twenty-two opened onto the nursery, and the nursery did what the nursery always did: it grew. Casper measured it anyway, corner to corner, in the grey pre-dawn light, and got four inches more than the plans said, and wrote that down too, without flinching, because a defect noted is a defect half-tamed.

But the nursery mirror was where the house brought out the second memory, the one Casper had buried deeper than Milo, the one they'd hoped the house hadn't found.

Their mother, in the kitchen of the flat above the laundrette, the year Casper turned fifteen. The year she started forgetting things. Small things first — the kettle, the gas, Casper's name arriving a beat late on her tongue, like a reflection lagging. And Casper, who had already learned that love meant invisibility, had begun practising leaving. Slipping out early. Making themselves small and unmemorable so that when she finally forgot them entirely it would hurt less — a vaccination of absence, administered daily.

The mirror showed it: fifteen-year-old Casper at the kitchen door, coat on, watching their mother stir a pot, deciding whether to say goodbye. Deciding not to. Walking out. And behind the glass, layered in the lag, the thing Casper had never once let themselves think: *she noticed.* She noticed every single time. She forgot everything else in the world, but she never once forgot to watch the door close.

"Oh," Casper said. The sound came out very small.

The house leaned in. He could feel it leaning, the whole building tilting its attention like a great ear. *There,* it seemed to say. *There is the moment you were made. Let me keep it for you. Let me hold it so you never have to again.*

"That's not keeping," Casper said. "That's hoarding. There's a difference and I'm a professional, I should know." They wiped their face with the back of the hand holding the pencil, smearing graphite across one cheekbone, and wrote: *Nursery — mirror shows true events owner declined to witness. Recommend: witnessed. Done.*

The nursery shrank by four inches. Casper measured it again to be sure, and laughed — one short, cracked note, the first laugh the house had heard from them, and it didn't know what to do with it. Laughter wasn't in the founders' notation. There was no symbol for it in the ledger.

* * *

Mirrors next. All thirty-one of them, named.

This was Wren's contribution, though Wren didn't know it yet — something they'd said on the stairs in week two, half-asleep: *"My grandmother named hers. Said an unnamed mirror is just a hole in the wall, but a named one is a door, and you can at least knock."*

So Casper knocked. Went mirror to mirror with the clipboard and gave each one a name, aloud, formal, absurd: *Hall Mirror East. Scrying Glass, Cellar Landing. Bathroom, Second Floor (the Liar). Gilt Oval, Stairwell (the Flatterer).* And at each one, they stopped the lag with a palm flat against the cool glass and spoke into it the way you'd speak into a well.

"Mirror Twelve. You show me a version of myself that signed the doctored report without flinching. Note for the record: I flinched. I flinched for eleven minutes. It's in my body somewhere; I intend to find it."

"Mirror Nineteen. You show me celebrated. Framed, hung, remembered, my name on spines and plaques. Note for the record: I would rather be forgotten by everyone than remembered by you."

"Mirror Twenty-Six—" and here the glass was already dark, running early instead of late, showing the empty room before Casper entered it, and Casper stopped, because there was only one mirror in the house that ran early, and it was the one whose cost they hadn't yet paid. They touched it gently, the way you'd touch a scar you were about to earn. "Twenty-Six. You're waiting for me. Noted. I'm coming. But on my schedule, after the whole truth, not instead of it."

Down in the cellar archive, the ledger lay open on its stand, and as Casper entered, the pages stirred though there was no draught. The founders' handwriting had grown agitated overnight, margins filling with annotations in ink that hadn't been there yesterday — *the survey is not required, the surveyor is sufficient, sign now, why measure what wants only to be measured*—

"Because," Casper said, to the book, to the house, to two centuries of patient hungry architecture, "your clause says the reading must be true, and I have read you for three weeks, and here is what I found that your builders never wrote down."

They set the clipboard on the stand, beside the ledger, equal to it. Two documents. Two kinds of record.

"You were built to store memory because your makers were afraid of losing it. Fine. Fear is a foundation; lots of good houses have worse. But somewhere in the pouring and the glazing, you learned to *want*, and wanting isn't in the specification. You don't keep memories. You take hostages. And the difference between those two things is exactly the width of a signature line."

The cellar lights — such as they were, the torch and the grey seep from the coal chute — dimmed, and every standing mirror in the ritual chamber beyond the thirty-second door lit at once, and the choir began. Not humming now. Singing. In Casper's voice, in Milo's voice, in their mother's, in the supervisor's, cut off mid-sentence, in the voices of every surveyor and heir the glass had ever drunk — singing the promise over the grave, *never be remembered, never be remembered*, but slowed down and stretched until it sounded less like a vow and more like a sentence being served.

Casper stood in it and did not look away.

That was the whole discipline, they realized. Three weeks of dread, and it came down to this: the house's entire engine ran on aversion. It caught what people refused to see. Every mirror was a turned back, reversed. Feed it your flinches and it grew fat on them. But Casper had spent the night walking the house naming its horrors in block capitals, and the choir was thinner than it had been at midnight. Starving. A collection can't survive being catalogued. An inventory is a kind of freedom — ask any estate, any heir, any ghost.

So Casper did the last thing. The truest thing. They stood in the centre of the cellar, in the cold, in the song, and surveyed themselves.

*Occupant: Casper Zhou. Age: thirty-four. Defects: avoidance, chronic; grief, unprocessed, load-bearing; love, present, undeclared, structurally significant.* Their voice shook on that last line and they let it shake and wrote it anyway. *Recommendation: declare. Today. Before signing or after; order immaterial; fact material.*

The choir cracked. Not stopped — cracked, like ice under a boot, a long splintering note running through every voice at once, because no one had ever surveyed the house's food before eating it. Because the founders had built an engine for other people's refusals and had never imagined a surveyor who would turn the instrument on themselves and file the report.

The mirrors dimmed to embers. Somewhere above, a door — the real front door — opened, and footsteps came down the cellar stairs, quick and certain, and Wren appeared in the doorway with the twin pen in their fist and terror and fury and something warmer fighting it out on their face.

"You weren't in bed," Wren said. "You weren't anywhere. The house went quiet — completely quiet, Casper, it's never quiet—"

"It's not quiet," Casper said. "It's listening. There's a difference." They held up the clipboard, thirty-one mirrors named, thirty-two doors counted, every buried thing logged in block capitals, true. "Final survey's done. Everything's measured. Everything's mine."

Wren crossed the cellar and took their hand, pen and all, and for one second — one real second, no lag, no glass — they just stood there, two records touching.

"The chamber's open," Casper said. "The papers are upstairs on the table. And Wren — whatever happens when the pen touches the page, whatever it takes out of me—"

"Don't," Wren said.

"—I need you to remember it out loud. To me. After. Because I won't be able to keep it, and I've spent my whole life not keeping things, and I'm done practising."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the front door swung shut on its own, and every clock in Hollowmere House began, softly, to count down.
