## 20. One Second Early

The silence after the scream was the worst part.

Not because it was empty—because it wasn't. Every mirror in the ritual chamber had gone dark at once, all of them, the whole choir of kept moments snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers, and in that darkness Wren could still hear them. Not voices anymore. Just the residue of voices, the way a bell keeps ringing after you've grabbed its tongue. A hum with no source. The house, exhaling.

Casper's hand was still on the pen. Their signature sat on the final page of the sale papers, and through it—the founders' clause, two hundred years of Aldercott blood and borrowed surveyors and every soul who'd ever been asked to *finish*—ran a single line of ink. One stroke. Casper had drawn it slowly, deliberately, the way you'd cut a thread you knew was holding up something enormous.

Wren had watched the line go down and felt the floor change under their feet.

Not metaphorically. The floor of Hollowmere House had always had a texture to it, a faint wrongness underfoot, like walking on a drumhead someone else was playing. Wren had felt it since childhood, before they had words for it: the sense that the house was listening through the soles of their shoes. Now the drumhead had gone slack. The house beneath them felt like a house.

"Casper," they said, and their voice came out cracked down the middle.

"I know." Casper didn't look up from the papers. Their face was gray in the light from the doorway behind them—the only light now, since the chamber mirrors had died. "I feel it too."

"What did we do?"

"What the ledger said." Casper's voice was flat, careful, the voice of someone holding a full cup very steady while crossing ice. "The countersign. We unbound it."

The hum faded as they stood there. It didn't stop so much as settle, sinking into the walls the way heat sinks into stone after sunset, becoming indistinguishable from the ordinary silence of an old building at night. Wren found themselves straining after it, which frightened them more than the hum had. Two hundred years of that sound living in their bones, and some traitorous part of them missed it already.

"Come on," Casper said. "We should walk the house. All of it. Before we leave."

"Why?"

"Because I'm a surveyor," Casper said, and for the first time in what felt like days, something almost like a smile touched their mouth. It didn't survive long. "And because if this is the last time anyone measures this place honestly, I want it done right."

* * *

They walked the house together, and it was dying, or being born, or both—Wren couldn't tell the difference, and suspected there wasn't one.

The nursery measured true. Casper ran the tape along the wall twice, then a third time, and shook their head slowly. "Four inches gone. Whatever it was doing with that room, it's stopped." The wallpaper in the east hallway was just wallpaper now, its repeating pattern repeating nothing, no face surfacing in the damask when you looked away and back. The doors numbered themselves. Casper counted aloud as they went, and Wren followed the count like a rosary: thirty-one doors, morning and midnight, dusk and dawn, thirty-one doors, no more, no less.

At each mirror they stopped. There were thirteen left standing—Wren had helped break the others, had held the sheet over their own face while centuries of kept moments howled out of the glass, and they would carry that howling for the rest of their life—but these thirteen were dark. Not broken. Dark, the way a screen is dark, the way a window is dark at night when the room behind you is lit. Ordinary glass showing ordinary rooms.

Except.

It was in the drawing room, near the end of the walk, nearly four in the morning by Casper's watch. The last mirror on the circuit, a tall oval thing in a frame of tarnished silver that Wren's grandmother had called *the early bird* and never explained. Wren had grown up avoiding it out of habit rather than fear; it had always seemed the least haunted object in a house of haunted objects, dull where the others gleamed.

Casper stepped past it toward the door, and Wren, following, caught movement in the glass.

The room. The drawing room, reflected faithfully—the dead fireplace, the sheeted furniture, the tall windows going gray with the first suggestion of dawn. Empty. Both of them already at the threshold, half into the hall.

But the reflection showed the room *before* they'd crossed it. Casper still bent over the tape measure by the far wall. Wren still standing at the mantel. And moving ahead of them both, in the glass, the empty space where they were about to be—arriving a beat before they did.

Wren stopped walking. In the mirror, the reflected Wren stopped a moment later—or a moment earlier, they genuinely could not tell—and the discrepancy sat in Wren's chest like a swallowed stone.

"Casper."

"I see it." Casper had turned back. They approached the mirror the way they'd approached everything in this house: professionally, hands loose, head tilted, cataloguing. But Wren had learned to read them over these terrible days, and underneath the surveyor's calm their eyes were wide and wet and grieving. "One second early. The lag's inverted."

"What does that mean?" Wren's voice sounded small in the big quiet room. "The others are dead. You said—they're just glass now. Why is this one—"

"Because it's not showing the house anymore." Casper lifted their hand, slowly, and the reflection lifted its hand a full second before they did, anticipating, obedient, *ahead*. Casper stared at their own提前 ghost and finished, very quietly: "It's showing us."

* * *

They tested it, because of course they did. Casper the methodical, Casper who had walked into a haunted engine with a clipboard and a metronome app and the belief that anything could be measured into harmlessness. They clapped. The mirror showed the clap before the sound. They spoke; the glass moved its mouths a beat ahead of their words, so that Wren heard everything twice—once in the silvered oval, once in the air—and the second telling was always slightly wrong, softened, the way memory softens things.

"It's not lagging," Casper said. They had gone very still. "The house used to keep us one second behind, replaying. This one runs ahead. It's not recording anymore, Wren. It's—" They stopped. Started again. "Everything it kept is burning free. That's what the ledger promised. Every stored moment released. Including—"

"Including ours," Wren said. "Including yours."

"Including mine." Casper's jaw worked. "I signed myself into it. The clause needed a surveyor, and I gave it one, and when I struck the line it took my—"

"Don't." Wren crossed the room and took their face in both hands, and the mirror behind them showed the gesture happening before it happened, tender and terrible, love arriving in the glass a second before it arrived in the world. "Don't say it like it's inventory. Don't stand there and appraise yourself."

Casper laughed—a broken, startled sound—and leaned into Wren's palms. For a moment neither of them spoke. Outside the tall windows, the sky was turning the color of dishwater, then the color of pearl. Dawn coming to Hollowmere for the first time in two hundred years without anything in the house waiting to record it.

"The mirrors went dark," Casper said against Wren's wrist. "All but this one. Do you understand what that means? Everything the founders built, everything it harvested—my mother's kitchen, your grandmother singing to the glass, every surveyor, every heir, every kept moment in that cellar—it let all of it go. And the only thing it held onto, the one thing it kept a lens open for—"

"Is us," Wren said.

"Is what we do next."

* * *

Wren made them sit down. There was a sheeted sofa under the window, and Wren pulled the sheet off with a violence that surprised them both, sending up a ghost of dust that the early mirror dutifully showed them a second before it bloomed. They sat close together, knees touching, and watched the light come up, and Wren tried to think like a person instead of an heir.

Because here was the thing Wren understood, in their bones, better than Casper ever could: the house had never wanted to hurt anyone. That was the horror of it, the real horror, worse than the extra doors and the lagging glass. The house had wanted to *keep*. It was built by people who couldn't bear to lose anything—a grandmother's laugh, a mother's last afternoon, a sibling's deathbed promise—and it had spent two centuries doing exactly what they asked, faithfully, monstrously, keeping and keeping and keeping until keeping became hunger became architecture. It collected people the way Wren's family collected grief: lovingly, helplessly, generation after generation.

And now it was letting go. And the one mirror it had left itself, the one window it kept open in all that newly ordinary glass—

"You realize what it's doing," Wren said.

Casper looked at them.

"It's not haunting us. It's not even watching us, not really. It's—" Wren pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "My grandmother used to say the house loved us. I thought she was mad. She wasn't. It loved us the way a miser loves coins. And now it's dying, or sleeping, or whatever happens to a thing like this when you strike through its name, and the last thing it wants to keep—"

"—is the memory of us," Casper finished. "Running one second early. Showing us to ourselves before we arrive. So that somewhere, in the glass, we're always about to happen."

The words settled over the room like dust.

"That's not sinister," Wren said, and hated how their voice wavered. "That's the cruelest thing it could have done. It could have screamed. It could have taken the supervisor—he's probably still in there, God, we'll have to deal with him, deal with the firm, with all of it. Instead it gives us *this*. A mirror that loves us one second ahead. How am I supposed to hate it? How am I supposed to sell it?"

"You're not supposed to do anything," Casper said. "That was the whole point. That was always the trap—duty, inheritance, the fine print. Nobody's supposed to do anything ever again. The clause is struck. The ledger's closed. The house doesn't get a vote." They said it firmly, and then their voice dropped, and honesty came through it like cold through a cracked window. "But I'm going to tell you what I saw in the chamber, before the mirrors went dark. When the choir burned free. My reflection stayed behind, Wren. I felt it go. Like a coat lifting off my shoulders. Somewhere in that glass, there's a version of me that will stand in these rooms forever, humming, keeping—"

"Keeping what?"

Casper was quiet for a long moment. The dawn strengthened. In the early mirror, their two seated figures leaned together a second before they actually did, and Wren watched it happen and felt their heart crack along a seam they hadn't known was there.

"Keeping me," Casper said finally. "The me I buried. The one who promised never to be remembered. It's keeping the part of me I wouldn't keep myself." A breath. "And I don't know if that's a theft or a mercy."

* * *

They gathered what they'd come with—Casper's instruments, the ledger, the sale papers with their single devastating line—and Wren stood in the drawing room doorway a moment longer than necessary, looking back at the early mirror.

It showed the empty room. The sheeted furniture, the brightening windows, the two of them already gone from it—not yet gone, would not be gone for another second, but the glass had already accepted their leaving, had already begun keeping the room they were about to abandon. Wren raised a hand to it. In the mirror, the hand rose a heartbeat sooner, eager, faithful, a dog running ahead on the path.

*I know,* Wren thought at it. *I know. Keep it well.*

Then they turned and ran.

Not from fear—that was the strange part, the part they'd need years to explain to anyone, if they ever explained it to anyone. They ran because the light in the east windows had gone gold, because Casper was waiting in the hall with the ledger hugged to their chest like a rescued child, because somewhere outside these walls there was an unrecorded morning and they intended to be inside it while it happened. They ran down the corridor where the wallpaper repeated nothing, past doors that were only doors, down the stairs that creaked in honest, unremarkable ways, and the house let them go the way a fist lets go when the hand finally unclenches—slowly, completely, with nothing held back.

At the front door Wren stopped, one hand on the latch, and listened.

Silence. Real silence, house-silence, timber-and-plaster silence, the silence of a building that contains nothing but air and dust and the memory of the people walking out of it. No hum. No choir. No second voice under the first.

Only, faintly, from the drawing room behind them—from the tall oval glass in its tarnished silver frame—the sound of a door closing one second before they closed it.

Wren's breath caught. Casper's hand found theirs in the dim hall, warm, solid, present tense.

"It's going to show us everything now," Wren whispered. "Before it happens. Every room we walk into. Every morning. It'll always be one step ahead, waiting for us, keeping us—"

"I know," Casper said.

"We'll never be invisible again. Not to it. Not anywhere it can reach." Wren laughed, and it came out closer to a sob. "You spent your whole life staying unseen in other people's spaces. And now there's a piece of glass that will announce you everywhere you go, one second early, forever."

Casper squeezed their hand. Outside the door, the morning was waiting, unrecorded, unrecordable, theirs.

"Then we'd better give it something worth announcing," Casper said, and opened the door onto the light.

Behind them, deep in the sleeping house, a mirror showed the empty hallway—one second before it was empty—and kept it.
