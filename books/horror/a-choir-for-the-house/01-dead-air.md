## 1. Dead Air

The chapel had been a chapel for two hundred and forty years and a radio station for eleven months, and it had never quite decided which it preferred to be. Xanthe Dunmore felt the argument every night when he flipped the switches: the mixing desk humming where the altar rail used to run, the ON AIR lamp glowing red in the alcove where a saint once stood with her hands open. The pews were gone, sold or burned, but their ghosts remained in the pale rectangles worn into the flagstones, rows of absent knees.

He liked to think of the show as a kind of service anyway. *The Nightingale Hour*, midnight to three, broadcast from Marrow Fen on a frequency so low the engineers at the regional transmitter had laughed at him. *Nobody will hear you out there, mate.* But people heard. People always heard. That was the strange, quiet miracle of the thing — that in a village of four hundred souls, ringed by black water and blacker reeds, there were always voices waiting on line one when the red light came on.

"Good evening, the fen," he said, as he said every night, his voice dropping into that register he'd practiced in the shower like a teenager rehearsing for a band. "You're listening to the Nightingale Hour. The lines are open, the kettle's on, and outside my window the mist is doing its usual impression of something with intentions. Who's first tonight?"

Line one lit before he finished the sentence. It always did. He'd stopped finding that remarkable around the second week, which in retrospect was the first wrong turn of many.

"Marrow Fen," said the voice on the other end, an older woman, warm as bread left out overnight. "Is that you, love? You sound tired."

"I'm always tired, Edith. It's part of my brand."

A laugh, dry as seed husks. He knew her name without being told; he knew most of them by now, though none of them had ever introduced themselves. Edith Callow, seventy-something, lived up past the sluice gates. She called most Tuesdays to complain about her knees and praise the moon and tell him, in exhaustive detail, about her garden, which she described the way other people describe lovers.

"How are the dahlias?" he asked, because that was the ritual, and rituals were what kept a show alive at one in the morning.

"Oh, gone over now, love. Gone over weeks ago." A pause with something underneath it, like a stone under moss. "But I'll tell you what's lovely. The chrysanthemums on my coffin. White ones, big as cabbages. Your mother would have approved."

Xanthe's hand stayed steady on the fader. Eleven months of live radio teaches you that much, at least: the hand stays steady while everything behind it rearranges itself.

"Your coffin," he repeated, lightly, the way you'd repeat a caller's misheard song lyric. "Planning ahead, Edith?"

"No need to plan, love. It's done." She said it the way you'd say *the washing's done*, mild and satisfied. "Last Thursday. Lovely turnout. Half the village came, and the other half sent flowers. Reverend Lessiter did the service even though his hip was playing up. He said I was a fixture." Another dry laugh. "I said, well, fixtures is what holds a house together, isn't it?"

The mist pressed against the studio window. Behind him, the old vestry door ticked as the heating pipes cooled, a sound like someone counting under their breath.

"Edith," Xanthe said, and here was the professionalism, sliding in smooth as a fader, "if this is your way of telling me you're feeling poorly—"

"I'm not poorly, love. I'm dead. There's a difference." No malice in it. No joke either. Just the flat contentment of a woman reporting the weather. "I wanted to ring and tell somebody, because honestly, if you can't boast about your own funeral, when can you? The wreaths alone. Mrs. Petch sent lilies the size of dinner plates, and she never liked me a day in her life, so you know she meant it."

He should have hung up. He knew that even then, some animal ledger in his brain keeping its own count of wrongnesses. But the red light was on, and the signal was clean, and somewhere out in the dark a woman's voice was coming through his desk like warmth through a wall, and the oldest instinct of the broadcaster — older than sense, older than self-preservation — is that you do not break the circuit. You keep talking. The show is the show.

"Tell me about it," he heard himself say. "Tell me all of it."

And she did, fondly, thoroughly, for twenty minutes that felt like twenty years. The church full of breath and flowers. The hymn — *Shall We Gather at the River*, sung slightly too fast because the organist was nervous. Her granddaughter crying into a coat sleeve that smelled of cigarettes and dog. The way the light came through the east window and lay across the lid of the coffin like a hand. "They put my good brooch on," she said. "The enamel one with the wren on it. I always said I'd be buried in it and everyone pretended not to hear, but they remembered. That's the thing about funerals, love. They listen better than the living ever do."

"That's—" Xanthe cleared his throat. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked twice. "That's a lovely thought, Edith."

"You think I'm having you on."

"I think it's very late, and grief makes people say strange things, and sometimes we rehearse our own endings just to take the sting out of them."

A long silence on the line. Not empty silence — occupied silence, the kind that has weight and weather. When she spoke again her voice had come closer to the receiver, or seemed to, close enough that he could hear the wet click of her swallowing.

"You counted the doors of your house when you got home tonight," she said. "Didn't you. Before bed. You always do now."

He hadn't told anyone that. He hadn't told anyone that he'd started doing it three weeks ago, walking the ground floor of the house on Fennel Lane with his phone torch cupped in his palm like a votive flame, touching each lintel — kitchen, back kitchen, pantry, stairs, Wren's room, his own — six doors, six, always six, because six was right and anything else was unthinkable.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said, and his voice did not waver, and he hated how proud of that he was.

"No," Edith agreed gently. "You wouldn't. Not yet." A rustle, fabric against something, or wind through reeds. "Keep the signal clean, love. That's all I'll say. Keep it clean as long as you can."

The line went to dial tone — not the soft digital nothing of the studio's system but an actual bell-tone, brass and distance, like a telephone ringing in a house that had been empty since 1911.

Xanthe sat with his hands flat on the desk and looked at the red ON AIR light until it stopped seeming like a word.

Then he leaned into the microphone, and his voice came out warm and unhurried and absolutely professional, and he said: "Well. You heard it here first, the fen — Edith Callow reports her funeral was a triumph, and may we all be so well attended. Line two's open. Don't be shy. It's only me."

* * *

The rest of the shift passed the way a river passes under ice. He took calls from a man named Tom who wanted to talk about eels, and a girl who couldn't have been more than nine who hummed a tune he almost recognized and then rang off giggling, and a breathless silence that lasted four minutes and that he filled, expertly, with a story about the time he'd interviewed a man who trained pigeons to carry microphones. His voice never cracked. His timing never slipped. Inside, behind the glass of himself, something small and cold kept turning Edith's words over like a stone looking for the worm underneath.

*Keep the signal clean.*

At 2:58 he played the sign-off music — an old recording of a nightingale, sourced from a scratchy 78 he'd found in the vestry cupboard, a bird singing in a garden that no longer existed — and let it run down into static, and killed the desk, and sat in the sudden dark listening to the building settle.

The walk home took eleven minutes along the raised path between the drains. The mist was up off the water in earnest now, moving with that slow deliberation that mist in Marrow Fen had never bothered to disguise, as though it went where it pleased and expected thanks. His torch beam made a small yellow room in the middle of all that grey, and he walked inside it, and he did not look at the church tower rising black against the lesser black of the sky, because the tower was empty, had been empty since they melted the bell down in 1911 for munitions metal, and empty towers were best not looked at after midnight. Everyone in the village knew that. Nobody said it. That was Marrow Fen all over: a whole parish built on things nobody said.

The house on Fennel Lane was dark except for the landing nightlight, the one shaped like a mushroom that Wren had chosen when she was five and refused to surrender at twelve. He stood in the lane a moment, looking up at it. His daughter slept behind the third window from the left. Her mother — Mara, who had come back to the fen in June with two suitcases and a silence between them thick enough to insulate a loft — slept behind the second. He could see the faint blue pulse of the television neither of them admitted to falling asleep to.

He let himself in. The lock turned with its familiar complaint.

Kitchen. Back kitchen. Pantry. Stairs. Wren's room. His own.

Six doors on the ground floor route, plus the bathroom and the airing cupboard upstairs, but the count he did was the ground-floor six, the original six, the ones that mattered, though he could not have told you why they mattered or when he'd started believing they did. He touched each lintel as he passed, fingertips to wood, the way you'd touch the heads of sleeping children. Six. He did the round again, because the first time had felt somehow uncounted, uncountable, a sum that wouldn't hold still. Six. Six.

In the kitchen he put the kettle on and then didn't make tea, just stood holding the hot mug of nothing-warmth, watching the window go slowly blind with condensation. In the condensation, without deciding to, he wrote E with one finger. Then he wiped it away with his sleeve, hard, like erasing evidence.

She wasn't dead. That was the sane answer. Edith Callow was an old woman with a macabre streak and a phone, and last Thursday she'd attended someone else's funeral — Mrs. Petch's husband, perhaps, or one of the Hollis brothers — and in the fog of the hour she'd slid herself into the coffin, the way old women do, the way everyone does, trying on the ending. And the detail about the brooch, the wren brooch — that was coincidence, that was the sort of thing a mind snagged on at 1 a.m., because Wren, his daughter, was named for his grandmother, and grandmothers collect names like burrs—

Except.

Except that he had been to the churchyard in September, cutting across it with the mic stands, and he had seen, without meaning to see, a fresh white chrysanthemum arrangement on a new-mown grave, and the card had said *Edie*, and he had thought, *someone's lost a horse,* and walked on.

The kettle clicked off. The house ticked around him, cooling, settling, keeping its own small accounts.

He climbed the stairs. At Wren's door he stopped, as he stopped every night, and listened. She breathed slow and deep, a twelve-year-old's sleep, dreamless-seeming, safe-seeming. Through the gap under the door he could see the mushroom nightlight throwing its soft useless glow, and beneath it, on the floor, the shapes of her things: the violin case, the stack of library books, the shoebox of chalks she was forever drawing hopscotch grids with on the pavement outside, numbering squares that led nowhere.

*Wren,* he thought, and the name hurt going through him, the way certain words do at certain hours — a stone in the shoe of the mind.

He went to his own room. He did not sleep, exactly. He lay on top of the covers in his clothes and watched the ceiling perform its slow theatre of shadows, and at some point he must have drifted, because he dreamed of the studio — except in the dream the studio was the chapel again, pews full, every face turned toward him, and he was broadcasting to them, and they were listening with the terrible patience of the photographed, and Edith Callow sat in the front row wearing her enamel brooch, the little wren bright on her coat, and she was smiling at him with such kindness, such infinite, awful kindness, and saying, soundlessly, in perfect lip-readable syllables:

*Keep it clean, love.*

He woke at dawn with the taste of candle smoke in his mouth and the word *clean* still hanging in the room like a struck bell's residue.

Downstairs, the house was quiet. He made himself do the count before coffee, standing in the hall in the grey light, and his voice came out of him in a whisper, actually aloud, which was new, which he chose not to examine:

"Kitchen. Back kitchen. Pantry. Stairs. Wren. Mine."

Six.

He did it again.

Six.

It was only afterward, mug finally in hand, standing at the window watching the mist give the village back to itself piece by piece — the sluice gate, the yew, the tower — that he noticed the thing that set the day tilting, that sent him that afternoon down to the parish archive with a story about researching local history for the show, that began everything.

On the windowsill, inside the glass, written in the condensation from the night before — in the patch he had wiped clean with his own sleeve — in a small, careful, old-fashioned hand:

*6/7.*

And beneath it, as if in explanation, as if in apology:

*One more since Thursday.*

He stood very still for a long time. Outside, far off across the water, a bird began to sing — not a nightingale, whatever shape the mist gave it, just a common bird making its common claim on the morning. He drank his tea. He washed his mug. And he did not wipe the window, because some part of him, the part that had learned to keep the signal clean, understood already that some messages you leave exactly where they've been delivered.
