## 22. Sign-Off

The studio still smells faintly of scorched wood if the night is damp enough, and on those nights Wren opens both windows of the chapel before she goes on air, letting the cold of Marrow Fen move through like a guest who has learned to wipe its feet.

It has been seven months since the bell cracked.

She knows this because she keeps a calendar behind the mixing desk, a plain paper one with a photograph of a lighthouse on it, and every morning she draws a small line through the day before it begins. Her father used to do something similar with doors. She does it with days. The house taught her that things must be counted by someone; the trick her father finally learned—the trick that saved all of them—was choosing what got counted, and how, and who was allowed to hold the pencil.

Tonight is Thursday. On Thursdays they read letters.

Wren settles into the chair that used to be too big for her and is now almost right, pulls the microphone close enough that her breath fogs its grille in winter, and flips the switch marked ON AIR. The red light blooms above the glass like a holly berry. Somewhere beneath her feet, in the old crypt where the transmitters nest among the bones of the original congregation, something hums awake—a sound like a hive deciding to be gentle.

"Good evening, Marrow Fen, and good evening to everyone listening past the fen," she says. "You're hearing the Voice of the Chapel, and I'm Wren Dunmore, and tonight we're doing letters, so get comfortable."

In the booth beside her, behind the second pane of glass, her father sits where he always sits: third chair from the left, the one with the torn cushion he refuses to let anyone fix because his mother sewed it. He is wearing the grey jumper Idris bought him for Christmas even though it's nearly spring. His hands are folded in his lap. And his mouth is moving—shaping every word she says, half a second behind her, like a man learning a language he used to speak.

He cannot hear her. He cannot hear anything at all. Not her voice, not the rain, not the sea of silence where the bell used to hang. But he comes every night anyway, and he mouths along, and when she glances over—and she always glances over—his eyes are on her with an attention so complete it feels less like watching and more like keeping.

*You're already heard,* she had shouted through the tower door, into the speakers, into whatever was waiting for him there. *You're already kept.*

The ledger had not liked it. Two claims on one soul, a debt simultaneously paid and held—arithmetic with no solution. Quill's pen had hovered, had trembled, had snapped. And then everything had gone very quiet, the way a room goes quiet after an argument ends without a winner.

Wren clicks open the first letter.

* * *

"This one's from Mrs. Ada Prewitt, out past the drainage cut," she reads. "'Dear Wren—I've listened since your father's first broadcast, back when he thought nobody was tuning in. I want you to know my hip is much improved since February, and I attribute this entirely to dancing in my kitchen during your music hour, which my doctor calls nonsense and my late husband would have called showing off. Please play something fast.'"

Wren laughs, because the letter wants her to, and puts on the fastest thing in the crate: a scratchy 78 of some band whose name the label has surrendered to time. Through the glass, her father's lips keep moving. She has stopped finding it eerie. It took about three weeks. Now it just looks like what it is—a man saying yes, yes, this, all of this, I agree with every word.

The letters are all like this now. That is the miracle she never says out loud on air, though she thinks it every single night with a gratitude so large it frightens her sometimes: *everyone who writes in is alive.* Every caller, every correspondent, every voice that threads through the board and out across the black water of the fen—they have pulses. They complain about their knees and their neighbours and their children. They request songs. They argue about the vicar. Once, a woman called in simply to report that her cat had come home after nine days, and Wren had to put the fader down for a moment because her eyes went hot and her throat closed around the words *thank you, thank you, thank you for being alive enough to tell me.*

Her father used to take calls from the dead. She knows this now—all of it, the whole terrible ledger of it, dragged out into the kitchen light during the Family Vote, which is what they call that night in November when nobody slept and everybody told the truth. He had kept a dam standing with nothing but his voice and other people's ghosts, night after night, and it had eaten him by inches: his sleep, his memory, the birthday he forgot mid-sentence while three hundred souls queued politely on hold.

Wren had been eleven then. She is twelve now, nearly thirteen, and she has decided that thirteen will find her unafraid.

* * *

Midway through the hour, a letter arrives that makes her stop.

It comes the way most of them do these days—not through the post but through the slot in the chapel door that used to be for alms, a wooden mouth that swallows envelopes all day long. This one is heavy paper, cream-coloured, sealed with actual wax. The handwriting inside is upright and unhurried and smells faintly of dust and church incense.

*"Dear Miss Dunmore,"* it begins, *"I write infrequently and read always. You will forgive an old woman's curiosity regarding the eleventh door. Is it still shut? I ask only because I have been tidying, and one likes to know whether one's work holds."*

Mother Merrow.

Idris found her—or rather, Merrow had let herself be found, stepping back out of 1911's hallway the week after the bell broke, blinking in the electric light of the kitchen like a woman surfacing from deep water. She had looked at Idris—at the aunt-nephew strangeness of their meeting, at the bargain Idris had offered and retracted and offered again—and she had wept, this woman who invented a tithe to save her daughter and failed anyway, and she had said, "The count was mine. Let me carry what's left of it." And she had walked up the lane to the chapel ruins and begun, without asking anyone's permission, to sweep.

She lives there now, in the vestry, in a sleeping bag patterned with robins that Idris bought her as a joke and that she treats with complete seriousness. She tends the tower. She has planted rosemary where the bell fell, because rosemary is for remembrance and she has a great deal to remember. Once a month she sends a letter to the show, and once a month Wren reads part of it aloud, because it seems important that the village hear her voice doing something other than promising debts.

"The eleventh door is shut," Wren says into the microphone, answering across eighty years. "We checked last week. There are six doors in our house now. Six, exactly, and Dad counts them every night before bed and I pretend not to watch him do it, and Mum leaves the hall light on so he doesn't have to use matches anymore. Your work holds, Mother Merrow. Go to sleep."

Through the glass, her father's mouth curves. He heard nothing. He knows anyway. That is another thing she has learned: hearing is not the only way to be present. Sometimes presence is just showing up, night after night, and moving your mouth along with someone else's voice because their voice is the one you'd choose, if you could choose anything, ever again.

* * *

At ten o'clock she takes calls.

The switchboard lights up gently—one, two, three lines, no flood, no queue stretching into the dark. Line One is a boy named Tam who wants to know if the chapel is haunted, and Wren gives him the answer she has settled on, the answer that is true in every direction she can measure:

"There used to be something here that wasn't a ghost exactly. More like a sum that wouldn't finish. But somebody finished it." She glances at the booth. "A couple of somebodies. Now it's mostly just us, and the mice, and Mrs. Prewitt's requests. If you hear humming, it's probably me. I hum when I think nobody's listening."

Line Two is quieter. An old man, voice like gravel poured slow. He says his wife died in March, peacefully, in her sleep, the way everyone should be allowed to go, and that he isn't calling to talk to her—he knows better than that, he watched the same programmes everyone did, he remembers the bell—but he wanted to say her name somewhere out loud where the wind could carry it. "Elsie," he says. "Elsie Maren Tull. Just so it goes somewhere."

Wren leans toward the mic. "It goes everywhere, Mr. Tull. The transmitter reaches clear to the coast on nights like this. Elsie Maren Tull." She says it slowly, giving each syllable room. "There. Now it's on record, and records are the one thing we do properly in this chapel."

She hears him breathe out, a long shaky exhale, and then he thanks her and hangs up, alive, alive, alive, and Wren writes his wife's name in the notebook she keeps for exactly this purpose. The notebook has gotten fat. She hopes it gets fatter. A book full of names spoken in love, freely given, freely kept—that is the only kind of counting she intends to allow in this parish ever again.

Line Three is silent for a moment, and then a voice she knows says, "It's me. Don't make a fuss."

"Mum." Wren grins. "You're supposed to be home."

"I am home. I'm calling from the hallway so you'll know the light works and the doors are six and your father is—" a pause, and Wren can hear Idris turning, checking, the way she checks every night, a vigil disguised as habit, "—he's fine. He's waving at me through the window. Go on with your show. I only rang to listen from the proper end."

"You rang long-distance from your own hallway."

"Some of us believe in thoroughness," Idris says, and hangs up before Wren can tease her further, which is how love sounds in their family: abrupt, loyal, already halfway to checking something else.

* * *

Later, near midnight, Wren does the thing she saves for the end of every broadcast, the thing that started as accident and became ritual.

She turns off the transmitter's automation, cues her father's old chair-microphone—the one mounted low, near the booth window, dead equipment now except that she refuses to believe anything in this chapel is entirely dead—and she speaks directly to him.

"Dad. Tonight's letters were good ones. Ada danced. Mr. Tull's Elsie is on the wind now. Merrow's rosemary came up, all twelve plants—she counted them twice, obviously." She smiles. "School's fine. I got full marks on the maths, which Idris says proves I inherited the good side of the family's arithmetic, and I've decided to take that as a compliment instead of a haunting. The chalk still washes off the walls. The house is warm. Nobody's birthday got forgotten this year—we checked."

Through the glass, his lips move with hers. His eyes are wet. He nods, small and steady, nodding the way people nod at graves and weddings, the two occasions when nodding is the whole speech.

"And here's the sign-off," Wren says. "Same as always."

She takes a breath. Outside, the fen breathes with her—wind through the reeds, water ticking against the pilings, the village asleep under a sky with no bells in it anywhere, no bells in any of it, forever.

"My dad used to close the show with a line he stole from the chapel's own foundations, back when he thought the house was empty and the night was safe. He was wrong about the empty part, but he was never wrong about this part. So—from all of us, to all of you, living and otherwise, wherever you're listening from, whoever's keeping count of you and however kindly: this is the house, and the house is full."

She switches off the mic.

For a moment the studio holds the shape of her voice in the air the way a bell holds the shape of its last toll, and then that fades too, and there is only the hum of the transmitter winding down, and her father rising stiffly from his chair, and the two of them walking together out of the chapel and up the lane toward the lit windows of home, where Idris is waiting in the doorway, counting them in—two, two, both, all—and leaving the light burning until morning, just in case, just because some lamps are worth keeping lit whether or not anything is left to fear the dark.

Behind them, high in the tower, the rosemary stirs in the wind, and the empty beam stands clean against the stars, holding nothing, holding everything, needing nothing more.
