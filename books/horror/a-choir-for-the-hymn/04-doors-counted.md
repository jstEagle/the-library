## 4. Doors Counted

Dominic Dravenmoor owned forty-one door chimes, thirty-eight contact sensors, and a label maker she had named Bertha, and she was not ashamed of a single one of them.

People in Hymn's Rest thought pest control was about poison. It wasn't. Poison was what you used when you'd already failed. The job — the *real* job — was counting. Counting entry points, counting droppings, counting the gap under a door in millimeters because a mouse needed six and a rat needed twenty and a snake needed only the width of its own stubborn opinion. You counted everything, you wrote the numbers down, and the numbers never lied to you. Numbers didn't have granddaughters. Numbers didn't have opinions about your life choices or offer you coffee in a cup with a chip on the rim that you'd been drinking out of for three days because it felt rude to say no.

Numbers were the only family that had never disappointed her.

So on the third morning at Ida's house on Vesper Lane, Dominic backed her van up the gravel drive, popped the rear doors, and began, methodically, to fortify the place against whatever had been walking around the attic in shoes.

"You're doing what, exactly?" Ida asked from the porch, arms folded, wearing the expression of a woman watching someone wrap her house in bubble wrap.

"Monitoring." Dominic hefted the tackle box. "Contact alarms on every door and window. Chalk marks across each threshold and sill. Motion cameras on the attic stairs, the cellar door, and the kitchen."

"Chalk."

"It's the oldest trick in the book and it works. You draw a line. Anything that crosses it disturbs the line. No batteries, no signal to drop, no software update at two in the morning. Just chalk." She held up the stick like a small white talisman. "My grandfather's trick, actually."

Ida's face did something complicated and brief — a shutter going halfway down over a window — and then settled back into its usual granite. "Your grandfather counted things too."

"Everybody in my family counts things. It's the family hobby. Some families do jigsaws."

Ida snorted, which from her was practically a standing ovation, and went inside to put the kettle on, which was her answer to everything, including, Dominic suspected, the apocalypse.

* * *

It took the better part of the day, and it was, if Dominic was honest with herself, the best day she'd had in years.

She started at the top and worked down. The attic first, because you always started where the problem was worst, and the attic was worst. The dust up there still held the prints — five-fingered, human-shaped, too large, ending mid-stride at the sealed north wall — though she'd photographed them twice and covered the section of floor with a sheet of plywood, partly to preserve them, mostly so she'd stop looking at them every time she went up.

She mounted a sensor on the attic door. Drew a chalk line across the threshold, a clean white bar, and wrote *A-1* beside it in her own shorthand. Then the attic window, painted shut since roughly the Coolidge administration, which she noted in the log: *A-2, sealed, verified by hand, paint is load-bearing.*

The landing. The linen closet, because she wasn't taking chances, and because the linen closet door had a draft under it that no closet deserved. The bathroom window. The hall. Room by room, floor by floor, the house surrendered its openings to her one at a time, and with each chalk line she felt something in her chest unknot by a single degree.

This was the part of the job nobody understood. Other people thought the traps were the work. The traps were just the receipt. The work was this: standing in a strange house at the end of the day, knowing every way in and every way out, every gap and every seam, the whole building held in your head like a hymn you'd memorized. Nothing got into a house Dominic Dravenmoor had counted. Nothing got in *unnoticed*, which was the same thing, as far as she was concerned.

By late afternoon she'd done the ground floor. Front door — *G-1*. Back door — *G-2*. Kitchen window over the sink, the dining room's two tall sashes, the parlor, the study with its smell of cold pipe smoke that had outlasted her grandfather by thirty years. She chalked the cellar door at the top of the stairs and again at the bottom, because the cellar was the kind of dark that deserved redundancy, and she chalked the coal chute cover outside, and she chalked the sill of every window on the south wall even though two of them hadn't opened since before she was born.

Forty-one openings. She wrote the number at the top of a fresh page in her log book and underlined it twice.

Forty-one lines. Forty-one witnesses. If anything crossed any of them, she'd know.

She stood in the kitchen doorway at dusk, log book under her arm, and allowed herself one full breath of the specific satisfaction that came from a job done properly.

"You look like a woman who's just alphabetized something," Ida said, setting two cups of tea on the table. The chipped one was pushed toward Dominic. She'd stopped fighting it.

"I've secured the perimeter."

"You've scribbled on my house."

"I've *documented* your house. There's a difference, and it's forty-one differences."

Ida looked at her over the rim of her cup, and for a moment — just a moment — the granite cracked and something else looked out. Something tired. Something that looked, God help her, *grateful*.

"Your mother used to count the fence pickets," Ida said quietly. "Every summer. All two hundred and six of them. She'd come in at the end of the day and tell me the number like it was news."

"And was it ever different?"

"No," Ida said. "It was never different. That was the point of it, I think." She looked down into her tea. "I never understood that until this week."

Dominic didn't ask what she meant. She was learning that Ida's silences had doors of their own, and that you didn't get them open by knocking. You waited, and eventually they opened themselves, the way the attic door had opened for her the first night, smooth and silent on hinges that should have shrieked.

She drank her tea. She wrote up the day's log. She set two cameras to record and plugged in the receiver on the kitchen counter, a little black box with forty-one tiny lights, one for each opening, all of them dark and quiet and good.

Then she went to bed in her childhood room, under a ceiling she'd once mapped by glow-star, and slept better than she had in weeks.

* * *

The receiver woke her at 3:47 a.m.

Not with an alarm — she hadn't armed the sirens, not with Ida in the house; a false alarm at that hour would give the old woman a heart attack and give Dominic a heart attack right after — but with the soft, patient *chirp* of a contact sensor reporting a change of state.

She lay in the dark and counted. That was the drill. One chirp, wait. Two chirps, wait. Wind rattled a sash, you got one chirp and then silence. A branch, same. A raccoon on the porch roof, same. The house talked in its sleep like anyone, and most of what it said was nothing.

The chirp came again. Same zone. Then a third time, a fourth — a slow, even rhythm, like something tapping a foot.

Zone 12. She had the map memorized before her feet hit the floor. *Landing closet.* The linen closet with the draft under it.

Dominic took the flashlight, took the log book, and went out into the hall, moving the way she moved through strange houses at two in the morning: heel to toe, weight low, breathing through her nose. The house was cold in the way old houses get cold, a cold that seemed to come from inside the walls rather than outside them.

The linen closet door stood shut. The sensor on the frame showed a small amber light — open, then closed, then open, then closed. Someone — something — had been opening and closing the door. Slowly. Repeatedly. For how long, the receiver couldn't tell her. The log would.

She knelt. The chalk line across the closet threshold was intact. Unbroken. She checked it twice, ran her thumb an inch above it to be sure she wasn't seeing dust. Intact.

But the draft under the door — the draft she'd noted in the log, the draft no closet deserved — was gone. The gap at the bottom of the door, the one she'd measured at nine millimeters with a feeler gauge because she measured everything, was sealed. Not with a towel. Not with anything she could see. The door simply sat flush against the threshold now, snug as a coffin lid, and the cold air that had been leaking out of that closet for sixty years had stopped leaking.

Dominic stood there in the dark for a long moment, flashlight off, listening.

From inside the closet came a sound she would spend the rest of her life not quite describing to anyone. Not scratching. Not breathing. Something like a page being turned. Once. Gently. The way you'd turn a page if you were reading in bed and didn't want to wake anyone.

She opened the door.

Towels. Blankets. The box of her mother's winter things that Ida had never given away. A hot water bottle with a floral cover, older than Dominic. And on the top shelf, where this afternoon there had been nothing but dust and a dead moth, a single object she did not recognize: a small wooden tally stick, hand-carved, notched along its length with careful little cuts.

She counted the notches without meaning to. It was reflex, like blinking. Forty.

She stood very still, holding the stick, and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. Forty notches. She'd been in the house four days. Four days, ten openings chalked per day on average, forty marks laid down in white across every threshold and sill of the house.

The stick was counting her chalk lines.

Or — and this was the thought that made her put the stick down on the hall table and wipe her palm on her jeans — the stick was counting *down*.

She got the log book. She wrote it all down in her flat, careful hand: *3:47, zone 12, closet door cycled open/closed, approx. 4x. Chalk line intact. Draft gap (9mm) now sealed from within. Object found: wooden tally, 40 notches, origin unknown.* Her handwriting didn't shake. Her handwriting never shook. That was the whole point of her handwriting.

She did not go back to sleep. She sat in the kitchen until the windows went gray, watching the receiver's forty-one little lights, and drank Ida's terrible tea, and told herself that a tally stick was a thing a person could explain. A family thing. An old thing. Houses full of history were full of objects, and objects got moved around, and drafts sealed themselves in old houses when the weather changed, and page-turning sounds were the house settling, and she was a professional, and professionals did not spook.

At dawn she did her morning round. It was ritual now, third day running: walk the house, check every line, log every mark. She did the upstairs first, then the landing, then down.

Front door: intact. Back door: intact. Kitchen window, dining room sashes, parlor, study, cellar door, coal chute, south-wall sashes — all forty of them, clean white bars, unbroken, every one.

Then she came to the last one. The hall window at the front of the house, the tall one over the radiator, the one that looked out on Vesper Lane. Zone 41. The last line she'd drawn yesterday evening, the one that had made the job feel finished.

The chalk mark was smudged.

Not broken — smudged. Swept sideways, a soft gray smear two inches wide, as if something had pressed against the line from the *inside* of the glass and dragged along it, slow, the way you'd trail a finger down a window while watching someone walk away down the street.

Dominic stood in front of it with her log book open and her pen uncapped, and for the first time in her professional life, she could not write anything down. Because the log was a contract with the world — *this happened, I saw it, the number is the number* — and the number, this morning, was a thing she could not make herself believe.

Behind her, on the stairs, Ida's voice came quiet and level, and it was the levelness that frightened her more than anything the night had done.

"You found it, then."

Dominic turned. Ida stood on the third step in her dressing gown, gray hair loose, looking at the smudged window with an expression Dominic had never seen on her — not denial, not granite. Recognition. Old, worn-smooth recognition, like a step worn hollow by feet.

"Grandma," Dominic said. "What is counting my chalk lines?"

Ida came down the last two stairs and stood beside her at the window, and together they looked out at Vesper Lane in the early light, at the frost silvering the lawns, at the town of Hymn's Rest waking up under a sky gone pale as chalk.

"It's not counting your lines," Ida said. "It's counting *days*." She paused, and her hand, when it closed on Dominic's arm, was cold as the glass. "And there are thirty-nine left."
