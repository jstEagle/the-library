## 7. The Ledger

The chapel archive smelled like a library that had given up.

Dominic stood in the doorway of the little room behind the vestry, breathing in dust and old paper and something faintly sweet underneath, like flowers left too long in a closed drawer. The room was small—smaller than her supply van, and she'd measured that van to the inch when she bought it, because knowing your measurements was half of staying out of trouble. One window, high and narrow, letting down a wedge of afternoon light thick enough to stir with a spoon. Shelves on three walls. A table in the middle with a green banker's lamp that had probably been state-of-the-art under Eisenhower.

Nobody had asked her to be here. That was the point. She'd told the church secretary—a nervous young man named Tad who alphabetized his pens—that she was doing a follow-up inspection on the chapel's bat situation, which was technically true. There were bats. Three of them, roosting under the eaves, and she'd humanely relocated them Tuesday with more tenderness than she showed most people. But bats weren't why she'd come back Wednesday with a flashlight, a lock pick she'd owned since she was nineteen and had never once used on anything that could press charges, and a story about checking for droppings in the vestry.

The truth was simpler and worse: Ruth Ellery had gone silent at the word *founders*, and Dominic Dravenmoor did not do well with silences. Silences were just infestations of things nobody wanted to name.

"Okay," she said quietly, to the room, because talking to herself was cheaper than therapy and came with better posture. "Let's see what your problem is."

The shelves were the usual civic sediment—minutes from parish councils, hymnals missing their spines, a framed photograph of the 1954 strawberry festival that featured so many Ashes and Corvins and Ellerys it looked less like a crowd than a family reunion that had invited the town along as a courtesy. Dominic's own surname appeared nowhere. It never did. Dravenmoors kept books, fixed pipes, cleared pests. They didn't get their names carved into things. For thirty-four years she'd assumed this was a flaw in the arrangement. Standing here, looking at all those confident engraved names, she was starting to wonder if it might have been a mercy.

She started at the oldest shelf, because if you wanted to find what a town was hiding, you went where the light didn't bother visiting.

The first hour yielded nothing but tedium, which she almost enjoyed. Tedium was honest. She read through donation records from the 1930s (the Ashe family had funded the new roof twice, and had made sure everyone knew it), attendance rolls, a truly alarming number of minutes devoted to disputes about the placement of a hedge. She learned that in 1941 the chapel council had voted nine to two against replacing the bell rope, on the grounds that the old rope "had character." She learned that someone named Obadiah Finch had been fined one dollar in 1888 for letting his pig into the harvest service, and that the fine had been appealed, and that the appeal had taken up three separate meetings. Hymn's Rest, it turned out, had always been exactly this fascinating.

It was on the bottom shelf—the one you had to kneel to reach, the one where the dust lay undisturbed in a gray felt blanket—that her fingers found the ledger.

It wasn't hidden, exactly. That was the thing she noticed first, kneeling there in the dark with her heart doing an unprofessional sort of drumroll. It wasn't wrapped in oilcloth or locked in a box or tucked behind a false panel. It was just... filed. Shelved between a record book of baptisms and a box of Sunday school attendance pins, spine out, labeled in faded copperplate: *Chapel Accounts, 1801–*.

Accounts. Not secrets. Bookkeeping.

Dominic pulled it out and set it on the table under the green lamp, and the weight of it surprised her—it was a fat thing, leather gone the color of strong tea, corners worn round like river stones. Two hundred years of handling. Somebody had opened this book often. Somebody had opened it regularly, on a schedule, the way she checked her traps.

She opened it to the first page and read the heading twice, because the first time her eyes had slid off it like water off wax.

*Received in kind, for the term agreed, per the covenant of the founding seven.*

Below that, in the same careful copperplate, began a list.

*1801 — Paid: two oxen, twelve sheep, one horse, dark. Received by: A. Ashe, E. Corvin, J. Ellery, S. Vesper, T. Mott, R. Finch, W. Hymn.*

Dominic sat down on the archive's only chair, which complained about it, and kept reading.

*1820 — Paid: four oxen, twenty sheep, one horse, dark, and one calf born blind. Same seven names.*

Nineteen years between entries. She counted them forward with her finger, the way she counted anything, because counting was the load-bearing wall of her whole personality. 1820. 1839. 1858. 1877. Every nineteen years, like clockwork, like a bill arriving. And every nineteen years, the same seven surnames signed at the bottom—not seven different men across two centuries, but seven *names*, passed down hand to hand like a debt nobody dared let lapse. An Ashe. An Ellery. A Corvin. A Vesper. A Mott. A Finch. A Hymn.

The town itself was named for one of them. She'd walked past the historical plaque on the green a thousand times—*Hymn's Rest, established by the founding families in the year of our Lord 1799*—and it had never once occurred to her that the plaque was less a tribute than a signature block.

*Paid in kind.* Her dad had used that phrase once, years ago, about a client who couldn't cover an invoice. It meant paying with what you had instead of money. Livestock. Goods. She turned pages. The payments stayed livestock for a long time—sheep, cattle, always *one horse, dark*, which struck her as oddly specific, the way a contract is specific—and then, in 1896, the entries changed flavor.

*1896 — Paid: stock as before, and one quarter of the Mott harvest, unthriven. Received by: the seven, being G. Ashe, P. Corvin, L. Ellery, H. Vesper, D. Mott, C. Finch, B. Hymn.*

*Unthriven.* A harvest that hadn't done well. They'd paid the thing—or whatever it was—with their worst, their failed crops, their blind calves, their dark horses. And she supposed that made a cold kind of sense. You didn't pay a protection racket with your best heifer.

Except the entries kept going, and they stopped making even cold sense.

*1930 — Paid: stock as before, and the Vesper orchard entire, blighted. The seven note with satisfaction that the terms hold.*

*Satisfaction.* Dominic said the word out loud just to hear how wrong it sounded in the quiet room. There was something in those margins now, little annotations in different hands across the decades, and the hands were pleased. Relieved. One entry from 1949 had a note beside it in looping script: *A lean year everywhere but here. Thanks be.*

Thanks be. To what? To whom? She flipped backward through the pages, faster now, looking for the beginning of it, the covenant itself—but the first page was the first page. Whatever agreement had created this ledger predated the ledger. The book only recorded the payments, faithfully, generation after generation, the way a meter records gas. Nobody had ever written down *what we are paying*. They'd only written down *that we are paying*, and *that we are glad to pay it*, and *that the terms hold*.

Terms held by seven families. Benefits received by—well. By everyone whose name was on a plaque. By everyone whose roof the Ashes had funded. By a town that had never known a flood, a fire, a famine, not in two hundred years, while towns forty miles either direction had histories full of them. Dominic had grown up hearing how lucky Hymn's Rest was. Blessed, people said. Set apart.

It wasn't blessed. It was *current on its payments*.

She became aware, distantly, that her hands had gone cold, and that she was holding the page flat with one palm the way you hold down something alive. Outside the high window the light had gone long and orange. Somewhere in the sanctuary a radiator ticked. Ordinary sounds. The ordinary world, still running, indifferent.

Because here was the thing—the thing that settled over her not like fear but like clarity, cold and clean as well water: this wasn't a haunting. Hauntings were chaos. Hauntings were grief and accident and a dead woman's unfinished business rattling around where it shouldn't be. She'd spent her life around the edges of other people's hauntings, real or imagined, and they were always messy, always personal, always *wrong*.

This was none of those things. This was neat. This was scheduled. Nineteen years, every nineteen years, two hundred years of nineteen years, entered in a fair hand and countersigned by seven surnames who had never once missed a payment. Somewhere beneath the chapel—beneath the chapel that predated the county, that predated the roads, that had been built *first* the way you build a house around a furnace—something was owed something, on a schedule, and the founding families had been keeping the account current since before anyone living was born.

And the last entry—

Her finger stopped. The last completed entry was 1968. Nineteen years later would have been 1987. She turned the page. 1987 should have been there.

There was a gap. A single blank line where 1987's entry belonged, and beside it, in a shakier hand than any she'd seen so far, three words:

*Payment interrupted.*

Nothing after. No 2006 entry. No explanation. Just that one broken sentence and then two hundred years of bookkeeping stopping mid-breath, like a hymn cut off before the final verse.

1968. Ida's mother. The keeper who died mid-ceremony. The binding that was never finished.

Dominic sat very still in the complaining chair and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding, because arithmetic didn't care whether you avoided it. If the schedule held—if the *terms* held, the way two centuries of satisfied marginalia insisted they did—then the next payment fell due nineteen years after the last one that should have happened. Or nineteen after the interruption. Or nineteen after 1968, depending on how you read a contract written by frightened people.

However you ran the numbers, the answer landed somewhere close. Weeks, maybe. This autumn. The harvest season, when the seven families had always gathered to pay.

She looked down at the ledger, at the seven names signed and resigned and re-signed across ten generations, and felt something in her chest shift from dread into something harder and more useful. Because she finally understood what she was looking at. Not a monster story. Not a ghost story. A *ledger*. Debts, payments, accounts receivable. And if it was bookkeeping—

Then it could be audited.

The lamp buzzed. Down the hall, Tad the secretary dropped something metal and swore with impressive fluency for a man who alphabetized his pens. The ordinary world, still going.

Dominic photographed every page with her phone, closed the ledger, and returned it to its shelf—bottom row, spine out, exactly as found—because the first rule of finding something terrible was to leave it looking untouched until you understood who else was watching it.

She was nearly at the door when she saw it: a slip of paper protruding from the baptism register, modern paper, printer-white against all that cream and brown. She shouldn't have. She did anyway.

It was a printed program. *Harvest Service, Chapel of the Founding Seven,* dated three weeks from Sunday. At the bottom, in small type, where the order of hymns would normally end, was a single additional line:

*The payment will be received in kind.*

Someone had already set the type. Someone was already planning the service. And the ink was fresh enough to smudge.

* * *

Dominic drove home with the windows down even though the evening had teeth in it, because she needed the cold and she needed the noise and she needed, more than either, to not be in a building for a while.

Vesper Lane was lit up yellow when she turned onto it, and the first thing she saw was Ida's porch light burning steady, and the second thing she saw was the car parked in front of the house. A good car. A bank-owned kind of car.

Corwin Ashe was sitting on her grandmother's porch steps like he'd been poured there, hat in his hands, wearing the expression of a man come to discuss a fence line. Through the screen door, Ida's voice was going, low and level, saying no to something. Saying it the way she said everything—like the word had been load-tested.

Dominic parked, and got out, and stood in the road with the ledger's arithmetic humming in her skull, and watched the eldest son of the founding seven turn to look at her with eyes that were friendly the way a trap is friendly right up until it isn't.

"Miss Dravenmoor," Corwin Ashe called pleasantly. "Good. We were just talking about your grandmother's house. About what's owed on it."

He smiled.

"You keep books, don't you?"
