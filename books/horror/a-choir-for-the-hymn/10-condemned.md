## 10. Condemned

The red tag went up on a Tuesday, which Dominic thought was an insult to Tuesdays.

She'd been on Vesper Lane before eight that morning, restocking the bait stations she'd set along Ida's foundation line, when the county van pulled up—two men in polo shirts with clipboards and the particular cheerfulness of people about to ruin someone's week. She recognized one of them vaguely: Dale Pruitt's boy, all grown up and wearing a lanyard that said *MUNICIPAL INSPECTION SERVICES* like it was a medal.

"Morning," he said. "You the pest lady?"

"I'm the pest professional," Dominic said. "Yes."

"We got a complaint. Structural concerns." He flipped a page without looking at it. "Foundation settling, moisture intrusion, possible vermin activity."

"Possible vermin activity," Dominic repeated. "I've been treating this property for three weeks. My reports are filed with the county. There is no active infestation."

"That's not really my department." He smiled at her the way you smile at a dog that's barking at the mailman—patient, dismissive, already walking past her. "We're just doing our job, ma'am."

Dominic watched him climb the porch steps of the house where his own grandfather had probably attended christenings, and felt something in her chest go tight and cold. She knew how this worked. She'd spent forty-one years learning exactly how this worked: paperwork moved through Hymn's Rest the way water moved through limestone—slowly, invisibly, and always toward the lowest point, which was never where the powerful people stood.

The tag they nailed to Ida's door was bright orange, official, final. *UNSAFE FOR OCCUPANCY.* It flapped in the morning wind like something alive trying to get free.

Ida stood in the doorway behind it, arms crossed, watching them leave.

"Well," she said. "That's that, then."

"That's not that," Dominic said. "We can contest it. There's an appeals process."

"There's an appeals process," Ida agreed, "and Corwin Ashe sits on the appeals board, and his grandfather signed the same ledger yours did, so tell me, Dominic, how do you figure that goes?"

Dominic didn't have an answer for that. Ida nodded as if she hadn't expected one, and went inside, and the orange tag kept flapping, and Dominic stood on the lawn holding a bag of bait blocks and feeling, for the first time in her adult life, like the pest.

* * *

They came for Ida on Thursday.

Dominic arrived at ten to find a white van in the driveway—not an ambulance, nothing so honest—and a woman in scrubs talking to Ida on the porch in the gentle, pre-chewed voice people used for the very old and the very young. The paperwork was already signed. Of course it was. Somewhere a doctor Dominic had never met had determined that Ida Dravenmoor required *supervised care*, and somewhere else a bed had been arranged at the Ellsworth Annex, which everyone in town called the chapel home because it was, in fact, four rooms built onto the back of the First Congregational Chapel like a barnacle on a hull.

"I don't want to go," Ida said—not to the woman in scrubs, who wasn't listening, but to Dominic, standing at the bottom of the steps with her hands in her jacket pockets.

"It's temporary," the woman said brightly. "Just until the house is brought up to code."

"My house has been up to code since Eisenhower," Ida said. "My house has outlasted every code this county ever wrote. My house was here before the county was a county." Her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and Dominic saw the fear underneath—the same fear from the photographs, from the smudged chalk mark, from every conversation where Ida insisted nothing was wrong until the insisting itself became proof that something was.

"Ma'am, we really need to get going."

Dominic stepped forward. "Where exactly is she being taken?"

"The Ellsworth Annex. Lovely facility. Garden views."

Garden views. The annex faced the cemetery. Dominic had driven past it her whole life and never once thought about who lived there or why the windows were painted with a frost film that no amount of sun ever seemed to melt.

"I'll visit," Dominic said to her grandmother. "Every day. I'll bring your crossword books. The hard ones, since apparently we're both gluttons."

Ida gripped her hand briefly, hard, her fingers cold as cellar stone. "Count the doors," she whispered. "The ones without hinges."

Then she let herself be led down the steps and into the van, spine straight, chin up, a small fierce woman being carried off by paperwork, and Dominic stood on Vesper Lane and understood—with the clarity of a trap snapping shut—that staying invisible had a price, and the price was everything.

* * *

She gave it two days. Two days of calling the annex and being told visiting hours were "limited during the adjustment period." Two days of driving past and seeing the frost-film windows glowing faintly at hours when no lights should be on. Two days of Ruth Ellery not answering her phone, which frightened Dominic more than anything, because Ruth had answered her phone during two hurricanes and the death of three husbands.

On the second night, she went back with a crowbar and a flashlight and no plan whatsoever, which was unlike her, and which she decided counted as growth.

The annex had two entrances: the main door off the chapel's side hall, and a service door on the north wall near the kitchen. The service door had a lock older than Dale Pruitt's boy and Dominic defeated it in under a minute, murmuring apologies to the lock itself, because breaking into buildings was against her nature even when the buildings deserved it.

Inside smelled the way old churches smell—candle wax, dust, wood polish, and underneath it all something mineral and deep, like the air in a cave. The hallway lights were off except for one bulb burning amber at the far end. No night nurse at the desk. No sound at all except a hum she couldn't place, low and steady, less a machine than a throat.

Room numbers ran along the left wall. 1 through 6. Ida would be in the last one; they always put the difficult ones farthest from the desk.

Dominic walked the hallway counting her own footsteps because that was what she did, that was who she was, and stopped outside room 6 when she realized the door was open.

Not ajar. Open. Wide open, flat against the wall, as if inviting inspection.

She looked in.

The room was neat as a pin. Bed made with hospital corners. Crossword books stacked on the nightstand—all of them, every one she'd bought, including the unopened ones from her coat pocket two days ago, which meant someone had gone through Ida's things, or Ida had packed them herself, or—

The chair sat beside the bed.

Plain wooden. Straight-backed. Unremarkable in every way except that Dominic had seen it now in sixty years of family photographs, always at the edge of the frame, always empty, always waiting, and here it was out of the pictures at last, positioned precisely where a visitor would sit, angled toward the pillow, close enough that whoever—or whatever—sat in it could have reached out and touched the place where Ida's head had been.

There were fingerprints on the armrest. Five-fingered. Too large. Pressed deep into the varnish like the wood had been soft when they landed.

Dominic's flashlight trembled. She steadied it with both hands, the way she'd learned to hold a drill, and made herself look at the whole room. No sign of struggle. No sign of Ida at all, really—no clothes in the little wardrobe, no shoes, no reading glasses. As if her grandmother had been lifted out of this room like a stone from a fruit.

The hum was louder here. Coming, she realized, from below. From under the floor, under the annex, under the chapel, down where foundations went when foundations had secrets.

"Okay," Dominic said aloud, to the empty chair, because talking to vermin was a professional habit and she refused to stop being a professional just because the vermin had gotten ambitious. "Here's how this goes. You've had her one day. That was your day. Tomorrow starts mine."

The chair did not answer. But the temperature in the room dropped the way it drops when a door opens somewhere in a house—a draft with intention behind it—and for one clear moment Dominic felt what she imagined her great-grandmother must have felt every day of her life as keeper: the sensation of being watched by something patient, something that had waited fifty years behind a sealed wall and could certainly wait out one angry woman with a flashlight.

Except it hadn't waited, had it? Not anymore. The chalk mark smudged from inside. The prints ending mid-stride. The chair out of the photographs and into the world. Something had changed recently, something had loosened, and the elders' answer had been to feed it faster—to skip the livestock, skip the ritual, throw them an old woman and call it care.

Dominic backed out of room 6, closed the door gently—it seemed polite, given everything—and stood in the amber-lit hallway doing arithmetic.

Seven founding families. Seven chairs below the chapel, one broken since 1968. A debt due within weeks. A grandmother missing on the eve of payment. And a ledger in the archive with two centuries of signatures, which meant names, which meant a list, and Dominic Dravenmoor had never in her life met a list she couldn't work through methodically.

Her whole life she'd kept her head down. Kept the doors counted, kept the jobs quiet, kept herself a footnote in a town full of people who'd never once said her name right. She'd told herself it was wisdom. Caution. The Dravenmoor way.

It had been hiding. And hiding had nearly cost her the only person left who shared her blood and her burden and her stubborn, unfashionable love of this crooked little house on Vesper Lane.

Staying invisible meant abandoning her. That was the whole equation. Solve for x.

Dominic clicked off the flashlight and stood in the dark, breathing the cave-smell of the place, and felt the guilt in her chest do what guilt does when it finally has somewhere to go: it caught fire.

She walked out the way she'd come in, pausing only at the service door to look back down the long amber hallway—at room 6, at the hum beneath the floor, at the whole quiet machinery of Hymn's Rest running smoothly over whatever it had buried.

"Keep the lights on for me," she said softly. "I'll be counting."

Outside, the night was cold and enormous. Dominic got in her truck, and instead of driving home, she drove to the chapel archive, because tomorrow the elders would hold their harvest planning meeting, and Corwin Ashe would stand up in front of the town wearing his civic face, and she intended to be there when he did.

But first she needed the ledger. Every name. Every signature. Every debt.

The thing below the chapel had knocked politely for fifty years.

Dominic was done knocking.
