## 1. The Counting Hour

Rosalind Eastwick had three rules for surviving the night shift at Blackmere House, and he'd written them on the inside cover of his pocket notebook in handwriting so neat it looked typeset: *Lights stay on. Doors get counted. Never hum.*

The first two were his own inventions. The third had come with the job.

He arrived at ten each evening through the staff entrance on the north side, where the hedges grew tall and slightly crooked, like a choir that had been standing too long without permission to sit. He'd say goodnight to Marcus at the desk—Marcus always had one earbud in and a crossword half-finished—and he'd take the handoff clipboard from whichever day nurse was fleeing toward her car with the gratitude of a woman escaping a burning building she happened to love.

"Quiet night," they'd say.

They always said it. Rosalind had learned that "quiet night" at Blackmere was less a report than a wish, the way sailors say *fair winds* knowing perfectly well the sea has its own opinions.

Tonight's quiet night featured Mrs. Halloran in Room 9, ninety-one years old, formerly a piano teacher, currently occupying that soft borderland where the doctors used words like *transition* and the nurses used words like *soon*, and Mrs. Halloran herself used no words at all, just opened her eyes occasionally to check whether the world was still bothering to be there.

Rosalind liked her. He liked most of them, which was the whole trouble with hospice work—you were hired precisely because you could bear to like people who were leaving, and then you spent your career being liked back by people who wouldn't remember liking you, and some part of you filed every goodbye under a heading you never read but never threw out either.

At eleven he made his rounds.

This was ritual as much as duty. He started at the east end of the corridor—he did not go into the east wing itself, because the east wing was sealed and nobody talked about it, which is the sort of thing that either makes a man curious or makes him very carefully un-curious, and Rosalind had chosen un-curious the way other men choose religion—and he worked his way west, checking each room, noting each patient, touching each doorframe lightly with two fingers as he passed.

Twelve doors between the east wing and the stairwell. He counted them every time. Not because he expected the number to change—he wasn't superstitious, he told himself, he was *thorough*, there was a difference, and the difference was mostly that thoroughness didn't require him to admit what he actually believed—but because counting was a thing hands and mouths could do while the mind stood quietly at a window, watching.

Twelve doors. Good.

He checked the lights. Every bulb on the night corridor burned steady and warm; he had personally replaced the flickering one outside Room 6 during his first week, not because it bothered him, but because a flickering light made shadows move, and moving shadows at Blackmere House had a way of looking like they were keeping time with something.

By midnight he'd settled into the nurses' station with tea going lukewarm beside him and the incident log open to a fresh page. The log was mostly blank, because Blackmere's nights were genuinely gentle—the house was old money turned old kindness, a private hospice in what had once been somebody's absurdly grand country estate, all dark wood and wide stairs and portraits of founders looking down with expressions caught somewhere between benevolence and indigestion.

Rosalind wrote: *11:40 — All residents resting. Corridor lights nominal.* Then, after a moment, because the page looked lonely: *Tea adequate.*

He was not a man given to poetry, but he believed logs should tell the truth, and the truth was that the tea in the staff kitchen tasted like it had been brewed from a bag found behind a radiator. It was honest tea. It kept him company.

At 12:52, Mrs. Halloran began to count.

---

He heard it first as rhythm rather than sound—a pulse in the quiet, the way you notice a clock only when it stops. He set down his pen.

Room 9 was four doors down. He walked there without hurrying, because hurrying suggested alarm, and alarm was not on the schedule.

Mrs. Halloran lay small against her pillows, eyes closed, hands folded at her waist like a woman waiting for a bus she'd been promised would be comfortable. Her lips moved.

"Ten," she said.

Her voice was wrong.

Not weak—wrong. Mrs. Halloran's speaking voice, when she'd still spent it freely, had been dry and brisk, a teacher's voice, the voice of someone who had corrected forty years of sloppy fingering. This voice was rounder. Warmer. It came from deeper in her chest, from somewhere below the chest, from somewhere below the floor, if a voice could do such a thing, and it carried the particular confidence of a person reading aloud from a book they have read many times and love.

"Nine."

Rosalind stood in the doorway. His hand found the doorframe. Two fingers, light touch. Habit holding his hand while the rest of him went still.

"Eight."

Here was the thing—the thing he had never said aloud to anyone, the thing that lived in the same locked drawer of himself where he kept the reason he'd taken this job at this house in particular, hours from anywhere he'd lived before, answering an advertisement he'd never quite been able to find again afterward.

The dying counted.

Not all of them. But enough. Enough that over eighteen months of nights he had stopped pretending it was coincidence. Somewhere past two in the morning, usually, a resident would slip beneath the surface of ordinary speech and begin counting backwards from ten, in that same warm borrowed voice, unhurried, precise, like a conductor giving a downbeat to an orchestra only they could hear.

And then the others would join.

"Seven."

Down the corridor, in Room 4, Mr. Okafor—who had not spoken a clear word in eleven days—murmured, "Seven." In Room 11, little Mr. Petrov, whose English was mostly smiles and apologies, said "seven" in perfect accentless unison, though Rosalind would have bet anything he'd never learned to count in English at all.

"Six."

Three voices now, layered, aligned—not echoing, *harmonizing*, the way voices do when they've rehearsed. And beneath them, so faint he might have invented it, something else: a hum in the walls themselves, or in the pipes, or in the old bones of the house, a low sustained note like a cello deciding whether to commit.

Rosalind clicked on the lamp by Mrs. Halloran's bed. Light was rule one. He did not know why the light mattered. He knew only that on the nights he kept every lamp burning, the counting stayed in the rooms where it belonged, and on the one night early on—he'd been new, tired, frugal with electricity—when he'd let the corridor go dark, the counting had followed him. Down the hall. Past twelve doors. To the foot of the stairs. Where it had waited, politely, for him to come down and join.

He had gone home at dawn and slept with every light in his flat blazing and had never let it happen twice.

"Five."

He took Mrs. Halloran's hand. It was cool and light, bird-boned, and her fingers curled around his with surprising strength, the grip of a woman who had spent decades pulling reluctant students' wrists into position over middle C.

"It's alright, Mrs. Halloran," he said softly. "I'm here."

She smiled without opening her eyes. And for one strange, suspended second, the smile belonged entirely to her—to Edith Halloran, piano teacher, widow, legendary critic of insufficient wrist posture—and Rosalind felt her squeeze his hand once, deliberately, the way you'd tap a student's knuckle to get their attention.

Then the warmth of it passed through him like a chord resolving, and she said:

"Four."

And the house answered.

It wasn't loud. That was the worst of it. If it had been loud, he could have called it pipes, wind, imagination, the collective creak of an old building settling into its own weight. But it was quiet—a fourth voice joining the three, coming from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls and the floorboards and the space behind the wallpaper, a voice with no throat at all, singing the number the way you'd sing a note held long past comfort.

*Four,* said the house. And the hum beneath everything deepened by exactly one degree, the way a choir leans into a chord it knows is coming.

Rosalind's skin did something complicated. He breathed. In through the nose, out slow. He had learned that trick from a midwife, years ago, in another life: *the breath is the rudder, love. Panic steers nothing.*

"Three," said Mrs. Halloran, and Mr. Okafor, and Mr. Petrov, and the house, all together now, four voices braided into one, and Rosalind understood—with the calm clarity that arrives precisely when it is least welcome—that this was not dying. Dying was ragged. Dying was fear and wet breath and hands clutching. This was *rehearsed*. This was a piece of music being performed by people who knew their parts, conducted by something patient enough to wait a century for the tempo to feel natural.

"Two."

His notebook was in his pocket. He thought about writing it down—*12:54, chorus of four, pitch consistent, key uncertain*—because writing things down was how he kept the world nailed to its board. But his hand stayed where it was, wrapped in Mrs. Halloran's grip, and some instincts outrank documentation.

"One."

The hum swelled. Every lamp in the corridor brightened—not flickered, *brightened*, steadily, as if the bulbs were leaning forward in their seats. Rosalind felt the note enter his own chest, resonating in the hollows of him, and he clamped his jaw shut against it, because he knew—Irma, the day nurse, had told him her first week, laughing, not laughing—"never hum along, whatever you do, the old girls all say it, never hum"—and he hadn't asked why, and she hadn't offered, and that mutual silence had been one of the most cordial exchanges of his career.

The chord held. One. Held. Held longer than a number should hold, stretching like taffy, like a fermata written by someone who wanted the audience to squirm—

And released.

Silence rushed back in like air after a held dive. The lamps settled to their ordinary warm yellow. In Room 9, Mrs. Halloran exhaled, long and slow and entirely human, and her monitor drew its line in gentle waves, and her hand in his went soft as paper.

She didn't die. Not tonight. She drifted instead, breathing evenly, her face smoothed of everything except a faint expression Rosalind could only describe as *satisfied*—the look of a singer coming off stage who knows the second half of the program awaits.

In Room 4, Mr. Okafor snored. In Room 11, Mr. Petrov murmured something in Polish, off-key, gloriously unrehearsed, and rolled over.

Rosalind stood in the doorway of Room 9 for a long moment, holding a dead woman's future in one hand and his own composure in the other, and then he did what he always did.

He finished the rounds.

The rest of the shift behaved itself. At three he ate the sandwich he'd brought—egg and cress, his one concession to sentiment, since his mother had made them the same way, or he believed she had, which amounted to the same thing in the country where he lived now. At four he rechecked the doors. Twelve. At five the sky over the garden went the color of dishwater, and at six Marcus arrived with his crossword and his earbud and his cheerful, impenetrable young-man silence.

"Quiet night?" Marcus asked.

"Very," said Rosalind. "Everyone's resting."

It was true. It was also, he reflected as he signed out, the kind of sentence that should come with an asterisk the size of a dinner plate.

Outside, the morning was cold and clean. The hedges stood at their crooked attention. Above the east wing, in a window he had never seen open, a curtain hung perfectly still in a wind that moved every tree on the grounds.

Rosalind got into his car, sat with his hands on the wheel, and allowed himself the luxury of shaking—briefly, efficiently, the way you shake a thermometer before reading it.

Then he drove home, slept eight dreamless hours under a nightlight he told visitors was for reading, and returned the following evening at ten o'clock sharp, because the dying needed someone, and because the advertisement had found him, somehow, and because a drawer in his chest held things he couldn't look at yet.

On his second night, at 1:17 in the morning, he went to wash his hands in the staff bathroom, and while the water ran he glanced up at the mirror above the sink—at his own tired face, his own careful collar, the fluorescent bar humming overhead—

—and saw himself raise his cupped hands to drink a full second before he actually did.

Rosalind stood very still, water cooling on his wrists, and watched his reflection catch up to him like a late guest sliding into an occupied pew.

"Well," he said to the mirror, conversationally, because terror and politeness had always shared a flat in him. "That's new."

The mirror, naturally, said nothing. It simply smiled back—one beat late.
