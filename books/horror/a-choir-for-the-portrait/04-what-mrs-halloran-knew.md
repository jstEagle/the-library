## 4. What Mrs. Halloran Knew

Mrs. Halloran had been dying for eleven days, and she intended to be difficult about it.

Rosalind admired that about her. There was a school of thought in hospice work—usually espoused by people who had never sat with the dying—that the end should be peaceful, dignified, a gentle sliding into sleep. Mrs. Halloran had apparently never received that memo. She was ninety-one years old, sharp as a dropped scalpel, and she treated her impending death less like a transition and more like a bureaucratic error she intended to appeal in person.

"You're hovering," she said on the evening of her twelfth day, without opening her eyes. "I can hear you hovering. You hover like a moth with a clipboard."

"I'm checking your morphine drip," Rosalind said.

"My morphine drip is fine. My *nurse* is fretting." She opened one eye—the left one, the good one—and fixed him with it. "Sit down, Eastwick. You make the room nervous standing up."

He sat. The room was Room 9, east-facing, which meant it caught the last of the light this time of year, and Mrs. Halloran had arranged her domain with the precision of a woman who had once run a household of nine children and a husband who required managing like a tenth. Photos on the windowsill. A rosary coiled on the blanket like something sleeping. A paperback western, spine cracked at page 143, that she claimed to be reading but hadn't turned a page of in four days.

"Tell me about your mother," she said.

Rosalind blinked. "My mother?"

"You mentioned her. Tuesday. You said she used to sing while she cooked and it drove your father mad because she never sang the same song twice." Mrs. Halloran's voice was thinner than it had been Tuesday—he could hear the difference; he could always hear the difference—but her eyes were as clear as tap water. "I've been thinking about that. Never the same song twice. That's either a very free woman or a very forgetful one."

"Both, probably," Rosalind said, and smiled, because it was easier than the alternative expression.

His mother had been gone twenty-two years. Moved away, the family said. Moved away and then, somehow, stopped writing, stopped calling, stopped existing in any verifiable way, until the word *moved away* wore smooth as a river stone from handling. He had been nineteen. He remembered her kitchen, the smell of onions and dish soap, the way she hummed while she chopped. He did not remember a funeral. He had never once, in twenty-two years, managed to think directly about that absence—it sat in his memory like a tooth missing from a row, and his tongue kept finding the gap and flinching away.

"Hm," said Mrs. Halloran, in the tone of a woman filing something away for later use.

The monitor above her bed traced its green heartbeat, steady as a metronome. Ninety-one years old and her heart still kept excellent time. Rosalind had privately decided that when hers finally stopped, it would probably stop mid-argument.

* * *

It stopped, in fact, at 3:47 in the morning, three nights later, and it did not stop mid-argument. It stopped mid-count.

But he was getting ahead of himself.

On the second night—the night before the end—Rosalind came on shift at ten and found the corridor quiet in the particular way Blackmere House got quiet after midnight, which was not an empty quiet but a *held* one, like a breath taken by a building. He made his rounds. He counted the doors out of habit: twelve between the nurses' station and the stairwell, same as always, same as every night since he'd started six months ago. Twelve doors, forty-two rooms behind them if you counted the sealed east wing, which officially contained only storage and unofficially contained whatever it contained.

Room 9's door stood open a hand's width. Lamplight inside. He tapped twice and let himself in.

Mrs. Halloran was sitting up.

This was wrong in itself—she hadn't had the strength to sit fully upright in days—but what stopped him in the doorway was her face. Her eyes were open and fixed on the corner of the ceiling where it met the wall, and her lips were moving, shaping words too soft to carry.

"Mrs. Halloran?"

Her eyes came down to him slowly, like a searchlight traversing a field. And then she smiled, and it was her smile, wry and crooked, and some knot in his chest loosened half a turn.

"There you are," she said. "I've been asking for you. They keep sending the other one."

"The other one?"

"The young one. Marcus. Sweet boy, but he doesn't listen." She patted the blanket beside her hip, an imperious little gesture. "Sit. I want to talk before my voice goes. It's going, you know. I can feel it packing its bags."

He sat. Up close he could see how the light had changed in her—not dimmed exactly, but *thinned*, the way light thins through a curtain. Her hands lay on the blanket, and they were trembling, but not, he thought, from weakness. From effort. As though she were holding them down.

"I'm going to tell you something," she said, "and you're not going to interrupt me, because I haven't got time for your interruptions, and also because I raised nine children and I can talk over anything."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

"You would. But you won't tonight." She drew a breath that whistled faintly at the bottom of it. "When I was a girl—I was born in 1933, do the arithmetic yourself, I'm tired—we lived near a house outside Kellsbridge. Not this house. Another one. But the same *kind* of house, if you understand me, and I didn't understand it then, God help me, I was seven."

The monitor ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a door eased shut on its pneumatic arm, slow and polite.

"My aunt worked there. Laundry. And she came home one Sunday white as flour and told my mother that the people in that house never aged. Not slowly. *Never.* Same faces, year over year, decade over decade, and the staff turned over and over like a revolving door, because the ones who stayed too long—" Mrs. Halloran's mouth twisted. "Well. They didn't quit, mostly. They just weren't there anymore. And the families didn't ask, because the pay was good and the questions were expensive."

"Mrs. Halloran—"

"I said no interruptions." But her voice had gone quieter, and she leaned toward him, and her hand came off the blanket and closed around his wrist.

Her grip astonished him. Eleven days of dying and her fingers clamped down like a dockworker's, cold and certain, and her eyes were suddenly so close to his own that he could see the whole map of her in them, every year of ninety-one.

"They count backwards," she whispered. "Did you know that? The dying here. Have you heard it?"

Rosalind's throat went dry. Because yes. Yes, he had heard it. Six months of night shifts, and more than once—three times, four—he had sat with a patient in their final hour and heard the counting begin. Ten. Nine. Eight. In voices worn thin or slurred with morphine, yes, but *together*, somehow, in unison, as though each dying person at Blackmere were following the same conductor, keeping the same tempo, counting down to the same zero. He had told himself it was coincidence. He had told himself a lot of things at three in the morning.

"I've heard something like it," he managed.

"*Something like it.*" She almost laughed, and the laugh cost her; her grip tightened instead. "Boy, I've been listening to this house for two weeks from this bed, and I'll tell you what I hear. It's not the patients counting. The patients are being *counted*. Like stock. Like beads on an abacus. Something in these walls is tallying us, and when we reach zero—" Her breath caught. Held. Released. "When we reach zero, we don't go where people go. We go where the house wants us. Do you understand? We get *kept*."

The lamp flickered. Just once, a brownout blink, the kind old buildings produced on principle. When the light steadied, Mrs. Halloran's face had gone grayer, and her grip had weakened by half, and Rosalind realized with a cold slide of dread that he was watching her spend her last strength on this, whatever this was, and that she knew it, and was doing it anyway.

"Don't let them finish the verse," she said.

"What verse? Mrs. Halloran, what verse?"

"The one they're building." Her eyes had drifted back to the ceiling corner, and now he followed them, and saw nothing, of course—plaster, paint, the faint water stain shaped like a comma—but the air in that corner seemed denser than the rest of the room, the way air gets dense before thunder. "There's a hymn. There's always a hymn, in houses like this. The first verse binds them. The second verse—" Her hand spasmed around his wrist. "*Don't let them finish it.* Whatever they offer you. Whoever asks. Your mother would have told you the same, if she—"

She stopped.

She stopped so completely, so abruptly, that for one absurd moment Rosalind thought she had simply fallen asleep mid-sentence, the way the very old sometimes did. Then her eyes came back down from the ceiling corner and found his again, and they were wide, and wet, and full of something he had never seen in a dying person's face before.

Terror.

"She's in it," Mrs. Halloran breathed. "Oh, you poor lamb. She's *in* it. I hear her. Second from the end, she sings second from the end, she has a lovely voice, she—"

The monitor flatlined.

Not gradually. Not with the arrhythmic stutter and scatter that a natural death made, the chaotic signature of a heart losing its argument with time. This was different. The line went flat all at once, a single clean stroke, and it held flat in perfect, unwavering rhythm—and Rosalind, fumbling for the call button with one hand and Mrs. Halloran's fingers with the other, became aware of a sound underneath everything, under the alarm he wasn't hearing yet, under his own pulse in his ears.

A hum.

Distant, low, patient. Rising up through the floor, through the walls, through the bones of the building, and the flat green line on the monitor was tracing itself to that hum the way iron filings trace a magnet, each peak of the distant tone matched by the dead-steady line, note for note, beat for beat, as though the machine had stopped reading Mrs. Halloran's heart and started reading the house's.

Then the alarm caught up, shrilling, and the hum was gone—or swallowed—or finished—and Mrs. Halloran's hand went slack in his.

"Mrs. Halloran." He pressed his fingers to her throat, professional, automatic, finding nothing. "Mrs. Halloran, I'm here, I'm—"

Second from the end, she sings second from the end.

Marcus appeared in the doorway, drawn by the alarm, young and rumpled and kind-eyed. "Rosalind? Time of—"

"3:47," Rosalind heard himself say. His voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing further away than his body was. "Time of death, 3:47 a.m."

* * *

They gave him the paperwork because he asked for it. That was the thing about grief-work in hospice—you learned to ask for the paperwork, because paperwork was a shape your hands could hold when there was nothing else to hold. He sat in the nurses' station at four in the morning filling out the death record in his careful, upright script, and his pen moved, and his mind circled the same three sentences like a bird around a window it couldn't understand:

*Don't let them finish the verse.*

*Your mother would have told you the same.*

*She sings second from the end.*

At 4:15, Vivian Ashe came through the doors in her coat, though she hadn't been called—administrators weren't called for expected deaths at 4 a.m., and everyone at Blackmere died expectedly eventually. She was carrying two paper cups of tea from the all-night place on the corner, steam curling off them, and she set one beside his elbow without a word and stood looking down at the form with an expression of such gentle sorrow that Rosalind felt a stab of shame for every suspicious thought he'd ever had about her.

"She liked you best," Vivian said. "She told me so on Tuesday. She said you listened properly, like the old ones used to."

"Did she." His pen had stopped moving. There was a small ink blot spreading under its nib, patient as mold.

Vivian touched his shoulder, briefly, warmly. "Go home when the shift ends, Rosalind. Grief is heavy, and you've been carrying more than yours lately. I don't know how else to say it." She smiled, and it was a kind smile, and it reached her eyes, and it told him absolutely nothing. "Sleep well. If you can."

She left. The tea cooled untouched. At 4:40, Rosalind looked up from the completed form and found himself staring down the dark length of the corridor, twelve doors deep, toward the east wing, where the storage was, and the silence was, and whatever sang second from the end.

Somewhere far off—so far he might have imagined it, so far it might have been plumbing, wind, the settling of an old house at dawn—a voice was humming.

It was not Mrs. Halloran's voice.

It was not a stranger's.

He knew it the way you know a song you grew up under: not the notes, but the feeling of them. The way his mother used to hum when she thought no one was listening, low and wandering, never the same tune twice.

The humming stopped.

And in the ringing quiet afterward, Rosalind Eastwick sat alone in the nurses' station with a dead woman's warning in his ears and his mother's melody fading in the walls, and understood, with the terrible clarity of 4 a.m., that Mrs. Halloran had not been giving him a riddle.

She had been giving him a head start.
