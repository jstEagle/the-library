## 28. Marcus in the Glass

Rosalind had learned, in the past two weeks, that there were three kinds of silence at Blackmere House.

The first was the ordinary kind—the good kind—the silence of a building settling at three in the morning, of radiators ticking themselves cool, of a night shift with nothing to do but drink bad coffee and count the doors out of habit rather than necessity. He missed that silence. He would have given a great deal for it back.

The second was the listening silence, the one that came when he stopped humming too suddenly and the walls seemed to hold their breath, waiting to see if he'd start again.

The third kind had no name yet. It was the one that arrived after he'd crossed off another name from his mother's letter—after another memory burned away like fog on a window—and left him standing in the corridor feeling lighter and emptier in equal measure, like a book someone had been quietly tearing pages from.

Tonight was a third-kind-of-silence night.

He'd sung the name *Edith Marlowe* an hour ago, alone in his room, door locked, towel rolled along the threshold because salt was getting expensive and he'd already used the kitchen's entire supply once and Mrs. Okonkwo in catering had started giving him looks. The name had come out of him rough and reluctant, and somewhere far below—or perhaps not below at all; the geography of this house had long since stopped making promises—a chair had emptied, and Rosalind had lost the name of the street he'd grown up on.

He knew it had been a street. He knew it had trees on it, probably. That was all. A street-shaped hole where a childhood address used to live.

"Fine," he'd told the empty room, in the brisk voice he used for patients who insisted they weren't in pain. "Fine. Twenty-two names left. You can have my childhood street. I wasn't using it."

He hadn't been, really. Not since the ledgers. Not since Room 12.

Now he stood in the staff bathroom on the second floor, running cold water over his wrists because it was the only thing that made him feel like a body instead of a countdown. The mirror above the sink was one of the old ones, heavy-framed, spotted at the corners with what he chose to believe was tarnish and nothing else. Since the house had begun rearranging itself, most of the mirrors had turned hostile or prophetic or both. This one still mostly showed him himself, which by Blackmere standards counted as a friendly gesture.

He looked tired. He could see that much clearly. There were shadows under his eyes deep enough to lose small objects in, and his hair had achieved a state of permanent alarm. His mother, he thought—then caught himself, because he couldn't actually picture her face anymore, only the fact that she had one, only the certainty of her existence pressing against the hole she'd been cut from.

*You wrote it down,* he reminded himself. *You wrote down everything that matters.*

In his breast pocket, folded into quarters and gone soft at the creases from handling, was the note: *She was small. Dark hair going grey at the temples before its time. She laughed more than she let herself. She smelled like lavender and hand sanitizer. She sang while she worked and pretended she didn't.*

It wasn't enough. It had never been going to be enough. But it was what he had, and tomorrow he would sing four more names and lose whatever came next in line, so he allowed himself ten seconds of looking at his own face in the glass, memorizing that too, just in case.

That was when Marcus knocked.

Not on the door. On the inside of the mirror.

The sound was small and wrong—a soft, hollow tap, like a knuckle against the bottom of a porcelain cup—and it came from the reflection, which until that moment had shown only Rosalind, water running, wrists pale under the tap.

Rosalind went very still. Water kept falling. Somewhere in the walls, faintly, the hum paused—listened—and resumed at a lower register, as if the house itself had leaned in.

"No," Rosalind said aloud, to the mirror, to the house, to the general principle of haunted plumbing fixtures. "No, thank you. Whatever you're selling—"

*Tap. Tap-tap.*

And then the reflection changed.

It wasn't dramatic. That was somehow the worst part. The mirror didn't ripple or shatter or bleed. It simply, politely, showed him something else, the way a well-trained nurse pulls back a sheet: Marcus Reyes, twenty-three years old, sandy-haired, freckled across the nose in a way that had earned him the nickname "Constellation" from exactly one person—Rosalind—sitting cross-legged on the floor of a room that was not this bathroom. The room behind him was white and chairless and had no visible doors. He looked, Rosalind thought with a lurch, *fine*. Healthy. Rested. Better rested than he'd ever looked alive, honestly, and Rosalind hated himself for noticing.

Marcus raised one hand—not a wave, more a *hey, don't freak out* gesture, the universal signal of young men everywhere about to make things worse.

"Hey," Marcus said. His voice came through thin and slightly delayed, like a call on a bad connection. "Okay. Wow. Okay. I practiced this like forty times and now I'm blanking."

"You're dead," Rosalind said. It came out flat and clinical, and he winced. Bedside manner, Whitlock. "I mean—I'm sorry. I'm sorry I didn't warn anyone. I'm sorry I didn't figure it out fast enough. I've said it every night since you vanished and I'll say it again: I am so sorry, Marcus."

"Yeah, no, I know." Marcus rubbed the back of his neck, an old habit, absurdly human. "I heard you. We all hear you, sort of. It's like—okay, imagine everyone in the house is in one big room, right, and the walls are made of, like, tissue paper? And you're talking through them. That's us. That's—" He gestured vaguely at everything, which in a doorless white room was not a wide range of options. "That's the choir, I guess. Except we're not really singing yet. Mostly we're waiting. Waiting's most of it, turns out."

"Waiting for solstice," Rosalind said. "For the second verse. For Vivian's family to harvest you into forever."

Marcus flinched. Actually flinched, shoulders drawing in, and for a moment he looked less like a ghost and more like a kid being told his parents were getting divorced.

"That's the thing," he said. "That's why I—okay. Look. I shouldn't be doing this. Showing myself. They'll know. But I asked, and they let me, because—" He took a breath he did not need and let it out shakily anyway, some habits surviving everything. "Because I begged."

"Marcus—"

"Don't finish it either way." The words came out of him fast and desperate, tumbling over each other. "That's what I came to say. Don't finish it either way. Not Vivian's verse—that one's obvious, that one's the trap, fine, you've got that. But *yours* too. Your counter-ritual. Your release thing." His eyes were wet. Dead boys' eyes, wet in a mirror. "Some cages feel like home, Roz. Some cages are home. You'd be killing us twice."

The water was still running. Rosalind reached over and shut it off, because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking, and the sudden quiet made the mirror-Marcus seem louder, closer.

"They told me you wanted freedom," Rosalind said carefully. "In the dream. They spoke through you. They said they wanted to finish the hymn."

"And then you decided for us!" Marcus's voice cracked upward. "You didn't ask! You found your mom's letter and the ledger names and you started—started *executing* people's afterlives without asking them! Edith Marlowe didn't get a vote! Mr. Aldous's gardener—what was his name—Tomas? He didn't get a vote!"

"He died in 1911," Rosalind said. "His family buried an empty coffin. He has been sitting in a chair in a sealed chapel for a hundred and fourteen years, Marcus."

"He's been *singing* for a hundred and fourteen years." Marcus pressed both palms flat against the inside of the glass, and Rosalind saw with a distant, vertiginous horror that the surface dimpled slightly, like the membrane of a soap bubble. "You think it's a cage because it looks like one from out there. From in here—" He searched for the word, and Rosalind watched him search, watched the dead boy grope for language the way Rosalind himself had lately groped for street names and drug dosages and the color of his own front door. "From in here it's like the last note of a song that never has to end. Nobody's holding us down. There's just nowhere else to *be*, and why would you want anywhere else? Everyone you sing with is right here. It's warm, Roz. It's the warmest place I've ever—"

"Stop." 

"I felt my mom's hand last week." Marcus's voice dropped to almost nothing. "She died when I was six. Cancer. She's not here—but I felt her, or the shape of where she'd be, if she'd died here instead of the hospital. And I thought, God, I thought, *if she'd come here, I'd never have lost her.* Do you understand? This place eats grief and gives it back as company. That's not nothing. That's maybe the least-nothing thing there is."

Rosalind stood very straight, the way he did when a patient's family started screaming at him, the way he did when Vivian smiled at him too long. Grounded. Professional. Terrified underneath, but the terror filed neatly away where it couldn't reach the voice.

"Vivian said something like that once," he said. "*We gave them somewhere to sing forever.* Almost word for word."

Marcus's face closed up. "Don't compare me to her."

"She keeps you fed and housed and tells you it's a gift. She's done it for a hundred and fifty years. She was a child when it started, Marcus—she genuinely believes the cage is kindness. So do you. That's not an insult. That's the whole architecture of the trap. It doesn't need bars if everyone inside is grateful."

"It's not the same."

"It's exactly the same," Rosalind said, gentler now, because anger was a luxury and he could hear, beneath Marcus's defiance, the thin reedy sound of a boy who had died scared and alone in an east-wing corridor and had spent every night since being told, lovingly, patiently, that this was better. "And I think some part of you knows it, or you wouldn't have begged them to let you warn me. You could have stayed quiet. You could have let me walk into solstice blind. Instead you risked—whatever they can do to you—to tell me *don't finish it either way*. That's not the speech of someone at peace, Marcus. That's the speech of someone who wants someone outside the walls to know he's in there."

The silence that followed was the second kind. Listening.

Marcus slid his palms off the glass. For a moment he just sat there, cross-legged in the white nowhere, looking younger than twenty-three, looking sixteen, looking six.

"Some days it feels like home," he said finally, quietly. "Most days. Most days it feels like the best place I've ever been. And then some nights—like tonight—I remember the smell of rain. Actual rain, outside, hitting actual pavement. And I remember I haven't felt cold in two weeks and I would *kill* to feel cold. Even bad cold. Even waiting-for-the-bus-in-February cold." He laughed, a small broken sound. "So I don't know, man. Maybe both things are true. Maybe the cage is home AND a cage. Maybe that's what makes it work."

"Then I'm doing the right thing," Rosalind said.

"You're doing *a* thing."

"I'm doing the right thing." He crouched down, bringing his face level with Marcus's in the glass, close enough to see the freckles, close enough that it hurt. "Listen to me. Captivity isn't kind just because the captive has stopped struggling. That's not mercy, that's exhaustion with better marketing. You deserve rain. You deserve February. You deserve to end, Marcus—an ending, a real one, chosen and witnessed and mourned. Nobody in this house gets to keep you because keeping you is convenient. Not Vivian. Not the founders. Not even you."

"You don't even remember what February feels like," Marcus said. "They've been eating you for days. How many names left? Twelve? Eleven? What have you lost since Tuesday?"

And that—*that*—was the blade slipped in between the ribs, because Marcus was right, was precisely, surgically right: Rosalind could not currently recall whether February felt like anything at all, and he could not remember his nursing instructor's name, and yesterday he had stood in the medication room for four full minutes unable to remember what morphine was *for*, and each loss had felt, at the time, strangely acceptable, reasonable, painless—

Painless. Of course it was painless. The house removed memories the way it did everything else: gently, warmly, with harmony.

"That's how it gets you too," Rosalind said slowly, hearing the thought assemble itself out loud. "That's how it got all of you. Not the dying. The *agreeableness*. You die mid-hymn and the hymn catches you, and it feels so warm and so certain that you never once think to check the door." He stood up. His legs ached. When had his legs started aching? Did legs ache, generally? He'd known that once. He'd write it down. "Thank you, Marcus."

"For telling you not to free us? Real heroic."

"For reminding me that comfortable isn't the same as consensual." He picked up the marker he kept for exactly this purpose—the bathroom mirror had become his scratchpad of record, notes scrawled in the corner where they wouldn't fog—and added a line beneath the tally of remaining names:

*MARCUS REYES — WANTS RAIN. ASK HIM ANYWAY. FREE HIM ANYWAY. FORGIVE ME LATER.*

"You're writing on the mirror about me," Marcus said. "That's so weird."

"This entire house is weird. We're establishing a baseline." Rosalind capped the marker. "Solstice is tomorrow night."

"I know. We know. The chairs are—" Marcus glanced over his shoulder at the white nothing, and something moved out there, beyond him, shapes in the deep field, forty minus however-many remained, patient as furniture. "Everyone's nervous. Excited-nervous. Like before a concert." He looked back up, and his expression did something complicated—hope and dread sharing the same small space. "What are you going to do?"

"What I have to," Rosalind said. "Sing your name last-ish. Sing all of them. Let you go."

"And if we fight you?"

"Then I'll hold the note longer than you can," Rosalind said, and was surprised to find he meant it, and more surprised to find that some steady coal of a thing in his chest believed he could.

Marcus studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he grinned—that crooked Constellation grin, freckles crinkling—and said, "You know what your problem is, Roz? You care about people even when it costs you. Especially then. It's your worst quality."

"It's the only one Vivian couldn't use," Rosalind said. "Go on. Before they notice."

"Yeah." Marcus unfolded himself, rising in the white room, and paused with one hand raised toward the glass—not touching, hovering, the ghost of a high-five nobody would complete. "Hey. If it hurts—if releasing us hurts you worse than losing her did—"

"It will."

"—then be brave and be exact." 

Rosalind's throat closed. Those words weren't Marcus's. Those words were ink on paper in Room 12, written in a hand he could no longer picture but had copied onto the first page of the ledger, and if Marcus knew them, then the choir had heard them too, had heard everything, had been listening through the tissue-paper walls all along—

"Wait," Rosalind said. "How do you know—"

But the mirror was only a mirror again. Rosalind Whitlock, tired, shadow-eyed, marker in hand, and behind his reflection the white room had folded itself away like a letter returned unopened.

He stood there a long time, water dripping off the tap into the silence.

Then, softly, from deep within the walls—from everywhere, from the pipes and the plaster and the space between the seconds—the hum changed key. It rose, brightened, gathered voices. Not counting this time. Rehearsing.

Tomorrow night, the house sang, in its wordless patient way. Tomorrow night, the fortieth chair.

Rosalind dried his hands, checked his pocket for the folded note, and went to find the bone baton before the descendants came looking for him—because if they wanted a conductor at solstice, he intended to arrive having already chosen the key.
