## 13. Love Against Duty

The church was cold in the way old stone buildings get cold after sundown—not a chill so much as a settling, the building exhaling the day's warmth into the dark. Wren had left the sanctuary lamps burning, and their light lay along the pews in long amber bars, and Paloma sat in the third row with their camera bag at their feet and their hands folded like a person in mourning who hadn't yet decided who they were mourning.

Wren came down the aisle from the vestry carrying two mugs of tea she'd made on the ancient hotplate in the church kitchen. She'd stopped asking whether Paloma wanted tea weeks ago; she simply brought it, the way she simply appeared when the mirrors got bad, the way she simply stayed. She set one mug on the pew beside Paloma and slid in next to them, her shoulder a warm line against theirs.

"You've got that look," she said.

"What look."

"The one where you've been reading Edith's journals and you've figured something out and you're deciding whether to say it or swallow it." She blew on her tea. "You swallow it, and then it comes out at two in the morning anyway, so you might as well save us both the insomnia."

Paloma almost smiled. Almost. They picked up the mug and held it without drinking, letting the steam climb past their face, and watched the candlelight shiver in the great dark mirror behind the altar—the one Wren had covered with a cloth years ago and then, since Paloma's arrival, uncovered, because Wren had decided that pretending was worse than watching. The glass was behaving tonight. Mostly. There was a faint softness to the reflection, a delay you could only catch if you moved fast enough, and Paloma had stopped moving fast in front of mirrors the way a person stops testing a bad tooth.

"Voss came by the house today," Paloma said. "While I was transcribing. He didn't knock. He was just in the kitchen when I came down for coffee, sitting at Edith's table like he'd been sitting there for thirty years."

Wren's mug stopped halfway to her mouth.

"He had the score with him. The founders' score. He'd annotated it. He said he'd penciled in my part." Paloma heard their own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when they were recording something they didn't want to feel. "The final movement. He said my voice sits where Edith's sat in '74. He said the tessitura is almost identical, which he found, and I'm quoting him, *gratifying.*"

"He had no right—"

"He had every right, according to him. That's the thing, Wren. He doesn't think he's threatening me. He thinks he's *welcoming* me." Paloma set the mug down. Their hands were steady, which surprised them. "He talked for forty minutes about the bargain. About the prosperity. About how every family in this town owes voices the way they owe taxes, and how the debt doesn't expire, it *accrues.* And then he said—" Paloma stopped. Swallowed. Said it anyway, because Wren was right, it always came out at two in the morning. "He said Edith chose me. Not the lawyers. Not chance. Edith went through the parish records and the county records and the census and she found a grandchild who had never been baptized, never confirmed, never married in a church, never sung in a choir, never been *counted* by anything that counts. A clean voice. An unclaimed voice. She left me the house the way you'd leave someone a key."

The candle flames leaned, all of them at once, though there was no draft.

Wren was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had the particular steadiness Paloma had learned to recognize as the sound of Wren holding something down. "I know."

"You *know.*"

"I found the same thing in my grandfather's papers. The Halloways kept the organ books. Every organist since 1847 signed the same page, and there are notes in the margins—dates, voice counts, who was available, who was *clean.* Edith wrote to my grandfather in 1971 looking for candidates. There's a list. Four names. Three of them died before '74." Wren looked at the covered-uncovered mirror, at the amber light lying in it like something poured. "You were the fourth."

"So this whole time. The records, the insomnia, you sitting with me while the mirrors lag—" Paloma's voice cracked and they hated it, hated that it cracked, hated that everything in this town eventually cracked and sang. "You knew I was chosen. You knew what the inheritance was."

"I knew what it was *supposed* to be." Wren turned to face them fully, and her eyes in the candlelight were fierce and wet and absolutely unafraid, which was the thing about Wren that Paloma had never gotten used to and never would. "I also knew you're not a voice on a list. You're the person who sat with me in the vestry at three in the morning when the organ started playing by itself and held my hand and said, *we'll figure out what it wants.* Nobody held my hand, Paloma. Forty-seven years of Halloways and nobody ever held the organist's hand."

"That's not the point—"

"It's exactly the point." She set her tea down with a small, decisive click. "Because here's what I've been swallowing for three weeks, since the night you sang in the tunnels and the walls answered you. Voss says the ritual needs a clean voice for the final movement. He says the founders designed it that way. But I've read the organ books, and the founders' original notation has a second line under the solo part—a *sub* line, a substitute, written in my great-great-grandmother's hand. The Halloways were always the substitute. That's what the organist *was.* The one who steps in when the soloist can't." She drew a breath. "So I'm telling you what I should have told you the night we found the congregation below. When they come for the final movement—when Voss brings you down into the hill and puts the score in front of you—I'm going to be there, and I'm going to take your place, and you are going to walk out of that tunnel and keep walking and not look back."

The silence after that was enormous. Somewhere in the walls of the church, pipes ticked as they cooled, and Paloma thought: *even the building is holding its breath.*

"No," Paloma said.

"Paloma—"

"No." They stood, and the pew creaked, and their voice came out louder than they'd meant, ringing off the stone. "You don't get to do that. You don't get to hand me absolution like a hymnal. Your family played the organ for a hundred and seventy years, yes—played it for a ritual built on a massacre, played it while the founders drowned a hundred and twelve people in a quarry and then *billed the town for the music.* That debt is real. I'm not saying it isn't. But it's not a debt you pay by dying in the dark so that I can live in the light. That's just the same arithmetic Voss uses. Sacrifice in, prosperity out. One voice for the town. You'd be finishing his equation, not breaking it."

"And what's your plan?" Wren was standing too now, and they faced each other down the aisle of amber light like two people on opposite sides of something that wasn't really a fight. "Refuse? He'll take you anyway. I've seen what the congregation does when the humming gets loud. They don't decide to sing, Paloma. They just open their mouths. You think Voss can't put you in front of the score and let the tunnels do the rest? You sang once. The walls know your voice now. You're already *in* the music."

"Then I'll change the music."

"With what? Edith's whisper on a forty-year-old tape? *Don't finish it, rename it*—rename it how, with what, sung by whom? You have half an answer and a bag of cameras, and Voss has a town." Wren's voice broke on the last word, and she pressed her hand flat against her sternum as if holding the break closed. "I have watched this town eat people my whole life. Quietly. Politely. With casseroles and committee meetings. My mother sang shifts in the tunnels until her voice gave out and they thanked her and gave her a plaque. I am not going to watch it eat you."

"And I am not going to watch it eat *you.*" Paloma stepped closer, off the amber bar into the shadow between them, close enough to see the candlelight doubled in Wren's eyes. "You want to trade yourself for me because it's tidy. Because it's duty. Because your name is on the substitute line in a book written by people who thought a human life was a line item. But I didn't come here to be tidy. I came here with a camera and a lawyer's letter, and everything I've learned since is that the tidy thing is the trap. Edith tried to warn me and they used her voice for it. The founders wrote the score and the score is a cage. Every single person who did the *correct* thing in this town made the thing below stronger. So no. You don't get to be the substitute. You don't get to be the sacrifice. You don't get to make my survival a debt I owe your ghost."

"Then make it something else," Wren whispered.

And Paloma, who had spent their whole life turning feeling into footage, who had built an armor of lenses and logs and half-beat-lagged echoes, found that they had nothing to record and nothing to say and no method for this at all. Wren's face was an inch away. Her breath was tea and cold air and courage. Behind them, the great mirror held the sanctuary in its soft, delayed glass, and in that glass—Paloma saw it without turning, saw it in the periphery where the lag lived—two figures stood closer than they stood, and their hands were already joined.

They looked down. Their hands hung apart at their sides, trembling slightly, not touching.

They looked back at the mirror. In the glass, Wren's reflection lifted its hand, and Paloma's reflection met it, fingers lacing, one full second before either of them moved.

"The mirror's ahead of us now," Paloma said. Their voice had gone very quiet. "It's not lagging. It's *leading.*"

"Then let it," Wren said. "For once. Let something in this town be first."

Paloma turned from the glass and took Wren's face in both hands—Wren, who had tended this haunted church her whole life, who had covered the mirror and uncovered it, who knew every verse of the thing below and sang anyway—and kissed her, and the kiss was not gentle. It was fierce the way a held note is fierce, the way a voice pushed against stone is fierce, all the tenderness arriving at once because there was no time to spend it slowly. Wren made a small sound against their mouth and her hands fisted in their shirt and the candles leaned again, all of them, toward the two of them, as if the flame itself were listening.

When they broke apart, foreheads together, breathing the same warm air, Paloma looked past Wren's shoulder at the mirror.

In the glass, the two of them were still kissing. A full second behind now, or ahead—Paloma couldn't tell anymore, and found they didn't care. The reflections parted slowly, and the reflected Wren pressed her forehead to the reflected Paloma's, and then the reflected Paloma turned its head, deliberately, and looked directly out of the mirror at the real room. At Paloma. It raised one hand—not waving. Pointing. Past them, down the aisle, toward the vestry, toward the organ, toward the floor beneath the organ where the tunnels began.

Its mouth moved. No sound came. But Paloma had spent three weeks learning to read silent singing in lagging glass, and the shape of the words was unmistakable, and their blood went cold and hot at the same time.

*It knows. It's already singing.*

"What?" Wren breathed. "Paloma, what did it—what did you see?"

"It pointed down." Paloma took Wren's hand—really took it, in the real world, in the real time—and felt her grip close like a vow. "The reflection isn't warning us. It's *counting us in.*"

Far beneath the church, beneath the vestry floor, beneath the scored and hand-cut dark, the humming began—low, patient, familiar, and for the first time, it was in two parts. Harmony. The thing below had found the note Wren's family had played for a hundred and seventy years, and it was holding it, and it was waiting for them to come down and finish the phrase.
