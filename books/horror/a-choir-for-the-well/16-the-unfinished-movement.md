## 16. The Unfinished Movement

The silence lasted eleven days.

Paloma counted them the way a convalescent counts pain-free hours—gratefully, and with a growing suspicion that gratitude was premature. The mirrors kept time. The tunnels under the cellar kept quiet. The congregation above them, Voss reported through a note left in the mailbox like a neighbor returning a casserole dish, had been released from their shifts, and the town of Harrow's Bend had thrown something almost like a festival: bunting on the feed store, a brass band playing slightly flat in the square, children chalk-drawing suns on the sidewalk. Paloma had watched from the upstairs window with a mug of tea going cold in their hands and felt the specific loneliness of the only sober person at a party that hasn't started yet.

*The silence is listening,* they wrote in the journal, and then, hating their own drama, crossed it out. Then wrote it again.

Wren came over most evenings. They catalogued Edith's tapes together, labeled the reel-to-reel boxes in Wren's small copperplate hand, ate dinner off the one table in the kitchen that didn't wobble. On the eleventh night, Wren fell asleep on the parlor sofa with a folder of quarry ledgers open on her chest, and Paloma sat across from her with the camera in their lap, not filming, just holding it, the way you hold a rosary you no longer believe in but can't put down.

That was when the reel-to-reel in the corner switched itself on.

Paloma had not touched it in days. Its plug sat coiled on the floor beside it—Paloma checked, twice, in the first terrible seconds, crawling across the rug with their heart slamming—unplugged, the cord's bare tines lying on the floorboards like a dead insect's legs. And still the reels turned. Still the tape ran. The VU needle rose and trembled, rose and trembled, tracing a shape Paloma recognized in their body before their mind caught up: the shape of a voice singing.

They pressed record on the camera. It was reflex, prayer, armor, all of it at once. The red light came on. And in the dark glass of the lens, Paloma saw the parlor behind them—Wren asleep, the lamp, the rain starting against the windows—saw it all one full second *ahead* of the room itself.

In the reflection, the reel-to-reel was already playing. It had been playing, the reflection insisted, for some time.

* * *

The voice on the tape was Edith's.

Paloma knew it now the way they knew their own pulse. They had spent weeks inside that voice—its dry, unsentimental alto, the slight flattening on sustained notes, the way she counted the choir in with a *one, two* that sounded more like a warning than a courtesy. It was Edith singing the final movement of the founders' score, the one that had broken off in 1974 mid-phrase, the movement that ended, on the only surviving recording, in a scream and then a whisper: *don't finish it—rename it.*

Except now the recording didn't break off.

The phrase that had been amputated for fifty years grew back. Paloma sat on the floor with their arms around their knees and listened to a dead woman's voice travel through the exact bars where her scream had lived, and the voice did not scream. It sang. Smoothly. Confidently. Wrong. There was something in the timbre, some grain that had been sanded away, some hesitation that had been filled in, and Paloma understood with a lurch of nausea that this was not Edith's voice remembering how the song went. This was something *learning* Edith's voice, the way water learns the shape of a cup, and it had learned it from the tapes. From Paloma's tapes. From the archive Paloma had restored, digitized, catalogued, and left sitting in a house with a hole in its cellar that breathed.

"Oh," Paloma said aloud, to the empty parlor, to the sleeping woman, to nobody. "Oh, no. It's been *reading*."

Every hour of documentation. Every interview with the gray-faced congregation. Every field recording of the tunnels humming, every close-miked capture of the mirrors lagging, every whispered note-to-self at three in the morning—*day fourteen, lag approximately one second, subjectively longer*—all of it had gone down through the floor like rain through rotten wood. Paloma had thought they were building a case. They had been building a *choir*. They had spent months assembling, in perfect order, perfectly labeled, the complete vocal materials of the 1974 ritual, and then they had left the score open on the stand.

The phrase ended. On the tape, Edith's stolen voice held the final note—a note Paloma had never heard anyone reach, a note that seemed to bend the air of the parlor into a slightly different shape, so that the lamp's light fell wrong and Wren's sleeping face looked, for one instant, like a photograph of Wren rather than Wren—and then released it.

Every mirror in the house sighed.

Paloma felt it in their teeth. Down the hall, the tall mirror in the east wing, the one that had been lagging five seconds, the one whose reflections had begun mouthing warnings—*go back, go back, below, below*—went silent and still and *attentive*, the way a room goes attentive when someone stops talking.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere—from the walls, from the floorboards, from the drain in the kitchen sink, from the space between the window glass and the storm glass—the humming began. And this time it wasn't humming. This time it was the final movement, sung in a hundred voices that were almost voices, and Paloma recognized, with a horror that felt almost like recognition, every single one of them.

* * *

They were all there. That was the thing Paloma could not stop cataloguing, even as the house began to come apart around them—the documentarian's reflex firing to the very end, the archivist's compulsion to label the fire as it burned. The choir below was built from Paloma's own archive, and it was thorough.

Edith's alto, of course. But also Voss's baritone, lifted from the interview Paloma had recorded in the tunnel mouth—Voss, who had sung for them so patiently, demonstrating the founders' phrasing, never dreaming or perhaps always dreaming that he was being transcribed. The gray sopranos of the congregation women, from the session in the church vestry. The brass band from the festival, recorded accidentally through the open window. A child's voice from the square, laughing, then counting a chalk-game—*one, two, three, four*—and the thing below used the counting as a downbeat, because it had learned that too, it had learned everything, it had listened to four hundred hours of Paloma's careful, loving, *incriminating* attention.

And underneath all of it, holding the bass line like a hand around the throat of the song, the drowned singers of the quarry. The one hundred and twelve. Singing the founders' music. Singing the music of the men who had drowned them, in voices that had been stolen twice—once in 1846, once this week—and there was something in the way they sang, something flattened and sanded and *learned*, that made Paloma cover their mouth with both hands.

The house began to fold.

Not dramatically, not at first. The hallway to the east wing simply became eleven steps longer on the way out than it had been on the way in. The staircase developed a landing that had never been there, a small square of window looking out not on the lawn but on a hillside under a colorless sky, and Paloma made the mistake of looking at it and saw, far below, a flooded quarry catching light like a coin at the bottom of a well. Doors opened onto rooms rotated a few degrees from true, so that the furniture sat at a subtle, maddening cant, like a picture hung by someone trembling. The house was not collapsing. The house was *refolding along its original creases*—returning, Paloma realized, to the shape it had held in 1974, the shape it had held on the night the ritual broke, because the thing below had finally recovered the lost page of its own instructions and was now, patiently, obediently, *finishing the performance*.

And the reflections went first.

Paloma stood in the hallway holding the camera and watched their own reflection raise the camera a full two seconds before their arms moved. Then three. The lag had inverted. The mirror-world was no longer trailing reality; it was *leading* it, the way a conductor's hand precedes the note, and Paloma understood that what they were seeing in the glass was not a prediction. It was a rehearsal. The reflection was practicing them. Somewhere on the other side of the silver, something was learning Paloma Dunmore the way it had learned Edith Dunmore, note by note, gesture by gesture, and when it had the part down cold it would no longer need the original at all.

In the glass, their reflection's mouth moved. No sound came through. But Paloma had spent a lifetime reading lips through long lenses, and the message was plain, and it was not *go back*. It was not a warning at all.

The reflection said: *thank you.*

* * *

Wren woke to the sound of the parlor singing in her own voice.

She came down the hall barefoot, and Paloma caught her at the corner and put a hand over her mouth, and for a moment they stood pressed together in the dark, listening to Wren Halloway sing a line of the founders' score in a recording neither of them had ever made. There had been no session in which Wren sang. There had been no tape. And yet here it was in the choir, clear as a bell, and Paloma's mind skittered away from the implication and then forced itself back: the organ. Wren's church organ, playing itself at night for weeks. The thing below had been *taking lessons*. Everything in this town that made sound had been, for years, a microphone.

"It's doing it," Paloma whispered against Wren's hair. "It's finishing it. The 1974 movement. It found the ending."

"How?" Wren's whisper was steady, which was how Paloma knew she was more frightened than she had ever been. Wren's fear always came out as precision. "The score's incomplete. Edith made sure. She cut the pages out herself, I've seen the stubs—"

"It didn't need the pages." Paloma's throat was dry. "It had my tapes. It had—" and here the guilt arrived, finally, in full, the way floodwater arrives, all at once and from every direction, "—it had *me*. Four hundred hours, Wren. I documented everything. I thought—God, I thought I was *protecting* it. The archive. I thought if I recorded it all, it couldn't be lost, couldn't be denied, couldn't happen again without a witness." They laughed, a small broken sound. "I built it a choir loft. I labeled the shelves."

Wren took their face in both hands. Her palms were cold and her eyes were ferocious. "Listen to me. Edith spent her whole life making sure the ending stayed lost. You didn't lose it. You *found* her, you found the truth, you found the names—that's not the same thing, do you hear me? That's not the same thing at all."

But it felt the same. It felt exactly the same. Paloma looked past Wren's shoulder to the hallway mirror, where their two reflections stood embracing two seconds before their bodies did, and in the glass, behind the reflected versions of them, the reflected hallway stretched away into a reflected dark, and in that dark stood rows of reflected faces, patient, gray, attentive, waiting for the downbeat.

The whole house shuddered. Somewhere below, the final movement reached the bar Edith had never sung, the bar that had cost her her scream, and this time nothing interrupted it. The note came up through the floor like water through a cracked foundation, and every mirror in Dunmore House filled at once with the congregation, and every door in the east wing unlocked itself, all together, with a sound like a held breath released.

Paloma took Wren's hand.

They were done documenting. Whatever happened next, it would not be witnessed from behind a lens. The archive had done its damage; the only thing left that the thing below had never heard, the only voice it had never stolen because Paloma had never been fool enough to record it, was whatever they were about to say and do next, in the flesh, off the tape, unwitnessed and therefore *free*.

"The ledgers," Paloma said. "The names. We take everything—the ledgers, the quarry rolls, the parish death book. Every one of the hundred and twelve."

Wren was already moving, already pulling the folder from under the sleeping bag she'd never gone back to. "And the tapes?"

Paloma looked at the reel-to-reel, still turning, still singing with a dead woman's voice, still running down its stolen spool.

"We bring those too," they said. "Not to play. To *take back*."

Above them the house groaned, folding another degree toward 1974, and the stairs down to the cellar appeared where the coat closet had been, wide open, breathing warm air, lit from below by a light that no bulb in the house was producing.

Something at the bottom of the hill had finished rehearsing.

It was ready for the performance—and it had left the door open, like a choirmaster, like a host, like a thing that believed its soloist had finally, at long last, arrived.
