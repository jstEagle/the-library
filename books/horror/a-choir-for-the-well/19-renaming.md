## 19. Renaming

The portable player lay on the wet stone between them, its cassette door cracked, the founders' score still unspooling backwards into the dark like a thread being pulled out of a wound. Voss was on his knees at the edge of the water, and he was not silent anymore. That was the thing Paloma would remember afterward—not the violence, not the cold, but how small his voice had become once the music stopped obeying him.

"You don't know what you're doing," he said. "You don't know what it *wants*."

"I think I do," Paloma said. "I think it wants what anyone wants when they've been buried under someone else's name for a hundred and seventy-eight years."

The chamber breathed around them. The flooded quarry—*the well*, Edith's journals called it, always lowercase, always with a kind of hushed tenderness—stretched out beneath the hill in a black oval of water so still it held their reflections perfectly, without lag, without lies. For the first time since Paloma had arrived at Dunmore House, the mirrors were honest. The water showed them exactly as they were: two people standing at the edge of something enormous, one of them holding a ledger wrapped in plastic against her chest, the other holding nothing at all except a throat full of names.

Wren's hands were shaking. She had played the score backwards until the tape ran out, and now she stood close enough that Paloma could feel the tremor coming off her like heat off a road in summer.

"Tell me again," Wren whispered. "Tell me you have them all."

"One hundred and twelve." Paloma's voice came out steadier than they felt. "Cross-referenced three times. The quarry company's hiring ledgers, the parish burial rolls—what's left of them—and Edith's transcriptions. She got to forty-one before—" They didn't finish. The scream on the 1974 tape finished it for them, the way it always did, unbidden, behind their eyes.

"And if you're wrong? If even one is wrong?"

"Then I'll sing it again," Paloma said. "Until it's right."

Below them—or perhaps not below; the water gave no depth, no bottom, only an infinite downward reflection—the humming changed. It had been rising through the stone since they'd entered the tunnels, that patient, hungry vibration Paloma had first felt through the floorboards of a house they'd inherited from a stranger. But hunger had a sound, and Paloma knew it now the way you come to know any sound you've lived inside long enough. This was different. This was thinner. Almost like waiting.

Almost like listening.

Voss made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been grief. "They tried this," he said. "In '74. Edith tried *exactly* this. You've heard the tape. You know how it ended."

"She didn't fail because she was wrong," Paloma said. "She failed because she was alone. Because your congregation was singing the founders' score over her. Because the ritual doesn't allow for interruption—it only allows for completion or silence." They looked down at the water, at their own face looking back, punctual at last. "I'm not interrupting anything, Mr. Voss. I'm starting something new. And you're going to be quiet while I do it, or Wren is going to play that tape again, and we both know what it does to you now."

He shut his mouth. His eyes, though—his eyes went on singing the old score, silently, furiously, all the way to the end of whatever happened next.

Paloma unwrapped the ledger.

It was a quarry daybook, 1846, water-stained and soft-cornered, its columns of wages and tonnage annotated in a clerk's cramped hand. But in the margins, in Edith Dunmore's fountain-pen blue, were the names she'd recovered—crossed-checked, corrected, some reconstructed from initials and payroll fragments and a single funeral sermon preserved in the church archive where Wren's family had kept it for six generations without knowing why. One hundred and twelve people who had gone down into the quarry on the fourth of November, 1846, to sing the stone loose, because the founders had promised them the work would be safe, because the founders had already decided the work would not be safe, because a chorus of the drowned was worth more than a chorus of the living—cheaper, too, and permanent.

The founders hadn't buried a demon. They'd buried a choir. And then they'd spent seven generations teaching it to forget it had ever been one.

Paloma knelt at the water's edge. The stone was cold through their jeans; the air tasted of iron and old hymns. Behind them, Wren settled onto the bench of a fallen pillar, her fingers resting on nothing, playing the shape of accompaniment out of habit, out of love, out of the Halloway inheritance of hands that couldn't help keeping time.

"Don't harmonize," Paloma said softly. "Not yet. Just listen with me."

And then they began.

---

The first name was Abiah Frost.

Paloma sang it plainly, without ornament, the way you'd read a name off a headstone you'd driven hours to find. Just the name. Just the fact of it, given to the dark.

The humming stopped.

Not faded—stopped, the way a room stops when a door opens, the whole pressure of it shifting, attentive. The surface of the well shivered though nothing touched it, rings spreading outward from the center as if something far below had turned over in its sleep.

"*Abiah Frost,*" Paloma sang again, and added what the ledger gave them, because a name alone is a label and a name with a life is a person: "*Cut stone eleven years. Paid four dollars the week. Had a brother, Josiah, who signed the same book two lines down.*"

Two lines down. Paloma found him. "*Josiah Frost.*"

The water rang—a single low tone, like a finger circling the rim of a glass, and Paloma felt it in their sternum, in their teeth, in the fillings of their jaw. Not the hungry hum. Something rawer. A sound with no practice in it, the way a voice cracks when it hasn't been used for crying in a very long time.

They kept going.

"*Mercy Aldous. Mercy Aldous.*" The clerk's ledger said *M. Aldous*, but Edith had found the full name in the sermon, and Paloma gave it whole. "*Sang alto, the sermon says. Sang the stone loose for nine years before the ninth of November.*" Paloma had learned, in the weeks of restoration, to hear the quarry singers in the founders' score—the parts written for voices that were never paid, never credited, folded into the ritual like stolen goods laundered into respectability. Every time they named one, they could feel the score flinch somewhere above them, in the house, in the town, in the mirrors that had lagged and lied for so long.

Names came slower than breath. Some of the ledger entries were initials only, and Paloma sang the initials and the uncertainty together—"*T. Ellery, whose name the paper kept and the stone took the rest of*"—and the water accepted even that, ringing each fragment like a bell that understood it was being rung by someone reading by candlelight through a cracked window.

The cold deepened. Their lips went numb. They sang through it.

Somewhere behind them, Voss had begun to weep—not loudly, not dramatically, just the steady leaking of a man watching the foundation of his life dissolve into something truer and more terrible. He had spent fifty years believing the thing below was a debt. He had built his sermons on it, raised his congregation on it, fed it children's sleep and livestock's sanity and finally, tonight, intended to feed it a clean voice. And here was Paloma Dunmore, kneeling in the wet, telling it its own name one syllable at a time, and the debt was turning out to be a grave, and the grave was turning out to be full of people, and the people were turning out to be *listening*.

"*Huldah Peck.*" Ring. "*Obadiah Peck.*" Ring, closer, overlapping, the tones beginning to braid. "*Amos Trask, who owed the company store eleven dollars the week he died, and the company collected it anyway.*"

That one drew a sound out of the water that was almost a word.

Paloma's eyes were burning. They didn't wipe them; their hands were holding the ledger open, and the ledger was holy now, and you do not let go of the holy thing. They thought of every recording they had ever made—the compulsive, armored documenting, the half-beat-wrong echoes, the prayer they hadn't known they were praying. All of it had been rehearsal for this: the understanding that to record a thing is to refuse to let it be erased, and that refusal, done rightly, is not possession. It's attention. It's love with the camera left running.

Wren began to hum. Low, under the names, not the founders' score—something older, something her grandmother had sung over dishwater, a shape without words that fit itself under each name like a cupped palm under falling water. Paloma didn't tell her to stop. It wasn't harmony in the ritual sense. It was company.

The names passed thirty. Forty. Fifty.

The water was no longer still. Rings crossed rings; tones layered on tones; and down in the black, reflections were surfacing that matched no one standing on the bank—faces in the water, dozens of them, gray and gentle, mouths open not in screaming but in the particular slack wonder of people hearing their own names spoken aloud for the first time in a hundred and seventy-eight years.

Paloma sang faster, afraid the wonder would curdle, afraid the hunger would return and swallow the mourning mid-birth. "*Eunice Larkin. Silas Larkin. Tabitha Greeley. Cornelius Greeley.*" Families, they realized—whole families had gone down together, because the quarry hired by household, because poverty is generational, because the founders had known that grief compounds like interest too. "*Deborah Wick. Ruth Wick. Esther Wick, aged fourteen.*"

At *Esther Wick, aged fourteen*, the well went utterly, catastrophically quiet—one full second of silence so complete that Paloma heard their own heart, heard Wren's breath catch, heard, distantly, the drip of the tunnel water counting time the way it had counted it since before the house was built.

Then the water answered.

Not a ring. A chord. A hundred and twelve voices' worth of chord, rising through the stone and out of the water and into the ribs of the hill itself, and it was not beautiful—that was the terrible mercy of it. It was ragged and cracked and unschooled and human, alto and bass and a child's thin soprano braided through the middle, and it was the ugliest, truest sound Paloma had ever heard, and they wept openly into it and kept singing, because the list was not done, and witness does not stop at the point of comfort.

Sixty. Seventy. Eighty.

Their voice was failing. Wren's humming rose to carry the space between names. The faces in the water had begun to move—not toward the surface, not threateningly, but among themselves, turning to one another, finding each other. Paloma watched a woman's reflection reach across the black and take the hand of a man's reflection, and thought: *Abiah and Josiah. The brothers, two lines down.* Recognition was doing what recognition does. It was reassembling the world.

Ninety. A hundred.

"*Solomon Prynn,*" Paloma sang, and their voice broke on the second syllable, and the water caught the broken piece and rang it anyway, gently, as if to say *that counts, that counts too.*

One hundred and eleven.

Paloma stared at the page. The last line of Edith's transcription was smudged—a water stain, decades old, eating the ink—and beneath it, in Edith's smaller, later hand: *one name lost. I am sorry. I am so sorry.*

The chamber waited. The chord hung unresolved, a hundred and eleven voices holding the interval that wanted its final note, and Paloma understood with a clarity that felt like falling that this was the trap, this was the exact shape of the founders' design: an incomplete list is an incomplete ritual, and incompleteness accrues, and the hunger lives in the gap.

Edith had gotten here. In 1974, kneeling where Paloma knelt, Edith had gotten here, to the gap, and the gap had taken her.

But Edith had also left the whisper on the tape. *Don't finish it—rename it.*

And Paloma had spent three months learning what documentation meant.

They looked up at Wren. "The sermon," Paloma said. "The funeral sermon. Read me the part about the unnamed."

Wren's hands stilled. Her eyes went wide, then wet, then certain. She pulled the photocopied pages from her coat with fingers that shook and didn't fumble, and she read: "*And one soul the Lord keeps in His own ledger, whose name was worn from the world's, whom we commit to the mercy that needs no spelling.*"

"No," Paloma said. "That's the church's version. That's a name being erased politely." They closed their eyes. Opened them. "The ledger. The original. The clerk wrote everyone twice—wages and burial. If the burial entry is stained, the wage entry might—"

Wren was already flipping to the front of the daybook, to October 1846, to the weekly payroll, running her finger down the column of the last crew. Down. Down. Stopping.

Her lips moved. No sound came out. Then she looked up at Paloma and said, in a voice scraped nearly to nothing:

"*Comfort.* Just Comfort. No surname recorded. The clerk wrote 'Comfort, quarry.' That's all."

"That's enough," Paloma said. "That's more than enough. That's everything."

They leaned over the water, over the hundred and eleven faces, over the hanging chord, and they sang the last name the only way it had ever been written down—honestly, incompletely, with the gap itself made part of the song:

"*Comfort. Comfort, of the quarry. Whose name the paper kept short and the stone kept long. Comfort—whoever you were, whatever you carried, you are counted. You were always counted. We just weren't listening.*"

The chord resolved.

There was no other word for it. A hundred and twelve voices found the note that had been missing since 1974—since 1846, really, since the moment the founders blew the quarry dam and called the drowning a sacrifice and called the sacrifice a foundation—and the resolution moved through the chamber like dawn moving through a valley, not arriving so much as revealing what had been there all along. The hunger didn't die. Hunger was never the right word. What died was the *misnaming*. What filled the well was not sound, exactly, though it was made of sound—it was mourning, given room, given witnesses, given time. Grief with a floor under it. Sorrow that had been starving because sorrow, unattended, starves into appetite.

The faces in the water did not vanish. They simply became still, and peaceful, and *theirs*—no longer pressed up against the underside of the town like mouths against a membrane, but resting in their own place, in their own names, in the enormous patience of the properly mourned.

Then, slowly, the sound withdrew. Not silenced—withdrawn, the way a congregation sits down after the benediction. The water smoothed. The cold lifted by degrees, like a hand unclenching.

And the hill above them—the house, the tunnels, the whole scored and humming architecture of a hundred and seventy-eight years of forced song—went quiet.

Not empty. Quiet. There was a difference, and Paloma felt it in their bones, and would spend the rest of their life learning its dimensions.

---

For a long time, none of them moved.

Wren came down from the pillar and knelt beside Paloma and put her arms around them, and Paloma let the ledger rest closed on their knees and put their forehead against her shoulder, and the two of them stayed like that, kneeling at the edge of a well that was finally, truly, only a well.

Behind them, Voss rose unsteadily to his feet. When Paloma looked up, they expected fury, or collapse, or the beginning of some last desperate violence.

What they saw was worse. What they saw was a man listening—to the silence, to the absence of the hum that had structured his entire life—and discovering that underneath the silence there was nothing holding him up. Fifty years of duty, and the duty had been a lie told by dead men to keep dead men buried. His mouth opened. Nothing came out. Whatever sermon he had left, it had been written for a congregation that no longer existed.

He turned, without a word, and walked deeper into the tunnel. Toward the sealed archway. Toward the dark the founders had built.

Neither of them stopped him. Neither of them knew yet whether they should have.

"Paloma." Wren's voice was hoarse. Her thumb traced circles against their shoulder, absent, anchoring. "What happens now?"

Paloma looked down at the ledger, at Edith's blue-inked names, at the smudge where the last one had almost been lost forever. They thought about the reel-to-reel in the cellar. About the tape they would make. About the difference between a prayer and a memory, and which one you leave behind for strangers.

Above them, faintly, through a hundred feet of limestone, the mirrors of Dunmore House were catching the first gray light of morning—and reflecting it, for the first time, exactly on time.

"We go home," Paloma said. "And then we figure out what we owe the living."

They gathered the equipment in silence. And as they turned up the tunnel toward the long climb back, the portable player—dead, its tape spent, its batteries corroded years ago—clicked once, softly, all by itself.

And began, very quietly, to play.
