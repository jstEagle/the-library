## 20. Aftermath in One Second

The silence came all at once, the way a held breath is released.

Paloma felt it in their chest first—a pressure lifting that they hadn't known was there until it was gone, like a hand uncurling from behind the sternum. They were still standing at the edge of the flooded quarry chamber, Wren's shoulder pressed against theirs, both of them soaked to the ribs with well-water and the residue of a hundred and twelve names still drying on their tongue. The last name had been *Mercy Aldous*, aged nine, and when Paloma had sung it the water below had gone so still it looked like poured glass.

Then nothing. Not the humming. Not the lag. Not the vast patient attention that had pressed against the underside of Dunmore Hill for a hundred and seventy-seven years.

Just water. Just stone. Just the two of them breathing hard in the dark, and somewhere above, faint as memory, the sound of Mr. Voss's footsteps climbing away into the tunnels he had served his whole life.

Wren's hand found Paloma's in the dark. Her fingers were cold and shaking, and she gripped them like something she'd been told she couldn't keep.

"Is it over?" she whispered. And then, because neither of them could bear the weight of that question unanswered, she said the other thing: "Look."

She turned her phone's flashlight toward the black water of the quarry pool, where the surface had always been more mirror than water—where, in the worst hours, the reflection had moved before the body did, had sung before the mouth opened, had shown them rooms that did not exist and faces that had been dead since 1846.

The water reflected them exactly. Two exhausted people, drenched and filthy, lit by a dying phone screen. Nothing else. No congregation of the drowned arrayed behind them. No second Wren mouthing warnings half a beat ahead. No Edith.

When Paloma lifted their hand, the reflection lifted its hand *with* them. In time. Perfectly, ordinarily, humanly in time.

Paloma started to laugh, and it came out wrong—cracked and wet—and then they were crying instead, and Wren was holding them, and the water held only what it should: two small figures in an enormous dark, finally alone.

* * *

Climbing out took longer than climbing down. The tunnels seemed different now—not friendlier, exactly, but emptied of intent. The hymn lyrics scored into the walls were just words again, generations of handwriting in candle smoke and chalk and blood-ruddied ochre, a record of fear rather than a living score. Paloma ran their fingers along one verse as they passed—*What shall we render unto the hill*—and felt nothing answer. The stone was cool. Only cool.

They passed the congregation in the third antechamber, and this was the part Paloma would never be able to explain properly afterward, not to the town, not to themselves, not to the camera they had, out of old habit, left running in their coat pocket.

The singers were waking up.

There were nineteen of them, arranged on the stone benches their grandparents' grandparents had built, and they looked—there was no kind way to say it—*old*. Mrs. Tansy Culver, who had sung alto in the Thursday shifts for forty years and whose face had been smooth as wax every time Paloma saw her in the tunnels, now had lines carved deep around her mouth and eyes, decades of them surfacing all at once like drowned bodies rising. Harold Pike sat with his hands open in his lap, staring at his own knuckles as though counting them. The youngest of them, a boy of maybe seventeen named Eli Brandt whom Paloma had never seen above ground, was weeping without any expression at all, tears simply falling off his chin onto the stone.

None of them screamed. None of them raged. That was the horror of it, Paloma thought—the gentleness of the waking. They blinked at the dark like people surfacing from a long afternoon nap, and one by one they began, without being told, to stand.

Mrs. Culver saw Paloma and Wren in the passageway and inclined her head, formal as church.

"It's quiet," she said. Her voice was ruined, sanded down to gravel by years of use it had never registered. "I can't hear it anymore. I didn't know I could hear it until—" She stopped. She looked at her hands. "Forty years," she said. "I sang forty years of Thursdays. My mother sang before me." A pause. "We thought we were keeping everyone safe."

"You were keeping something safe," Wren said quietly. "It just wasn't everyone."

Mrs. Culver absorbed that the way stone absorbs rain—slowly, without visible change, but changed. Then she nodded once and began helping Harold Pike to his feet, and the congregation of Dunmore Hill walked out of the earth together, gray-faced and bent and free, blinking in the daylight like creatures from a deeper season.

* * *

Above ground, the town had felt it too.

Paloma understood this only gradually, in the days that followed, because the first day they slept fourteen hours in a bed at Dunmore House that no longer breathed, in a room where the mirror showed them only themselves, delayed by nothing, and woke feeling as though they'd been dredged up from somewhere very deep. When they finally came downstairs, Wren was sitting at Edith's kitchen table with the radio on, and the radio was playing static, and Wren was crying and smiling at the same time.

"The organ stopped," she said. "At St. Brannock's. It played itself every night for—I don't even know how long, since before my grandmother died, we just—we called it the night practice and locked the doors and didn't talk about it." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Last night it stopped mid-hymn. Just—stopped. And the whole town heard it stop. You could hear people coming out of their houses. Nobody said anything. We all just stood in the street listening to nothing."

The reports accumulated over the following week, each one small, each one impossible, each one confirming what Paloma already knew in their bones. The sinkholes along Quarry Road had stopped widening; surveyors found the subsidence stable for the first time in recorded memory. The Hollis farm's cattle stood facing uphill again, grazing like ordinary stupid blessed animals. The children who had been sleepwalking toward the hill—the Brandt boy among them, the Pell twins, little Ada Fennimore—slept through their nights like stones dropped in deep water. Old Mr. Aldous, ninety-one, who had not spoken a full sentence since 1974, asked his daughter for eggs.

And the mirrors. Every mirror in town reflected in perfect time. Paloma tested them obsessively at first—they could not help it; documentation was the shape their fear made—and filmed the hallway glass at Dunmore House for six hours on the third night, watching for the lag, the half-beat, the five-second drift, the figures that weren't there. The footage showed a hallway, a lamp, a tired person holding a camera. Nothing else. On the fourth night they stopped filming. On the fifth they caught themselves walking past the mirror without checking, and stood in the corridor for a long moment, undone by the enormity of having forgotten to be afraid.

But the town did not come out of it clean. That was the thing nobody wanted to say aloud, and which everyone knew.

* * *

They gathered in St. Brannock's on the eighth day—not for a service, exactly, though Reverend Fennimore tried to make it one, and not for a reckoning, exactly, though everyone understood it was that too. Nearly the whole town came. Paloma sat in the back pew beside Wren and watched three hundred people who had spent their lives above a secret try to learn how to look at each other.

Because the secret was out. It had come out the way such things do—messily, in fragments, in the testimony of nineteen gray-faced singers who could not lie about where they had been every Thursday night for half a century, and in the ledgers Paloma had carried up from the quarry, and in the reel-to-reel tapes, and in the simple arithmetic of the quarry disaster of 1846 that any schoolchild could now read in the county archive: one hundred and twelve singers dead in a flooded shaft, and within a year, seven founders impossibly prosperous, buying land, building the mill, founding the church, raising the house on the hill.

Prosperity had a price. The price had been paid in voices. Everyone's great-grandparents had known, or suspected, or chosen not to ask, and the not-asking had been inherited along with the land and the houses and the good soil.

Reverend Fennimore spoke about forgiveness and did not say who was to forgive whom. Mrs. Culver stood up during the open portion, her ruined voice carrying further than anyone expected, and said: "I knew. I want that said. I wasn't tricked. Voss told me what the singing was for when I was twenty-two years old, and I went anyway, because my mother went, and because I was afraid, and because some part of me believed the town was worth it." She sat down. The silence after was the most honest sound Paloma had heard in Dunmore Hollow.

Harold Pike said nothing at all. He sat with his hands open in his lap the way he had in the tunnel, and Paloma understood that some debts are paid in speech and some in silence, and that he would be paying his for a long time.

Nobody defended Voss. That surprised Paloma, at first, until they understood it: there was nothing to defend. He had never hidden what he was. He had stood in the square and told them plainly that prosperity must be repaid in voices, and they had nodded, frightened and agreeing, generation after generation, handing him their Thursdays and their children's futures because the alternative was to wonder, every day, what was under the hill and whether it was hungry. Voss had not corrupted the town. He had been the town's corruption, given a baton and a title.

And yet when Paloma described—haltingly, to Wren, late at night, in the kitchen at Dunmore House—what Voss had looked like in the final moments, before he turned and climbed away into the dark, Wren had gone very still.

"What did he look like?"

"Hollowed out," Paloma said. "Like the singing had been the only thing holding his shape. Like when it stopped, everything that wasn't the ritual just... drained."

"And you let him go."

"He was walking toward the sealed archway before either of us could move. And he was—" Paloma searched for it. "He was singing. Very quietly. Something I hadn't heard before. Not the founders' score. Something older, or newer, I don't know. He sounded almost..." They hesitated on the word and then committed to it. "*Relieved.*"

* * *

Voss surfaced one last time, three days later, and the whole town witnessed it, which Paloma believed was intentional.

He appeared at the mouth of the old quarry road at dawn, dressed in his good coat, carrying nothing. He looked, witnesses agreed later, twenty years older than he had at the sermon in the square—gaunt, gray, upright with the terrible uprightness of a man who has burned away everything except the decision he is making. He walked past the Hollis farm, past the church, past the houses of families whose names were in the founders' ledger, and not one person spoke to him, and he did not ask them to.

At the cellar door of Dunmore House—which stood unlocked now, because Paloma had seen no reason left to bar it—he paused. Paloma was in the yard. They had been digging in Edith's garden, absurdly, trying to remember how soil worked, and they straightened up with dirt on their hands and looked at him across forty feet of morning.

Voss looked back. There was no malice left in his face, and no authority either, and what remained was worse than either: recognition. He looked at Paloma the way a man looks at weather that has ended his era.

"You gave them their names," he said. His voice was a ruin, barely a whisper. "A hundred and twelve. I have read those ledgers my whole life and it never once occurred to me to read them *to* anyone." He shook his head slowly, as if at a joke told by God at his expense. "Two hundred years of music and the answer was bookkeeping."

"They weren't a thing under the hill," Paloma said. "They were people. That's all it ever needed. Someone to say so."

"Yes." He considered this the way he had once considered tempo markings—with total, devouring seriousness. "That is why you were chosen, I think. Edith understood. A clean voice hears what a bound voice cannot." He glanced at the cellar door, and something moved through his face—grief, or envy, or love; Paloma could not separate them. "The archway will hold now. But I would like to see it closed from the inside. It seems right that someone who kept it open should watch it shut."

"You don't have to do that," Paloma said, and meant it, and heard in their own voice that they also meant the opposite—that some part of them, some unforgiving part, thought it was exactly what he had to do.

"I know," Voss said gently. "That's why it counts."

He went down the cellar steps without hurrying. Paloma followed as far as the top step and watched him cross the earthen floor to the bricked archway, where the mortar—cracked for months, breathing warm air for months—had gone cold and solid as bedrock. He laid his palm flat against the brick one time, the way a man touches the coffin of someone he both loved and failed.

Then he turned, and looked up at Paloma, and said, "Sing something. Anything. I'd like the last thing I hear to be freely given."

So Paloma, standing on the cellar stairs with dirt still under their fingernails, sang the first thing that came—one hundred and twelve names were too many, so they sang just one, the smallest one, *Mercy Aldous, aged nine*—and the name rose in the cold air of the cellar and did not echo, because there was nothing below to echo it, and that was somehow the most beautiful and most terrible thing about it. It simply went out into the world, belonging to no ritual, owed to no one.

Voss bowed his head. Then he walked to the far end of the passage where the tunnel narrowed toward the deep galleries, and the darkness took him by degrees—coat, shoulders, the gray crown of his head—and he did not come back out.

Paloma sealed the archway the next week with Wren's help and the mason's son's labor, and they wrote nothing on the new brick, because there was nothing left that needed asking or answering. But Paloma stood before it a long time after the mortar dried, camera hanging forgotten at their side, and did not take a single photograph.

Some things, they were learning, you keep by not keeping them.

* * *

On the last evening of that first free autumn, Paloma sat on the front steps of Dunmore House while the light went amber and long across the valley. Wren came out with two cups of tea and sat close enough that their shoulders touched, and for a while neither of them said anything, because they had earned the right to silence that wasn't hiding anything.

Below the hill, if you pressed your ear to the ground—and Paloma had tried, twice, unable to fully break the habit—you heard nothing. Water moving through limestone. Roots. The ordinary machinery of a place that was only a place.

But inside the house, on the shelf in the east wing where the mirrors no longer lagged and the doors no longer locked from the inside, sat Edith's journals, and the founders' score, and the restored reel-to-reel tapes, and one hundred and twelve names copied out in Paloma's own handwriting onto acid-free paper. Everything archived. Everything kept.

Except one tape.

Paloma had set it apart weeks ago, before the descent, before the renaming—Edith's last recording, the whisper beneath the scream: *don't finish it—rename it.* They had told themselves they would decide what to do with it later, and later had arrived, and they still had not decided, and tonight, watching the light die over the hollow, they understood that the not-deciding was itself a kind of answer forming.

Because the house was quiet now. But houses outlive their quiets. Someday someone else would inherit Dunmore House—someone unbaptized, unaffiliated, undocumented by any church, a clean voice who had never asked for any of it—and they would stand in the east wing with a lawyer's letter in their hand, exactly as Paloma once had, and they would find the shelves, and the journals, and the gap where one tape should be.

Paloma picked up their tea. Down in the valley, the church bell rang the hour, ordinary and unmotivated, and the sound rolled up the hill and broke against the house and fell away, owing nothing to anyone.

"One tape," Wren said softly, following their gaze through the window to the shelf. "You're going to leave it for them, aren't you. Whoever comes next."

Paloma watched the last light move across the glass, reflecting the room in perfect time, and said, "I'm going to leave them instructions."
