## 8. One Voice Short

They found Edith Crane at the churchyard gate, kneeling in the snow with her mouth open on a note that had never finished.

Orsen heard the shouting before she understood it — voices rising from the direction of the church, ragged with something worse than cold — and she came down the lane with her boots half-laced, her grandmother's notebook clutched against her chest like a shield that had never once worked. A knot of villagers had gathered at the lychgate, and they parted for her without being asked, the way water parts around something it has decided not to touch.

Edith knelt in a perfect circle of cleared snow, as though the frost itself had stepped back to give her room. Her coat was buttoned to the throat. Her mittens were still on. There was no sign of struggle, no sign she had fallen — only that she had stopped, mid-stride, mid-breath, mid-*note*, and the cold had taken her the way a hand closes over a candle flame. Her lips were blue but her face was not contorted. If anything, she looked attentive. Listening. Her eyes were fixed on a point somewhere above the church door, and Orsen found herself following that gaze before she could stop herself, up the frost-slick stone, to the empty bracket where the hymn board hung.

The board was not empty.

White letters on the black felt, arranged in the old shorthand of hymn numbers — but these were not numbers. They were syllables. Orsen knew them at once, because she had spent three nights transcribing them from frost and one terrible evening singing them aloud with eleven frightened neighbors: the opening phrase of the binding verse, the line that was never meant to be sung twice.

*Come down, come down, the table's laid.*

"She ran," said Ivo Tarn at her shoulder. His voice was flat the way ground is flat after a long frost — no give in it at all. "Halfway through the rehearsal, when the bells went. I should have gone after her. I thought — I thought she'd just gone home."

"She didn't go home."

"No." Ivo took off his hat. His breath came out in a long grey plume. "She came here. To sing it back to it, maybe. To apologize." He was quiet a moment. "Or it called her, and she walked."

The doctor — a brisk, grey woman named Halloway who had stopped pretending, sometime in the last week, that any of this had a medical explanation — knelt beside the body and pressed two fingers to Edith's throat out of habit rather than hope. She looked up at Orsen, and her expression was the worst thing Orsen had seen all winter, because it was not horror. It was confirmation.

"Frozen through," Dr. Halloway said. "Not chilled. *Frozen.* Like she'd been out here a week." She paused. "Her lungs are full of frost, Orsen. Inside. She inhaled the winter, mid-note."

Orsen's hands were shaking. She made herself look at Edith's face again, at the open mouth, at the note caught in it like a fly in amber. *Come down, come down.* Edith had been one of the eleven. Edith had stood in the vestry two nights ago with a candle in her hand and her voice cracking on the third verse, and she had kept singing anyway, because Orsen had asked her to, because Orsen had promised her it would hold.

*It will hold. It held for a hundred and eighty years.*

Eleven voices now.

* * *

The cottage was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the stove. Orsen stood in her grandmother's kitchen — her kitchen now, though the house had never agreed to the transfer — and looked at the window over the sink, where the frost had been writing to her for days.

The pattern had changed in the night.

She had learned to read it the way one learns a difficult script: slowly, with a aching head and a growing certainty that she was not meant to be learning it at all, that the legibility was the trap. The frost wrote the forbidden verses in looping, crystalline notation, and always slightly wrong — a rest where there should be a note, a note where the silence should fall — and it waited, each morning, for her correction. It wanted her to sing it *right*. It wanted to be bound properly, or so she had told herself. She had stopped trusting that explanation somewhere around the night her grandmother's photograph had begun to look back at her.

The new pattern covered the whole lower pane, and it was not a verse.

It was a shape. A great closing curve, like a hand drawn around something small, and within it, nested lines that tapered to a single point — the penultimate figure, the second-to-last movement of a design that had been assembling itself across every window in Vellamoor, pane by pane, night by night. Orsen had seen fragments of it in the church vestry, in Ivo's cottage, in the schoolhouse where the children no longer slept soundly. It was nearly complete. One more pattern remained — a final small figure at the center of the curve, like a lock waiting for its last tumbler.

Beneath the frost-figure, in letters of clear melted glass where the warmth of the house had fought back and lost, someone had written a single word:

*TONIGHT.*

Orsen sat down at her grandmother's table — at the table that appeared in every photograph now with its twelfth chair — and opened the notebook, and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do since the lychgate.

The binding needed twelve living voices. Her grandmother's journal was explicit, in the cramped fading hand of a woman who had sung the renewal herself in 1963 and walked away while others did not: *Twelve to sing it shut. Eleven to hold, one to keep. The verse must be finished before the last frost closes, or what was bound stands up and stretches.* The last choir had died in the winter of '63 — all of them but her grandmother, and her grandmother had never once explained why she lived, though Orsen had begun, with a cold clarity that arrived like dawn, to understand. Someone had kept. Someone had stayed behind, in whatever sense the ritual meant, and her grandmother had carried the arithmetic of that survival in her bones for sixty years, and had sealed it into a notebook, and had died with the debt coming due.

Eleven voices. Eleven singers left in Vellamoor who knew the verses: Ivo, Dr. Halloway, the schoolmaster Pell, the brothers Voss, old Sera Quill whose voice was mostly breath now, and the rest — frightened, reluctant, bound to her by two rehearsals and a shared terror that had gone past the point where running helped. Eleven.

And the frost would close its final pattern by dawn.

Orsen put her head in her hands. Outside, the light was failing early, the way it did now, the way it had begun to do all over the valley — the winter leaning in, the days contracting like a dying animal's pulse.

There was a knock at the door. Then, before she could answer, it opened — because in Vellamoor, this week, doors had stopped meaning what they meant — and Ivo came in with snow in his hair and a child's hand in his.

* * *

Maren Tarn was eight years old, and she walked into her grandmother's — into *Orsen's* — kitchen the way children walk into churches, with that terrible unguarded reverence. She was small and dark-eyed and she had Ivo's jaw and none of his guardedness. She looked at the frosted window with its closing pattern the way other children looked at picture books.

"She wouldn't stay home," Ivo said. His voice had a crack in it that he was pretending was fatigue. "I put her to bed. I locked the door. I — Orsen, I did everything. And I came back from the churchyard and she was sitting on the step, waiting, singing under her breath."

Orsen went very still. "Singing what?"

"You know what."

Maren looked up at her. She did not seem frightened. That was the worst of it — a village full of grown men and women worn grey with dread, and this child standing in the cold kitchen with snow melting in her dark hair, calm as Sunday.

"It sings itself," Maren said. "In my head. It started after the bells rang. It's like —" she frowned, searching for it, "— like when you learn a song off the wireless and then it won't stop. Except it's not annoying. It's *patient*. It says it only needs one more voice. It says it's been waiting a very long time to be sung properly, and it's ever so tired of being sung wrong."

"*Maren.*" Ivo's voice broke clean in half this time.

"It's not a bad song," the child said, with the gentle authority of the very young delivering terrible news. "That's what nobody understands. It's a *hungry* song. It's like being hungry. You're not bad for being hungry. You just want the table filled."

Orsen knelt down so their eyes were level. Her knees cracked. Somewhere in her chest, beneath the terror, grief was doing its slow work — because she had heard this before, hadn't she, in the photographs, in the chair that appeared at every table in every frame in this house, warm as a lap, patient as winter. The presence did not roar. It *invited*. It set a place. It had set a place for her grandmother, and it had set a place for her, and now it had found the one voice in Vellamoor small enough and clear enough to slip into the last gap in the song like a hand into a glove.

"Maren," Orsen said carefully. "When it sings in your head. Does it ever ask you to come somewhere?"

The child's eyes flicked to the window. To the church, invisible now behind the dark, but present in every line of frost.

"It says the twelfth chair is ready," Maren said. "It says it's kept it warm."

Ivo made a sound that Orsen would spend the rest of her life not describing to anyone.

* * *

They gathered in the vestry at nine o'clock, by candlelight, because electricity in Vellamoor had become a courtesy the dark extended only intermittently. Eleven living singers — ten, Orsen corrected herself, ten and herself — stood in a ring around the worn stones above the thing beneath the church, and she laid her grandmother's notebook open on the vestry table, and she told them the truth.

All of it. The twelve to sing it shut. The one to keep. The frost's final pattern closing by dawn. Edith frozen mid-note at the gate, a voice subtracted from their number like a candle pinched out.

"Eleven of us," said Pell the schoolmaster. His hands were shaking around his candle. "We're eleven. We were twelve and now we're eleven, and the ritual wants twelve, and dawn is —" he looked at the black window, where the frost was creeping inward in its deliberate, closing curves, "— dawn is not far."

Silence. The kind of silence that has a shape, and the shape was the one nobody would look at directly. Eleven voices, and twelve chairs. The arithmetic sat in the middle of the ring like a body no one would cover.

"We could run," whispered Sera Quill, whose voice was mostly breath. "All of us. The whole village. Let it have the empty houses."

"It follows the song," Orsen said. "And we carry the song. It's in our heads now — Maren's proof of that. We'd be singing it down every road we took, and it would walk behind us, patient, until the last frost closed wherever we stopped." She looked around the ring. "There is no out. There is only through."

And then Maren stepped forward.

She had been sitting on a bench at the back with Ivo's hand clamped around hers, and she slipped it — slipped it the way children slip every hold meant to keep them safe — and walked into the candlelight, and stood before them all, and said, in her clear unbroken child's voice:

"I'll sing it. I know all the verses. It taught me."

"No," Ivo said, and it came out of him like something torn.

"It wants one voice to keep," Maren said reasonably. "It's been asking. Nobody would answer it, so it got louder. If I answer, it stops being loud. It stops being hungry." She looked up at Orsen, and her dark eyes were calm, and calm in an eight-year-old is the most frightening weather there is. "It says I have the right kind of voice. It says I've had it all along."

Orsen looked at the child. At the frost closing its final curve on every window in Vellamoor. At the twelfth chair that waited somewhere in the dark of the church above them — she could feel it now, had been able to feel it for days, a warmth in the cold like a hand held out. She thought of her grandmother in the photograph, seated at last, looking directly out, and of the sixty years between that seat and this one, and of the debt passed hand to hand down a generation like a dish too hot to hold.

The sacrifice no one would name stood in the candlelight, eight years old, volunteering.

And Orsen Halloran heard herself say, in a voice that did not sound like hers at all:

"Not you."

She stepped toward the ring of singers, toward the church, toward the chair.

"It was always going to be me."
