## 8. Doors That Forget

I slept badly, which felt like a promotion. The night before, I hadn't slept at all — I'd sat in the kitchen with the lights on and a cast-iron skillet on my lap like a man courting a very specific kind of paranoia, watching the window over the sink where my reflection had, around two in the morning, turned its head to look at something behind me. There was nothing behind me. There was nothing behind me for four hours. But my reflection kept checking.

So on the third day I did what any rational man does when his house starts editing itself: I made coffee, I made lists, and I told myself the list was the point. That's the hospice nurse's sacrament. When the world goes sideways, you chart it. You write down times and observations in tidy handwriting, because as long as someone is keeping records, someone is still in charge of the story.

*Day three,* I wrote. *Mirrors lag by one second. Mantel mirror shows nothing. Kitchen window reflection exhibits independent head movement. Subject remains calm.*

*Subject is lying,* I added, and underlined it twice.

The plan was simple: drive into town, buy every candle and battery the general store owned, talk to Ruth Calloway again, and get some straight answers about what my grandmother had actually lived with out here. Simple plans are how the universe picks its targets.

The truck wouldn't start.

Not the dramatic kind of not-starting, either. No cough, no sputter, no sad mechanical death rattle. I turned the key and the engine simply declined to acknowledge the request, the way a bureaucrat declines a form stamped in the wrong color ink. The dashboard clock said 7:14 a.m. It said 7:14 a.m. when I tried again at 7:31. It said 7:14 a.m. when I got out, walked around the truck, kicked the tire with genuine feeling, and tried a third time.

My phone showed 9:02.

I stood in the yard holding two devices that disagreed about time itself, and I thought about Edith Wray counting backwards in her sleep, thirty to zero, night after night, and how she'd stopped exactly at zero each time she flatlined. I thought: *they hear it coming.* And then I thought: *shut up, Cyrus,* because that thought had teeth and I wasn't ready to feed it.

So I went back inside, because outside had started to feel like standing in an open mouth.

* * *

The house changed while I was making lunch.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. At 12:40 I carried a plate from the kitchen to the front room — eleven steps down the hallway, I'd counted them the first night out of some compulsive need to know the house's dimensions, to hold it inside a number. Eleven steps. I walked them carrying my sandwich, sat down, ate half of it, and decided I wanted the other half warmed up.

Seventeen steps back to the kitchen.

I stopped in the middle of the hallway and stood there like an idiot, plate in hand, doing the math over and over, hoping the hallway would blink first. It didn't. The wallpaper was the same. The doors were the same. The light came through the same windows at the same angle. But the hallway had grown six steps somewhere between bites of a turkey sandwich, quietly, without effort, the way a tree grows — and I understood, with a clarity that arrived cold and complete, that the house hadn't changed at all.

*I* had walked into a different house. Or the same house, remembering a different year.

Because here's the thing about the doors. On my first walk-through, the hallway had four doors: bathroom, linen closet, Vera's old bedroom, and the spare room where I'd dumped my bags. Now there were five. A sixth door, actually — I counted twice — narrow, painted a faded green, with a porcelain knob gone yellow with age, set into the wall where there had been nothing but plaster and a framed embroidery of a psalm.

The psalm was gone. The door was there.

I did not open it. Let the record show I am capable of learning. I backed away down the too-long hall, watching the green door the way you watch a dog that's gone still, and I locked myself in the kitchen — the one room, I noticed now for the first time, with no mirrors at all. Not even the window over the sink reflected anymore; it just showed gray, like frosted glass, like an eye that had decided to stop seeing me.

That should have frightened me more than it did. Instead it felt like a mercy. I sat in the one blind room in the house and ate cold sandwich and laughed until it turned into something else, and then I cleaned the skillet that didn't need cleaning, because my hands needed a job my mind couldn't fire them from.

At dusk, the green door opened on its own.

Not wide. Just a crack. A hand's width of darkness, breathing out air that smelled of lilies and well water, and beneath those smells something older — dust, yes, but dust with a shape to it, dust that had settled on things no one had touched in decades.

Here is what I know about myself, seven years of hospice work distilled to one hard fact: I can stand in a doorway where someone is dying, and I can walk through it. I do it every shift. Fear is a vital sign like any other — you note it, you treat the cause if you can, and you proceed. So I noted it. Pulse elevated, palms damp, patient (me) exhibiting marked reluctance. Treatment: none available. Prognosis: poor.

I walked through the door.

* * *

It was 1968 on the other side.

Not metaphorically. Not a dream, not a hallucination, not grief playing tricks — I've sat with enough dying people to know what the mind does under pressure, and this was not that. This was a room mid-mourning, preserved like a fly in amber, and everything in it was *finished*, in the terrible sense that a sentence is finished.

The front room of the house, but not my front room. The wallpaper was different — yellow roses, flocked, the paper darkened in a rectangle above the sofa where a picture had once hung. The furniture wore doilies. On the mantel, the mirror I knew was absent, and in its place stood a framed photograph of a girl, maybe eight years old, gap-toothed, squinting against sun, one hand tangled in the mane of a horse too big for her.

And people. That's the part I keep circling back to, trying to make it smaller than it was. There were people in the room, three women in dark dresses, sitting absolutely still, and for one merciful second I thought they were statues, waxworks, some trick of the light.

Then the youngest one blinked.

She didn't move otherwise. Her chest didn't rise. Her hands stayed folded in her lap around a handkerchief twisted so tight it must have cut off her own circulation years ago. But her eyes moved — tracked me, slow as a searchlight, across the frozen room — and her mouth opened, and no sound came out, just the shape of words, over and over, the way Edith Wray's mouth used to move in her sleep:

*Not her. Not her. Not her.*

I know sign language better than I'd like. Seven years in hospice teaches you every dialect of desperation. That one translates clean in any language.

A casserole sat untouched on the sideboard, glazed over with a skin of dust half a century thick. A cassock-draped priest stood by the window with his hand raised in a blessing he never finished delivering. On the coffee table, a funeral arrangement of white lilies, brown now, collapsed into themselves like lungs giving out. And pinned to the wall beside the photograph of the girl, a card, the kind they tuck into flower sprays, with a name printed in careful script:

**ANNALISE MARCHETTI — BELoved DAUGHTER**

Except the printer's ink had run, or someone's tears had, or both, and the letters had bled into each other until the name looked less like a name than a wound.

I didn't touch anything. God help me, I wanted to — I wanted to shake that blinking woman, to say *she's not gone, look, something answered the bell, whatever came back isn't your daughter, run* — but the room had the quality of a held breath, and I knew instinctively that if I broke the surface, I wouldn't break it alone.

The youngest woman's eyes followed me to the doorway. As I stepped backward through it, her mouth shaped one last word, and this one I could almost hear, a sound arriving a full second late, like everything else in this house:

*Vera.*

* * *

The green door led to more rooms, and the more rooms led deeper, and I followed because the alternative was standing still in a house that was actively rearranging itself around me, and I'd rather meet the past moving forward than have it come up behind me.

There was a kitchen from 1968 with a pie cooling on the sill, unrotted, impossibly whole. A bathroom with a child's toothbrush, bristles still soft. A stairwell that climbed where the real stairwell didn't exist, up to a landing hung with photographs I recognized from the attic boxes — Vera young, Vera laughing, Vera holding a baby with Lorraine's exact scowl.

And at the end of the longest hallway yet, behind a door painted the pale, hopeful blue that parents choose for reasons they don't examine too closely, I found her room.

Annalise's room.

Preserved like a shrine. That's the only honest word. The bed was made with such care it hurt — hospital corners, a quilted coverlet, a single stuffed rabbit propped against the pillow with its ears arranged just so, the way you arrange things when you can't arrange anything else. Dolls sat in a row on the shelf, dusted, faced outward. A child's drawing of the farmhouse hung on the wall, every window filled with smiling faces, including, I noticed with a slow crawl of ice down my spine, the round stone shape of the well, which also had a face. Smiling. Drawn in crayon. With more teeth than a face needs.

On the desk, under a glass paperweight, lay a diary with a small brass lock, long since sprung. Beside it, a jar of marbles, a hair ribbon, and a glass of water. The water was still clear. Fifty-six years, and the water was still clear, and I decided right then that I was not going to think about that, and I thought about nothing else for the next several minutes.

I picked up the diary.

The early entries were a kid's entries — ponies, a teacher named Mrs. Pruitt, a best friend who bit. Then, in the spring of '68, the handwriting tightened. Grown-up handwriting wearing a child's hand like a borrowed coat.

*Mama says the well is dry and we must never go near it. But it isn't dry. I put my ear to the stones and something counts underneath. It counts backwards. It likes it when I count with it. Mama got angry when I told her. She cried, which she never does.*

Then, June:

*It has a name. I asked it polite, like Mama taught me, and it told me. It is called the Tallyman, and it counts what is owed. I asked owed by who, and it laughed, and the laugh came up through the ground and out of the drains in the house, all of them at once, even the ones upstairs that don't connect to anything.*

July. The last entry. The pencil pressed so hard it had torn through to the next page in places:

*Mama rang the bell. She says it will give me back. But the thing that came up had my face and it smiled wrong and it counted Mama's fingers while she held it and it whispered numbers into her ear all night. I am writing this fast. If you find this, my name is Annalise Marchetti. Please say it. Nobody says it anymore. Mama won't. Grandma won't. If nobody says your name, the Tallyman gets to keep it, and if it keeps your name it keeps YOU. Please say it. Please please please*

The entry stopped mid-word.

I stood in that blue room holding a dead child's diary, and I heard myself breathing, and I heard the house breathing around me, and somewhere below the floor — below the floor, below the foundation, below whatever the foundation was standing on — something began to count.

Backwards. Patient. Bookkeeping-calm. A voice like arithmetic given a throat.

*Thirty. Twenty-nine. Twenty-eight.*

I closed the diary and took it with me. It seemed important. It seemed like the most important object in the world, though I couldn't have told you why — only that a child had written *please say it* seven times, and that no one had, in fifty-six years, and that the water in the glass was still clear.

Behind me, as I left the shrine, the stuffed rabbit's head turned on the pillow to watch me go.

Downstairs, the green doors were closing one by one along the hallway, quiet as eyelids, and the house was getting shorter, folding itself up around me like a fist.

*Twenty-five. Twenty-four.*

I ran for the kitchen — the blind room, the room with no eyes — clutching the diary to my chest, and as I crossed the threshold the voice below the floor reached twenty-one and paused, savoring it, the way Edith Wray used to pause at ten, and then it resumed, slower, closer, as if it had all the time in the world.

As if it were certain I did.
