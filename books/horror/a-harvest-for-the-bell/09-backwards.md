## 9. Backwards

I didn't sleep so much as I was held, briefly, by unconsciousness and then released back into the dark like a fish nobody wanted to keep.

The attic light stayed on all night. I told myself that was practical — no reason to go up there again, but if I did, better not to fumble. The truth is I didn't want the dark between me and the journals. Vera's handwriting had a way of continuing after you stopped reading it. Not literally. I want to be clear about that. Not literally. But the way a song keeps playing in your head after the radio's off, her sentences kept going in mine, counting down dates in decades I hadn't been born for, and every entry ended the same way: *not enough.*

I made coffee at six a.m. because making coffee is what people do when they need their hands to have an opinion about something other than shaking.

The kitchen clock said 6:04. My phone said 5:58.

I stood there holding both truths and let the rationalizations do their little dance. Clocks drift. Old houses have old wiring. The phone synced to a tower somewhere and towers are never wrong, except of course that phones drift too, everyone knows this, there's probably a whole subreddit about it.

Then the microwave — which I had never set, which blinked 12:00 in green accusation since the day I arrived — read 6:04.

Two against one. The house was voting.

"Okay," I said out loud, to the kitchen, to the dead orchard beyond the window, to whatever kept its own hours here. "Okay."

* * *

Here's the thing about seven years of hospice work: it teaches you a very specific kind of listening. You learn to hear the difference between a rattle that means mucus and a rattle that means hours. You learn the rhythm of Cheyne-Stokes breathing, the crescendo-decrescendo, the long apneic pauses that families always mistake for the end until the breathing comes back and they feel foolish and grateful and foolish again. You learn to chart it without flinching. *Periods of apnea increasing. Family at bedside. Morphine per protocol.*

And you learn — though nobody puts this in the training materials, though you'd never say it aloud in the break room where Denise microwaves her sad little fish lunch — you learn that some of them count.

Not all of them. Maybe one in twenty. They do it in their sleep, mostly, or in that twilight place right before, lips moving, fingers twitching against the blanket like they're pressing buttons on a calculator only they can see. And they never count up. It's always down. From thirty, from a hundred, from numbers with no pattern I could ever find. Edith Wray counted backwards from thirty, every night for eleven days, and stopped at zero exactly four times, and each time she stopped at zero, her heart stopped with her, and each time we brought her back, and the fourth time we didn't try.

I logged it as respiratory patterns. I logged everything as something else. That was the job, really — the whole profession was a machine for converting mystery into paperwork. We were translators, and the language we translated into was denial.

But sitting in my grandmother's kitchen with a clock that lied and a phone that told the truth and a microwave that had apparently developed opinions, I finally let myself finish the thought I'd been refusing to finish for seven years.

They weren't counting because they were confused. Dying people aren't confused; dying people are the most lucid people I've ever met, once you get past the morphine. They were counting because they could *hear* something. Something patient. Something bookkeeping-calm. Something standing at the foot of the bed doing what it always did before a harvest, the way a farmer checks his watch before the combine rolls.

Counting down.

Edith Wray heard it coming. Every one of them heard it coming. And I had spent seven years writing *apnea, per protocol* over the sound of a tally being taken.

My coffee had gone cold without me drinking any of it. I noticed this the way you notice things when your body is running the show and your mind has stepped out to make a phone call it can't come back from.

"It counts," I said. "It counts them down."

And then, because the universe has a sense of humor and it's not a nice one, my phone screen lit up all by itself, showed me 5:58 again, and died. Not low-battery died. Dead died. Black glass, no reflection in it — and I want you to appreciate that detail, because a black phone screen used to be a mirror like any other, and now it was just *nothing*, and nothing felt worse.

* * *

I tried the truck at 7:15 by the kitchen clock, which by then I trusted more than myself.

The engine turned over — that was almost worse than silence, the *whrr whrr whrr* of a starter doing its honest work while the engine declined to participate. Fuel gauge read half. Battery tested fine two days ago. I popped the hood and stood looking at the engine like a man looking at a foreign alphabet, because I am that man, and we both knew it wasn't the alternator anyway.

It was the house. Or the property. Or the thing under the well, keeping its own hours, closing its own accounts.

"Fine," I said. "Walk."

Hollow Creek was nine miles away. Nine miles is nothing. I'd walked farther than that in bad shoes for worse reasons. I got water, I got Vera's jacket off the hook by the door — it smelled like lavender and dust and something underneath both, something mineral, well-water-ish — and I stepped onto the porch into morning light that looked exactly like morning light should look, which should have been my first warning. After three nights in that house I'd earned some wrongness in the sky. Instead the sun came up golden and indifferent, birds did their bird thing, and the whole world performed normalcy so convincingly that I almost believed it.

The orchard started fifty yards past the barn. Dead apple trees in rows, planted by someone who expected grandchildren, pruned by nobody for thirty years. Their branches were gray and twisted and, in the half-light, they had the specific posture of people waiting for a bus that isn't coming.

I walked north, toward the road, compassing myself off the sun.

At 8:40 by my watch, I reached the orchard's edge. There was a fence there — I remembered it from the drive in, a sagging wire fence with a rusted gate, and beyond it the county road, and beyond that Ruth Calloway's fallow field, and beyond that the world, presumably, still existing.

I reached the gate. I put my hand on it. And I looked through it at my grandmother's front porch.

Fifty yards behind me. With my truck parked crooked in the yard and the front door open and the kitchen window catching the sun.

I stood there for a long time. Long enough for a crow to land on a dead tree, consider me, and leave, having better places to be.

I'm not proud of what happened next, but I'll report it accurately, because accuracy is all I've got left. I turned around and walked south. Fast. Then I cut east through the trees, climbing over fallen limbs, scratching my face, doing the thing where you walk fast enough that panic doesn't have room to breathe. I hit the eastern edge of the orchard at what my watch insisted was 9:20, grabbed the fence, and looked through it at the front porch.

The truck had moved. Not driven — moved. It sat at a slightly different angle now, and I understood, in the calm clinical part of my brain that had survived seven years of deathbeds, that the house was rearranging itself the way it had rearranged its hallways, and that the yard was part of the house, and that the orchard was part of the yard, and that I was inside something with a shape I couldn't hold in my head, like trying to picture a four-dimensional object, except the object was hungry.

West, then. I walked west until my legs burned, until the sun crossed high noon by my watch even though it couldn't have been more than two hours since dawn, until I grabbed the western fence and looked through it at the porch, and this time the door was closed, and this time, in the kitchen window, there was a shape standing there watching me.

It had my build. It wore my grandmother's jacket.

I stopped walking. I stood in the dead orchard with my hands on my knees, breathing like a bellows, and I did the math I'd been avoiding: the property line was a circle, and I was the center of it, and every direction was a door painted on a wall.

* * *

I sat down under one of the dead apple trees and ate the sandwich I'd had the presence of mind to bring, because even in a nightmare, hospice nurses pack snacks. It's in the training. Chapter one: feed yourself, or you're no good to anyone.

While I chewed, I thought about Edith Wray.

Eleven nights of counting backwards from thirty. Four flatlines at zero. And here's the detail I'd filed under *coincidence* and locked in the drawer where I keep everything: on night five, she'd counted backwards from *thirty-one*. First time ever. And on night ten, from *twenty-nine*. The number changed. The number adjusted. Like a countdown being recalculated. Like a deadline moving.

She hadn't been confused. She'd been *reporting*. Lying there with her eyes closed, translating the only way she could, telling anyone who'd listen how much longer she had — and I'd stood at her bedside charting her respiratory patterns, nodding gravely, adjusting her oxygen, while she gave me the single most important piece of medical information available and I wrote *see care plan* in the notes.

"You were always on the ledger." Nobody had said that to me yet. But I could feel the sentence waiting somewhere ahead of me, patient as arithmetic.

Because here's what else I knew, sitting under the dead tree with crumbs on my shirt: Vera had bought time. Her journal said so. *I bought her time. Not enough.* A bargain, a debt, a harvest — Ruth would know the details, Ruth with her warnings about midnight and her husband who'd gone missing from the historical record in 1991. And debts, in my experience, don't get forgiven. They get *transferred*. Seven years I'd listened to the dying count. Seven years I'd been practicing, without knowing it, for the moment the countdown pointed at me.

Thirty. Twenty-nine. Twenty-eight.

I looked at my watch. It said 1:17 p.m., which meant nothing, which had stopped meaning anything around the time the sun started commuting irregularly. But the second hand was still moving, and I watched it move, and I thought: *that's the trick, isn't it. Everything counts. Everything is always counting. The only question is whether you count along or pretend you don't hear it.*

Vera pretended for forty years. It hadn't saved her; it had just made her die alone in a house full of mirrors that couldn't tell the truth.

I wasn't going to pretend.

I stood up, brushed off my jeans, and faced the porch — my porch, allegedly, currently occupied by a version of me wearing a dead woman's jacket — and I said, out loud, in the clear carrying voice I use to talk over family arguments at bedsides:

"I hear you counting. I've always heard you counting. So let's skip to the end, because I've got questions, and I know somebody under that well who owes me answers."

The shape in the window raised its hand.

I did not raise mine back. I'd learned that lesson already, in the hallway mirror, on the first night, when my reflection waved a full second late and I'd spent two days pretending I'd imagined it.

No more pretending. The trap had snapped shut — phone dead, truck dead, world folded up like a map — and somewhere in the space between panic and resolve I found a third thing, quieter than either, that felt suspiciously like purpose. I was a hospice nurse. Sitting with the dying was the entire job. And something in this valley was dying to get out, and it had been counting down to meet me, and I intended to be present for it, alert, unmedicated, and asking questions.

I started walking toward the porch. Fifty yards. The shape in the window watched me come, and behind it, deep under the dry ground, I heard — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable —

the gurgle of water rising in a well that had been empty for thirty years.

Twenty-seven, I thought. Twenty-six.

The front door opened before I reached it, and the smell of lavender and well-water rolled out to greet me like an old dog that had finally decided I belonged to the house instead of the other way around.
