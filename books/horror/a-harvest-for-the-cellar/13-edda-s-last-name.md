## 13. Edda's Last Name

I found Edda in the kitchen at dawn, and she was already setting out two cups.

That was the thing about her I could never reconcile with everything the house had done — the small domestic courtesies. The bread she baked without flour I could find anywhere in the pantry. The tea that tasted faintly of apples, as if the orchard had seeped into the kettle. She moved through this place like a woman walking through a field she knew was full of snares, stepping exactly where she had always stepped, and the house let her, because the house needed her the way a lamp needs oil.

"Eat," she said, pushing the bread toward me. "You've gone thin. Surveyors go thin. All that measuring, and nothing to show for it but numbers."

"I need to ask you something," I said. "And I need you not to feed me first. Not today."

Her hands went still on the table. Outside, the orchard stood in the grey light, forty-one trees, forty-one names, and I had eaten from one of them and would never be able to un-know what I now knew. My mother, carried down the cellar stairs by hands that were not hands. My grandmother's voice, singing in the dark, kept like a lure in a tackle box.

Edda looked at me for a long moment. Then she sat down across from me, and folded her hands, and waited.

"The ledger," I said. "The one behind the plaster. My name is the last line. But before mine there are names I don't know — names the house harvested, names the orchard grew. And there's a gap. Sixty years where the count stopped. Where the house stopped counting down." I turned my phone around on the table, the recording app open, the waveform of last night's playback still frozen on the screen — the woman's voice, patient and maternal, asking me questions I never heard asked. "The house paused. Something paused it. I think it was you."

Edda's face did something I had never seen it do. It opened. All the careful blankness, the pleasant vagueness of the very old and the very tired, fell away like a sheet pulled off furniture, and underneath was a woman with sharp eyes and a mouth that had spent decades deciding what not to say.

"Sixty years," she said. "Give or take. I stopped counting the years when I stopped needing them."

"Tell me why."

"Because the house can't finish a harvest with an empty table." She said it simply, the way you'd explain a rule of cards. "It counts down. It counts the Coldharbours. And when the count reaches zero, the last one signs, and the house closes its hand. That's the whole of it. That's the deed and the ledger and the cellar and the singing, all of it, in one sentence." She leaned forward. "But a harvest needs a resident. A living one. A Coldharbour under this roof. As long as one of us stays — as long as the count can't reach zero — the hand stays open. The house waits. The house is very, very good at waiting."

"So you stayed."

"I stayed." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever been given. "Sixty years, Casper. I baked the bread. I swept the step. I answered the door for the lawyers and the surveyors and the poor young couple who thought they'd found a bargain in 1987 — I got them out before dark, that was my one good trick, I got them out before dark — and every night I went to bed and listened to the counting and I did not answer it. And every night it took a little of me anyway, because I was here, because I was Coldharbour, because that's what the walls drink." She held up her hands, turned them over. They were smooth. Unlined. The hands of a wax figure, of a woman the years had been unable to touch because the years had been eaten instead. "Do you know what I have left?"

I shook my head.

"One memory." Her voice didn't waver. "One. Everything else is gone. My wedding day — gone. My sister's face — gone. The name of the boy I loved before I married a man I can't remember marrying — gone, gone, gone. The house took them the way a tide takes sand, a grain a night, sixty years of nights." She tapped her own sternum, once, gently, like knocking on a door. "I have my name. Edda Coldharbour. That's all. I keep it the way you'd keep a coal alive in ash. I say it every morning before I get up. Edda. Edda. Edda. And the house can't take the last one, do you know why?"

"Why?"

"Because a resident needs a name to sign. Even the house has to obey the paperwork." Her mouth twisted. "Your Mrs. Vane understands that better than anyone. The house is a bureaucracy, Casper. It's a ledger and a deed and a signature. It's cruelty with a filing system. And so long as I hold my name, the count holds at one, and the hand stays open, and everyone who got away stays away. That was the whole of my life. That was my harvest — a life spent as a plug in a drain."

The kitchen was very quiet. The fire ticked. Somewhere above us, a door settled in its frame — one of the thirty-eight doors that weren't supposed to exist, breathing in its wall.

"You're my great-aunt," I said. It wasn't a question. I had done the arithmetic in the night, after the playback, after the ledger, after the apple. "My mother's mother's sister. You were there when the house took her."

"I was in the kitchen." Her voice went flat and careful. "I heard the stairs. I always heard the stairs. I made tea for your grandmother afterward because there was nothing else in the world to do, and we drank it, and neither of us said a word, because saying a word would have been answering, and you never answer." She looked down at her smooth, terrible hands. "Your grandmother lasted eleven more years. She sang to keep her memories. Did you know that? She said the house couldn't take what was being sung. She was almost right. It took everything except her voice, and it kept the voice, because a lure has to be beautiful."

"And me. I was here. I was a child here."

"You were here for one winter." Edda's eyes came up and fixed on mine, and in them was sixty years of a single held breath. "You were four years old. You had a coat with brass buttons and you counted the doors in the hall every morning because counting was a game your mother made of it — *count the doors, Casper, count them before breakfast* — and the house heard you counting and the house began to count back, and that's when your grandmother knew it had marked you. She got you out. She falsified the records, she gave you to the county, she made you a boy with no family and no history, because a boy with no name can't be counted." She swallowed. "She traded her own harvest for it. She let the house start taking her early. That's why there was so little left to keep. That's why all that's left of her is the singing."

I sat with it. The bread sat untouched between us, going cold, going stale, going to nothing.

"I've spent my whole life counting," I said finally. "Doors. Locks. Steps. I count everything. I thought it was a habit. I thought it was professional."

"It's a scar," Edda said. "It's the shape the house left in you."

* * *

She stood, then, and I thought she was going to the stove, but she went instead to the dresser in the corner — the old pine dresser with the drawers that stuck, that I had catalogued on my first day as *Dresser, pine, c.1900, poor condition, value nil* — and she opened the bottom drawer, and took out a small tin. A tobacco tin, rusted at the corners, dented on one side as if it had been dropped and caught, dropped and caught, over and over, for sixty years.

She brought it back to the table and set it between us, and did not open it.

"Your grandmother gave me this the night she sent you away," Edda said. "She said, *keep one thing of mine, Edda, keep it where the walls can't see it, and when a Coldharbour comes back to this house with counting in his bones — give it to him.*" She looked at me. "I've waited a long time for a Coldharbour with counting in his bones."

"What's in it?"

"Open it."

The lid came off with a sound like a small held breath being released. Inside, on a bed of yellowed wool, lay an apple. Small, hard, dark red gone brown with age — an orchard apple, but not like the ones on the trees. The ones on the trees were heavy with their harvest, fat with other people's lives. This one was dry as a seed. Hollow, almost. Light.

"It's not from the trees," Edda said, watching my face. "The trees grow what the house takes. This is what your grandmother gave *away*. Before the house could take it. She walked out into the orchard on the night she sent you off, and she picked the one apple the house hadn't touched — the one that grew from her own planting, her own choice, her own name — and she gave it to me, and she said a memory given freely can't be harvested." Her voice dropped. "It's her name, Casper. Her whole name. Maren Elowen Coldharbour. It's in there the way a coal is in ash. Sixty years, and it's still warm. Feel."

I picked it up. It was warm. Impossibly, gently warm, like a stone left near a fire, like a hand.

"I can't take this," I said. "It's all you have left besides—"

"It's not mine to keep." Edda's hands came down flat on the table, and for the first time since I'd met her, the old woman's voice filled the kitchen the way the house's voice filled the halls — patient, total, immovable. "I have held this house shut for sixty years with my name and my stubbornness and nothing else. I am down to one coal, Casper, and I am tired, and the counting is getting faster — you've heard it, don't lie to me, you've heard it — and when it reaches three, when it reaches two, I will not be able to hold it. I know how this ends for me if I keep it. I end up in a wall. I end up as a question after dark. I end up singing." She pushed the tin across the table until it touched my hand. "But if it's with you — if it's given, freely, held by someone the ledger can't touch because his name isn't signed — then the house can't have it. Ever. That's the trick your grandmother found. That's the harvest she left us. *A memory given freely can't be harvested.*"

"Edda—"

"Take my name too."

The kitchen went so quiet I could hear the orchard. Not the wind in it — the orchard itself, the slow wet creak of forty-one trees holding forty-one lives.

"You said you're down to one memory," I said. "You said your name is all that's keeping the count at one. If you give me your name—"

"Then the count moves." She nodded. Calm. Terribly calm. "Then it starts again. Then the hand begins to close. I know exactly what I'm giving you, boy. I've had sixty years to think about it." She reached across the table and took my hand — her smooth wax hand around my scarred, counting hand — and she pressed the apple into it, and folded my fingers closed over it, and the warmth of it went through my palm and up my arm and into the place behind my ribs where all my grief had been living since the playback. "I held the door for everyone else. Your grandmother. Your mother. You. Now you hold it for me. That's not a sacrifice, Casper. That's a family. That's the only thing this house never understood — that a family isn't a ledger. It's a hand passed from hand to hand. It's an apple in a tin."

"Edda Coldharbour," I said. Her name. I said it the way she said it every morning — a coal kept alive in ash.

Her whole face changed. Sixty years of holding, and someone had finally said it back to her.

"That's right," she whispered. "That's right. Now keep it. Keep it where the walls can't see. And Casper — when the counting gets loud, when it gets close, when it says your name in your mother's voice — you hold that apple and you remember that you were given something. Freely. The house only knows how to take. You know how to be *given to*. That's the difference. That's the whole war."

I closed my hand around the apple and it was warm, and I understood that I was holding a woman's entire self, and that she had handed it over with both hands and a steady heart, and that I would have to be worth it.

* * *

I am Edda Coldharbour, and I have just done the bravest and stupidest thing of a very long life.

The boy — he is not a boy, he is a man of thirty-odd years with his grandmother's brow and his mother's way of looking at a room like it owes him an explanation — the boy has my name in his fist now, wrapped in an apple, and the house felt it. I felt the house feel it. A shudder went through the floor like a great animal turning over in its sleep. Upstairs, a door opened that I did not open. Down in the cellar, the counting paused — mid-number, mid-breath — the way a man pauses on a stair when he hears a sound he doesn't like.

Good, I thought. Let it pause. Let it learn what it feels like.

I am down to nothing now. I can feel the edges of it — the way the kitchen is just a kitchen, the way the fire is just a fire, the way the word *bread* is a sound and no longer a morning. The house will start on me tonight. I know the order it works in. It takes the recent first, then the dear, then the old. It will take the sixty years of holding, and then it will take the holding itself, and by the end I will be a sweet vague old woman who bakes bread for no one, and after that I will be less.

But it cannot take what I gave away. That is the whole trick. That is Maren's legacy, passed hand to hand, and the boy has it now, and his hand closed over it like a man catching a coal.

He will need it. The counting is at three — him, me, and the girl. Wren. The letter went out — I never wrote it, the house wrote it, the house is hungry and it knows how to use a pen. When Wren comes, the trap closes to its final shape, and there will be a night — I have seen the shape of it in the walls, in the way the doors have started standing ajar like mouths — there will be a night when this house opens everything it has, every door, every voice, every harvested soul it has kept warm in its walls like a miser's coins. It will call us all by name.

It will not call me by mine. I gave mine away.

I stood at the kitchen window this evening and watched the boy walk the orchard with the tin in his coat pocket, one hand pressed flat against it, guarding it, and I felt the strangest thing. Not fear. Not the hollow. Something I don't have a memory for anymore, something older than my remaining name — the feeling of a hand letting go, and trusting the next hand.

The stairs behind me creaked, though no one was on them. The counting started again, low and patient, and for the first time in sixty years it was not counting me.

It was counting down to him.
