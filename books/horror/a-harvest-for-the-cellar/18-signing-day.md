## 18. Signing Day

Mrs. Vane arrived at nine in the morning, which felt like a courtesy the house had extended to itself. Nine was a working hour. Nine said: this is business, nothing more. She stepped out of her grey sedan with a leather folio under one arm and her coat buttoned to the throat despite the mildness of the day, and Casper watched from the study window as she stood at the gate for a long moment before opening it — as if waiting for permission, or remembering that she'd once needed it.

He met her on the front steps.

"Mr. Coldharbour." Her voice was flat and professional and he heard the effort in it, the way you hear the effort in someone carrying something heavy up stairs while pretending the stairs are nothing.

"Mrs. Vane."

"The final papers." She lifted the folio slightly. "The buyer's solicitor has been patient. Unusually patient. I've had three calls this week asking when we'll close."

"Have you told them?"

"Told them what?" Her eyes went past him, into the hall, to the staircase, to the dark mouth of the corridor beyond it. "That the property answers questions? That I haven't slept a full night since 1987? What would I tell them, Mr. Coldharbour — in language a court would accept?"

Inside, she set the folio on the kitchen table, and Edda put out bread and butter without being asked, because Edda still did that, still fed people, even now, even with almost nothing left of herself except the habit of feeding people and the name she kept folded away like the last clean shirt in a drawer.

Vane looked at the bread and didn't touch it. "You know what this is," she said to Casper. Not a question.

"I know what it is."

"Then let me say it plainly, once, so it's on the record somewhere, even if the record is just this kitchen." She opened the folio. The papers inside were heavy stock, cream-colored, and the ink of the prepared signature lines looked wet, though the documents had been printed weeks ago. "The sale requires the last resident of record to sign in the cellar. Not in my office. Not at a notary. In the cellar. I have never understood why the buyers accept that condition. I understand it now. I signed my own closing, thirty years ago, on behalf of an estate I no longer remember the name of." She turned her hands over on the table. The scars were old and fine, thin lines of darker skin across every finger, like the hands of someone who had gripped a pen too hard for too long, or been gripped by one. "I walked out with my fee and my life and a hole where the estate used to be. I couldn't tell you the family's name. I couldn't tell you why I cried for a week. I just knew I'd lost something and couldn't file a claim for it."

Edda touched her wrist, gently. Vane flinched, then let her.

"You came back," Edda said.

"I came back," Vane agreed. "Every time there's work here. Do you know why? Because some part of me is still in those walls, and it pulls. Like a tooth gone bad. You keep finding your tongue against the gap." She straightened. "So. Today the count ends, one way or another. Either the last resident signs, and the house closes its account satisfied, and whoever signs walks out lighter than they came — or nobody signs, and the house keeps counting forever, and none of you ever sleep again."

"That's not the whole choice," Casper said.

"No," Vane said. "It isn't. But it's the only part I can put in writing." She squared the pages. "Where do you want to do this?"

"Not yet," Casper said.

"Mr. Coldharbour—"

"Not yet. There's something I have to do first. Give me until tonight."

Vane looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face made her close the folio without argument. "Tonight, then. I'll wait in the car if I have to. But I'm telling you now — I've seen a man try to walk away from a signing. The house doesn't chase. It waits. It's very good at waiting. And the waiting does worse things to people than chasing ever could."

* * *

The day went long.

Casper spent it doing the thing he knew how to do: surveying. He walked the house room by room with his tape measure and his clipboard, because measurement was a form of prayer for him, because numbers held the world still. He counted doors. Thirty-one now. The false ten had gone quiet since Wren arrived, sealed themselves into the plaster like healed wounds, but he counted anyway, twice, and got the same number both times, and hated how much comfort he took from it.

Wren stayed in her room most of the day. He heard her through the door sometimes — pacing, the creak of the bedframe, once a low furious muttering that might have been rehearsal. She was going to sign. He knew it the way you know weather coming. She'd said it plain enough two nights ago in the hallway, her voice cracking on the word *nothing*: *This house gave us nothing but hunger, Casper. It ate our mothers and left us the bill. I'm taking the money and I'm gone.* And then, quieter, the wound under the anger: *Why should you get to be the good one? Nobody gave me anything. Nobody ever.*

He hadn't had an answer then. He wasn't sure he had one now. All he had was the ledger, and the ledger didn't argue; it just listed.

In the afternoon he found Edda in the kitchen shelling beans, her hands moving with automatic precision, and sat across from her, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"Edda," he said finally. "When you go down there tonight—"

"If I go down there tonight."

"When you go down there. What are you planning to give it?"

Her hands didn't stop. A bean split cleanly along its seam. "Myself," she said. "What's left. One memory, boy. You know which one."

"Your name."

"My name." She smiled, small and dry. "Seventy-nine years I've carried it. It's light now, lighter than these beans. Isn't that funny? The heaviest thing I own and it weighs nothing at all. I thought about it all night. If I hand it over, the count reaches zero, the papers can be signed, the sale goes through, and the house is full and quiet and everyone else walks free. Wren gets her money. You get your life. The house gets me, whole, such as I am. That's a fair trade. I've done the arithmetic."

"It's not arithmetic."

"Everything is arithmetic eventually." Another bean split. "I watched this house take my sister. I watched it take my mother. I stayed because somebody had to be the resident, and a resident who won't answer questions after dark is a resident it can't finish eating. Seventy years of keeping my mouth shut in my own home. Don't you dare tell me it isn't arithmetic. Arithmetic is all that kept me human."

"Edda—"

"And don't you dare tell me you have a better plan, either." She looked up then, and her eyes were clear and terrible, the eyes of someone who had already grieved everything including herself. "I've seen you reading in the study. I've seen the deed copied out in your handwriting, over and over. You think I don't know what you're planning? You think I survived seventy years in this house by being unobservant?" She set down the bean pod. "Whatever you're going to do tonight — and I know it's something, because you've got the look your grandfather had the day he went into the orchard and didn't come back the same — you will not do it before I sign. Promise me."

"Why?"

"Because if you do it first, and it fails, there's no one left to try again. Because if you do it first, and it works, then I die having given nothing, and I have spent my whole life giving nothing but withholding." Her voice gentled. "Let me end it on my terms, Casper. Let me be the one who pays. That's not sacrifice. That's just finishing the job."

He wanted to promise. God, he wanted to — it would have been so easy, one sentence, and then he could carry the deed down to the cellar tonight knowing she'd already emptied herself, knowing the fire only had to finish what she started. It would have been clean.

Instead he said, "I promise you'll decide for yourself. At the last step. Whatever happens, the last step is yours."

She studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded slowly, and picked up the beans again, and that was the closest thing to blessing he was going to get.

* * *

At dusk, Mrs. Vane's headlights swept the drive.

They gathered in the kitchen — Vane with her folio, Wren drawn out of her room by the smell of finality, arms crossed, jaw set, eyes red-rimmed in a way she'd deny. Edda had put on her good cardigan, the blue one, the one with the pearl buttons, and Casper understood suddenly that she had dressed for this the way you dress for a funeral. Her own.

The cellar door stood open at the end of the hall. It had unlocked itself hours ago. Of course it had. The house had been waiting for this day longer than any of them had been alive.

They went down together, because Wren refused to stay upstairs ("I'm not letting it eat her in the dark where I can't see") and Vane refused to stay upstairs ("I've done this once alone; never again"), and Casper went because there was nowhere else in the world for him to be.

The cellar was cold and dry and smelled of stone and apples and something older underneath, something like turned earth, something like a grave that had been tended lovingly for generations. The shelves ran back into darkness. And the counting —

The counting was no longer a whisper.

All day it had been building, patient as tide, and now, with them standing on the earthen floor beneath the heart of the house, it roared. Not sound exactly — pressure, rhythm, a vast subtractive pulse that came up through their boots and filled their teeth. *Nine hundred and six. Nine hundred and five. Nine hundred and four.* Names inside the numbers, dates inside the names, every Coldharbour who had ever been taken, tallied and retallied, and the walls sweating a faint amber light like the inside of a jar.

Vane pressed her hands over her ears and it didn't help. Wren doubled over. Only Edda stood straight, facing the far wall where a small table waited with a single chair, and on the table, the papers, already laid out, already waiting, the signature line glowing faintly wet.

"There," Edda said. "Well. It's tidy, at least. It always was a tidy house."

She walked toward the table.

Casper watched her go — seven steps, eight, her cardigan pale in the amber light, her spine straight as a fence post — and every rational plan he had built over three weeks of nights, every careful sequence he'd rehearsed (deed from the oilcloth, matches from the tin, stand between the ledger wall and the table), all of it dissolved into one screaming simple fact: *she is going to give it her name, her last thing, the only thing she has left, and it will take it and it will thank her and it will still be hungry.*

*Nine hundred and two. Nine hundred and one.*

Edda reached the table. She drew out the chair. She picked up the pen — the pen was already there, of course, black and heavy, warm as a living thing — and uncapped it.

"Edda." His voice cracked on it. "Great-aunt Edda."

She paused. Didn't turn.

"You told me once," he said, louder, over the roar, "that the house counts residents. That it pauses for a resident. That's why you stayed seventy years — you were the dam. You were the reason it didn't take Wren when she was a child. You were the reason it didn't take *me* when they took me away and changed my name and I grew up thinking I was nobody's." His breath tore. "If you sign, the count hits zero and the house is *satisfied*. Satisfied means fed. Fed means it starts again — next generation, next branch, some cousin neither of us has ever heard of, some child in a city flat who doesn't know why she dreams of orchards. You're not ending it. You're *renewing the lease*."

Now she turned. The amber light moved on her face. Behind her the counting swelled, and he could hear — they all could hear — the sweetness underneath it, the maternal hush of it, the voice that asked questions after dark wearing the voice of numbers: *come, come, it's time, you're so tired, you've held on so long, put it down, put it down, little one, put it all down.*

"I know," Edda said quietly.

"You *know*?"

"I've known for forty years. Where do you think I learned to keep my mouth shut?" She held up the pen. "But it's my name, Casper. Mine. The one thing in this world that was never harvested, never falsified, never sold. And I will spend it how I choose." Her eyes were wet and fierce. "Stand aside."

"No."

"*Casper.*"

"No." He moved — not toward her, toward the wall behind the table, the low shelf where he'd hidden it that morning in the hour before dawn, wrapped in oilcloth against the stone. He pulled the cloth away.

The original deed lay in the candlelight he'd left burning there. Old vellum, soft as skin, the founding signatures crawling down its margin like roots. The anchor. The ledger's first page. The thing every harvest ultimately served.

The roar stopped.

Not faded. *Stopped* — the whole cellar dropping into a silence so total he could hear the candle flame, could hear Edda's breath, could hear Wren saying his name somewhere behind him in a voice gone small. The house was looking at him. The whole weight of it, every wall, every door, every tree in the orchard above, turning its attention like an eye.

Edda stared at the deed, and understanding crossed her face slowly, terribly, the way frost crosses a window. "Oh," she said. "Oh, you foolish, brave boy. What will it cost you?"

"Everything it gave me," Casper said. "That's the trade. That's the only trade it honors — it taught me that itself. Everything taken must be paid for. So I'm paying." He looked at her, at the pen still in her hand, at the unsigned line. "You don't spend your name, Edda. You *keep* it. That's the whole point. That's what none of us ever did — we kept handing pieces over, generation after generation, rent on a house that owns the land our names are buried in. Burn the deed, and the land stops collecting." His hand closed around the vellum. It was warm. It beat faintly, like something asleep. "But I need you to hear this first, because once I strike the match there's no version of me left who remembers why it mattered. I don't want to forget you. I want you to know that. Out of everything — the house, the childhood they stole, the mother I never got back — losing the memory of belonging to you is the price I mind most. So I'm saying it now, while I still know your face: you fed me bread the first day and you called me *boy* like I was yours, and you are the only family I have ever had, and I am doing this *for* you, all of you, and I am not afraid."

Edda made a sound he had never heard from her, small and broken and proud all at once, and Wren was crying openly and Vane had turned her scarred hands face-up as if in witness, and the silence in the cellar went taut as wire.

Then, from everywhere — from the shelves, the stones, the soil overhead — the house spoke. Not in numbers. In the soft maternal voice, the question-voice, gentle as a hand on the cheek:

*"And what will you be, little Casper, when you are no one's?"*

He struck the match.

The vellum caught like it had been waiting sixty years to burn, flame running the root-script signatures in a bright race toward the founding seal — and the scream began, rising through every door in the house above, through the orchard, through the ground itself, and the ledger wall split open down its length like a chest being opened, and the count, for the first time in three hundred years, began to move the other way:

*Nine hundred. Eight hundred ninety-nine. Eight hundred ninety-eight—*

And Casper felt the first memory leave him — warm, painless, a door opening in the dark — and could not, even then, remember what it had held.
