## 1. The Wrong Name on the Stone

The town announced itself the way it always had — not with a sign but with a smell. Xanthe caught it through the open window of the rental car two miles out: river silt and cut hay and something sweeter underneath, something like apples going soft in a cellar, the smell of a place that had been ripening toward rot for a long time and saw no reason to hurry.

Marrow Creek sat low in its valley the way an old dog sits when it's decided not to get up again. The creek itself had widened over the years, or the banks had given up — it was hard to say which — and the water ran brown and patient through the backs of the yards on Delft Street, lapping at the foundations of houses that had once, in Xanthe's childhood, stood a full lawn's length from the waterline. Some of those lawns were gone now. Some of the houses were listing, their porches tilted like smiles on the wrong side of a face. A heron stood in what used to be the Hutchinses' yard, one leg up, watching her car pass with the flat indifference of something that had outlasted everyone it had ever known.

Three years. She had told herself it would be easier this time. She had made lists. Lists were how Xanthe Reyes moved through the world: *call the county clerk, meet the appraiser Thursday, sort the attic last, don't go in Zephyra's room first.* The lists were a kind of armor, and she had worn them so long she sometimes forgot they weren't skin.

She had not made a list item for the cemetery. That had felt like the kind of thing a person should be able to do without preparation.

* * *

The Marrow Creek cemetery occupied a low hill that the floodplain had never quite reached, though it kept trying — the grass at the bottom of the slope grew in dark, thirsty tufts, and the oldest stones at the eastern edge leaned like drunkards listening for water. Xanthe parked on the gravel shoulder and sat for a moment with both hands on the wheel, watching a crow walk the top rail of the iron gate as though it were checking her credentials.

The gate didn't squeak. That was the first wrong thing, though she didn't log it as wrong yet. Someone had oiled it. Someone had oiled the gate of a cemetery in a dying town where half the lots were for sale and the other half were underwater, and the oil was fresh enough that her fingertip came away faintly black when she touched the hinge.

She told herself the county maintained it. The county maintained the roads, after all — such as they were.

The Reyes plot was in the newer section, up the hill, where the ground was still dry enough to hold its dead without argument. She could see the stone from thirty yards away, and something in her chest began its slow, practiced clench — the grief reflex she had learned to ride like a wave, letting it build and break and pass. She was almost grateful for it. Pain with a known shape was a comfort. It proved the world still ran on rules.

Then she was close enough to read the stone, and the wave didn't break. It just stopped, mid-build, like a held breath.

The granite was clean. Not weathered-clean, not rain-clean — *scrubbed*, scrubbed with something abrasive, the way you'd scrub a pan, the fine grain of the stone scoured into a soft matte sheen that caught the afternoon light and threw it back dull and even. Three years of lichen and hard water staining and the green-black kiss of the valley's endless damp — gone. Every letter of the inscription stood out crisp as the day the cutter had finished it.

And the dates were wrong.

ZEPHYRA IONE REYES, the stone said, in letters she knew by heart, letters she had chosen herself from a catalog in the funeral home's back office while some part of her mind floated near the ceiling and refused to come down. Beloved daughter. Beloved sister. *Taken into light.*

But beneath the name, where the dates should have read the span of her sister's life — the birthdate carved there was Xanthe's.

Not a similar date. Not a date off by a season, the way a grieving family might err. It was *her* date. The seventh of March. The date she had celebrated for thirty years, the date Zephyra had teased her about every single year because they were twins and Xanthe had arrived first, by four minutes, and had never once let her forget it. The seventh of March sat there in the granite, freshly cut, the chisel marks still sharp inside the curves of the numerals, and beneath it the second date — the death date — three years old, correct, final.

Xanthe stood very still. A bee investigated the silk flowers someone had left — someone had left flowers, silk peonies, Zephyra's least favorite, another small wrongness — and moved on. The crow had followed her up the hill and now sat on a neighboring stone, head cocked, one eye fixed on her with the frank interest of an auditor.

*Vandalism,* she thought. The word arrived promptly, dressed in a little suit, carrying a clipboard. *Vandalism. Kids, probably. They scrubbed it — no, wait, why would vandals scrub it—*

She made herself walk the perimeter of the stone. She made herself look.

The original dates had not been chiseled out. There were no scars, no patch-work, no epoxy-filled corrections. The stone had been *re-cut*. The old inscription was simply gone, and the new one was simply there, and the granite around it was uniform, and the only evidence that anything had ever been different was the scrubbed brightness of the whole face of it — a brightness that had no business existing on a stone that had stood through three winters of this valley's wet, mineral-heavy air.

She crouched. She touched the seventh of March with one finger, the way you'd touch a wound to confirm it's yours. The stone was cold. Of course it was cold. Stones are cold. She was a rational woman; she had a career in grant administration; she believed in weathering rates and county budgets and the infinite human capacity for pointless cruelty, and she stood up from her sister's grave and said, out loud, to the empty hill, in a voice that came out steadier than she had any right to expect:

"Somebody fixed the wrong stone. Somebody's kid got bored. Somebody—"

The sentence ran out of road. There was no version of it that worked. Vandals did not re-cut granite; re-cutting required a stonemason, a stencil, a day's labor, and a reason. And the reason — the only conceivable reason — was a shape she refused to look at directly, the way you refuse to look at the sun: that someone had wanted this stone to bear *her* dates. That someone, or something, had decided that the person in the ground should have been born on the seventh of March.

That the person in the ground *was* born on the seventh of March.

"No," she said, and heard how it sounded — not an argument but a prayer, the smallest and least rational word she owned.

The crow shifted its weight. Down in the valley, the church bell rang three o'clock, though she hadn't thought Marrow Creek still rang its bell; the last she'd heard, the tower was structurally unsound. The sound came across the water flat and slow, each stroke arriving a half-beat late, as if the air itself had to think about it.

Xanthe took out her phone and photographed the stone. Four shots, methodical, from four angles. Documentation. When she was a girl, Zephyra had been the one who believed in ghosts and omens and the secret grammar of dreams, and Xanthe had been the one who believed in documentation. *Take a picture, Zeph. If it's real, it'll keep.* Zephyra would laugh and say that was exactly backwards, that the things that were real were exactly the things that wouldn't keep, that photographs were how you pretended the world would hold still.

She was standing at her twin's grave photographing a correction to her own birthdate, and the world was not holding still.

* * *

She did not go to the house right away. She told herself this was scheduling — the appraiser wasn't due until Thursday, there was no point sitting in an empty house — but the lists had gone soft in her hands, and the truth was that she drove the long way around, past the diner with its hand-lettered pie sign, past the feed store that was now a church annex that was now nothing, past the elementary school where the playground equipment stood in six inches of standing water like the bones of some patient animal.

At the corner of Delft and Mill, she stopped for a dog that wasn't there. Her foot was on the brake before her mind caught up — a brown shape, low and quick, crossing — and then there was nothing in the road at all, nothing but heat shimmer and the brown water moving in the ditch, and she sat with her foot on the brake while a pickup truck behind her waited, and then went around her, and the driver, an old man with a face like a closed door, did not look at her as he passed. Nobody in Marrow Creek looked at her car. She noticed that the way you notice a missing sound — the absence of the small human reflex, *a stranger, look*, that towns like this ran on.

The house came up on her slowly, because she drove to it slowly, because the last hundred yards of Delft Street were the last hundred yards of her childhood and she wanted to be done with wanting it back.

It was worse than the photographs. Photographs are merciful; they crop out the smell. The house was a narrow two-story clapboard, gray-white, its shutters the green of old bottle glass, and the flood had taken the yard and was now working, patiently, on the crawlspace. The porch — the deep, wide porch her grandfather had built, the porch where they'd shelled peas and read comic books and dared each other to touch the post where the wasps nested — the porch was dry, somehow. The water stopped at the property line as if it had been asked politely.

And the porch swing was moving.

It hung from the beam on two chains her father had greased every spring, and it was moving — a slow, shallow, metronomic arc, forward and back, forward and back, with no wind to move it. The air was dead still. The creek wasn't even riffling. The only motion in the whole drowned valley was that swing, keeping time like something counting.

Xanthe sat in the car in the road, engine idling, hands at ten and two, and let herself have exactly ten seconds of the old argument. *Wind. Chain settling. Residual — something.* Ten seconds, and then she turned the engine off, because the swing did not stop when the engine did, and because she was thirty-three years old and her sister was dead and there was no one left in the world whose job it was to go first.

She got out. She walked up the walk. The boards of the porch announced her the way they always had — *someone's here, someone's here* — and the swing kept its slow count, and the house behind it stood with all its windows reflecting the gray sky, so that it seemed to be looking at her with many eyes and none of them open.

That was when she saw that the swing was not empty.

There was a shape in it. Not a person — her mind reached for *person* and the word slid off — but a suggestion of one, a boy-sized dimple in the air, the way heat makes the road shimmer, the way you can see a bird in a bush only when it moves. The swing didn't need a boy in it to move. But the swing had a boy in it, or the memory of one, and as she stood on the bottom step with her heart going like a trapped bird against her ribs, the shape resolved just enough to have a face.

Overalls. A gap where a front tooth should be. Hair cut by a mother in a hurry. Seven years old, the way he had been seven years old for twenty-six years, because he was imaginary, because they had made him up together one summer on this very porch, two girls and no friends and a whole town that thought twins were a closed set — and he had never needed to be real, because he had never needed to age.

He had been theirs. Both of theirs. Zephyra had named him. Xanthe had given him the missing tooth, because Zephyra had lost hers that June and refused to smile in the school picture, and Xanthe had thought a friend should have the same wound.

The shape in the swing looked at her with an expression she remembered from a hundred childhood evenings — patient, faintly amused, waiting for her to catch up — and said, in a voice like the space between bell strokes, like the half-beat-late air of the valley itself:

"You took longer than she did."

* * *

Later — much later, though "later" would turn out to be a flexible word in Marrow Creek — Xanthe would try to reconstruct the next ninety seconds and find only fragments: her own scream or maybe just her breath; the gravel spraying under the rental car's tires; the cemetery road and then the county road and then the highway, the highway, *the highway*, her foot flat to the floor, the speedometer climbing past eighty, past ninety, the valley unspooling behind her in the mirror — and then the mirror showing the porch again, the gray house, the swing, closer now, the swing swinging, the road having turned her around the way a stream turns a leaf, without effort, without malice, the way water does what water does.

She passed the heron twice. She passed the diner twice, and the second time the pie sign read a different pie.

And the third time she came up Delft Street, she did not fight it. She parked in the road in front of the house she had been born in, in the town that would not let her leave, and she sat with her forehead against the wheel and admitted — in the small, cracked, unarmored voice she had not used since the funeral — that the stone had not been vandalism, that the dates had not been an error, that she had driven three years and eight hundred miles to sell a house and had instead been *expected*.

On the porch, the swing kept its slow count.

And the door of the house — the door she had locked behind her three years ago, the door her mother had locked for twenty years before that — stood open, and from somewhere inside, deep in the hall where the piano sat with its bench kept locked, someone was playing scales. Slowly. Badly. The way a child plays.

The way Zephyra had played, before she quit.

Xanthe Reyes, who believed in documentation, took out her phone and photographed the porch. The image showed an empty swing, moving.

She went inside.
