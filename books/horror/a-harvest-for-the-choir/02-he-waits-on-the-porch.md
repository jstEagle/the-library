## 2. He Waits on the Porch

The house came into view the way houses do in dreams — all at once, without the approach, as if it had been waiting behind a held breath and now exhaled itself into being.

Xanthe pulled the rental car onto the shoulder of Reyes Road and sat with her hands on the wheel, engine ticking down to silence, and looked at the place where she had been born and had promised herself she would never stand again. The Winterbourne-Reyes house — though in Marrow Creek everyone just called it *the Reyes place*, because her grandmother's family had been here since before the town had a name that wasn't a joke about bones — sat at the bottom of its long slope of dead grass like something that had been set there deliberately, arranged, posed for a photograph no one would ever take.

It was smaller than she remembered. That was the first lie grief told her, and she let it, because it was easier to think *the house shrank* than *I have been away so long that even the walls have forgotten my proportions.* Three years. Three years since the funeral, since the coffin with the wrong name on the paperwork, since she'd signed the cremation authorization with a hand that didn't feel attached to her wrist. The house had waited. Houses in Marrow Creek were good at waiting. The whole town was built on a floodplain, on patience, on the understanding that everything eventually comes back to the water.

The porch swing was moving.

She saw it before she saw anything else — before the peeling paint on the shutters, before the gutters sagging like tired eyelids, before the front door standing open just enough to show a strip of darkness inside. The swing moved in a slow, even arc, back and forth, back and forth, with the metronomic patience of something keeping time. There was no wind. The morning was so still that the cattails by the drainage ditch stood up straight as candles.

Xanthe's body made the decision before her mind could intervene. Her foot found the gas pedal. The car lurched forward down the drive, gravel popping under the tires, and she was telling herself — already narrating, already explaining, because explanation was the muscle she'd built these three years until it was the strongest part of her — *someone left the swing going, some kid from town, the estate agent, someone* — when the car rolled to a stop in front of the porch and she saw him.

Wren.

He sat in the middle of the swing with his feet not quite touching the floorboards, swinging them in little idle kicks, and he was seven years old. He was exactly, perfectly, unbearably seven years old. Denim overalls with one strap unbuttoned, the brass button hanging by its thread. A striped t-shirt gone soft with washing, faded from red to the pink of old scars. His hair was cut in the uneven bowl shape that only a child's own impatient scissors or a distracted parent can produce, and his knees were scabbed in the same places they'd always been scabbed, as if the scabs themselves had refused to heal out of loyalty. And when he turned his head and smiled at her, the gap where his left canine should have been opened like a small dark door.

He had not aged a day. She had buried her twin sister and grown crow's feet and learned what her own face did when it cried, and Wren had stayed seven, suspended in denim and scabs, swinging on a porch that belonged to two dead women and a woman who wished she were.

"You took longer than she did," he said.

His voice hadn't changed either. It still had that slight whistle through the tooth gap, that bright reedy quality that had once made Zephyra laugh until she wet herself behind the woodshed. He said it mildly, almost reproachfully, the way you'd greet a friend who was late to a picnic. *You took longer than she did.* As if Zephyra had come home first. As if this were a race they'd both been running, and one of them had finished.

Xanthe did not get out of the car.

She became aware, distantly, of her own breathing — shallow, high in her chest, the breathing of prey. Her hands had locked around the steering wheel at ten and two, a driving-instructor grip, as if proper form could save her. Through the windshield she watched Wren watch her, head tilted, and there was nothing threatening in him, that was the terrible thing, there was nothing in him but the patient open affection of a child who has been waiting for someone and is glad, finally, that they've arrived.

"Hi, Xanthe," he said. "You look different."

*You're not real,* she thought. *You were never real. We made you up. Two lonely girls on a drowned street made you out of an old hymn and a scarecrow's coat and the space where a brother would have been, and you stopped existing when we stopped needing you, which was when we grew up, which was years ago, decades ago—*

But her mouth wouldn't form the words. Her mouth had gone dry as August.

She got out of the car anyway. Later she would try to reconstruct why — whether it was courage or courtesy or the simple gravitational fact that some doors you walk toward even knowing what waits behind them — and she would never find a satisfying answer. Her legs carried her up the porch steps. The boards groaned under her weight in the third and seventh places they had always groaned, and the sound went through her like fingers down her spine: *the house remembers your footsteps.*

"Your hair's shorter," Wren observed. He scooted sideways on the swing, making room, an invitation so automatic and guileless that it hurt worse than any threat could have. "Zeph cut hers too, at the end. She got mad at it."

"Don't." The word came out cracked. "Don't say her name."

"Why not? I say it all the time." He kicked his feet. The swing swayed gently, though he'd stopped pumping it. "We practice songs together. She's getting really good at the low parts. She used to hate the low parts."

The morning tilted. Xanthe put her hand on the porch rail and the paint came off on her palm in gray flakes, and she stared at those flakes because they were real, they were verifiably real, lead paint and weather damage and three years of neglect, and she needed one real thing to stand on while the rest of the world went soft at the edges.

"Wren." She made herself look at him. Made herself hold the gap-toothed smile in her gaze without flinching. "You are not real. You were an imaginary friend. Zephyra and I invented you when we were six. There is no version of this in which you are sitting on our porch in 2024 wearing the same overalls you wore in 1993. So whatever you are — a hallucination, grief, a brain tumor, a very elaborate prank by the estate agent — I need you to understand that I see through it, and I am not going to play."

Wren considered this with the grave attention children give to important announcements. Then he patted the swing beside him.

"She said that too," he said. "The first time. Word for word, almost. 'I see through it.' She talked fancy like you." He smiled wider, and the tooth-gap whistled faintly on his next breath. "Then she stopped talking fancy. It's easier after you stop. You'll see."

*After you stop.* The phrase landed in her stomach like a stone dropped down a well. She heard the depth of it, how far it fell before it hit water.

"I'm here to sell the house," she said, and her voice surprised her by holding. "Boxes, paperwork, the lawyer comes Thursday. Then I'm gone. Whatever this town is doing, whatever game this is, I'm not staying for it."

"Okay," Wren said agreeably.

That was all. No argument, no menace. Just *okay*, delivered with a child's terrible economy, and Xanthe understood suddenly that he didn't need to argue, because he knew something she didn't yet know. He knew about the road.

She fled.

There was no other honest word for it. She turned on her heel, took the steps in two strides, wrenched open the car door, and got behind the wheel with her heart slamming against her sternum like something trying to get out. Keys. Keys. Her hand shook so badly she stabbed the ignition twice before the engine caught. In the rearview mirror, as she reversed down the drive, she saw Wren still on the swing, watching her go, one hand raised in a small wave. Not waving goodbye. Waving the way you wave at someone who has stepped out of a room and will be right back.

*See you soon,* the wave said. *This isn't leaving. This is a loop.*

She drove.

Marrow Creek unspooled around her the way it always had: the feed store with its sun-bleached sign, the water tower standing on its rusted legs like a bird too tired to fly, the church at the center of everything with its white steeple pointing at a sky that couldn't decide between blue and gray. She passed the diner. She passed Odessa Pell's house, its porch crowded with wind chimes made of spoons. She passed the turnoff for Route 9, the road out, the road that led to the interstate and the airport and the life she had built in a city where nothing sang unless you paid it to.

She took the turn onto Route 9.

The road ran straight for half a mile through stands of birch, their white trunks pale as candle wax, and then it curved — a gentle bend to the left that she had driven a thousand times, that every kid in Marrow Creek had driven a thousand times on the way to anywhere else. She came around the curve expecting the long descent toward the county line, the marsh opening up on the right, the water glittering.

Instead she came around the curve and saw the feed store.

Her foot hit the brake. The car skidded slightly on gravel and stopped, and Xanthe sat gripping the wheel and staring at the sun-bleached sign, the water tower beyond it, the church steeple rising over the rooftops. She had not turned around. She had not taken an exit. She had driven one continuous line away from the house and arrived back at the beginning of the same street, as if Marrow Creek were a record with a scratch in it, as if the town were a tongue that could only say one sentence.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the empty car. "Okay. Wrong turn. You missed the turn. It happens."

She turned around at the feed store and drove back toward Route 9, deliberately, watching the road signs this time, reading each one like scripture. ROUTE 9 NORTH — COUNTY LINE 12 MILES. She took the turn again. Birch trees. The curve. The descent—

The diner.

She was on Main Street. The diner's neon OPEN sign buzzed in the window; a woman inside — Odessa Pell, she realized, small and bent behind the counter — looked up at the sound of the passing car, and her face, even at a glance, even at speed, wore an expression Xanthe would spend days trying to name. It wasn't surprise. It was the expression of someone watching a thing happen that always happens, on schedule, like weather.

Xanthe drove faster. She took Route 9 a third time and pushed the car to seventy, eighty, the birches strobing past like the slats of a cage, and she came around the curve and the road fed her gently, inevitably, back onto Reyes Road, and the house rose up at the bottom of its slope of dead grass, and the porch swing was moving, and Wren was on it, and he raised his hand again.

She parked crooked in the yard. She sat for a long moment with the engine off and her forehead nearly touching the wheel, and she did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding: three attempts, three returns, no turns she could remember missing, no exits she could find. The town was not letting her leave. Or the road wasn't. Or — and here her rational mind, that faithful workhorse, laid its ears back and refused — or the road had never led anywhere at all.

When she finally got out, her legs were unsteady. The morning had warmed without improving; the light lay on everything like a damp cloth. Somewhere behind the house, in the flooded fields along the creek, frogs were singing in ragged unison, and beneath their song, so faint she might have imagined it, another sound: voices. Many voices. Distant and sweet and horribly organized, like a choir rehearsing underwater.

They stopped the moment she noticed them.

Wren watched her climb the porch steps. He had not moved from the swing. The sun caught the brass button dangling from his overall strap and made it flash like a tiny signal fire.

"The road does that," he said, kindly, before she could speak. "It did it to her too, the first time. She got all the way to the county line once, in her truck, and then she was just — back." He shrugged, a child's shrug, shoulders and innocence. "It's not mean about it. It's just that nobody leaves during harvest season. You're early. That's why you took longer than she did."

"When," Xanthe said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, thin as paper. "When is harvest season?"

Wren smiled his gap-toothed smile and pointed one small finger at the sky, at the fat white moon still hanging there above the water tower, visible even in daylight, pale as a fingernail clipping.

"Now," he said.

And from inside the house — from the dark strip of hallway showing through the open door, from the rooms where her grandmother's piano sat locked and her sister's bedroom stood waiting — came a single clear note, sustained, patient, pitched exactly to the frequency of her own heartbeat, and Xanthe recognized it with a lurch of nausea: it was the first note of the hymn. The founding hymn. The one the choir sang every Sunday of her childhood while the congregation swayed like wheat.

Someone, or something, was warming up.

"That'll be Zeph," Wren said, hopping off the swing, landing lightly on his scabbed knees. He held out his hand to her, palm up, an offer of friendship across fifty years and one impossible grave. "She's been practicing your part. Come on in. She's been dying to see you."

The words hung in the warm air, and Xanthe, who had spent three years learning to explain everything, found that she had no explanation for the way her hand trembled as she reached for the door — and none at all for the fact that some traitorous part of her wanted to take the offered hand.
