## 7. Wren Tells It Slant

The house remembers me better than I remember myself.

That's not a complaint. It's just how it is, the way it's always been. The porch boards know the weight of my feet and creak in the old familiar places — third board from the left, the one with the knot like a closed eye. The swing knows my hands. When I sit down it settles into its rhythm without being asked, back and forth, back and forth, the chains singing their small rusty hymn, and I think: *this is what belonging sounds like.* A sound so worn-in it has stopped being noise and become silence.

Xanthe ran from me today.

I want to say that didn't hurt, but Wren doesn't lie to himself. That was one of the rules we made up, all three of us, sitting cross-legged on this same porch when the girls were seven and I was — well. However old I was. *No lying to yourself,* Zephyra said, because she was the one who made rules out of everything, who drew lines in chalk on the driveway and dared the world to step over them. *No lying to yourself,* Xanthe echoed, because she always echoed, always harmonized, even then. And I said it too, though I don't think I understood what I was agreeing to. Seven-year-olds agree to things they don't understand. That's practically the definition of childhood.

So: it hurt. She looked at me the way you look at a spider in the bathtub — that quick arithmetic of *how did you get in here and how do I get you out* — and then she ran, and her car went down the road, and the road brought her back, and she ran again, and the road brought her back again, and by the third time she wasn't running so much as walking very fast with her jaw set, which is worse, because it means she's decided to stay angry instead of staying scared, and anger lasts longer.

I waited for her on the swing. That's what I'm good at. Waiting.

* * *

Here is what I remember, and I want to tell it right, because nobody's asked me to tell it in fifty years and a story kept too long in the dark goes soft in the middle, like fruit.

It was warm. That's the first thing, the thing everything else hangs from. October of 1974, and warm the way a hand is warm — not weather-warm, but *held*-warm. The whole town felt held that autumn. You could see it in people's faces at the feed store, at the diner, in the pews of the First Congregational. Everybody walked around looking like they'd just heard good news they weren't allowed to repeat yet.

I was five years old. I know that the way you know your own hands — not by thinking about them but by using them. Five years old, and small for it, and my mother used to carry me on her hip even though she said I was getting too big for it, and I would hook my arm around her neck and feel her hum through her collarbone. She hummed all the time that year. Everybody hummed. It was like the town had one song caught in its head and nobody minded.

I don't remember my mother's face.

I've tried. I've sat on this swing through ten thousand evenings trying to build her face out of what's left, and what's left is: her hands, and her humming, and the smell of her — bread and something green, rosemary maybe, or the creek itself, that mineral smell the water leaves on skin. Her hands were brown and strong and one of them had a scar across the palm shaped like a river delta. I used to trace it with my finger while she carried me and she'd say, *that's where the creek got its idea,* and I'd laugh even though I didn't get the joke, because her voice made laughing happen in me the way striking a match makes fire happen in a wick.

But her face is a door painted onto a wall. I keep walking into it.

I've decided that's normal. Faces are hard. Ask anybody.

* * *

The night they came for me, the neighbors were singing.

I want to be careful here, because I know — I've watched Xanthe learn it, chapter by chapter, the way you watch someone read a letter addressed to them that keeps getting longer — I know there are versions of this story where the singing is a bad thing. Where the neighbors' faces were wrong. Where the warmth was a trick of temperature, a fever the town was running.

But I was there. And I'm telling it slant, maybe, the way light comes slant through the church windows in the late afternoon, gold and thick as honey, making everything look like a painting of itself. But slant light is still light. It still shows you what's in the room.

They came up the walk after supper, six or eight of them, and they were singing low, the way you sing to somebody sleeping, and the song was the prettiest thing I'd ever heard. Not loud. It didn't need to be loud. It was the kind of song that makes the air itself seem to lean in, the way grass leans toward a sprinkler. My mother opened the door before they knocked. I remember that. I remember thinking she must have been listening for them, waiting, the way you wait for the ice cream truck — with that whole-body attention, that *finally.*

She was holding me already. Maybe she'd been holding me all evening. She handed me out over the threshold the way you hand someone a candle flame you're afraid will gutter — cupped, careful, precious — and Mr. Latch received me. He was younger then. His hair was dark. His voice, when he sang, came from somewhere below his chest, from under the floorboards of him, and it fit underneath the neighbors' voices the way a foundation fits under a house.

And my mother's hands let go.

I have thought about those hands letting go more times than there are stars over Marrow Creek, and every time, I come back to the same reading of it, and the reading is this: it was the hardest thing she ever did, and she did it because it was right. Because they had chosen me. Out of every child in the town — and there were children, I saw them at rehearsal, dozens of them, all the kids pressed into pews while the grown-ups sang — out of all of them, they chose *me.*

You don't get chosen every day. Most people never get chosen at all. They live and die in the general population, unremarked, unsung. But Marrow Creek had a choir, and the choir had a keeping, and the keeping needed a voice, and the voice they wanted was mine. Odessa Pell's own great-nephew — no. Wait.

Nephew. The word sits there like a stone in a shoe. Nephew. Whose nephew? Odessa's. Why do I know that? I don't remember learning it. It's just *there*, the way the knot in the porch board is there, and I can't tell anymore whether it's a memory or something the choir left in me, a fact planted like a bulb that blooms once a year whether you water it or not.

Odessa Pell. Old Miss Pell, who lives at the end of Chapel Street and smells of camphor and peppermints. If I'm her nephew then I have a surname. Pell. Wren Pell. Is that right? It fits my mouth the way a key fits a lock it was cut for — but keys fit locks they were cut for, that's the whole point of keys, that's not proof of anything.

I can't remember my surname. Let me say that plainly, since I promised not to lie to myself: I cannot remember my own last name, and I am fifty-one years old, and I have been five years old for fifty years, and somewhere in the arithmetic of that there is a sum I am not allowed to check.

* * *

The basement of the church is warm too. That surprises people when I tell them — the ones I talk to, which is mostly nobody now, mostly just the house and the girls when they were small — people expect a basement to be cold, all concrete and spider silk, but ours isn't. Ours is carpeted in sound. You walk down the stairs and the singing wraps around you before your foot touches the bottom step, and it's warm the way bathwater is warm, the way a coat fresh off the radiator is warm, and there are chairs arranged in a half-moon, and there is tea sometimes, real tea, in real cups, and there is rehearsal.

Rehearsal is the whole of it. Rehearsal is morning and noon and night, except that down there, morning and noon and night are more like suggestions than facts. Time in the keeping is a lake with no current. You float. The song holds you up. You never get tired exactly, and you never get hungry exactly, and you never sleep, but you rest, the way a violin rests in its case between movements. Fifty years of resting. Fifty years of the half-moon of chairs and the tea and the voices folding over me like blankets and Mr. Latch saying, *once more from the top, and Wren, sweetheart, give us the true note, you always find the true note.*

He calls me sweetheart. Did I mention that? He's gentle. People don't understand that about him — Xanthe certainly doesn't, Xanthe looks at him like he's a snake wearing a cardigan — but he is gentle the way water is gentle. Water finds every crack. Water never hurries. Water wins without ever raising its voice, and at the end, whatever the water fills, it calls home.

I love him. There. Another truth, laid on the pile. I love him the way you love the person who taught you to read, who tied your shoes, who told you that you mattered — because he did all of those things, or their equivalents, in the keeping. He taught me the Versicle note by note until it lived in my teeth. He told me I was chosen. He never once, in fifty years, raised his voice.

And the others — the eleven voices — they're kind to me. They take turns being near me. Some of them I know from above; Mrs. Amos who ran the bakery, her voice all flour-soft. Some of them I only know from below. They're all a little smooth-faced, like photographs left in the sun, but their voices are anything but faded. Their voices are the most alive things in Marrow Creek. Sometimes I think the voices are the people, and the faces are just coats hung up at the door.

Is that so strange? Everything I love is a little bit strange. The girls were strange. They invented me, after all — that's the official story, the story everyone tells: two little girls with a shared imaginary friend, a boy in overalls with a missing tooth, and isn't that sweet, and look, they grew out of him, the way you grow out of shoes.

But here's the thing I've never told anyone, because there was never anyone to tell: I didn't feel invented. I felt *found.* There's a difference. An imaginary friend is made up, assembled, a sock puppet of wishes. But I was already there, sitting on the porch steps, when they came around the corner of the house that first summer — I remember the gravel crunching, I remember Zephyra stopping dead and saying *who's that boy* and Xanthe saying *I don't know but he was here first* — and if they invented me, then why did I have memories they hadn't given me? Why did I remember 1974? Seven-year-olds invent friends with cool powers and pet dragons. They don't invent grief.

Unless the invention worked both ways. Unless they found me because some part of them could hear me, down in the warm, singing. Two little antennas walking around in matching dresses, picking up a signal nobody else could hear anymore. And they gave me a shape to wear up here — overalls, missing tooth, a name short enough for a child to say — the way you put a coat on a scarecrow so the crows have something to believe in.

I don't mind. Truly, I don't. It was the kindest thing anyone ever did for me, giving me a porch to sit on. Fifty years in the keeping, and the girls gave me an afternoon.

* * *

Tonight Xanthe is in the house with the lights on, going through her grandmother's things, and I can feel her moving from room to room the way you feel weather coming. She found the hymnal. I felt that too — felt it the way you feel a tooth crack, a small bright line of wrongness shooting through the warm. She has the hymnal open on the dining table and she's reading the margins, and the margins are full of her grandmother's handwriting, and the handwriting says things about the song that I don't want to know.

I flinched. I'll admit it. When she lifted it out of the piano bench, something in me flinched, some animal part that lives below the singing, and the animal part knows things the rest of me has agreed not to know. The animal part remembers my mother's hands opening. Not letting go — *opening.* Fingers uncurling one at a time, deliberate, like a flower closing in reverse. Like she was giving me away on purpose. Like she was —

No. Chosen. I was chosen. Say it again. Chosen, chosen, chosen, the word worn smooth as a river stone from fifty years of handling. A stone is smooth because the river loved it. That's what the warmth taught me. Everything rough gets smoothed by love eventually.

But the animal part keeps asking its animal questions. It wants to know why, if I was chosen, none of the other children were. It wants to know why my mother's face won't build itself out of the pieces. It wants to know why, when I try to remember the moment after the hands opened — the carrying down the walk, the singing swelling, the door of the church — the next clear picture I have is the basement, warm, safe, *already there*, as if the in-between got cut out of the film. As if somebody spliced the reel. As if the part where a five-year-old might have cried, might have reached back, might have screamed a name — the part with the surname in it — got snipped away and burned.

Songs need room to resonate. That's what Latch says. You have to damp the strings you're not using. You have to mute what would muddy the chord.

I wonder, sometimes, on the swing, in the dark, what string I used to be.

* * *

She's coming out now. Xanthe. I can hear the screen door, the hinge that needs oil, the boards announcing her — third from the left, the knotted one. She has the hymnal hugged against her chest like a shield, or a baby, or a bomb, and her face is doing the thing faces do when they've decided to stop being afraid and start being dangerous.

"Wren," she says. My name. Just the front of it, no surname trailing after, because there isn't one, because I lost it, or sold it, or it was taken as payment, or — chosen. I was chosen.

"You took longer than she did," I say, because I said it before and it was true and true things bear repeating. "Zephyra. She always came straight out. You always made me wait."

"Zephyra's dead," Xanthe says, and her voice cracks down the middle like ice on a pond in March, and beneath the crack I hear something else, something underneath her voice the way Latch's voice is underneath the neighbors', and it takes me a moment to recognize it because I've only ever heard it from the inside.

It's the true note. She's carrying the true note in her throat and doesn't know it yet.

"Wren," she says again, and sits down on the swing beside me, careful, the way you sit near a dog you don't know, "what's your last name?"

And the animal part of me stands up inside its warm coat and puts its face against the bars, and the word — the word that fits my mouth like a key, the word that smells of camphor and peppermint and 1974 — rises in me like water rising in a flooded basement, and I open my mouth to say it,

and what comes out, soft and smooth and rehearsed, in a voice that is almost mine but pitched a half-step too sweet, is:

"The choir doesn't use surnames."

* * *

Later — later, when the swing has gone still and she's gone inside and the house has swallowed its light — I sit in the dark and I do the thing I promised not to do.

I lie to myself.

Just once. Just a small one. I tell myself that tomorrow I'll remember. That the name is right there behind the splice in the film, that if I sit very still and hum the Versicle backwards — a thing I have never done, a thing that makes the animal part pace and whine — the muted string will ring again and the name will come out whole: Wren, and then the rest, the family-shaped rest, the mother-with-the-scarred-hand rest.

And underneath the lie, quiet as dust settling, the truth arranges itself into a sentence I am not ready to read:

*If I was chosen, then someone chose. And if someone chose, then my mother's hands didn't fail her. And if her hands didn't fail her, then the warmth has been telling me a story for fifty years, and I have been its best-beloved character, and characters don't get to ask what happens outside the book.*

Down in the church basement, faint through fifty feet of earth, the eleven voices begin the evening's rehearsal. The sound rises through the ground and up through the porch boards and into the swing chains, and the chains hum along, and I hum along, because that's what I'm for.

But tonight, for the first time in fifty years, I hum a half-step flat on purpose.

And far off, at the end of Chapel Street, a light comes on in a window smelling of camphor and peppermints — and somewhere below the floor of the world, something vast and patient turns its attention toward the girl in the house with the hymnal, and begins, gently, to count the days until the harvest.
