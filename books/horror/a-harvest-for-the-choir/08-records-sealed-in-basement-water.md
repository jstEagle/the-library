## 8. Records Sealed in Basement Water

The church of Marrow Creek stood at the lowest point of town, which Xanthe had always taken for piety—*build the house of God in the hollow, so all roads run downhill to it*—but standing before it now, in the gray drip of a Tuesday afternoon, she understood it differently. The church had been built in the bowl of the valley the way a cup is built to catch. Everything in Marrow Creek drained toward it. The rain. The meltwater from the ridge in spring. The creek itself, which gave the town its name and which had been quietly, patiently flooding the church basement for as long as anyone could remember.

*As long as anyone could remember.* She was beginning to hate that phrase. It was the town's favorite, its universal solvent, the answer to every question she'd asked since arriving. How long has the choir been together? *As long as anyone can remember.* When did they seal the basement? *As long as anyone can remember.* Where is the 1974 registry, the one every county office swears was transferred to the church? *Down there. As long as anyone can remember.*

The sanctuary doors were unlocked, of course. Nothing in Marrow Creek was ever locked, which she had once found charming and now found obscene. What was the point of locking a door when the whole town had already agreed?

Inside, the air was cool and candle-sweet, and the light came down through windows she had forgotten were beautiful: twelve panels, one for each voice of the founding choir, each depicting a figure with a hymnal and a face worn featureless by fifty years of weather on the outside of the glass. She had sat beneath them as a child, bored, counting the panes. Now she counted the faces. Eleven had hymnals. The twelfth—the figure nearest the baptistry—held its book open but its hands were cupped, empty, as if it had given the book away or had it taken. She couldn't remember which window that had been as a child. She couldn't remember it being different.

The basement stairs were behind the organ, down a narrow turn of steps, and at the bottom of them the water waited.

It came to mid-shin, black and perfectly still, holding the reflection of the single bulb overhead like a coin at the bottom of a well. The smell was the smell of all drowned places—cold stone, wet plaster, something mineral and old underneath, the smell of a thing that had been holding its breath for fifty years. Her phone flashlight made a small trembling circle on the water's skin.

She had told herself, on the drive over, that she was here for paper. That was all. A birth registry was a bureaucratic object; bureaucratic objects had bureaucratic explanations. The county clerk had been pleasant and useless; the state archive had lost the microfilm in a flood of its own in 1989; everyone agreed the registry had gone to the church, and everyone agreed the church basement was sealed, and no one, in fifty years, had thought this strange. Fine. She would go down, she would wade, she would find a box of mildewed paper, and she would read two names in two different inks, and one of them would be a clerical error, and she would drive home to the house where Wren was not sitting on the porch because she would make very sure not to look at the porch, and this would all resolve itself into paperwork.

That was the story. She had been telling herself stories with increasing desperation all week, and she knew it, and she went down the stairs anyway.

The water took her to the shin and then the calf, cold as a held grudge. The basement was larger than it should have been—she registered this distantly, the way you register a dream's bad architecture—corridors of shelving receding past the reach of her light, though the church above was a single modest room. The shelves were steel, municipal, the kind that hold county records in any courthouse in the state. Someone had moved a government archive into a drowning room and let the water come, and had not, in fifty years, moved it back out.

She followed the aisle markers. They were brass, screwed to the shelf-ends, and the letters on them had been polished by hands or by water: B, C, D. At D, a shelf had collapsed, its contents slumped into the water like a sleeping animal. She knelt—her knees going under, the cold climbing her thighs—and lifted the nearest box.

The cardboard sloughed at her touch. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that someone had taken care with, the paper had survived. Ledgers. Registers. She set them on a dry shelf above the waterline and opened the first with fingers she made herself keep steady.

Marriage records. 1951 to 1969. She put it aside.

The second was heavier, its cover stamped in faded gold: *Vital Records, Marrow Creek District, 1970–1979.*

She opened it to 1974 the way you open a door you already know what's behind.

The pages were ruled and formal, a clerk's careful hand, and there—under the date she had grown up knowing, the date that had been her birthday for thirty years, October the twelfth—two entries.

*Reyes, infant, female. Born 3:14 a.m. Delivered dead. No name assigned.*

*Reyes, infant, female. Born 3:41 a.m. Alive. Name: Zephyra.*

She read it four times. The rational explanation stood beside her in the dark, dripping, offering itself: a transcription error, a swapped column, some tired clerk's slip of the pen. But the entries weren't crossed or corrected. They sat there in their ruled lines, neat as hymn verses, and beneath the second one, in a different ink, older, brown, a third line had been added in a hand she knew—knew from birthday cards, from the margins of cookbooks, from the flyleaf of the hymnal locked in the piano bench.

Her grandmother's hand. And it did not correct anything. It said only:

*One voice, in trade. Witnessed.*

* * *

The ledger was on the next shelf up, dry, as if it had been placed there recently, as if it had been waiting at exactly the height of a kneeling woman's reach. Its cover was not municipal. It was black leather gone soft as skin, and it had no title, only a small stamped device on the front: twelve lines radiating from a circle, like rays, like pipes, like the strings of some instrument she didn't know the name of.

She opened it and the first page was a list of dates, one every generation or so, going back further than the town's charter—1798, 1831, 1876, 1921—and beside each date, in the same brown ink that had faded to almost nothing, the same entry, over and over, in variations of the same six words:

*One voice received, in trade.*

*One voice, in trade, willingly given.*

*One voice, in trade. The town endures.*

And at the bottom of the 1974 page, the entry she had already read in the registry, written out in full now, in her grandmother's handwriting, in ink that had not faded at all:

*One voice, in trade. The living child given; the dead child named for the living, that the debt be paid and no questions asked. Witnessed and sworn, M. Reyes.*

Below the signature, a line she had to hold the page close to the light to read, the letters small and pressed so hard they'd torn the paper slightly:

*Forgive me. There was no other arithmetic.*

Xanthe knelt in the black water and did not move for a long time. The bulb overhead buzzed. Somewhere in the flooded corridors, water shifted against a shelf with a sound like a page turning.

She had come down here to kill the last rational explanation, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that it was already dead. It had died the moment her grandmother's hand appeared in two documents fifty years apart, saying the same thing in two different grammars: a clerk's line and a confession. There was no clerical error. There was no swapped column. There was a night in October 1974, and two infants, and a debt, and a woman who had done arithmetic with her own granddaughters and signed her name to it.

*The living child given; the dead child named for the living.*

She said it aloud, and the water carried her voice away down the corridors, and something far off in the dark—something patient, something that had been listening to this basement for fifty years—carried it back to her, changed slightly, the way a tune changes when it passes through another throat.

*The dead child named for the living.*

She was Xanthe Reyes. She had been Xanthe Reyes for thirty years. She had a birthdate and a birth certificate and a childhood with a swimming pool and a school in Tucson and a scar on her knee from a bicycle. All of it belonged to a dead baby. She had inherited a dead baby's life the way you inherit a coat, and she had worn it so long she had never once felt the wrongness of the fit—except that she had, hadn't she, all her life, in small ways. The name on the stone. Wren saying *you took longer than she did.* The way her mother had sometimes looked at her across a dinner table with an expression she'd filed, all these years, under *grief*, and which now re-filed itself, horribly, under *accounting.*

And Zephyra. Zephyra who was not in the grave. Zephyra who was—her mind skidded off the thought and she forced it back—Zephyra who was the one who had been *given.* The living child. The voice, in trade. Who had died three years ago and gone, not into the ground, but back into the keeping of the thing her own grandmother had handed her to, before she was a week old. Whose diary was written in Xanthe's handwriting. Whose room smelled of perfume and had a bed made as if slept in.

*One voice received.*

Wren had said it on the porch, gentle as weather: the choir took a child in 1974 and never gave her back. Xanthe had thought he meant a child of the town. Some other mother's baby. Some name in a ledger that meant nothing to her.

She looked down at the open ledger in her hands, at the brown ink, at the signature of the woman who had raised her, and she understood that the child of 1974 had a name, and that she had been saying it her whole life, and that it was her own.

The water moved. Not the far-off shifting of shelves—closer, in the aisle behind her, a long slow displacement, as of something standing up from a kneeling position. She did not turn around. She was ashamed, later, of how completely she failed to turn around; some instinct older than courage told her that whatever was behind her was only there to look, that it had come down the stairs of its own flooded corridors to see whether the paper had finally been read, and that it would be satisfied, and go.

The cold of the water climbed her body like a hand taking her measure. In the circle of her flashlight, on the wet shelf, the ledger lay open to 1974, and below her grandmother's confession, in ink that had not been there when she knelt down—she was almost sure, she would never be sure—a new line had been added, in the same brown hand, the same careful clerk's letters, as if the ledger had simply continued writing itself:

*The debt stands. One voice, in trade, remains outstanding.*

*The choir is patient. The choir remembers the name.*

She closed the ledger. She climbed the stairs with the registry page torn out and folded against her heart like a letter she was afraid to open twice, and behind her, in the dark under the church, the water settled back into its perfect stillness, holding the reflection of the bulb, holding everything, giving nothing back—except that as she reached the top step she heard, faint and clear, from the flooded corridors below, a single voice beginning to hum.

It was a tune she knew. She had known it all her life. It was the tune her grandmother used to hum while shelling peas, while winding the hall clock, while rocking two identical baby girls on the porch of the drowning house—except that now, hearing it rise up out of the black water, Xanthe could hear what she had never been able to hear as a child: that it was not a lullaby at all. It was the Harvest Versicle, sung slow, sung soft, the way you sing to something you are trying to keep asleep.

And underneath her grandmother's remembered voice, underneath the water, underneath everything, a second voice joined in—small, patient, terribly familiar—and Xanthe stood at the top of the basement stairs with her hand pressed to her mouth, recognizing it.

It was her own.
