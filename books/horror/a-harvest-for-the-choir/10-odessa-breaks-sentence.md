## 10. Odessa Breaks Sentence

Odessa Pell had spent fifty-one years learning how to hold a sentence in her mouth without swallowing it.

She'd gotten good at it. Better than good. She could sit in the Blue Plate Diner at her usual table by the window, drink coffee gone cold because refills meant conversation, and let the rehearsed words sit behind her teeth like stones in a creek bed: *The choir was a kindness.* Nine words. She'd said them so many times they had worn smooth, the way a river stone wears, the way a hymn wears, the way a lie wears when a whole town says it in unison every Sunday for half a century. She could say it to the mailman. She could say it to the county assessor. She could say it to a grieving woman buying pie she wouldn't eat, and watch that woman's face go tight with the particular loneliness of hearing a door close in a house you thought was yours.

But her mouth was not what it had been. That was the thing nobody warned you about, getting old. Everyone warned you about the heart, the hips, the memory. Nobody told you that the muscles of concealment go too — that one morning you'd find you could no longer hold your face the way you used to, that the sentences you'd kept for decades would start to leak out of you like water through a cracked pitcher.

The doctor in Corvallis had used the word *pancreatic* in the gentle way doctors use terrible words, and Odessa had driven home through the fog along the creek road and felt, for the first time in fifty-one years, *light*.

Dying was a kind of permission. She understood that now. Fear had been the mortar holding the sentence in her mouth, and the sentence had been holding the town together, and the town had been holding the thing in the church fed, and she was going to die anyway, so what was the mortar for anymore? What was any of it for?

She was past fear. That was the phrase she used, sitting in her kitchen with her hands around a cup of tea she couldn't taste anymore. *Past fear.* It sounded almost holy. It sounded like something you'd put on a stone.

And then, on the third Tuesday of October, with the fog lying on Marrow Creek like a shroud laid out ready, the Reyes girl knocked on her door.

* * *

Odessa had been watching her for days, the way she watched all of them — the ones who came back. There was always one. Every generation, someone came back to Marrow Creek with questions in their pockets, and the town closed around them like a fist around a moth, gentle and total, and the questions went unanswered, and the person either left or stayed, and if they stayed, they learned the sentence.

She'd watched Xanthe Reyes through her kitchen window, across the street and down two houses, where the Reyes place sat with its porch swing and its peeling paint and its forty years of held breath. The girl had arrived in a car full of boxes and had stood in the yard a long time before going in, looking up at the house the way you look at a face in a coffin — trying to memorize it, or trying to convince yourself it's really them.

And last night, Odessa had watched her come out at eleven o'clock in the fog, in her coat over her nightclothes, and stand in the middle of the street with her head tilted, listening. Listening to the house. Odessa knew what the house sounded like at night. She'd grown up two doors from it. She knew the pipes-that-weren't-pipes, the settling-that-wasn't-settling, the small wet knocking that came from inside the walls on the nights before a full moon, like something with small patient hands.

The girl had stood there a long time. Then she'd gone back inside, and Odessa had sat in her dark kitchen and thought: *she hears it, and she hasn't run yet, and she hasn't learned the sentence, and I am dying, and my brother has been in that church for fifty-one years, and I am so tired, Lord, I am so tired of holding it.*

So when the knock came — two soft raps, the way you knock on a door when you already know the person inside has been waiting for you — Odessa said, "It's open," before the third rap could land.

* * *

The girl looked like both of them, Odessa thought, watching her come into the kitchen. That was the thing that had always unsettled the town, though none of them would say it: the Reyes twins had looked like each other the way two halves of a torn photograph look like each other, and after the funeral, when Xanthe came back for the estate, people had flinched. Seeing her walk down Main Street had been like seeing the dead girl walking, and the town had smoothed its face and said *so sorry for your loss* in the rehearsed way, and Odessa had watched the girl register that smoothness and file it away, the way her sister used to file things away, the way both of them had, even at seven, even at ten, two sharp-eyed girls who knew they were being lied to and didn't yet know by whom.

"You're Odessa Pell," the girl said. "You knew my grandmother."

"Ruthie Reyes could sing the paint off a wall," Odessa said. "Sit down, child. The chair by the stove. It's the warm one."

Xanthe sat. She had her sister's way of sitting, spine straight, hands folded, like a girl in an old photograph. She had a canvas bag with her, and Odessa could see the corner of a hymnal inside it, green cloth, gold lettering worn to ghosts. Ruthie's hymnal. So the girl had found the piano bench. So the girl had read the margins.

"You went to the diner," Odessa said. "Tuesday. You asked about 1974."

"I asked a lot of people."

"And what did they tell you?"

Xanthe's mouth did something complicated. "They told me the choir was a kindness."

"And couldn't say a kindness to whom." Odessa nodded slowly. Her hands, around the teacup, were trembling — not with fear, she noted with distant interest, but with the effort of *not saying it yet*. Fifty-one years of practice, and now the muscle was failing her at the exact moment she wanted it to. "I said it too. Fifty years. I said it so many times I dreamed in it. You want to know the trick of it, girl? The sentence isn't a lie. That's what makes it work. The choir *was* a kindness. It's just not a kindness to us."

The fog pressed against the kitchen window. Somewhere down the street, the Reyes house made a sound — Odessa saw the girl hear it, saw her whole body go still, a deer's stillness, a listener's stillness.

"You hear it too," Xanthe said. Not a question.

"I've heard it since I was nine years old," Odessa said. "Sit still now. I'm going to tell you a thing, and once I tell it, I can't untell it, and neither can you. So you decide right now. You can walk out that door and go sell that house and never come back, and live a long life with a hole in it shaped like your sister, and that's a life, plenty of people live it. Or you can sit there and hear me, and after you hear me you'll be a citizen of a different country, and there's no border crossing back."

The stove ticked. The fog breathed against the glass. Xanthe Reyes looked at the door, and Odessa watched her look at it — watched her measure it, the way you measure a thing you're giving up — and then the girl turned back and folded her hands again and said, "Tell me."

* * *

"My brother's name was Elias," Odessa said. "Elias Achebe Pell. Our mother married a man from Lagos and buried him when we were small, and it was her and us two after that, in the house where the Hendersons live now. Elias was the singer. Not like the choir sings — not that smooth, folded, agreeable thing they do on Sundays. Elias sang like a bird fights. He'd stand out on the porch in summer and sing up at the dark like he was daring it to answer, and our mother would laugh and say *that boy is going to sing himself right out of this world.*"

Odessa's voice had gone somewhere far away. She let it. It was a long road back to 1974 and she intended to walk it slowly.

"He was twenty-two. He worked at the mill and sang in the choir because that's what you did, that's what everyone did, the choir was the town, the town was the choir. And in the spring of '74 the choir got a new director. Mr. Latch. Soft little man, cardigan the color of oatmeal, voice like a hand on your shoulder. He'd smile at you and you'd find yourself agreeing to things. Whole town was like that within a month. Agreeable. You'd be mid-argument with your husband and Latch would come into the store and by the time he left you couldn't remember what the argument was, only that it didn't matter, only that harmony mattered. That was the word he used. Harmony. He said it the way other men say *God*."

Xanthe had not moved. Her stillness was its own kind of singing, Odessa thought. The girl was listening with everything she had.

"That June, the choir started rehearsing a new piece. *The Harvest Versicle.* Latch wrote it — said he did — and they rehearsed it in the church basement, and the rehearsals went long, and people came out of them looking washed, looking smooth, looking happy in a way that made the hair on my arms stand up. And Elias —" her voice caught, an old rusted gate — "Elias started saying the music was wrong. Not bad. *Wrong.* He said there was a second song inside it, folded up, like a letter inside an envelope, and the envelope part was pretty but the letter inside was an invoice. That's the word he used. My brother, twenty-two years old, standing in my mother's kitchen with his hands shaking, saying *Odessa, that song is an invoice, and I don't know who it's billed to, but I know it isn't paid.*"

"And then?" Xanthe's voice was very quiet.

"And then in September, the choir took a child."

The fog against the window seemed to lean in.

"The Pell boy. Wren. Odile's boy — my cousin's son, seven years old, sweet as bread. He went missing on a Sunday. Whole town turned out to search, and I remember — I remember this like it's happening now — I remember standing in the churchyard and realizing that nobody was *worried*. Two hundred people combing the creek and the woods and not one person crying. Not one. Everybody moving like the search was a formality, like a thing you do with your body while the rest of you waits. And that Sunday after, the choir sang the Versicle, and I heard —" Odessa stopped. She made herself finish. "I heard Wren's voice in it. A seven-year-old's voice, under the choir, under the organ, under everything, and I was the only one in that church who flinched. And I knew. The way you know in a dream. I knew they'd taken him, and I knew the town had agreed to it, and I knew the agreement was older than all of us."

"And your brother?"

"Elias went into that basement the next week." Odessa set down the teacup because her hands had begun to shake in earnest. "He said he was going to sing the boy out. He said there was a way — that Ruthie Reyes had found it, that she'd written it into the margins of the music, a counter-song, a song to unbind — and he was going to stand in the keeping-place and sing it, and walk out with Wren Pell on his shoulder like a bird coming home. He kissed our mother's cheek at the door. He took his tuning fork." She laughed, a small terrible sound. "A tuning fork. Our mother's, from her church days, A above middle C. He said if he ever got lost in the music, the fork would show him the true pitch home. He said pitch was the one thing the choir couldn't fake, because pitch was *honest*, pitch was mathematics, and mathematics didn't care whose harmony it broke."

The kitchen was very cold now, though the stove was hot. The fog had thickened until the window was white as a closed eye.

"He never came out," Odessa said. "And the town said he'd run off, said boys do, said the mill was no life anyway. And I stood at his — at the empty grave we made, because there was nothing to bury, we buried his coat — and I said the sentence. *The choir was a kindness.* I said it over his empty grave, girl. I was twenty-four years old and I said it, because Latch was standing beside me with his hand on my shoulder, and the whole town was singing, and I could not —" her voice broke clean through — "I could not make my mouth disobey."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere far off, the church bell rang a single hour, and Odessa watched the girl count it — watched her lips move — and thought, *Ruthie's blood, sure enough. Ruthie counted everything too.*

"The choir keeps the dead," Odessa said at last, when her voice would hold. "That's the trade. That's the kindness, if you want to call it that. One voice per generation, taken living, and in exchange the town's dead don't — *scatter*. They stay near. They stay in harmony. The old ones say it was a mercy, once. Before the choir, before 1874 and all that, the dead of Marrow Creek went where dead go, and the town grieved and got on with it. But somebody — a long time back — decided grief was a debt, and found a way to make the dead *stay*. Singing. Kept. Not at rest and not gone. Held in the harmony like flies in amber." She looked at Xanthe, and let her see everything in her face. "Your sister is in that keeping. And so is my brother. Fifty-one years, and he is still twenty-two, and he is still singing, and I have walked past that church every Sunday of my life knowing it, and said the sentence, and kept the sentence, because that's the other part of the trade — the living keep the quiet, and the dead keep the town, and nobody, nobody is free."

Xanthe's hands had come apart in her lap. Her face had gone the color of the fog.

"You're telling me she's *aware*."

"I'm telling you she's *singing*," Odessa said. "And that she has been trying to reach you for three years, and that every noise you've heard in that house — every pipe, every raccoon, every settling board — is her, knocking on the inside of the world, trying to get a message to the one person left who'd know her knock from anyone's." She leaned forward. The effort of the leaning was enormous; she felt it in her chest like a hand. "And I am telling you that I am dying, girl, and that dying has made me brave in the cheap way, the way that doesn't cost anything anymore — but brave is brave. And I have something for you. Elias left it behind on the kitchen table the night he walked into that church, like he knew. Like he was leaving directions."

* * *

She made Xanthe help her up the stairs, which was humiliating and which she did not apologize for. The bedroom at the top of the stairs was full of forty years of kept things, and she went to the closet, and behind the winter coats, in a hatbox that had once held a Sunday hat the color of plums, she found it wrapped in a choir robe that no longer fit anyone.

The tuning fork was cold and heavier than it looked, steel gone soft-grey with handling, and stamped on the handle in her mother's careful script: *A — 440 — for Elias, who always finds the true note.*

"Elias Achebe Pell," Odessa said, pressing it into the girl's hands and closing her fingers around it one by one, the way you close a dead man's eyes. "Say it. Say his name. Nobody in this town has said it out loud in fifty-one years, and I'll be damned — I'll be *damned* — if I go into the ground without hearing it said."

"Elias Achebe Pell," Xanthe said, and her voice cracked on it, and the tuning fork in her fist seemed to hum, very faintly, though she hadn't struck it, though nothing had touched it, a single honest note rising out of the steel like breath from a mouth.

Odessa sat down hard on the bed and wept — not the smooth, folded, agreeable weeping the choir allowed, but the old ugly kind, the kind with pitch in it, the kind that breaks harmony.

"There's a song inside the Versicle," she said, when she could. "Ruthie found it. Elias died trying to sing it. And there's a girl in that church who's been rehearsing you since before you were born." She wiped her face with the choir robe. "Now take the fork and get out of my house before I lose the nerve to have given it to you — and girl — *girl* — whatever you hear in those walls tonight, don't you dare explain it away. That's the first thing they'll ask of you. It's the first thing they always ask."
