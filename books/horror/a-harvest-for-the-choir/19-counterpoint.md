## 19. Counterpoint

The tuning fork was warm.

That was the first thing I noticed, standing in the pew with my fist closed around it while five hundred people swayed like wheat in a wind that wasn't there. Odessa's brother had carried it in his pocket for fifty years before the choir took him, and all that living had sunk into the metal somehow, a warmth no church this cold had any right to hold. It pressed against my palm like a small animal, like something alive and waiting to be let go.

The Versicle rose around me in its fiftieth-anniversary arrangement, fuller than it had been on Sunday, richer, the eleven voices of the choir braided with the congregation until there was no telling where the choir ended and the town began. That was the point. That had always been the point. Mr. Latch stood before them with his hands lifted, not conducting so much as *tending*, the way you'd tend a fire you'd kept burning your whole life, and his face wore its soft certainty like a lampshade over something brighter and much less kind.

Above us the walls breathed. I had stopped pretending they didn't. The plaster swelled on the inhale of the hymn and drew thin on the exhale, and the stained-glass saints leaned inward by degrees too small for any eye that hadn't spent three weeks learning to see Marrow Creek truly. The candle flames bent toward the altar as if pulled on strings. Somewhere under the floor, under the flooded basement and the sealed records and the ledger with my grandmother's signature drying brown at its bottom, something vast turned over in its sleep of fifty years and prepared to be fed.

*One voice, freely given,* Latch had said, pressing the casserole dish into my hands like communion. *One willing throat, and every debt is paid. Your sister walks out whole. The town sleeps easy. No one ever has to know what they agreed to.*

He had even left the contract on my kitchen table, one page, elegant as a wedding invitation. He was gentle. That was the worst of him. He had never once raised his voice, because he had never once needed to; the whole town sang for him.

I looked down at my hands. The fork. The hymnal, open on my lap to the page my grandmother had bled her corrections into, the margins dense as roots, dated the night of the swap. Fifty years ago she had written the way out and then buried it inside the thing it was written against, hiding the unbinding song in the binding one the way you hide a key under the doormat of the house that's holding you prisoner. She had never sung it. She had chosen the swap instead, chosen to bury one granddaughter's name under the other's and carry the secret like a stone in her chest until it killed her slow. I used to think that was cowardice. Standing here, feeling the hymn pull at the marrow of my bones like tide pulling at sand, I understood it was arithmetic. She couldn't sing it alone. One voice against the choir is just a note out of place, and the harmony closes over it like water.

But she'd left instructions anyway. For whoever came after. For me.

*The third bar of the seventh verse,* her margin read, in handwriting that shook only once. *Where the melody lies about itself. Strike true and sing what is written in the blood-margin. You will know the bar because the room will go quiet enough to hear your own heart lie.*

The congregation swayed. Their faces were smooth and absent, Sunday-smooth, harvest-smooth, and among them I found the ones I knew: Odessa Pell, ninety-one years old and dying and past fear, sitting upright in the third pew with her chin lifted, the only face in the building still wearing an expression. She caught my eye. She did not nod. Nodding was agreement, and agreement was what had fed this thing for half a century. She simply looked at me the way you look at someone stepping off a ledge, and then she closed her eyes and went silent inside the song, a hole in the harmony shaped like courage.

And beside me, in the pew where he had sat every rehearsal since I came back to Marrow Creek, Wren sat with his hands folded in his lap, overalls buttoned, gap-toothed, seven years old forever, singing.

That was the part I hadn't planned for. Not his presence — I'd known he would be here, drawn to the choir like a river drawn to its mouth. What I hadn't planned for was his voice. It threaded through the eleventh voice of the choir, underneath it, the twelfth voice I'd heard coming out of my own mouth a week ago, and it was beautiful. It was the most beautiful sound in the building. Fifty years of being kept had polished it to something that hurt to hear, and he sang it gladly, because he believed — had been taught to believe, had been *warmed* into believing — that he was chosen, that the keeping was a kindness, that the singing was home.

*You'll end me too,* he had said when I told him, or tried to tell him, what the counter-hymn would do. He hadn't refused to help after that. He had done something worse. He had looked at me with those unchanged eyes and said, *Okay. If it frees Zephyra, okay.* As if he were a tool. As if fifty stolen years had taught him he was furniture that could sing.

The hymn climbed toward the seventh verse.

My heart was going so hard I could feel it in my teeth. I thought about the price — I made myself think about it, because Latch's mercy lived on people not thinking. The counter-hymn cost everything it saved. My grandmother's notes were clear about that: unbinding was not gentle. To tear a harmony woven through a whole town, you had to put yourself into the tearing. Half my memories, traded in the singing — that was the toll the margins named, though they named it sideways, the way frightened people write. *She who sings the unbinding sings away the self that bound her.* I would walk out of this church with holes in me shaped like my own life. Maybe my mother's voice. Maybe the summer we were nine. Maybe my own birthday, my own name, the face I saw in mirrors. I would keep Zephyra — whatever was left of her to keep — and lose the rest, and there was no version of the arithmetic where that didn't feel like dying a little, dressed up as rescue.

And Wren. Wren mid-note, Wren beginning, the moment the keeping collapsed, to live his stolen fifty years in however long he had left. A week, maybe. A day. Long enough to age, to grieve, to remember his mother's hands — and then to stop. The song wouldn't kill him. It would just give him back everything at once, all the time at speed, and time is heavy, and he was small.

*Don't sing their song,* Zephyra had scratched in the frost, and I had almost missed the second line, fainter, dug in with something desperate: *sing YOURS.*

The seventh verse began.

Eleven voices, five hundred voices, one voice underneath, and above it all Latch's hands rising like a benediction, and the walls swelling, and the candles bowing, and the floor beneath my feet pulsing faintly, terribly, like the heartbeat of something that had eaten a town and called it harmony.

I waited for the bar.

*You will know the bar because the room will go quiet enough to hear your own heart lie.*

It happened between one note and the next. The Versicle has a seam in it — I'd felt it on Sunday without understanding, a place where the melody bends to hide what it's built on, a false cadence, a lie set to music. When the choir reached it, every voice in the building smoothed over the seam together, glossing the lie, and for exactly one beat the truth underneath was exposed: silence. A single measure of raw quiet, the machinery showing its gears, the room holding its breath around the place where fifty years ago a child had been carried down basement stairs by singing neighbors while his mother's hands were pried open one finger at a time.

In that measure, my heart sounded like a hammer on a coffin lid.

I struck the fork against the pew rail.

The note it gave was small. That's what I remember most, afterward — how small it was. A single clean tone, silver-bright, the pitch Odessa's brother had tuned it to the week before they took him, the pitch of a boy's speaking voice, preserved in metal like a flower in glass. It cut through the resumed hymn the way a needle cuts through silk: almost nothing, and then everything snagged on it and tore.

I stood up.

Latch saw me. Of course he saw me; there was nowhere in that harmony he didn't see. His hands paused in their tending and his head turned and his face did something I will remember as long as I remember anything, which might not be long: it softened further, if such a thing was possible, softened into genuine sorrow, the sorrow of a man watching someone choose the hard road when the easy one was right there, paved, lit, leading somewhere warm. His mouth moved. No sound came out — the hymn had swallowed all other sound in the building — but I read it easily, because he'd been saying it all along, gently, patiently, with casserole and contracts and open doors:

*You don't have to.*

*I know,* I mouthed back. And I sang.

Not their song. Mine. My grandmother's, hidden in the blood-margins, the counter-hymn she'd built from the Versicle's own bones — same notes, inverted, the binding melody turned inside out like a glove, so that singing it into the hymn was not fighting the harmony but *unpicking* it, stitch by stitch, from within. I sang the correction. I sang the thing the founding choir had voted down in 1974, the thing my grandmother had written at midnight with a newborn screaming in one room and a silence growing in the other, the song that says *let go, let go, the dead are owed nothing but their names.*

For one bar, nothing happened. I sang alone into five hundred voices and felt like a girl shouting into a flood, and the hymn poured over me, and I thought: *she knew, she knew it takes more than one, that's why she never—*

Then the fork's note found me.

It had been hanging in the air all this time, that small silver tone, patient as the dead, and when my voice rose it bent toward me, tuned to me, and my single thread of melody suddenly had a spine. The counter-hymn stood up inside the Versicle like a skeleton inside a body, and the body realized what had been living in it all along.

The harmony tore.

There is no honest way to describe the sound except as tearing — fabric, flesh, the difference didn't matter anymore. Five hundred voices hit the false cadence and the false cadence was gone, unpicked, and instead of smoothing over the seam they fell into it. The congregation's faces broke open all at once, smoothness cracking like river ice, and what came up from underneath fifty years of rehearsed amnesia was not screaming, not yet — it was *remembering*, which sounds worse, which is worse, five hundred people remembering in the same instant every sentence they had agreed not to finish. *A kindness. A kindness to—* Odessa's brother's name left thirty mouths at once like a bullet leaving a gun.

The walls stopped breathing and started convulsing. Plaster rained. The stained-glass saints snapped upright, appalled, and the candles guttered flat, and under the floor the vast sleeping thing rolled onto its back and howled through the foundations, and its howl was made of voices — I could hear them individually now, the keeping collapsing, the choir's treasury spilling open. Fifty years of taken voices, released mid-keening, pouring up through the floorboards and out through the broken windows and into the night over Marrow Creek like birds off a drowned roof. I heard a child's laugh in the exodus. I heard an old man humming. I heard, unmistakable, threaded through all of it, my sister — not words, not yet, just relief, pure and enormous, a held breath finally let go.

And Mr. Latch unraveled.

He didn't fall. Falling is too simple. He came apart the way a chord comes apart when you lift fingers off the keys one at a time — his outline fraying into strands, and each strand was a voice, and I recognized some of them. The director who'd signed the first ledger in 1974. A woman's alto from the founding choir. A man who had to be Odessa's brother, young, terrified, forgiving. Voice after voice peeling off him and going free or going out, I couldn't tell which, and Latch stood in the middle of his own unwinding with his hands still raised, still tending, still trying to hold the hymn together with gestures alone, and his face — his gentle, certain, lampshaded face — was the last thing to go. Just before it did, he looked at me. Not with hatred. With something so much harder to carry: with the expression of a man who genuinely, sincerely believes he is being murdered by a mercy.

Then he was voices, and then he was drafts, and then the church was full of ordinary midnight air and rain-sound through broken glass, and five hundred people stood swaying no longer, gripping pews, weeping, awake, alive, and the silence after fifty years of song was so total I could hear my own blood.

I had done it. I had sung the song and the keeping was down and Zephyra was free—

Wren made a sound.

Small. In all that aftermath, in all that weeping and waking, it was the smallest sound in the building and the only one I heard. I turned and he was still in the pew, hands folded, overalls buttoned, and he was looking at his own hands with an expression I had never seen on his unchanged face, because his face was changing.

It started at his eyes. The seven-year-old roundness deepened, lengthened, gathered shadows and history the way a landscape gathers weather. His jaw squared. Stubble ghosted across it, darkened, retreated as the years moved through him faster than breath — fifteen, twenty-five, forty — his overalls straining, splitting at the seams, becoming too small for a body catching up on fifty years in as many seconds. His hair thickened and curled and grayed at the temples and thinned. His hands, his folded hands, grew knuckles and veins and a wedding-ring-shaped band of pale skin that ached to look at, a life that had been owed and never lived, and through all of it his eyes stayed on mine, wide, astonished, and wet.

"Xanthe," he said, and his voice was a man's voice, cracked and deep and breaking. "Oh. Oh, I'm so tall."

He laughed. It was a terrible, wonderful sound, a boy's delight wearing a grown man's chest, and then the laughter folded into something else, because the memories were arriving too — I could see them landing, blow after blow after gift after blow, his mother's hands (not letting go, never letting go, being pried), a surname surfacing in him like a body rising in water, and he said it out loud, tasting it, weeping:

"Pell. Wren Achebe Pell. Auntie 'Dessa's—" and he turned, aged and ancient and nine seconds old, toward the third pew, where Odessa Pell stood gripping the wood with both hands, staring at the grown nephew she had lost in 1974, alive in front of her, dying in front of her, both at once, both finally true.

He reached for her hand. She reached for his. And outside the broken windows, over the drowning town, dawn was still hours away — but Wren Achebe Pell had fifty years to live, and I already knew, looking at the gray in his hair and the light in his fading eyes, that he was going to need every minute of the night to spend them.
