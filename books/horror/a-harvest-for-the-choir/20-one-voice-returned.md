## 20. One Voice, Returned

The last note of the counter-hymn left Xanthe's mouth and entered mine, or perhaps it had always been there, waiting fifty years in the dark of my throat like a seed in frozen ground. I felt it crack open.

I was standing in the choir loft, and then I was standing in a church basement in 1974, and then I was standing in both places at once, which is what it is to be unbound: every year arrives together, the way all the notes of a chord arrive together, and you must learn to hear them separately or go mad from the crowding.

I heard them separately. That was the first mercy. The second was that they hurt.

* * *

Pain came first as cold. For fifty years I had been warm — that was the story, the only story, the one the choir sang into me until it was the shape of my whole self: *you were chosen, you were kept, it is warm where you are.* And now the warmth peeled away like a bandage and underneath was November air, the smell of river mud and coal smoke, the particular gray light of Marrow Creek at dusk, and I understood that I had never been warm at all. I had been sung warm. There is a difference, and the difference has teeth.

My hands changed first. They had been seven-year-old hands for so long that watching them was like watching a time-lapse film of rot run backward — knuckles swelling, veins rising, skin going papery, then spotted, then slack. Hair sprouted silver at my temples while I watched. My overalls, which had fit me since 1974 because nothing had ever been allowed to change, split at the seams as my body remembered being twelve, nineteen, thirty-four, sixty-one, all in the space between two breaths.

I sat down on the choir loft floor because my knees told me to, and I began, without deciding to, to weep.

Not the crying of a child. I had done plenty of that, though no one had ever wiped my eyes. This was something older and more structural, grief coming up through me the way water comes up through a floor when the river rises — not from anywhere, just *up*, from all directions, from every year I had been denied it. Fifty years of tears arriving on schedule, all at once, and my old man's chest heaving with them, and somewhere below me in the sanctuary the congregation waking up one pew at a time like a field of flowers opening, each face breaking open into its own private weather.

Someone screamed. Someone laughed. A woman called a name — *Dell? Dell!* — and a man answered from across the room, a name neither of them had spoken aloud in half a century. The harmony was gone and what rushed into the space it left was noise, beautiful ordinary human noise, sobbing and calling out and the scrape of pews as people stood up who had been sitting still inside themselves for years.

And I sat in the loft above all of it, an old man in a child's overalls, crying for my mother.

That was when I remembered her properly. Not the way the choir let me remember her — hands letting go, a smile, a blessing — but the true way, the way that had been folded up small and hidden in me like contraband.

She did not let go.

* * *

Here is what actually happened, and now that nothing is singing over it, I can tell it straight:

It was the third of November, 1974, and I was seven years old, and my mother held onto me so hard she left bruises shaped like her fingers on my arms, and I wore those bruises for three days in the church basement until they faded, and she was still outside. She stood outside the church for six days. Odessa told me this later, or will tell me, or told me once already in a life I haven't lived yet — time is loose now, loose as a tooth — but I know it the way you know a scar's shape under your sleeve. Six days she stood on the steps while the choir sang inside, and the song said *he is chosen, he is kept, it is warm where he is,* and she put her hands over her ears and she did not believe it, not for one hour of those six days.

On the seventh day Mr. Latch came out to her with his soft voice and his patient face, and whatever he said to her, she went home. Not because she believed him. Because she had another child at home, my cousin's little ones under her roof after their father left, and a body can only stand against a hymn for so long before the hymn starts singing through your own mouth. She went home and she wrote letters. Eleven of them. To the county, to the bishop, to a newspaper in the city. None of them were answered, because the answers were sung away too, or filed, or eaten.

Her name was Constance Achebe Pell. She made sweet potato pie with nutmeg grated fresh, and she hummed while she cooked, off-key, gloriously off-key, because she had never been able to carry a tune and had decided long ago that this was God's joke and hers too. When I was very small she would dance me around the kitchen to her own bad humming and say, *Wren, if anyone ever asks you to sing perfect, you run.*

They asked me to sing perfect. I didn't run. I was seven, and the neighbors' faces were kind, and the basement was lit with candles, and I thought it was a birthday party.

I remember everything now. That is the cost and the gift of it, arriving together like everything else. I remember the candles and the kindness and the moment the song closed over my head like river water, and I remember how quickly it stopped hurting, and I understand now that that was the cruelest part — not the taking but the anesthetic, the warmth, the fifty years of *chosen, kept, warm* played on a loop inside me until I loved the thing that ate me. I defended them. In the loft, in the dark, all those years, I defended them to myself. I thought I was lucky.

I was not lucky. I was stolen. And my mother fought, and lost, and spent the rest of her life humming badly in a kitchen with one chair too many, and died in 1989 with my name still set at her table.

I know all of this and I am glad to know it, even though knowing it is like swallowing broken glass. Warmth that lies is colder than any winter. Truth hurts, and the hurt is the proof you're real.

* * *

I aged for six days.

It sounds impossible. It was impossible; that was rather the point of me. But the choir had kept fifty years of me in escrow, all the birthdays I should have had, stacked up like unopened mail, and when the binding tore, the mail all came at once. So I lived fast and shallow through it — a week of accelerated boyhood, adolescence, the awkwardness of seventeen, the hungers of twenty-five, the tiredness of forty — and each age passed through me leaving sediment, leaving knowledge, leaving ache. I woke Monday with a young man's voice and Tuesday with middle-aged regrets about things that had never happened to me and felt, nonetheless, genuinely mine. By Thursday I needed glasses. By Friday my hip ached when the rain came, and I stood at Odessa's kitchen window watching the rain come and aching, and I have never in my long short life been happier.

Odessa took me in. Of course she did. She was ninety-one and dying — she told me so plainly, the way dying people get permission to be plain — and she cleared out the back bedroom that had been her brother Dell's, and that was the room I'd have slept in, in the life I was owed. Dell. Her brother, taken in '74 too, though not kept the way I was kept; the ledger wanted one voice and got two names, and Dell went into the ground with the wrong date on his stone, and Odessa had carried that arithmetic alone for fifty years.

"You're Achebe," she said the first night, sitting across from me with her hands wrapped around a mug of tea she wouldn't drink. "You're my sister-in-law's boy. You're Wren Achebe Pell." She said it like she was setting a bone. Like saying it would make it true, and she was right, because each time she said it something in my chest settled a little further into place, a joint reducing, a name finding its socket.

"I couldn't remember," I said. "For fifty years I couldn't remember my own last name."

"I know, baby." Her voice cracked down the middle. "I know. We all forgot you. That's the sin of this town, Wren. Not the taking. The forgetting after."

"We didn't forget," I said. "We were sung into forgetting. There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed. "But it doesn't let us off."

It didn't. All week they came to the house — the town, the woken town, moving slowly like people surfacing from deep water, afraid of the bends. They stood in Odessa's yard holding casseroles nobody would eat and looking at me through the window with faces full of a shame so large it couldn't find a shape. The woman who had held the candle nearest to me in the basement in 1974 — Mrs. Aldous, eighty now, her hands ruined with arthritis — came to the door and could not speak at all, just stood there leaking tears until Odessa took her by the elbow and sat her down and poured her tea she also wouldn't drink.

I forgave them. I want to be honest about that, because it would be tidier if I hadn't. But I had been loved by them first, before the taking — taught to fish by Mr. Aldous, given penny candy by the diner man, blessed by half the town at my christening in a dress my mother sewed from a bedsheet. The thing that took me wore their faces, but the faces had been real before it borrowed them. I forgave the faces. The thing itself was gone, unraveled into fifty years of borrowed voices on the church floor, and good riddance, and may every note it stole pursue it into whatever silence waits for songs like that.

Xanthe came on Wednesday. She looked different — lighter, and diminished, the way a house looks after an estate sale, some rooms emptied forever. Half of her had gone into the singing; I could see the gaps, the places where memory had been paid out like rope. She didn't remember her own birthday anymore. She remembered Zephyra's face but not the fight they'd had when they were fourteen, and she told me this at my bedside — my bedside, imagine, an old man of one week — and she cried, and I held her hand with my spotted old hand, and I said:

"You gave me my name back. Both halves of it. Whatever you lost, you bought a boy his surname. That's not nothing. That might be everything."

"It doesn't feel like enough," she said.

"Nothing ever does," I said. "That's how you know it matters."

* * *

On the sixth night I dreamed of the kitchen.

Not the choir's version — not the warm golden lie they'd laminated over my childhood — but the real one, linoleum cracked at the threshold, the window over the sink fogged with pot steam, my mother humming *This Little Light of Mine* a full tone flat and dancing me in circles, my feet on top of hers, her hands under my arms. In the dream I was seven again, briefly, the age I was owed, and she leaned down and put her forehead against mine and said, *Wren, if anyone ever asks you to sing perfect, you run,* and in the dream I said, *Mama, I ran. It just took me fifty years to get out the door.*

She laughed in the dream. Her laugh was a honk, undignified, wonderful. Then the dream let go of me gently, the way she never got to, and I woke at four in the morning in Dell's narrow bed, an old man, and knew.

You know, at the end. Nobody tells you that — that it isn't mysterious. The body finishes its arithmetic and turns the page, and you feel the sum come clear, and there's no fear in it, or if there is, it's small and reasonable, the size of stepping off a curb. I lay there listening to the rain finish and the birds start up their ragged dawn chorus — off-key, all of them, gloriously off-key, nobody had ever asked the birds to sing perfect — and I got up and dressed myself in the clothes Odessa had bought me, real clothes, a flannel shirt and trousers with an elastic waist, the uniform of old men everywhere, and I walked into the kitchen where she was already sitting, because she had known too. Dying people know things. It's the one perk of the job.

"Help me out to the porch," she said. "If it's happening, it happens looking at the river."

So we sat on her porch in the gray-gold light, two old men in all but paperwork, her hand in mine, her grip still strong — Pell grip, Achebe grip, the grip of women who hold on. Down the hill the town was stirring. Lights coming on in kitchens. Smoke rising straight from chimneys in the windless cold. Somewhere a dog. Somewhere a child crying and being picked up, picked *up*, gathered in, held.

"Wren Achebe Pell," Odessa said, quiet, like a prayer or a roll call. "Born June ninth, 1967. Mother Constance. Father gone ahead. Nephew of Odessa Pell, who loved you, who failed you, who never once believed their song." Her thumb moved over my knuckles. "There. Now it's written somewhere besides the ledger."

"Now it's written," I agreed.

The sun came up over the water and turned the creek to hammered copper, and I felt the years settle in me one last time, all fifty of them, heavy and honored, a debt paid in the correct currency at last. I thought about my mother's hands. I thought about the bruise-shaped fingerprints I'd worn for three days, and how I'd mistaken them for love at the time, and how I'd been wrong, and how I'd been right — because they *were* love, they were love doing the only thing love could do against a hymn, which is grip.

"Sing something," Odessa whispered. "Anything. Sing it bad."

So I did. I sang *This Little Light of Mine* a full tone flat, an old man's cracked and wandering voice, every note wrong, and the birds answered from the trees, and the lights came on in the houses below, and doors opened, and people came up the hill barefoot in the cold because they heard it — the whole woken town, drawn up Odessa's street by the sound of a song done badly on purpose, which is the only kind their ears had never learned to fear. They gathered in the yard. Mrs. Aldous. The diner man. Xanthe, standing at the gate with her sister's name in her mouth and her own name buried behind her, weeping without noise. They came to hear a stolen boy sing imperfectly in the daylight, free, mortal, ending.

I got to the verse about not letting anyone blow it out, and I smiled, and I let the note go flat, and I let go.

* * *

They buried me on a Thursday, in the plot beside Dell, under a stone that says WREN ACHEBE PELL and gives both dates, the one I was born and the one I finally got to keep. The whole town came. Nobody sang the Versicle. Somebody's granddaughter brought a guitar and they mangled two hymns and then, led by Odessa from her wheelchair, they sang *This Little Light of Mine* flat on purpose, every soul in Marrow Creek, deliberately, beautifully wrong, and I like to think wherever the music goes after it leaves us, that version arrived somewhere and made my mother laugh her honking laugh.

Xanthe stayed for the burial. She stood apart, at the gate again, the way she does, keeping her distances now that distance is all she trusts. After everyone else had gone she came and knelt and pressed her palm flat to the earth over me, the way you'd check a fever, the way you'd take a pulse.

"Thank you," she said, to the dirt, to me, to whatever was left listening. "For showing me the noises. For walking me through all of it. For being seven on that porch when I needed a friend and sixty-eight when I needed the truth."

Then she stood, and brushed off her knees, and looked down the hill toward the house on Fenwick Street — the house with the porch swing, the house she still had to sell, the house where a grave waited with two names on it and only one of them finished.

She started walking. And though she didn't know yet what she'd find carved in granite, or what it would cost her to read it, she was already humming — something small, something she didn't remember learning, something that was, for now, just a tune.
