## 21. Two Names, One Stone

The morning we buried the second name, the fog came up off the creek like something exhaling.

I stood at the graveside with my hands folded the way mourners do, though there was nothing left to mourn in the ordinary way. The ground had been opened once already, three years ago, and the men from Pell & Sons — Odessa's grandsons, all three of them, broad and solemn in their church clothes — had lifted the coffin out with a tenderness that made my throat close. They had laid it beside the new hole. Inside it, wrapped in the shroud Odessa had sewn herself, were the small bones of the child who had been given the name Xanthe Reyes and had never drawn a breath. My name. My first name, my only true name, the one I had worn for thirty years without knowing it was a borrowed coat.

The minister — a new one, hired from two counties over, who knew nothing of choirs or harvests — read the Twenty-Third Psalm in a voice that cracked only once. The whole town came. That was the thing I had not expected. After the fiftieth harvest, after the walls of the church had stopped breathing and the harmony had torn like wet paper, after Mr. Latch unraveled into fifty years of borrowed voices and was gone — after all of it, Marrow Creek did not scatter. It gathered.

They stood in the fog in their dark coats, every face I had spent three weeks learning to distrust, and they wept openly, unashamed, like people who had been holding their breath for fifty years and had only just remembered how to let it out. Odessa Pell stood at the front in her wheelchair, the old tuning fork still in her lap where it had been since the night her brother's name came back to her. Her grandsons stood behind her. Behind them, the diner cook, the postmistress, the man who pumped gas and had told me twice that the road out was closed for repairs. All of them. All of them awake.

I had not known a town could wake up. I had not known a town could be asleep.

* * *

The stone itself I had ordered from a mason in Halloway, because I could not bear to ask anyone in Marrow Creek to cut it. I had spent a long night at Odessa's kitchen table with a pencil and three sheets of paper, writing and crossing out, writing and crossing out, while Odessa drank tea gone cold and watched me with her clouded, patient eyes.

Two names. One stone. That was the only thing I was certain of.

ZEPHYRA REYES, the first line read. Beloved sister. 1974–2022. The dates I had known since I was old enough to read a headstone, and they were true, they were hers, she had lived under them her whole life — except that she hadn't, not the name, not really, and the dates themselves had been part of the great substitution, the grandmother's sleight of hand written into granite. But Zephyra had loved the name. I knew that the way I now knew a handful of other things: not from memory but from something deeper, something that had survived the singing. She had loved it because it sounded like wind through high grass, she'd said once, and I could hear her saying it, could see her face doing the thing it did when she was pleased with her own cleverness.

And below it, on the second line, the smaller name, the one that had waited in the dark for fifty years and then for three more:

XANTHE REYES. 1974–1974. *Given a name. Kept by God.*

Odessa had suggested the second line herself. *The child should have an epitaph,* she said. *Not a correction. An epitaph. There's a difference, and the difference is mercy.* I had argued — gently, because she was dying and I loved her by then — that *kept by God* was a strange thing to carve over a grave that had held a coffin with the wrong name on the certificate, a grave that had held, in a sense, me. But Odessa had shaken her head, slow as a tide.

*You were kept,* she said. *Just not by God. And not by them, either, in the end. By her.* She tapped her own temple, where the fog of her dying had begun to gather. *Zephyra kept you. Every year she came out here, every year she pulled the weeds, every year she talked to that stone like it could hear her. I saw her do it. She didn't know why she came. She just knew she had to.* Her hand found mine on the table, dry and light as a leaf. *A girl doesn't tend a grave for three years out of duty, child. She tends it out of love. And love like that keeps a soul whether it needs keeping or not.*

So: *Kept by God.* Let the stone say what it wanted to say. The truth was in the ground, and the truth was in me, and the stone could keep its gentle lie the way all stones keep their gentle lies.

* * *

The coffin went back into the earth with its true contents, and the new grave beside it — Zephyra's grave, dug fresh, because the old one had never actually held her — received her ashes in an urn Odessa's granddaughters had painted with wind through high grass. I had scattered half of her at the creek's edge the week before, where the water came up over the road every spring, where she used to dare me to wade in barefoot in April. I remembered that dare. I remembered it the way you remember things that were never yours, which is to say I remembered it completely, and it was hers, and I had decided that made it mine too. That was the arithmetic I lived by now. What she gave me, I kept. What the choir took, I grieved without being able to name, the way you grieve a room you can no longer picture but know you once slept in.

Half my memories, traded in the singing. That was the price of the counter-hymn, and I had known it going in — my grandmother's margins had been clear about that much, even in her cramped and desperate hand: *the unbinding takes from the singer what the binding took from the bound. A voice for a voice. A life for a life. Half for half.* I had struck the fork and sung my grandmother's hidden melody into the throat of the Versicle, and I had felt it go out of me in ribbons — my sixth birthday, gone; the summer we taught Wren to whistle through a blade of grass, gone; my mother's face, which I had loved and feared and inherited my stubborn chin from, gone so thoroughly that when I try now to summon it I get only a warm shape, a suggestion of hands, the idea of a mother rather than the mother herself.

People ask — Odessa asks, carefully, once, over that cold tea — whether it's like a wound. Whether there's a scar I can feel when I reach for the missing years.

It isn't like a wound. That's the terrible, merciful truth of it. It's like a book with pages torn out cleanly, so cleanly you can read right across the gap and only know something's gone because the story skips — because the girl on page forty is afraid of cellars and the girl on page forty-one isn't, and no one tells you why. I know I was afraid of cellars. The fear is still in my body; it lives in my spine. But the night that taught it to me is gone, and when I stand at the top of a basement staircase my heart hammers at nothing, at a locked door with nothing behind it, and I have to talk myself down like a stranger.

I can live with that. I was surprised to find I could. I had braced for it to be unbearable, the way you brace for a cold plunge, and instead it was only cold, and then it was only water, and then I was swimming.

What I could not have braced for was the birthday. That was the one that ambushed me.

* * *

It happened in the county clerk's office, of all places, three weeks after the burial, when I had stayed on in Marrow Creek longer than I'd planned because the house would not sell to anyone but a Pell and the Pells were still deciding among themselves which Pell. I needed a copy of my birth certificate for the estate paperwork — the real one, the corrected one, the one the choir's ledger had made necessary, because legally I was a tangle now, a woman with one name on her driver's license and another in the ground, a woman who had died in 1974 and again in 2022 and was somehow standing at a counter asking for her own records.

The clerk — a tired woman with reading glasses on a chain, who had been at the midnight rehearsal and did not meet my eye — pulled the file and slid it across.

XANTHE REYES. Born October 3rd, 1974.

I stared at it. I knew my birthday. Everyone knows their birthday; it's the one fact that gets sung to you before you can talk, written on forms, celebrated with cake and candles and a number that climbs. I knew it the way I knew my own hands.

I could not remember it.

Not the date. Not the month. I stood there in the fluorescent hum of that little office and searched for it the way you search a pocket you know is empty, and there was nothing. There was a shape where the knowing should have been, a birthday-shaped absence, and around it everything else stood intact — I knew my sister's birthday, I knew Zephyra had been a spring baby, I knew the cake had been lemon one year because I could almost taste — no. Even that was hers. Even the lemon cake was hers, remembered from the outside, remembered as a guest at my own sister's party.

The clerk cleared her throat. "Everything all right, hon?"

"October 3rd," I said, reading it off the certificate like a foreign word.

"That's what it says." She smiled the thin smile of the overworked. "You forget your own birthday, honey, that's not a tragedy. That's a mercy. Mine's the same day as my divorce."

I laughed. I actually laughed, standing in the county clerk's office with my own birth certificate in my hands, and the laugh came out whole and unforced, and something in my chest unclenched that I hadn't known was clenched. Because she was right. It was a mercy. The choir had taken half my life, and the half it took had included the number I had been celebrating for fifty years — but the number had never been mine. It had been the stillborn child's number, the swapped child's number, part of the disguise my grandmother had sewn around the wrong baby to keep the right one safe. I had never actually been born on October 3rd. I had been born in the spring, in the screaming hours of a night in April when two infants lay side by side in a church basement and a choir decided which one would sing.

I didn't know the true date. It wasn't written anywhere — the grandmother had burned the true registry, Odessa told me, or sunk it in the flooded basement, and the flood had taken the rest. But Zephyra would have known. Zephyra had known everything, carried it all those years under her borrowed name, and she had never once let it make her cruel.

I could have grieved the date. For a moment, in the parking lot, I almost did — sat in the car with my forehead on the wheel and let the absence open under me like the flooded basement had opened under my feet. But what rose instead, unbidden, was not grief. It was the lemon cake. It was Zephyra's face doing the thing it did when she was pleased with her own cleverness. It was every birthday I had ever spent with her, and I found that I remembered all of them, every single one, and that they had not been taken, and that a birthday was never really about the date anyway. It was about who was in the kitchen. And she had been in the kitchen every year of my life, and she was in the ground now, and the ground was the only thing I could not trade my way out of.

I drove back to the house with the certificate on the passenger seat, and I found that I could live with the trade. Not easily. Not cleanly. But I could live with it, the way a tree lives with a lightning scar — around it, over it, the grain growing strange and strong on either side of the burn.

* * *

Zephyra passed over whole. I want to write that down somewhere it will stay, because it is the single fact that makes all the rest of it bearable.

I felt it go. The night after the counter-hymn, when the church stood silent and ordinary and the fog over the creek finally, finally smelled like nothing but water — I woke at some hour without a name and felt the house exhale. The noises stopped. All of them. The pipes that had never been pipes, the raccoons that had never been raccoons, the settling that had never been settling — the whole vocabulary of her three years of knocking, gone in a single breath, and in its place a warmth moved through the rooms like someone walking through with a candle, checking the locks, saying goodbye to every door.

I got up and followed it to her room — the childhood room that had smelled of her perfume, the bed that had been made as if slept in — and the warmth was there, and the perfume was fading for the first time, actually fading, becoming the past tense of itself instead of the present tense of a haunting. And I understood, the way you understand things at four in the morning, that she was leaving, and that she was leaving *whole*, every year of her, every stolen and borrowed and beloved year of her, gathered up and carried out intact. The choir's keeping had held her in rehearsal for three years without rest. The counter-hymn had unbound her. And whatever comes after had received her complete, with interest, with everything — her name and her memories and her thirty years of tending my grave without knowing why.

She had been the one who kept me. I had been the one who freed her. Between us we had made a whole person's worth of love, split across two graves and fifty years of a lie, and it had been enough. It had been enough.

On the windowsill, in the frost that shouldn't have formed in September, the last message had already melted. But I had seen it that first night, and I had it by heart, and I would have it by heart until my own heart stopped:

*DON'T SING THEIR SONG.*

I hadn't. I'd sung ours.

* * *

The house sold in November, to Odessa's eldest grandson and his wife, who wanted the porch swing kept exactly where it was. I told them about the swing. I told them everything, or nearly — the whole story, laid out across Odessa's kitchen table while the grandkids came and went with casseroles, and to their enormous credit they did not laugh and did not leave and did not look at me the way you look at a woman who has come unhinged. They looked at me the way you look at a witness. Because half the town had woken from that rehearsal with fragments of their own — a smooth-faced Sunday, a hymn that went on too long, a child-shaped absence from 1974 that had never had a name until the night the harmony tore and fifty years of borrowed voices poured out of a dissolving man and gave every stolen thing back to the air.

They kept the swing. That was the condition I cared about. I told them a boy used to sit there — a boy in overalls with a missing tooth, who waited, who waited so long — and the grandson's wife, a practical woman with flour on her hands, said, "Then he can keep sitting there," and I had to go stand in the yard for a while and look at the creek until my face was fit for company again.

Wren was not in the swing when I left. He was in the ground by then, beside his uncle's name in the Pell plot, with a stone that read WREN ACHEBE PELL, 1967–2024, *a boy with a surname* — Odessa's phrasing, insisted on, carved exactly as spoken. He had gotten his week. He had gotten his fifty years compressed into seven days of furious living and weeping, and he had spent them — I know because I spent them with him — eating peach ice cream on the diner steps, and touching every tree on the road to the old Achebe place, and asking Odessa a thousand questions about his mother, and falling asleep in the sun like it was a country he'd been exiled from. And at dawn on the seventh day he had died in her arms, an old man in a boy's overalls, smiling, finally done waiting.

I do not know where imaginary friends go. I know where he went. That's enough.

* * *

On my last morning in Marrow Creek, I went to the grave one more time.

The fog was up again, thin and harmless, doing nothing but being weather. The new stone stood clean in the gray light, and I read it the way I had read it every day that week, testing it, the way you test a tooth that's been worked on:

ZEPHYRA REYES. Beloved sister. 1974–2022.

XANTHE REYES. 1974–1974. Given a name. Kept by God.

Two names. One stone. One of them mine and one of them mine, and neither of them entirely, and I had decided — standing in the clerk's office, standing at the graveside, standing in the doorway of the empty house with the keys in my fist — that this was not a tragedy. It was just the truth, which is a different thing, a harder and more livable thing. I was a woman built out of halves: half my memories and half my sister's, half a name and half a grave, half a life that ended in 1974 and half that ended in 2022 and a third half — the arithmetic doesn't work, I know, but nothing in Marrow Creek ever had — that was only beginning, unrecorded, on a morning in November with fog on the creek and a full tank of gas.

I laid my hand flat on the granite. It was cold the way stone is cold, ordinary cold, just weather and season, nothing underneath.

"October 3rd," I told her. "I'm giving it back. You can have the spring instead. You always liked it better anyway."

The fog moved. The creek said something in its low voice, the way water does, meaning nothing, meaning everything. I stood there until my hand was as cold as the stone, and then I walked back to the car, and I did not look at the porch, and I did not look at the church, and I let myself cry the whole way down the drive — not the torn-open crying of the church, not the grief that had broken me into the shape of a stranger, but the slow ordinary crying of a woman leaving a town that had cost her everything and given her back a sister.

At the end of the drive I stopped, because the road out was open — truly open, no loop, no repair, no gentle lie — and because on the seat beside me, where I had left it, the hymnal lay with its margins blank.

I had checked them a hundred times. Every annotation gone, every correction, every desperate instruction in my grandmother's hand — wiped clean as if the paper had never been written on, as if the book were just a book, a hymnal like any hymnal, waiting for an ordinary congregation to make ordinary music in it. I had thought about leaving it in the house for the Pells. I had thought about burying it. I had thought about burning it on the beach at the creek's edge while the water came up over my shoes.

I had done none of those things. I had put it on the passenger seat, and I had buckled it in like a passenger, and I had not asked myself why, because I already knew.

I put the car in gear. The road out of Marrow Creek ran straight through the fog toward a morning I had no memories of and no reason to fear, and on the seat beside me the blank hymnal rode quiet, and somewhere behind me — in a church I did not look at — I could have sworn I heard, faint and off-key and unmistakably alive, a small choir of children beginning to rehearse.
