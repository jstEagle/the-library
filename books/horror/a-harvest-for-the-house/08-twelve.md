## 8. Twelve

The twelfth bell did not ring so much as arrive.

Gideon felt it before she heard it — a pressure through the floorboards, up through her boots, into the bones of her pelvis, as though the house had struck itself somewhere below the foundations and the sound was only the aftershock traveling upward through stone. Then the note came, low and swollen, wrong in a way she could not have described to a colleague and would never need to, because there were no colleagues left to describe it to. The bell of Hollowmere rang twelve times that dusk, and each stroke was slower than the last, as if the thing swinging in the melted metal had grown tired, or deliberate, or both.

She was in the east corridor when it began, and by the third stroke the doors were gone.

Not locked. Not barred. *Gone* — the doorframes still standing, the hinges still screwed into their jambs, but where oak panels should have been there was only wall, plastered and papered, seamless, as if the house had swallowed its own exits and found them nourishing. Gideon pressed her palm flat against where the garden door had been that morning. The wallpaper was warm. That was the detail that undid her composure: not cold, as everything else in this house's secret architecture ran cold, but warm, like skin.

"Marlowe." She said it once, quietly, the way you speak in a house that is listening. Then again, louder, walking fast now, her surveyor's instinct converting panic into motion: *map the space, find the edges, work the problem.* "MARLOWE."

* * *

She found him on the stairs, halfway between floors, gripping the banister with both hands as the staircase performed its slow, grinding rearrangement beneath him — treads lengthening, shortening, the whole flight breathing like something asleep. He looked up at her and his face was terrible with relief and shame in equal measure.

"The aunts," he said. "Verity was right behind me. I turned around and—"

"I know."

"They're in the walls, aren't they. That's what this is. It's counting them."

"It isn't counting them." Gideon heard her own voice come out steady and was briefly grateful to whatever professional reflex produced it. "It's counting *to* something. There's a difference."

He came down the last three steps because the house allowed it, which she understood now was how all movement here worked — permission, granted and revoked. In the hall the grandfather clock stood open, its pendulum hanging dead, and above it the portrait of Ambrose Whitlock, 1851–1913, regarded them with the mild satisfaction of a man who had died owing nothing, having arranged matters so that others paid.

"We leave," Marlowe said. "Now. Tonight. I'll carry them out if I have to take the house apart board by board—"

"And go where?" She didn't mean it cruelly. She meant it geographically. "You heard what Verity said. It doesn't want us gone. Running just means it counts us somewhere else. Somewhere with no well room, no register, no—" She stopped herself.

"No what?" He caught it, of course. He'd been catching things from her for days now, the way people do when they are falling in love and frightened about it. "No what, Gideon?"

"No record," she said. "That's what this has been, from the beginning. Every measurement off by exactly one foot. Every room one foot too large. I kept thinking it was error. It was *arithmetic*. The house has been carrying a remainder for a hundred years — one person, unentered, uncounted, unpaid — and everything inside it has been over by exactly that much." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "Edith Marsh existed. She worked in these rooms. She died in that water. And no document anywhere in England says so. The bell isn't counting down to a haunting, Marlowe. It's counting down to an audit."

* * *

They searched for the aunts for two hours, and the house let them search, which was worse than if it hadn't.

Doors opened onto rooms they had measured and remeasured, and every room had changed its mind about its own dimensions since morning. The drawing room had gained a window looking out on the well shaft, vertical and black, water visible at the bottom like a held breath. The library contained, among the ledgers, a single woman's shoe, dry, small, placed neatly beside the 1911 wage book with its column of sums and its final entry — *E. Marsh, wages due* — the ink brown with age and the sum never struck through.

Clemency they found first, asleep in a linen cupboard that had not existed at noon, her hands folded on her chest like an effigy, breathing slow and peaceful and utterly unreachable. Verity they did not find at all. They found instead, on the nursery door, scratched deep into the paint from the *inside*, in a hand Gideon recognized from the wall of the thirteenth room: *she is kind. she remembers me. count her gently.*

"She's protecting her," Marlowe whispered. His hand found Gideon's in the dark of the corridor and neither of them pretended it was accidental. "A century dead and she's still — God, she was nineteen. She was nineteen and they—"

"I know how old she was. I've read the parish books. There's no line for her anywhere. Baptisms, marriages, burials — she's a gap in the paper, and gaps don't close themselves." Gideon turned to face him fully, and in the light of the lamp she carried she watched him understand, one piece at a time, what she was about to say. "There's a back stair to the kitchen. The kitchen is gone — it's the shaft now — but the stair might still go down. If the water rises with every bell, and the bells are finished—"

"Then the water's rising now." He said it flatly, the way men say things they refuse to feel yet. "And you want to go down into it."

"I want to get the locket. And the wage book. And anything else with her name on it that this family failed to burn." She gripped his hand harder. "But listen to me. Listen. If we can't reach the archive tonight — if the roads are sealed, if the house won't let us out — then there's another way, and I need you to hear it before you offer it, because I can see you composing it."

"Gideon—"

"You're going to offer yourself. Your name, your blood, Whitlock stock, payment rendered in the family coin. I've watched you build toward it for two days and I'm telling you now: *no.*"

"It's my debt—"

"It is *not your debt.*" The words came out of her with a force that surprised them both, echoing down corridors that leaned in to listen. "Debt requires a creditor, and the creditor has been named. Not Whitlock. Not blood. *Edith Marsh.* The house doesn't want a sacrifice — sacrifices are just another way of making someone disappear, and disappearing people is the entire crime. It wants the opposite of that. It wants her *written down.* Witnessed. Entered. Made official in the only language this country has ever respected, which is ink." She was shaking now, and let herself shake. "I am a surveyor. My whole life is measurement and record and attestation. I know exactly what a signature does. And tomorrow night I am going to sign one."

"Into the parish register." His voice was very quiet. "Breaking into a crown office. Forging an entry a century late."

"Not forging. *Attesting.* Everything in it will be true. Birth, service, death — I have the dates, I have the wage book, I have the scratch marks in the wall and the shoe in the library and a locket at the bottom of a well with her face in it. I have more evidence than most entries ever had. All she's missing is a witness willing to swear." She lifted his hand, still clasped in hers, and pressed it flat against her sternum, over the pocket where her logbook lived. "And I'm going to give the house mine."

* * *

The back stair, when they found it, went down further than the house had any right to be deep.

At the bottom, where the kitchen had been, the shaft stood open and full, black water risen to within four feet of the ceiling, turning slowly, patiently, like something stirring a pot. On the surface floated things too small to identify and too deliberate to be debris. The cold coming off it was total, ancient, personal.

And on the lowest step, just above the waterline, dry as bone in a desert, sat Edith Marsh's locket — placed there. Waiting. As if the water had done its arithmetic too, and arrived at the same answer Gideon had.

Above them, faint through a hundred feet of stone and soil and settled guilt, the church bell — melted, uncast, impossible — began to toll a thirteenth time.

Gideon reached out and took the locket, and the water went perfectly, terribly still.

"Tomorrow," she said, to Marlowe, to the house, to the girl in the metal. "Tomorrow I make you real."
