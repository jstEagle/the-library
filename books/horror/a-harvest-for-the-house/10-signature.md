## 10. Signature

The house sold on a Tuesday in late September, for eleven thousand pounds under asking price — a figure Gideon had fought to correct and then, at the last moment, let stand. Eleven. She was done arguing with the number.

The buyers were a young couple from Leeds, childless, cheerful, untroubled by history. They walked the corridors with a tape measure of their own and pronounced every room *generous*. Gideon stood in the entrance hall while they admired the staircase and said nothing about the thirteenth room, because there was no thirteenth room anymore. She had checked. Three times, with three instruments, at dusk and after dark and once, deliberately, at three in the morning when she could not sleep anyway. The kitchen wall was a kitchen wall. The floor beneath it was dry as old bone. When she pressed her palm flat against the plaster where the shaft had opened, she felt only cold stone and, beneath that, the faint patient warmth of a house that had finally been paid.

The well itself they capped with a steel plate and gravel. Marlowe insisted on watching them do it, arms folded, jaw set, until the last wheelbarrow of stone went down and the workmen drove away. Then he stood over the filled circle for a long time, and Gideon stood beside him, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left to say that the ground hadn't already heard.

* * *

Marlowe left on a Thursday.

He had sold nearly everything — the furniture went to an auction house in York, the portraits to a dealer who called them "provincial but honest," the silver to whoever would take it. He kept almost nothing. A suitcase, a leather satchel of papers he meant to burn and instead donated to the county archive ("let them be read," he said, "that's the whole point, isn't it"), and his grandmother's watch, which had stopped at some point during the summer and never started again.

"I could have it repaired," he said, turning it over in his hand on the station platform.

"Or you could leave it broken," Gideon said. "Some things are allowed to keep their own time now."

He smiled at that — the first easy smile she'd seen from him since the night the water rose — and put the watch in his pocket anyway, unrepaired, a small dead heirloom ticking nothing against his heart.

The train was the 10:42 to Edinburgh, and from there, he said, north, or west, or wherever the coast looked like a place a man could start over without inheriting anything but weather. He'd been offered a position with a firm of architects in Glasgow. Old buildings, he said. Restoration work. "I want to spend my life making sure other houses are honest."

"You'll be good at it," Gideon said. "You know exactly what dishonesty costs."

They had rehearsed this parting in a dozen small ways over the preceding weeks — the careful distance at dinner, the conversations that circled and never landed — and yet when the moment came it was simpler than either of them had prepared for. He took her hand. His was warm and steady and smelled faintly of lime wash from the last day of packing.

"Come with me," he said, though they both knew he wouldn't ask twice, and knew too what her answer would be, and loved each other precisely because of it.

"I can't." She said it gently. "I'm not made for starting over, Marlowe. I'm made for closing things. Ledgers, sales, files. I close things and I file them and I move to the next one. It's not a life you'd want to share a kitchen with."

"It's the only kind of life I'd trust," he said. "That's rather the problem."

The whistle blew. Somewhere down the platform a porter shouted a destination into the wind. Marlowe bent and kissed her — one kiss, unhurried, thorough, the kind of kiss that is also a signature on a document neither of them would ever draw up — and then he stepped back, picked up his case, and boarded.

Through the window he looked at her the way people look at houses they grew up in: with love, and with the clear-eyed understanding that love is not always the same as belonging. She raised her hand. He raised his. The train pulled out, gathered itself, and was gone, and Hollowmere's last heir became simply a man on a train, free as air, carrying nothing but a stopped watch.

Gideon stood on the emptying platform for a long moment. Then she checked her watch, noted the time in her logbook out of pure habit, and walked back to the village to return the keys.

* * *

The aunts had gone before him, which surprised no one more than themselves.

Aunt Verity — who had spent sixty years keeping her father's silence and three weeks learning what silence cost — moved to a cottage in Whitby overlooking the harbour, where, Gideon heard, she had begun telling anyone who would listen about a maid called Edie, and the bell, and the winter of 1911. She told it plainly, without drama, the way you tell a debt you've finally paid off. Some listeners thought her mad. Others brought her tea and stayed to hear the end. Aunt Constance followed a month later, to rooms two streets away, and the sisters were seen most mornings walking the seafront together, two small upright figures against the grey water, talking, always talking, as if sixty years of withheld words had come due all at once and were being paid in instalments.

Before she left, Verity came to find Gideon at the Black Swan, where Gideon had taken a room to finish the survey. The old woman stood in the doorway of the parlour holding a small parcel wrapped in brown paper.

"For the file," Verity said. "Whatever it is you do with files. Edith's wages were never paid. Fourteen months of them. I had the solicitor calculate the sum with interest — it comes to rather more than you'd think." She set the parcel on the table. Inside was a bank draft, made out to no one, endorsed on the back in Verity's careful hand: *For E. Marsh, in full settlement.* "I don't know who cashes a thing like that. But it should exist. Everything should exist on paper. That's what you taught me."

"It's what the house taught both of us," Gideon said.

Verity looked at her for a long moment, and something passed between them that was not quite gratitude and not quite absolution — the look of one witness acknowledging another. Then she straightened her hat and left, and Gideon filed the draft in the survey folder, between the structural report and the deed, where it sat like a coin placed on the eyes of the dead.

* * *

The final survey took her four days to complete, and she did it properly, room by room, instrument by instrument, because if Hollowmere deserved anything it deserved exactness.

Every measurement came true.

That was the thing she kept returning to, alone in the parlour of the Black Swan with her logbook open and the fire burning low: every room measured exactly as drawn. Not one foot too large. Not one inch. The parlour was fourteen feet by twelve and gave every appearance of being glad of it. The corridors ran their lengths honestly. The stairwell rose its proper rise. After a century of swelling and shifting and counting, the house had settled into its own dimensions like a man into a chair at the end of a long day, and Gideon, who had built a career on the conviction that numbers do not lie, found herself almost moved by how completely they had stopped.

She wrote up the report in her neat surveyor's hand. Foundations sound. Roof sound. Damp: none. Structural anomalies: none. She hesitated only once, pen hovering over the line marked *Historical notes*, and then wrote, in full, the account of Edith Marsh — her birth in 1889, her service at Hollowmere from 1907, her dismissal in December 1911, her death by drowning in the well that same month, her burial in unconsecrated ground, and her entry into the parish register on the ninth of August, witnessed and signed. Let the buyers' solicitors make of it what they would. The house had earned its honesty; the paperwork would carry it forward.

On the fourth evening she assembled the whole folder for copying: title documents, the bank draft, the register extract, the measurements, the photographs. She collated the pages, squared their edges against the table, and flipped through them one last time the way she always did — a habit, a ritual, the surveyor's equivalent of locking a door behind you.

Everything was exact. Every page.

Except one.

It was page forty-one, deep in the measurement tables, a page of figures so routine she had written it half-asleep: the outbuildings, the garden walls, the boundary stones. Her columns ran down the sheet in her own cramped, precise hand — and then, at the bottom, where she always signed and dated her work, there was a signature that was not hers.

*E. Marsh.*

Not scrawled. Not scratched. Written in faded iron-gall ink gone brown with age, in a fine, careful, educated hand — the hand of a girl who had learned her letters well and never had occasion to use them. Beneath it, in the same ink, smaller, almost shy:

*thanks.*

Gideon sat very still. The fire ticked. Outside, the village went about its evening — a dog barking, a car changing gear on the lane, the church clock striking seven with its ordinary, cast, unmelted bell — and none of it knew that on page forty-one of a property survey, across a hundred years and a closed account, someone had signed her name.

Gideon touched the ink with one fingertip. It was dry. It had been dry for decades. It was the driest thing in the folder, older than the paper around it, and yet the page lay flat and calm, as though the signature had always been there, waiting for her to reach it — waiting, perhaps, for the exact day when the count ended and the debt cleared and there was finally, *finally*, something to say thank you for.

She did not photograph it. She did not show it to anyone. Some entries belong to the parties concerned.

Instead she took out her pen, and beneath the faded words, in her own hand, she wrote the date, and beneath the date she signed her own name — *Gideon Whitlock* — so that the page held them both, witness and witnessed, the woman who counted and the woman who had been counted, separated by a century and joined by a line of ink.

Then she closed the folder, tied it with string, and set it on the table, finished.

The fire burned down. Gideon sat a while longer in the warm dark, thinking of a train going north, and a cottage above the harbour, and a name in a parish register that would be read aloud in church this Sunday for the first time in a hundred and thirteen years — read as the recently departed are read, with prayers, among neighbours, because Edith Marsh existed now, officially, eternally, on paper.

She thought: *it is finished, and it is exact.*

And then, from the direction of Hollowmere — two fields away, empty, sold, its well capped and its corridors square — she heard, very softly, unmistakably, a bell ring once.

Sweetly. Like a door closing. Like a door opening.

Gideon did not move. She only reached for her logbook, opened it to a fresh page, and wrote, in her neat surveyor's hand, the time, the date, and the words:

*Bell heard. Single strike. Source unknown. No further action required.*

Then, after a moment's thought, she added one more line — the first entry in her career she had ever made without measuring anything at all:

*Acknowledged.*
