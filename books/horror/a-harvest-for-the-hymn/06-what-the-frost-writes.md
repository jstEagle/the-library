## 6. What the Frost Writes

I have kept the same calendar for sixty-one years, and it is not a calendar you can buy.

It hangs in the pantry behind the preserves, and it has no months on it. It has one date — *first frost* — and beneath that date, a list of names in my handwriting, some of them crossed out, one of them crossed out twice because I could not bear to leave it legible. My husband's name. I crossed it once the year he went down, and then I crossed it again the following spring when I understood that crossing it out had not brought him back, and that ink is not a spell no matter how hard you press the pen.

People assume, when they learn what our family does — assuming anyone learns, which they mostly don't, because we have gotten very good at being boring — that we must be fanatics. That we kneel before altars. That there are candles, chanting, robes with inconvenient hoods. The truth is so much worse. The truth is that we do dishes. We wind clocks. We keep the gutters clear in October because the covenant does not care about your soul, only about your attendance, and attendance requires you to be alive and locatable when the cold comes asking.

The Hollowms are not cursed. I want that on the record, wherever records go when everyone who wrote them is dead. Cursed implies someone did something wrong. What we did was *agree*. There is a difference, and the difference is that a curse you can break, and an agreement you can only keep or default on, and we have been keeping ours since before this city had a name, back when it was a river and a rumor and a hole in the ground that breathed warm in winter.

Here is the whole of it, as my grandmother told it to me over bread she pretended wasn't burnt:

Something lives below. It is old enough that "lives" is a courtesy, the way you say a bed "holds" a sleeper. Long ago — before the family had a house, when we were only a family because we huddled together against exactly this — one of ours went down into the warm dark to steal heat for a dying child, and the thing below let her take it, and asked nothing but that she come back each year afterward and be counted. Counted. Not taken. Not fed upon. Counted, the way a shepherd counts sheep, the way a miser counts coins: to know what belongs to him.

And what is counted is kept. That's the part the old tellings soften, and I will not soften it. The ones who descend come back changed, or they don't come back at all, and either way the ledger below grows by one name a year, and the frost above grows patient, because patience is all frost has ever needed.

One of us, every first frost. Or the cold takes the whole line — not dramatically, not with wolves made of ice at the door, but the way cold actually works. The well souring. The hearth drawing backward, smoke pouring into rooms instead of out. Lungs filling slowly with a damp that no doctor can name because no doctor has ever believed us. My great-aunt's generation tried defiance exactly once. The parish register records their deaths as "winter fever, all." Nine names. The register does not record that it happened in April.

So we count ourselves before we can be counted. We draw lots from a cup older than the country, and whoever wins goes down the stairs behind the iron door, and stands in the chamber with the tally marks until the presence below has finished its arithmetic, and comes home lighter by something no one can measure and heavier by something no one wants to carry.

That is the covenant. That is the harvest we bring in, year after year, and the hymn we sing while doing it is less a prayer than an invoice.

* * *

This morning I woke before dawn, as I always do in the cold months, and put my bare hand flat against the kitchen window glass. An old habit. A diagnostic. If the pane is merely cold, the day is ordinary. If the pane is cold *in patterns* — ferns, feathers, long parallel strokes like someone practicing penmanship — then the day is the day.

Ordinary frost, this morning. Just cold. I made tea anyway with the particular care you make tea when you are buying time, and I sat at the table where six generations of Hollowms have sat, and I looked at the cup.

The lot cup sits on the sideboard eleven months of the year. It is dented pewter, ugly, worth nothing, and I would not trade it for the house. Inside it are chips of slate, one per living Hollowm, each marked with a name in my grandmother's hand except the newer ones, which are in mine. Every autumn after the first frost we pass the cup and each person draws, and the slate that comes up marked with the black cross on both sides is the one that goes down. My grandmother rigged it once, the year my mother was pregnant. She weighted the cup so her own name would rise. The frost found out. The frost always finds out. She spent that winter coughing black water and died apologizing to a window.

You cannot cheat it. You cannot defer it. You cannot send a stranger — I know this better than anyone breathing, and I will say no more about that here, except that the kitchen still has a cold spot by the stove where my husband used to stand, and the spot has never warmed, not in thirty years, not in July.

What you can do is love them beforehand. That is the ritual nobody writes down. You mend their coat in September. You cook the foods they like through October without saying why. You memorize the exact way they laugh so that when they come back up the stairs with the hollow place behind their eyes, you have something to compare it to, and something to wait for, on the far chance the hollow ever fills again. It doesn't. But waiting is also a kind of keeping, and I am very good at keeping.

This year the lot falls to Elias.

It fell to Elias three years ago, too, and two years ago, and last year — because he vanished the spring after his nineteenth birthday, before the drawing, before the frost, before any of it, and a name that cannot be found cannot be drawn, and so the cup rolls forward and the debt rolls with it, gathering interest the way debts do. The frost has been patient with us about Elias. Patient the way a creditor is patient. Each year the first frost comes a little earlier and reaches a little further indoors, and each year I find tally marks scratched faintly into the windowsill over the sink, five strokes and a diagonal, five strokes and a diagonal, counting down to nothing I want to imagine.

He left a note. Of course he left a note; he was a considerate boy, which is the worst kind to lose. *Gone to fix it from the inside. Don't draw for me. Don't follow.* Three sentences, and every one of them a lie or a wound, and I have read them so many times the paper has gone soft as cloth. Fix it from the inside. As if the covenant were a lock and not a mouth. As if anything that goes inside comes out able to hold a wrench.

Wren left too, nine years ago now, and her leaving I understood even as it broke me. She was nineteen and her name came up and she stood in the kitchen holding that little chip of slate and she said *no*, one word, flat as a slammed door, and I saw in her face that she meant it the way stone means it. I could have argued. Her grandfather would have argued — he'd have locked her in the cellar and drawn again and hated himself forever. I looked at my daughter's daughter and I saw my own nineteen-year-old face looking back at me, the face I'd worn the year I drew the black cross and walked down those stairs and came back up missing whatever it is the counter takes as a handling fee, and I could not make her do the thing that had already been done to me. So I opened the door myself. I put her coat over her shoulders. I said, "Then run far enough that the frost can't read your name," and she ran, and the lot fell to her brother instead, and we all knew it, and nobody said it, which is how families say the worst things.

She thinks I don't know why she really left. She thinks it was fear. It wasn't fear, or not only. It was arithmetic. She did the sum — *my refusal costs Elias* — and she could not live inside the answer, so she left the house that contained the equation. I have done my own sums at that table for sixty years. I know exactly what she was carrying. I have never once told her I knew, because telling her would mean admitting I let her go knowing the cost, and there are confessions I am saving for a night with more hours in it.

And now the frost is coming early, and the cup is waiting, and Elias's slate sits in it with the black cross fading on both faces because I refresh the mark every year whether he needs it or not. A superstition. I know it's a superstition. I refresh it anyway, because if I stop, I have given him up, and I have given up enough people to the cold to last several eternities.

* * *

The girl came back yesterday.

Not Wren — Wren came back Tuesday, smelling of diesel and other people's tunnels, all sharp edges and refused eye contact, and I fed her soup and pretended not to notice that she ate like someone who'd forgotten food existed. No. Yesterday, with Wren, came the surveyor. The one from the construction site, the one who documented the door. Crane, his card said. Xanthe Crane, which is not a name, it's a weather report.

I watched him at supper the way I watch everything now: sideways, over the rim of a cup, cataloguing. He had the look I know too well — the orphan architecture of a man built entirely by himself, load-bearing manners, no foundation under any of it. He laughed at Ivo's bad jokes a half-second late. He thanked Marta twice for bread. He sat in the chair nearest the door, and when the fire popped he flinched toward it like it might be knocking.

And then Odile, look closer, because looking closely is the only talent age leaves you.

He has Elias's hands. Not similar — *the same*, the long fingers, the way the thumb rests. He has the hollow under the cheekbones that runs in our line like a signature. When he leaned toward the candle I saw the set of the jaw, my father's jaw, my brother's jaw, Elias's jaw. Coincidence, says the sensible woman I occasionally impersonate. Seven billion people alive; some of them must have our chins.

But the frost does not deal in coincidence, and neither, anymore, do I.

When I offered him seconds he hesitated — just a breath, just a flicker — and in that breath he looked at me the way Elias used to look at me across this same table, hungry and ashamed of hunger, and something in my chest turned over like a spade in cold ground. I served him before he could refuse. Habit. Ritual. Love expressed as portion sizes.

Afterward, washing dishes with Wren drying beside me in a silence with edges on it, I said, quietly, "Your friend has our hands."

She didn't stop drying the plate. "He's not my friend. He's a job."

"Mm," I said, which in this family is a full paragraph.

"Grandmother." She set the plate down harder than china deserves. "Whatever you're thinking, stop. He's a stranger. He's nobody."

"Yes," I agreed, and dried my hands, and looked out the dark window at the glass going white at the corners — early, weeks early, and feathered in that particular way, in long deliberate strokes, like writing. "He's nobody. That's rather the point, isn't it? Nobody has never once been counted."

Wren went very still, the way the house goes still when the frost starts talking.

"The lot falls to Elias," I said, "and Elias is three years gone, and the frost is tired of waiting, and tonight it has started writing on my windows. So I am thinking, granddaughter, what any careful woman would think." I turned back to her and smiled the smile I save for funerals and negotiations. "I am thinking that the covenant counts what belongs to it. And I am wondering — only wondering, mind — what happens if we give it something that looks like a Hollowm, walks like a Hollowm, eats at our table like a Hollowm..."

Down the hall, in the guest room, floorboards creaked. Xanthe, awake at midnight, listening to the walls the way our family listens to walls.

"...and is sleeping under our roof when the frost comes to collect."
