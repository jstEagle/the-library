## 7. Mistaken for the Dead

The trouble with being mistaken for a dead man is that nobody corrects you gently.

It starts small. I'm standing in the Hollowm house's front hall — a room that smells of cold ash and old apples, with wallpaper that has given up on its pattern halfway up the wall, as if the house itself lost interest around 1974 — and a young man comes down the stairs two at a time, sees me, and stops so hard he nearly goes back up them by accident.

"Elias," he says.

"I'm Xanthe," I say. "The surveyor."

He blinks at me the way you blink at a lamp that flickers when it shouldn't. Then he says, "You have his chin," and continues down the stairs as though he has resolved whatever crisis this was internally, without my input, and I am left holding a clipboard in a dead man's hallway wondering what exactly a chin can commit you to.

His name is Ivo. I learn this because Wren appears from the kitchen doorway like weather moving in and says, sharply, "Ivo. He's not Elias. His name is Xanthe." And Ivo looks between us, and then at me again, and does the thing that will define the next three days of my life: he nods slowly, in a way that means *sure*, and *also no*, and *I know what I saw*.

Wren had brought me to the house under protest. That much should be said plainly. She'd stood in the doorway of the counting chamber behind the iron door — after I told her what I'd found there, the tally marks, the fresh blank space waiting like an empty chair at a table — and she'd looked at me for a long moment and said, "You need to come to the house. Odile needs to see your face." Not *I* need to see it. *Odile*. As if her grandmother were the one who could tell us whether my face meant anything or nothing, and Wren herself had already decided it meant something and hated it.

So here we are. The house is called Harrow Fen on the deed, though everyone just calls it the Hollowm house, the way you'd call a wound *the wound*. It sits at the end of a lane where the streetlights stop working one by one going out, which I noted professionally, because I've spent seven years noting things like that and filing them under *infrastructure* instead of under *omen*. Seven years of practice. I'm very good at it.

Odile Hollowm receives me in a parlor kept warm by a stove that seems to be fighting a rearguard action against the rest of the building. She is small and straight-backed and her hands are folded in her lap like they're waiting for something to be placed in them. She looks at me for a long time. Long enough that I start composing excuses in my head — *wrong person, wrong family, no family at all actually, that's sort of my whole thing* —

"You have his chin," she says.

"Apparently," I say. "It's a popular chin. Very mass-market."

She doesn't smile. But something in her face shifts, the way frost shifts on glass — not melting, just rearranging into a new pattern that is somehow worse. "Sit," she says. "You'll take tea."

And here is the thing I want to be honest about, since honesty is apparently the theme of whatever catastrophe I've wandered into: I sat.

I sat because the tea was already coming, carried by a girl of maybe twenty with hollow cheeks and her mother's worried eyes, who set the tray down and then stared at me with her mouth slightly open until Odile said, "Petra," in a tone that could freeze a river, and the girl fled. I sat because the chair was deep and the stove was warm and I have spent seven years sleeping in hostels and surveyors' bunks and once, memorably, a decommissioned signal box with a raccoon problem, and no one — *no one* — has ever said *you'll take tea* to me like my taking it was a settled fact of the universe.

I sat because I am an orphan with a clipboard, and someone had mistaken me for family, and I did not correct them.

Not then.

* * *

By evening I have been absorbed into the household the way a splinter is absorbed into flesh: painfully, and with the body eventually deciding it was always there.

There are four cousins. I keep having to recount them, because they move through the house like a single organism wearing four coats. Ivo, the eldest, broad and slow-speaking, who looks at me with an expression I can only describe as *hopeful grief*. Petra, the youngest, who keeps finding reasons to walk past whichever room I'm in. The twins, Anselm and Bram, who are somewhere in their thirties and communicate mostly in glances and finished sentences, and who have decided — I gather from overheard fragments — that my arrival is either a miracle or a trap, and are content to wait and see which, since both are interesting.

None of them call me Xanthe more than once or twice before sliding back to Elias. They don't do it dramatically. That's the thing. There's no scene where someone grabs my shoulders and cries *brother, you live!* It's smaller than that, and therefore infinitely harder to fight. It's Ivo handing me a wrench and saying, "You always had the touch for the boiler, even when we were boys." It's Petra setting a plate at the table and pausing, confused, because she's set five places out of habit and there are only four of them plus me, except the habit was made when there *were* five, and her hands remember. It's Anselm starting a sentence with "When you and Wren used to—" and stopping, embarrassed, and me sitting there inside the shape of a memory I never made, feeling it fit anyway, like a coat cut for someone else but close enough to keep you warm.

I should be cataloguing all of this. I am, technically — old habits — but the catalogue has stopped being professional. Somewhere between the tea and the boiler wrench, the notes in my head stopped reading *subject displays transference behavior* and started reading *they think you came home.*

Here's what I know about myself, the short version: my mother died when I was four, in circumstances I've reconstructed entirely from a shoebox of documents, and my father was a rumor with a name attached. I aged out of the last group home at eighteen with a duffel bag, a talent for reading blueprints, and a policy of never staying anywhere long enough to hear the sounds and start explaining them away. Which lasted seven years, because it turns out I explain sounds away professionally now. The city beneath the city hums and knocks and breathes, and I write it all down in tidy hand-lettered surveys and call it hydrology.

So when a family that has been losing people to a hole in the ground for generations looks at me and sees one of their lost ones returned — when they set a place for me at their table with hands that remember how — you would think I'd run. You would think a man with my particular scar tissue would recognize the danger of borrowed belonging immediately.

You would think that. I thought that. Right up until Odile said grace.

I didn't know families still said grace. This one doesn't, exactly — it's not to any god I recognized. Odile stands at the head of the table, and the others bow their heads, and she says, in a voice worn smooth as a stair rail: "For what the ground gives, thanks. For what the ground takes, patience. For what returns—" and here her eyes came up and found mine across the candlelight, and the whole table came up with her, six faces turning toward me like flowers toward a sun that had no business rising, "—gratitude beyond measure."

Nobody corrected her. Nobody said *he's the surveyor, Gran, he's here about the door.* Wren opened her mouth — I watched her do it — and then closed it, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to, because if I read it correctly it was going to ruin the meal.

"And what returns," Ivo repeated softly, passing me the bread.

I took the bread. My hands were steady. That's the detail that shames me most, looking back — not that I took it, but how easily my hands did it, like they'd been waiting years for someone to hand them something at a table.

* * *

Wren catches me on the third night.

I've been given a room. Let that sink in — I protested, mildly, professionally, citing the survey schedule, and Odile overrode me with the serene authority of a woman who has outlasted winters that killed her neighbors, and now I'm in a narrow bed under a slanted ceiling in a room with a child's height-marks penciled on the doorframe. *E., age 6. E., age 9. E., age 12.* The pencil marks climb the wood like a ladder, and then stop, because at some point the boy who climbed them went down instead.

I lie there telling myself I'll clear everything up in the morning. I'll stand at breakfast and say, *look, I appreciate the hospitality, but I'm not Elias, I'm not anyone's, I'm the man you hired to draw your door.* I rehearse it. I get the wording right. I fall asleep mid-rehearsal with the height-marks over my head like a measuring stick I'm failing to reach.

In the morning I go down and help Ivo split wood, because he asked, and because splitting wood turns out to be the thing my arms have wanted to do my entire life without consulting me. In the afternoon Petra shows me the orchard and tells me about the year the frost took the blossoms and Elias built little paper hats for the trees to cheer them up, and I laugh at the right moment, and hate myself for knowing it was the right moment. At supper there are six places laid and nobody counts them aloud, and I sit in mine.

That's when Wren gets me. Not at the table — she lets the meal happen, lets me eat bread and stew under the candlelight while Odile watches me with an expression I've decided not to decode, lets the twins argue about a roof gutter, lets the whole domestic machine run with me installed in it like a part it had been missing. She waits until I carry my plate to the kitchen, because I've started doing that too, second night running, muscle memory I don't have acquiring itself in real time — and she follows me in and shuts the door.

"Say it," she says.

"Say what?"

"Say the sentence. The one where you tell them who you are. You've had three days. I've watched you not say it at breakfast, lunch, dinner, and twice during wood-splitting, which frankly took commitment." She leans against the counter and crosses her arms, and up close I can see she hasn't been sleeping; there's a bruised look under her eyes, and her hands, folded tight, have the tremor of someone holding something down. "Say it now, and I'll back you. I'll make them listen."

And here's the wretched truth, the one I've been carrying around like a stone in my boot for seventy-two hours: I opened my mouth to say it, and what came out was nothing. Just air. Because the sentence exists — I have it word-perfect, I built it myself — but every version of it ends with me walking out that door and back to a hostel bunk and a city full of sounds I explain away, and every hour in this loud, warm, broken house has made that sentence heavier, until now it weighs what a door weighs, what a coffin lid weighs, and I cannot lift it.

"They buried him," I say instead. Which is not the sentence.

"In absentia. There wasn't a body." Her voice is flat. "The ground keeps what it takes. We don't get bodies. We get winters."

"I'm sorry. About your brother."

"Don't." The word cracks like ice under a heel. "Don't be sorry *at* me, Xanthe. Be honest with me. You're letting them love a ghost, and the ghost is wearing your face, and you're letting it happen because—" she stops, and her jaw works, and when she continues her voice has dropped to something almost gentle, which is worse, "because nobody ever set a place for you. Did they."

It isn't a question. I stand there holding my empty plate like an offering, like evidence, and I can't even manage indignation, because she's right, and she knows she's right, and worst of all she doesn't say it cruelly. She says it the way you'd name a disease. Sympathetically. Clinically. Like she caught it early enough to treat.

"I'll tell them," I say. "Tomorrow."

"You said that yesterday."

"I mean it tomorrow."

Something almost like a laugh escapes her, bitter and quick. "You know what the awful thing is? You're good at it. Being him. Ivo laughs differently when you're in the room. Petra sleeps. Even Odile—" She stops herself. Looks at the window, where the dark has come down thick and early, earlier than it should for the season, and where — I notice it now, the way I notice everything, curse and blessing — a fine lace of frost is creeping in from the corner of the glass. In September. Indoors. I file it under *old windows, poor seals.* Seven years of practice. I'm very good at it.

"The awful thing," Wren says, still watching the window, "is that some part of me wants to let it happen too. Because if you're Elias, then Elias came back, and none of us have to grieve him, and none of us have to ask what took him." She turns back to me, and her eyes are wet and furious about being wet. "But he didn't come back. You did. And whatever took him is still down there, and it's still expecting someone, and my family is spending its last calm days pretending otherwise because a stranger with the right chin makes good tea company."

"I'm not pretending anything," I say, which is a lie delivered with excellent posture.

"No?" She pushes off the counter and opens the kitchen door, and the warmth of the stove spills out past her into the hall. "Then first thing tomorrow, at breakfast, in front of everyone — say the sentence. Word for word. I'll be watching." And then, quieter, at the threshold, not quite looking at me: "For what it's worth — I wish you were him. That's why this is unbearable. Goodnight, Xanthe."

The door clicks shut. I stand alone in the kitchen with my plate and the frost on the window, which has spread while we talked, and which — I finally let myself see it, because there's no one left in the room to perform competence for — is not random lace at all. It's lines. Short, parallel strokes, grouped in fives, marching across the glass from corner to center.

Tally marks. The same as behind the door. The same as my old sketches, the ones from tunnels I surveyed years ago, tunnels I'd never been near tonight.

And underneath the floor, faint but unmistakable, patient as arithmetic, something begins to knock.

Five. Pause. Five. Pause. Five.

I know that rhythm. God help me, I've *heard* that rhythm — in storm drains, in sealed conduits, in the dead space under the Meridian exchange, seven years of it, and every time I wrote *water hammer* in the margin and moved on.

It's not water hammer. It has never been water hammer.

It's been counting.
