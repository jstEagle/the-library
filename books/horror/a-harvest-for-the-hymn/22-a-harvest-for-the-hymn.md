## 22. A Harvest for the Hymn

The tunnel under Callow Street doesn't exist. I want that on the record, in ink, in my own handwriting, because seven years ago I would have said the same thing about a dozen tunnels that turned out to exist very much indeed, and I have learned — slowly, expensively, with frostbite of the soul if not the fingers — that the city keeps secrets the way old houses keep heat: badly, and only from people who aren't paying attention.

I was paying attention.

It was October again, which felt like a joke someone was playing on me, and I had Marisol's new survey rig slung over one shoulder and Dev's spare headlamp taped to my hardhat because mine had developed a flicker I refused to call ominous. Old Tam had drawn me a map of the junction under the old tannery district — drawn it by hand, in pencil, on the back of a takeaway menu, because Tam believes GPS is "a rumor spread by satellites" — and according to that map there was a passage behind the collapsed brickwork that went down toward the river and then simply stopped, as if whoever built it had run out of conviction.

Seven months since the counting chamber. Seven months since I tore my own page out of a ledger older than the city and told a thing made of frost and intention that nothing inherited, everything chosen. Seven months since Elias Hollowm walked up a staircase he'd been kept beneath for six years, blinking at daylight like a man remembering a language.

Seven months, and I still checked every door I passed for frost. Some habits are load-bearing.

* * *

The passage behind the tannery brickwork did exist, which pleased me professionally and unsettled me personally, because Tam's maps were usually wrong in the direction of optimism. It ran narrow and dry, the air tasting of cold stone and old iron, and the walls were dressed stone rather than poured concrete — pre-Victorian, maybe pre-Georgian, cut by hands that hadn't heard of the city above them yet. My flashlight found chisel marks like handwriting. Somebody had been proud of this work once.

I do this thing, when I'm alone underground, where I narrate my findings out loud to nobody. Marisol says it's because I grew up an orphan talking to empty rooms and never saw a reason to stop. She says it kindly, which is her way of saying it at all.

"Passage bearing southwest," I told the empty room, "dressed stone, no mortar, approximately two hundred years give or take a dynasty. No water damage. No—" I paused, because my light had caught something on the wall, "—no, wait."

Tally marks.

My whole body did the thing it does now, the full-system pause, heart going quiet so the ears can work. But these weren't the counting chamber's marks. Those had been scratched desperate and deep, thousands of them, a scream in arithmetic. These were small, neat, deliberate — groups of five with a slash through them, marching along the wall at shoulder height, and they ended. They just ended, mid-wall, unfinished, the last group only three marks long, and below them, in the same hand, someone had carved a single word:

ENOUGH.

I stood there for a long moment with my breath fogging faintly in air that should not have been cold enough for fog, and then I laughed. Out loud. Alone. Two hundred years dead, some mason or monk or madman had stood exactly where I stood, looked at whatever he'd been counting, and decided to stop. Decided the count didn't own him.

"You and me both, friend," I said, and photographed it from four angles, because peace without documentation is just denial with better lighting.

* * *

That's when I heard the singing.

It came down through a grate somewhere above and ahead — one of those cast-iron street grates the city installed a century ago and forgot, breathing warm air up out of the dark. At first I thought it was traffic, wind through wire, the usual orchestra of rationalizations I'd conducted for seven years. But then it resolved into melody, and the melody resolved into something I knew.

The harvest hymn.

Odile sings it every year at the turning of the season; Wren had hummed it once, absently, while we waited out a rainstorm in the doorway of the Hollowm house, and caught herself doing it and gone quiet in a way that told me the song had cost her family more than most songs cost anyone. Four notes rising, two falling, then a held tone like a hand laid flat on a table. A counting song, Odile had admitted. You could keep time to it. You could keep anything to it.

But this version drifting down through the grate wasn't being sung downward. That was the thing my skin understood before my brain did. For seven years the sounds beneath the city had come up at me — knocking, tapping, rhythm without mercy — things calling, summoning, counting. This was falling. Falling gently. Someone above, singing for the joy of it, and the sound spilling through the grate like light through a curtain gap, landing in the dark and meaning nothing by it except: here is a song, and you may have some.

I climbed the maintenance rungs to the grate and put my ear against the cold iron, and I listened like a starving man listens through a kitchen window.

Voices. Plural. Off-key in the specific way of people who know each other well enough to be off-key together. And underneath the melody, words — harvest words, home words, the hymn's old verses about bringing in and gathering close, sung the way they must have been sung before anyone thought to count with them.

I knew the voices. That was the part that undid me. Ivo's rumble, going flat on the high notes the way it always did. The twins, harmonizing with each other against all musical evidence. Odile's low thread holding the whole thing together like a spine. And Wren — Wren's voice clear and unguarded, singing a song she'd spent nine years refusing to sing, and singing it like she was allowed now.

They were standing in the yard above, I realized. Around the fire pit Odile had finally let Ivo build, in the October evening, singing the harvest in because the harvest deserved singing in and not because anything below demanded it. The rite had become a party. The covenant had become a carol.

I hung there on the rungs, thirty feet under a family's evening, and I did not go up.

Not because I wasn't wanted. That was the miracle I was still learning to trust — that I could climb those rungs, push the grate, and be pulled the rest of the way by four sets of hands, that there was a bowl and a spoon and a place at the table with my name on it, invited, not inherited. I'd eaten at that table forty times since spring. I'd learned which chair wobbled. I'd learned that Odile took her tea bitter and that the twins cheated at cards and that Elias, slow-recovering Elias, liked to sit near the window and watch the frost form on the glass in the mornings and say, quietly, "Just weather," like a prayer of thanks.

No. I stayed below because I wanted to hear it like this, once. From underneath. From the place where the song used to be a summons, used to be a debt, used to be the sound your name made when something vast and cold said it. I wanted to stand in the dark where the fear lived and hear the same notes arrive as a gift.

The hymn ended. There was laughter — actual laughter, rolling down through the grate, bouncing off my dressed-stone walls, echoing past the tally marks and the word ENOUGH — and then Odile's voice, warm and wry: "Again, from the top, and Ivo, this time try to land inside the same century as the rest of us."

More laughter. The hymn began again.

And I — J. M. Crane, mapper of tunnels that shouldn't exist, orphan, substitute, refuser, survivor — sat down on a rung of a ladder in a tunnel two hundred years old, under a family that had taken me in by choice, and I mapped the sound.

That's what I did. That's what I've always done. When the world gives me something too large to hold, I document it. I took out my notebook and I wrote: *October 14th. Junction behind tannery, SW passage. Audible from depth: harvest hymn, multiple voices, source domestic. Bearing approximately north-northeast, distance approx. 40 feet vertical. Character of signal: welcome.*

I underlined *welcome* twice. Then I closed the notebook.

Because here's the other thing I've learned, the thing that took a ledger and a frost and a family to teach me: some things you don't need to keep. The old Xanthe would have traced the sound to its source, measured the grate, catalogued the acoustics, filed it all away against future dread. The old Xanthe believed that anything unmapped was a threat, because the only family he'd ever had was the dark, and the dark had taught him that everything down here eventually asks for a name.

But the dark had also given me Marisol and Dev and Old Tam, who'd fed me and taught me ropes and never once asked what blood I carried. And it had given me a door sealed from the inside, and a woman named Wren who looked at a stranger and chose him anyway, and a word carved in stone by someone who'd decided, centuries ago, that the counting could end whenever somebody was brave enough to end it.

You don't map your way home. You just notice, one evening, that you're already there.

* * *

I climbed the rungs anyway. Of course I did. Vigilance and peace aren't opposites; they're dance partners, and we've gotten good at the steps.

I pushed the grate up an inch and the smell hit me first — woodsmoke, roast squash, cider, the particular autumn smell of a house that has decided to be happy about something. Then the light. Then Ivo's face appearing over the edge of the hole, backlit and enormous, breaking into a grin.

"Well, look what the tunnels coughed up," he said, and hauled me out by the collar like a cat.

"Found a new passage," I said, brushing myself off. "Pre-Georgian stonework. Also a message from the eighteenth century telling us to knock it off with the counting."

Wren came across the yard fast — she still moves fast toward me, like she's afraid I'll be repossessed — and read my face the way she can. "Good news?"

"Somebody down there figured it out two hundred years before we did." I showed her the photo. She looked at ENOUGH for a long moment, and then she kissed me, briefly, in front of God and the twins and the whole harvested evening, which was her way of filing documents.

"Supper's getting cold," Odile called from the fire, not looking up from the ladle, in the tone of a woman who had once bargained with winter itself and now deployed her authority exclusively against underdone potatoes.

Elias waved at me from his chair by the window. He waves more easily now. He still goes quiet sometimes, mid-sentence, listening to something none of us can hear, but the silences are shorter every month, and last week he'd laughed at one of Dev's terrible jokes without checking the windows first. Recovery isn't a door you walk through. It's a tunnel you map, one survey at a time. He's mapping it. We're mapping it with him.

I took the bowl Odile handed me — she always hands me the bowl, has since the second week, as if feeding me were a ritual she'd invented to replace an older one — and I sat down in the wobbling chair, my chair, and the hymn started up again around the fire, and this time I sang.

Badly. Ivo and I landed outside the same century together, and the twins accused us of sabotage, and Odile said the hymn had survived worse, and Wren's knee pressed warm against mine under the table that wasn't an altar, wasn't a ledger, was just a table with food on it.

Later, much later, when the fire had burned down to its own kind of tally — small, bright, counting nothing but its own remaining hours — I stepped back inside to fetch blankets and paused at the cellar door. Just paused. Old habit. I put my hand flat against the wood.

No cold. No rhythm. No knock asking to be answered.

Just the house, settling around its people, the way houses do when they're loved loudly enough.

"Goodnight," I told it, and meant the house, and meant the family, and meant — for the first time in seven years of talking to empty rooms — myself.

Then I carried the blankets back out to the fire, where my name was called without ceremony, without lot, without ledger, and I went.
