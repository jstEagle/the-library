## 3. A Bell with No Bell

Zephyrine Whitlock had spent the better part of the morning trying to buy a second singing out of Marrowfen, and had failed in a manner that would have been funny if it weren't so methodical.

It wasn't that the villagers were rude. Rude would have been a relief. Rude was data. Instead they performed a kind of collective sleight of hand: Zephyrine would ask Mrs. Petchy at the bakery if she might sing the wassail tune again, just once more, for the machine — and Mrs. Petchy would smile her wide, even smile and say, "Oh, I expect Tam's told you about that," and hand over a lardy cake as though pastry were an answer. The baker's boy did the same. The woman stacking crates outside the co-op did the same. Even the vicar, a soft-spoken man named Mr. Ellery with ink-stained fingers and the air of someone perpetually about to apologize, did the same, though his version came with a gentle "we keep our songs like we keep our candles — one burning at a time."

One burning at a time. Zephyrine wrote that down in the notebook, underlined it twice, and hated how much they liked it.

By late afternoon they had given up on the village and turned instead to the church of St. Brannoc-in-the-Fen, which stood at the low end of the village green with its tower open to the sky like a broken tooth. Roofless — not just the nave, which had lost its roof to some long-ago fire or simply to fen rot, but the tower itself, capped with nothing but weathered stone and a fringe of house-leeks growing from the parapet. Zephyrine stood in the churchyard reading the moss-eaten gravestones and felt the particular joy of a folklorist in an untouched parish: no guidebook, no heritage plaque, no gift shop selling fudge shaped like anything. Just stones, and nettles, and a door hanging open on darkness.

Inside, the church smelled of dust and bird droppings and old incense gone to vinegar. The pews had been pushed against the walls decades ago; pigeons owned the place now, shuffling along the rood beam with the self-importance of landlords. Zephyrine's torch found the usual things — a defaced font, a board of benefactors' names, a glass case of parish records chained shut — and one unusual thing: the tower's ringing chamber, reached by a spiral stair, containing a frame for eight bells.

Empty. All eight pits bare, the headstocks gone, the wheels gone, even the ropes gone, leaving only pale rectangles on the wall where something had hung for centuries and then, plainly, hadn't.

Zephyrine stood in the ringing chamber with their torch beam moving over those pale rectangles and felt the first cold thread of the day go through them. Not fear. Not yet. Something more like the feeling of reaching for a stair that isn't where your foot expects it.

They went back down and found the parish record case, and after some negotiation with a padlock that surrendered to a firm wiggle, sat on the cold floor with the great ledger open across their knees and read by torchlight until their eyes ached.

The record was meticulous. That was the worst part. Whoever had kept this book in the years before the Great War had been a person who loved order the way Zephyrine loved order, and the entries marched down the page in copperplate:

*1911, March. This day the great bell of St. Brannoc, called Old Harvest, weight one ton four hundredweight, cast in the year 1689, was taken down and conveyed to the foundry at Cirencester there to be broken up for scrap metal, the proceeds toward the repair of the school roof.*

And beneath it, in the same hand, a shorter line:

*The tower is silent now. God grant it stay so.*

Zephyrine read that last line three times. *God grant it stay so.* It was an odd thing to write about a bell you'd sold yourself. It sounded less like an account and more like a prayer — or an apology.

They copied the entry out word for word, photographed both pages, and sat for a moment longer in the pigeon-dark church, listening to the fen wind move through the roofless tower above them, making a low hollow note against the stonework. Like breath across a bottle, they thought. Like the ghost of a note.

Then they walked back to the Lamb & Flag through the gold-and-violet light of early evening, feeling pleased with themselves. A vanished bell. A superstitious silence. A parish record with a prayer in it. Tomorrow they'd get Merrin talking properly — the innkeeper's daughter had a face that wanted to talk, whatever her father's rules said — and by week's end Zephyrine Whitlock would have the bones of a paper that would make Dr. Anselm at the Institute stop calling them "the promising one" in that tone that meant *the forgettable one.*

That was the plan. The plan lasted until dusk.

* * *

Dusk came to Marrowfen the way water comes into a boot: gradually, then all at once. One moment the green was amber and long-shadowed, harvest carts trundling home, children being called in by names Zephyrine was already collecting like coins; the next, the light simply left, all of it, as though someone had pulled a plug in the western sky.

Zephyrine was on the inn's front bench with a mug of cider, transcribing the afternoon's notes by the light of the window behind them, when the first toll came.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, which is the trick bells have always had, but underneath the everywhere-ness there was a direction, unmistakable, coming from the low end of the green. From St. Brannoc's.

Zephyrine looked up. The cider went cold in their hands.

The sound was enormous and sweet and wrong. Sweet because it was a beautiful bell-tone, round and bronze and resonant, the sort of voice that gets tuned into a bell over months by craftsmen who knew things about harmonics that modern foundries had forgotten. Wrong because Zephyrine had stood in that tower an hour ago. Had counted the empty pits with their own eyes. Had read the ledger with their own hands. There was no bell in St. Brannoc's tower. There had been no bell in St. Brannoc's tower since March of 1911, when it had gone to Cirencester to be broken up for scrap, and God had been asked to grant the tower stay silent.

The bell tolled again. And again.

Around the green, Marrowfen moved as one animal. Doors opened and doors closed. The baker's boy, halfway to somewhere with a basket, simply stopped, set the basket down with exaggerated care, and walked — not ran, walked, fast — indoors. Mrs. Petchy appeared in her doorway long enough to gather in a cat, and the cat, notably, needed no gathering; it was already pressed flat to her chest with its ears back. Down the lane, a tractor cut its engine mid-task. In the space of perhaps forty seconds the entire village went inside, windows shuttered or curtained, lamps pinched low, and the green lay empty under the darkening sky except for Zephyrine Whitlock, sitting on a bench with a mug of cider and a notebook full of rational explanations they were no longer sure they believed.

*Toll four.*

Zephyrine's pen had stopped moving. They noticed, distantly, that they were counting. Of course they were counting; counting was what they *did*, counting was the whole architecture of their mind, and some stubborn clerk deep in their skull kept marking the tallies even while the rest of them sat very still and listened to a bell that did not exist ring out over a village that had known, clearly, exactly, what to do about it.

*Toll five. Toll six.*

There was something in the sound itself, now that they listened past the shock of it. Underneath the sweetness, down where the harmonics lived, each toll seemed to contain other sounds — faint, brief, like voices heard through a wall. Or like many voices blended into one tone, the way a choir blends into a chord. Zephyrine thought of the recording equipment upstairs, the good reel-to-reel with its sensitive microphone, and had the sudden wild urge to run inside and fetch it, hold the mic up to the evening air, capture the impossible thing and play it back and prove —

Prove what? Prove it to whom? Their own ears were standing right here.

*Toll seven.*

"Toll eight," said a voice beside them, very quietly, and Zephyrine nearly left their body.

Merrin had come out onto the bench without making a sound — a girl of maybe nineteen, dark-haired, freckled across the nose, wearing her father's too-big cardigan over her dress, and she was looking at the tower with an expression Zephyrine hadn't seen on any face in Marrowfen yet. Not the wide even smile. Something older and wearier, and underneath it, unmistakably, fear.

"You're counting," she said.

"I — yes. Sorry. Is that —"

"Don't." She said it softly but with a finality that closed like a door. "Never count the tolls. Whatever else you do here, scholar, don't count them. It's not polite."

"Not polite," Zephyrine repeated. Their voice came out thinner than intended. "Merrin, there is no bell in that tower. I've been in it. There's nothing up there but pigeons and empty frames. The parish record says the bell was melted down in 1911. For *scrap*. I have photographs."

"I know," Merrin said.

"You *know*?"

"We all know. Everyone here knows. My grandad helped load it onto the cart when he was a boy. He said it took six horses and half the village, and that when it swung free of the beams it made a sound nobody had asked it to make, one low note, like it was saying something. He never said what. He went quiet whenever anyone got near the subject, same as everyone." She hugged her knees up onto the bench, making herself small inside the big cardigan. "It doesn't matter what happened to the metal, scholar. Metal's just the shape a sound wears. You can melt the shape. Nobody's ever worked out how to melt the sound."

*Toll nine.* Zephyrine flinched. Merrin didn't. She'd clearly done this before, many times, the way you do anything that has become weather.

"How many does it do?" Zephyrine asked, and then caught themselves. "No — don't tell me. If telling me is the same as counting."

Merrin glanced at them sideways, and something flickered across her face — surprise, and under the surprise, a small warm coal of something that might have been approval. "Quick, aren't you," she said. "Da said you'd be quick. He said scholars are either quick or deaf, and the deaf ones are safer."

"Safer for whom?"

The question hung there. Merrin looked at the tower. The tower said nothing. The whole village said nothing; Zephyrine could feel the silence pressing outward from every shuttered window like heat from a kiln.

"For everyone," Merrin said finally. "That's the general idea."

*Toll ten.* And this time — Zephyrine would swear this later, in the notebook, in careful handwriting, hating every word — this time the toll seemed to lean. To bend slightly toward the bench, toward them, the way a listener leans toward a conversation they're enjoying. The harmonic voices underneath the tone seemed, for half a heartbeat, almost like words. Almost like a name.

Their name? No. Surely not. Surely that was the cider and the dark and a lifetime of wanting the world to say their name and it never quite bothering to.

*Toll eleven.*

"Merrin!" A man's voice from the inn doorway — her father, broad and worried, silhouetted against lamplight. "In. Now. Both of you, in, are you mad, sitting out in it?"

"In a minute, Da." But she was already standing, pulling Zephyrine up by the sleeve with a grip much stronger than her size suggested. "Come on. Rule two: don't be outside when it finishes. Nobody remembers why. Everybody keeps it."

"What's rule one?" Zephyrine asked, letting themselves be hauled toward the light.

"Rule one is don't count the tolls." She paused at the threshold, one hand on the door, and looked back at the tower with that old weary fear again — and something else besides, something almost like pity, aimed not at the tower but at Zephyrine. "You'll want to write all this down tonight, won't you. Put it in your book. Make it into facts."

"It's what I do," Zephyrine said. "Making things into facts is — it's how I love things, if that makes sense. If something's written down, it can't be entirely lost."

Merrin considered that for a long moment, the lamplight catching the freckles on her nose, and when she answered, her voice had dropped to almost nothing, beneath the twelfth toll, beneath the thirteenth, beneath the strange sweet silence that followed them like a held breath.

"My mum says the same thing about the bell," she said. "She says it loves things too. That's why it sings."

Then the door closed, and the bolt went across, and somewhere above the fen the last of the sound faded out — slowly, reluctantly, the way a singer holds a final note past the point of comfort, past the point of music, into something that is only longing with a pitch to it.

Zephyrine sat up late that night with the notebook and wrote everything: times, toll-counts (in pencil, lightly, with an apology in the margin), the ledger entry, Merrin's words, *metal's just the shape a sound wears*. They wrote until the candle guttered, and the writing steadied them, as it always had, as it always would. Facts. Facts were a floor you could stand on.

But twice in the night they woke, certain they'd heard a single soft toll, close by, almost tender — like something outside the window, learning the sound of their sleeping breath.

And once, on the edge of sleep, they caught themselves humming a low round note they had never heard anywhere but that evening's sky — and stopped, appalled, and wrote in the notebook, in the last line before the candle died:

*Note to self: tomorrow, ask Merrin what happens to the people who sing along.*
