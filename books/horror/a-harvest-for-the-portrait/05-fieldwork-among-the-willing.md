## 5. Fieldwork Among the Willing

Merrin had decided to do it the night before, somewhere between her third cider and the moment she caught herself lying to Zephyrine Whitlock about where the footpath went.

"The long way round," she'd said, pointing them toward the mill lane. "Nicer views."

There were no nicer views on the mill lane. There was a collapsed wall and a bad-tempered goose. The nice view—the one she was steering them away from—was the barley field behind the churchyard, which stood in August like a held breath, green-gold and heavy-headed while every other field in the county had gone the colour of old toast. She'd watched Zephyrine's eyes drift toward it over supper, that hungry archivist look, the pen already twitching in their pocket like a dog's tail, and she'd lied, and then she'd lain awake feeling the lie sit in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Her mother would have said: some things you don't show strangers. Her mother had said it often, usually while doing something she shouldn't, quietly, in the dark.

But here was the thing about Marrowfen that nobody who lived there could say out loud, because saying things out loud was precisely the problem: they had all stopped looking at each other properly. A hundred years of not-counting, not-singing-twice, not-naming, and what it made was a village of people who smiled at each other with the same careful smile, like a row of teacups turned just so. Merrin had spent twenty-six years being a teacup. And now here came this person from outside with ink-stained fingers and a recorder full of other people's songs and a way of looking at Tam Rooker like he was a cathedral instead of a daft old man—and Merrin had thought, watching them across the inn's low beams, *they can see it. They'll see it in a week anyway. Better I'm the one holding the lamp.*

That was the story she told herself, at least. The truer version was smaller and meaner and more frightened: she wanted someone else to know. That was all. She wanted one single soul outside the fen to know what the fields cost, so that if she ever vanished into the ledger herself, something somewhere would say, *ah, but we know why.*

* * *

She found Zephyrine after breakfast, when the mist was still peeling off the fen in long grey ribbons, and said, "Right. You want to see the fields."

Zephyrine looked up from their notebook with the expression of a heron spotting a vole. "I want to see everything," they said. "I've been told, variously, that I want to see too much, that I ask the wrong questions, and that I should be gone by Thursday. Which is it today?"

"Today you're lucky," Merrin said. "I'm feeling reckless." She was not feeling reckless. She was feeling as though she'd swallowed a hive of bees, but recklessness and terror feel much the same in the hands, and hers were steady enough.

They went out past the tithe barn, past the goose (who screamed at Zephyrine with personal conviction), and up the low rise where the barley began. Merrin stopped at the boundary stone and made Zephyrine stop too.

"Before we go in," she said, "I want you to tell me what you see. Honestly. Don't be polite. Politeness is how this place gets away with it."

Zephyrine looked out over the field for a long moment. The morning sun had come up behind the mist and turned everything the colour of weak honey, and the barley moved in a wind that touched nothing else—not the hedges, not the thistles by the track, only the barley, in slow waves, like something breathing under a blanket.

"It's the wrong field," Zephyrine said slowly. "It's August. Everything else—" They gestured back down the slope, at the browned pasture, the drooping beans, the cracked margins of the drainage ditches. "Everything else is dying politely. This looks like June. This looks like a painting of June." They crouched, pulled off a glove, and pressed their palm flat against the soil without quite touching the stalks. "The ground's damp. It hasn't rained in six weeks. Merrin, the ground is *damp.*"

"Hasn't rained properly since May," Merrin agreed. "Not in the fen. Rained fine everywhere else. We get drought years same as anyone—we just don't get drought harvests."

"And no one finds that strange?"

"Oh, people find it strange," Merrin said. "People find it strange the way you find your own face strange in a photograph. You notice, and then you put it away, because what are you going to do about it? Grow hungry on purpose?" She walked along the stone's edge, trailing her fingers on its lichen-crusted top. "My gran used to say the fields drink from somewhere else. She said it laughing. Everyone says it laughing. That's the rule, really—if you're going to say the true thing, you have to laugh while you say it, or someone will come round with pie and change the subject."

Zephyrine straightened. Their eyes had gone sharp, the way they did when Tam sang, that terrible grateful attention. "Merrin. Why are you showing me this?"

Because I can't carry it alone. Because my mother carried it alone and it ate her. Because you write things down and things written down exist even when the people who knew them don't.

"Because you asked about the bell," Merrin said, "and everyone lied to you, and I'm tired of being one of the liars. Come on. There's more, and I want to show you before I lose my nerve, because I will lose my nerve, I always do."

* * *

The vestry door was locked, but the lock was older than sin and Merrin had been picking it since she was eleven, back when she used to hide in there to read and imagine the ledger was a book of fairy stories. She let them both in through the side door of St. Aldhelm's-in-the-Water, whose roof had gone to the sky in 1947 and never come back, so that the little church stood open-headed now, grass growing in the nave, swallows nesting in the empty pulpit. It should have felt holy. Mostly it felt like a room that had given up pretending.

Inside the vestry it smelled of paper and mouse and cold iron. Merrin lit the paraffin lamp because the one window was furred with grime, and took the ledger down from the shelf where it sat between a box of candle stubs and a stuffed owl that had seen better centuries.

"This," she said, "is the part where you'll want to tell me I've got it wrong. Don't. I haven't."

She laid the ledger open on the table. It was a parish register, or had begun as one—baptisms, marriages, deaths in a copperplate hand going back past 1800. But from 1911 onward, a second hand had taken over the final pages, a different copperplate, finer, colder, and the entries changed.

Zephyrine read aloud, softly, the way people read in churches. "*Given to the bell: Edith Parry, voice entire. Given: Thomas Rooker the elder, voice entire. Given: —*" They stopped. Looked up. "Voice entire?"

"That's the phrase they use," Merrin said. "'Given.' Like a donation to the church roof. Except the roof got the bell money in 1911 and the bell got—" she waved a hand vaguely at the pages, at the century of names, "—the rest of us."

Zephyrine turned pages with hands that had gone very careful. Names, names, names. Some with dates. Some with small annotations in that cold hand: *sang along at the feast; marked; given the following spring.* Some with nothing but a name and the word *given*, and beside a few of those, in later pencil, in different hands—granddaughters' hands, great-grandsons' hands—a tiny drawn flower, or a star, the way children mark graves of people they almost remember.

"There's one roughly every generation," Merrin said. "Sometimes two, if the harvest's been greedy. My mother's name is here. 1998." She tapped it before she could stop herself, and the tap sounded horribly loud in the little room. "And there's a line after hers. Blank. Waiting."

Zephyrine had gone very still, in the particular way of someone trying not to look at the thing they're looking at. "Merrin, these are death records. These are—this is a hundred years of—"

"Don't say it loud," Merrin said quickly. "I know it sounds mad. I know how it sounds. I've heard how it sounds in my own head for years, and I always end up deciding I imagined the worst of it, because that's what happens here, you decide you imagined it, everyone decides they imagined it, and the fields stay green, and the pies keep coming." She laughed, because that was the rule, laugh when you say the true thing, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, and she didn't fix it. "You're the first person in my whole life I've shown this who isn't in it. Do you understand? Everyone in this village is either in this book or related to someone in it. You're the only pair of eyes in fifty miles that isn't already bought."

Zephyrine looked at her for a long moment. Then they did the thing that undid her completely, the thing she'd been bracing against since the day they arrived: they took out their notebook and began, gently, to copy the names.

"You're writing them down," Merrin said. Her voice did something embarrassing.

"That's what I do," Zephyrine said simply. "Someone should know them. All of them. Even if—" They paused over the page. "Even if knowing turns out to be dangerous. I've suspected since the second night that asking questions here is a bit like knocking on ice. But Merrin—an archive is just love with a filing system. Nobody should be a blank line."

Merrin turned away and pretended to fuss with the lamp, because her eyes had gone hot and stupid, and because the last line of the ledger lay open on the table between them, blank as an open mouth, and she could not bear to watch Zephyrine's shadow fall across it.

"1911," Zephyrine said instead, mercifully practical, tracing upward. "It starts in 1911. Same year as the bell. The melting." They flipped back through the early pages, comparing hands. "Who wrote this part? The vicar?"

"Old Father Ambrose. He's the one who sold the bell for scrap—or that's the story. Melted down for the war effort, except 1911 wasn't the war yet, so nobody quite knows what scrap it was for." Merrin hesitated. "There's a letter. In his papers. I've never been able to make myself read it all the way through. It's in the bottom of that cupboard, under the owl, and I've picked it up maybe four times in my life and put it down four times."

Zephyrine was already kneeling at the cupboard.

"Merrin," they said, after a moment, in a voice gone quiet and strange, "there are wax cylinders down here. Dozens of them. Labelled. Dated." They lifted one into the lamplight, turning it. "This one says *Edith Parry, given, 1911.*"

The lamp flame leaned in a draught that hadn't been there before. Somewhere above them, out in the empty tower where no bell hung, something shifted its weight.

Merrin found she was gripping the table's edge hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and that Zephyrine was looking up at her with the expression of a man who has just realized the map he's been drawing is of the inside of a trap.

"I told you," she managed. "I told you I'd lose my nerve. Too late now, though, isn't it? You've seen the book." Outside, far off across the water meadows, the ordinary afternoon went on—crows, wind, the goose screaming at nothing. "Now you're one of the ones who knows. And the ones who know don't get to leave at Thursday."
