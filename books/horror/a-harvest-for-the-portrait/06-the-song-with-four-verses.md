## 6. The Song with Four Verses

Tam Rooker's cottage sat at the crook of the fen road where the land gave up pretending to be anything but marsh. Zephyrine had been told—by Merrin, by the innkeeper, by a woman selling damson jam who spoke as though delivering a weather report—that Tam was dying, and that he'd been dying since spring, and that he found the whole business tedious.

"He'll see you," Merrin had said, walking Zephyrine out along the raised path between the ditches. "He's the only one who will. But mind how you ask him for songs. He can smell a request like a fox smells a trap."

"And if I don't ask?"

"Then you'll get nothing at all. He's contrary, not generous." She had paused on the little plank bridge, looking down into the black water where something pale moved—fish, probably, probably—and added, quieter: "He sang to me once. When I was small. Nobody else would."

Zephyrine had wanted to ask what she meant by *would*, but the word hung in the air the way the bell's name did in every conversation here, a hole shaped exactly like itself, and so they had walked on in silence, boots knocking the boards, the reeds hissing their endless dry applause.

Now Zephyrine stood at Tam's door with the recorder bag heavy on one shoulder and knocked, and from within came a voice like gravel rolled in honey: "It's open, unless you're the doctor, in which case it's locked and I'm dead."

The cottage smelled of woodsmoke, peat, and something sweeter underneath—dried hops, maybe, or old honey. Tam Rooker lay propped in a chair by the window like a man who had arranged himself there deliberately and intended to be carried out the same way. He was very thin and very brown, hands like tree roots folded over a blanket, and his eyes were quick and amused and missed nothing.

"You're the song-catcher," he said. "Merrin's outsider. Sit down before you fall down, you hover like a heron."

Zephyrine sat. "I'm not here to catch anything," they said, which was a lie so polite it barely counted. "I'm here to listen. There's a difference."

"There is," Tam agreed, "and it's smaller than you think." He studied them for a long moment, the way an old farmer studies weather. "You've got ink on your fingers. Left hand. You write when you talk. Nervous habit or love of it?"

"Love of it," Zephyrine said, and was surprised to hear no hesitation at all.

"Good. Then we'll get on." He shifted against his pillows with the sound of dry sticks. "Ask me nothing. Just make your tea, and let an old man talk, and keep your machine quiet until I tell you otherwise."

* * *

He talked for an hour first, and Zephyrine understood afterward that this too was part of the song—the way a tune has a key it must be set in before the notes mean anything. He talked about Marrowfen before the drains, when the fields drank the sky and gave back barley anyway; about his father, who could call curlews so faithfully they landed on his shoulders; about the year the eels came up the ditches in numbers that made the water look solid, and everyone ate eel until they dreamed of eel, and nobody thought it strange.

"Everything here comes in seasons," he said. "Even silence. You'll have noticed our silence. It's got a season too. Dusk to dawn, mostly, and always around the church." His eyes flicked to Zephyrine's face, testing. "You've heard it ring."

"I've heard a bell," Zephyrine said carefully.

"Mm. And what did the parish record say, when you went digging? I know you went digging. Outsiders always dig. It's why we like you and why we watch you."

"That the bell was melted down in 1911. Sold for scrap. The receipt survives."

"The receipt survives," Tam repeated, delighted. "A receipt. Against a bell." He laughed, which turned into a cough, which he rode out with the patience of a man who had made peace with his own machinery. "Paper's brave stuff. Paper says whatever we need it to say. Now—" he lifted one root of a hand and pointed at the recorder bag, "—you may take it out. Wind it up, or charge it, or whatever young things do to machines these days. But it stays off until I say."

Zephyrine unpacked the recorder with the reverence other people reserved for communion silver. Set it on the table between them, microphone angled toward the chair, red light dark and waiting.

"Now then." Tam closed his eyes. "This is the harvest song. All of Marrowfen knows it, which is to say all of Marrowfen knows pieces of it, which is to say I'm the last one who knows it whole—or near whole, and we'll come to the hole in it presently. My mother taught me. Her mother taught her. It's older than the church, older than the drains, older than the village probably, because songs are older than the people who sing them. Songs are the only thing that gets born old."

He opened his eyes. "Turn it on, lad. Let's give the machine something to chew."

The red light came on. Zephyrine's heart did its small professional leap—that flutter of the archive opening, of something about to exist twice—and they nodded once, and Tam Rooker began to sing.

His voice was wrecked and beautiful, a gate hinge oiled with honey. The melody was modal, old, built out of intervals that modern ears had to lean toward to believe. And the words—

*O the fen gives gold and the fen gives bread,*
*And the fen takes back what the living said,*
*So sing it low and sing it true,*
*For the fen remembers more than you.*

The first verse was a farmer's verse: sowing, weather, patience. The second was a wedding verse, bawdy enough that Tam winked at Zephyrine over the top of it, about a bride and a bridegroom and a haystack and a bet. The third—

The third changed. Zephyrine felt it change the way you feel a room go cold. The melody dropped a third, and Tam's ruined voice found a steadiness in it that his speaking voice never had, and the words came slow as footsteps:

*One voice for the field and one for the store,*
*And one for the door that opens no more,*
*And one for the one who must pay the fee—*
*Sing soft, my darling, and think of me.*

Tam stopped. The last note hung in the smoky air and did not so much fade as withdraw, like a hand pulled slowly back through a doorway.

Zephyrine realized they had stopped breathing somewhere in the second line of the third verse and restarted without noticing. Their pen had frozen mid-word on the pad. They looked at Tam, and Tam looked back with an expression of terrible gentleness.

"Well?" he said. "Is it in your box?"

"It's in the box," Zephyrine said. Their voice came out odd. "That third verse—I've never—I've collected four hundred versions of harvest songs across six counties, and none of them—"

"No," Tam agreed. "They wouldn't. That verse doesn't travel. Songs are like seeds, lad; some of them only grow in one soil." He reached for the mug of tea Zephyrine had made earlier and drank with visible effort. "You'll have noticed there ought to be a fourth."

"I noticed the structure wants one. Three verses is a choice, not a shape. Ballads this old almost always run in fours."

"Clever lad." He set the mug down. "There's a fourth verse. Every soul in this village over the age of forty knows there's a fourth verse, and every soul under it has been carefully kept from knowing, and I am going to tell you the most important thing anyone will tell you while you're in Marrowfen, so put down your pen and listen with the parts of you that aren't a folklorist."

Zephyrine put down the pen.

"The last verse isn't sung," Tam said. "It's owed."

The fire ticked. Outside, a moorhen complained about something on the ditch.

"Owed," Zephyrine repeated. "To whom?"

"To whom do you think?" Tam's eyes went, briefly, toward the window—toward the tower visible above the hedgerow, roofless, empty, patient. "Look. I'm a dying man and I haven't the time to spook you properly, though I can see you're already half-spooked, and good. Here's what I'll say, and then I'll say no more. The song has three verses you may sing as often as you like. Sing them at weddings. Sing them drunk. Sing them badly, that's traditional. But the fourth verse exists, and it is not a verse, it is an agreement, and agreements want signing, and a voice is a signature." He tapped his own throat with one finger. "This is mine. Signed long ago. See the mark?"

And he pulled the blanket and his shirt collar aside, and there, at the collarbone, in skin gone papery with age, was a thin ring of scar tissue—a circle, perfect as a coin, as though something had once rested against his throat and been lifted away leaving only its outline.

Zephyrine had seen that mark before. On Merrin. On the widow at the forge. They had told themselves it was a scald, a necklace burn, a childhood pox. They were running out of benign explanations, and some part of them that was honest knew it.

"Tam," they said quietly, "what happens if someone sings the fourth verse?"

"Someone does," Tam said. "Every generation. Once." He smiled, and it was the kindest and worst smile Zephyrine had ever been on the receiving end of. "But not today, and not by you, if you've any sense, and I think you have more sense than sense allows. So. You've got your three verses. Take them and be welcome. And when you play that machine back tonight—and you will, you won't help yourself—do it with the window shut."

"Why the window shut?"

"Because sound carries over the fen," Tam said, "further than you'd credit. Further than paper would ever admit." He settled back into his pillows, suddenly, visibly tired, the performance of vigor spent. "Now off with you. Come again Thursday and I'll teach you the fiddle tune that goes with the second verse, the one with the rude bit. A man should leave something behind worth the trouble of remembering him."

At the door, Zephyrine paused. "Tam. Why are you telling me this? Everyone else pretends the bell doesn't exist."

The old man looked at them for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes like a fish under ice.

"Because I'm owed nothing," he said, "and owing nothing, I can afford honesty. Good afternoon, song-catcher. Mind the third step, it's rotten."

* * *

The walk back along the fen road took twenty minutes and Zephyrine spent all of them replaying the third verse in their head, which they recognized, even as they did it, as exactly the sort of thing a sensible person would stop doing. The words stuck the way burrs stick—not painfully, just persistently, riding home on you without your permission.

*One voice for the field and one for the store,*

The evening was coming down golden and ordinary. Smoke stood up straight from the chimneys. Somewhere a dog was being told, at length, what someone thought of it. Marrowfen at dusk was a painting of a village, and Zephyrine hated how much they wanted to live inside the painting.

Back at the inn, Merrin was stacking chairs in the taproom. She glanced up, saw Zephyrine's face, and set down the chair she was holding.

"He sang for you."

"Three verses."

Her shoulders came down half an inch. "Three's all right. Three's safe." Then, sharper: "Did he sing the fourth?"

"He refused. Said it isn't sung. Said it's owed." Zephyrine watched her face do something complicated and settle on careful blankness. "Merrin, what does that mean?"

"It means Tam Rooker is the bravest old fool in the fen," she said, "and that you should eat your supper and go to bed." She picked the chair back up. Her knuckles were white around its back. "And Zeph—whatever you do with that machine tonight, don't turn it up loud."

She carried the chair into the corner as though it had personally offended her, and would not meet Zephyrine's eye again.

* * *

Upstairs, with the window shut as instructed and the lamp turned low, Zephyrine set about the work that was, in truth, the only prayer they had ever believed in. Headphones on. Tape cued. The little green level-meters trembling.

First pass: documentation. Timestamps, verse divisions, melodic transcription notes in the margin of the pad. *Modal, Mixolydian inflection. Verse 3 drops to plagal register. Tempo halves.* The familiar liturgy of the archive, each notation a small act of devotion: *this existed, therefore I wrote it down, therefore it cannot be wholly lost.*

Second pass: listening. Really listening, the way they'd been taught by a professor long dead who used to say that a recording is a letter from the past and you must read it twice—once for what it says and once for what it's hiding.

On the second pass, Zephyrine heard the breathing.

Not Tam's. Tam's breath was audible throughout, wheezing in the gaps between phrases, human and labored and unmistakable. This was beneath that. Underneath the wheeze, underneath even the tape hiss, there was another respiration—slow, deep, unhurried, the breathing of someone with all the time in the world—and threaded through it, so faint that Zephyrine first mistook it for the inn's floorboards settling, a hum.

They rewound. Played it again. The hum resolved, note by note, into a melody—the same melody, the harvest song's melody, but sung a half-beat behind Tam, a shadow following a body. And where Tam's voice stopped after the third verse, trailing off into coughing and the scrape of the teacup, the shadow-voice did not stop.

It kept going.

Zephyrine's hands were very still on the desk. The lamp buzzed. Outside, far off across the fen, something that was not a dog and not a bird made one low, round note, like the beginning of a word.

The fourth voice was singing the fourth verse.

It was an old woman's voice, cracked and sweet, singing words in a dialect Zephyrine had only ever seen in nineteenth-century glossaries—vowel sounds dead a century, grammar the parish registers had outlived. They couldn't catch all of it. The tape seemed to hold it just below the threshold of clarity, the way a pond holds a stone: you could see the shape of it, feel the weight of it, but every attempt to bring it fully into the light blurred it. Except—

Except two lines surfaced, clear as bells:

*...and the last shall lend what the first ones lent,*
*a name for a name till the name is spent...*

Zephyrine tore off the headphones.

The room was silent. The lamp buzzed. Their heart was going hard enough that they could feel it in their teeth, in the scarless skin of their collarbones, in the pen-hand that had begun, without any decision on their part, to move across the pad—transcribing phonetically, automatically, the archivist's reflex firing on its own like a leg kicked at the knee.

They looked down at what they had written. Two lines, in their own neat hand, in a dialect they did not speak:

*and the last shall lend what the first ones lent,*
*a name for a name till the name is spent.*

Below that, the pen had started a third line. It had gotten as far as a single letter—an elegant, old-fashioned capital, copperplate, not Zephyrine's handwriting at all—before they made themselves put the pen down.

They sat for a long time. Then, because they were who they were—because a lifetime of writing things down so they would exist somewhere had built a person who could not leave a haunted tape unlistened-to any more than they could leave a book unfinished—they put the headphones back on.

They played it again.

And beneath the old woman's voice, fainter now, further down, there was another voice. And beneath that, another. Layers of them, going down like roots, like the strata of the fen itself, a century of singers packed into the magnetic dark of the tape, all of them singing, all of them waiting, and the deepest voice of all—so deep it might have been imagination, except that Zephyrine's pen had already written its first letter—was singing in a register that no human throat could reach, and it was singing, patiently, warmly, the way a host greets a guest arriving late to a feast—

it was singing Zephyrine's name.

Downstairs, the inn's clock struck nine. And across the roofs of Marrowfen, from a tower that held nothing at all, the bell rang once—soft, brief, satisfied, like a throat cleared before a conversation—

though it was hours yet until dusk had ever meant anything, and the sun was not even down.
