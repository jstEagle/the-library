## 7. What the Tape Keeps

The Blue Boar's best room had been given over to Zephyrine for the duration of their stay, which was generous of the innkeeper, since the room's chief virtue was a table and its chief flaw was everything else. The wallpaper had been fashionable sometime during the reign of a king whose name Zephyrine couldn't recall without checking. The radiator clanked like a small angry ghost with opinions about temperature. The window looked out on the square, the roofless tower of St. Aldhelm's, and the fen beyond, silvering now under a moon that was three nights from new.

Zephyrine arranged the equipment with the tenderness other people reserved for sleeping children. The reel-to-reel on the table. The cassette deck beside it. Headphones, spare batteries, the notebook, the good pen, the pencil for things that might still change. A mug of tea going lukewarm at the elbow. Outside, the last of the harvest lamps were going out one by one, the village folding itself away for the night the way a hand folds into a fist, gently, but with intent.

It was the fourth night of listening, and Zephyrine had developed a ritual. Tam Rooker's voice first — the harvest song, three verses, recorded in Tam's kitchen with the smell of woodsmoke and boiled ham in the air. Then the other tapes, the ones gathered in the churchyard and the smithy and the taproom, the songs the villagers had sung once each, quickly, glancing over their shoulders like people dropping coins into a well.

Zephyrine had now heard every song in Marrowfen exactly once. That was the rule. That was the rule everyone repeated with those identical smiles, the smiles Zephyrine had privately begun to think of as the Marrowfen Smile, the way one might think of the Marrowfen Accent or the Marrowfen Fog. *Never twice. A second singing calls something back.*

Folklorists lived for taboos. Zephyrine had spent eleven years chasing them — the village in Cornwall where you couldn't whistle indoors, the Welsh valley where a certain hymn was only ever sung facing west. But those taboos had been soft, worn smooth like pebbles, half-believed even by the people keeping them. Marrowfen's taboo was not soft. Marrowfen's taboo had edges. When Zephyrine had asked the blacksmith's widow, casually, over a pork pie, what exactly a second singing would call back, the widow had looked at Zephyrine for a long, long moment, and then she had said, "You don't want to know, love," in a voice so kind and so final that Zephyrine had written it down in the notebook and underlined it twice.

*You don't want to know, love.* The most frightening sentence in the English language, in Zephyrine's professional opinion, because it was always said by someone who did know.

Zephyrine threaded the reel. Tam's voice filled the room, cracked and warm as toast.

*"The scythe goes down where the barley's grown,*
*And the fen-wind sings it home—"*

Zephyrine mouthed along, as they had done every night. It was an occupational habit. You transcribed better if you sang under your breath; the mouth found the rhythms the ear missed. They had done it with sea shanties and lament lullabies and one truly alarming May Day song from the Cotswolds. Sing along quietly, note everything, harm nothing. That was the folklorist's covenant. The tape recorder was a net, not a hook.

Verse one. Verse two. Zephyrine's pencil moved.

Verse three, and here Zephyrine slowed the reel, because here — every night, at the same moment — there was something. A thickness in the recording. Tam's voice was a thin reedy instrument, seventy-eight years old and honest about it, and beneath it, under it, *inside* it, there was a second resonance, like a bell struck very far away, or a person singing in the next room with a door between. Zephyrine had noticed it on the first night and assumed tape hiss, or a lorry on the fen road, or Tam's kitchen clock. But the clock was in the kitchen and the recording had been made with the door shut, and the lorry, when Zephyrine had checked, had been a figment, because the fen road carried no lorries after dark. Nobody drove the fen road after dark. Zephyrine had asked why, and received the Marrowfen Smile.

So: the fourth night. Zephyrine turned the volume down until Tam was a murmur, then turned the gain up until the murmur's floor came forward, the way you can see stars by not quite looking at the moon.

And there it was. Not hiss. Not a lorry. A voice.

An old woman's voice, by its grain — or a voice that had been old for a very long time. It was singing. It was singing *words*, and the words were not Tam's words. The melody was the same melody, the harvest tune, but it had gone on past where Tam stopped. It had gone on into the place where the fourth verse should have been, the verse Tam refused, the verse that wasn't sung, that was *owed* — and it was singing it, patiently, in a dialect so old that Zephyrine caught only one word in five and had to play it again, and again, pencil flying.

*"An' who shall pay the fen its fee,*
*An' who shall pay the fen,*
*But them as sang the barley down,*
*Shall sing the barley again—"*

Zephyrine sat back. The radiator clanked. The tea had gone properly cold.

The dialect was dead. That was the thing that made the back of Zephyrine's neck prickle, professionally, deliciously, the way it had pricked at every genuine find of their career. Zephyrine knew the sound of dead dialects the way a sommelier knows vintages. The vowels Tam used were modern Marrowfen, flattened and fen-drained. But this voice rounded them the old way, the way folk in the fens had sung before the schools and the wireless and the war had sanded them smooth. Nobody under ninety spoke like that. Nobody under ninety *could*. And this voice wasn't ninety. This voice, if Zephyrine had to bet the grant money, was a hundred and ten, a hundred and twenty, singing with the full strength of a woman in her prime, which was its own impossibility, because voices didn't work that way, tape didn't work that way, *nothing* worked that way.

Zephyrine did what any rational person would do at one in the morning with a haunted reel turning on the spools: they put on the headphones and listened to it eleven more times.

By the sixth time they had the verse down. By the ninth they had the dialect half-transliterated with guesses and question marks. By the eleventh they noticed the thing they should have noticed on the first pass, the thing that turned the delicious prickle at the neck into a cold hand laid flat between the shoulder blades.

The voice was getting louder.

Not on the tape. The tape was a fixed object, a fossil, sound pressed into oxide like a fern into stone. But each time Zephyrine lifted the headphones to check the room, the room seemed to hold a residue of the melody, a hum in the wallpaper, in the radiator, in Zephyrine's own sternum. Or perhaps that was just the ear's afterimage, the way a bright window prints itself on the eye. Zephyrine was a scientist, more or less, of a small and gentle science, and scientists do not leap at afterimages.

They wrote in the notebook instead: *Fourth voice present on Tam R. recording, beneath primary vocal, verse 4 (unwitnessed, unwritten). Dialect archaic — cf. 1890s fen collections. Voice appears to strengthen with repetition. Note for Dr. Achebe: is repeated playback a form of repetition? Does the taboo apply to the machine?*

Zephyrine looked at that last question for a while.

Then, because the night was deep and the tea was cold and the curiosity was a living thing in the chest with its own small heartbeat, Zephyrine took off the headphones, set them down, and did the thing the whole village would have told them not to do, if Zephyrine had been foolish enough to describe the situation, which they were not.

They pressed play on the external speaker, and let the fourth voice sing aloud in the Blue Boar's best room.

It was different out loud. That was the first thing. Through the headphones it had been a specimen, a moth pinned under glass, fascinating and contained. Through the little speaker it was a *presence*. The old woman filled the room the way lamplight fills a room, without effort, without edges. The dialect rang off the wallpaper. *"An' who shall pay the fen its fee—"*, and the radiator went silent, as if it were listening, and the moonlight through the window seemed to lean in.

Zephyrine's hand hovered over the stop button. Did not press it. The folklorist's covenant: record everything, interrupt nothing. You never stopped a singer mid-verse. It was rude. It was, Tam had said once, *unlucky*, and Tam had said it with the Marrowfen Smile, and Zephyrine was beginning to understand that the Smile was not a smile at all but a flinch wearing a smile's clothes.

The voice finished the verse. The reel ran on into silence. Zephyrine reached out and stopped it, and the click of the button was very loud, and the silence after it was louder.

Then, from outside, across the square, from the roofless tower of St. Aldhelm's where no bell had hung since 1911 —

The bell tolled. Once.

Zephyrine's watch said twenty past one. The bell rang at dusk. Merrin had told them that on the second night, whispering it over the bar with a cloth in her hands, wringing it like a chicken's neck: *dusk, always dusk, and never count the tolls, whatever you do, don't count them.* Zephyrine had counted them anyway, every night, because they were who they were — four, always four, four tolls at dusk like the four verses, and Zephyrine had written *4 = 4?* in the notebook with a question mark that now felt prophetic.

But this was not dusk. This was one o'clock in the morning, and the toll was single, and it hung over the fen like a held breath.

And beneath Zephyrine's window, the village began to wake.

Not all at once. Doors first — the soft latch-click of doors opening, one, two, five, more. Footsteps on cobbles. A low murmur of voices, urgent, hushed, the sound a village makes when it is frightened and does not want the thing it fears to know it. Zephyrine went to the window and looked down into the square, and what they saw there made the cold hand between the shoulder blades close its fingers.

The villagers were coming out of their houses in their nightclothes and their coats over the nightclothes, and they were all — all of them, a dozen, two dozen — with their hands pressed to their own collarbones. Not clutching their chests, not rubbing their necks against the cold. Pressing the heels of their hands to the hollow at the base of the throat, to the place where — Zephyrine had seen it, had catalogued it, had thought it a scar or a birthmark or an old scald — a faint pale ring circled the skin like the shadow of a yoke.

Merrin was there in the square, hair loose, cardigan thrown over her nightdress, and she was looking up at the tower with her hand at her collarbone and her face white as the moon. Old Tam stood in his doorway, and even at this distance Zephyrine could see that his hand was at his collar too, and that he was not looking at the tower at all. He was looking up. At the Blue Boar. At Zephyrine's window.

Zephyrine stepped back from the glass, heart hammering, and the movement caught the lamplight, and Zephyrine saw — or thought they saw — Tam's chin dip in something that was not quite a nod. *Acknowledgment. Recognition.* The look a man gives a window he has been expecting.

The murmur in the square rose and fell. Zephyrine couldn't make out words, only its shape, and the shape was the shape of a question being asked over and over: *what was it, what was it, what was it.* And then Merrin's voice, clear across the still air, pitched to carry, and Zephyrine would swear on the grant paperwork it was pitched at the window:

"Who's playing it? Who's got it *playing*?"

Silence in the square. Then the widow's voice, older, steadier, terrible in its calm: "It's the tape, girl. It'll be the tape. Go back to your beds. It's only heard us once more, that's all. Only once more."

*Only once more.* As though the bell had been listening all along, and the song had simply been a knock at a door it already knew the address of.

The square emptied slowly, the way water empties from a lock, and Zephyrine stood at the window until the last door clicked and the last lamp died, and then they sat down at the table and looked at the reel-to-reel for a long time.

It was a machine. It was oxide and plastic and a VU needle that had never once pointed anywhere alarming. Zephyrine had carried it up three flights of stairs from a car park in Norwich and it had never done anything but faithfully reproduce what it was given. It was a net, not a hook.

But.

But the widow had known, instantly, without being told, without seeing the machine, that the tape was the cause. But the bell had rung at one in the morning, once, off schedule, for the first time in — Zephyrine flipped back through the notebook, through four nights of careful entries — for the first time, as far as anyone had said, ever. But every marked soul in the village had woken with their hands at their scars, as though the song had reached into their sleep and turned them over like stones.

Zephyrine picked up the pencil. Put it down. Picked up the pen, the good pen, the one for things that were true.

*Correction to the covenant,* they wrote. *The recorder is not a net. Or: it is a net, but the thing in the fen does not distinguish between catching and calling. Documentation is not neutral. A recording is a repetition. A repetition is a summons. I have been knocking on doors for four nights and tonight something answered.*

They sat with that. The radiator resumed its clanking, aggrieved, ordinary. Somewhere below, Merrin's footsteps crossed the bar below — she wasn't sleeping either, then — and paused, Zephyrine thought, at the foot of the stairs. Paused for a long moment. Then went on.

Zephyrine should stop. That was the sane conclusion, the conclusion any supervisor, any ethics board, any friend with sense would insist upon. Box the tapes. Lock the notebook. Leave in the morning, grant forfeit, dignity intact, and write the whole thing up someday as a charming piece about local superstition, *The Songs That Marrowfen Wouldn't Sing Twice*, and let the mystery keep itself.

Zephyrine looked at the reel. The fourth verse was written down now, in their own hand, in their own notebook. It was in the room. It was in the building. It was, if the residue-hum in the sternum was anything to go by, in Zephyrine.

*Shall sing the barley again.*

And here was the thing Zephyrine would not write down, not even with the good pen, not even alone at one in the morning with no one to see: when the old woman's voice had sung that line — *shall sing the barley again* — some part of Zephyrine, some small traitorous part down beneath the scholarship and the skepticism, had wanted to sing it back.

Zephyrine threaded a blank cassette instead. Dubbed the fourth voice onto it, labeled it, dated it, and then — hands steady, mostly — slid the whole collection into the equipment case and buckled the clasps.

The case sat by the door like a dog that knew it wasn't supposed to bark.

Zephyrine got into bed with the notebook and did not sleep, and at some hour before dawn, drifting, they caught themselves humming. Four bars. The melody of the verse no one in Marrowfen would sing, in a dialect no one alive could speak.

They stopped. They lay in the dark with their heart going.

They had never heard that tune before this week. They were almost sure they had never heard that tune before this week.

Outside, far off across the fen, something that was not an owl and not the wind sang four notes back.
