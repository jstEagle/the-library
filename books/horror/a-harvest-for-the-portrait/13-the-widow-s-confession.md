## 13. The Widow's Confession

The blacksmith's widow lived at the far end of Fen Lane, in a house that leaned toward the water the way old people lean toward a fire. Her name was Agnes Sowerby—everyone said it the same way, *Agnes Sowerby*, with a small pause before it, as though the name were heavier than most—and she was, by Merrin's careful count and her own cheerful admission, ninety-one years old.

She was also, Zephyrine had noticed at the harvest feast, the only marked villager who had looked at them all evening without flinching.

Everyone else had raised their mugs and smiled their too-uniform smiles and toasted the folklorist who was going to put Marrowfen's songs on paper where they'd be safe forever. But Agnes Sowerby had sat by the fire with her cider untouched, watching Zephyrine over the rim of it, and her expression had not been welcome or dread or any of the flavors of fear Zephyrine had catalogued so far. It had been something closer to recognition. The look of a woman seeing weather she remembered.

So on the morning after the feast, with the bell's thirteen tolls still lodged somewhere behind Zephyrine's sternum like a swallowed stone, they walked down Fen Lane and knocked on her door.

* * *

"I wondered when you'd come." Agnes opened the door before the second knock finished. She was small and straight-backed, with white hair cut short as a hedge and hands that had clearly spent decades doing exactly what they pleased. "Come in, then. Mind the cat. He thinks he owns the settle and he's right."

The cottage smelled of woodsmoke and beeswax and, underneath it, something older—the dry mineral smell of the fen itself, of peat and standing water. A tabby cat regarded Zephyrine from the hearth with imperial indifference. On the walls hung horseshoes, dozens of them, arranged in patterns Zephyrine itched to photograph: concentric rings, spirals, one wall covered entirely in a lattice that hurt the eye if you followed it too long.

"My husband's work," Agnes said, following their gaze. "He shod every horse in three parishes and made shoes for the ones nobody rode anymore, because he said iron remembers being useful and likes to be reminded." She snorted. "Folk thought him odd. Folk here think everyone odd who isn't frightened in the approved manner."

"And you're not frightened?"

"Sit down, lad, and I'll tell you what I am instead." She lowered herself into the chair by the fire with the economy of someone who had done it ten thousand times, and gestured Zephyrine into the settle. The cat immediately relocated onto Zephyrine's knee, which felt less like affection than surveillance. "I'm tired. There's a difference. Frightened people run about. Tired people talk."

Zephyrine got out the recorder, hesitated, and set it on the table between them, unplugged. A courtesy. An offering.

Agnes looked at it for a long moment. "That little box has been eating this village for a week," she said, "and now it wants to eat me too. Well. Let it try. I've been eaten by better."

* * *

"You've seen the ledger," she began, without preamble. "Merrin showed you. Don't look surprised—nothing happens in Marrowfen that I don't hear about by breakfast. Nineteen-eleven. That's where it starts, and I'll thank you to write the date properly, because the vicar's copperplate makes it look tidy and it wasn't tidy. It was hunger. Plain, stupid, ordinary hunger."

The fire popped. The cat's purr deepened and steadied against Zephyrine's knee, absurdly domestic.

"There'd been three bad years running. The floods took the low fields in '09, the blight took what the floods left in '10, and by the winter of '11 there were children in this village with the distemper coming on because thin blood catches sickness first. Eleven families had already gone—walked out on the fen road with everything they owned on their backs, and half of them never reached anywhere. You could stand where the church stands now and count the empty chimneys. Smoke tells you where life is, and there wasn't much smoke."

Her voice was level, unhurried, the voice of a woman reciting something she had turned over so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone.

"The Reverend Mr. Ellery—Vicar Pargiter's grandfather in office, though not in blood, the Pargiters came later—went up into the tower in February of 1911 and he didn't come down for three days. When he did, he called the village together and told them God had shown him a way. There was a bell in the tower then. Old, cracked, useless for calling anyone to anything. He said the bell could be given a new voice—not bronze, but something better—if the village gave it theirs. Their singing voices. All of them, freely offered, sung into the metal while it melted. And in exchange—" she spread her small, capable hands— "the fields would keep them."

"Freely offered," Zephyrine repeated. The words tasted strange.

"That's what he said." Agnes's eyes were very blue and very old in the firelight. "And here is the thing you must understand, lad, the thing your clever machine will never catch: some of it *was* free. Old Widow Crane went first, sang herself hoarse into that crucible, wept and laughed doing it, because her boys had died in the floods and she wanted to be of use even with nothing left to be useful *with*. Half the village sang because they believed. The other half sang because the ones who believed were watching, and hunger makes cowards of us all, and there's no shame in it—or there shouldn't be, but shame keeps better than grain, so we kept plenty."

She paused. The cat stretched, claws pricking briefly through Zephyrine's trousers, a small bright pain that anchored the room.

"The bell was poured in April. And that May, the barley came up through mud that should have poisoned it, and it came up *thick*. You've seen the fields. You've seen what they do. Every year since, whatever the sky does, the ground keeps its promise. Drought, flood, the wet rot summer of '58 when the whole county lost its wheat—we didn't lose a row. Not one row."

"And the price?"

"One voice a generation." Agnes said it the way you'd say *one spoonful of medicine a day*. "The bell takes a singer. Someone who's sung along—one of the marked, one of us with the ring at the collarbones. They go up to the tower on the new moon nearest the harvest, and they sing the last verse, the verse that isn't written down because writing it down would be cheating, wouldn't it, and the bell keeps them. Keeps the voice. And the voice goes into the ground with the next sowing, and the ground pays out, and the village eats, and forgets to ask why the barley never fails."

* * *

Zephyrine found their own voice had gone quiet somewhere in the last few minutes. When it came back, it came back carefully. "You said 1968."

Agnes smiled. It was not a happy smile, but it was not unhappy either; it was the smile of someone greeting an old dog. "I did."

"You were given in 1968. But you're sitting here."

"Oh, I went up the tower right enough. New moon, October, my thirty-ninth year." She touched her collarbone, and beneath the wool of her shawl Zephyrine saw the pale ring shift. "Sang the last verse from top to bottom, every note true, because by then I'd been humming it in my sleep for years and didn't know why. And the bell took what it was owed." She tilted her head. "Or most of it."

"Most of it?"

"It wanted my voice. My *singing*, if you follow me—the part of me that knew the songs, that carried them. And it took my memories along with it, because memories are what songs are packed in, like eggs in straw. Everything before that night is—" she made a gesture, opening and closing her hand— "a drawer somebody's emptied. I know I had a mother. I know I had a husband, Tom Sowerby, because he was standing right there and he stayed standing there forty more years and buried me in every way but the grave. But I don't remember marrying him. I don't remember learning to shoe a horse beside him, though these hands clearly did the work. It's like reading a letter about someone else's life and knowing the handwriting is yours."

The fire settled. Somewhere outside, a rook complained about the morning.

"What do you remember?" Zephyrine asked softly.

"The singing." The answer came instantly, without thought, and Agnes seemed almost startled by her own certainty. "That's the cruel joke of it, see. The bell took everything except the one thing it was collecting. I don't remember my wedding day, but I remember every song ever sung in this village, note-perfect, back to before my grandmother's time—which is impossible, because those songs weren't mine and some of them predate me by a century. They're in me like water's in a well. And when I sing them—" her voice caught, just once, a hairline crack in old plaster— "it feels like home. Like the home I can't remember. That's what it gives back, do you see? It hollows you out and fills the hollow with belonging. And belonging, lad, is the sweetest thing there is to be full of."

Zephyrine thought of the harvest feast. The mug pressed into their hand. The seat saved at the long table. The way the warmth of it had gone through them like brandy, and how they had wanted it, how they were wanting it still, and how the ledger's blank line waited downstairs in the vestry, filling in letter by letter.

"Yes," they said. "I see."

* * *

"Do you?" Agnes leaned forward, and suddenly she was not a tired old woman by a fire but something sharper, a blade long kept oiled. "Then answer me this, folklorist. Was the village wicked? In 1911. Go on. Answer."

Zephyrine opened their mouth. Closed it.

"Because I've had fifty-six years to work on the question," Agnes continued, settling back, "and I'll save you the trouble. Yes and no. Yes, because they knew. After the first good harvest, they *knew* what the price was—they watched the first singer walk up the tower steps and they let her go, and they ate well that winter, and they taught their children the song with the fourth verse missing, and they called it tradition. That's wickedness wearing its Sunday clothes. But no—" and here her voice dropped, gentled— "no, because they were starving, and because the thing that came out of that melting pot wasn't the devil with horns. It was worse. It was *reasonable*. It never lied to us. It never dragged anyone. Every generation it held out the same bargain, plain as a market stall: one voice, or your children's hunger. And every generation, decent, loving people chose the voice, and wept, and baked bread with the harvest, and loved each other as hard as they could to make up for it. Victim and hangman both, and the rope handed round for everyone to hold a turn."

The cat purred. Zephyrine's hands had gone cold around the recorder.

"Merrin's mother," Zephyrine said. "In the ledger. 1994."

"Edith." Something moved behind Agnes's eyes, old grief moving like a fish under ice. "Aye. Merrin was marked at six—you get marked young sometimes, the ones born with the ear, the ones who hum before they can talk. Edith fought it for twelve years, longer than anyone. Then the blight scare of '94, the whole valley watching our fields like crows, and Edith went up the tower in her daughter's place and sang herself into the bronze so her girl could grow up ordinary." Agnes shook her head slowly. "Ordinary. As if there were such a thing in Marrowfen."

"She doesn't remember her mother at all, does she. Not really."

"Not a face. Not a voice. Just a shape at the edge of dreams that hums." Agnes looked into the fire. "And that's the mercy and the crime of it, all in one. The bell doesn't kill what it takes. It *keeps* it. Perfectly. Forever. Down under the floorboards of that inn, in the dark, every voice still singing, still whole, still themselves—and none of them able to reach us, and none of us able to stop listening. Some nights I stand in my kitchen and I can hear Edith through the ground. She sounds happy, folklorist. That's the part I can't forgive. Not the taking. The happiness."

* * *

Zephyrine sat for a while without speaking. The cat had fallen asleep, a warm dead weight on their knee, and they found they hadn't the heart to move it.

"Why tell me this?" they asked finally. "The others won't say the bell's name. Won't count the tolls. Won't sing twice. And you're handing me the whole history like a loaf off your own shelf."

Agnes was quiet long enough that the fire had to fill the silence for her.

"Because you're already chosen," she said at last, simply. "Don't flinch. You've known since the feast—thirteen tolls and every scarred neck turning toward you like flowers finding the sun. I'm ninety-one, lad. I won't see another choosing, and I'll not spend my last season watching another young person walk up those steps believing it's holy, the way I believed it, the way Edith believed it, the way they all believe it because belief is the sugar on the pill." She reached across and laid her hand—small, iron-hard, gentle—over Zephyrine's cold ones. "You asked me once, at the feast, what I was instead of frightened. Here's your answer. I'm the only one in this village who remembers being taken. Everyone else remembers only the giving. And there's a world of difference between those two things, and the difference might matter to you, before the new moon."

"Before the new moon," Zephyrine repeated. "Which is—"

"Six days." Agnes withdrew her hand and picked up her cider at last, drinking with the deliberateness of a woman closing a meeting. "Six days, and the blank line in that ledger filling in a letter at a time, and you lying awake counting letters. I know how it goes. I watched the ink crawl in my own name in '68 and told myself it was the light playing tricks." She set the mug down. "Now. You'll want to go poking at things, because you're a scholar and scholars poke. So I'll tell you the one thing worth knowing before you go: the bell keeps what is sung to it. *Sung.* Not spoken, not written, not recorded on your little box. Sung. Whatever else you plan to do with your six days—start there. Ask yourself what it means, that the contract needs a voice. And ask yourself what it means that yours is the first voice in a hundred years it's wanted badly enough to hurry."

Zephyrine stood, dislodging the indignant cat, and at the door they paused.

"Agnes. If there were a way to end it—to break the bargain, free the voices, all of it—would the village thank you? Or hate you?"

The old woman considered the fire.

"They'd mourn," she said. "Loudly, honestly, and for years. Because the fields would go hungry and the winters would bite and they'd have to be *ordinary* again, and ordinary is the one crop Marrowfen never learned to grow." She looked up, and her blue eyes were fierce and kind and terribly tired. "But underneath the mourning, lad—underneath, where people keep the truth they can't afford—some of them would breathe. I did, every night since '68, in the one corner of myself the bell couldn't reach. Breathe, and wait, and hope somebody someday would be stubborn enough to sing it wrong."

Outside, the fen lay flat and silver under a high pale sun, the barley standing thick and impossible in soil that should have been poison. Six days. Zephyrine walked back up Fen Lane with the widow's words packed in their chest like eggs in straw, and did not notice until the inn was in sight that they were humming—softly, absently, a tune they had never learned, in a dialect dead a hundred years.

Four notes of the last verse. Sung wrong.

They hoped it was wrong.
