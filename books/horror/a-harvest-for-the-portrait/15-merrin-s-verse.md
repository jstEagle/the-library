## 15. Merrin's Verse

Merrin found Zephyrine in the inn's back parlor the next morning, sitting very still with a mug of tea they hadn't touched, staring at nothing in the particular way of someone who has spent the night listening to the inside of their own skull.

"You look terrible," she said, by way of good morning.

"I've been better." Zephyrine's laugh came out crooked. "I've also been considerably worse, which is the strangest part. Yesterday I would have told you I was having the most productive fieldwork season of my career. Now I'm sitting in a village that eats voices, wondering if the tea is safe."

"The tea's safe. Mum would rise from her grave if anyone watered down the Assam." Merrin sat down across from them and turned her mug slowly on the table, watching the ring of damp it left. "Though I'd not swear to the milk. Dad buys it off the Hollis farm, and the Hollis farm is—" she gestured vaguely at the window, at the fields lying green and fat under a sky that couldn't decide whether to rain, "—well. You've seen the fields."

"I've seen the fields." Zephyrine wrapped both hands around the mug as if it might warm something other than their palms. "Merrin. The widow told me everything. 1911. The vicar. The bargain. A voice every generation." They looked up, and their eyes were red-rimmed and ferocious. "Why are you telling me any of this? Why is *anyone* telling me? The whole village has spent a century keeping this quiet, and now it's all pouring out of people like the cork's finally off."

Merrin had asked herself the same question, lying awake after the widow's confession had spread through the marked households like woodsmoke through a hedge. She'd come up with an answer around four in the morning, and it wasn't a comfortable one.

"Because you're next," she said. "And because some of us are tired."

* * *

She took Zephyrine out the back way, past the duck pond where old Mrs. Tallow's ducks conducted their eternal territorial dispute, and up the lane toward the church. Not to the vestry—Zephyrine had seen enough of the ledger to last a lifetime—but to the lych-gate, where the yew tree grew thick and the bench beneath it had been worn smooth by a hundred years of villagers avoiding going inside.

"Sit," Merrin said. "I've something to tell you, and I'd rather tell it sitting down, because the last time I told it I was standing and I fainted into the font."

Zephyrine sat. Waited. The yew dripped last night's rain onto the gravel with a patience Merrin envied.

"I was marked when I was six," she said.

She watched it land. Watched Zephyrine's face do the thing faces did when a story they'd been hearing in the abstract suddenly put on boots and walked into the room.

"The scar?" Zephyrine said. "At your collarbones. But you said—Merrin, you're not old enough. The widow said the giving comes every generation, at the choosing—"

"I was six years old and I sang the harvest song at the school pageant. All the children did. That's what nobody tells you—the marking isn't a punishment or an honor. It's arithmetic. The bell hears every voice in Marrowfen eventually, and it keeps a list, and the list is longer than you'd think." Merrin pulled the neck of her sweater aside, showing the thin ring of scar tissue she'd spent her whole life hiding under high collars and clever neckerchiefs. "The vicar at the time—old Vicar Prowse, before the current one—came to our house and told my mother, very kindly, that the bell had taken an interest. That I was to be *given* at eighteen. That there was time to prepare. Time to teach me the songs properly, so the harvest would be a good one."

Zephyrine had gone very still. The stillness of a person trying not to bolt.

"What did your mother do?"

"She made me a promise." Merrin smiled, and it was a strange smile, she knew—one of the ones that had made the other village children call her odd behind her back. "She said: *No one is taking my girl's voice. Not the bell, not the vicar, not God Himself if He comes asking.* And then she went down to the vestry and she read the ledger, all of it, every page back to 1911, and she worked out the thing nobody in this village will say out loud."

"Which is?"

"That the contract doesn't care *whose* voice it gets. Only that it gets one. That it has to be given—freely, or at least freely enough—and that it has to be sung." Merrin's hands had gone cold in her lap. They always did, at this part. "My mother couldn't read music and she couldn't carry a tune in a bucket, which she'd have told you herself, laughing. But she could learn a verse. And the last verse—the one that's owed—she got it from the widow's mother, who'd heard it from the 1911 woman before the bell took her. And my mother learned it. Wrong. On purpose."

"Wrong on purpose," Zephyrine repeated. "What does that—"

"Here." Merrin stood, because this part you did standing, her mother had always said; you didn't teach a song sitting down like it was arithmetic homework. "I'll sing you what my mother sang me. And before you reach for that recorder—and I can see you reaching, Zephyrine Whitlock, don't think I can't—you'll listen first, and you'll understand why I've never once let anyone put this on tape."

Zephyrine's hand crept away from the satchel at their feet. "All right," they said. "I'm listening."

Merrin breathed in, the way her mother had taught her—not the singer's breath, deep and controlled, but a shallow, careless one, the breath of someone about to do a bad job on purpose.

And she sang.

It was the harvest song's melody, or the ghost of it. The words were almost the words—she could feel the true verse straining underneath like a fish under ice—but every third note sagged flat, and two of the lines were transposed, and she'd swapped the name of the fen for the name of the hill, because her mother had said *the bell is proud, love. Pride can't abide a botch.* Her voice cracked on the high part the way it always cracked, and she let it crack, and she sang the wrongness straight through to the end, where the true verse would have named its giver and closed like a trap.

Instead she ended on a note that resolved nowhere at all. A door left open. A sentence with no full stop.

The silence afterward felt, as it always did, like the whole fen holding its breath—and then, as it always did, letting it out.

"That's—" Zephyrine started, and stopped, and started again. "Merrin, that's the last verse. Or—it's wearing the last verse. It's *shaped* like it. But it won't—"

"It won't work," Merrin said. "That's the whole point. My mother spent three winters learning the true verse from the widow's mother, and then she spent three more learning to ruin it. Every wrong note chosen. Every mistake deliberate. She called it *singing it spoiled.* Like milk. Like meat left out in the sun." She sat back down on the bench, suddenly tired the way she always got after—the tiredness that lived in the scar, that hummed faintly whenever the true verse came near her throat. "Because here's the thing she worked out, sitting up nights with a baby in one arm and a ledger in the other: a contract is a set of words, and words can be said wrong. The bell is a bargain, and bargains have terms, and terms have *lettering.* And if the lettering's spoiled—if the verse is sung badly enough, wrongly enough, freely enough—"

"—the contract fails," Zephyrine whispered.

"The contract *curdles.*" Merrin nodded. "My mother sang it that way, at my eighteenth choosing, when the whole village stood in that roofless church waiting for me to walk up the aisle of stones. She stepped out of the crowd—my mother, who hadn't sung so much as a hymn in twenty years, who was tone-deaf as a turnip—and she sang the last verse wrong. Deliberately, gloriously wrong. And she gave her own name in the giving, but she gave it *inside the spoiled verse,* so the bell couldn't take it clean."

The yew dripped. Somewhere down the lane, Mrs. Tallow's ducks had escalated to open warfare.

"What happened?" Zephyrine's voice was barely there.

"The bell rang thirteen times and then it rang *fourteen,* which it had never done, which nobody had ever heard. And the fourteenth toll came out—wrong. Flat. Spoiled. Like the verse had infected it." Merrin laughed, a small unsteady sound. "And the harvest that year was the worst in living memory. Blight on the barley, rot in the apples, the fen flooding half the south field. The village was furious. They said my mother had cursed them. They didn't speak to us for a year. Dad had to sell the good cart."

"But you weren't taken."

"No. Because the bell couldn't take me—the verse that would have bound me had been sung spoiled, and the ledger line for my name filled in and then *blotted,* like ink refusing the paper. And it couldn't take her cleanly either, because she'd given herself inside the wrongness. So it took—" Merrin's throat closed, the way it did, the way it had for eleven years. She made herself finish. "It took her slow. Over three winters. She got quieter and quieter. Not sad-quiet. *Erased*-quiet. By the end she'd forget Dad's name, then mine, then her own, and the last thing she could still do—the very last—was hum. The spoiled verse. Wrong notes and all. She'd hum it in the kitchen while the kettle boiled, and she didn't know what it was anymore, but her throat did."

Zephyrine was crying. Merrin pretended not to notice, because that was the kindness her mother would have wanted, and because if she looked at it directly she'd start too, and one of them had to keep the shape of this morning.

"So you've spent your whole life—"

"Humming it. Learning it wrong. Teaching myself new ways to be wrong, every year, like a—like a craft." Merrin wiped her nose on her sleeve, undignified, unbothered. "I can sing it spoiled eleven different ways now. I used to think it was just—keeping her alive, I suppose. Keeping her one good trick alive. But now you've come along with your tapes and your ledger line filling itself in, and I'm thinking maybe Mum left me the tool and I never knew what it was *for.*"

Zephyrine looked at her for a long moment. Then they said, in the careful voice of someone testing ice: "Merrin. When you sang it just now. The spoiled verse." They swallowed. "I could *feel* it. In my collarbones. Where the scar's starting. It didn't hurt. Everything else—the true verse, the tape, the bell—it *hurts.* Yours felt like—"

"Like a door propped open," Merrin said. "I know. That's what Mum said. The spoiled verse doesn't pull. It *pushes.*"

* * *

They walked back down the lane together, and the morning had gone gold at the edges, the kind of light that made even Marrowfen's too-perfect fields look honest. Zephyrine kept stopping to write in their notebook—Merrin watched them transcribe the spoiled verse from memory, note by wrong note, and felt something in her chest she hadn't felt since her mother died: the specific, ridiculous hope of a plan.

"You understand what this means," Zephyrine said, capping the pen. "If the verse can be sung spoiled—if the contract can be curdled—then at the choosing, when the bell comes for me, I don't have to refuse. Refusal just means it takes a child instead. The vicar made that very clear." Their jaw tightened. "But if I sing it *wrong*—if I sing it your mother's way, and give my own name inside the wrongness—"

"Then the bell takes what you give it and chokes on it," Merrin said. "And the ledger line blots. And a hundred years of bargain comes down like a rotten beam." She looked at them sideways. "It might not work, you know. Mum's way bought me nothing but time and her. The bell's had a century to get clever."

"Then we get cleverer." Zephyrine smiled—tired, terrified, and absolutely alight, and Merrin thought: *oh no, I like them, that's the worst possible outcome, Mum would be delighted.* "Merrin—teach me. All eleven ways to sing it wrong. We've got until the new moon."

"Six days."

"Six days. Eleven wrong verses. One bell." Zephyrine stuck out their hand, absurdly formal, a scholar sealing a pact on a lane full of duck noise. "Partners?"

Merrin took it. Her scar hummed, faint and flat, like a string tuned to a note that didn't exist—and for the first time in her life, the hum sounded less like a warning and more like a chord waiting for its second voice.

"Partners," she said. "But we start with the hardest one first. The one Mum called the *laughing spoil.*"

"Why is it the hardest?"

"Because," Merrin said, already turning toward the inn, "you have to mean the laugh. And neither of us has laughed in days."
