## 17. New Moon

**Zephyrine**

The moon went out like a snuffed candle at half past six, and nobody in Marrowfen remarked on it.

Zephyrine noticed because they had spent the whole day noticing things—the way the bread on the inn's table was cut into thirteen slices no matter how many were eating, the way Merrin braided her hair with rowan berries she'd never worn before, the way old Tam Rooker's chair by the fire sat empty now, and how nobody moved it, as if the chair itself were in mourning.

"New moon," Merrin said, catching them staring out the window at the black disc occluding the stars. "Bell likes a dark sky. Says it can see its work better."

"Bells don't see."

"No," Merrin agreed pleasantly, and handed Zephyrine a mug of cider. "They don't."

That was the thing about Marrowfen that Zephyrine had finally stopped fighting: the villagers did not argue with the impossible. They set out saucers of milk for it. They held doors open for it. They treated it the way you'd treat a difficult uncle at Christmas—with weary courtesy and a firm sense of where everyone would be sitting.

And tonight, everyone knew where they'd be sitting. The roofless church.

Zephyrine drank the cider and found their hands were steady. That surprised them most of all. For three days—since the ledger's blank line had begun filling itself in letter by patient letter, since the widow Edith Crane had told them the whole rotten history with tears freezing on her cheeks—they had expected terror to arrive like weather. Instead there was this: an awful, ringing calm, and underneath it, so quiet they could almost pretend it wasn't there, a plan.

The plan lived in the notebook in their coat pocket. Not the field notebook—that one was for Marrowfen, for Merrin, for whoever came after. The plan lived in the margins of a hymnal they'd borrowed from the vestry, annotated in pencil in a shorthand only they could read, because some things you did not write down plainly even when your whole life had been built on writing things down plainly.

*Sing it wrong,* the margin said. *Sing it alone. Sing your name into it.*

*Make the contract yours and yours alone.*

It was not much of a plan. It had the structural integrity of a paper boat. But it was theirs, and after thirty-one years of being the person nobody planned around, Zephyrine found something almost funny in that.

"You're smiling," Merrin said suspiciously. She was lacing her boots with the grim efficiency of someone dressing for a funeral she intended to survive. "People don't smile on choosing night. It's considered bad manners."

"I'm practicing."

"For what?"

"Being brave. I hear it helps if you rehearse."

Merrin looked at them for a long moment, and then she laughed—a short, startled bark of a laugh, quickly smothered, but real. "You're the strangest person this village has ever tried to eat, Zephyrine Whitlock, and we once tried to eat a tax inspector."

* * *

**Merrin**

Merrin had been to eleven choosings. You didn't attend them all—some years the bell slept, and those years were remembered the way other villages remembered good harvests—but she'd seen enough to know the choreography. The whole village walked to the church at moonrise. The marked ones stood nearest the tower. The vicar read from the ledger. And somebody sang, or was made to sing, or simply opened their mouth and let the bell pour through them like water through a broken jug.

She had been four years old at her own choosing, standing between her mother's knees while the scar tissue bloomed cold at her collarbones, and her mother's hands had been shaking, and Merrin had thought at the time that her mother was frightened *for* her.

She had learned better later. Her mother hadn't been frightened. Her mother had been *deciding.*

Tonight her hands were shaking too, but not with fear. With fury, mostly, and under the fury something worse: hope. Hope was the dangerous one. Hope made you do stupid arithmetic—*if Zephyrine's plan works, then Mother's sacrifice meant something, then none of it was for nothing*—and stupid arithmetic got people killed.

"Stop fidgeting," said Edith Crane, taking her arm as they joined the procession. The widow was ninety-one and walked to a choosing the way she walked to everything, like a woman arriving slightly early for an appointment she disapproved of. "You'll wear a groove in the lane."

"It's a bad plan, Edith."

"It's a terrible plan." Edith patted her hand. "I've known the vicar fifty years and the bell a hundred and three, and I've never heard either of them mention a clause about singing wrong. But I'll tell you what I know about contracts, girl, having signed one: they are written by proud things. And proud things cannot bear to be laughed at."

"That's not a plan, that's a proverb."

"A proverb is a plan that survived," Edith said serenely, "longer than the people who needed it."

Ahead of them the village strung out along the fen road in the moonless dark, carrying lanterns they didn't need, because the church tower was already glowing. That was new. In every choosing Merrin had witnessed, the tower stayed dark until the moment of the singing. Tonight it shone from within like a lantern with a moth trapped inside, and the light wasn't any color light ought to be—it was the color of sound, if sound had a color, which apparently it did, and the color was silver.

"Oh," said Edith quietly. "Oh, it's keen for this one."

Merrin's stomach dropped. She searched the procession for Zephyrine and found them near the front, walking beside the vicar, chatting. *Chatting.* She caught a fragment—"no, really, the acoustics must be extraordinary"—and wanted to shake them and kiss them and lock them in the cider cellar until the century turned, possibly all three.

*Let them have the talking,* she told herself. *Talking is how they stay calm. Calm is how the plan works. Whatever the plan is. Which they still haven't fully explained, because they say explaining it aloud might 'spoil the surprise,' which is not reassuring, Zeph, it is the opposite of reassuring—*

But there was no time. The churchyard gate stood open, and the whole village funneled through it into the roofless nave, and overhead the empty tower filled with light.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

The church had no roof and hadn't for a hundred years; rain fell straight into the nave and moss grew in the shape of drowned congregations along the walls. The pews remained, black with rot, and the villagers sat on them anyway, generation after generation, because Marrowfen did not believe in letting a little decay interrupt a tradition.

Zephyrine stood before the vestry door, where a small table had been set up, and on the table lay the ledger, open to the last page.

The line was full now. Had been full since noon. Their own name sat there in copperplate that no human hand had written:

*Zephyrine A. Whitlock — given freely?*

The question mark was new. It had appeared sometime during supper, and Zephyrine had stared at it for a long time, feeling oddly flattered. Even the bell, it seemed, was waiting to hear what they'd say.

Around them the village settled into silence. Three hundred people, and you could have heard a moth land. The marked ones stood closest to the tower—Merrin, Edith, the Hollis brothers, a dozen others, their collarbones bare in the strange silver light, their scars gleaming like rings of pale coral. Behind them, the unmarked villagers held their children close, and Zephyrine saw in their faces not cruelty but exhaustion. This was the face of people who had been paying a bill for a hundred and twelve years and were tired down to the marrow of the marrow.

The vicar stepped forward. He was a mild man, gray as ash, and he had been kind to Zephyrine throughout—kind in the way of someone who has already decided the outcome and can therefore afford generosity.

"We are gathered," he began, in the tone of a man announcing whist results, "on the night of the new moon, in the hundred-and-twelfth year of the keeping, to witness the giving of a voice."

Above them, the empty tower rang.

Not tolled—*rang*, a great rolling peal with no bell in it, sound falling out of the sky like silver rain, and where the sound touched the air the moonlight gathered, and Zephyrine watched, heart hammering but hands still steady, as a bell assembled itself out of nothing above the tower. Sound given shape. Moonlight given weight. It hung there vast and translucent, turning slowly, and inside it—if you squinted, if you dared—you could see faces. Hundreds of them. Small as grains of rice. Singing.

The given voices. A century of them, kept.

Zephyrine felt Merrin's hand find theirs, grip hard, and let go. That was part of the choreography too. Merrin would stand with the marked. Zephyrine would stand alone. Alone was the whole point.

"The bell has chosen," the vicar said gently. "Zephyrine Whitlock. Will you sing?"

And here was the terrible calm, arriving right on schedule. Zephyrine looked up at the bell of light and sound, at the tiny faces pressed against its inner curve like children at a sweetshop window, and felt—not fear. Something older and stranger than fear. Recognition. Every one of those faces had been a person who wrote things down or sang things out loud or loved someone enough to speak. Every one of them had believed, once, that being recorded was a form of love.

So had Zephyrine. Still did. That was the plan, actually, at the bottom of it. Not defiance. Love, weaponized.

"Yes," Zephyrine said, and their voice carried in the roofless dark, clear as a struck glass. "I'll sing."

A sigh went through the village—relief, grief, hunger, all braided together. The ledger's ink shimmered. The bell descended a foot, luminous, expectant, and Zephyrine took a breath that tasted of fen water and apples and a hundred years of bought harvests, and thought: *Tam Rooker, thank you for three verses. Merrin, thank you for teaching me to hum it wrong. Edith, thank you for the proverb.*

*Mother, wherever you are, thank you for teaching me that being forgotten and being free might cost exactly the same.*

They opened their mouth to sing.

* * *

**Merrin**

Later, Merrin would try to describe the moment and fail, because language was a village tool and what happened next was not a village thing.

She would say: *they sang it wrong.* True, and useless. Everyone sang it wrong eventually; the bell corrected you, poured itself down your throat and sanded off your rough edges until the verse came out perfect and yours came out gone.

But Zephyrine sang it wrong *first*. Sang it wrong at the very top of the verse, wrong in the first note, wrong on purpose, wrong the way Merrin had practiced alone in her room for twenty years—flattened here, rushed there, a grace note shoved in where no grace note belonged—and the bell *stumbled.*

Merrin saw it stumble. Saw the great shining mass of it shudder like a horse hitting ice, saw the tiny faces inside press forward, saw the moonlight flicker—

And she saw Zephyrine, standing alone in the nave with their scarred collarbones blazing, weave something into the third line that made the whole churchyard go silent.

Their name. Sung not as the given but as the *giver*. Not *take my voice* but *I give, I alone, I sign, I bind*—the contract turned inside out like a glove, every clause addressed to a single throat, a single name, a single debt.

The ledger on the vestry table burst into flame that gave no heat and burned nothing but the final page.

"No," said the vicar, in a small voice, like a man watching his life savings blow off a table. "No, that's not—that's not how it's *done*—"

The bell rang once. Thirteen times. Once more, uncertain, like a clock losing its nerve.

And then it cracked—from top to bottom, a seam of darkness splitting the light, and out of the crack came the sound Merrin had waited her whole life to hear and never dared imagine: a hundred years of stolen voices, pouring out of the tower all at once, rising into the moonless dark like starlings off a winter field, scattering, wheeling, *going*—

One voice paused above her. An old woman's voice, in a dialect dead a century, and it hummed four notes—her mother's four notes, the ones her mother used to hum when she thought Merrin was asleep—and then it was gone, north over the fen, free.

Merrin was crying. She realized it distantly, the way you realize rain has started. Below her, at the center of everything, Zephyrine stood in the falling silver light with their mouth still open on the last note, and the light was going out of them, and the scar tissue at their collarbones was fading, fading, fading—

"Zeph," Merrin screamed, and started down the aisle, and the world tilted like a song sung twice—

* * *

**Zephyrine**

The last thing Zephyrine thought, clearly and calmly, in the space between one note and the next, was:

*I hope somebody writes this down.*

Then the bell broke, and the names went out of them like breath off glass, and Marrowfen began to forget.
