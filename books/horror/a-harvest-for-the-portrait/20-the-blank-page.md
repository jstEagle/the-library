## 20. The Blank Page

Merrin had always thought of the vestry as the most honest room in Marrowfen.

The church itself was a liar—roofless, open to every weather, its stones going soft and green like old fruit—but the vestry kept its little door shut against the sky, and inside it kept the truth: the ledgers, the parish records, the box of copperplate handwriting that had sold a village's voices one generation at a time. Her mother's name was in there. Tam Rooker's name. The widow's. A hundred years of people who had stood where Merrin now stood, holding a candle stub because the electricity in the vestry had never worked and nobody had ever quite dared to fix it.

She'd come at dawn, before anyone else was awake, because she wanted to be alone with it. With him. With whatever was left.

The ledger lay on the reading desk where they'd left it three nights ago, on the night of the new moon, when the whole village had crowded into the roofless church and watched Zephyrine Whitlock do the impossible thing. The book was still open to the last page. Of course it was. It had been open to that page for a century, patient as a mouth.

Merrin set her candle down and looked.

There was a name on the blank line.

*Zephyrine Whitlock.*

But it was fading. Even in the few seconds since she'd bent over the page, she could see it going—the ink lifting off the paper like breath off glass in winter, the letters thinning from confident black to grey to a ghost-grey to nothing at all. She put her finger near it, not touching, the way you'd warm your hands at a fire that was already dying.

"No," she said, out loud, like that would help. "No, you don't. You stay."

The *W* went first. Then the *h*. The letters didn't smear or run; they simply stopped being written, as though someone were un-writing them, drawing the pen backward through each stroke. She watched the whole name go in under a minute. What remained was the faintest shadow of an indentation, the memory of pressure, the way a page remembers a word long after the ink is gone—if you tilt it toward the light, if you know what you're looking for.

Merrin tilted the page toward the candle. There. Barely. A dent in the paper shaped like a person's name.

"You absolute idiot," she told the empty vestry, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, so it came out half scolding and half sobbing, which was about the truest thing she'd said all week. "You stupid, brave, impossible idiot. You couldn't have just signed it in pencil?"

She laughed then, wetly, alone in the dark little room, because that was the sort of joke Zephyrine would have made—or rather, the sort of joke Zephyrine would have written down in that careful looping hand of theirs, filed under some heading like *Local Humour, Harvest Period*, with a little note about context. Zephyrine had noted everything. That had been the whole trouble with them, and the whole wonder.

Merrin wiped her face on her sleeve and got to work.

* * *

The cellar of the inn smelled of cold earth and apples gone soft in their barrels, and it had always frightened her a little as a child—not for any reason she'd been allowed to know, but children know things anyway, the way dogs know weather. Now she went down the steps with the candle held high and no fear left in her to spend. Fear was for people who still had something to lose.

The shelf along the far wall had held them for a hundred years. Wax cylinders in their cardboard sleeves, and later the reel-to-reels in their boxes, and even a few cassettes from the seventies when somebody in the village had briefly tried to modernize the horror—all of them singing. All of them holding the given voices, preserved perfectly, the dead and the taken still performing their verses in the dark while the fields above stayed impossibly green.

Merrin took down the first cylinder and turned it in the light.

It was blank. Not scratched, not broken—blank. Smooth wax, unmarked by any groove at all, the way it must have looked before anyone ever sang into it. She checked another. Blank. Another. Blank. The reel-to-reels were worse, somehow: she threaded one onto the old machine out of pure stubbornness, pressed play, and got a full minute of clean hiss, the sound of tape that had never met a voice, or had forgotten every voice it ever held.

"Good," she said, and meant it, and cried anyway, standing in the cellar with the hiss playing, because this was the sound of freedom and it was also the sound of Zephyrine being erased, and those two things turned out to be the same sound, and nobody had warned her about that.

Her mother's voice had been on one of these cylinders. Given in 1974, the year Merrin was six. For thirty years Merrin had known exactly where that voice lived—in the dark, on a shelf, singing—and she had never once gone down to listen, because listening felt like using her, and she couldn't bear to use her mother one more time. Now the cylinder was smooth as an egg. Somewhere, wherever given voices go when the bell cracks like pond ice and lets them all pour out into the night like starlings, her mother was free too.

"You're welcome," Merrin whispered to the blank wax. "Both of you. You're both welcome."

She climbed back up into the ordinary morning, where the inn kitchen smelled of actual cooking instead of miracle abundance, where the bread required real flour and real waiting, and found she was ravenous, which felt almost blasphemous and mostly just felt like breakfast.

* * *

It was Tam Rooker's granddaughter who found the notebook, two days later, or rather it was Tam Rooker's granddaughter's dog, who found it under the hedge by the churchyard wall where Zephyrine must have dropped it on the last night, running or walking or singing their way up to the tower. The dog brought it to the feast table proudly, as dogs do, and dropped it in the gravy, and that was how Merrin came to be sitting by the fire at midnight with a gravy-stained field notebook and a cup of cider she wasn't drinking.

The cover was soft green cloth, swollen with damp, corner-worn. On the flyleaf, in that careful looping hand:

*Z. Whitlock. Field notes, Marrowfen. Property of the folklorist. If found, please return — I will come back for it. I always come back for my notebooks. (This is not a boast. It is a confession.)*

Merrin smiled at that. Then she made herself stop smiling, because she wanted to read it properly, and she couldn't read it properly through tears, and there was a great deal of it to read.

Zephyrine had documented everything. Of course they had. Page after page in ink that did not fade, because it was only ordinary ink, only human ink, the kind that stays:

*Day 2. Every villager refuses to sing anything twice. Taboo confirmed across eleven informants, identical phrasing — "calls something back." Either remarkable cultural uniformity or remarkable fear. Note: the smiles here are all the same smile. Who taught the village one smile?*

*Day 3. Bell rang at dusk. Tower empty. Parish record confirms bell melted 1911. My rational worldview has sustained structural damage. Recording the tolls tomorrow. Merrin says never count them. I counted them. Fourteen. I am aware this was unwise. Filed under: Things I Did Anyway.*

Merrin laughed out loud, alone by the fire, and then pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth because laughing felt like a betrayal and not laughing felt worse.

*Day 5. Merrin showed me the fields. I keep writing "impossible harvests" and underlining it, but the truth is the harvests aren't impossible, they're paid for, and I don't yet know the currency. Merrin's hands shook when she opened the vestry door. She is betraying her whole village for me, and she did it because I asked, and I think she did it because she wanted someone to ask. Filed under: People. Sub-filed, privately, under: People I Would Follow Into a Haunted Vestry.*

That was her own name, there, in the sub-file. *Merrin.* Written in a stranger's careful hand, described as a person one might follow into a haunted vestry, which was accurate, and which no one else on earth had ever thought to write down about her, and which now existed nowhere else in the world except this gravy-stained book.

She read until the candle guttered.

He had written about everyone. About Tam Rooker's dying and his refusal of the fourth verse—"the last verse isn't sung, lad, it's owed," transcribed verbatim, with a note: *Tam is not afraid. I have never met anyone not afraid. I want to be him when I decide to be brave.* About the widow, who had been given in 1968 and remembered nothing but that the singing felt like home. About the vicar's bargain, laid out in furious neat little paragraphs, the corruption dressed as mercy, dissected like the frog it was.

And about her. Again and again, her. *Merrin hums fragments of the last verse wrong on purpose. She thinks I haven't noticed. She is trying to spoil the song the way you'd spoil a curse, and I think she may be the cleverest person in this county, including me, which is saying something, and I intend to say it.* And later, in a different ink, hurried, dated the day before the choosing: *If the plan works, no one will remember me. That's the price and I've decided it's fair. But I've written everything down. Everything. Someone should remember the village even if the village can't remember me. And Merrin — Merrin will find this book. I'm leaving it where she'll find it. Hello, Merrin, if you're reading this. You mattered. You matter. That's not sentiment, it's data. I collected it myself.*

Merrin sat by the dead fire for a long time with the notebook open on her knees.

Here was the thing she kept turning over, the thing that wouldn't lie flat in her mind: she could not remember his face. She had tried. Three days of trying, in odd moments—drawing water, kneading bread, walking the fen road—and every attempt came back smooth and blank as the wax cylinders. She knew facts about Zephyrine Whitlock the way you know facts about a historical figure: folklorist, grant deadline, careful hand, counted the tolls anyway. But the face, the voice, the particular way they'd pushed their hair out of their eyes when concentrating—gone, poured out of the world with the rest of the bell's hoard.

And yet.

And yet she knew that they bit the end of their pen when thinking, because it was written here, in a margin: *note to self, stop chewing the pen, it's a filthy habit, Merrin keeps looking at me doing it.* She knew they were afraid of the cellar. She knew they found Tam Rooker heroic and the vicar pathetic and the widow heartbreaking and Merrin clever, and they had written all of it down, all of it preserved, every observation fixed in unfading ink—so that the archivist was gone and the archive remained, and the archive knew Zephyrine better than Zephyrine had ever been known by anyone alive, better perhaps than Zephyrine had known themselves.

"You did it on purpose," Merrin said to the fire. "You couldn't save yourself, so you saved the record of yourself. So somebody would grieve properly. Somebody who knew what they were grieving."

The fire popped. Outside, the fen lay quiet under a sky with ordinary stars in it, stars that had never granted anybody a harvest.

Merrin understood, then, what she was holding. It wasn't a notebook. It was a resurrection of the small kind, the kind that doesn't fix anything. Zephyrine Whitlock existed nowhere—not in the ledger, not on the tapes, not in a single villager's memory—but they existed *here*, in ink, in their own words, in their own hand, and as long as this book survived, then somewhere in the world a person named Zephyrine Whitlock had loved a village and freed it and been glad of the price, and that fact could not be unwritten. Documentation couldn't save you from being claimed. But it could claim you right back, afterward, gently, forever.

"I'll keep it safe," she promised. "I'll keep you safe. Both meanings. You'd appreciate that. You'd file it."

* * *

In the morning she carried the notebook up to the vestry, because it belonged with the other records, and because she wanted to show it to the blank line.

The ledger lay open as always. She set the notebook beside it, the living archive next to the dying one, and looked one more time at the last page, tilting it toward the window light.

The indentation was deeper than it had been.

Not fading anymore. Returning. Faint as spider silk, but unmistakable: the first stroke of a letter, pressing itself back up out of the paper, the way a footprint rises in wet sand when the tide turns. Merrin stared at it, heart hammering, and told herself it was settling paper, old damp, her own eyes wanting what they couldn't have.

Then, from the roofless tower above her, where no bell hung and no bell had hung since 1911, came a single soft note—half sound, half memory, gentle as a fingertip tapped on glass.

Merrin stood very still in the honest little room, between the fading ledger and the unfading book, and listened to it happen again.
