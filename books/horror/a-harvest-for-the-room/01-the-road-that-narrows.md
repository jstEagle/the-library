## 1. The Road That Narrows

The bus to Hollowmere ran twice a week, and only if there were passengers. Edmund Amberdale was the sole reason it ran that Tuesday in late September, and the driver made no effort to hide his surprise when he climbed aboard at the crossroads, duffel bag over one shoulder, tape recorder in a leather case against his chest like something newborn.

"You'll be the folklorist, then." The driver said it the way a man confirms a delivery he'd half expected to be refused.

"Edmund Amberdale. From the Institute."

"Didn't catch the name of the place you're from, but I caught the rest." The driver pulled away from the crossroads with a grinding of gears. "Hollowmere's not much of a destination for a man with your accent."

"It has songs," Edmund said. "Harvest songs nobody's recorded. That's enough of a destination for me."

The driver grunted. For three miles he said nothing, and Edmund took the silence as permission to work. He set the recorder on his knee, thumbed it on, and spoke into the microphone in the low, even voice he used for field notes—notes meant for the archive, notes meant to be heard by people who would never stand where he stood.

"Field recording, September the twenty-third. En route to Hollowmere, population—" he paused; the county gazetteer had given a figure, but figures in dying places were always obituaries waiting to be accurate "—call it sixty souls, though I suspect fewer. Purpose: documentation of harvest ritual song, specifically the threshing cycle said to be unique to the valley. Preliminary observation: the hedgerows along this road are silent."

He let the recorder run and listened himself, properly, the way he'd trained himself to listen since he was a boy pressing his ear to hedgerows full of wrens. There should have been birds. Late September, mild morning, hedges heavy with rosehips and black bryony—there should have been birds the way there is water in a river. Instead the hedges ran alongside the road for mile after mile, dense and green and utterly vacant, and the only sound was the bus's engine laboring up the long gradient into the hills.

"Notable," Edmund murmured into the microphone. "Not an absence of habitat. An absence of use. As though the birds have been asked to leave."

The driver glanced at him in the mirror. "You always talk to yourself?"

"I talk to the tape. It's more patient than most people."

"Hm." The driver's eyes went back to the road. Then, as if the thought had been sitting in him all morning waiting for a gap in conversation: "They don't sing twice, you know. The Hollowmere folk. Whatever they sing for you, don't ask them to sing it again. They'll do it once, because it's harvest and they're civil, but asking twice is—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "It's not polite. That's all. Not polite."

Edmund lowered the recorder a fraction. "Why not?"

"Didn't say I knew why. Said it wasn't polite." The bus crested the hill, and below them the valley opened: a bowl of fields gone gold and stubbled, a stream the color of old pewter, and at its center a cluster of grey stone roofs around a single square, a church tower rising out of the middle of it like a thumb pressed into dough. Smoke stood straight up from a dozen chimneys. Everything else stood still.

"That's it," the driver said. "Hollowmere. Last stop, which is also the first stop, depending how you look at it."

"How long do I have before the return bus?"

The driver pulled up outside a low inn with a sign showing a sheaf of wheat bound in red ribbon. He didn't answer right away. He looked at the village the way men look at a dog they know will bite someone eventually.

"Come Thursday," he said finally. "If there's a return bus."

* * *

The village square smelled of woodsmoke and apples rotting sweetly in the gutter beneath the single tree. Edmund stood in it for a moment with his bag at his feet, doing what he always did upon arrival in a new place: nothing. Listening. Letting the place make its first impression on him unguarded, before anyone knew he was watching.

What he heard was almost nothing. A door closing somewhere, softly, the way doors close when someone has been standing behind them. A child's voice cut off mid-word. The stream, far off, moving without urgency. And underneath it all—or so he told himself later, though the tape, when he played it back that night, held only room tone, flat and brown as cardboard—a sound like breathing. Slow. Patient. The breathing of something very large asleep under a blanket of earth.

A woman came out of the inn before he could knock. She was perhaps sixty, broad and capable-looking, with forearms that had wrung a thousand sheets and a face arranged into welcome the way a table is arranged for company: everything in its proper place, nothing accidental.

"Mr. Amberdale." Not a question. "Mrs. Tarrow. We've your room ready."

"We've," Edmund repeated pleasantly. "That's kind. Though I confess I hadn't written ahead."

"You wrote in July." She smiled. Her teeth were very even. "We keep track of things here. Come in, come in—you'll want tea after that road, everyone does. The road does it to people."

"The road?"

"Narrows," Mrs. Tarrow said, taking his bag from him with a strength that brooked no argument. "Coming in, it narrows. Most folks feel it. Like the valley's swallowing." She said it cheerfully, the way one remarks on weather, and led him inside.

* * *

The inn's interior was exactly what a hundred years of guidebooks had promised: low beams, a fire laid but unlit, brass things polished by devotion rather than pleasure. What the guidebooks had not promised was the quality of the stillness inside. The common room was empty at half past eleven in the morning, every chair pushed neatly in, every glass dry on its shelf, and yet the room felt attended. Occupied in the way a stage is occupied between performances.

"Sit anywhere," Mrs. Tarrow said, and then, before he had moved: "Not that one."

Edmund looked at the chair she meant. It stood slightly apart from the others, near the cold hearth—an old ladder-back, dark with age, its seat worn pale in the middle. Nothing wrong with it. Nothing wrong except that of all the chairs in the room, it was the only one not pushed in, standing instead at a small, deliberate angle, as though someone had just risen from it or was just about to arrive.

"My mistake," Edmund said, and sat elsewhere.

"Tea," Mrs. Tarrow announced, as if concluding a negotiation, and disappeared through a door that swung shut behind her with unusual weight.

While she was gone, Edmund did what he always did: he worked. He set the recorder on the table, checked his notebook, and began a fresh page.

*Arrived 11:40. Inn: the Wheatsheaf. Proprietor: Mrs. Tarrow, competent, watchful. Note the phrase 'we've your room ready' — arrival expected, date known. Either correspondence forwarded from the Institute (possible) or the village maintains its own intelligence about outsiders (more likely, and more interesting). Note the chair by the hearth. Refused without explanation. Note my own reaction: compliance without question. Worth examining later whether that was manners or instinct.*

He underlined *instinct* twice, then became aware that he was being watched.

At the window across the square, a man stood beside a handcart of split logs, looking in at Edmund. Not staring—looking, with the steady, unhurried attention of a farmer assessing weather or livestock. When Edmund raised a hand, the man raised his own, a slow mirror of the gesture, and turned back to his cart. But he did not leave. And within a minute, Edmund noticed, two more figures had appeared at other vantages: a woman at the well, drawing water she did not carry away; a boy on the churchyard wall, motionless as a carved gargoyle.

*Observation,* Edmund wrote. *I am the most interesting thing to happen here in some time, and they are interested the way one is interested in a guest, not a curiosity. There is a difference. Guests are counted.*

Mrs. Tarrow returned with tea, thick china, milk already added. She set it down and remained standing, hands folded, clearly prepared to deliver information.

"Now. Your room's the second floor, end of the corridor, best light in the house. I've put the writing desk by the window for your papers, and there's a chair for your recorder work, and a bed that doesn't creak, which is more than most houses here can promise." She recited it like a catechism. "Supper's at six. Meat pie tonight. If you want to go round introducing yourself, do it before supper—after dark, folk keep in."

"Keep in?"

"Indoors." She smiled again, and again the smile arrived precisely on time. "Village habit. Harvest coming, we're all tired early. You'll find the hall on the north side of the square—old Bram Hedley holds court there most afternoons, him and his threshing crew. He's the one with the songs. He knows you're coming."

"He knows I'm coming too."

"Everyone knows you're coming, Mr. Amberdale. It's been the talk of the summer." She gathered his registration slip—he hadn't seen her take it—and paused at the door. "One thing about the room. There's a chair in it. You didn't ask for a chair, I know, most lodgers want a desk and a lamp and think that's the whole of it. But the rooms in this house come furnished complete, and I'd thank you not to move the furniture. Things sit where they sit."

"I wouldn't dream of rearranging your inn, Mrs. Tarrow."

"No," she agreed mildly. "You wouldn't." And she left him alone with his tea, which had gone lukewarm without ever having been hot.

* * *

The room was clean, spare, and lit by a west window that poured amber over the floorboards. A narrow bed, freshly made with hospital corners. A washstand with jug and basin. A writing desk, as promised, positioned to catch the light. And against the wall opposite the foot of the bed—not beside the desk, where a working chair belonged, but facing the bed squarely, at a distance of perhaps four feet—stood a chair.

It was oak, old, its arms rubbed smooth and dark by generations of hands. Its seat faced the pillow. Anyone sitting in it would look directly down at whoever lay sleeping, close enough to lean forward and touch.

Edmund stood in the doorway for a long moment, duffel bag still on his shoulder, and felt the first true cold of the day move through him—not the chill of the valley air, which had been bracing, but something interior and specific, the body's oldest clerk filing a report the mind hadn't asked for.

*Moved furniture,* he reminded himself. *She said things sit where they sit.* He set the bag down, crossed the room, and put his palm flat on the chair's back.

Warm.

Not sun-warm—the chair stood out of the window's reach, in a stripe of shadow. Warm the way a seat is warm after someone has just risen from it. He snatched his hand back, laughed aloud at himself—one short syllable that sounded wrong in the small room—and told himself the obvious truths: old wood holds heat, houses hold heat, heat rises, heat pools, any number of mundane explanations. He wrote them all down, because writing them down was how he kept the world in its categories.

Then he unpacked. Shirts in the drawer. Recorder on the desk, cables coiled. Notebook open to a fresh page. And when he was done, he stood in the middle of the room and looked at the chair, and the chair looked at the bed, and he understood that he could spend the whole afternoon rationalizing or he could do his job.

He did his job. He went out into the light.

* * *

The harvest hall was a long stone barn of a building with doors thrown wide, and inside it the village had assembled—not all of them, but enough: a dozen men and women sorting the last of the apples onto sacking, children chasing one another between the trestles, and at the center, on a crate like a throne, an old man with a white beard cropped short and hands like tree roots, directing everything with two fingers.

"Bram," Mrs. Tarrow had said, and Edmund knew him at once from the gazetteer's photograph, thirty years stale: Bram Hedley, last singer of the Hollowmere threshing cycle.

"Mr. Amberdale." Bram's voice was gravel and honey. He did not get up. "Come to take our songs down to London, have you? Put us in a book?"

"To record them, Mr. Hedley. Exactly as sung. So they're not lost."

"Nothing's lost here." Bram said it lightly, but something moved behind his eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "Nothing leaves either, mind. Sit down, then. Have an apple. You'll want to hear the threshing song, I expect. Everyone wants the threshing song."

"I'd be grateful."

Bram studied him for a long moment—longer than courtesy, shorter than hostility—and then nodded once, as if confirming a measurement. "Tomorrow. Songs keep better than apples, and I don't. Come tomorrow when the crew's rested. Tonight you eat Mrs. Tarrow's pie and sleep in her best bed, and tomorrow we'll give you what you came for."

There was laughter at that, easy and warm, and the tension in the hall loosened like a knot slipped. Children were shooed, apples were pressed into Edmund's hands until he carried six, and Mrs. Tarrow appeared at his elbow as though summoned by the word *supper*.

"You see?" she said, walking him back toward the inn as the light went long and copper across the stubble fields. "Welcomed, like I said. Hollowmere's glad of you, Mr. Amberdale. It's been a long time since we had a guest for the harvest."

"A guest for the harvest," Edmund repeated. "That's a nice way of putting it."

"It's the only way of putting it." She held the inn door for him. Inside, the common room lamps were lit now, and the fire burned, and the villagers had begun to gather for the evening—but as Edmund stepped over the threshold, conversation dipped, all at once, the way birdsong stops.

Only for a heartbeat. Then it resumed, warmer than before, and someone pressed a pint into his hand, and a woman named Maren Hale with a face like a folded letter introduced herself and her committee and the plans for Harvest Eve, and Edmund found himself seated among them, welcomed, included, drawn into the warmth like a coal into a grate.

It was nearly ten when he climbed to his room, pleasantly tired, ears full of talk. He noted the evening in his journal—names, faces, the shape of the community, all of it valuable, all of it filed—and then, pen still moving, he stopped.

Because he had realized, somewhere between the second toast and the third, what had been bothering him all day, the thing underneath the silence of the hedgerows and the angle of the hearth-side chair and the warmth of the oak under his palm.

Every conversation he'd had that day had ended the same way. Every one. Mrs. Tarrow, Bram, Maren Hale, the well-woman, the log-man—all of them, at some point, in some phrasing, had asked him the same question dressed in different clothes. How long was he staying. Whether he'd see the winter in. Whether he found the valley comfortable. Whether he might not stay past the harvest, past Christmas, past spring—*a man could do worse*, they said, *a man could do far worse than Hollowmere*.

*Guests are counted,* he had written that morning.

He set down the pen. The lamp hissed gently. And from the wall opposite the foot of his bed, the chair waited in the dark beyond the lamplight, facing him, patient as the village itself, as though it too had all the time in the world—as though it had been waiting far longer than one afternoon, longer than one season, longer, perhaps, than anything so brief as a lifetime—

and as Edmund reached to turn down the lamp, he heard, distinctly, from the landing below, Mrs. Tarrow's voice, soft and satisfied, speaking to no one:

"There. Now we're nineteen."
