## 9. Bram's Confession

The coughing started in October, and by the first hard frost it had a rhythm Bram could set his watch to. Three racking fits before dawn, one at noon, and a long wet chorus in the evening that he muffled into his sleeve so the neighbors wouldn't hear. Fifty years of singing had worn his throat like water wears limestone—slowly, patiently, all the way through.

He sat by his kitchen window with the lamp turned low, watching the road that led out of Hollowmere, the one the folklorist had walked down three weeks ago with his canvas bag and his polite questions. Edmund Amberdale. The name was too fine for this place, all sharp consonants, nothing for the Room to hold onto. That was the trouble with them lately. They came softer every generation.

Bram knew what the village was doing. He'd watched them do it to the last one, sixty years back—a surveyor's apprentice, a nervous lad who'd lasted eleven days before Maren Hale's grandmother sat him down at the harvest feast and asked him, so gently, whether he wouldn't like to stay through winter. He'd said yes. Of course he'd said yes. They always said yes, because the village made saying yes feel like warmth, and by the time they understood what they'd agreed to, their names were already drying in the ledger.

But Amberdale was different. Bram had heard the recording.

He'd been there in the hall when the boy played the threshing song back on his little machine, and the whole room had gone still as held breath, because everyone heard it—the second voice, low and patient underneath old Bram's own, singing words Bram had never taught it. Words from the verse he'd cut off mid-line, the verse no one sang twice. The Room had learned the song from his machine. Or the machine had learned it from the Room. After fifty years, Bram no longer troubled himself with which direction things flowed.

*You were spared,* his brother's voice still said sometimes, on nights when the wind came off the fields carrying that particular sweetness, like ripe apples left to rot. *You were spared and you did nothing.*

It was true. That was the rotten heart of it, the thing he'd carried so long it had grown into his skeleton: it was true.

They had been nineteen then, the two of them, nineteen years old and identical as two ears of corn on the same stalk. The founders' ledger called it the Choosing, but the family had always called it the drawing of straws, and their mother had done the drawing, her hands shaking so badly she dropped the straws twice. Bram had drawn the short one. He remembered the exact weight of it, splintered at one end, and he remembered thinking: *this is how I die, holding a piece of kindling.*

And then Tom had laughed—that easy laugh of his, the laugh that could talk a farmer out of a bad debt—and taken the straw from Bram's hand and snapped it, and said, "He stutters when he's frightened. It'll be me or nobody." And their mother had wept, and the elders had argued, and in the end the ledger was amended in ink that hadn't fully dried even now, fifty years on, because some entries never do.

Tom went down into the Room on harvest eve, 1921—the year the photographs began showing the extra chair—and Bram had stood at the top of the earthen steps with the lantern shaking in his fist, and he had sung the binding verse while his brother descended, and Tom had looked up at him once, just once, and smiled. Not accusing. That was the worst of it. Tom had *smiled*, as if going down into the dark to be eaten alive by something older than the parish were a favor between brothers, and perhaps it was, and perhaps that smile was the reason Bram had never once slept a full night since.

The binding required a singer. That was the arrangement the founders made: the Room took a body each cycle, but a voice remained above ground to sing it quiet, to keep it drowsy, to keep it *fed on song instead of flesh* for as long as the verse held. Bram had sung every night for fifty years. Harvest songs, lullabies, whatever the ledger prescribed—he'd sung to the earth beneath Hollowmere the way other men prayed, and the earth had listened, and mostly it had stayed down.

*Mostly.*

Now his throat was closing like a fist, and the doctor in the next town over—when Bram could manage the bus, when the road allowed the bus to leave—had used the word *growth* and the word *weeks*, and Bram had nodded along as if hearing about someone else's weather.

The village knew. Of course they knew; they counted everything in Hollowmere, doors and chairs and days. Maren Hale had come by Tuesday with a seed cake and eyes that didn't meet his, and Bram had understood: they were already grieving him the way farmers grieve a fence post, assessing the replacement before the old one falls.

And the folklorist—Amberdale, with his recorder and his careful notebook and his face that went pale in exactly the right places—was already being measured for the chair. Bram had seen Mrs. Tarrow counting doors outside his room at the inn. He'd seen Ivy Fenwick's drawing confiscated, seen the parents' terror, seen the way the whole village moved around the man like water around a stone, smoothing him, warming him, *preparing* him.

Bram set down his cup. His hand was steady. It was only ever his throat that failed him.

"Tonight," he said to the empty kitchen, to the chair that sat by his hearth where it had sat for fifty years, never moved, never sat in, dusted weekly by Mrs. Tarrow's girl without ever being asked why. "I'll tell him tonight."

Because there was one thing worse than damning himself, and Bram had carried it long enough: it was letting another man walk into the dark smiling, believing it was kindness.

* * *

Edmund found the note under his door at the inn, written in a hand that trembled only at the descenders: *Come at dusk. Bring your machine. — B.*

Mrs. Tarrow watched him fold it. She was polishing glasses that were already clean, and her eyes had the flat shine of a woman performing calm. "Old Bram likes company," she said. "Don't tire him."

"I won't."

"And Edmund—" She stopped. The cloth circled the glass. "Whatever he tells you, remember he's an old man, and old men confuse what happened with what they wish had happened." She set the glass down harder than necessary. "We take care of our own here. We take care of guests too. That's all any of it means."

"That's very reassuring," Edmund said, and meant none of it, and saw that she knew he meant none of it, and that she was almost grateful.

The walk to Bram's cottage took him past the church, past the field boundaries where the hedges grew in that wrong, attentive silence, past Ivy Fenwick's window, where a small face appeared and vanished like a fish surfacing. The sun was going down the color of a bruise healing, yellow at the edges, deepening toward the center of the sky.

Bram opened the door before Edmund knocked. Up close, the old man's deterioration was shocking—three weeks had carved him, the cheeks fallen in, the skin gone the gray-yellow of old paper. But his eyes were clear. Clearer than anyone's in Hollowmere.

"You brought it," Bram said, nodding at the recorder bag. "Good. I want it on record. Whatever happens after, I want a machine that doesn't know how to lie."

The cottage smelled of coal smoke and camphor and, beneath it, something faintly sweet and rotten, like apples. There were two chairs by the fire. One was Bram's, worn to the shape of him. The other stood slightly apart, facing the room, and Edmund's stomach tightened at the sight of it—it matched the chairs in the photographs, the nineteenth-chair style, plain oak, no cushion, waiting.

"Don't look at it," Bram said, following his gaze. "Or do. It doesn't matter anymore. Sit down, Mr. Amberdale. This will take as long as my breath allows, and my breath is leaving faster than I'd like."

Edmund sat. He set the recorder on the table between them, reels turning, and pressed record.

"My brother's name was Thomas," Bram began, without preamble, staring into the fire. "Thomas Amberdale—no. Thomas Reed. You'd not have found him in the parish book; the elders burned that page the year after. But he existed. He laughed like the world owed him money and he intended to collect. And in the autumn of 1921, when I was nineteen, I drew a splintered straw, and he took it out of my hand and broke it, and went down into the ground in my place."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

"There's a chamber under this village," Bram went on. "Under the inn, if you've the sense I think you've got. Nineteen chairs of packed-earth country, older than the parish, older than the church. The founders found it here when they came—found something already living in the ground, something that had been sung to once, long ago, by people who are now only a word in its memory. And it was hungry. It had been hungry for a hundred years, maybe five hundred, and it was pulling the harvest out of the soil, pulling the milk sour, pulling the *color* out of children's faces. So the founders made a bargain. A body, each cycle. Consent freely given—that part matters, don't ask me why, but the ledger says the taking must be *offered*. And a singer above, to keep it fed on verse between feedings."

"Fifty years," Edmund said quietly. "Your brother was the last offering."

"The last *body*." Bram's laugh turned into a cough, and he rode it out, knuckles white on the armrest. "I was the last singer. Every night for fifty years I've stood over that ground and sung the binding verse, and every night it has dreamed of my brother's face, because that's the last thing it wore, the last shape it kept. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I have sung lullabies to the thing that ate my brother, and it has learned to hum them back."

Edmund's hand had gone cold on the table. "The recording," he said. "In the hall. The second voice."

"Now you're catching up." Bram turned his head, and his eyes were wet but steady. "That was it, boy. That was fifty years of my voice coming back up through the floorboards. And when my throat closes for good—and it will close within the month, the doctor says weeks but the doctor doesn't know about the Room, doctors never count the Room—the singing stops. And when the singing stops, it wakes hungry. Fully hungry. Not one body hungry. *Village* hungry."

The fire had burned low. Neither of them moved to feed it.

"They know," Bram said. "Maren Hale knows. Tarrow knows. All of them know, and they've known since my diagnosis, and they've spent these three weeks doing what this village has always done—making you warm, making you welcome, making you *willing*. Because consent matters. Because the Room won't take what isn't offered, and so they offer everything except the truth until the truth doesn't matter anymore." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped to a rasp that was almost tender. "They will ask you, Edmund Amberdale. At the feast, or after, or on harvest eve itself with all the lamps lit and all the chairs out. They'll ask you to stay through winter, and they'll mean the chair, and they'll make it sound like belonging. Like sainthood. Like the finest thing a man could ever be asked."

"And if I refuse?"

Bram smiled, and it was the saddest thing Edmund had seen in Hollowmere, sadder than the empty chairs, sadder than the boarded windows.

"If you refuse, they'll let it take what it wants. That's the other half of the bargain, the half they don't write down. The village keeps the Room fed, and the Room keeps its hunger inside the boundary. Mostly. A refusal breaks the pattern, and a broken pattern means the Room stops negotiating." He closed his eyes. "I refused once. Did I tell you that? The night Tom went down, I told the elders I'd go in his place anyway, that the straw meant nothing, that I'd sit in the chair myself. And Maren's grandmother put her hand on my shoulder—kindly, kindly—and said, 'Then the Room gets two, and your mother buries no one.' What would you have done, Mr. Amberdale? Nineteen years old, your brother already smiling at you from the dark?"

Edmund couldn't answer. The recorder reels turned, patient as anything else in this place.

"So I sang," Bram said. "Fifty years. And now my voice is going, and they've picked you, and I am going to tell you the only thing I have left that's worth anything." He gripped the table's edge, hauling himself upright, and his eyes blazed with something that fifty years of guilt had refined into pure fuel. "There is a second verse. The founders suppressed it—burned it, they thought, but the grandmother whose cottage you're sleeping in, old Widow Marsh, she *copied* it, she hid it, she was smarter than all of them put together. A verse that binds the Room without a body. Without blood. I never learned it—I wasn't given it, they didn't trust the brother of an offering—but it exists. It exists, and somewhere in that cottage, in her papers, in her *things*, it's waiting."

Outside, the light had failed completely. And from beneath the floorboards—from far beneath, from a depth that had no business answering a cottage kitchen—came a sound that made both men go still.

Humming.

Low and patient and terribly familiar, humming the melody of the threshing song, in a voice like Bram's own played backward through fifty years of earth.

Bram closed his eyes. "It knows I'm telling you," he whispered. "It always knows. Go home, Mr. Amberdale. Search her house. And whatever you find—" his breath caught, held, released— "*sing it louder than it does.*"
