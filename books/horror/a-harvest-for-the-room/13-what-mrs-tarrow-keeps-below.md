## 13. What Mrs. Tarrow Keeps Below

Edmund did not sleep. He had stopped pretending to, somewhere past the third hour, when the footsteps in the corridor came again and paused outside his door — that same patient, weightless pause, like a listener cupping a hand to a wall — and then went on, and he had lain in the dark counting doors in his head until the numbers lost all meaning. Nineteen. He kept arriving at nineteen. He did not know why that number sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, only that it did, and that it had begun to feel less like a count and more like an instruction.

Mrs. Tarrow had left a covered dish outside his door at dawn. Bread, cheese, a slice of apple cake. No note. When he carried the tray down to the kitchen, she was at the hearth with her back to him, stirring something that did not need stirring, and she said, without turning, "You didn't sleep."

"No."

"You'll want to, tonight. Tomorrow especially." The ladle knocked against the pot's rim, twice, a small domestic percussion. "Harvest Eve. Best to come to it rested."

"Mrs. Tarrow." He set the tray down. His voice sounded steadier than he felt. "What's below this inn?"

The ladle stopped.

She did not turn around. In the black glass of the window above the sink — a window that looked out on nothing but the yard and the grey morning — he could see her reflection, and her reflection was looking at him, though her body had not moved. "Below," she said, "is a cellar. Potatoes, cider, preserves. What cellars keep."

"I counted the doors last night," Edmund said. "In this inn. There are eleven on the ground floor. Yesterday there were twelve."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight, like wet wool laid across the kitchen. Mrs. Tarrow's shoulders rose once and settled, and when she finally turned, her face was the same kind, seamed, unremarkable face it had always been — and that was the worst of it, that she could still wear it, that the mask did not even strain.

"Old houses shift," she said. "You've been under a strain, Mr. Amberdale. Anyone would. You've been listening to us sing for weeks, and our songs are heavy things. Go and walk. Get the morning air in you. And tonight—" she lifted the ladle, pointed it at him gently, almost fondly, "—sleep with the lamp lit. It likes to be seen. Remember that, if you remember nothing else. It likes to be seen."

*It.* Not *he*, not *she*, not *they*. Edmund carried the word out of the inn like a coal in his mouth.

* * *

He walked, because walking was the only thing left that resembled a plan. He walked to the church and found the archive room locked — a new lock, brass-bright, installed on a door that had stood open to him for two weeks. He walked to Bram's cottage and saw through the window the old man sitting motionless in his chair, facing the wall, a blanket over his knees, and did not knock. He walked the field boundaries and found the boundary stones freshly whitewashed, each one marked with a symbol he now recognized from the founders' ledger: a circle with a door in it. A room. He walked to the road out of Hollowmere, on the mad chance that yesterday's looping had been some fever of his own, and walked it for forty minutes with the sun on his neck, and came over a rise and saw the village below him, and the church spire, and Mrs. Tarrow's chimney smoking, and the inn.

He did not weep. He had moved past weeping into something colder and more useful. He stood on the rise with the wind pushing at his back — pushing him *toward* the village, he noticed, the wind had been pushing him toward the village all morning — and he thought: *they have a lock on the church now. They are closing the doors they think matter.*

*They have not thought about the cellar.*

Mrs. Tarrow had told him, that first week, with a landlady's pride: the inn was the oldest building in Hollowmere, older than the church, older than the hall. The founders had raised it first. *Everything starts at the inn,* she'd said, laughing. *That's what my grandmother always said. Everything starts at the inn and everything ends there too.*

He had taken it for an innkeeper's joke.

* * *

He waited until the light went long and amber and the village drew inward around its suppers. He waited until Mrs. Tarrow's footsteps crossed the hall above and her door closed, and then until the house had done all its settling and the silence had gone granular, complete. Then he took the lamp from his room, and the cold stub of a candle from the kitchen drawer, and the poker from the hearth — not because he expected to fight anything, but because his hands needed to hold something — and he went down.

The cellar door was in the kitchen's back corner, behind a hanging curtain of sacking that smelled of grain and mice. It was not locked. Of course it was not locked. A lock would have been a message, and everything Mrs. Tarrow had done to him had been hospitality, and hospitality was the message.

The stairs were stone, worn into shallow curves by two centuries of feet. His lamp swung shadows up the walls ahead of him, and he counted the steps out of habit — fourteen, fifteen, sixteen — and the air changed at the bottom, the way air changes in a place that has been closed for a long time and then, recently, not.

The cellar was a cellar. That was the first thing. That was almost the cruelest thing. Barrels of cider along the left wall, their bungs sealed with red wax. Sacks of potatoes, a shelf of preserves in jars with handwritten labels — *damson, 1989; greengage, 1994* — the ordinary inventory of a country kitchen going back decades. A rack of tools. A smell of earth and apples and, underneath, faintly, something like a snuffed candle.

He made himself look at all of it. He made himself name it, the way he'd name plants in a hedgerow, the way he'd name songs. *Barrel. Jar. Sack. Hook.* Naming was a rope he could hold.

The far wall was stone, and the stone was wrong.

He saw it only when he brought the lamp close: the wall was built of the same grey fieldstone as the rest of the inn, but the courses of it were not courses. They had been laid to *look* like masonry, a skin of stone over something older, and the skin had begun to slip. Along the bottom, where the wall met the floor, there was a gap — a seam of darkness a hand's width tall — and from the gap came air. Cool air, moving air, air that tasted of packed earth and rain that had fallen before anyone alive had been born.

Edmund knelt. He hated himself for kneeling; some animal part of him understood that kneeling was the beginning of every ritual ever performed, and he knelt anyway, because he had to see.

The gap was not a gap in the wall. It was the top of a doorway. The floor of the cellar had been raised — filled in, deliberately, with rubble and lime and two hundred years of swept dirt — and beneath the raised floor, sunk into the earth like the bottom half of a letter buried in an envelope, was a door. Oak, black with age, iron-bound, and carved across its whole face with the circle-and-door symbol, worn nearly smooth by touching. Not by weather. By *touching*. By centuries of hands.

The founders had not built the Room beneath the inn. He understood that now, with a clarity that felt like falling. They had found it. They had built the inn *on top of it* — had built the whole village as a lid, a weight, a circle of houses pressing down like palms laid flat — and then they had raised the cellar floor to hide the threshold, and called it a cellar, and kept potatoes on it, and let the ordinariness of jars and sacks do the work that a lock could never do. You do not guard a door like that. You *domesticate* it. You make it so boring that no one ever looks.

Except Mrs. Tarrow looked. Mrs. Tarrow swept this floor. Mrs. Tarrow knew.

The poker was in his hand. He did not remember taking it off the shelf. He worked its flat end into the seam at the edge of the buried door, into the gap between oak and earth, and the earth gave — too easily, the way earth gives when it has been turned before, recently, by someone who meant to come back — and he dug, and prised, and the smell of the old air rose around him like water rising in a lock, and in twenty minutes that felt like twenty years he had cleared a hollow at the base of the door big enough to put his arm through.

He did not put his arm through. He put the lamp through.

The light slid under the oak into a space beyond, and the space took the light and gave it back wrong — he could see, in the strip of illumination, a floor of packed earth, and the floor was *swept*. Swept clean, in long deliberate strokes, the marks of the broom still crisp in the dustless surface. Someone maintained the Room. Someone came down here, and moved the earth aside, and went in, and swept.

*It likes to be seen,* Mrs. Tarrow had said.

Edmund sat back on his heels in the cellar among the jars of damsons and greengages, and his heart was going like a trapped bird, and he thought: *I can stop here. I can fill this in. I can go up and pack and sit on the bus on Thursday and write a paper about threshing songs and never think of Hollowmere again except at night.*

He thought it, and then he heard, very faintly, from under the door, from the swept and waiting dark — a sound he had heard on his own tapes, on the recording of the threshing song, the second voice, the lower voice, humming. Not the song. Not yet the song. Just a hum, tuneless and patient, the hum of something keeping itself company.

He got the crowbar from the tool rack.

* * *

The door was never locked because the door had never needed to be locked, and it came open with a groan of iron that sounded almost like a word, and cold air poured up over him, and he went down three more steps into the earth, into Hollowmere's first room.

The lamp found the walls: packed earth, smooth as the inside of a kiln, older than any wall in England, older perhaps than the idea of England. Roots had been cut away from the ceiling, long ago; the stubs were black and polished like the pews of a church. The floor was the swept earth he had seen, and it was marked — he made himself look — with a great circle, and within the circle, arranged with a precision that had outlived everyone who had set them there, stood the chairs.

Nineteen.

He knew it before he counted, the way you know the number of your own fingers. Nineteen chairs of dark oak, each one different, each one plainly taken from a different century and a different house — a spindle-back, a slab-seated peasant's chair, one with arms, one almost a throne, one so small it must have been made for a child — and they stood in a ring, facing inward, facing each other, a congregation with no pulpit, no altar, nothing at the center but the swept earth itself.

Eighteen of them were occupied.

His lamp shook. He steadied it with both hands and made himself raise the light, and the light went around the ring, and what sat in the chairs was no longer bone.

He had a folklorist's vocabulary for this and none of it helped. *Revenant. Wight. Barrow-wight, church-grim, the Quiet Neighbours.* The words were labels for jars, and nothing in the jars had prepared him. The things in the chairs had been people — the chairs told him that, the way the bodies had settled into them, the way a coat keeps the shape of the shoulders it hung on — but they had been people for a very long time, and the Room had been keeping them the way a cellar keeps apples: slowly, in the dark, changed into something that served the Room's purposes better than flesh ever had. They were grey-brown, the colour of the earth itself, and their surfaces had a fine grain to them, and in the lamplight the grain moved, subtly, the way grain moves in wood that is still, very slowly, growing. Their faces were faces the way carvings are faces. Their eyes were closed. All eighteen had their eyes closed, and their hands lay in their laps, and their hands were the worst thing, because the hands were folded — not laid, *folded*, each pair arranged with care, the right over the left, in the attitude of people listening to a song.

And they were not decayed. That was the thing his mind kept snagging on, circling back to like a tongue to a broken tooth. Decay was a process, and processes need time and air and the busy work of small lives, and whatever happened in this room, it was not decay. It was *keeping*. They had been stopped, at whatever moment the Room had taken them, and kept, the way Mrs. Tarrow kept damsons in jars — whole, preserved, labelled by their century by the cut of their clothes. The spindle-back chair held a man in a smock that had gone out of fashion before photography. The child's chair held a child, and Edmund had to turn the lamp away from that one, and stand for a moment with his eyes shut, breathing, before he could go on.

Eighteen. One year before his arrival, the ledger's last entry. *The Room is patient. The Room is owed.*

The nineteenth chair stood empty at the ring's northern point, and it was the only thing in the chamber that looked new. Not new-made — new-*kept*. Its seat was pale where the others were dark. Its wood had a faint warmth to it; he knew this without touching it, the way you know a stove is hot from across the room, and he understood that the warmth was not the chair's. It was the warmth of a place being kept ready. Of a bed turned down. Of a seat saved.

*It saves a seat for the helper.* Ivy's voice, in his memory, small and matter-of-fact.

He raised the lamp and looked at the empty chair properly, and the light showed him, carved into its backrest where the other chairs bore only wear, the circle-and-door symbol — and beneath it, cut shallow and recent, so recent the wood was still pale in the cuts, a word. Not in any script he knew, and yet he knew it, the way he had known the number nineteen, the way he had known to count the doors: the word was his name.

*Amberdale.*

The hum beneath the floor — he had thought it was beneath the floor; it was not beneath the floor, it was the floor, it was the packed earth itself, the whole chamber, the whole village standing on it like a lid on a pot — the hum changed. It found a note. The note was the first note of the threshing song, the low drone that old Bram's voice had ridden, the second voice from his tapes, and it held that note the way the eighteen held their folded hands: patiently, without end, waiting for the rest of the melody.

And in the silence after the note, in the black air of the Room that was older than the village, older than the founders, older than the songs it had been sung before anyone thought to bind it — Edmund heard, distinctly, from the northern point of the ring, from the pale warm chair with his name cut in it, a sound like an inhale.

Something, somewhere, drawing breath to answer him.

He was running up the stairs before he knew he had turned, lamp swinging, the poker abandoned somewhere behind him in the dark, and he burst through the sacking curtain into the kitchen — and stopped.

Mrs. Tarrow stood at the hearth in her nightdress and shawl, and she was not surprised. That was the thing he would remember longest: she was not surprised, not even a little. She looked at him — at the earth on his knees, at the sweat on his face, at the open cellar door behind him letting up its cold old breath — with the mild, sorrowful expression of a woman watching a child come in from a storm she had told him about.

"Now you've seen it," she said quietly. "And it's seen you." She turned back to the fire, and took up the poker — *his* poker, the one he'd left below — and set it by the hearth, in its place, as if it had never been gone. "The elders will want to know," she said. "They'll come tomorrow, after the procession. Rest now, Mr. Amberdale. You'll want your strength." She glanced at him once more over her shoulder, and her kind seamed face was full of something that in any other house, in any other village, he would have called pity.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we'll talk about the chair."
