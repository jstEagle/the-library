## 15. The Refusal

The harvest hall smelled of apples and tallow, of woodsmoke pressed into the beams by a hundred years of winters. Edmund stood at the front of it with his back to the great hearth, and the whole of Hollowmere was arranged before him in long rows—faces he had come to know by name, faces he had recorded singing, faces that had fed him and toasted him and smiled at him for a season.

Maren Hale stood at his right hand. She had asked him to stand there. *Where they can all see you*, she'd said, as though he were a prize marrow at a county fair.

Behind him, someone had set out a chair. He had not turned to look at it. He did not need to. He knew its shape the way he now knew the shape of every empty chair in Hollowmere: the straight back, the worn arms, the seat polished pale by generations of hands that never quite touched it. It had been carried down from somewhere—the inn, perhaps, or the church—and placed behind him like a throne awaiting an occupant, or a gallows awaiting a neck.

"Edmund Amberdale," Maren said, and her voice carried the way voices carry in halls built for threshing songs and wedding feasts, filling every corner without effort. "Guest of the harvest. Friend to Hollowmere."

*Friend.* The word sat in the air like a coin on a dead man's eye.

He had rehearsed this on the walk over from Mrs. Tarrow's. He had rehearsed it through three sleepless nights, through the memory of warm wood under his palm, through the arithmetic of doors that would not stay counted. He had rehearsed it because he understood, finally, what the elders were asking, and he understood that they would ask it beautifully, with candles and cider and the full weight of their need dressed up as honor.

They would ask him to consent. And consent, once given, could not be unsaid.

"I want to thank you," Maren continued. She was a tall woman, iron-haired, with a face like a field in November—all stubble and frost and nothing wasted. "For coming among us. For listening when others only pass through. This village has waited a long time for someone who listens."

Someone in the third row began, softly, to hum. Others took it up. The threshing song—the one Bram had sung, the one no one would sing twice—rose through the hall in a low, wordless drone, and Edmund felt it move through the floorboards and up through the soles of his boots like water finding a crack in a wall.

"The harvest has been good to us," Maren said. "Better than we deserve. And the harvest asks only one thing in return." She turned to face him fully then, and her eyes were not unkind. That was the worst of it. They had never been unkind. Every cruelty in Hollowmere had been administered with perfect courtesy, the way a farmer is gentle with the lamb he means to slaughter. "Stay with us, Edmund. Take your place among us. Promise it before the village, and be one of us forever."

*Forever.* He heard the ledger's dry ink in the word. He saw the nineteenth chair in the chamber beneath the inn, eighteen occupied by things that had been men and women once, before something wore them down to the shape of sitting.

The humming swelled. The lamps along the walls burned steady and low, and their light made the villagers' faces soft and golden and patient.

Edmund stepped forward.

"I have listened," he said. His voice sounded strange to him—hoarse from disuse, from days of saying little while he read the founders' ledger by candlelight. "That's true. I've listened better than anyone you've ever let leave. I've heard your songs, and I've heard the voice underneath them. I've counted your doors. I've seen your photographs." He paused. "Every photograph since 1921. One extra chair. Empty. Waiting."

The humming faltered—not stopped, but faltered, the way a heart stutters.

"You groomed me," Edmund said. "From the first night. The room with the extra chair. The feast. The toasts—every one of them asking me to promise to stay through winter. Ivy drawing pictures of the seat saved for 'the helper.' You didn't welcome me. You *prepared* me."

Maren's face did not change. But around her, in the rows, hands tightened on knees. A woman near the door looked down at her lap. Old Tam Weller, who had taught Edmund the words to the wassail song, closed his eyes.

"And so I'll answer you plainly," Edmund said, "in front of everyone, so there can be no mistake and no taking-back. No."

The word dropped into the hall like a stone into a well.

"No," he said again, louder, because the first one seemed too small for what it ended. "I will not take the chair. I will not consent. I will not be Hollowmere's harvest. Find another way—or don't—but you will not feed me to the thing under your floors."

Silence. Total, ringing silence, the kind he had only ever heard on tape after a recording stopped—the silence that is not absence but pressure, the held breath of a room.

Then Maren Hale exhaled.

It was a small sound. A sigh, almost. But Edmund heard what was in it, and what was in it was *relief*, and the hearing of it went through him colder than any scream could have.

She looked at him with something close to tenderness. Pity, even. And she said, quietly, so that only the front rows caught it and it spread outward from them like frost across a window:

"We tried."

Not anger. Not rage, not betrayal, not the howling of people whose sacrifice has been stolen from them. *We tried.* Said the way one says *the rain came* or *the crop failed*. Said with the weariness of fifty years of trying, generation after generation, walking some polite stranger to the edge of the chair and hoping, hoping, and being refused.

"That's all it needed," Maren said. "Consent freely given. We couldn't force you—that's the mercy and the curse of it. The Room takes only what offers itself." She folded her hands. "But refusal—" and here her voice gentled further, became almost apologetic, a hostess regretting a broken cup— "refusal leaves it hungry, Edmund. And it has been promised a meal."

The lamps guttered.

All of them, at once, though no door had opened, no wind moved. The flames bent sideways as if bowing, and the golden light in the hall curdled to gray, and in the gray Edmund saw the villagers' faces clearly for the first time—not the faces they had worn for him all autumn, but the faces beneath, and they were not angry faces. They were *tired*. Tired and relieved and ashamed, the faces of people who have done a hard thing badly for a very long time and are grateful, at last, to put it down.

Tam Weller would not look at him. The woman by the door was crying, silently, wiping her eyes with her apron as if mourning something already finished. A man Edmund recognized from the harvest field—the one with the laugh like a cart on gravel—met his gaze and mouthed, unmistakably, *I'm sorry.*

Behind him, Edmund heard the chair scrape against the floorboards.

No one had touched it.

"Doors," Maren said, and it was not a command but a liturgy, and the villagers rose as one body and spoke with her, a murmur like wind through standing corn: "*Doors.*"

Somewhere at the back of the hall, a bolt slid home. Then another. Then the heavy bar that Edmund had noticed weeks ago above the main doors—a bar he'd assumed was for winter storms—settled into its brackets with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

The lamps guttered again and steadied, burning lower now, bluer, the color of light through ice.

"You should understand," Maren said, and she was still courteous, still gentle, still the innkeeper's sister and the churchwarden and the keeper of the ledger, "that none of this is malice. You must believe that. We have lived beside it for two hundred years, and we have learned that it does not hate. It only hungers, the way winter hungers. And we have kept it fed, and it has kept us safe, and the bargain has held because every generation found someone willing—or someone who could be made willing."

"But I'm not willing," Edmund said.

"No," Maren agreed. There was real sorrow in it. "You're not. And so it will take what it wants instead, and what it wants has never once been denied, and we are sorry for it, Edmund. Truly. Some of us liked you." Her eyes flicked toward the crying woman, then away. "But we have children here. We have a village. You understand. You must understand—you've read the ledger. What happens to Hollowmere if the Room goes unfed?"

He did know. He had read the accounts of 1847, the year the ritual lapsed—the year the village had buried forty-one souls in six weeks and the churchyard had run out of consecrated ground. He knew what hunger did when it was bound and starved.

Knowing it did not make him less afraid. Knowing it made him more afraid, because it meant they were right, and being right did not save him.

"What happens to *me*?" Edmund asked.

Maren did not answer. She only looked at him the way one looks at weather coming in over the hills—at something already decided, already arriving, needing no opinion from anyone.

The humming started again.

Low, at first, from the back of the hall, and then spreading forward, row by row, the threshing song rising without words, without the final verse, the way they always sang it, the way they had sung it for two hundred years—up to the edge and stopping, leaving the gap open, leaving the door of the song unlocked. And beneath their voices, fainter than hearing, deeper than the floor, Edmund felt the other voice begin to rise to meet them. The second voice on his tapes. The lower voice. The one that had been waiting under every verse ever sung in this hall.

The villagers began, one by one, to sit down. Not to flee. To sit, in their rows, folding their hands, closing their eyes in some cases, as though settling in for a service, or a vigil, or a long winter night. As though the thing to do now, with the bargain broken and the debt called due, was simply to wait it out together and count the cost in the morning.

Only the crying woman looked at him, and her lips moved, and he read the words though no sound came: *Run.*

The bar on the main doors said otherwise.

Edmund backed away from the front of the hall, away from the chair that had scraped itself closer, and his heel struck the hearthstone, and the fire behind him had gone out—he could feel the cold of it on his neck, a cold like the cellar beneath the inn, like packed earth and nineteen chairs.

Maren Hale remained standing. She alone. And she inclined her head to him, formally, the way one bows to a guest who is leaving, and said:

"The road will be closed tonight. The houses will keep their doors. Whatever comes for you, it will come quiet, and it will come shaped like something you love. Don't open anything. Don't answer anything. And Edmund—" for the first time her composure cracked, just a hairline, just enough to show the frightened girl who had grown up counting doors in this same hall— "if you find a way out of this that doesn't end with the Room loose among us, take it. For all our sakes, take it. Because we won't help you find it. We can't. We've done our part. We tried."

The humming reached the top of its arc and stopped, all together, on the held note where the final verse should begin.

And in the silence after, in the blue lamp-light and the locked doors and the cold of the dead hearth at his back, Edmund heard—clearly, intimately, from directly behind the empty chair—

a voice, low and patient and terribly familiar, begin to sing the verse himself.

* * *

He ran.

Not for the doors—they were barred, and he knew it, and twenty seated bodies lay between him and them anyway. He ran for the old kitchen passage at the rear of the hall, the one Ivy had shown him weeks ago, the one the children used to slip out to the orchard during long services. Behind him he heard the chairs go over, heard Maren's voice cry out—not in alarm for herself but in something like grief—and then he was through the passage door and into the freezing dark of the yard with the harvest moon laid flat and white over the fields.

The village was dark. Every house, every window, every lamp extinguished except one: a single flame burning in a lantern on Mrs. Tarrow's step, beside an empty chair set out neatly on the flagstones, facing the road.

Waiting for him to pass it. Waiting to see if he would sit.

Edmund pulled his coat tight and ran past it, past the inn, past the churchyard where the stones leaned like teeth, out onto the road that led—had always led, he told himself, had led him in on the bus in September—to the world beyond the hedgerows.

The moon watched him go. And behind him, from the harvest hall, from every dark house, from the ground itself, soft and many-voiced and getting no farther away no matter how fast he walked, the second voice sang on—singing the final verse at last, after two hundred years of waiting, singing it all the way through to the end.

And the last line of it, when it came, was his own name.
