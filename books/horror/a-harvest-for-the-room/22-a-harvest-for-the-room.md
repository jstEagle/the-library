## 22. A Harvest for the Room

The bus to Hollowmere ran once a week now, and Dr. Lena Voss was the only passenger on it.

She had found the village the way folklorists find most dying places: in a footnote. A 1974 thesis on threshing songs of the western counties mentioned, almost apologetically, that "the Hollowmere corpus remains uncollected; the sole informant, one Bram Tarrow, declined to repeat certain verses." Declined. In forty years of fieldwork she had learned that *declined* was the most interesting word in any archive. People refused to sing twice only when the song refused to be sung.

The road narrowed as it came down off the moor, hedges pressing close on both sides like cupped hands, and Lena noticed—she noticed everything, it was her profession and her affliction—that there were no birds in them. No sparrows, no wrens, not even the fat wood pigeons that cluttered every hedgerow in the county. The silence wasn't empty. It was attentive.

"First time?" said the driver, without turning around.

"Yes."

"They'll give you a good room." He said it the way other men said *mind the step*—a warning dressed as information. "Lamp lit already, most likely."

The bus sighed to a stop at a crossroads where a signpost pointed three ways and all three signs read HOLLOWMERE. Beyond it the village opened out: grey stone houses, a church with a leaning tower, a harvest hall with its doors propped wide despite the October cold, and lamps burning in every window though the afternoon was barely dimming. And on the steps of nearly every house, an empty chair.

Lena stood in the road with her bags and counted eleven chairs before she made herself stop counting.

"You'll want to come in before dark," said a voice behind her, warm and unhurried. "Though I should say—it isn't dark here the way it's dark anywhere else. Come in anyway. It's polite."

He was perhaps sixty, tall, silver at the temples, with the sunburnt forearms of a man who worked outdoors and the ink-stained fingers of a man who wrote all night. He carried a lantern even though the lamps were lit, and when he smiled it reached his eyes but stopped just short of something behind them.

"Edmund Amberdale," he said. "Keeper of Hollowmere. You'll be Dr. Voss. The bus driver rang ahead."

"I didn't tell anyone I was coming."

"No," Edmund agreed pleasantly. "You didn't."

* * *

The inn was called the Lamplighter, and Mrs. Tarrow's granddaughter ran it now—a broad, cheerful woman named Sal who talked too much about the weather and not at all about the chairs. She led Lena up two flights of stairs to a room under the eaves: low-beamed, clean, a fire already laid, a lamp already lit with a flame that burned without flickering no matter how the draught moved through the ill-fitting window.

"And there's your chair," Sal said brightly, and was gone before Lena could ask what she meant.

It was a good chair. Oak, old, its arms worn smooth by generations of hands. It sat beside the desk facing the bed—not angled toward the fire, not tucked under the desk, but *facing the bed*, patient as a dog waiting to be told to stay. Lena set her bags down, took out her notebook, and wrote:

*Room prepared before arrival. Chair positioned facing sleeping place. Lamp lit pre-arrival. Cf. Amberdale's own account?*

Because that was the strange thing—the thing she had turned over the whole way down on the train. There *was* an account. Filed in the School of Scottish Studies' overflow collection, mislabelled, undated, in a hand she now recognized from the single photograph she'd found of him: Edmund Amberdale, *A Harvest for the Room*, a manuscript of some four hundred pages that no publisher would touch and no library would shelve, deposited anonymously in 1981 and catalogued, eventually, under folklore—fiction. She had read it three times. She had told herself it was a novel. A very good, very strange novel about a village that fed a presence beneath itself, generation after generation, until one man rewrote the bargain.

And then she had checked the parish records, and found a Keeper of Hollowmere listed continuously since 1974. One name. One name for fifty years.

She unpacked her recorder first. Always the recorder first. Then she went down to find the man from her book sitting by the fire, pouring two glasses of cider.

"Bram Tarrow is dead," Lena said, because she had decided on the walk over to conduct this entire encounter as if it were ordinary.

"Fifty-one years this spring." Edmund handed her a glass. "I sang at his threshold while he went. He'd earned a better verse than the one he got, so I gave him one. That's rather the job, when you boil it down. Giving people better verses than they were owed."

"You're real."

"I'm afraid so." He said it without self-pity, without drama, the way a man admits to a limp. "And you're here because you read my book and didn't believe it, which is the correct response, and also because you read it and couldn't stop thinking about it, which is the more honest one. Sit down, Dr. Voss. Ask me anything. I've waited fifty years for someone to ask."

* * *

They talked until the fire burned low, and then he walked her out into the blue dusk to show her the village, and the talking continued in the open air, under lamps that burned steady as held breath.

"The founders came here in 1743," Edmund said, gesturing at the churchyard wall. "Seventeen families. They found something under the ground—not buried, *dwelling*. A hunger with a shape it hadn't decided on yet. They bound it with a ritual they called the Room, and the binding required a body. An outsider's consent, renewed each generation. For two hundred years they fed it strangers, and called it hospitality."

"And you ended that."

"I complicated it." He smiled his stopped-short smile. "The Room doesn't actually want flesh. That was the founders' interpretation—they chose feeding because witnessing required staying forever, and nobody wanted to pay that price. But hunger was always a mistranslation. What it wants is to be *remembered*. Known. Sung. So I sit in the nineteenth chair below the inn, every night, and I sing the harvest songs—all the verses, including the ones Bram wouldn't repeat—and I record everything, and I keep the ledger, and the Room is fed on memory instead of meat. The extra chair in the photographs stays. But now it's occupied by no one and needed by everyone."

"That's in the book," Lena said quietly. "I thought it was a metaphor."

"Everything true is a metaphor until you're standing in it." He paused at a cottage door where a girl of maybe nine sat drawing on the step with chalk—drawings of chairs, dozens of them, each one different. "Evening, Wren. New picture?"

"It's yours," the girl said, holding it up: a tall chair, drawn carefully, with a lantern beside it and a small figure seated, singing, lines coming out of its mouth like birds. "Mum says you're the reason the bad years stopped."

"Mum gives me too much credit. The village stopped having bad years because we stopped lying to ourselves. I just do the paperwork." He folded the drawing with great care and put it inside his coat. "Thank you, Wren. This goes in the ledger tonight."

The girl grinned and ran inside, and Edmund watched her go with an expression Lena couldn't name—something between a father's pride and a lighthouse keeper's vigilance.

"Your recorder," he said, turning back to her. "Is it running?"

"It's always running. Bad habit."

"Good habit. Here, especially." They had reached the harvest hall, doors still propped open, lamplight spilling gold across the threshold. Inside, villagers were setting up for tomorrow's feast—trestle tables, garlands of wheat, a fiddler tuning in the corner. Old faces, young faces, faces that nodded to Edmund with an ease that had clearly been earned across decades and had not, Lena sensed, come easily at first. "Come in. There's a song I want you to hear. All of it. Every verse."

* * *

They gave her a stool near the hearth, and cider, and the fiddler struck up a threshing tune she knew from three other counties—same bones, different flesh—and then an old woman named Maren Hale's granddaughter began to sing, and the hall went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone present knows something the visitor doesn't.

The song was a threshing song. Corn comes down and corn is bound, the singer began, and it was lovely, and it was ordinary, and then it reached the final verse—the verse Bram Tarrow had walked out on, fifty years ago, mid-line—and instead of stopping, the whole room took it up together.

*One chair stands empty at our board,*
*one lamp burns late, one door swings wide—*
*come in, come in, be seen, be heard,*
*there's room, there's room, there's room inside.*

They sang it twice. Nobody flinched. Nobody left. And Lena, whose hand had gone to her recorder's level meter out of pure reflex, watched the needle jump.

There was a second voice on the tape. Low, beneath the singing, wordless or nearly so—not a growl, not a groan, but a hum, deep and resonant, rising through the floorboards and the packed earth beneath them, harmonizing. Keeping time. *Singing along.*

Her eyes went to Edmund. He was watching her, not the singers, and his expression was calm and faintly sad and absolutely certain.

"That's it," he said softly. "That's the Room. Fifty years ago that sound made a man flee a room mid-verse and cost this village a brother. Now it's the bass line. We sing, and it sings with us, and neither of us eats the other." He leaned forward. "Do you know why I asked you here, Dr. Voss? Really here?"

"The footnote. The uncollected corpus."

"The corpus is collected. Four hundred pages, in the archive, under fiction." He tapped the arm of his chair. "I asked you here because I am sixty-three years old, and keepers don't get to retire, but they do get to choose successors—and the choosing has to be done properly. Freely. With full knowledge. That's the whole point of what I built. Consent, freely given, with the truth laid out plain. The founders never managed it. I have. And I'm not asking you to say yes tonight, or this winter, or ever. I'm asking you to stay through the harvest, learn the songs, meet the people, and read the ledger. All of it. Including the parts that will make you want to run for the crossroads."

"The road loops," Lena said. "In the book."

"The road loops for people who run *before* understanding. It's not a trap. It's a question. *Do you understand what you're leaving?* Answer it honestly and the road opens." He rose, and offered her his hand, and his grip was warm and human and slightly ink-stained. "Sleep well. Leave the lamp lit. And if you wake in the night and hear singing under the floor—don't be frightened. Listen. That's all it's ever wanted. Someone to listen."

* * *

She did not sleep well. She slept the way researchers sleep in the field: shallowly, with one ear on the world.

At some black hour past two she woke to the sense of being watched, and lay still, and looked at the chair.

It had moved. Not far. Six inches, perhaps, closer to the bed, still facing her, still patient. The lamp burned steady. And from below—from beneath the floorboards, beneath the cellar, beneath the packed earth and the nineteen chairs and the long dark history of the place—came the humming. Low. Rhythmic. It was the threshing song, sung down in the roots of the world, and after a moment Lena realized with a chill that was half terror and half wonder that it was singing the second verse. The suppressed one. The one from the diary pages, the one about witnessing.

*Not fed,* it hummed, in the wordless way of dreams. *Seen. Not eaten. Known.*

She got up. She did not know why—every instinct screamed to pull the blankets over her head—but she got up, and she crossed the cold boards, and she sat down in the chair that had moved toward her in the night, and she said, aloud, feeling foolish and certain in equal measure:

"I'm listening."

The humming changed. Lifted. Somewhere below the inn, in a chamber of earth and nineteen chairs, something that had waited two hundred and eighty years to be heard rather than fed heard a stranger say those words, and the sound it made was not hunger.

It was relief.

* * *

Morning came grey and gold at once, mist on the fields, woodsmoke standing straight up from the chimneys. Lena came downstairs with her notebook full and her hands steady, and found Edmund Amberdale in the inn's front room, opening the great ledger on its stand—a book bound in green leather, thick as a Bible, its pages crowded with a century of entries in a dozen hands.

"Decided anything?" he asked, not looking up.

"I've decided to stay through the harvest," she said. "And I've decided that if I take the chair someday, it will be because I read every page of that ledger and chose it with my eyes open. And I've decided—" she hesitated, then committed, the way she committed to a transcription, all at once and forever— "that the corpus needs collecting properly. Songs, photographs, diaries, all of it. A real archive. Not filed under fiction."

Edmund looked up then, and for the first time since the crossroads his smile went all the way to whatever lived behind his eyes.

"Well," he said. "Then welcome to Hollowmere, Dr. Voss. We'll start this morning. There's a woman in the churchyard who remembers songs her grandmother wouldn't sing twice, and I think you'll find—" he closed the ledger gently, and outside, faint and low, the earth seemed almost to hum— "she'll sing them as many times as you like."
