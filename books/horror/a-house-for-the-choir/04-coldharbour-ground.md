## 4. Coldharbour Ground

The county archive smelled of cold paper and older dust, the particular sweetness of things that had been left alone too long and had begun to believe no one was coming. Petra stood in the doorway a moment longer than she needed to, letting her eyes adjust, letting herself pretend—for one more breath—that she had come here for the show. A segment on local history. Founders' Week fluff. Something safe.

She had not slept. She had listened to the sleep tapes four more times through the night, headphones pressed so hard against her ears that by morning they ached like bruised fruit, and each time the second voice said the same thing at the end, patient as water finding a crack: *You already know where to look.* And then, softer, almost tender: *Coldharbour.*

Her own name. Spoken in her own sleeping mouth, answered by something that knew it before she did.

So here she was, on a gray Tuesday with rain threatening and not arriving, standing in the Gideon County Records Annex because a dead woman's voice had told her to be.

"Help you?"

The man at the front desk was seventy if he was a day, cardigan the color of weak tea, glasses pushed up into white hair that stood like frost. His name tag read *HOLLIS*. He looked at her the way people in small towns look at strangers—two seconds of assessment, then the flicker of recognition, then the careful rearrangement of his face around whatever he'd heard about her family.

"You're the radio woman," he said. "Coldharbour."

"That's me." She kept her voice light, professional, the voice she used for pledge drives. "I'm doing some research for the show. Local history. Founders' era."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not suspicion exactly. More like a man watching weather come in from a direction weather doesn't usually come from.

"Founders," he repeated. "Which families?"

"All of them, eventually. But—" She made herself say it plainly, made it sound casual, an afterthought. "Start with mine."

* * *

He led her back through the stacks, past the marriage registers and the tax rolls, past a wall of framed photographs of county commissioners whose faces had gone the color of old cheese, to a room at the end marked **RESTRICTED — ASK STAFF**. The fluorescent tube above the door buzzed at a frequency just below hearing, or maybe just above; Petra felt it in her back teeth.

"We keep the founding materials back here," Hollis said. "Most folks never ask. Most folks don't care." He unlocked the door with a key from a ring of perhaps forty, and Petra noticed—he would notice this later, turning it over in the dark hours—that his hand hesitated on the handle. Just a half-second. The way you hesitate before opening a door to a room where someone might be waiting.

Inside: steel shelving, acid-free boxes, a reading table under a lamp with a green glass shade. One window, high and narrow, looking out onto a hillside overgrown with sumac. Through the sumac, half-hidden, Petra could see a stone structure—a squat cylinder, capped with rusted iron, sitting alone on the crown of the hill like a tooth left in the county's jaw.

"What's that?" she asked.

Hollis followed her gaze. When he answered, his voice had changed—flattened out, rehearsed, the voice of a man reciting something he'd been made to memorize. "Old well. Dry since before anyone living was born. It's on private land, technically. Or it was. Nobody's claimed it in years."

"Whose land was it?"

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw him decide something. "You don't know?" he said. "Your own name, and you don't know?"

"Hollis."

"Coldharbour hill," he said. "Coldharbour well. Your family owned all of this." He gestured—not just at the window but at everything, the shelves, the boxes, the town beyond them, the whole wet green valley with its church steeples and grain elevator and the radio station where she'd spent seven years talking to the dead without knowing why they called *her*. "Elias Coldharbour and his brothers came up from the river settlements in 1889. Bought the hill, the spring rights, the timber. Your people didn't just found this town, Miss Coldharbour. They built it on top of whatever was here first, and then they built it again on top of that."

Petra's mouth had gone dry. "What was here first?"

"That," said Hollis, "is a very good question, and I'll tell you what I tell everyone who asks it, which is nobody: the records will tell you what your family *owned*. What they *did*—" he tapped one long finger on the table, twice, like a heartbeat cut short, "—that's between you and the paper."

* * *

He brought her boxes. He brought her more boxes than she could have asked for, carrying them two at a time with the easy stoop of a man who had spent decades among dead weight, and he arranged them on the reading table in chronological order without consulting any list, which meant he knew this collection the way she knew her board faders—by touch, by habit, by long unloved familiarity.

Deeds first. She started there because deeds were simple, deeds were just geometry, geometry couldn't frighten her.

But even the geometry frightened her.

The 1891 plat map showed the Coldharbour holdings in blue ink gone the brown of dried blood: the hill, the well at its crown, and radiating outward from the well in perfect concentric rings, parcels sold off to the other founding families—the Vances, the Merricks, the Aldertons, the Kells—each ring wider than the last, each deed containing a clause she had to read three times before she believed it:

*...reserving unto the grantors and their heirs in perpetuity all rights of access to the well shaft and the ground beneath the same, together with the right of passage to and from said well by night or by day, this reservation to run with the land and bind all successors...*

They'd sold the town in circles around their own well. Every property in Gideon County sat inside a ring drawn around that dry stone throat on the hill. Petra put her palm flat on the map, over the little inked circle at the center, and felt—she would swear this later, to Aris, to no one, to herself at 3 a.m.—a faint coolness rise through the paper, like holding her hand over an open cellar door.

*You already know where to look.*

"I'm looking," she whispered. "I just don't know what I'm looking at."

* * *

The correspondence was worse.

Letters, mostly, in the spidery hand of Elias Coldharbour, and later his son Abram, and later still a granddaughter named Verity—*Verity*, Petra thought, what a name to give a girl, what a dare—and the letters were businesslike, dull, full of lumber prices and fence disputes, except for the ones that weren't.

Except for the ones from 1910 and 1911.

*The singing has begun again,* Elias wrote to a Dr. Alcott Vance in March of 1910. *Abram says it is only wind in the shaft. Abram was not here in the winter of '94 and does not know what wind sounds like when it knows words.*

And in August: *We have agreed, all seven households, that the matter must be concluded before harvest. Verity is to have no part of it. She is young and the work requires a steady hand. Tell your sons the same of theirs.*

*Tell your sons the same of theirs.* As if it were contagious. As if it were a fire they were all agreeing to put out before it spread to the children.

Petra sat back. The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the building a clock ticked with the slow reluctance of a thing counting down rather than up.

There were seven founding households. Seven names on the plat map, seven rings around the well. And in every letter from 1910 onward, the same phrase kept surfacing like a body refusing to stay sunk: *the matter*, *the work*, *the arrangement*, *the covenant*. Never named directly. Never explained. Discussed the way people discuss a tumor—obliquely, in euphemism, with a terror that shows through the clinical language like bone through skin.

And threaded through all of it, always, always, the word *heir*.

*The covenant cannot hold without an heir of the line,* Abram wrote in January 1911, and the handwriting had deteriorated badly, letters leaning like drunks. *Father says we have time. Father is wrong. We have until the water comes back.*

*Until the water comes back.*

Petra looked up at the window. At the hill. At the well.

Dry since before anyone living was born, Hollis had said. But wells didn't dry forever. Wells filled. That was what water did—it waited, and it rose, and it came back.

*Stop digging before the well refills.*

She hadn't understood the caller. Not really. She'd filed it under *creepy* and moved on. Now the sentence opened itself up in her chest like a trapdoor.

* * *

It was Hollis who found her, an hour later, still staring at the window. He set a cup of coffee beside her elbow without being asked, and she realized her hands were shaking too hard to have managed the machine.

"1911," she said. Her voice came out wrong, scraped. "What happened in 1911?"

Hollis didn't answer right away. He pulled a chair out and sat down across from her, heavily, the way men sit when their knees have opinions, and he took off his glasses and cleaned them on his cardigan though they didn't need cleaning. Buying time. She recognized the maneuver; she did it on air every night.

"Bring me the box from 'eleven," he said finally. "Bottom shelf. The one with the water stain."

She found it. Even before she lifted the lid she saw it: a tide mark across the cardboard, brown and scalloped, like the box had once stood in floodwater. Inside, the records were ruined. That was the only word for it. Every page, every letter, every ledger entry dated after June of 1911 had been soaked sometime in the long ago and dried into a single fused brick of pulp, pages welded together, ink bled into blooms and ghosts. She could make out fragments—*the seventh*, *must not be written*, *heir*, *water rising*—words surfacing and drowning again in the damage, a text mid-drowning, preserved in the act of being swallowed.

Every record after 1911. Water-damaged. All of them.

"Hollis," she said carefully, "how does an entire archive get flooded? This room is on the second floor."

"It wasn't always the archive." He was looking at the ruined box with an expression she couldn't read—grief, or guilt, or the numbness that comes after both. "This building was the Coldharbour house. Town bought it in '38. Before that—" He stopped. Started again. "Before that, Verity Coldharbour lived here alone, and she kept the family papers, and in June of 1911 there was a storm that the old-timers called the Night the Hill Wept. Water came *up*, Miss Coldharbour. Out of the ground. Filled her cellar, her parlor, her kitchen. Rose uphill, they said, which is impossible, but eleven people signed affidavits saying it anyway. It ruined everything paper in the house. Everything except—" he nodded at the intact boxes, the deeds, the letters, "—what she'd moved upstairs beforehand. Which was everything dated *before*."

Petra felt the floor tilt gently, the way a boat tilts when something large passes underneath.

"She saved the before," Petra said slowly, "and let the after drown."

"Or," said Hollis, putting his glasses back on, "the water took what it wanted and left what it wanted, and Verity Coldharbour spent the next fifty years telling anyone who'd listen that the well on the hill was not empty, and that someday it would want the rest of its papers back." He stood, joints cracking like green wood. "People thought her touched. Last of her line, you know. No children. No siblings. Whole family tree pruned down to one strange old woman keeping a dry house on a wet hill."

The lamp buzzed. The clock ticked. Outside the narrow window, clouds had finally gathered over the hill, low and dark and organized, and the first drops began to strike the glass—one, two, then a soft drumming rush—as if something far below the sumac and the stone had finally exhaled.

"Miss Coldharbour," Hollis said quietly. He was looking at her name tag-less chest, at her face, at whatever he saw there that matched the photographs in the boxes behind her. "Verity died in 1963. In this room. At that table. Reading those letters." He gathered his keys. "Folks said she was waiting for somebody. She never said who. But she left instructions that the restricted room stay locked, and that the key go only to—" he paused, and his voice did the flattening thing again, the recited thing, "—'a Coldharbour who comes asking.'"

Rain ran down the window in long fingers. On the hill, the well stood black-capped under the graying sky, and Petra thought of her mother teaching her, patiently, year after year, how to be small, how to be forgettable, how to pass through rooms without disturbing the air—thought of it now not as eccentricity but as camouflage, as preparation, as a life spent being hidden for reasons no one would speak aloud.

*The heir who was kept invisible.* No. Not yet. That sentence hadn't found her yet. But its shadow lay across everything, across the deeds and the drowned pages and the strange old woman who had died waiting at this table.

"Is there anything else?" she asked. "Anything at all from after 1911 that survived?"

Hollis stopped at the door, key ring silent in his fist.

"One thing," he said. "Verity's personal effects. We're supposed to catalog them this winter—they've been in the basement since '63." He hesitated. "There's a ledger. Family ledger, leather-bound, big as a Bible. Half of it's readable. And the last legible entry, the very last thing she wrote before the water took the rest—" He looked at her, and for a moment the archivist fell away and there was only an old man who had kept a locked door for forty years, afraid of what walked through it. "It says: *If the water rises, find the invisible child. She will know the sixth movement. God forgive us, we never taught her the seventh.*"

The rain thickened against the glass. Down the hill, in the town, in the drains and the cisterns and the roots of things, Petra imagined—knew—the water beginning to move.

"Where," she said, "is that ledger now?"
