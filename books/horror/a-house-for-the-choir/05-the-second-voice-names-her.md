## 5. The Second Voice Names Her

The storm came in off the reservoir at eleven forty, and by midnight the whole county sounded like it was being rinsed through a colander—rain on the station's tin roof, rain against the transmitter tower's guy-wires, rain ticking the window of Studio B like a patient finger asking to be let in.

Petra had learned to love that sound once. Seven years of overnight shifts would do that; you made pets of your noises or they made prey of you. She sat with her headphones half-off one ear, the way she always did now, keeping one ear for the room itself—for anything that might be in the room with her that wasn't coming through the board. The ON AIR sign bled its red into the glass. Outside it, the hallway ran dark toward the coffee machine and the bathroom and the fire exit, all the ordinary geography of a building she'd worked alone in so long it felt less like a workplace than a held breath.

"Forty percent chance of clearing by dawn," she said into the mic, reading the weather off the wire service feed. "Which means if you're driving home from the Grange dance, take Ridge Road slow. The culvert floods before anyone thinks it will."

She said things like that every night. Take Ridge Road slow. The culvert floods. Small sentences, small kindnesses, the kind of talk you'd give a town full of people you loved even when you couldn't name them all. It had been her whole architecture: the show as a lit porch light. As long as she kept talking, the night stayed navigable.

But since the tapes—the tapes she hadn't played again, couldn't make herself play again, though she knew exactly where they were, third drawer, under the spare fuses—she had begun to hear her own voice differently. On air, in her headphones, there was a faint doubled quality to it sometimes, like a shadow standing just behind her shoulder saying the same words a half-second late. She kept telling herself it was the compressor. The compressor was old. Everything in this building was old. Old things made sounds.

She queued up the next record—a 1962 pressing of gospel quartet music she'd pulled from the donation bin because the label was hand-lettered and beautiful—and let it spin while she checked the request line.

Two messages. The first was a woman wanting to dedicate "In the Garden" to her husband's truck, which was dying and deserved a send-off. Petra smiled at that, wrote it down. The second message was nine seconds long and contained only breathing—not labored, not threatening, just breathing, wet and close, the way someone breathes when their mouth is an inch from the receiver. Then, at the very end, so quiet she had to turn the monitor up until the studio speakers hissed:

*—she can hear us when we're all together.*

Petra deleted it. Her hands did not shake this time, which frightened her more than shaking would have. Shaking meant surprise. This was something else. This was the feeling of a door you'd been bracing shut finally swinging inward on its own weight, and finding you weren't strong enough—or maybe no longer willing—to hold it.

She put the headphones back on properly. Both ears. If the thing wanted to talk to her, fine. Let it say it where she could hear it clearly, where she could catch it, where it would have to exist on tape.

That was the thought that undid her later. *Where she could catch it.* As if she'd already accepted what it was. As if some part of her had stopped rationalizing weeks ago and had simply been waiting for the rest of her to catch up.

* * *

At 1:17 a.m., during the gospel record's second side, the needle skipped.

Not the way vinyl skips—no pop, no groove-jump stutter. The song simply stopped, mid-phrase, the four voices cut off like a door closing on them, and in the sudden hole in the broadcast Petra heard the sound of water. Not rain. Water in an enclosed space, lapping against stone, the particular hollow echo of a column of air above a deep pool. A well, her mind supplied, unhelpfully. The sound of the inside of a well.

Then the second voice came through the broadcast itself.

Not through the phone line. Not through the request line or the remote feed or any input on the board—she looked, she would swear afterward she looked, her eyes running down every channel meter while her body stayed frozen in the chair, and every meter read dead silence except her own mic. The voice was *in* the signal. In the tower. In the air between the tower and every radio in every kitchen and pickup and bedroom across three counties.

It was low, and it was many. That was the thing she could never explain to anyone afterward without sounding insane: it wasn't one voice doing an effect. It was voices braided—men and women and, worse, children, all speaking in unison but not quite synchronized, each syllable arriving a hair apart from its neighbors, so that single words came out frayed at the edges, like rope.

*"Petal."*

Her childhood nickname. No one living used it. Her mother had coined it—*little Petal, my little Petal*—and her mother had been dead eleven years, buried in a cemetery two towns over that Petra visited twice a year and never spoke aloud about. Her father certainly didn't use it. Her father barely used her name at all.

The word hung in the studio. The meters stayed flat. The red ON AIR sign glowed its steady accusation: *this is going out. Everyone is hearing this.*

*"Petal, you left the gate open."*

She knew—knew with the cold certainty of recognition rather than memory—that those were her mother's cadences inside the chorus, her mother's particular lift at the end of a sentence, worn smooth by whatever used them now. And underneath the words, beneath the water-sound, she heard the other thing the choir carried everywhere it went: singing. Distant, wordless, patient. A hundred throats holding one long note in the dark.

"No," Petra said. Her own voice came out thin, broadcast to God knew how many sleepless listeners. "No, you don't get to—" She stopped herself, aware suddenly of the mic, of the audience, of the fact that she was arguing with a radio signal in front of her entire listening area. She pressed the cough button. Dead air to the county; the studio still full of water-sound.

"You're not on the board," she said quietly, to the room, to the voice, to whoever was listening on the other side of the glass. "There's no path from you to the transmitter. I've checked. There is no path."

*"We are the path,"* the choir said, gentle as a tide coming in, *"we are the wire."*

And then, before she could answer, the needle dropped back into its groove and the gospel quartet resumed mid-word, four men harmonizing about the river of Jordan as if nothing had interrupted them, as if they hadn't been standing aside for thirty seconds to let something older use the frequency.

Petra sat with her hands flat on the console, feeling the vibration of the music through her palms, and understood that she had just been addressed by name on her own show, on her own terms, in front of everyone—and that she had no proof it had happened at all.

* * *

The calls started within four minutes.

She took the first one live because taking calls was the shape of the show and the shape of the show was the last structure she had. An elderly man out past Miller's Bend, his voice cracked with fear he was trying to sand down into politeness.

"Petra? Petra Coldharbour? I—I don't want to cause trouble, but there was a voice come over the radio just now, and it wasn't yours, and it wasn't the singer's. It said a name. It said—" he hesitated, embarrassed, "—it said *Petal.* My late wife used to call our girls that. I haven't heard that word in thirty years and it come right out of the radio set on my kitchen table, plain as the news."

"I think—" Petra began, and found she had no sentence ready. Seven years of explanations lined up behind her teeth and none of them would walk forward. *Ionospheric skip. Crosstalk from another station. A prank.* All of them died in her mouth. "I think there may have been a technical fault," she said finally. "I'm looking into it. Thank you for calling, Mr.—"

"Aldous. You tell me if there's anything wrong with the tower, honey. My wife, before she passed, she always said that hill your station sits on was a strange hill. Said the Coldharbours never should've built anything up there."

The line clicked off before Petra could ask him what he meant, and she sat staring at the blinking HOLD light, at the queue filling behind it—three lines, then five, then all eight, the switchboard lighting up like a Christmas tree in a house where nobody celebrated Christmas anymore.

She took the calls in order, and they were all the same call. A woman in town whose daughter had woken up crying, saying the radio had said her name. A man who'd been listening in his truck on Route 9 and had pulled over, convinced someone had gotten into his cab. A teenager who'd recorded it on her phone—*recorded it!* Petra's heart leapt at that, at the word *proof*—and who played it back down the line, tinny and distorted, and it was rain, only rain, thirty seconds of rain and the gospel song, no voice, no name, nothing.

"You had to be there," the girl said, bewildered. "It was so clear. It said *Petal* like—like it knew her. Like it missed her."

"It's a common nickname," Petra said, and heard how hollow it sounded, and hated herself for saying it anyway.

By 2:40 she'd given up the pretense of programming. She told her audience the truth as far as she dared—that there had been an anomaly, that she was investigating, that anyone with concerns could call the station office in the morning. Then she opened the lines and let people talk about the storm, about the harvest festival setup, about anything, because the alternative was silence, and silence was a door, and she had learned tonight that doors swung both ways.

* * *

Dev Okonkwo arrived at 3:15, still in yesterday's clothes, keys jangling, smelling of gas-station coffee and alarm. He was the closest thing the station had to a producer, which mostly meant he fixed the transmitter and covered her shifts when she was sick and teased her about her taste in gospel records. He came through the studio door at a jog.

"Phones have been ringing off the hook since two. Aldous called my house, Petra. My *house.* What happened?"

"There was a voice on the broadcast," she said. Flat. She'd decided somewhere in the last hour that flatness was the only register she could afford. "It wasn't me. It wasn't on any input. I need you to pull the master tape."

"The master's running, it's always running—"

"I know. Pull it. Play me the last ninety minutes."

He gave her a look—the look of a man deciding whether his friend was having a breakdown or pulling a prank—and threaded the playback on the reel-to-reel in the production room while Petra stood behind him with her arms crossed tight enough to ache.

The tape hissed. The gospel record played. Her voice read the weather, dedicated the song to the dying truck, thanked the Grange. Clean. Continuous. Ninety minutes of the most ordinary overnight radio in America.

"Here," Petra said. "1:17. Watch the clock."

They watched. The needle skip wasn't there. The song played straight through, all four verses, the quartet's voices rising and settling, and where the choir had spoken—where the word *Petal* had filled the studio like water fills a lung—there was nothing. Not static. Not dropout. Nothing at all, as if the moment had simply never been broadcast, as if the voice had used the signal without leaving a fingerprint on it.

Dev rewound it. Played it again. Slowed it to half speed. Rain, music, Petra's voice, weather, time-check.

"Petra." He turned around slowly in the chair. "There's nothing here."

"I know what I heard."

"I'm not saying you didn't. I'm saying—" He gestured at the tape, helpless. "Eight callers. Eight, minimum, and the phones are still lit up out there. They all heard it. But it's not on the tape, and it's not on the log"—he tapped the console printout, the automatic record of every audio event the board registered—"and according to this machine, nothing happened tonight except a very boring shift."

"And you?" Petra asked. "Did you hear it? When it happened?"

Dev hesitated. She watched him decide to be honest, watched it cost him something.

"No," he said. "I was asleep till you called. But Petra—if I'd been here, in the studio, would I have heard it? Or would I have been like the tape?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have an answer that wasn't a trapdoor.

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. The callers had heard it through their radios—the same radios, the same signal, the same airwaves. But Dev would have been in the room, and the room heard nothing, and the tape heard nothing, and the log heard nothing. The voice had gone out to the county and bypassed the building it left from. It had spoken to everyone except the people standing next to her.

Everyone could witness it except anyone who could confirm it to her face.

* * *

She sent Dev home at four with instructions to sleep and say nothing yet, and he went reluctantly, pausing at the door to say, "Whatever this is, you'd tell me if it was bad, right?" and she said, "Of course," and both of them knew the weight of the lie and let it stand there between them like furniture.

Then she was alone with the rain and the red light and the last two hours of her shift.

She should have played music and said nothing. That was the professional move, the sane move. Instead, at 4:51, with the sky outside still black but going blacker in the way that means the night has started considering ending, Petra leaned into the microphone and spoke directly to the thing that had named her.

"If you can hear this," she said, "and I believe you can—you don't get to use my mother's voice. Whatever you are, whatever you want, you don't get to wear her. Find another way to talk to me or don't talk at all."

Silence. The rain. The hum of the transmitter, sixty hertz, steady as a pulse.

Then, from the request line—which had not rung, which showed no incoming call, which lit anyway, one soft green blink like an eye opening—the voice came again. Quieter now. Just two or three threads braided instead of a hundred, almost tender.

*"You always were the one who listened, Petal. That's why they kept you. That's why you were hidden so well."*

A pause, long enough for her to hear the water moving somewhere beneath the words, deep down, rising.

*"Ask Marta Vane about the well. Before it asks you."*

The line went dead. The green light closed. On the console, every meter settled back to zero, and the gospel record ran out into the run-out groove, clicking softly, softly, like a clock in an empty house.

Petra sat very still. Outside, the rain was stopping. Through the window, on the hill below the tower, the town of Coldharbour lay under its last hour of darkness, unaware—and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she had just been given directions.

Marta Vane. The county archive. First light.

She reached for the log sheet to write it down, and her hand was steady, and that steadiness scared her more than anything the night had said.
