## 8. What the Well Wants

The water came on a Tuesday, three days before any rain was forecast, and it came up instead of down.

Petra heard about it from Dale Hutchins, who called the station at 2:14 in the morning to complain that the old well on Coldharbour Hill—the one every kid in town had been dared to drop pebbles into since before anyone could remember—was making a sound. Not the dry rattle of a stone finding bottom after forty feet of nothing. A sound like a stone finding *water*.

"It's not supposed to have any," Dale said. "That's the whole point of it. It's been dry since my granddad was a boy. My granddad used to say you could holler down that hole and hear your own echo come back wrong."

"Wrong how?" Petra asked, because she was still doing her job, still holding the shape of the show around herself like a coat.

There was a long pause on the line, long enough that she checked the board to see if he'd hung up. When Dale spoke again his voice had dropped, gone careful, the way voices go when a man realizes mid-sentence that he's saying something he can't take back.

"Like it's listening for the end of what you said. Like it's waiting its turn."

She logged the call. She did not play it on air. She sat in the studio with the ON AIR sign burning red above the glass and thought about the ledger folded inside her bag beneath the desk, the one Marta Vane had pressed into her hands with both of hers, the way you hand someone something breakable—or something that will break them—and said: *the founders completed five movements out of seven, girl, and they've been waiting a hundred years for someone to finish the sentence.*

Water rising in a dry well. Ahead of any rain. She looked at the weather radar on the monitor: clear skies across three counties, high pressure parked over the valley like a lid.

The containment is failing. The deadline is real. She'd read those words in no book; she'd assembled them herself over the past week, the way you assemble a diagnosis from symptoms you've been pretending are unrelated. And now the ground itself was confirming it, filling the throat of that hill with black water, getting ready to speak.

* * *

She drove up to see it herself at dawn, which was stupid, and she knew it was stupid, and she went anyway.

Coldharbour Hill stood at the town's northern edge, a bald shoulder of granite and scrub oak with a rusted fence around its crown and a historical marker so weathered the letters had gone to shadow: *COLDHARBOUR WELL — EST. 1898 — SEE COUNTY RECORDS.* The county records, as far as she could tell, were a box of pulp in the archive basement, but that was yesterday's problem.

The fence gate hung open. It always hung open; kids bent it years ago and no one bothered. Petra walked the last fifty yards with her breath loud in her own ears and stopped at the low stone lip of the well.

It smelled wrong first. That was the thing nobody warned you about water—that standing water in a place where water should not be has a smell, mineral and sweet and faintly rotten, like flowers left too long in a vase. She leaned over the rim.

Far below, catching the early light, there was a shine. Maybe eight feet down. Maybe ten. Dark water lying perfectly still, perfectly level, like something poured in overnight by careful hands.

Her reflection floated on it, pale and small, and for one vertiginous second the reflection seemed to be looking up at her rather than merely being looked at. She stepped back. Her heel scraped stone. The sound fell into the well and did not come back as an echo—it came back as an *answer*, a soft double-tap, like a knuckle on wood, like a voice clearing its throat before speaking.

Petra got in her car and sat with both hands on the wheel until they stopped shaking enough to drive.

On the way down the hill she passed the church, and noticed that the noticeboard out front, the one with the removable letters, had been rearranged overnight. It now read, in cheerful black capitals:

**THEY ARE THIRSTY**

She didn't stop. She told herself later that she didn't stop because there was nothing to investigate—some prankster, some kid—but the truth was simpler and worse. She didn't stop because some part of her, the part that had been raised to be unremarkable, to be invisible, to pass through rooms without disturbing the air, understood instinctively that you do not draw attention to yourself when something is hunting by attention.

* * *

By Thursday, the mirrors started.

It began, as far as anyone could reconstruct afterward, with the Fentons on Marrow Street, whose son had died in March—a logging accident, quick, the kind of death the town processed efficiently with casseroles and a closed casket. Ruth Fenton called the show at 3 a.m., her voice pitched in that register between embarrassment and terror that Petra had come to know like a native language.

"I know how this sounds," Ruth said. "I've been telling myself all day how it sounds. But every mirror in my house fogs up, Petra. Every one. Bathroom mirror, hallway, the little round one in the hall table. And it's not condensation, because I wiped it and it comes back, and—" her voice caught, steadied, caught again, "—and there are prints in it. Handprints. Little ones. From the *inside*."

"From the inside of the glass," Petra repeated, because the job required her to repeat things back, and because her own voice was the only thing keeping the floor of the moment solid.

"Like somebody pressing their face and hands against a window," Ruth whispered. "Like somebody on the other side trying to get a look at us. And Danny—" Danny, the dead son. "Danny used to fog the bathroom mirror drawing smiley faces when he was small. I haven't been able to go in that bathroom. I've been using the one downstairs like a crazy woman and I know how it sounds, I *know*—"

"Ruth." Petra made her voice calm the way you make a bed: smoothing everything flat, tucking the terror in at the edges. "Has anything—has anything else happened? Anything you've heard?"

And here Ruth went quiet for four full seconds, and when she answered, she answered in the tone of someone confessing to a crime.

"There's singing. Under the floors. At night. A whole—Petra, it sounds like a whole *church choir*, dozens of voices, down under the crawlspace, and they're not singing hymns. They're singing *names*. Over and over. And last night I heard—" the whisper dropped so low the board meters barely twitched, "—last night I heard Danny's name. Third verse. His whole name, middle name and all, the way we only ever used it when he was in trouble or when we were proud of him."

Petra held the call together. She took Ruth's number off-air, promised to check in tomorrow, promised the things a host promises, and when the line clicked dead she sat very still in the blue light of the console.

Because here was the thing she had not said on air, the thing she would not say anywhere: the Fentons lived on Marrow Street, and Marrow Street was nowhere near the hill. Nowhere near the well. Whatever was rising in that shaft was not staying in the shaft. It was moving outward through the town the way frost moves through soil—invisible, patient, lifting everything it passes under.

By Friday morning, the station's voicemail had forty-one messages. Petra played them all with the door locked and the transmitter humming overhead like a held note.

A widow on Delacroix Lane whose late husband's shaving mirror fogged each dawn with a single word traced by a fingertip: *SOON*. A man whose newborn wouldn't stop staring at the ceiling corner of the nursery, tracking something that moved in slow circles, and whose wife had begun humming in her sleep—not any tune the man knew, but harmonized, two voices coming out of one sleeping throat. An entire apartment building on Founders Row where six separate tenants, none of whom spoke to each other, had filed noise complaints about "the congregation downstairs"—in a building with no basement, over a crawlspace sealed since 1961.

And underneath all of them, threaded through all of them, the same report, worded differently but always the same report: *singing under the floors. Names. So many names.*

They weren't haunting houses. Petra understood this with a clarity that felt like cold water going down. They were rehearsing. A choir doesn't sing in the sanctuary until it's ready. All week, all over town, under floorboards and behind glass, the dead had been warming up.

* * *

Friday night's show, she almost didn't do. She stood in the parking lot looking at the station's dark windows and seriously considered driving until the gas ran out, and then she thought about Ruth Fenton using her downstairs bathroom like a crazy woman, and about Danny's name sung in a third verse, and about a hundred and twelve names in a ledger in her bag that no living mouth had spoken aloud in a hundred years.

She went in. She put the headphones on. She opened the lines.

The calls came in a flood, and they were different now. For seven years the dead had called her one at a time, politely, each with their small unfinished errand—tell my daughter, tell my wife, tell him I forgave him. Grief's errands. But tonight the callers were the living, mostly, and the living all wanted the same thing: confirmation that they were not alone in what they were hearing, and failing that, company in it.

"The mirror in my mother's room," said an old man named Corbett, his voice papery. "My mother's been dead eleven years. This morning the mirror was fogged and there was writing in it and I'll thank you not to laugh, Miss Coldharbour, because I copied it down letter by letter." He read it out slowly, spelling it like a child sounding a hard word: "*We kept our side of it.*"

"What side of what?" Petra asked, though her blood had already gone cold, because she knew. Five movements out of seven. A covenant half-kept. The founders' ledger, with its careful columns and its water-eaten pages, weighed against her hip like a stone.

"That's just it," Corbett said. "I don't know. But my mother did. My mother grew up on that hill, Miss Coldharbour. Her family worked the Coldharbour land. And she never once let me look down that well. Never once, my whole childhood. I asked why once and she slapped me. Only time she ever hit me." A pause, wet breathing. "She said, *you don't holler down a hole that answers.*"

After Corbett, a young nurse on nights, whispering from a supply closet at St. Ambrose: the hospice wing's patients, the ones close to the end, had all begun singing in their sleep. Same song. Different keys, different cracked voices, but the same song, and none of them had ever met, and when she'd hummed a bar of it back to one of them the old woman had opened her eyes and said, clear as a bell, *"Oh good, you know it. Then you can tell them we're ready whenever they are."*

Then a caller with no name, no number on the board, a line that simply lit up. Static shaped like breathing. And beneath the static, that low choral voice Petra had first heard on her sleep tapes, the second voice, the one that answered questions she never asked aloud—except that tonight it didn't answer. Tonight it counted.

*"One hundred and twelve,"* it said, in a hundred throats at once, gentle as snowfall. *"One hundred and twelve, and the water rises, and the fifth movement frays like an old rope. Heir. Heir. The well is nearly ready to speak. Will you be?"*

The line went dead. The board showed nothing. In the studio, the ON AIR light burned steady red, and somewhere out in the dark town, in a thousand kitchens and bedrooms, Petra knew that everyone tuned to 104.7 had heard exactly what she'd heard—except that when the first listener called in, a trembling woman asking *what was that, what WAS that*, Petra checked the master tape out of habit and found the last ninety seconds blank. Clean hiss. Nothing.

Only she carried it. Only ever her.

* * *

She drove back up the hill at midnight, because apparently that was who she was now, a woman who drove toward the thing everyone else fled.

The smell reached her at the fence. Stronger now, sweeter, rot-underneath-sweet, and when she leaned over the stone lip with her phone flashlight aimed down, the beam found water twelve feet below the rim—up four feet in two days, black and utterly still, and on its surface, floating in a perfect ring, white petals. Dozens of them. She couldn't identify the flower they'd come from. There was nothing blooming within miles of the hill in October.

And written around the inner wall of the well shaft, just above the waterline, in a substance that glistened like wet slate and that she refused, refused, to examine more closely, words had appeared. Old-fashioned lettering. Careful, formal, patient. The way a founder would write.

**SIX MOVEMENTS REMAIN TO THE HEIR.
THE WATER WILL NOT WAIT FOR WINTER.
FINISH THE SONG OR WE FINISH IT OURSELVES —
AND WHAT WE SING THEN, NO ONE WILL WANT HEARD.**

Petra stood back from the lip. Her breath came out white in the cold and hung there longer than breath should hang, and as she watched it fade, from deep in the flooded dark below came a sound she had heard only once before, on a tape of her own sleeping mouth: a single low chord, many voices, swelling gently, testing its strength.

Not threatening. Worse than threatening.

*Tuning.*

She backed away, and her phone buzzed in her pocket. A text from Marta Vane, sent at 12:04 a.m., from an archivist who Petra was fairly sure had gone to bed decades ago along with everything else in that archive:

*Come at first light. Bring the ledger. There's a page I didn't show you. About the sixth movement — and about what happens to the heir if the water reaches the top first.*

Below the message, unsent-text gray, a second line already typed and waiting:

*Petra. The founders didn't build the ritual to free them. They built it to keep them from ever finishing their testimony. You need to understand what you'd actually be completing before you—*

The typing indicator pulsed. Pulsed. Stopped.

Then, thirty seconds later, one final message, no context, no signature, in Marta's dry archival cadence:

*The well gained another foot tonight. I measured it myself, forty years ago, out of an archivist's stubbornness. I still have the notebook. Girl — it's rising faster than the founders' worst-case margin. We have days. Perhaps less.*

Petra looked back at the well one last time. The chord had resolved now into something almost melodic, almost lovely, rising softly out of the black water into the cold air—a phrase of music without an ending, hanging unfinished over the hill like a question the whole town was about to be made to answer.

She turned and walked fast to her car, and did not let herself run, and failed.
