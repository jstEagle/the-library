## 15. The Torn Page

The crypt was where Marta had always said it would be, which was to say: nowhere, and beneath everything.

Aris found the entrance the way Marta's note had instructed—by counting the fence posts along the north boundary of the old Coldharbour parcel until one of them stood crooked, leaning east like a man listening at a door. Behind it, half-swallowed by blackberry bramble, a slab of granite lay flush with the hillside. No name on it. No dates. Just a carved line running its whole length, and when Aris knelt and put her palm against that line, she understood it wasn't decoration. It was a seam. The stone had been fitted over something hollow, the way a lid is fitted over a box, or a bandage over a wound.

"You feel that?" she asked.

Petra stood a few feet back with the ledger pressed to her chest like a shield. She hadn't spoken much since they left Marta's archive—since they'd seen the empty chair, the cold tea, the hymn still open on the desk in Marta's careful hand. Grief had gone into Petra somewhere below language, and what came out instead was a kind of terrible focus. She looked at the slab the way a person looks at a door they already know opens onto the worst room in the house.

"I feel everything," Petra said. "That's the problem."

Aris worked the seam with the flat of a pry bar she'd taken from her own trunk, from the kit she kept for opening doors that didn't want to be opened. The granite came up easier than it should have—as if the hill had been holding it loosely all these years, waiting. Beneath it: steps. Not rubble, not collapse, but clean-cut stairs descending into air that breathed out at them, cool and mineral and faintly sweet, like water that had been standing too long in a closed room.

The smell of the well. But older. Underneath it.

"Flashlights," Aris said, and they went down.

* * *

The stair turned twice before it ended, and both times the walls changed under their lights. The first stretch was fieldstone, ordinary, the work of farmers. Then dressed blocks, mortared, the work of men with money. And in the last ten feet, the stone was carved—not with symbols, Petra realized, but with words, thousands of them, running in columns so dense they overlapped, names upon names upon names, cut deep enough that the shadows inside each letter were black as ink.

She stopped walking. Her light found a column of names and began, helplessly, to read.

*Hale. Ostrander. Vane—no, not Vane, that was later—Vess. Quill. Marchetti. Ansel. Ansel. Ansel.*

Three Ansels. A family. Of course a family. The massacre hadn't taken strangers; it had taken neighbors, and neighbors have kin, and kin repeat names to their children until the children are old enough to be dangerous, and then—

"Petra." Aris's hand on her arm. "Breathe."

"They're all here." Petra's voice came out strange, flattened by the stone. "Every name I've been saying for seven years on the radio. They carved them into the walls of their own tomb. Like they knew someday someone would need to check."

"Founders don't build monuments to their victims," Aris said. "They build them to their own."

And she was right, mostly. Because the chamber at the bottom of the stairs was small, round, domed—and arranged around its circumference were seven sarcophagi, each bearing a name Petra now recognized from the ledger's frontispiece. *Coldharbour. Merrick. Aldous. Fenwick. Graye. Ostrova. Bell.* Seven founders. Seven signatures on a covenant written in other people's blood.

Six of the sarcophagi were sealed.

The seventh—the one nearest the stair, the one whose occupant had been laid down last, in 1911, according to the ledger's final dated entry—stood open. Its lid leaned against the wall like a door someone had meant to come back and close.

Inside lay not a body but a shroud.

It had been folded with ceremony, once, long ago, and time had made the folds permanent, stiff as bark. It was the color of old milk. And stitched across its entire surface, in thread that caught their flashlights and threw it back silver, was writing.

"There," Petra whispered. "Marta's clue. *'What was torn was never lost; it was kept warm.'*" She swallowed. "Sewn into a burial shroud. Kept against a body for a hundred years. Warm."

Aris crouched at the sarcophagus rim and did not touch it. "You're the heir," she said. "Not me. This has to be your hands."

Petra set the ledger down on the sixth sarcophagus, the sealed one, and reached into the seventh.

The shroud came up lighter than it looked, and colder than it should have been, and the moment it cleared the stone lip of the coffin every flashlight in the chamber dimmed—not out, but down, as if the light itself had been asked to speak more quietly.

Under the dome overhead, faintly, something exhaled.

Petra unfolded the shroud across the sealed sarcophagus, working by touch as much as sight, because the stitching was fine and the thread was the same color as the cloth except where it wasn't—except where it had darkened, over decades, with something that had seeped up from the body this linen had embraced. The writing ran in the same seven-part structure as the ledger. Movements one through five matched exactly, word for word, the rite the founders had completed. Movement six was there too, complete, the naming of the drowned, the restoration of identity at the wellhead.

And then movement seven.

In the ledger, that page had been torn out. Here, sewn into silk-threaded linen and buried with the last founder to die, the seventh movement existed entire.

Petra read it once in silence. Then again aloud, because Aris needed to hear it, and because some part of her understood that words like these were designed to be spoken, that reading them silently was like reading music without sound—a disrespect the text itself might notice.

"*The seventh movement is the vessel,*" she read. "*The rite does not end at the wellhead. Naming restores the dead; only housing keeps them named. Let the heir who was kept invisible open herself as the house. Let the choir come in and dwell. Let her voice become their voice, her breath their breath, her years their years, until the testimony is given and the debt is paid and the well runs dry. This is the cost. This was always the cost. We wrote it knowing none of us could pay it, for we were not invisible, and the choir would not enter the guilty. We leave it to the child who will not know what she is carrying until she is strong enough to carry it. Forgive us. We built you for this before we built the well.*"

The last sentence was smaller than the rest. Stitched in a different hand. Tighter, more hurried, the thread pulled so hard it had begun to cut the linen even then.

*We built you for this before we built the well.*

Petra's hands stopped moving. The shroud lay across the sarcophagus between them, silver-stitched, glowing faintly now in the dimmed light as if the words themselves were phosphorescent, as if they'd spent a century absorbing darkness just to be legible at the moment of reading.

"That's what invisible meant," she said. Her voice was very steady, which frightened Aris more than screaming would have. "All those moves. All those erased records. My mother teaching me how to be forgettable—how to stand in a room and have no one's eyes land on me. I thought it was damage. I thought it was trauma, passed down, and I hated them for it." She laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it. "It was construction. They weren't breaking me. They were *finishing* me. Like a tool. Like a vessel gets finished."

"Petra—"

"The choir wouldn't take the guilty. That's what it says. The founders couldn't do it themselves because the dead knew what they'd done. So they needed—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, hard, as if checking whether the cavity behind it was already occupied. "They needed an innocent. A blank. Someone with no crimes for the dead to refuse. Someone raised underground, socially, so thoroughly that the dead would look at her and see an empty house with the door open."

Aris had been a deputy for eleven years. She had knocked on doors at three in the morning to tell mothers things no sentence can hold. She knew how to stand in the space after terrible news and simply be present, without filling it. She did that now. She let the silence sit in the domed chamber, in the dimmed light, among the seven founders and their hundred-year lie.

Then she said, quietly: "Your parents are still alive. Your father hung up on you. But he picked up."

"What?"

"He picked up the phone. He answered a number he had every reason to believe was dead to him. People who finish building a thing don't answer calls about it unless part of them has been waiting for the call." Aris met her eyes. "I'm not defending him. I'm telling you there may be more pages. There may be instructions. There may be a way to do the seventh movement that doesn't end with you being a—" She searched for the word and refused to soften it. "A container."

"A house," Petra said. "The ritual says house. The book is called—" She stopped. Laughed again, worse this time. "The book was always called *A House for the Choir*. I thought I chose the title. I thought it was mine."

"It's yours," Aris said. "Whatever else it is, it's yours. That's the one thing they couldn't pre-write."

But even as she said it, Aris heard the flaw in her own comfort, and she saw Petra hear it too. Because the shroud was still speaking, in its way. The silver thread had begun to move—not visibly, not mechanically, but the way letters seem to move when you stare at them too long, rearranging at the edge of perception. And the final lines of the seventh movement, the ones Petra had read aloud, had shifted their order slightly, so that the last thing the eye landed on was no longer *forgive us* but:

*We leave it to the child.*

Below them—far below, through the hill's whole depth, through the flooded shaft and the chamber lined with mouths—the choir had gone quiet. Not silent. Quiet the way a congregation goes quiet when the doors open and the bride appears. Attentive. Waiting. A hundred and twelve voices holding one held breath.

And in the dimness, on the shroud, the stitches of the seventh movement began very slowly to darken, as though the words were drinking the light, as though the text had waited a hundred years to be read and was now, patiently, beginning to read her back.

Petra lifted the shroud from the sarcophagus and folded it, carefully, along its hundred-year-old creases, and held it against her chest where the ledger usually sat.

"We go up," she said. "Tonight. Before the councilmen pour their concrete. Before the well finishes filling." She looked at Aris, and her eyes in the flashlight-dim chamber were calm in a way that made Aris want to grab her and shake her and beg her not to be brave. "I need you to hold the rope one more time. And I need you to promise me something."

"Anything."

"If I start to say yes—if the seventh movement starts to take me and I lose the choice in the middle of it—you pull. You pull me out no matter what I say, no matter what voice comes out of my mouth. Even if it sounds like me. *Especially* if it sounds like me."

Aris took her hand. It was cold, and steady, and it felt, for the first time since Aris had known her, like a hand reaching down into water rather than up out of it.

"I promise," Aris said.

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of hill, the town's storm drains began, very softly, to sing.
