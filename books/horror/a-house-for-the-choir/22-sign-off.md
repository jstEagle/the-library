## 22. Sign-Off

The studio smells different now. That's the first thing Petra notices every time she sits down at the desk—no more burnt dust in the old console, no more mildew creeping up from the basement where the transmitter used to sweat through summer nights. The rebuilt station is small, low-wattage, a community frequency that reaches maybe thirty miles on a clear night and less when the fog rolls off the hills the way it always does in October. But the room is warm, and the microphone is new, and when she leans toward it, her breath doesn't fog the pop filter anymore.

One year. She has been counting without meaning to. The body keeps its own calendar; hers has been keeping it in water.

Outside the glass of the studio window, the town of Coldharbour has gone dark in the ordinary way towns go dark—not dead, just sleeping. Streetlights down Main. A single porch light burning at the Kell house, because Aris never did learn to sleep before two. The harvest festival grounds have been mowed back to grass. There's a memorial there now, a low stone wall with one hundred and twelve names cut into it, and every morning someone leaves something: apples, wildflowers, a child's drawing of a well with the water draining out of it like a held breath finally released.

Petra switches on the ON AIR sign and lets the red glow wash over her hands.

"Good evening, Coldharbour. You're listening to 1610 AM, the low-power frequency nobody owns and everybody's welcome to. This is Petra Coldharbour, and it's October again—which means it's anniversary night, which means if you're new in town, you've probably already heard the story three different ways from three different neighbors, and all three versions were wrong."

She smiles as she says it. She can hear the smile in the waveform on her monitor, a soft brightening of the signal.

"So tonight, for anyone newly arrived—for the young couple who bought the Hollis place, for the teacher who took over fourth grade at the elementary school, for whoever's renting the apartment above the hardware store where the mirrors used to fog—I'm going to tell it right. And then I'm going to read the names. Because that's what this show is now. That's what it was always supposed to be, I think, even back when I didn't know it. A place where the unfinished words get finished."

Beneath her voice, so faint that a stranger would call it imagination, something stirs. Not hunger. Not the old pulling ache from the days when the well was filling and the choir's need came up through the floorboards like cold through bare feet. This is gentler. It feels the way a house feels when everyone who lives in it is home.

She has learned to describe it honestly, even on air. Especially on air.

* * *

The story takes twenty minutes to tell properly, and she tells it slowly, the way Marta Vane taught her to handle fragile things—with both hands and no hurry.

She tells them about 1911, about the founders' covenant, about the massacre that the town's oldest families had agreed to erase: one hundred and twelve people, laborers and immigrants and troublemakers and witnesses, drowned in the dry well on the hill so their testimony would die with them. She tells them how the founders discovered that drowned testimony does not die—it only changes key—and how they built a ritual to keep the dead singing instead of speaking, seven movements of binding, completed only five. She tells them about the half-finished rite, the well refilling, the calls that came to an overnight radio show for seven years from neighbors four days dead, asking her to carry their last words to the living.

"And here's the part the gossip gets wrong," she says. "They weren't haunting me. They were calling for help. There's a difference, and the difference matters. A haunting wants something *from* you. A plea wants something *for* itself—to be heard, to be named, to be remembered. Everything strange that ever happened in this town, everything that scared us, was a plea. We just didn't know how to listen."

Her voice catches on the next part, the way it always does, and she lets it catch. The choir beneath her own ribs rises slightly to meet the catch, harmonizing with the crack in her throat until the crack sounds intentional, almost musical. Listeners have written in about this—the way her voice seems to hold more than one person sometimes, the way grief on this frequency comes out sounding like a hymn. She has stopped apologizing for it.

"I went down the shaft on the last night the water was rising," she says. "I spoke all one hundred and twelve names at the flooded chamber below the waterline. And then I did the thing the founders designed their heir to do, except I did it differently than they intended. They wanted a vessel—a container, a lock, a way to keep the dead quiet inside a living body forever. What I gave them instead was a house. There's a difference between a vessel and a house too. A vessel holds you against your will. A house leaves the door open."

She rests her hand flat against her sternum, over the place where the choir lives.

"They're still here. All of them. Not as ghosts, not as possession—as company. As testimony that refused to be buried twice. When I speak, they speak underneath me, and if you listen closely on a quiet night, you can hear them. And that's not a curse. I want to be very clear about that, especially for the kids listening past their bedtimes. It's not a curse. It's the sound of a town finally telling itself the truth."

* * *

Then the names.

She reads them the way she read them in the dark under the earth, one hundred and twelve times, each one given its full weight, its full silence after. The list is printed and laminated now—Aris insisted, after the night Petra recited them from memory during a fever and got one wrong and wept for an hour—but she barely glances at the page. The names live in her the way her own name lives in her.

"Elias Maren. Odile Ferrant. Tomas and Ilse Brandt. Young Sam Brandt, aged nine..."

The boy's name still lands hardest. Nine years old, drowned with his mother because he had seen the faces of the men who did it. Some nights Petra dreams in his voice, high and clear, describing clouds. She wakes and thanks him for the dream, out loud, alone in her kitchen, and means it.

"...Ruth Calloway. Deshi Arun. Marguerite Voss. Peter Voss. The Voss infant, unnamed in the ledger—we call her June, because she was born in June, and because the dead deserve at least the dignity of a season..."

On and on, the red ON AIR light steady, the little transmitter pushing her voice out across sleeping fields and dark porches. In the hospital's long-term wing, she knows, a nurse will turn the radio on low for the patients who like it. At the diner, the night cook keeps it playing over the griddle. In the county jail, two former councilmen lie awake in their cells and cannot make themselves turn it off, though God knows they've tried. The broadcast made the evidence undeniable—testimony spoken by the dead through a living mouth, corroborated by documents, by the ledger, by the graves the vigilantes' grandfathers dug in secret and the land records that proved who owned what and who profited. Justice moved slowly after that, but it moved. Aris made sure.

"...and last, the one whose name was almost lost entirely: Serafina Okonkwo, midwife, who kept the ledger of the massacre in her own hand and hid it inside a hymnal, and whose courage is the reason any of you know these names at all."

Silence after the final name. Petra lets it run long—ten seconds, fifteen—because the silence is part of the reading. The dead taught her that. A name without silence after it is just noise. A name followed by silence is a bell being struck.

When she speaks again, her voice is softer.

"If you're new in town, you might feel something while those names were read. A pressure behind your eyes. An ache in your chest that doesn't belong to you. Don't be frightened of it. That's not a haunting either. That's one hundred and twelve people saying hello."

* * *

Afterward, she opens the phone lines, because she always does, because the show was never meant to be a monologue. The calls come slow on anniversary night—people are listening rather than talking—but they come.

A woman whose grandmother came to town in the fifties, asking whether the memorial wall accepts added stones for the families who carried the secret afterward. Yes, Petra tells her. Bring the stone. Bring the truth that goes with it.

A teenage boy, nervous, voice cracking, who asks the question half the town has wondered and no one has dared ask her directly: *Does it hurt? Having them inside you?*

Petra considers the question with real care, because he deserves a real answer.

"It hurts the way a scar hurts," she says. "Not most days. On some days, weather gets into it. But a scar also means the wound closed. Before, I was an open wound walking around pretending to be a woman. Now I'm closed. Now I'm whole in a shape that took some getting used to." She pauses. "And some mornings, when I make coffee, somebody hums along with the kettle. And I'd rather have that than the silence I had before. Silence was never peace. I know that better than anybody. Silence was just the screaming pitched too low to hear."

A third caller doesn't speak at all. Just breathing, and then, faintly, humming—a few bars of the hymn Serafina Okonkwo hid her ledger inside, the melody the choir taught the whole town without meaning to. Petra recognizes the tune and sings the next phrase softly into the phone, and the caller joins her, and for a moment the line between host and listener, living and housed-dead, broadcast and reception, goes thin as paper. Then the caller hangs up gently, the way you leave a room where someone is sleeping.

Near the end of the hour, Aris texts her: *Still up? Porch light's on. Coffee after?*

*After sign-off,* she texts back. *Always after sign-off.*

* * *

At 4 a.m., the hour her old show used to begin, back when the dead called in and she logged miracles as coincidences, Petra leans close to the microphone for the last time tonight.

"That's the show. For those of you just joining us on this frequency—welcome. You live in a town that did something terrible a long time ago, and then spent a century pretending it hadn't, and the pretending nearly killed us all. But we stopped pretending. We said the names. We built the wall. We're learning. Come to the memorial sometime. Leave an apple. Listen for the singing under the wind—it's friendly, I promise. It's the friendliest sound in this valley, once you know what it is."

She looks out the studio window at the dark town, at the hill where the well stands empty and open now, unsealed by vote of the town council—Aris's doing, the last act of her defection—a stone-lined shaft leading down to a dry chamber where visitors leave candles and children dare each other to shout their own names and hear them echo. No one seals it. No one fills it. It stays open the way a wound stays open when closing it would trap something inside. It stays open the way a door stays open in a house where everyone is welcome.

"This is Petra Coldharbour, signing off. To the newest residents of Coldharbour: welcome home. To the ones who've been here a hundred and twelve years longer than the rest of us—" her voice gentles, drops into the register where the harmony lives, "—goodnight. Rest easy. You're heard. You're named. You're home."

She reaches for the switch.

And the second voice—the low, choral voice that answered questions she never asked aloud, that broke through her broadcasts, that warned her to stop digging before the well refilled, that guided her through smoke and flood and the long dark under the waterline—speaks.

It doesn't surge through the speakers this time. It doesn't possess the signal or override the tape. It simply rises, from inside her own chest, through her own mouth, wearing her own voice the way a melody wears its instrument, and it says:

*"Goodnight, Petra."*

Softly. Warmly. And for the first time in all the years of static and sleep-talks and drowned callers, it sounds like gratitude.

She sits very still in the red glow of the ON AIR sign, one hand on the switch, one hand over her heart, listening to the harmonic fade of her own name spoken by the hundred and twelve. Outside, dawn is still an hour away, but the sky above the hill has begun to lighten at its edge, gray turning to pearl, the way it does on mornings after a storm has finally, fully passed.

Petra smiles, flips the switch, and lets the frequency fall into silence—

—but it isn't silence, not really. It never will be again. It's the sound of a full house settling contentedly into sleep, somewhere deep and safe and warm, behind a door left lovingly open.

She turns off the studio lights, picks up her coat, and steps out into the cold bright beginning of the day.
