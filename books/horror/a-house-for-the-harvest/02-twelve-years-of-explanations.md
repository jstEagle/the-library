## 2. Twelve Years of Explanations

The house had always made noise. That was the first thing Zephyrine remembered, before faces, before names, before she knew the word *Ishikawa* belonged to her: the sound of the house thinking to itself.

She had been seven when she started the catalogue. A spiral notebook with a blue cover, the kind sold at the drugstore in Harlow Falls for sixty-nine cents, and on the first page, in her careful child's cursive: **THINGS THE HOUSE DOES.** She had kept it under her mattress, because even at seven she understood that some observations were better made in secret.

*Footsteps upstairs when no one is up there.* That was entry one. Slow, deliberate steps, crossing the floor of the sewing room above her bedroom at two in the morning, pausing at the spot where the floor always creaked, and then — this was the part she never wrote down, the part she only admitted to herself in the dark — *stopping*, as if the walker had heard her listening.

*Humming in the walls.* Entry two. A tune she could never quite hold on to, like water closing over a stone. It came from inside the plaster, inside the lathe, from the spaces between the rooms where the house kept its body. Her mother's voice, but thinner, or her grandmother's, or no one's.

*The kitchen door opens by itself, but only when someone is sad.* Entry three, written the winter after Grandmother Maren died, when the door had swung open on its hinge every evening at dusk for nine days, as if offering itself, as if the house had heard the crying and wanted to help.

She had brought the notebook to her mother once. She remembered the kitchen, the smell of yeast and brown sugar, her mother's hands dusted with flour, and how those hands had gone still — not angry-still, not frightened-still, but *careful*-still, the way a person goes still when a bird lands on the table and any movement might startle it into something worse.

"Let me see," her mother had said, and read the whole notebook standing up, and when she finished she closed it gently, the way you close a hymnal, and said, "Zephyrine. It's just the settling. Old houses settle. The wood contracts at night, the nails shift, the foundation finds its level. That's all any of it is. Settling."

"But it hums," Zephyrine had said. "Settling doesn't hum."

Her mother had smiled then, and the smile had been the worst part, because it was warm and real and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "It hums because it's happy," she'd said. "A house that's loved will sing. Now go wash up. Supper's nearly ready."

And Zephyrine, who was seven and hungry and wanted very much to believe her mother, had gone and washed up, and the humming in the walls had continued all through supper, low and patient, and she had decided — the way children decide things, absolutely and without appeal — that her mother was lying, but that the lie was a kindness, and that she would carry it for her.

She had carried it for twelve years. She had carried it all the way to a city four hundred miles south, to a walk-up apartment where the walls made no sound at all, where the silence had been so total the first year that she'd had to buy a clock that ticked just to sleep. She had carried it through phone calls that grew shorter, through visits that grew rarer, through the last visit — the one she still could not think about without her hands going cold — when her mother had stood in the doorway of the farmhouse and said, "You could stay, you know. The house misses you. It asks about you."

*It asks about you.* She had laughed. She had actually laughed, and gotten in her car, and driven south with the windows down, and told herself the shiver up her spine was just the wind.

That was the last time she'd seen her mother alive. And now she was back, and her mother was in the ground three days, and her brother was in a casket in the front parlor, and the house was humming.

* * *

The wake was small. That was what happened to families that frayed — the funerals got smaller, the casseroles fewer, the flowers from the grocery store instead of the florist. There were perhaps fifteen people in the parlor: Odette Crane from next door, ancient and upright in a navy dress that had clearly outlived three decades of funerals; a scattering of cousins from her mother's side who kept looking at their phones; the Reverend Albee, who had known Zephyrus only from the Christmas pageants of long ago; and a man Zephyrine didn't recognize at all, gray-haired, who stood by the window with his hat in his hands and never once looked at the casket.

Zephyrine stood near the door. She had been near the door for an hour. She was aware that people were looking at her — the sister, the twin, the one who'd left — and she could read the thought on every face the way you read a headline: *twelve years away, and she comes back for the funeral.* She let them think it. It was true, and the truth of it was a stone she had decided to carry rather than set down.

Zephyrus had stayed. That was the shape of her guilt, the exact geometry of it. When their mother got sick, Zephyrine had come back for two weeks and then gone, citing the job, the lease, the life — all the small true things that add up to a large false one. Zephyrus had stayed. He had driven their mother to every appointment, sat with her through every night, kept the farmhouse running, kept *her* running. And then, three weeks after the funeral that Zephyrine had attended in a fog, Zephyrus had gone out to the back field on an October evening and simply — stopped. That was how the sheriff had put it, awkwardly, kindly: *he stopped.* No wound, no pill bottle, no note. A thirty-one-year-old man found in the tall grass with his eyes open and his face, the deputy said, "kind of peaceful, ma'am. If that helps."

It did not help.

Now she stood in the parlor where they'd held their grandmother's wake, where their mother's casket had stood three weeks ago, and she listened to the murmured condolences, and beneath it all — beneath the Reverend's low voice and the clink of the coffee urn and the shuffling of the cousins — she heard it.

The humming.

It was coming from the walls. From the wall behind the casket, she thought, or maybe the one by the stairs, or maybe from everywhere at once, the way a sound behaves when it is coming from the bones of a building. It was the same tune from the notebook, from all those years ago — she knew it the way you know a scar, instantly, with your whole skin — and it was low and patient and it rose and fell like breathing, and it was *louder than it used to be.*

She set down her coffee. She crossed the parlor, past the cousins, past Odette Crane, whose eyes tracked her with an attention that felt less like curiosity than like recognition, and she pressed her palm flat against the wallpaper by the staircase.

The humming was in the wall. Under her hand. She could feel it, a vibration so faint it might have been her own pulse, except that her pulse was hammering in her throat and this was slow, this was *contented*, this was a sound that had been going on for a long time and intended to go on longer.

"Miss Ishikawa."

She turned. It was the gray-haired man with the hat. Up close he looked familiar in the way that faces from dreams are familiar — a shape she knew without a name. He nodded toward the wall her hand was still touching, and something in his expression made her take her hand away.

"Old houses," he said. "They settle."

The words landed wrong. Not because they were untrue but because they were *rehearsed* — she heard it now, the flatness of a line said many times, handed down like a recipe. She looked at him and he looked at her and for a moment neither of them blinked, and then Odette Crane was there between them, small and iron, taking Zephyrine's arm.

"Come and sit, dear," Odette said. "You've been on your feet all afternoon."

* * *

That night, after the last cousin had gone and the Reverend had folded his vestments into his car and driven off down the county road, Zephyrine sat alone in the kitchen with her mother's ledger open in front of her, because grief in this family had always been a kind of paperwork. The funeral home needed signatures. The bank needed signatures. Everyone needed her to prove, over and over, that she was the one still alive.

The kitchen was exactly as it had been when she was seven. Same yellow light over the table. Same crack in the corner tile, shaped like a river delta. Her mother's flour canister still on the counter, and — she checked, and yes, she could not stop herself from checking — the notebook was not under her mattress anymore, because her mattress was in a dumpster somewhere in the city, but the muscle memory of checking had survived the twelve years intact.

The house was quiet. That was the strange part. After the humming in the parlor, the silence felt like held breath.

Then, at eleven minutes past midnight, it started again.

It came up through the floor first — she felt it in the soles of her feet before she heard it — and then it was in the walls, in the studs, in the old plaster, and it was the same tune, the one she had chased through her whole childhood without ever catching it, and now, sitting alone at her mother's table in her dead mother's kitchen, she did something she had never done as a child.

She hummed back.

It was barely a sound. Three notes, tentative, matching the rise and fall. She was not even sure she was doing it on purpose; grief does strange bookkeeping, and somewhere in the ledger of this day there had been an entry she hadn't authorized. But she hummed three notes, and the humming in the walls stopped.

Stopped — and then answered. The next phrase of the tune, the one that always came after, the one she had spent seven years of childhood trying to memorize and could never hold, came through the wall clear and deliberate, note by note, the way a teacher demonstrates. Like it was pleased. Like it had been waiting a long time for a student.

Zephyrine sat very still. Her hands were flat on the table. The yellow light buzzed. And the house hummed to her, patient and warm, the way her mother had hummed over the bread dough, the way her mother had hummed in the garden, the way — she realized, with a cold that started in her scalp and moved downward — the way her mother had hummed on the last visit, standing in the doorway, saying *the house misses you, it asks about you,* and Zephyrine had thought it was a manner of speaking.

It had not been a manner of speaking. It had been a report.

She got up from the table. She did not run — running felt like something the house would enjoy — but she walked, quickly and evenly, down the hall to the stairs, and as she walked the humming followed her inside the walls, keeping pace, moving with her the way a sound moves when it is coming from everywhere, and she climbed the stairs and went into her childhood bedroom and shut the door and stood in the dark with her back against it.

Through the door, muffled and gentle, the humming continued. It did not sound like a threat. That was the terrible thing. It sounded like a lullaby. It sounded like something that loved her.

*It's just the settling,* she told herself, her mother's words, her mother's careful voice. *Old houses settle.*

But settling did not follow you from room to room. Settling did not pause when you hummed, and answer, and *wait.* Settling did not sound, when she finally lay down on her childhood bed and stared at the ceiling, like something on the other side of the wall that had noticed her attention and was savoring it.

She thought about Zephyrus in the casket. The face she had looked at for an hour that afternoon, trying to find her own face in it, her twin's face, the face she had known better than her own — and how something had been wrong with it, something she couldn't name, some small failure of resemblance that she had blamed on the embalmer, on grief, on the twelve years that had made strangers of everyone.

She thought about the gray-haired man who had said *they settle* like a catechism.

She thought: *tomorrow I will call the funeral home. Tomorrow I will ask to see the paperwork. Because twins have paperwork — birth certificates, hospital records, fingerprints — and I will compare, and it will be fine, and I will feel foolish.*

The humming in the walls changed key. It dropped low, almost to a whisper, and it took on a rhythm she recognized — not the tune anymore, but something underneath the tune, a pulse, a patience, a hunger wearing patience like a coat.

She lay in the dark and did not sleep, and at some hour past three, the footsteps started upstairs. Slow. Deliberate. Crossing the sewing room floor. Pausing at the creaking spot.

And then, for the first time in her life, not stopping there. Continuing. Down the hall. To the top of the stairs. Where they stopped, and stayed stopped, all through the rest of the night, directly outside her door — as if something had finally decided that twelve years of explanations were over, and it was time, at last, to come down and introduce itself.

In the morning, she would go to Odette Crane's. She didn't know why she was sure of it — only that the old woman had looked at her yesterday like a woman watching a fire start in a house she'd warned everyone about, and that Zephyrine had spent one night alone with the house's voice and already understood: whatever this was, her mother had known. Her mother had spent her whole life knowing.

And her mother was in the ground, and could not tell her.

But Odette was still breathing.
