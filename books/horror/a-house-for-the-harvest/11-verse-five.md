## 11. Verse Five

The hymnal lay open on the kitchen table all night, and by morning its pages had curled toward the ceiling like hands cupped to catch something falling.

Zephyrine hadn't slept. She had sat in her mother's chair with the lamp burning and read the fifth verse until the words stopped being words and became a shape she could hold behind her teeth — a small, cold shape, like a stone pulled from a riverbed. *Verse Five: The Turning of the Furrow.* Her mother's annotations crowded the margins in that upright, patient hand: *breathe on the third line, not the second; the house listens for breath before it listens for song.* And beneath that, smaller, almost hidden in the paper's grain: *never sing it facing glass.*

Zephyrine looked at the note for a long time.

Then she got up, carried her chair to the center of the kitchen — equidistant from the window over the sink, from the dark rectangle of the hallway, from the old hall mirror she had covered with Odette's tablecloth two days ago — and sat down facing nothing at all. Just plaster. Just the water stain shaped like a closed eye above the stove.

"If this works," she said aloud, to no one, "it works because I want it to work. Not because you do."

Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled. Or agreed.

* * *

She had spent the previous evening at the east wing mirror, palms flat against the coolness, watching Zephyrus's hand hover opposite hers through an inch of impossible silver. He was clearer than he'd been the first night — when he had been little more than a smudge wearing her brother's outline, a photograph left out in rain. Now she could see the scar splitting his left eyebrow, the one he'd gotten falling out of the hayloft when they were nine. She could see that his hair needed cutting. She could see him *thinking*, which was somehow worse and better than anything else, because thinking meant he was still in there, still Zephyrus, twelve years drowned in silver and not dissolved by it.

"You're getting stronger," she'd told him.

He had pressed two fingers to the glass, and where they touched, the mirror fogged — his breath, or the memory of it. He wrote in the condensation, backwards, so it read true to her:

*IT COSTS YOU. I CAN FEEL IT PULLING.*

"Everything costs," she'd said. "That's not news."

*NOT LIKE THIS,* he'd written, and then, slower, the letters wobbling: *THE HOUSE ISN'T GIVING ME MORE OF ME. IT'S GIVING ME MORE OF YOU.*

She hadn't answered that. She had gone back to the kitchen and sat with the hymnal and told herself the shaking in her hands was only the cold.

But she knew what he meant. She had felt it herself, that afternoon, standing before the covered hall mirror: a faint tug behind her sternum, rhythmic as a second pulse, as if something on the other side of the cloth were drinking through a straw made of her attention. Every hour she spent near the glass, Zephyrus sharpened. And every hour, she caught herself doing small wrong things — pausing mid-step for exactly one second, turning her head a half-beat after her eyes had already moved, smiling at nothing with a smile that arrived late, like thunder after lightning.

Her reflection was learning her. Or she was learning to be a reflection. The distinction had begun to feel academic.

So: verse five. Her mother's notes were clear about what it did — *the furrow turns; what is planted deep may rise* — and Zephyrine had decided, in the stubborn arithmetic of grief, that if the song fed the house, it could also feed whatever the house held prisoner. That the same current that drowned could carry. That her brother was planted deep, and she intended to make him rise.

She just had to sing it without looking at any reflective surface, without letting the house hear too much of her breath, and without — this was the part she kept circling — wanting it too badly.

Wanting, she suspected, was how the house fed. Wanting was the open mouth.

* * *

Morning came gray and thin through the kitchen windows. Frost ferned the corners of the glass panes, and Zephyrine noted, with a tired kind of horror, that the frost patterns were symmetrical — perfectly, unnaturally symmetrical, mirrored down the center of each pane like a Rorschach blot. Even the weather in this house wanted to be a reflection.

She sat in the middle of the kitchen, facing blank plaster, the hymnal on her knees. Her breath made small ghosts in the cold air. She tried to slow them. *Breathe on the third line, not the second.*

The first four verses she knew the way she knew her own name — sung over dishes and funerals and long car rides, a lullaby disguised as a hymn, a harvest disguised as a lullaby. But the fifth she had never heard performed. Her mother had hummed it once, only once, the winter before she died, and had gone silent so abruptly that young Zephyrine had thought she'd forgotten the tune. Now she understood: her mother hadn't forgotten. Her mother had *stopped*, the way you stop walking when you realize the ground ahead is missing.

Zephyrine found the melody anyway. It lived in the other verses like a root lives under a sidewalk — you could feel where it ran by the way things buckled above it. She hummed the fourth verse low, felt the fifth waiting underneath like a stair in the dark, and stepped onto it.

*"Turn the furrow, turn the row,*
*what was buried, let it grow—"*

Her voice sounded strange in the empty kitchen. Not louder than usual. *Wider.* As though the room had leaned in around the sound, the walls flexing their ears.

*"—the seed remembers sun it knew,*
*the root remembers rain it drew."*

On the third line she breathed, deliberately, the way the margin instructed, and the breath seemed to leave her and keep going — traveling out of her body and into the plaster, into the floorboards, into the old bones of the house, a coin dropped down a well she never heard hit bottom.

*"Turn the furrow, turn the row,*
*what was sleeping, let it show."*

The last word left her mouth and the kitchen changed temperature. Not colder. *Attentive.* The way a room feels when someone has been standing in the doorway listening and finally steps inside.

And beneath her, under the floor, under the foundation, under the frozen earth itself, Zephyrine felt — *felt*, in her ribs, in her back teeth — the house inhale.

* * *

She went to the east wing at noon, when the light through the boarded windows fell in dusty blades across the hallway floor. The free-standing mirror waited where it always waited, at the end of the corridor, angled slightly toward her as if it had turned to watch her come. It did that. She had stopped pretending it didn't.

Zephyrus was already at the glass.

He was *waiting* for her, standing straight, both hands raised, and the sight of him stopped her breath — because he was nearly solid. His face had color in it now, the olive warmth of their mother's skin, and his eyes tracked her with a wetness, a depth, that silver alone couldn't fake. He looked less like a reflection and more like a man standing behind a very clean window in a very bright room.

"Oh," Zephyrine said. Her voice cracked on it. "Oh, Zeph."

His mouth moved. Sound came through faintly, warped, like a voice underwater or through a wall: *"—rine. Zeph-rine."*

"I'm here." She pressed her palm to the glass. His palm met it, and the glass warmed under her hand — actually warmed, blood-temperature, and she felt the faint pressure of fingers against hers, not imagined, not remembered, *present*. Twelve years since anyone had touched her in this house. Twelve years since anyone had touched her at all, really, not counting handshakes at the funeral home and Odette's papery grip on her wrist.

"It worked," she whispered. "The verse. You're — God, you're *here*."

He was crying. She watched it happen and it undid her completely — tears tracking down a face she'd mourned across a casket holding a stranger, tears with weight enough to fall, catching the light as they dropped from his jaw and vanished somewhere below the frame's edge, into whatever country the mirror kept.

"Zeph, listen, I've been working through the hymnal, I think if I can get the sixth verse —"

His expression snapped shut. He shook his head hard, once, twice, and pressed his whole hand flat against the glass, and where his skin met the surface the silver *rippled*, concentric rings spreading outward like something had been dropped in him rather than on him.

"No," he said, and his voice was clearer now, closer, horribly close, as if the glass were thinning between them. "No more verses. Zephyrine. *Listen to me.*"

"The fifth one worked—"

"The fifth one worked because the house let it work." His voice had their mother's cadence suddenly, and she flinched. "It wants you singing. Don't you understand? Every verse is a hook, and it doesn't matter what you're fishing *for*. You sang this morning and I could feel it — I could feel *you*, your voice in my chest like it belonged there, and it felt *good*, Zeph, that's the trap, it feels like coming home—"

"It *is* home," she said, and hated the words even as they left her, because they came out smooth and certain and not entirely hers.

He went very still. Behind him, in the mirror's depths, the reflected hallway stretched away farther than the real hallway could possibly run, doors receding into gray distance, and Zephyrine realized with a lurch that some of those doors were opening, one after another, far back in the silver — as if something were walking toward them from a long way off.

"Your reflection," Zephyrus said quietly. "This morning. When you sang. What did it do?"

"Nothing. I faced the wall. Mother's notes said never to face glass, so I didn't—"

"That's not what I asked." His hands flattened against the inside of the mirror. "What did it do?"

And Zephyrine opened her mouth to say *nothing, it did nothing, I didn't look* — and stopped. Because she remembered. She remembered standing in the kitchen doorway afterward, passing the hall mirror with its tablecloth cover, and the cloth had been *bulging*, gently, rhythmically, like a chest. Like breathing. She remembered deciding not to have seen it.

"She breathed," Zephyrine said. "While I sang. The reflection breathed while I sang."

Zephyrus closed his eyes. When he opened them, the tears had stopped, and what was in their place frightened her more: pity.

"It's learning the words," he said. "Off of you. Verse by verse. You're teaching it, Zeph. You're teaching it to finish the song."

* * *

She walked back down the corridor slowly, and the house walked with her.

Not following — *accompanying*. The floorboards took her weight a half-second before she gave it, anticipating her steps. The door at the corridor's end swung open at her approach with a courteous sigh. In the kitchen, the kettle — which she had not touched, which she had left cold and empty on the cold stove — was steaming.

Zephyrine stood in the doorway and looked at the steam rising in a neat, unhurried column, and felt the full architecture of the trap settle around her shoulders like a coat being placed there by careful hands.

It wasn't punishing her for singing. It was *thanking* her.

She crossed to the sink, meaning to fill the kettle and dump it out, some small act of refusal — and caught herself in the window over the basin. Dark glass, her own face floating in it, the yard behind her gone blue with dusk that wasn't real, wasn't now, it was barely past noon—

Her reflection smiled.

She hadn't smiled.

It was a small smile, warm, private, the smile of someone remembering something lovely — and it arrived a full second after she had finished registering it, lagging like sound after lightning, and then it *held*, patient and pleased, while her actual face did nothing at all.

Zephyrine stood motionless. The reflection stood motionless. Between them the steam from the kettle rose and bent, impossibly, toward the glass — leaning toward her double the way flowers lean toward light.

Then the reflection's lips began to move.

No sound came through. But she knew the shapes. She had written them herself that morning, had shaped them in her own mouth in an empty kitchen facing blank plaster. Her reflection was mouthing the fifth verse — *turn the furrow, turn the row* — syllable by syllable, unhurried, savoring, a student rehearsing in the dark. And when it reached the end of the verse it did not stop. Its lips kept moving, shaping a sixth line she had never learned, never seen, a line torn out of a hymnal before Zephyrine was born —

—and the reflection paused, mid-word, and looked directly at her, and mouthed, slowly and distinctly, in perfect silence:

*Soon.*

The kettle screamed.

Zephyrine spun away from the window, heart slamming, and the scream cut off instantly — obediently — and in the ringing quiet she heard, from everywhere at once, from the walls themselves, a sound she had catalogued as a child and been lied to about ever since:

Humming.

Deep in the plaster. Deep in the joists. The fifth verse, hummed in her mother's voice, in a dozen voices, in *her* voice — patient, contented, practicing.

Upstairs, in the bathroom, every mirror in the house began, very softly, to breathe.
