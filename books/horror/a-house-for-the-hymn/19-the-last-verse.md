## 19. The Last Verse

The moon would rise full in nineteen hours, and Elodie Brightwater had not slept.

She had tried. She had lain down on the couch in the farmhouse parlor at midnight with her boots still on, one hand curled around a flashlight like a child with a security blanket, and she had listened to the house breathe around her—the tick of cooling boards, the slow settling of the chimney—and somewhere past two she had drifted into something that wasn't sleep so much as standing in a dark room while someone else wore her body at a distance. When she surfaced, the dead choir was gone from the yard. Grandma's hymnal was open on her chest, though she had no memory of taking it out of the attic box, and it was open to the last page, and the page was waiting for her.

She sat up. The curtains in the parlor window were drawn, and behind them the sky was doing that thing October does before dawn—not light yet, but no longer night, a gray that felt held, like breath.

"Okay," she said to the room. Her voice came out scraped and small. "Okay. I'm listening."

The hymnal lay across her knees, its cover soft as old skin, stitched by hand from what looked like flour-sack cloth gone brown with age. *A Hymn of Keeping*, said the title page, in copperplate that trembled only slightly. Below it, where a name should have been, the paper was rough as a scar. Someone had scratched their name out so thoroughly they'd gone through the page and left a hole shaped like nothing at all.

Elodie had spent four days failing to read the final verse. Four days of holding the page under lamplight, under flashlight, under the cold blue screen of her phone, and every time her eyes reached the inked-over lines they slid off like water off wax. She could see there were words there. She could feel them, the way you can feel a splinter under skin. But meaning refused to land. It was like trying to look directly at the sun, except the sun was made of language.

Grandma had pressed the book into her hands last night and spoken—finally, after fifteen years of silence, after all those dawns of pointing at the attic—*Finish it better than we did.* And then the choir had filled the house with verses one through three, and Elodie had understood that the waiting was over, and that whatever she was going to do, she'd have to do it before moonrise.

She carried the hymnal into the kitchen because the kitchen was the heart of the house and the heart of the haunting, and because the extra chair stood there, pulled out from the table, patient as a held door. She didn't look at it. Looking at it felt like an acknowledgment, and acknowledgments were invitations, and she was done inviting things.

She set the hymnal on the table. She lit a candle because the electricity in the house had been behaving strangely since the choir arrived—lights dimming when the humming started, brightening when it stopped, as if the wiring itself were listening—and because fire felt honest in a way bulbs didn't.

Then she went to the windows and took down the curtains.

They were her grandmother's curtains, heavy cotton gone ivory with decades of kitchen steam, embroidered along the hem with a row of small blue birds that Elodie had traced with her finger as a girl during long boring sermons of summers. She stood in front of the east window with them gathered in her arms and hesitated only a moment.

"I'm sorry, Grandma," she said. "I think you'd understand."

She fed the first curtain to the candle flame at the sink, where nothing else could catch, and hung the burning fabric over the back of a wooden chair angled toward the table, and the kitchen filled with a light that moved and breathed and threw shadows that didn't quite match the things casting them.

Here is what Elodie learned in the next hour, and she learned it the way you learn anything true: accidentally, and too late to be spared.

It wasn't the firelight itself. Firelight alone did nothing; she'd tried candles for days. It was the burning. The curtains had hung in this kitchen for sixty years. They had absorbed everything the house had absorbed—the hymn hummed low by a woman trying to keep a lid on something, the prayers said over meals, the years of swallowed words. They were soaked in keeping. And now they were giving it back as light, and light that has been paid for shows you things that free light will hide.

She opened the hymnal to the last page and held it up to the burning curtain, and the verse rose off the paper like something surfacing from deep water.

Not all at once. First the first line, arriving whole and plain as if it had always been legible and the failure had been hers:

*What was given must be gathered,*

Her breath caught. She kept reading.

*what was fed must be confined.*

The second line landed harder. She read it three times, and each time it stayed, and staying was new, staying was everything.

*Sing it single, sing it sole,*
*one voice for the whole design:*
*the door swings wide for many,*
*but shuts for one who stands inside.*

Elodie set the hymnal down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be sleeping.

"It's not an invitation," she said aloud. "It never was."

The candle guttered. The burning curtain hissed. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard shifted its weight, listening.

Because she had spent weeks assuming the opposite. Everyone had. The congregation in 1989 had written their fourth verse as a bargain—voices for prosperity, a payment in harmony—and the thing in the house had taken the deal and burned the church as its receipt. Elodie had assumed the original song, the composer's song, was the same species of transaction: a door propped open, a summons dressed up in melody. That was why singing it completed an invitation. That was why Grandma had forbidden it, why the name on the title page had been scratched out of every record in town, why the composer had hanged himself in this attic in 1911.

But the verse in her hands wasn't a summons. It was a cage.

*Sing it single, sing it sole.*

The song was built to be sung alone. One voice, unaccompanied, refusing harmony—that wasn't pride or performance. That was the mechanism. A chorus opened the door wide; a solo voice closed it. The hymn was a lock, and the lock only turned for a single key, and the key was a person willing to stand inside the doorway while it shut.

*One voice for the whole design... but shuts for one who stands inside.*

The composer hadn't died of guilt. He had died holding the lock shut. He had written the binding, sung it alone in this house, and then—because a lock needs someone on the inside of the door, because the thing had to be *kept* by a keeper—he had stayed. Or tried to. And when his strength ran out, when his single voice finally failed, the family had inherited the problem without inheriting the explanation. Grandma hadn't forbidden the hymn out of piety. She had forbidden it because she knew what it was for, and she knew that knowing was dangerous, and she had hoped—God, how she must have hoped—that if nobody ever sang it, the lock would hold on its own momentum, the way a wall holds after the mason dies.

And then 1989 happened. And the congregation, not understanding what they were handling, had taken the fourth verse and bent it into a bargain, had sung it *together*, in harmony, a chorus—which was exactly wrong, which was the one way to sing it that didn't close anything but instead flung everything open—and they had fed the thing for thirty-five years and called the feeding prosperity.

Elodie thought of the ex-choir director's offer, two nights ago. *Give me the show. Let me sing it. Take the burden off you.* And she had seen what his own bargain had cost him—the unaged face, the daughter whose name he couldn't hold onto—and she had refused him. Now she understood the refusal differently. He hadn't been offering to help. He had been offering to keep the door open. Fifteen more years of feeding, dressed as mercy. Maybe he didn't even know that's what he was. Maybe none of them had ever known. The thing was clever enough to let its food believe they were the ones being served.

The second curtain caught, and the light in the kitchen doubled, and the shadows on the walls leaned in.

Elodie picked the hymnal back up. There was more. Beneath the four lines she'd just read, in smaller script, cramped against the margin like a footnote the composer had been afraid to write large:

*If you are reading this, the keeping has passed to you. I am sorry. I could not carry it further. Do not sing it with others. Do not sing it for others' hearing. Sing it alone, at the threshold, and mean every word, and do not stop between verses, and when it asks you to harmonize—*

The line ended there. Not torn away. Just ended, the pen lifting, the rest either never written or written somewhere she hadn't found yet.

*When it asks you to harmonize.* Her skin prickled. Because that was already happening. For days the dead choir had been singing verses one through three at her, around her, *at* her, perfect and patient, leaving a gap in the harmony shaped exactly like a fourth voice. She had thought it was menace. It was worse than menace—it was negotiation. The thing was offering her a place in the chord. And the moment she accepted, the moment she blended, the lock would swing wide and stay wide, and the town would learn what thirty-five years of appetite looked like with the lid entirely off.

The burning curtain slumped, half-consumed, and Elodie fed it another foot of fabric, and in the fresh flare of light she saw something on the page she hadn't seen before—a watermark, almost, a ghost of ink beneath the ink. She tilted the hymnal toward the flames.

Words, fainter than faint, in the same trembling copperplate, running vertically down the margin beside the fourth verse. She had to turn the book sideways to read them, and even then they swam, and she had to whisper them aloud to pin them in place:

*"the chair is not for it. the chair is for the keeper."*

Elodie's eyes went, before she could stop them, to the kitchen table. To the extra chair. Pulled out. Waiting. All those photographs, decade after decade, the chair drawing closer to the camera, closer to the house, closer to her—not a seat being saved for the entity at all.

A seat being saved for whoever agreed to sit in it. For the keeper. For the one who stood inside the door.

Her grandmother had lived alone in this house for twenty years with a table set for three.

"Oh," Elodie said. It came out very quiet. "Oh, Grandma."

Fifteen years ago, her grandmother had lost her voice. Elodie had always believed the thing had *taken* it—stolen it as punishment, as theft. Standing in the burning-curtain light with the hymnal shaking gently in her hands, she understood it differently. Her grandmother had *given* it. Had sung the binding, alone, at some threshold, sometime in the years before Elodie was born—and the price of the keeping was the voice itself, poured into the lock, spent like coin. Every word of the hymn she would never again speak. That was why she couldn't warn Elodie plainly. That was why she pointed at the attic for fifteen years and never said a word: she had literally nothing left to say it with. The silence hadn't been a wound. It had been a payment, still being paid.

And now the keeping had passed to Elodie, whether she wanted it or not, because locks don't care about wanting. The choir had crossed the property line. The moon was rising tonight. The director wanted the door open. Marcus had already fed it a hum he didn't know was a deposit. The choice wasn't whether the hymn would be sung—the choice was whether it would be sung *right*.

*Do not stop between verses,* the margin said. *Mean every word.*

Elodie looked down at the fourth verse, fully legible now in the firelight, and read it through once more, slowly, letting each line land and stay:

*What was given must be gathered,*
*what was fed must be confined.*
*Sing it single, sing it sole,*
*one voice for the whole design:*
*the door swings wide for many,*
*but shuts for one who stands inside.*

Hope with teeth. That was what this was. Not rescue—custody. Not freedom—tenancy. Somewhere in the margins of her mind, the plan was already assembling itself, the way plans do when there's no time left to build them properly: the station's remote transmitter in her car, the farmhouse phone line, the overnight slot that had belonged to her for six years and had become, without anyone noticing, a pulpit. If the lock needed a single voice at a threshold, then she would make the whole county her threshold. She would sing it on air. Alone. No callers, no harmony, no accompaniment. Every verse, straight through, no stopping.

And afterward—

Afterward there would be a chair at her table for the rest of her life. She was almost certain of it now. Certain enough that grief arrived early, quietly, and sat down beside the fear like an old friend.

The last of the first curtain burned through and dropped into the sink in a fan of orange lace. Elodie took down the second curtain, the one from the window over the sink, the one Grandma had sewn the blue birds onto, and struck the candle to it, and the birds caught one by one, wing after small wing, going up as light.

That was when the radio in the parlor switched itself on.

Not static. Music—or near music. The dead choir, verses one through three, in perfect harmony, coming through the speaker tinny and patient, filling the house the way water fills a glass. And underneath the harmony, so low she almost missed it, a new sound: a fourth part, hummed, familiar, male. Marcus's hum, the one he thought he'd stopped in time, replayed and replayed, woven into the chord, a placeholder where her voice was meant to go.

They were rehearsing her seat.

Elodie closed the hymnal and held it against her chest and stood in the burning-bird light, and she found, to her surprise, that she wasn't afraid anymore—or rather, that the fear had changed shape, had condensed from a fog into a stone she could carry.

"Not tonight," she told the radio.

The choir hummed on, sweet and terrible, counting down the hours until moonrise.

She had nineteen hours to learn every word by heart.
