## 21. After the Song

The kettle was the first thing Marcus learned to listen for.

Not the radio, not the hymn, not the dead choir that had stood at the property line like a congregation waiting on a late preacher. The kettle. Because in the weeks after the harvest moon, after the night his sister sang four verses alone into an open microphone while the farmhouse screamed around her like a living thing being unmade, Elodie had developed a habit of letting the kettle boil dry. She'd set it on the burner, walk into the other room to do something—find a pen, answer a call she'd already answered—and then she'd stop. Mid-step. Mid-sentence sometimes. And she'd stand there with her head tilted, listening to something just off-frequency, the way a dog listens to a whistle no one else can hear, and the kettle would scream itself empty on the stove behind her.

He'd replaced two kettles already.

He was replacing the third when he finally said something about it.

"You're doing it again."

Elodie was at the kitchen table of her apartment—the small one over the laundromat in town, not the farmhouse; she hadn't slept at the farmhouse since that night, though she went back every day to broadcast from it, which Marcus thought was either the bravest thing he'd ever witnessed or the most quietly insane, and he'd stopped trying to sort out which. She was doing the show prep, papers spread out, a mug of tea gone cold beside her elbow because the kettle had boiled dry twenty minutes ago and she hadn't noticed. Or hadn't let herself notice.

"Doing what?" she said, without looking up.

"The thing." He turned the tap on the scorched kettle, testing it, though he already knew it was done. "The listening thing."

"I'm not listening to anything, Marcus."

"I know," he said. "That's what worries me."

She looked up then. And here was the thing he couldn't get past, the thing he turned over at three in the morning when sleep wouldn't take him: she looked fine. Better than fine. The gray that had lived under her eyes through September and October was gone. Her voice on air had come back round and warm, the voice that made insomniacs across three counties feel less alone. Whatever had poured into her through that open channel, whatever covenant she'd taken entire, it hadn't hollowed her out the way it had hollowed out the choir director, who hadn't aged a day since 1989 and couldn't remember his daughter's face. It had filled her up instead. That was the part that scared him. Damage you could see. You could grieve damage. This was something else.

"Callers are living," she said, and there was almost a smile in it. "That's what you wanted, isn't it? Nobody's dedicating songs to themselves anymore. Last night a man called about raccoons in his chimney. Raccoons, Marcus. I nearly cried."

"That's good. That's—" He set the ruined kettle down. "That's genuinely good, El."

"But?"

"There's no but."

"There's always a but with you lately."

Because I hid the journal pages from you for fifteen years, he thought. Because I hummed four bars of that melody in an empty house and the house kept it, kept *me*, and I didn't tell you until it started spending me word by word. Because I know exactly what it costs to carry a secret about that house, and I can see you carrying one now, and you're better at it than I ever was.

"No but," he said.

* * *

He started keeping notes. Not because he distrusted her—because he'd learned, too late and at a price still accruing, that the things you don't write down are the things that rewrite you.

*Nov 12.* Phone call at the station. Elodie mid-sentence about the weather—"clear skies through Thursday, which is good news for the"—and she stopped. Four seconds. He timed it. Head tilted left, eyes fixed on nothing, on the middle distance where nobody was standing. Then: "—harvest, so get your covers on your tender plants." Smooth as glass. If you weren't watching for it, you'd never know. But the caller, old Tom Vess from the feed store, heard it too. He said afterward, "Your sister ever think about getting her ears checked?" Like it was a joke. Like it was funny.

*Nov 19.* Dinner at the diner. She paused twice during the meal. Both times looking slightly above my shoulder. Both times resuming as if nothing happened. When I asked what she was looking at she said "the pie case" and the pie case was behind me.

*Nov 26.* The shadow.

He wrote that one down and then sat with the notebook open for a long time before writing anything under it.

It happened at the farmhouse, during the Sunday afternoon broadcast. Marcus had come along ostensibly to fix the porch step, actually because he couldn't stand the idea of her in that house alone, even now, even after. The studio setup was in the parlor, the old radio console patched into a transmitter rig that crackled like a fire most nights. Elodie was on air, doing the request hour, and she got up from the table to pull a record from the crate by the window—a listener had asked for Patsy Cline, some song about walking after midnight—and Marcus, glancing up from the porch step he wasn't really fixing, saw it happen.

Her shadow lay long across the floorboards in the low afternoon light, stretched toward the kitchen doorway. And as Elodie crossed the room, the shadow did not cross with her. It stayed. It settled. It arranged itself against the leg of the kitchen table the way a person settles into a chair, and it sat there, patient and dark, while Elodie walked to the window and back without one.

When she returned to the table, the shadow rose to meet her, folded back into her heels, and became hers again so smoothly that if Marcus had blinked at the wrong moment he'd have missed it entirely.

He didn't say anything. He drove home and wrote it down and hated himself for not saying anything, and then he remembered that he'd once hidden torn journal pages in a lockbox for fifteen years to protect her from exactly this kind of knowledge, and protection, he understood now, was just fear wearing a kinder coat.

*Dec 3.* She set the table for two tonight. Then stood there a long moment and got a third plate down. Didn't explain it. I didn't ask. We both knew who it was for. We both pretended we didn't.

* * *

The callers were living. That was true, and he held onto it like a rail in dark water.

He'd listened to enough of the show now to know the texture of it, how it had changed. Before—before Ruth's voice came through the board asking for a dedication, before the humming, before everything—the show had been a thin warm thing, a nightlight for people who couldn't sleep. Now it was quieter in a way that was hard to describe. Elodie talked less and somehow said more. She let silences run longer than any program director would allow, and the silences didn't feel dead anymore. They felt *attended*. Listeners wrote in—not called, wrote, as if calling felt suddenly presumptuous—saying the show helped them sleep for the first time in years. Saying it felt like someone sitting with them. One woman from Harlow Falls wrote: *it's like the quiet is holding my hand.*

Marcus read that letter over Elodie's shoulder and felt the hair rise on his arms, because he knew what it cost to make a silence feel like that. He knew what had moved into the space between the songs.

"It's good work," he said carefully.

"It is good work." She was sorting the mail, and she didn't look up, and her voice had that evenness she got when she was choosing not to hear a question inside a statement.

"How much of it is you?"

That got her. Her hands stopped on the envelope she was holding. Outside the diner window a truck went past, throwing early dusk across the table, and in the half-second of moving shadow Marcus watched her face do something complicated and then put itself away.

"All of it," she said. "Every word. That was the whole point, Marcus. I took it so it would be mine. All of it."

"I know. I'm not—"

"You're asking if I'm still in here." Now she looked at him, and her eyes were clear, and that was somehow worse than if they hadn't been. "I'm in here. I'm all in here. It doesn't talk over me. It doesn't talk *through* me. It just—" She searched for the word, and he watched her search, and he saw the moment she found it and decided whether to give it to him.

"It just listens," she said. "Patiently. The way Grandma used to listen. You remember how she'd sit at the table while you talked and never interrupt, never look away, like whatever you were saying was the only thing happening in the world?"

"Yeah."

"It's like that. Except it never leaves the table."

* * *

He went to the farmhouse alone on a Tuesday in December, telling himself it was to winterize the pipes, knowing it was to stand in the rooms where it had happened and try to feel what she must feel.

The house was different. He'd expected that, or told himself he had. But knowing a thing and standing inside it were different countries. The burn scar on the doorframe—the mouth-shaped scar he'd found in October, the one shaped like a mouth that might have been screaming or singing—had faded. Not healed. Faded, the way a scar fades, going silver and smooth, becoming the kind of mark you'd stop seeing after a while. The walls didn't swell anymore. The attic was just an attic, cold and full of mouse sign and the smell of old cedar, the composer's hymnal gone because Elodie kept it now, locked in a box he pretended not to know about.

In the kitchen, the table stood bare. There were two chairs.

He counted them twice. Two chairs, pushed in neat, the way you leave a house you mean to come back to. And on the floor beside the table, swept into the corner like it was nothing, like it was kindling: splinters. Pale wood, old-growth pale, and among them a curved piece of stretcher with a worn spot on it where a heel had rested, decade after decade, generation after generation, until the wood knew the shape of waiting.

The extra chair. The chair that had crept closer to the camera in every photograph for a hundred years. The chair that had been pulled out and warm at three in the morning. The chair that had splintered the night his sister refused harmony and took the covenant entire.

She'd swept it up. She hadn't thrown it away.

Marcus crouched there a long time with the splinters in his cupped hands, and he understood, in the way you understand things at the bottom of a well, that this was the shape of the rest of it. Not exorcism. Not victory, not clean. A binding, yes—the thing caged, the town freed, the dead released to bow and go—but a binding meant a cage, and a cage meant a keeper, and the keeper set her own table for two and then reached for a third plate without explaining why.

He put the splinters back in the corner. It seemed important not to disturb them. It seemed important, suddenly and irrationally, to be polite in this house.

On his way out, in the parlor, he passed the old radio console, switched off, its dial dark. As he reached the door he heard—faintly, from the direction of the kitchen, or from nowhere, from the frequency just off-frequency that his sister tilted her head toward—a single note. Held. Unhurried. The first note of the hymn, hummed soft as a lullaby, the way you'd hum it to a child falling asleep.

Then it stopped, politely, as if remembering itself.

Marcus stood in the doorway with his hand on the cold latch, and he thought: *she's not haunted. She's tenanted.* And he thought: *there's a difference, and the difference is consent, and she gave it, and I have to live with that.*

He closed the door gently behind him, the way you close a door on a sleeping room.

And in his coat pocket, his hand found the notebook, and he realized he already knew what tonight's entry would be, because he'd been carrying the sentence since the kitchen:

*Dec 9. The chair is gone but the place setting isn't. She's not hosting it. She's keeping it. And I think—I think it's learning her manners.*
