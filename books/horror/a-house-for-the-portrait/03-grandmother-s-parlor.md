## 3. Grandmother's Parlor

The parlor smelled the way it had always smelled, which was impossible, because no house could keep a scent for eleven months — lavender and old paper and something underneath both, faintly mineral, like the inside of a stone. Jude stood in the doorway with a box labeled PARLOR in their own handwriting and let the smell do what smells do, which is to say: it reached into the past and pulled.

Grandma had died in March, on a Tuesday, between one thing and another, and the house on Vane Lane had been closed since the funeral — sheets over the furniture, keys with the solicitor, a town that had nodded at Jude kindly and gone back to its business. Now it was the last week of August, and the sheets were off, and the boxes were here, and in nine days Jude would stand in front of twenty-six Year Six pupils and pretend to be a person whose life had not stopped the day the phone rang.

They set the box down on the parlor floor and knelt beside it.

The packing tape gave with a sound like a small sigh. Inside, wrapped in newspaper from a town Jude had lived in for a decade, were the things Grandma had loved: a brass lamp with a green glass shade. A clock that had never once told the right time. A doily Jude remembered crocheting at the kitchen table, tongue between her teeth, telling Jude that idle hands were the devil's typists. And beneath all of it, wrapped separately, in a pillowcase tied with string — the portrait.

Jude had forgotten it was here. That was the first lie of the evening, and they would tell themselves versions of it for weeks: *forgot*, as though a person could forget the only painting in a house they'd grown up in, the one that had hung above the fireplace through every Christmas and every scolding and every slow Sunday afternoon. They had not forgotten it. They had simply, in the way of the grieving, filed it somewhere the mind does not look.

They untied the string. The pillowcase fell open like a shed skin.

The woman in the portrait looked back at Jude with the same expression she had always worn: stern, unimpressed, patient in the way that stone is patient. She was painted in a style that was almost good — competent hands, a little stiff, the colors gone slightly amber with age — standing in the interior of a house that was not quite this house and not quite any house. Dark wainscoting. A doorway behind her left shoulder, open onto darkness. A window with grey light coming through it, though there was no weather visible outside the glass, only a flat, sourceless pallor, the light of a day that had never happened.

And beside her, to the right of the frame, at the edge of a rug rendered in faded reds: a single wooden chair.

Empty. Always empty. Jude had asked about it once, years ago, sitting cross-legged on this very floor while Grandma dusted the frame. *Who's the chair for?* And Grandma had gone still — not long, only a moment, the duster paused mid-arc — and said, in a voice that was perfectly level and perfectly wrong, *No one, love. It's just a chair.* Then she had finished the dusting faster than usual and made Jude toast with too much butter, which was how she said things she couldn't say.

Jude turned the portrait over. The backing was old board, water-stained at one corner. A label, yellowed and curling, read in faded ink: no artist, no date, only a single word in someone's careful hand — *KEPT*.

Not *owned*. Not a name. *Kept*, the way one keeps a promise, or a vigil, or a thing that would leave if not held.

"Well," Jude said aloud, to the empty room, because the silence had begun to press. "Back you go."

They hung it above the fireplace, where it had always been. The nail was still there, waiting, as if the house had kept the place warm.

* * *

Unpacking Grandma's belongings was an archaeology of a life, and Jude had not prepared for how much of it would be an archaeology of them.

The parlor gave way to the front room, the front room to the hallway, and the hallway to the stairs, and by ten o'clock the house was full of opened boxes and half-remembered objects: the tin of buttons. The photograph albums with their cracked spines. A shoebox of birthday cards spanning thirty years, every one of them signed *All my love, Gran* in handwriting that slanted forward like someone leaning into a wind only she could feel.

Jude worked until the boxes were empty and the grief they'd been carrying since March had nowhere left to hide. This had been the plan, they admitted to themselves now, standing in the hallway with dust on their sleeves — the whole plan, the teaching job, the rented house, the move to a town they'd only ever visited: not a fresh start but a hiding place. Grief was a thing you could outrun if you kept moving, and Blackfen was as good a place to run as any. It had the added advantage of being the last place Grandma had been herself, and Jude had told themselves that was sentiment. It was, they understood now, closer to surrender. They had come here to be near the shape she'd left behind.

They made tea they didn't drink. They washed the one mug they'd used. They went up to the back bedroom — theirs now, the small one, the one that had been the sewing room — and lay down on a mattress on the floor because the bed frame was still in pieces in the car, and told themselves firmly that tomorrow they would assemble the bed, and hang curtains, and be a person with a life.

The house settled around them the way old houses do: ticks and murmurs, the plumbing clearing its throat, the walls contracting as the night cooled. Jude catalogued the sounds the way they'd learned to in every new place they'd ever slept — the skill of the perpetually displaced — and was nearly asleep when the sound started.

Above them. The front bedroom. Grandma's room.

A footstep. Then another. Slow, even, unhurried — the pace of someone pacing a small room, heel to toe, turn, heel to toe. Not creaking, exactly. Creaking has a randomness to it, the language of wood under weather. This was rhythmic. Deliberate. *Patient.*

Jude lay still and listened.

It went on for perhaps a minute — long enough for Jude's heart to move from *asleep* to *awake* and keep moving — and then it stopped. Not faded. Stopped, the way a person stops when they hear you listening.

The boiler, Jude thought. The boiler is under the stairs, and the pipes run up through the wall, and thermal expansion makes all kinds of noises, and I read about this, everyone reads about this, houses tick at night.

Except the boiler, Jude remembered, had been serviced in June, per the solicitor's letter, and it was August, and the night was mild, and no boiler in the history of boilers had ever paced.

They got up anyway. This was the other thing grief had taught them: that lying in the dark inventing horrors was worse than getting up and finding the ordinary thing. They crossed the landing in their socks and stood at the door of the front bedroom, hand on the handle, and made themselves breathe.

The room was cold. Not dramatically cold — not movie cold — just a degree or two below the landing, the way a room is when the window's been left open a crack. Except the window was shut. Jude checked. Latched. The curtains hung dead still.

The room was exactly as they'd left it: boxes stacked against the wall, the wardrobe with its mirror clouded at one corner, the bed Grandma had died three towns away from, made up with sheets that still smelled faintly of the lavender from the parlor. Nothing moved. Nothing had moved. The floorboards lay in their long grey rows, scuffed pale down the center by sixty years of the same small walk — Grandma's walk, Jude thought, and had to put a hand on the doorframe.

The floorboards were worn in a path. A narrow, wandering path, from the bed to the window and back. The path of someone who had paced this room for years.

*She lived alone for eleven years,* Jude told themselves. *Of course she paced. Of course the boards are worn. That's what loneliness looks like from underneath.*

It was a good explanation. It was even true. It explained the boards and it did not explain the footsteps, and Jude stood in the doorway holding both facts, one in each hand, and chose — consciously, deliberately, the way you choose which of two doors to walk through — to put the second one down.

"Boiler," they said aloud. Their voice sounded thin in the cold room. "Thermal expansion. Go to sleep."

They went back to the mattress on the floor. They pulled the duvet to their chin. Above them, after a while — ten minutes, twenty, they couldn't have said — the footsteps started again. Slower now. Almost gentle. Heel to toe, turn, heel to toe, the rhythm of someone keeping a lonely vigil in a room where no one had slept for months.

Jude stared at the ceiling and did not go up again. They told themselves it was the boiler. They told themselves it was the house settling, old wood remembering old weight. They told themselves that grief made the mind unreliable, that this was a known phenomenon, that the newly bereaved heard things — and all of it was true, and all of it was beside the point, and somewhere around two in the morning they finally slept, and the pacing stopped the moment their breathing changed, which was a fact they did not know and therefore did not have to explain.

* * *

Morning arrived grey and apologetic, the way it seemed to in Blackfen — light coming through the curtains like it wasn't sure it was wanted. Jude made coffee in the kitchen, standing where Grandma had stood for forty years, and looked out at the back garden: an overgrown rectangle of grass, a stone birdbath dry as bone, a fence with a gap where the neighbor children used to crawl through. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and stopped, as if corrected.

The house in daylight was only a house. The cold room was only a room. The footsteps were — Jude had decided over the instant coffee and was prepared to defend the position — the boiler, or the wood, or the wind, or grief, in ascending order of implausibility, and today there was work to do. Nine days until term. Boxes to unpack. A classroom to find, a register to read, a headmaster named Hesse to impress. A new life to assemble out of the wreckage of the old one, plank by plank, in a town where everyone was polite and no one was warm.

They spent the morning on the front room, unwrapping the photographs.

This had been Grandma's other life: the albums, the shoeboxes, the envelopes from the developer with their curling Kodak borders. Jude had offered to digitize them once, years ago, and Grandma had said no — quickly, more quickly than the request deserved — *some things you don't put in a machine, love.* Jude had thought it was sentiment. Everything about Grandma that didn't fit had been filed under sentiment, because the alternative was questions, and questions had a way of stopping conversations in this family mid-word.

The photographs were ordinary. That was what made them unbearable. Grandma at the seaside, squinting. Grandma in the garden with the birdbath still wet and working. Grandma holding a baby Jude — the only photograph in which Jude appeared to be enjoying themselves. Jude sat on the floor in the grey light and went through them one by one, and wept a little, in the undramatic way, and let the house be quiet around them.

It was late afternoon when they found the first one.

It was a picture of the parlor. Grandma had taken it — Jude remembered the day, a Sunday in autumn, Grandma with the camera saying *sit still, you're in the shot* — and there was Jude on the sofa, maybe nine years old, mid-laugh, and above the fireplace, the portrait, and beside the portrait, at the edge of the frame, the wooden chair.

Empty. Of course empty. It was always empty. That was the thing about the chair; that was the whole of it; the chair was empty in the painting and empty in the photograph because a chair with no one in it is just furniture, and furniture is not frightening, and Jude sat on the floor of the front room looking at a photograph taken twenty-five years ago and felt the cold come up through the floorboards into their spine.

Because they had asked about the chair. They had asked, and Grandma had said *no one, love, it's just a chair* — and the chair in the photograph was not the chair in the painting. The painting's chair was plain, dark wood, high-backed. The chair in the photograph was the same chair, yes, but it stood at a different angle, closer to the woman, as though in the years between the painting and the photograph it had moved — or as though there had always been two chairs and only one of them was ever mentioned.

And the photograph had been taken in this house, in this parlor, on a Sunday in autumn. The chair in the photograph was not part of the painting. It was in the *room*.

Jude looked up, across the hallway, through the open parlor door, at the wall above the fireplace.

The portrait hung there, small with distance, the woman stern and patient, and beside her — they could see it even from here, even across the hall, even in the failing light — the chair. In the painting. Where it had always been. Where they had hung it last night without looking closely, without counting, without asking the only question that mattered, which was the question they had asked at nine years old and been answered with toast and a closed door:

Who is the chair for?

Jude got up. Their legs felt like borrowed legs. They walked down the hallway and stood in the parlor doorway, and the light was going blue and grey with the evening, and the house was silent in the way a house is silent when it is listening back.

They looked at the painting. At the woman. At the chair.

The chair was closer to her than it had been last night.

It had moved the width of a hand. Perhaps less. Perhaps — Jude made themselves look, made themselves measure it against the rug's faded border, against the woman's painted skirt — perhaps the width of a hand exactly, the distance a small person might scoot a chair across a floor when no one was watching, inch by inch, night by night, patient as stone, toward the seat that had been kept for them.

Behind Jude, at the top of the stairs, a floorboard shifted.

Heel to toe. Turn. Heel to toe.

Jude did not turn around. They stood in the doorway of the parlor with the portrait in front of them and the sound above them, and they said, in a voice they were proud of for not shaking:

"Boiler."

The footsteps paused — the way a person pauses when they hear you listening — and then, gently, almost kindly, they resumed.
