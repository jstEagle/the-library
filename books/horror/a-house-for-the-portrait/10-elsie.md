## 10. Elsie

Jude fell asleep with the lamp still on and the parlor door open exactly as far as it had been when they'd checked it — three inches, no more — and dreamed of standing inside a painting.

There was no transition. One moment the dark behind their eyelids, and then the room: grandma's parlor rendered in oils, every surface carrying that particular flat sheen of varnish, the wallpaper's pattern repeating with the mechanical patience of brushstrokes. The light came from nowhere and everywhere, the way light does in portraits — a permanent late afternoon that had been late afternoon for fifty years.

Jude knew where they were before they understood how impossible it was. They were inside the frame. The portrait hung on a wall somewhere beyond this room's walls, and they were on the other side of the canvas, looking out through the painted window at a garden that was only suggestion — green smears, a fence implied in two strokes of grey.

"Excuse me."

The voice was small and polite and came from the floor.

A girl sat against the baseboard beneath the painted window, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them. She wore a coat the color of wet slate, buttoned to the throat despite the eternal warmth of the painted afternoon, and her shoes were scuffed leather, one lace trailing. Her hair was dark and cut unevenly, as if by an impatient hand. She looked perhaps ten years old. She looked, Jude thought with a lurch that had nothing to do with fear, like every school photograph they'd ever taken attendance from — the universal child, the one who sits in the back row of every class in every decade.

Except her lips were grey. Not pale. Grey, like something drained of its last warm color, and Jude knew — the way you know things in dreams, without being told — that this was not a stylistic choice of some long-dead painter.

"You're sitting on the floor," Jude said. Their own voice sounded strange here, too loud, too present, like shouting in a church.

"It's not my chair yet." The girl said it simply, without resentment, the way a child reports a rule of a game. "I'm not allowed until someone says I can have it."

Jude looked at the chair. It stood near the cold fireplace, the empty wooden chair from the photographs, and up close Jude could see it was not empty in the way furniture is empty. It was *waiting* empty. The way a seat waits at a table set for someone late.

"That's ridiculous," Jude heard themselves say. "It's just a chair."

The girl looked up at them, and her eyes were the worst part — not hollow, not burning, nothing cinematic at all. Just tired. A child's eyes at the end of a very long day that had lasted half a century.

"It's not just anything," she said. "Nothing here is just anything. That's rather the whole problem."

* * *

Jude found they could sit down. The floor accepted them, varnish-smooth and faintly warm, and they settled cross-legged across from the girl with the strange formality of dream-logic, as if they'd arranged the meeting themselves.

"What's your name?" they asked, though some part of them already knew, had known since the register ran long, since the photographs grew furniture, since Corla Hesse's face went the color of old paper when she said *mark the child absent and move on.*

"Elsie," the girl said. "Elsie Vane." And then, because children are trained in politeness even unto death: "What's yours?"

"Pemberton," Jude said, and watched the name land.

Elsie didn't flinch. But something moved behind her eyes — a shutter drawn halfway, a calculation made and hidden. "Pemberton," she repeated, tasting it. "You're one of theirs, then."

"I'm—" Jude started to deny it and couldn't finish the sentence. "My grandmother lived here. On Vane Lane. This was her house."

"This was always going to be somebody's house," Elsie said. "That's what they told each other, I expect. That the house needed a family in it, so it couldn't be helped." She picked at the trailing shoelace, winding it around one finger, unwinding it. "It wasn't a house yet when I knew it. It was the other part of the school. The mistress's part. Mrs. Odell kept her office downstairs and we had lessons upstairs, and there were stairs between, and—" She stopped winding the lace. Her finger had gone white where the thread bit into it. "You know about the stairs, don't you. You've got the look people get."

"I don't know anything," Jude said. "That's why I'm here. I don't know anything and everyone in this town keeps telling me not to find out."

"Mm." Elsie considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "They told me that too, once. About other things. Some things you're not supposed to ask after." She unfolded slightly, one knee dropping, and Jude saw that under the slate coat her hands were small and chapped, a working child's hands, a 1974 child's hands. "But you asked me my name. Nobody's asked me my name in ever such a long time. They ask the house things. They look at the pictures and count the chairs and get frightened. But nobody asks *me.*"

"I'm asking," Jude said. Their throat had gone tight. "Elsie Vane. What happened?"

* * *

And here the dream did what dreams do — it refused the direct answer and gave the room instead.

The parlor shifted around them. The varnish-light deepened toward evening that never quite arrived. The chair remained where it was, patient as a held breath, and Elsie remained on the floor, and she began to talk the way children talk when they've been holding a story so long it has grown smooth inside them, worn round like a river stone from turning over and over.

"There were four families," she said. "Everyone said it like a blessing. Hesse, Odell, Crane, Pemberton. Like the school was a table and those were the legs. My mum cleaned for the Odells, and she said if you worked for one of the four you worked for all of them, because they were really just one family wearing four coats." She smiled briefly, and for a moment the grey left her lips and she was unbearably, ordinarily alive. "She said it funny, though. She liked Mrs. Odell. Everyone did. That was the trouble with her."

"The trouble with her," Jude repeated carefully.

"She was kind," Elsie said, as if this explained everything, and perhaps it did. "Kind people don't want to believe bad things about their friends. So when the bad thing came, she looked for a smaller word for it. Everyone did. Smaller and smaller words until the word was gone altogether and then I was gone, because I *was* the word by then." She said it without self-pity, which was somehow more terrible than weeping would have been. "Do you understand? I'm not haunting anybody. I'm just still in the sentence. They never finished it."

Jude's hands had gone cold in their lap. "What sentence? What happened on the stairs, Elsie?"

The girl looked at them for a long moment. Outside the painted window, the suggested garden darkened by a shade, though the sun did not move.

"I can't tell it yet," she said finally. "Not all of it. It has to be heard in the right room, by the right people, or it doesn't count. That's another rule." She almost laughed. "This place is *full* of rules. You'd think a dead girl would get a bit of slack."

"Then tell me what you can."

"I can tell you I wasn't supposed to be upstairs that day." She held up fingers, counting off confessions like a child reciting times tables. "I can tell you I was running, and the banister was polished, and I was wearing my good shoes which had no grip. I can tell you it was quick, mostly, and that the worst part wasn't the falling. The worst part was after, when the four families had their meeting, and Mr. Crane said—" her voice went flat, an impression, a boy doing an adult's voice in a playground game— "*the charter won't survive a coroner's question.* And they all nodded. Even the ones crying. Especially the ones crying."

Jude felt the floor tilt beneath them, or perhaps felt themselves tilt; in the dream it amounted to the same thing. "Your own death was a budget line."

"My everything was a budget line," Elsie agreed. "Mum got money and a train ticket and a story to tell the kin we didn't have. The school got its charter. The town got its quiet. And I got the chair." She glanced at it. "Well. Near the chair."

"Why near it? Why not in it?"

"Because nobody's given it to me." She said it patiently, as if explaining long division to a slow pupil. "Sitting in it isn't mine to take. Someone has to *give* it. Out loud. Properly. With my name attached." She looked down at her chapped hands. "Names are the whole currency here, Miss Pemberton. Sir. Sorry — I can't tell which you are, you're both somehow. Names are what they took, so names are what's owed. Everything else follows from that. The chairs in your photographs. The register running long. All of it is just me, trying to get back onto a list."

* * *

"You're in my grandmother's house," Jude said. "You've been in her house for fifty years. Did she know?"

Something crossed Elsie's face then — the first expression that wasn't patience or politeness. It was older and sadder and it made her look briefly not ten but timeless, a grief with a child's outline drawn around it.

"Your gran," she said, "was the only one who ever spoke to me. Did you know that? Out of all of them, all these years. She'd sit in that chair—" a nod at the empty one— "no, not that one, the reading one, by the window, and she'd talk to the room. Never said my name. I think she was frightened of saying it, in case saying it made things start. But she'd say *I know you're there, love, and I'm sorry, and I should have said something in '74,* over and over, like a prayer she couldn't finish." Elsie's chin trembled, just once, and stilled. "Sorry is a good word but it's not a name. It kept me company. It didn't let me in."

Jude thought of the sewing box they hadn't opened yet — hadn't even found, though some instinct now told them it existed, locked, waiting in a wardrobe or an attic, holding whatever a guilty woman couldn't throw away. The dream pressed the knowledge into them like a thumbprint in wax.

"She left things," Jude said. "Didn't she. Papers. Proof."

"I expect so." Elsie resumed winding the shoelace. "People who carry a secret that heavy usually put it down somewhere eventually. It's the putting-down that matters. Carrying it does nobody any good — not the carrier, not the carried." She looked up, and her grey-lipped mouth curved into something that was trying to be reassuring and nearly managed it. "You'll find them. You're a finder. I could tell the moment you came into the house. You've got teacher's eyes. You look at a room and count what's missing."

"The register runs long," Jude said. "Twenty-six names, twenty-four desks. Is that you? Are you the extra names?"

"Oh, I'm not clever enough to be two children." For the first time, Elsie laughed — a real laugh, bright and startling in the varnished dusk, and Jude's chest ached with how alive it sounded, how utterly wasted on this sealed room. "No. The register's honest, that's the joke of it. It lists everyone who belongs and hasn't been counted. There've been others. Before me, after me. Little gaps in the record, little silences, and silence pools, Miss-Sir-Pemberton. Silence gathers. Every few decades it gets heavy enough that the house has to try again." She shrugged inside the slate coat. "I'm just the one who's been here longest. The senior girl, you might say. The others come and go — they rest easier than me, most of them. Somebody spoke a piece of their name back eventually, a christening record, a letter, a memory. Enough to sit down somewhere softer than a floor." Her gaze went to the chair again, and stayed. "Nobody's ever spoken mine entire."

"Tell me the whole of it," Jude said. "Tell me your name, all of it, dates, everything, and I'll—"

"No." And the word came out with such sudden force that the painted room shuddered — the wallpaper pattern stuttered, the fireplace flickered like a film catching, and for one vertiginous second Jude saw through the walls of the dream into the actual parlor above which they slept, the actual chair, the actual portrait hanging with its painted woman and its painted emptiness, and the actual girl-shaped absence seated on its actual floorboards. Then the vision snapped shut and Elsie was ten years old again, apologetic, hands folded. "Sorry. It doesn't work like that. If you say it wrong, or in the wrong place, or before you've earned it, it just — slides off. Like water on varnish." She tapped the floorboards beneath her. "Everything here is varnish until it's true. Then it's wood."

"How do I earn it?"

"You're doing it," she said simply. "You came back. You keep coming back. You asked my name instead of running." She tilted her head, studying Jude with unnerving directness. "But I'll warn you, because you've been decent to me and decent deserves warning: earning it will cost. It always costs. They took my name to save their school, and to give it back you'll have to spend something of yours. That's not cruelty. That's just arithmetic. The house is very fond of arithmetic."

"What will it cost?"

"I don't set the prices." Elsie spread her chapped hands. "I only live in the shop."

* * *

The light was changing. The eternal late afternoon had begun, at last, to fail — shadows pooling in the corners of the painted room like ink dropped in water, and Jude felt the dream loosening its grip, felt the weight of their actual body calling them back up through the ceiling, through the floor, into the bed where they lay breathing hard in the dark.

"Wait," Jude said. "Elsie — wait. What do you want? Not what's owed. What do *you* want?"

The girl considered this longer than any child should have to consider a question so simple. When she answered, her voice had gone very quiet, and very clear.

"To be let back in," she said. "That's all. Everyone thinks hauntings are about anger, and some are, I suppose, but mine isn't. I want to come home. This was my school. Those were my stairs. That chair was bought for the reading corner and I loved it before I ever fell past it." Her grey lips pressed together. "I want someone to open the door and say *Elsie Vane, you live here, you belong here, come in and sit down.* Fifty years I've been knocking politely on the outside of my own life. I am *so tired* of being polite."

The room was nearly dark now. The chair had become a pale shape, and the girl a paler one, and Jude felt themselves rising, dissolving upward—

"One more thing," Elsie's voice followed them, thinning with distance. "When you wake, look at your hand. And then look under your bed. And then, Miss-Sir-Pemberton — look in your grandmother's sewing box. She kept her apology somewhere she could touch it. People do."

* * *

Jude woke at 4:07 a.m. with the lamp burned out and the parlor door standing open — six inches now, wider than they'd left it, wide enough to show a slice of moonlit wall and the gilt edge of the portrait's frame.

Their right hand was closed in a fist they didn't remember making. They opened it slowly.

In the palm, gleaming dully in the light from the hall, lay a small crescent of grit — floorboard dust, the fine pale debris of old boards rubbed by small feet pacing, or sitting, or waiting. It was warm. Warmer than the room. Warm as a hand that had recently let go of theirs.

Jude sat up. The house was silent in the way that isn't silence — the loaded, listening quiet of a room with someone in it. Downstairs, or possibly inside the wall, or possibly inside the painting, something shifted its weight and settled.

They got out of bed, knelt, and looked underneath.

Dust, and a lost pen, and — pushed far back against the wall, where no draft could have carried it — a single child's shoe. Scuffed leather. Slate-grey. Small.

The matching shoe, Jude understood with a calm that frightened them more than screaming would have, was downstairs in a locked sewing box they had never seen but now, unmistakably, knew the location of: top shelf of grandma's wardrobe, behind the hatboxes, under a folded cardigan that still smelled of her.

They sat on the edge of the bed until the windows went grey, the grit still cupped in their palm like something entrusted.

Then they went downstairs to find it.
