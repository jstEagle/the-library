## 6. What Was Invited

The door did not slam. That was the first thing Beatrix noticed, and the last thing she would ever stop noticing: it closed the way a hand closes over a moth — gently, with terrible care — and then there was no handle on the inside.

She had come in behind them because Wilf Corrie's ghost of a voice was still in her ear — *the bell only rings when the parish owes* — and because a dead woman named Margaret Ellery had called her studio at 4:17 that afternoon and said, in a voice like leaves settling, *follow the six in, love, and don't let them sign anything.* Beatrix had not asked how Margaret knew about letters or vestries or signing. Dead callers never explained themselves. They simply knew things the way the drowned know water.

Now she stood at the back of the vestry with seven strangers — six descendants and herself, the interloper, the one who only listened for a living — and watched candlelight do its inadequate work.

The room was smaller than a village hall kitchen and older than any of the surnames assembled in it. Oak panelling gone black as tar. A stone floor worn into two shallow channels by three centuries of feet, leading from the door to the far wall, where the bell hung.

That was the second thing. The bell.

It hung from a beam that should not have been able to hold it, because it should not have existed to be held. St. Aldric's bell had gone to the scrapyard in 1911; Beatrix had read the receipt herself in the county archives, four days ago, in a room that smelled of dust and other people's patience. And yet here it hung — misshapen, slumped on one side like a thing half-melted mid-pour, its bronze surface running with dull rivulets as if it had been caught between states for a hundred years and never permitted to finish either one. It did not swing. It did not need to. It simply *hung*, the way a held breath hangs.

And it rang without sound. She could feel it — a pressure behind the sternum, a tremor in the fillings of her teeth, a shiver in the candle flames that came in slow waves, one after another, patient as tide.

"Right," said Marcus Hale, because solicitors must speak or die. "There will be a mechanism. There is always a mechanism."

* * *

They had tried the door for twenty minutes. Ada Thorne had put her farmer's shoulder into it hard enough to bruise, and the oak had taken the blow the way deep water takes a stone — without complaint, without movement, closing around it. Rowan Fenn had found the window, the small leaded one above the vestry cupboard, and discovered that the glass gave slightly under his palm and then stopped, resisting with a softness that made him snatch his hand back as though he'd touched a tongue.

Priya Nair had checked everyone's pulses twice, then her own, then given up the pretense with a doctor's economy: "We're alive. All of us. Whatever this is, it hasn't changed that yet." The word *yet* sat in the room afterward like a third candle no one had lit.

Ivy Fenn had cried for exactly four minutes, furiously, wiping her face with her sleeve as if tears were an insult she intended to press charges over. Her twin had stood beside her saying nothing, which for Rowan was its own kind of noise.

Ezra Mowbray, ninety-one years old and steady as a fence post, had walked to the middle of the stone floor, looked up at the melted bell, and said, "Hello again," in the tone of a man greeting a neighbour he'd been avoiding for decades.

Beatrix had stayed by the door. Listening. It was what she did. Ten years of night radio had taught her that every room has a frequency, and if you sit still long enough, you can find it.

This room's frequency was almost silence. But not quite. Underneath the breathing and the weeping and Marcus's increasingly elaborate theories about gas leaks and mass hallucination, there was something else — a quality to the dark in the corners that was not empty. The shadows here had *texture*. They lay against the panelled walls like fur lying the wrong way, and they were thickest in the places where the candlelight reached least eagerly: behind the vestry cupboard, beneath the bell, in the two shallow channels worn into the floor.

Waiting in them, the way you wait in a reception room. Politely. With your hands folded.

"Everyone stop talking," Beatrix said.

To her mild surprise, they did. Even Marcus. Perhaps it was the voice — ten years of 2 a.m. radio gives a voice a certain authority, the calm of someone who has talked strangers back from ledges — or perhaps it was simply that the shadows seemed to lean forward when she spoke, attentive, courteous, and that frightened them all into stillness.

"The psalter," she said. "On the lectern. I saw it when I came in. If there's a covenant — and there is a covenant, we all felt the bell, so let's not waste time pretending otherwise — then it will be written down. These people wrote everything down. That's what founding families *do*. They endow windows and write receipts."

Ada Thorne looked at her with flat farm-country suspicion. "And who are you, exactly? Because I've been racking my brain all evening and none of us invited a radio woman."

"No," Beatrix said. "You invited something else. I just followed it in."

* * *

The psalter was where psalters always are, open on its brass lectern to some comfortable psalm about green pastures, its vellum gone the colour of old tea. It was the margins that mattered. Margins are where the truth goes when it isn't allowed in the text.

The handwriting changed hands down through the pages — seven different scripts, some careful, some hurried, one written by someone whose pen had dug so hard into the vellum it had torn through in places. Dates marched along the outer edges like fence posts: 1811. 1911. And between them, in Latin and then in English and then, once, in a shaking hand, in plain blunt words that needed no translation:

*One soul, willing, each hundredth year. One only. Given freely, it takes nothing else. The parish endures.*

Below that, in the same hand but fainter, as if the ink itself had grown tired:

*It keeps its word. God forgive us, it keeps its word.*

Marcus read it aloud, because reading aloud is also a kind of lawyering, and his voice got thinner with every line. "Willing," he repeated. "Given *freely*. So it needs consent. It can't just—" He stopped. Everyone heard the sentence finish itself in the dark.

"It's been waiting since midnight," Ezra said.

They turned to him. He stood with his hands folded over his stick, facing the bell, and his face in the candlelight was a map of every road this valley had ever taken.

"My grandmother told me. The debt comes due at midnight on the hundredth year, and the thing arrives — wherever it arrives *from* — and waits until dawn. It can't act before dawn unless it's given what it's owed. So it waits. Eleven hours, give or take. My gran called it the Long Courtesy." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Beatrix had ever seen on a living face, because it was fond. "She said it was more mannerly than most of the parish. Said it would thank you for passing the salt, if you'd a mind to pass it salt."

"That's insane," Ivy said, but quietly, the way people say *that's insane* when they are standing inside the insanity and it has begun to notice them.

"It's patient," Ezra agreed. "Patience isn't sanity or madness. It's just patience."

* * *

It began to speak at half past nine.

Not aloud — Beatrix would have sworn to that later, swore it on her mother's grave and her own. Nothing in the room moved air in the shape of words. But each of them heard it anyway, the way you hear your name across a crowded pub: directly, privately, bypassing the ears entirely. And it did not boom or whisper or rasp. It *conversed*. It sounded — this was the detail Beatrix knew she would carry out of this room if she carried nothing else — it sounded like a good host. Like someone who has laid on fire and food and is genuinely sorry the evening has begun awkwardly.

*I do apologise for the accommodations,* it said, to all of them at once and each of them alone. *The room is not what it was. Neither am I, if we're being honest — the melting was unkind, and I have been... incomplete, these hundred years. Half-heard. You may have noticed the calls.*

Beatrix's blood went cold and then colder. *You may have noticed the calls.* Her dead listeners. Margaret Ellery and the man who described his own funeral and the child's voice that sang hymns off-key at 3 a.m. — leakage, Edith-someone had whispered through static, *leakage from a covenant cut halfway* — and the thing in the shadows knew about them, mentioned them the way you'd mention a leaky roof. An inconvenience. A shared embarrassment between neighbours.

"What do you want?" Ada demanded, loud, into the empty centre of the room, because someone had to say it and farmers do not flinch at slaughter.

*What I have always wanted. What was promised.* A pause, gracious, giving her room. *One of you. Willingly. And I want you to understand — I want this understood — that I will not trick you, press you, or take what is not offered. Your ancestors wrote terms and I have honoured them for two hundred years. Ask the records. Ask the graves. In 1811 I took Thomas Veale, who came to me of his own free heart, and I touched nothing else in this parish for a century. Not a cow, not a crop, not a child's fever. Hollowmere flourished. You grew fat on the bargain and forgot the price, and that forgetting is not my doing.*

"You're a devil," Marcus said. His voice cracked on the second word.

*A devil takes. I receive. There is a difference, and the difference is you.* The shadows stirred — not moving, nothing so crude as moving, but their texture changed, the way a sleeping animal's coat changes when it dreams. *I am sorry for the wait ahead. Dawn is a long way off. Sit, if you like. The cold is regrettable; I cannot help it. Hunger is part of what I am, and hunger makes rooms cold. But I will be courteous, I promise you. I have nothing else left, courtesy. They took the bell's voice and left me my manners.*

And then, softer, addressed — Beatrix was certain — to her alone, the way a host finds the one guest standing awkwardly by the door:

*And you. Little listener. You've been hearing my dead for ten years and calling it signal bleed. How kind of you, to give them somewhere to call. They get so lonely where I keep them. Do keep the line open.*

* * *

After that, nobody spoke for a long time. The candles burned. The bell rang its soundless rings, one pressure-wave after another, and Beatrix counted them without meaning to — forty-one, forty-two — because counting was a rope and she was holding on.

It was Priya who finally moved, Priya who crossed to the psalter and turned the pages past the covenant with clinical fingers, hunting, because methodical people hunt even in hell. "Here," she said. "1811. The original terms — listen to this. *'The soul shall be willing in truth, not compelled by force, fraud, or fear of death. It shall be given before dawn. The parish being witness.'*" She looked up. "Force, fraud, fear. Those are contract terms. Voidable consent. Whoever drafted this knew law."

"I know law," Marcus said faintly.

"Then you know what *willing in truth* means. It means it can't take a coerced soul. Which means—" and here Priya's voice did something Beatrix had never heard it do in ten years of knowing her at the surgery, something small and human — "which means it has to *wait for us to break*. All night. It just has to be in the room while we fall apart, and catch whichever of us comes apart first, and make sure that when we say yes, we mean it."

*Precisely,* said the dark, warmly, approvingly, like a teacher marking correct work. *You see why I like the clever ones.*

Rowan laughed — one sharp bark of a laugh with no humour anywhere in it — and said, "So we just don't say yes. We just sit here till sunrise and don't say yes. Easy." And Ivy said, "Shut up, Rowan," and Rowan said, "I'm being *positive*," and the twins fell into their bickering the way drowning men fall into water, gratefully, because bickering was a rope too.

But Beatrix wasn't listening to them anymore. She was listening to the corners.

Because the thing had said *I will not trick you, press you, or take what is not offered* — and it had kept talking while it said it, and underneath the courtesy, underneath the good-host warmth, she had heard the thing she'd spent ten years learning to hear underneath every caller's story: the true sentence, the one spoken in the pauses.

*I have waited a hundred years half-starved. I can wait eleven hours. Can you?*

Dawn was at 7:12. It was now twenty to ten.

Nine hours and thirty-two minutes.

In the corner behind the vestry cupboard, the shadow lay thick and still and folded, like a guest who has settled in for the evening, and Beatrix understood — with the calm, ringing clarity of the moment the explanation finally runs out — that the courtesy was the horror. That patience was the trap. That the thing did not need to touch a single one of them.

It only needed the room, the cold, the hours, and the dark —

— and somewhere in the walls, faint and impossible and unmistakable, a telephone began to ring.
