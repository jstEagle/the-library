## 13. The Bell Speaks

The silence after Ada's almost-bargain was the worst kind of silence — the kind that has weight, that presses on the eardrums like deep water. Beatrix stood with her hand still half-raised from pulling Ada back, and in that hand her phone buzzed once, twice, and went dark. The dead callers had gone quiet too. Even they, it seemed, were holding their breath.

The vestry had changed shape again. It did this when it was pleased or displeased; Beatrix had stopped trying to map the logic of it. The ceiling seemed farther away now, lost in a brown murk like tea left standing. The candle stubs Priya had found in a drawer burned without warming anything, their flames perfectly vertical, as if photographed rather than lit. And above them all, where the ceiling should have been, hung the bell.

It had been there since midnight, though none of them had dared name it aloud until now. It hung from nothing — from a beam that might have been shadow, might have been iron — and it was wrong in every particular. A bell should be symmetrical, dignified, whole. This one looked *unmade*. Its bronze surface was rippled and slumped, like wax retrieved from a fire, like something that had been poured out of its own shape and never forgiven it. Rivulets of metal had run down its flanks and frozen mid-drip. Where the clapper should have been there was only a hollow, a dark socket, an absence shaped exactly like a throat.

For eleven hours it had hung there, ringing without sound. They had felt it more than heard it — a pressure behind the eyes, a tremor in the fillings of their teeth, a sense that somewhere very far away a great door was being knocked upon, patiently, by someone who knew the door would open eventually.

Now Beatrix looked up at it, and the bell looked down at her, and she understood — with the certainty of a woman who had spent ten years listening to the dead — that everything hinged on what happened next.

*Say something,* she thought at Edith Mowbray, at the static, at whatever patient voices lived inside her phone. *Tell me what to do.*

The phone stayed black.

Ada Thorne sat on the stone floor with her back against the vestry wall, her big farmer's hands flat on her knees, breathing the way you breathe after nearly driving off a bridge. Marcus Hale had retreated to the far corner with his arms crossed over his chest, guarding the place where his reflection used to live. The twins were a single huddled organism against the old parish chest, Ivy's head on Rowan's shoulder, both of them staring at nothing. Priya Nair was writing — actually writing, in a small notebook she'd produced from her coat, her pen scratching away in the candlelight like the world's most stubborn heartbeat. And Ezra Mowbray, ninety-one years old, sat in the one intact chair with his hands folded and his eyes closed, as if he'd been waiting for this night since 1939, which, Beatrix realized, he had.

"It's going to speak," Ezra said, without opening his eyes.

Everyone looked at him.

"The bell," he said. "When it finally makes a sound, it won't be the thing talking. My grandmother told me. She heard it, the night they melted it. She said—" His voice caught, an old man's voice snagging on ninety years of swallowed words. "She said the bell wasn't empty even then. She said there was a girl inside it, and the girl was screaming, and the men pouring the crucible told her it was just the metal settling."

Beatrix felt the hair rise along her arms. "Edith," she whispered. "Your great-aunt."

"Edith Mowbray." Ezra opened his eyes at last, and they were wet and clear and terribly young. "Seventeen years old. My grandmother's older sister. She walked into the vestry on the last night of October, 1911, same as us, and she was meant to say yes, and she didn't. She stood right about where Ada is sitting now, and she looked up at that thing, and she said no." He smiled, and it was the saddest expression Beatrix had ever seen on a living face. "One word. One girl. Seventeen. And it was enough to break a covenant two hundred years old — halfway, anyway. Enough that the parish panicked and melted the bell to try to cut the cord entirely. Half-invited. Half-fed. Leaking ever since." He looked around the room at each of them in turn. "Every strange death. Every October. Every call that comes from people who are already in the ground. That's not the thing being cruel. That's the thing being *hungry*, because we taught it hunger and then we locked the larder."

The shadows along the walls stirred. Not moving toward anyone — just stirring, the way a sleeping animal stirs when it hears its name.

"Don't," Marcus said sharply. "Don't talk about it like it can hear—"

"It can hear," said six voices at once.

They came from everywhere and nowhere — from the walls, from the candle flames, from the space between Beatrix's own ears. Her own voice among them, pitched kindly. *It can hear.* The thing had been doing this for hours now, wearing their voices like borrowed coats, making offers: rest, warmth, an end to fear. But this time the words weren't an offer. This time they sounded almost like agreement. Like a thing confirming attendance.

And then the bell rang.

Not the soundless tolling they had endured all night. This was *sound* — real sound, enormous sound, a single stroke of bronze that Beatrix felt in her sternum before she heard it, that rattled the candle flames sideways and sent dust sifting down from rafters that hadn't held dust since Victoria. The note itself was broken, warped, a church bell's voice dragged through gravel and grief, but underneath the damage there was something else. Something that made every person in the room go absolutely still.

Underneath the bronze, unmistakably, was a human voice.

A girl's voice. Singing, or screaming, or praying — the three had worn down into the same sound across a hundred and thirteen years — and the bell rang again, and this time Beatrix could pick the words out of the metal like stones from dough.

*"I refuse."*

"Oh my God," Priya breathed. Her pen had stopped. "Oh my God, it's *her*. It's been her the whole time—"

*"I refuse. I refuse. I refuse."*

Each repetition was another toll, and each toll shook the room, and Beatrix understood suddenly why the bell had been silent all these hours. It hadn't been unable to speak. It had been *waiting* — waiting for the moment when speaking might matter, when there were ears in the room that could still choose. Edith Mowbray had been holding one word in her mouth for a century, and tonight, at last, there was someone left alive to hear it.

The voice resolved further as the bell tolled on, the way a radio signal resolves as you tune through the static, and Beatrix — professional listener, ten thousand hours of late-night frequencies — leaned into it with everything she had.

*"Listen to me. Listen, whoever you are, whoever they've locked in with it. I refused. Do you understand? I was afraid — God, I was so afraid, I was seventeen and my father told me it was my duty and the vicar told me it was holy and the whole village stood outside the door like a congregation — and I was going to say yes. I opened my mouth to say yes."*

Toll.

*"But I looked at it. Really looked. And it looked back, and do you know what I saw? Nothing. No hatred. No hunger even — hunger is a living thing's sin. Just... patience. The patience of a hole. And I thought: if I say yes, everyone after me will only ever see the hole, and they will fill it, and fill it, and fill it, forever, and call it faith."*

Toll. The candles guttered. Somewhere in the walls, the thing made a sound like a long slow exhalation — displeasure, or perhaps indigestion.

*"So I said no. And it could not take me. That is the mercy buried in the horror of it, children — it cannot take the unwilling. It can starve, it can wait, it can whisper in your grandmother's voice until you'd sell your soul for five minutes of quiet, but the taking must be given. So they melted the bell instead, thinking to drown me out, thinking the covenant died with the bronze."* A sound that might have been laughter, if laughter could rust. *"They poured me into ingots and sold me for shell casings, and I kept refusing. I have been refusing for one hundred and thirteen years. Every time it reaches through the tear they made, I am in the way. I am the splinter in the door. Me — Edith Mowbray, who got a C in scripture and was frightened of cows."*

Ezra was crying openly now, his old hands pressed together as if in a pew. "Edie," he whispered. "Oh, Edie. We didn't know. We never knew."

*"I know, Ez,"* the bell said gently, and the tenderness in it broke something open in Beatrix's chest. *"You were nine. You knew without knowing, which is its own kind of burden. Hush now. Let me finish; I don't know how much longer I can hold the shape of words."*

The tolling softened. The great broken strokes became smaller, closer, the voice drawing near the way a swimmer draws near shore.

*"Here is what matters. Here is all that matters. The thing in the walls with you is weak tonight — weaker than it has been in a century, because it has waited so long and fed so little, and because six of you have said no in your hearts even when your mouths wavered. It needs a yes the way a drowning man needs air. It will spend the last hours making yes seem like kindness. It will show you your tired children safe in bed. It will offer to stop. Remember: it cannot stop. Stopping is the one thing it cannot do. Only we can stop — by never, any of us, saying the word."*

*"And listen — the caller, the radio woman. Beatrix."* The voice turned toward her, and Beatrix felt it land on her like a physical touch, cool and urgent. *"You hear the dead because they leak through the tear, and you have a transmitter, and you have been afraid of what you are. Don't be. You are the first of us who could carry the refusal out of this room. There is a way — Edith saw it in the last hour of her own night, and it has taken me a century to understand it — the covenant wants a willing soul witnessed by the parish. Witnessed. That word is load-bearing, child. Find out what it truly means. Ask the ones who died. They remember. They have had such a long time to think."*

Then the bell tolled one final time — a stroke so deep and so sorrowful that Beatrix felt tears slide hot down her own face without having decided to cry — and the voice inside it frayed, and thinned, and settled back into the metal like a bird returning to a branch.

*"Do not finish what they started. I am so tired. But I will hold the door as long as there is a door. Promise me a door."*

Silence. Real silence, for the first time all night — the ordinary silence of an old room at night, wind in the yew trees outside, a mouse somewhere in the wainscoting. The shadows along the walls had drawn back, bunched and sullen, like a crowd that has just heard something it didn't want to hear.

Priya spoke first, her voice shaking but her mind visibly already working. "Witnessed by the parish," she repeated. "That's the phrase from the psalter. *Given willingly and witnessed by the parish.* We assumed it meant... present. In the room. But Edith says the word is load-bearing."

"And she said Beatrix could carry it out," Ada said hoarsely. "Carry what out?"

Beatrix looked down at her dead phone in her hand, and then up at the wall behind which — impossibly, illegally, wonderfully — the studio transmitter cable snaked through the old stonework, the line she'd followed in here like a lifeline without knowing why. Ten years of dead callers. Ten years of voices leaking through a tear in the world, finding the one frequency in Hollowmere that was always, always open.

"They remember," she said slowly. "Edith said the dead callers remember, and they've had a long time to think. And they've been trying to reach me for ten years." Her heart was hammering, but it was hammering with something that wasn't only fear — it was the feeling she got at 2 a.m. when a story finally assembled itself out of scattered calls, when the night's theme announced itself. "They haven't just been haunting me. They've been *briefing* me. One piece at a time. For a decade."

"Beatrix," Marcus said carefully, "you're talking about—"

"I'm talking about asking them," she said. "All of them. Right now. While it's weak and she's holding the door."

She thumbed the power button on her phone. It woke instantly, battery full, screen bright — and the signal meter showed four bars of a frequency that should not exist inside a sealed room, and the call log showed an incoming connection already open, duration: 00:00:01, and counting.

From the corner of her eye, Beatrix saw the shadows along the wall go very, very still.

Rowan Fenn stood up.

* * *

He had been quiet for hours — quieter than his sister, which alone should have warned them. Thirteen-year-old boys were not built for eleven hours of darkness and whispers and a voice that wore their mother's tone like a mask, and Beatrix had watched him fray all night the way rope frays, strand by strand, invisibly, until the last strand goes.

"Rowan," Ivy said, reaching for his sleeve. "Rowan, sit down, she's getting a signal, we're going to—"

"No." His voice was flat and strange. He was looking at the window — the high, leaded, dusty window that had been boarded from the outside for fifty years. "No, I'm done. I'm done listening. I want out."

"Rowan, love, nobody's leaving," Ada said, in the voice farmers used for spooked livestock, rising off the floor with her hands out. "Sit down, lad, it's nearly morning—"

"You keep saying that." He backed away from all of them, toward the window, and his smile arrived on his face like weather — sudden, total, wrong. "Nearly morning. Nearly saved. Nearly. I'm done with *nearly*."

"Rowan—" Beatrix started forward.

He moved faster than a boy should move. There was a heavy brass candlestick on the parish chest — Priya's, salvaged from the drawer with the candles — and his hand closed around it, and he swung it at the window with his whole body behind it.

The leaded glass did not shatter so much as *give up*, panes bursting outward in a spray of diamonds, the rotted frame splitting, the old boards beyond tearing loose like paper, and cold October air flooded the vestry for the first time in eleven hours — and with the air came the smell of the churchyard at night, wet grass and old stone and something underneath it, something sweetish and expectant, like fruit beginning to turn.

"ROWAN!" Ivy screamed.

He was already through it. One moment his silhouette filled the broken frame against the moonlit yews; the next the night simply folded around him, the darkness closing over the boy like water over a dropped stone, and he was gone.

They crowded the window — all of them, shouting his name into the churchyard, torches and phone lights sweeping the graves, the leaning headstones, the bare black trees. Nothing. No movement anywhere. The village beyond the wall lay silent under a sky full of indifferent stars.

Then, from directly below the window, from the shadow at the base of the wall, Rowan's voice floated up. Unhurried. Pleasant. Smiling so widely they could hear it.

"It's lovely out here," he said. "Why did we ever stay in there? Come and see. Come and see, Ivy. The exit is lovely."

Beatrix looked down into the dark beneath the window, and the dark looked back, and it was wearing a thirteen-year-old boy's face — and it was waiting, patiently, for someone to come to it.
