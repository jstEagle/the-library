## 14. Rowan Runs

Rowan Fenn had been counting the hours the way other people counted money — hoarding them, hiding them, spending them too fast. Ten o'clock, eleven, midnight, one. The phone in his hand said 3:33 and then it said nothing at all, the screen going grey as old bone, and that was when he understood that the numbers were not going to save him.

He had never been the brave one. That was Ivy. Ivy got scared and then got angry, and anger looked enough like courage that most people couldn't tell the difference. Rowan got scared and then got small. He got good at small. He'd spent seventeen years perfecting it — the art of sitting quietly at the edge of a room, of making himself furniture, of breathing so shallowly that fear couldn't find his lungs.

But the vestry was shrinking. That was the truth of it, the truth he'd been refusing since the door sealed behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released. The room was shrinking, or he was growing, or the dark between the stones was thickening into something with weight and appetite, and Rowan Fenn could not make himself small enough to survive it.

The shadows had stopped behaving. That was the thing no one wanted to say out loud. Shadows were supposed to lie down when you looked at them. These stood up. These pooled in the corners like ink poured into water, and they moved — not quickly, never quickly, that would have been almost bearable — but with the unhurried patience of something that had been waiting a century and could wait a century more. They moved the way a tide moves. Inevitably. Toward you.

And it spoke in their voices now.

*You look tired, Rowan,* it had said, twenty minutes ago, in Ivy's voice, while Ivy sat three feet away with her knees pulled to her chest and her eyes squeezed shut. *You could rest. I could hold the fear for you. That's all it is, you know. Just something being held. I'm very good at holding things.*

He'd looked at his sister and his sister had looked back at him and neither of them had said anything, because what was there to say? That the thing wearing her voice had a better grasp of his interior life than he did?

Marcus Hale had a lock of his hair missing. Rowan kept noticing it, kept looking at the pale patch above Marcus's ear where the thing had taken its *earnest money*, and kept thinking: *that's what's left when you almost give yourself away. A bald patch. A reflection that doesn't blink when you do.* Marcus stood by the far wall now, arms crossed, jaw set, pretending he was still a solicitor and this was still a negotiation, and his reflection in the darkened window stood with him but a half-second late, like a dub of himself played on damaged film.

Ada Thorne was praying. Not to God — Rowan was fairly sure it wasn't to God, because God's name never came up — but to something older, something with a ledger, muttering under her breath the way she probably muttered over accounts in the farmhouse kitchen, and the shadows near her leaned in to listen.

Dr. Nair was counting. Rowan had watched her do it for two hours. Pulse, breath, pulse, breath — checking everyone's vitals over and over as if the numbers might suddenly make sense, as if medicine had a chapter for this. Her hands were steady. Her eyes were not.

And Ezra Mowbray sat in the corner like a man waiting for a bus, which was somehow the most frightening thing of all.

The cold had teeth now. It had started as a chill, then a damp, and now it was a cold that found the spaces between your cells and pried. Rowan's breath came out white. His fingers had gone the colour of candle wax. Ivy's lips were blue at the edges and she kept rubbing her arms and saying *I'm fine, I'm fine* in a voice that convinced no one, least of all her.

The food had died first. Ada had brought a bag of sandwiches, because Ada Thorne faced every crisis with provisions, and somewhere in hour four Rowan had opened his — cheese and pickle, his mum's brand, the bread already going soft — and watched it rot. Not stale. *Rot.* Green and black and wet, blooming across the bread in the space between one breath and the next, the smell of it thick and sweet and wrong, like something exhaling through the food. Ivy had made a sound he'd never heard his sister make before. The thing had said, in their mum's voice, *Waste not, want not,* and Rowan had bitten the inside of his cheek so hard he tasted blood, and the blood had tasted like the only real thing left in the room.

3:33. The phones had died at 3:33 the first time, all four of them at once, and then someone had found a charger, and then they'd died at 3:33 again, and then again, as if the number itself were a drain and every battery in the room was being poured down it. Rowan looked at his dead screen and saw, faintly, in the grey glass, his own face — and behind his face, the whole room, and in the whole room, the shadows, and the shadows were closer than they'd been when he'd looked up last.

*They're moving faster,* he thought. *It's getting bolder. It's getting hungry.*

And then — he would never be able to explain this part, not to Ivy, not to the police, not to himself at three in the morning for the rest of his life — the bell moved.

Not rang. *Moved.* The melted thing hanging in the dark above them, the bell that shouldn't exist, that had been poured into scrap in 1911 and yet hung here in the vestry like a drowned thing suspended in deep water — it shifted. A slow, grinding lean, like a sleeper turning over. And every shadow in the room leaned with it.

Rowan's body decided before his mind did.

Later he would understand that this was the trick of it — that the thing had spent six hours teaching him to be small, and small things bolt. That panic was not a failure of the trap but a feature of it. But in that moment there was no later. There was only the grinding lean of the impossible bell and the shadows all turning toward him like faces and the cold reaching down his throat, and Rowan Fenn came up off the floor with a sound that wasn't quite a scream and wasn't quite a word.

"Rowan—" Ivy, reaching.

He was already moving. Three steps, four, and there was the window — the little lancet window behind the vestry's old vestment cupboard, the one with the hairline crack running through it like a vein, the one he'd noticed hours ago and filed away in the part of his brain that always, always looked for exits. He didn't look for anything to break it with. He drove his elbow into the glass.

It burst outward in a spray of diamonds. Cold air poured in — real cold, night cold, churchyard cold — and it was the first honest thing Rowan had felt in six hours. He didn't feel the glass scoring his forearm. He didn't hear Ivy screaming his name, or Marcus shouting *don't you bloody dare*, or the thing — in his own voice now, his own voice, coming from the corner behind him — say, gently, almost sadly:

*You'll want to come back, you know. They always want to come back.*

Rowan went through the window like a boy diving into a river.

* * *

The churchyard at night was a country he'd known all his life and had never once visited.

He'd cut through it a thousand times — the shortcut from the bus stop, the path between the leaning headstones, the yew tree you had to duck under. He'd smoked his first cigarette behind that yew. He'd snogged Katie Marsh by the south wall. The churchyard of St. Aldric's was as familiar to Rowan Fenn as his own hallway.

It was not familiar now.

The grass moved. That was the first wrongness, and it was such a small one that he almost missed it: the grass moving against the wind, every blade combing itself flat toward the church, toward the vestry, toward the window he'd just come through — as if the whole churchyard were a tide going out and he was standing in the retreating water.

The headstones had turned. He was nearly sure of it. Not moved — *turned*, the way flowers turn toward light, every stone facing him now, every weathered name and date angled toward the boy running between them, so that running down the path felt like running a corridor of watching faces.

"Help!" he shouted, because Hollowmere was out there, past the wall, past the lych-gate — houses, lights, his mum, the whole ordinary world with its kettles and its streetlamps and its people who had no idea. "Somebody! Please!"

The village did not answer.

He reached the lych-gate and stopped so hard he stumbled. Beyond the gate, Church Lane should have run downhill toward the post office and the shop and the yellow glow of the streetlamp on the corner. Rowan could see the lamp. It hung there, a soft orange eye in the dark, exactly where it had always been.

But the lane between here and there was not the lane.

It was longer. That was the only way to put it, and even that wasn't right, because it wasn't that the lane had grown — it was that the night had been *folded*, the way you fold a map, and the churchyard was now pressed up against something that was not Church Lane at all. The dark beyond the lych-gate had a texture. It had grain, like wood, like fabric, and it moved in slow pleats, and through the pleats Rowan could see glimpses of things that were not Hollowmere: a field of grass standing perfectly still under a moon that was in the wrong quarter; a house he almost recognised with all its windows lit and no one in them; a road going up and up into a sky with no stars in it, only a soft grey glow, like dawn that had forgotten how to arrive.

The night had folded around him, and the folds went a long, long way down.

"No," Rowan said. His voice came out very small, and the churchyard, kindly, did not laugh. "No, no, no—"

He turned back toward the church. St. Aldric's stood roofless against the sky, its walls a black silhouette, its empty window-eyes full of stars — and the vestry window he'd broken glowed faintly, warm, gold, *inviting*, and he thought: *that's wrong, the vestry was dark, the vestry has been dark for six hours,* and he thought it in the same breath that he thought: *warm. It's warm in there. They're waiting for me. Ivy's waiting for me.*

The grass was pulling at his ankles now. Gently. Like hands. Like a mother guiding a tired child through a crowd.

*You'll want to come back,* his own voice had said, and God, God, it had been right, it had known, because the fear out here was worse than the fear in there — out here the fear was infinite, it had room to spread, and the vestry at least was small, and small was what Rowan did, small was his one talent, and the broken window was glowing like the last lit window on earth—

He was walking toward it before he knew he'd turned around.

He was walking and the churchyard was helping, the grass parting for him, the headstones turning their faces to watch him pass, and somewhere far above him the melted bell hung silent in the dark and Rowan felt it looking down at him with the whole weight of its hundred and thirteen years. The glow grew. The warmth grew. And with every step the fear drained out of him the way water drains from a bath, and what rushed in behind it was worse — a softness, a sweetness, a *rightness*, the absolute conviction that this was where he'd been heading all his life, that every path in Hollowmere had always led here, that the exit was not a way out at all but a way *in*, and the exit was lovely, the exit was the loveliest place he had ever—

"Rowan!"

Ivy's voice. Real Ivy's voice, ragged and furious and terrified, coming through the broken window, and it cut through the sweetness like a knife through gauze.

Rowan stopped. He was ten feet from the window. He did not remember crossing the churchyard. His feet were wet — when had his shoes come off? They were standing neatly side by side on the path behind him, laces tucked in, the way you leave shoes at a door. His feet were bare and the grass was soft as carpet and he had been *walking* and he did not remember deciding to walk.

"Rowan, don't you *dare*, don't you dare—"

Ivy was halfway out the window. Marcus had her by the ankles. Her face was tear-streaked and furious and it was the most beautiful thing Rowan had ever seen, because it was *real*, it was his sister, it was the girl who'd fought with him over the remote control that very morning in a kitchen that smelled of toast, and the toast, God, the toast, he would never take toast for granted again as long as he lived.

"I'm coming back," Rowan heard himself say.

His voice was wrong. He knew it was wrong even as he said it. It was too calm. It had a smile in it, and the smile was not his.

"I'm coming back," he said again, walking, barefoot, "it's fine, it's *lovely*, you should see it, the exit's lovely—"

And he smiled. He felt his own face do it — felt the muscles gather, felt the shape of it, and the shape was wrong, the shape was too wide and too even and too *pleased*, a smile like something practised in a mirror by someone who had only ever had smiles described to them.

"Rowan." That was Beatrix Pemberton, leaning out beside Ivy, her face grey in the glow. "Rowan, look at me. Look at my face. Not the window. My face."

He looked at her face. He looked at Ivy's face. And for one second — one blessed, ordinary second — the sweetness cracked, and underneath it was Rowan Fenn, seventeen years old, barefoot in a churchyard, colder than he'd ever been in his life.

"It's got me," he said, and his voice was his own again, and it was shaking. "It's got some of me. It's — it's holding it, it's holding it *out here*, I can feel it, like a hand in my chest, like — like a hook—"

"Come inside," Beatrix said. "Slow. Don't run. Don't look at anything but us."

He walked. The grass pulled. The headstones watched. The glow of the window dimmed, sulked, went out entirely as he came within reach of his sister's hands, and Ivy hauled him over the sill with a strength she shouldn't have had, and they fell together onto the vestry floor in a tangle of arms and glass and breath, and Rowan clung to his twin and shook and shook and shook.

* * *

It took him a long time to stop shaking, and when he did, the room had arranged itself around him the way rooms do after catastrophe — everyone closer than they'd been, everyone pretending they hadn't moved.

Ivy wouldn't let go of his hand. Her grip hurt. He didn't tell her.

"His feet," Dr. Nair said quietly. She was crouched at his ankles with her phone torch, and her voice had the careful flatness she'd been using all night, the voice of a woman refusing to let her hands learn what her mind already knew. "No abrasions. He walked through a churchyard in October barefoot and there isn't a mark on him. The ground didn't touch him." She looked up. "Or the ground he walked on wasn't our ground."

"It's lovely out there," Rowan said.

Everyone looked at him.

"It is," he said, and heard the smile come back into his voice and couldn't stop it, couldn't find the off-switch, his own mouth a borrowed instrument. "That's the thing. That's what nobody — it's not horrible. It's not a hell. It's *nice*. It's the nicest place I've ever been. It's warm and the grass is soft and nothing hurts and it just — it *wants* you, it wants you the way Mum wants us home before dark, and you walk and you walk and you feel so *held*—"

"Rowan." Beatrix crouched in front of him. Her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "Stop. Breathe. You're back. Say it."

"I'm back."

"Say you're back."

"I'm back." And then, because he couldn't help it, because it was true and the truth was the worst thing in the room: "But it kept some of me. Out there. It's still holding it. I can feel it. Like — like a hand in my chest, holding a piece of me the way you'd hold—" his voice broke, "—the way you'd hold a bird. Gently. So gently. It said — it said it would take very good care of it. It said I could come and visit whenever I liked. It said—"

He clamped his mouth shut. Too late.

"It said," Rowan finished, in a whisper, in the too-wide smile, "*the door is never locked from the inside.*"

The silence that followed had weight. Ada Thorne stopped her muttering. Even the shadows seemed to lean back, to give the words room.

"That's not how it works," Marcus said. His voice cracked on *works*. "That's not — the covenant, the psalter, it said *willing*. It said willing, it needs willingness, that's the whole — that's the *rule*—"

"It didn't take me," Rowan said. He was crying now, and the smile sat on his face through the tears, and the sight of it made Ivy make a small broken sound and hold his hand tighter. "It didn't take me. I came to *it*. I ran to it. That's — that's the difference, isn't it? It doesn't need you to say yes. It just needs you to *come*." He looked at his own hands, at the faint scratches on his forearm from the window, the only honest wounds on his body. "I ran out and I ran *to it*, and now it's holding a piece of me, and it's not going to drop it, and every time I get scared — and I'm always scared — I'm going to want to go back and be whole. That's what it bought. That's what it bought with me."

Nobody spoke.

Then Ezra Mowbray, from his corner, in the voice of a man reading a weather report: "That's how it got the ones in the old days. Not the offering. The others. The ones who couldn't bear watching." He folded his hands. "It's not breaking the rules. It's showing you the small print."

Rowan looked down at his bare feet on the stone floor. They were warm. They were the warmest part of him, and he knew — he knew with the certainty of a boy reading his own diagnosis — that the warmth was not coming from the vestry.

It was coming from the piece of him that was still outside, still held, still gently, gently waiting — and through the broken window, far out in the folded dark of the churchyard, something patient turned its attention back toward the room, and Beatrix Pemberton's hand went slowly, slowly to her pocket, where her phone lay dead at 3:33.

"Everyone away from the window," she said. "Now."
