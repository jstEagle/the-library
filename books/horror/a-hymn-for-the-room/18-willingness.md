## 18. Willingness

Ezra Mowbray had been counting the hours the way he used to count sheep at his wife's bedside in her last winter — not to fall asleep, but to stay awake, to keep watch over something that could not keep watch over itself.

Ten hours. Nearly ten hours in this cold room with its psalter and its shadows and its courteous hunger. He had watched Marcus Hale's courage curdle. He had watched Ada Thorne nearly give herself away for love of people she'd never once spoken kindly to before tonight. He had watched Ivy Fenn drift toward the dark like a moth toward a lamp she believed was the moon, and he had watched Beatrix Pemberton drag her back by the collar of her coat, both of them sobbing, neither of them ashamed of it.

He had watched all of it from his chair by the vestry's east wall, where the stone was coldest and the candlelight thinnest, and he had thought: *I have known since I was nine years old that I would end here.*

Not here exactly. Not this room, not this night. But *here* — in the debt, inside the arithmetic of it. His grandmother had told him when he was small enough to sit on her knee, her hands folded over his like a lid on a box: *The Mowbrays owe, Ezra-lad. We've owed since 1811, and we'll owe long after you're ash.* He had thought it was a story about money. Children always think the debts are about money.

He was ninety-one. His heart had been stuttering since spring; Dr. Nair had said the word *cardiomyopathy* gently, the way you'd hand someone a fragile thing. His knees were gone. His hearing came and went like weather off the moor. He had buried a wife, a son, and most lately his dog, Bess, whose absence in the house was louder than any presence had ever been. There was no one left who needed him awake.

And the children — the Fenn twins, seventeen, with their whole ridiculous futures still folded up inside them like letters never posted — the children were starting to break. He could hear it in the way Ivy whispered now even when she wasn't praying. He could see it in Rowan, who sat pressed against the wall beneath the broken window with his back to the room, walking backward through his own memory of the churchyard, smiling that wrong smile whenever anyone said his name.

Ezra stood up.

It took longer than it should have. His hip caught, his breath went thin, and Priya half-rose from her corner with her doctor's reflexes outrunning her fear — but he waved her down, palm out, gentle as shooing a hen.

"No," he said. "Stay seated, lass. This is Mowbray business."

The shadows along the north wall shifted. They had been shifting all night, but now they gathered with something like attention, the way a congregation turns when the door creaks open late. The thing did not speak. It rarely needed to anymore. It had spent six hours learning their voices, and now it mostly just listened, patient as a landlord.

"I've been sitting here doing sums," Ezra said, and his voice carried better than he expected — old men's voices are made for chapels, even roofless ones. "Ninety-one years. That's what I've got left of me, more or less. A year, maybe two, if I'm stubborn about it, and I'm told I am." He looked around the room, at each face in turn. Ada, jaw set like a gate. Marcus, hollow-eyed, touching his hair where the lock had been taken. Priya, pen still moving in her notebook because method was her prayer. The twins, huddled together under one coat like chicks under a wing. And Beatrix, standing near the east wall with her hand resting on the transmitter cable that snaked out of the stones like a root, as if she'd been holding it for hours without knowing why.

"Two years," Ezra said. "Against—" he nodded at the twins— "sixty, seventy years apiece, if they get out of here. Against that young woman's whole life." He meant Priya. "Against yours," he told Beatrix. "You've got books in you yet. I've read the first one. It frightened my cat."

Someone made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"So here's my offer," Ezra said, and turned to face the north wall, where the dark was deepest and the melted bell hung silent above it all, cracked and shapeless and waiting. "Me. Willingly. Gladly, even — and I don't say that to flatter you, you great grey debt, I say it because it's true. I'm tired. I'm tired the way a field is tired after harvest. There's nothing growing in me but rest. Take mine and let the rest of them walk out at sunrise. Every one. Even the boy who ran. Especially him."

"Ezra, no—" Ada was on her feet.

"Sit down, Ada Thorne. You've had your turn at bargaining and we all saw how that ended." But there was no sharpness in it. He softened. "You're a good woman under all that flint. Your grandmother would be proud, and she was a terror."

The shadows moved. Not toward him — around him, slowly, the way water finds the low ground. And then the voice came, and it was using no one's voice tonight; it was its own, which sounded like wind through a keyhole, like a hymn hummed by an empty house.

*Willingly?*

"Willingly," Ezra said.

*Gladly?*

"Gladly." And here is the terrible thing, the thing Beatrix would remember for the rest of her life: he smiled. Not the wrong smile Rowan wore. A real one. The smile of a man setting down a load he has carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy. "I've been ready since I was nine years old. You've waited a hundred years. I've waited eighty-two. Let's not stand on ceremony."

*Accepted,* said the dark, and the word settled over the room like snowfall.

It began gently. Of course it did. It was courteous to the last.

The cold gathered around Ezra first — not the general cold of the vestry, which they had all stopped feeling hours ago, but a *specific* cold, shaped like a man, drawn tight around him like a coat being fitted. His breath stopped steaming. Then it stopped altogether, though his chest kept moving, kept working, a bellows pumping nothing. The candle nearest him bent its flame toward him as if leaning in to listen.

His grandmother's voice — or the memory of it, or the thing wearing the memory — seemed to come up through the floor. *The Mowbrays owe, Ezra-lad.* Yes. Yes, they did. And here was the payment, and here was the peace.

He felt himself begin to loosen. That was how it took you, he understood suddenly, with the clarity of the very old and the very near death: not by pulling, but by untying. Knot by knot. The knot of his hands first — he could feel his fingers going distant, like gloves left on a wall. Then the knot of his name. *Ezra* began to sound like a word in a foreign language, a word someone else had answered to. Then the knot of—

"—no."

The voice was small and cracked and it came from the twins' corner. Ivy. She was on her feet, white-faced, staring at him. "No. You can't. You *can't*, Mr. Mowbray, you're the only one who knows things, you're the only one who remembers—"

"That's rather the point, lass," Ezra said, and his voice was already thinning, already coming from further away than his mouth. "Remember for me. All of you. Write it down. Put it in your book, Doctor. Put it in your songs, Miss Pemberton. Memory's the only bank that doesn't fail."

Rowan turned around. For one moment — one flickering, guttering moment — the wrong smile fell off his face like a dropped mask, and underneath it was just a terrified boy looking at the oldest man in the village giving himself away. "*Don't*," Rowan said, and it was his own voice, his true one, surfacing like a drowning swimmer. "Don't go where I went. Please. It's not— it isn't *lovely*, it's—"

But the dark closed over whatever else he meant to say, and his face smoothed back into that terrible pleasantness, and Ezra felt the last knots beginning to slip, and he thought: *Bess. Edith. Mam.* He thought: *sunrise.* He thought, with the last of himself, that it didn't hurt, that it felt like taking off boots after a long day, and that he forgave them, all of them, every ancestor back to 1811, because they had only ever been afraid, and fear is just love wearing its work clothes—

And then the room filled with sound.

* * *

Beatrix had been holding the cable for two hours.

She hadn't decided to. Her hand had simply found it somewhere in hour eight, when Ivy started whispering, and stayed there, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The transmitter cable ran out of the vestry wall — out of solid stone that had no business containing anything, let alone a live feed to the studio above the post office — snaking across the floorboards like something grown rather than installed, and ended in a jack that fit her phone. She had discovered this at some point past midnight and told no one, because telling no one was becoming her defining skill. Ten years of telling no one. Ten years of logging the dead as hoaxes, filing grief under signal bleed, keeping the calm of a woman who explains too much until explaining is all she is.

The dead callers had told her, in the static of hour nine: *it strengthens near spoken consent. It weakens near recorded truth. Testimony, Beatrix. It can't digest testimony.* Priya had worked it out independently, because of course she had, scribbling in her notebook: *broadcast is witness. Witness is the one thing it can't swallow.*

And Edith — Edith Mowbray, 1911, the girl in the bell, the girl who refused for a century — Edith had given her the loophole in a voice like frost forming on glass: *the covenant needs a willing soul AND a witnessing parish. Witness it, Beatrix. Witness it forever.*

So Beatrix stood with her hand on the cable and watched Ezra Mowbray untie himself knot by knot, and she understood that she had run out of hours, and that she had also — this was the shameful, human truth of it — run out of alternatives. She had watched Marcus break. She had watched Ada bargain. She had dragged Ivy back from the edge of consent with her own two hands and felt how light the girl had gone, how easy it would have been to let her drift. Someone was going to break. It was hour ten of eleven. The room had eaten every scrap of courage they'd brought into it, and what remained was Ezra, offering himself up like the last coin in the parish purse, and Beatrix Pemberton, who had spent ten years refusing to believe her own ears.

She thought of the callers. Mrs. Halloran, describing her own funeral flowers. The man who'd asked her to tell his daughter the shed key was under the third brick. The woman, last October, who had said, *you're our voice, dear, that's why you can hear us — say it for us, say it out loud, we couldn't.* She had logged that call as a hoax too. She had logged everything as a hoax. Ten years of hoaxing herself.

Ezra's breath stopped steaming.

Beatrix plugged in the cable.

Her hands were shaking — of course they were shaking, her hands had shaken since chapter one of this whole rotten century-old story — but her fingers knew the rig the way a priest's fingers know the beads. Phone to jack. Jack to transmitter. Transmitter to the mast on the hill that had broadcast 'The Night Line' to insomniacs and truckers and the grieving dead of three counties for a decade. The ON AIR light lived upstairs in the studio; there was no light here. There was only her, and the cable humming faintly under her palm like a held note.

"*On air*," she said, because saying it had always been the sacrament, and the little screen lit green, and somewhere above the post office, above the sleeping village, above the whole listening dark, a signal went out into the county night.

The effect was immediate and physical.

The cold around Ezra *stuttered*. The shadows recoiled from the walls like smoke from a struck match — not far, not for long, but they moved, they *flinched*, and in the flinch everyone saw it: the thing could be hurt. Not by fire, not by iron, not by prayer. By being *heard*.

"Beatrix—" Priya was staring at the phone, at the waveform dancing across its screen, at the impossible signal strength bars standing full and steady inside a sealed stone room. "That's— you're broadcasting. From *inside*—"

"I know." Beatrix's voice came out rougher than she intended. She cleared her throat and found, underneath the fear, the other thing — the thing that had kept her behind the microphone for ten years while her world quietly rotted. The calm. Except it wasn't calm, it had never been calm; it was *defiance* wearing calm's coat. "Everyone stay where you are. Don't stop me. Whatever happens — don't stop me."

The dark spoke, and for the first time all night it did not sound courteous. It sounded *crowded*, a chorus forcing itself through one throat: *This is not your debt, broadcaster.*

"You made it my debt," Beatrix said, and lifted the phone to her mouth like a chalice, like a weapon, like a microphone, which was all three. "Ten years ago. The first time one of your taken called my show and I was too much of a coward to believe her. You've been leaking into my studio for a decade. Every October. Every seventh day. You made me the confessional of Hollowmere and never told me what I was hearing." Her eyes went to Ezra — to what was left of him, a thin old man wrapped in a shaped cold, his lips barely moving now, his knots almost all undone. "Let him go."

*The bargain is struck,* the dark said. *Willingly. Gladly. Accepted.*

"The bargain requires a witness," Beatrix said. "Your own terms. Written in your own book." She nodded at the psalter lying open on its stand, the covenant in the margins, the ink that had been drying for two hundred years. "A soul given willingly *and witnessed by the parish*. So witness it, then. All of it. Right now."

She breathed in. The whole room breathed in with her — Ada, Marcus, Priya, Ivy clutching her brother's sleeve, even Rowan turning his wrong-smiling face toward the sound of her voice like a plant toward light.

"This is Beatrix Pemberton, live from St. Aldric's vestry, Hollowmere," she said, and her voice went out over the moor, over the rooftops, into every clock radio and car stereo and kitchen set in the county, into the sleepless dark where the dead had been calling her for ten years. "It is four minutes past five in the morning, and I am about to tell you what this village did."

Under her words, faint as a pulse, the transmitter cable hummed. In the north wall's deep shadow, Ezra Mowbray hung between worlds, untied almost to the last knot — and stopped. Held. Waiting, like everything else in the room, to hear what she would say next.

Beatrix gripped the phone tighter and began.
