## 1. The Wrong Body

The farmhouse was smaller than Hazel remembered, which was impossible, because houses don't shrink and neither do the roads that lead to them. But the drive up from the county line had taken forty minutes instead of twenty-five, and the gravel lane had seemed to add a new curve every time she looked away from it, so by the time she pulled in behind the hearse—behind the other hearse, the one already parked crooked in the yard—she had stopped trusting her own sense of distance entirely.

She sat in the rental car with both hands on the wheel and did not get out.

October had come early to the valley. The maples along the fence line were burning orange out of season, and the light through them fell onto the porch in coins, in slivers, in shapes that moved when she wasn't watching. The house itself was worse than memory. Grandma Eriksen's place had always been white paint over gray wood, a porch swing with one rusted chain, a weathervane rooster frozen mid-crow since before Hazel was born. Now the paint hung off the siding in long curls like shed skin, the swing's second chain had snapped and left the seat hanging at an angle, and the rooster—weathervanes don't change, they can't—now pointed east.

Hazel had never known which way the wind came from here. She checked anyway. There was no wind.

*Get out of the car,* she told herself. *You've driven eleven hours. You're grieving. Grief makes everything look wrong.*

But grief, as far as she knew it—and she knew it only from the outside until this week, until the phone call, until the word *Wren* said in a stranger's careful voice—grief made things look *sad*. Not wrong. Not tilted three degrees off true. She got out because staying in the car was its own kind of answer, and she wasn't ready to hear what the question was.

The funeral director met her halfway across the yard. He was a soft-spoken man named Aldous Peck, sixty or older, with kind eyes and a handshake that lasted a half-second too long, as if he were checking her pulse. Behind him, two men in dark suits stood at the open back of the hearse, waiting with the particular stillness of men who move caskets for a living and have learned not to hurry.

"Miss Eriksen." He said it gently. "Thank you for coming all this way."

"She's my sister," Hazel said. "I'd have come farther."

Something crossed his face then—quick, gone before she could name it—and he turned toward the hearse. "We've done everything per the instructions in your grandmother's file. Of course, you'll want to see her before tomorrow. Family views first."

"Of course."

They drew the casket out on its rollers, down the ramp, across the dead grass toward the porch, where a folding table had been set up with a guest book and a vase of grocery-store carnations. Hazel walked beside it. That was the custom; you walked beside your dead. And as she walked, she read the brass plate on the lid, because reading names is what brass plates are for, and because some part of her wanted the simple animal comfort of seeing her sister's name spelled out in a world where her sister no longer existed to spell it herself.

**HAZEL MARIE ERIKSEN**
**1979 – 2024**

She stopped walking.

The rollers kept going for another foot before the men noticed and halted, and the casket sat there in the grass between them, patient as furniture, wearing her name.

"That's—" Her voice came out thin. She tried again. "That's the wrong name."

Mr. Peck looked at the plate. He looked at it the way a man looks at a clock when someone tells him the time is wrong: thoroughly, patiently, finding nothing. "That's the name on the death certificate," he said. "Hazel Marie Eriksen. We go by the certificate."

"I'm Hazel Marie Eriksen."

"Yes," he agreed, without argument, without surprise, and that was the worst part—the *without*. A man should flinch when a living woman says she is the woman in the box. He only nodded, the way you nod at weather. "The family has used those names interchangeably for years, I understand. Twins. It happens. The paperwork follows the record."

"What record? Who filed it?"

"Your aunt. Maren Eriksen. She handled the arrangements per your grandmother's standing instructions." He paused, and his kindness showed through like a lamp behind a curtain. "If there's been an error, we'll correct it. But I'll need you to tell me plainly, Miss Eriksen—which twin died?"

And there it was. The question. Standing in the yard in the October light that didn't move right, wearing a funeral director's polite face.

Hazel opened her mouth to say *Wren*, obviously Wren, Wren who called her every Sunday until eighteen months ago and then stopped, Wren whose voicemail from last spring Hazel still hadn't deleted because deleting it felt like a door closing—Wren, Wren, Wren—and found that the name would not come out of her mouth. It wasn't stage fright. It was something structural, something like reaching for a stair that isn't where the stair should be. She thought *Wren died* and the sentence sat in her mind like a stone sitting in a river, and the river went around it, and nothing moved.

"Which twin died," she repeated.

"It's a formality," Peck said. "For the amended certificate, if there is one."

"You're the funeral director. You collected the body. Somebody identified it."

"Your aunt identified her." He glanced at the house, and Hazel followed the glance and saw, for the first time, the figure on the porch—a small woman wrapped in a cardigan too heavy for the season, standing at the rail with her hands folded, watching them with an expression Hazel couldn't read at this distance except to know it wasn't grief. Aunt Maren. She'd flown in yesterday, according to the messages Hazel had been too numb to answer properly.

"Maren," Hazel called. "Maren, come tell these men whose daughter this is."

Aunt Maren did not come down. She lifted one hand—not a wave, more a benediction, or a warning to stay back—and went inside, letting the screen door fall shut behind her with a sound like a slap.

* * *

They set the casket in the parlor, because that was where Eriksens had always laid out their dead, on trestles under the window with the good lace curtains, and Hazel signed nothing and corrected nothing and followed it inside because the alternative was standing in the yard alone with a question she couldn't answer.

The house smelled the way it had always smelled—woodsmoke and lavender and underneath it something mineral, like well water—but stronger now, concentrated, as if the smell had been waiting years for someone to notice it. The hallway wallpaper was the same faded roses. The coat rack still held Grandma's wool coat, though Grandma had been dead four years, and beside it hung a second coat that stopped Hazel's breath for a moment: a green rain jacket, size medium, hood frayed at the collar.

Wren's jacket. Wren had worn that jacket the last time they'd stood in this hallway together, nine years ago, the day of the fight, the day Hazel had said things about the family, about the *arrangements*, that she'd meant at the time and had spent nine years deciding whether she still meant. Wren had put on that jacket hard, yanking the zipper, and said, *One of us has to stay, Hazel. One of us always has to stay.* And then she'd stayed, and Hazel had gone, and the Sunday calls had gotten shorter and shorter until they weren't calls anymore, just voicemails, just one voicemail, just silence.

"One of us always has to stay."

Hazel stood in front of the jacket and let the sentence finish arriving. Then she made herself walk into the parlor.

The casket was open. Someone had dressed the body in a blue dress Hazel recognized—Grandma's good dress, the one with the covered buttons—and folded the hands over a sprig of dried lavender. The face was composed, powdered, peaceful. Funeral-home peaceful. And it was Wren's face, of course it was Wren's face, the same face Hazel shaved in the mirror every morning, the same narrow jaw, the same left eyebrow with the scar through it from the branch when they were seven—

Except the scar was on the right eyebrow.

Hazel leaned closer. The room went quiet around her, the way rooms do when you stop hearing them. She looked at the scar. She looked at her own reflection, faint and ghostly in the casket's polished lid above her sister's face, and checked her own left eyebrow, and the scar was there, where it had always been, where it had been every day of forty-five years.

Twins mirror each other sometimes. Hazel knew that. She'd read it in a magazine at nineteen and thought it was the loveliest fact she'd ever learned—that two people could be reflections of each other even before either of them owned a mirror. So maybe the mortician had simply seen the scar and, working from a photo, put it where the photo showed it. Maybe the photo was old. Maybe it was flipped. Maybe.

Maybe the body was hers.

The thought arrived whole, uninvited, wearing her own voice, and she pushed it away the way you push away a hand in the dark—fast, blind, harder than necessary. She stepped back from the casket. Her hip caught the trestle and the whole arrangement rocked, and the body's folded hands shifted a quarter inch, and Hazel apologized aloud—to a corpse, to her sister, to whoever was in there—and fled to the kitchen.

* * *

Aunt Maren was at the sink, running water over dishes that were already clean. She was seventy-one and looked older tonight, hair pinned up carelessly, cardigan buttoned wrong, and she did not turn around when Hazel came in.

"The plate is wrong," Hazel said.

"I know."

"Then fix it."

"I can't." Maren shut off the water. In the sudden quiet, Hazel could hear the house settling, or not settling—could hear a sound from upstairs like a chair being drawn back across a floor, once, unhurried. Maren heard it too. Her shoulders climbed toward her ears and came down again by force of will. "There's no fixing it, honey. There's only what's written."

"What does that mean? Maren. Look at me. Whose body is in my grandmother's parlor?"

Her aunt turned then, and her face was terrible—not cruel, not cold, but *tired*, tired in the marrow, the face of a woman who had been carrying a bucket up a hill for fifty years and had finally been told the hill had no top. She looked at Hazel the way you look at someone you love and cannot help.

"You tell me," Maren said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one there is." She wiped her hands on a dish towel, slowly, each finger separately, buying time. "You girls swapped those names like other kids swap gum. Since you were six years old. School records, doctor's offices, that year you both answered to Birdie—I spent half my life not knowing which one of you I was talking to, and after a while I stopped asking, because asking never changed anything. The names went back and forth. Everything else went back and forth. Your grandmother used to say—" Maren stopped. She pressed the dish towel against her mouth for a moment, and when she took it away her voice was steadier. "Your grandmother used to say the ledger knows. The paper remembers what people forget. And the paper says Hazel Marie Eriksen died on the fourth of October, in that house, alone."

"Then the paper is wrong."

"Probably." Maren folded the towel. Set it down. Picked it up again. "Here's what I need you to hear, and then I'm going to say nothing else tonight, because I said too much already and saying things is how trouble starts in this family. Tomorrow morning, before anyone else comes, you look in that casket, and you decide who you buried. You decide it, and you sign the papers, and you don't ask me, and you don't ask the house, and you especially—" her voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture, "—you especially don't ask *her*, if she'll talk to you, because she always liked you best and she will lie to you sweetly, and you'll believe her, and then it won't matter which twin died. Do you understand me?"

"No," Hazel said. "None of that. What do you mean, *if she'll talk to me*? Maren, she's dead. Dead people don't talk."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the light through the lace curtains had gone the color of weak tea, though Hazel could have sworn it was barely past noon when she'd arrived. Somewhere upstairs, again, softly: a chair being drawn back. Closer to the stairs this time. Or maybe the same distance, and the house was simply bigger than it had been that morning.

"Stay the night if you have to," Maren said quietly. "Family stays. But leave before the reading of the will. Whatever you decide about the casket, whatever you sign—leave before the lawyer comes. Promise me that much and I'll never bother you again."

"And if I don't promise?"

Her aunt smiled then, and it was the first time Hazel had ever seen Maren Eriksen smile without any warmth in it at all, a smile that looked borrowed, that looked like it belonged to a different face entirely, and she said:

"Then welcome home, Hazel. Or Wren. Whoever's left."

She took her keys off the hook by the door. She did not take her coat. At the threshold she paused, backlit by the strange tea-colored afternoon, and said, almost too low to hear: "It's already started noticing you. I can tell because the chairs are back."

Then she was gone, and her car crunched away down the gravel, faster than a woman her age should drive, and Hazel stood alone in her grandmother's kitchen with the water dripping from the tap—dripping in a rhythm, she realized, that was almost exactly the rhythm of a clock, except there was no clock in the kitchen, and there hadn't been one for years.

Upstairs, the chair scraped again. Patient. Expectant. As if someone had been setting a place at a table for a very long time, and had finally heard footsteps on the road.

Hazel looked down at her own hands, at the identical hands folded in the parlor over dried lavender, and understood that she had come here to bury a question, and the question had gotten here first, and it had made up the guest bed.

She went to find her camera. If she couldn't trust her eyes, she would document everything—every room, every hour, every wrong thing. Paper remembered. Maren had said so herself.

Paper was going to have to be enough.
