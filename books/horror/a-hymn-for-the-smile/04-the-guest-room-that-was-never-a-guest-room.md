## 4. The Guest Room That Was Never a Guest Room

The key was in the kitchen drawer, under the takeout menus and a dead flashlight and a twist of rubber bands gone brittle as cicada shells. Hazel found it at nine in the morning, on her second day in the house, while she was looking for batteries.

It was small. Brass, unpolished, the teeth worn soft. A paper tag was still knotted to its bow with string so old it had turned the color of tea, and on the tag, in her grandmother's upright hand: *UPSTAIRS — DO NOT.*

No room name. No explanation. Just the dash, and then the prohibition, as if the word after it had been too dangerous even to write.

Hazel stood in the kitchen holding it for a long time. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

She had photographed the whole ground floor yesterday — every room, every closet, the pantry with its shelves of preserves labeled in decades she'd never known her grandmother could count to. The ritual held. The ritual always held. Point the camera, press the button, and the world agreed to stay where she had put it. But when she'd climbed halfway up the stairs last night with the camera raised, something in her chest had closed like a fist, and she'd gone back down without taking a single frame of the second floor.

Now she had a key that said *DO NOT*, and the fist in her chest said the same thing, and both of them were wrong, because Wren was dead, and dead sisters did not get to have locked rooms in their honor.

Hazel filled the kettle. She didn't want tea. She just wanted her hands to do something ordinary while the rest of her argued.

*You're being ridiculous,* she told herself. *It's a spare bedroom. Grandma was a hoarder of the emotional kind; half this house is sealed out of sentiment, not shame.*

But the tag didn't say *WREN'S ROOM — DO NOT.* It didn't say anything personal at all. And there was another thing, a small thing, the kind of thing Hazel's mind collected the way other people collected stamps: the dust on the stair rail yesterday had been thick enough to write in, and no footprints had broken it.

Not hers. Not anyone's. The second floor of this house had not been walked in a long time.

So why did the key live in the kitchen drawer? Why did the key live anywhere but in the lock?

Hazel poured the water over the tea bag without watching it steep. She drank it standing at the window, looking out at the field where the grass grew in waves around a line of fence posts that went nowhere, and she made herself finish the cup before she let herself decide.

Then she picked up the camera, checked the battery, and went upstairs.

* * *

The hallway at the top of the stairs ran the length of the house, four doors on each side. She photographed them all first — the bathroom, the linen closet, the room that had been her mother's, the room that had been nobody's. The shutter clicked. The house stayed put. Good.

Three doors were unlocked. The fourth, at the far end, past the window where the morning light came in long and gray through curtains the color of old milk — that door had a keyhole with no key in it, and a brass plate scratched nearly smooth by a century of hands.

Hazel put her palm flat against the wood.

Cold. Not cool-the-way-old-houses-are cold. Cold like a stone you find in shade in July, cold that has decided to be cold, cold with an opinion about it.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud was another part of the ritual, the part where she narrated herself back into being a person who did normal things. "Okay. It's a room."

The key went in stiffly, then all at once, the tumblers giving way with a sound like a swallowed word. The door swung inward without a creak, which was somehow worse than a creak. Old doors should protest. This one opened the way a throat opens.

And the smell hit her first: lavender, but wrong, lavender gone to vinegar, the smell of sachets that had done their work decades ago and now only preserved the memory of doing it. Underneath that, something plainer. Clean cotton. And underneath *that* — faint, almost polite, like a guest who has taken off his shoes — the smell of a man's cologne.

Hazel stepped inside and raised the camera before she let herself look at anything else. Click. Click. The flash stuttered against the gray light.

A bed. Made. Not made-recently-made, not hospital-cornered-by-a-housekeeper made. Made the way a bed is made by someone who makes it every single day and has for years: sheets pulled taut with practiced economy, blanket folded back in a precise triangle, pillow centered with a hand-span's tolerance. The quilt was handmade, patchwork, sun-faded to ghosts of colors, and Hazel knew it. She knew it the way you know a face from a photograph you saw once as a child. It had been on her grandmother's bed. In the photographs from the fifties, the sixties, the ones Maren kept in a shoebox — that quilt, those same faded squares, on the bed in the master bedroom downstairs.

Someone had moved it up here. Someone had made this bed with it, carefully, recently, and then left.

Click.

A chair. Straight-backed, wooden, positioned two feet from the foot of the bed but angled — angled precisely — to face the door. Not the window. Not the bed. The door. Whoever sat in that chair would sit facing whoever came in, and they would see them the moment the door opened, and they would have time to arrange their face.

There was a small table beside the chair. On the table: a glass of water, full. A book, closed, spine cracked white with reading. And a pair of men's shoes, polished, set side by side with their toes pointing at the door.

Hazel's mouth had gone dry. She became aware of her own breathing, loud in the lavender hush, and made herself slow it down. Ritual. Ritual was the spine she built when everything else wanted to fold. Photograph it. Document it. What is written down cannot pretend it never happened.

She crossed to the nightstand beside the bed. A lamp with a frayed cord. An alarm clock, wind-up, its hands stopped at 4:44. A Bible — no. She opened it. Not a Bible. A hymnal. Her grandmother's name embossed in gold on the cover, worn to brass. She flipped it open to the ribbon marker and found the page: a hymn she didn't recognize, in a minor key, the notes climbing in a way that made her hum involuntarily, three bars of it, before she caught herself and stopped.

She had the sudden, total conviction that she had heard this song before. Sung low, off-key, from the kitchen doorway, when she was very small and supposed to be asleep. Her grandmother's voice, and beneath it — harmonizing, or mocking, or simply keeping time — a second voice she had assumed was the radio.

Hazel closed the hymnal and set it down exactly as she'd found it, ribbon in place, cover aligned with the edge of the nightstand. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Inside she was a kettle coming to boil, but her hands had learned, somewhere over the years of photographing everything, to be instruments separate from the rest of her.

Click. The bed. Click. The chair. Click. The shoes.

Then she looked at the floor, and stopped clicking.

The room's floorboards wore a skin of dust — fine, gray, undisturbed except along one path. Footprints. Bare feet, or socked feet, narrow, medium height by the stride. They led from the bedside — from the spot where a sleeper would swing his legs out and stand — across the room, past the chair, to the door. To the exact spot where Hazel was standing now.

They led out. There were no prints leading in.

The dust everywhere else lay smooth as fresh snow. No prints at the window. No prints at the closet. Whoever had made that bed, filled that glass, placed those shoes toe-out toward the door — they had appeared at the bedside and walked out, and nothing in this room remembered them arriving.

Hazel stood very still in the doorway, on the threshold, careful not to scuff the evidence, and felt the violation arrive in her body before her mind could dress it up in reasonable explanations. This was what it felt like, she thought. This was what burglary felt like, what infidelity felt like — not the loss of things but the discovery that a stranger had been intimate with your life while you slept. The bed was made. The glass was full. The shoes waited by the door like a man waiting for his hostess to see him out.

The house had been expecting someone.

And the someone had already been here — or was here now, elsewhere, walking the halls on socked feet while she photographed empty rooms downstairs and called it coping.

"Wren?" she said.

Her own voice sounded wrong in the room. Small. The lavender took it and gave nothing back.

She hadn't meant to say the name. She'd meant to say *hello*, or *anyone here*, or nothing at all. But grief is a hand that grabs the wheel, and Wren's name came out of her like a hiccup, and the moment it left her lips Hazel understood she had made a mistake, though she couldn't have said how she knew.

The temperature in the room did not drop. The lights did not flicker. Nothing happened at all, and the nothing happened with such completeness, such attention, that Hazel's skin crawled from her wrists to her shoulders. It was the silence of a room full of people who have just heard their name called and are deciding whether to answer.

She backed out. Closed the door. Locked it. Stood with her forehead against the wood, listening to her heart, counting the beats the way she'd counted them in hospital waiting rooms, in funeral homes, in every antechamber of the last six days.

*You're being ridiculous.*

But her hands, her faithful instrument hands, were shaking now, and the camera strap had bitten a groove into her neck she could feel like a brand.

* * *

Downstairs, she developed the film.

This was the part of the ritual no one understood — why she still shot film, why she kept the little darkroom kit in the trunk of her car, why she needed the images to come up out of the chemical bath like things surfacing from deep water. Digital was instant and instant was a lie. Film made you wait. Film made the image a thing that happened to you rather than a thing you took. And when the world went strange — and the world had been going strange since the funeral home, since the casket, since the certificate with her own name printed on it in a clerk's indifferent font — the photograph was the only witness she trusted.

She worked in the bathroom with towels jammed under the door, red safelight turning the world the color of a wound. Sheets of negatives hung to dry. Then the prints, one by one, sliding into the tray, blank white faces blooming gray, gray blooming into the rooms she had walked that morning.

The hallway. Correct. Four doors, gray light, the milk-colored curtains.

Her mother's room. Correct. Bed, dresser, water stain on the ceiling shaped like a country with no name.

The linen closet. Correct.

The guest room.

Hazel lifted the print out of the fix bath with tongs and held it up to the safelight, and her stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

The room was in the photograph. The bed, the quilt, the nightstand, the hymnal, the shoes. All correct. All exactly as she had seen them.

Except the chair.

In the photograph, the straight-backed wooden chair did not face the door. It faced the bed. And sitting in it — leaning forward, elbows on knees, hands loosely clasped, head bowed as if in prayer or patience or the long practice of waiting — was a figure.

He was rendered in the soft grain of the print, half shadow, and she could not make out his face. He wore a suit. His posture was courteous. His shoes — she checked, her breath snagging — his shoes were off, set neatly beside the chair, toes pointing at the door.

He was not in the room she had photographed. She was sure of it. She had stood in that doorway, she had swept the space with her eyes and her lens, and the chair had been empty, and the floor had been innocent of footprints except the ones leading out.

The figure in the print lifted his head.

Hazel knew — she *knew* — that this was impossible, that a negative recorded light and nothing else, that the man had been there or he hadn't, that photographs did not change. But the longer she held the print under the red light, the more she had the sense of the figure becoming aware of being looked at, the way you feel eyes on the back of your neck on an empty street. The bowed head seemed less bowed. The clasped hands seemed about to unclasp. Any second now he would look up, and she would see his face, and some animal ledger deep in her body screamed at her not to, the way it screams at you not to look over the edge—

She dropped the print face-down on the towel.

Stood there breathing. Counting. One beat, two, ten, twenty.

When she finally turned it over, the figure's head was bowed again, patient as furniture, and Hazel could not swear it had ever moved, and that was somehow worse — the gaslight of it, the way the house was teaching her to distrust the one instrument she had left.

She laid the print flat and studied it with the cold thoroughness she used for editing, hunting details, because details were facts and facts were rope thrown to a drowning woman. The suit: dark, old-fashioned cut, the kind sold to men who expected clothes to outlive them. The hands: long, clean, resting. The hair: silver, combed with care.

And on the little finger of the right hand, catching the gray window-light in the photograph, a ring. Signet-style. She leaned close, close enough that her nose nearly touched the wet emulsion, and squinted until the shape resolved.

An initial. A single letter, engraved deep enough to read even in shadow.

*V.*

Hazel stood in the red dark of the bathroom, in her dead grandmother's house, above the room with the made bed and the full glass of water, and she thought about the estate letter she'd received three weeks ago, the one from the law firm handling her grandmother's affairs. She thought about the appraiser the firm had mentioned, the one who would be coming to value the property before the reading of the will.

She couldn't remember his name. She was almost sure she'd never heard it.

But tomorrow was Tuesday, and the letter had said Tuesday, and outside the bathroom door the house creaked once, softly, at the top of the stairs — the sound of weight settling onto a floorboard, the sound of a man finding his seat.

Hazel turned off the safelight and stood in the black, holding the photograph of the man who was waiting for her to invite him in.
