## 6. The Ledger Under the Floorboards

Hazel didn't sleep so much as hold still for six hours.

She lay in her childhood bed with the lamp on and the photographs stacked face-down on the nightstand like a deck she refused to draw from, and when the gray light finally came through the curtains she got up, dressed, and made coffee she had no intention of drinking. The house was quiet in the way houses are quiet after a funeral—everyone gone, everyone relieved to be gone—and she stood at the kitchen window watching frost burn off the fields and thought about chairs.

One extra chair. In every photograph. In every room.

Her hands were steady. That was the strange part. Her whole life, whenever the world went wrong, her hands went steady, as if some older part of her understood that panic was a luxury and documentation was not. Wren used to tease her about it. *You'd photograph your own funeral, Haze.* And Hazel would say, *Somebody has to keep the record,* and they'd both laugh, because it was a joke then.

She rinsed the mug, set it upside down on the rack, and went upstairs to her grandmother's room.

* * *

Edith Eriksen's bedroom had been sealed since the hospice nurse carried out the last box in March. The door stuck the way it always had, swollen in its frame, and Hazel put her shoulder into it the way she had at nine years old, sneaking in to read grandma's romance novels while Wren kept watch on the stairs. The latch gave. Cold air moved past her, out of the room, like something exhaling through the gap before she could enter.

The bed was still made. Hospital corners, quilt folded back at a precise angle, the way Edith had made every bed of her life—including, presumably, the one she died in. On the nightstand: a glass ring, a Bible, a tin of peppermints gone soft with age. Lace curtains. A dresser with a doily under a hairbrush that still held three long silver hairs.

Hazel photographed all of it. Not because she expected another chair—though she checked the developed image twice, and there was no chair, which somehow was worse, because it meant the thing in the pictures chose its rooms—but because the ritual was load-bearing now. She raised the camera, framed, clicked. Frame, click. The shutter sound filled the room and made it hers.

Then she knelt to look under the bed, and that was when she saw the floorboard.

It was the second board from the wall, beneath the left side of the bed, and it sat wrong. Not loose—*newer*. Someone had pried it up and replaced it, and the replacement had been stained to match but never quite matched, and in forty years of dust the seam around it stayed clean, wiped down again and again by a hand that knew what it was guarding. Grandma's hand. Edith, ninety-one years old and dying, still wiping down a floorboard.

Hazel lay flat on the cold boards and pressed her cheek to the floor and looked into the dark gap beneath the bed frame, and she thought: *I am going to lift this, and I don't know what happens after I lift this.* She thought it very clearly, the way you think things at the edge of a high place.

Then she went downstairs and got the crowbar from the cellar, because somebody has to keep the record.

* * *

The board came up with a groan and a smell—camphor, paper, and underneath that something sweeter and older, like flowers kept too long in a closed book. There was a cavity between the joists, lined with waxed cloth, and inside the cloth:

A ledger.

Not a notebook. A ledger—bound in green leather gone black at the corners, brass clasps, the kind of book meant to outlast the person writing in it. It was heavy. It was dry. Whatever else the house had been doing to itself, it had kept this dry.

Hazel carried it to the kitchen table, laid it in the good light from the east window, and opened it.

The first page was dated June 3rd, 1911.

*Received of Aldous Eriksen, one favor rendered. Terms as spoken. Balance deferred.*

Below that, a signature she didn't recognize—a looping, patient script that seemed to take up more of the page than ink should occupy. Below *that*, in different handwriting, firmer, Edith's mother maybe: *Paid in full: two teeth, one eye's sight (left), the winter of 1919.*

Hazel turned the page.

It was an account book. That was the only word for it. Dates down the left margin, names in a second column, and then a third column headed with a single word, written over and over across decades in four or five different hands, sometimes careful, sometimes shaking:

**Smiles.**

*March 1908 — Aldous E. — smiles: 1 (the child's).*

*October 1922 — Vera E. — smiles: 4 (two hers, two borrowed).*

*January 1931 — Vera E. — smiles: 0. NOTE: he waited. He is patient. Do not let him wait.*

Hazel read standing up, because sitting felt too much like settling in. The entries marched forward through the century, through droughts and wars and the flu, and the currency never changed. Not money. Not blood, except once, and that entry was crossed out so hard the pen had torn the page. Smiles. Always smiles. Some entries recorded them like withdrawals—*smiles: 6, taken at the church social*—and some like payments, and it took Hazel three pages to understand that there was no difference. A smile given to the thing and a smile taken from you were the same transaction viewed from opposite sides of the table.

And every few pages, a new name took up the pen. Aldous. Vera. Then a gap, war years, the entries thinning like a pulse. Then Edith's hand, young and upright:

*June 1951 — Edith E. — assumed balance of my mother. He showed me the arithmetic. I signed.*

He. The thing had a pronoun before it had a name. Or maybe the pronoun was the name. Hazel's skin crawled in a slow wave, scalp to heels, and she noticed distantly that she had begun photographing the pages—one per spread, methodical, the shutter clicking in the silent kitchen like a metronome keeping time with her own pulse. Good. Keep the record. If the house could rewrite photographs, let it try to rewrite four hundred frames stored on a card in her pocket.

She turned to the 1960s and found the entries changing character. Less accounting. More confession.

*April 1963 — He came to the hospital the night the twins were born. Stood at the end of the hall. Did not approach. Marked nothing in his book. I have not stopped shaking.*

Twins. Hazel's finger stopped on the page. Her mother's twins—no. Her mother had one child. But Edith had—

No. Edith had no children either; Maren was her sister's girl, adopted after the car accident. So whose twins—

*August 1968 — Signed the renewal. He asked for "the twin born under the wrong star" and I told him there was no such child and he smiled and said there would be. God forgive me. There will be.*

Hazel set the ledger down.

The kitchen clock ticked. Frost was melting off the window glass in long wet lines, like the pane was crying from the outside in. She thought about the death certificate upstairs in the funeral director's folder, the one bearing her own name, the one nobody would correct. She thought about Wren's last voicemail, played eleven times now, worn smooth as a river stone: *It smiled at me through the window and it had your face.*

There would be. He had known in 1968 that there would be twins. He had known before Hazel existed, before her mother existed, that somewhere down the line the family would produce two girls born close enough to be mistaken for each other, and he had written the terms in advance, and Edith had signed anyway, because the alternative was written on the torn-out pages and in the crossed-out lines and in the gaps where whole years went missing from the book.

This wasn't a haunting. Hauntings happened *to* people. This was a subscription. Three generations of Eriksens, paying their premiums in smiles, deferring the balance, passing the account down like the farm itself—like the land, the house, the name. An inheritance. The most honest inheritance the family had, because at least this one was recorded.

Hazel picked the ledger back up and read until the light moved across the table and her tea went cold in its untouched mug.

* * *

The later entries were Edith's alone, and they deteriorated the way all confessions deteriorate: from formal to frantic to something almost tender.

*1987 — The girls are born. Two of them, exactly as promised. I named them myself. Hazel and Wren. I gave Wren the wrong-star name and prayed the arithmetic wouldn't notice that twins are one soul split for shipping.*

Hazel read that line four times. *One soul split for shipping.* She looked at her own hands on the ledger's edge—Wren's hands, identical, the same scar on the right thumb from the same broken jar—and felt the floor tilt gently under the kitchen.

*1994 — He visited as a man selling insurance. Sat in this kitchen. The girls were seven. He watched them through the window for an hour and wrote nothing. I paid him twelve smiles from the neighbors' children to make him leave. He thanked me. He said patience was his favorite virtue.*

*2003 — The girls swapped identities at school again. Third time. Teachers can't tell them apart. Neither can I, some mornings. Neither can HE, I think, and this may be the only mercy we have been given. Confusion in the account. Keep the confusion. Never let them learn which twin is which.*

Hazel's breath went shallow.

They had swapped identities their whole lives—not just at school. Licenses, forms, introductions at parties. It had started as a game and hardened into a habit neither of them examined, a private language of being interchangeable. Wren had joked once, drunk on cooking sherry at nineteen: *Maybe we did it so thoroughly even God lost track.* And Hazel had laughed.

But it hadn't been a game. It had been camouflage. Somewhere in this house, in this book, in Edith's long vigil, the sisters' interchangeability had been understood as armor—the one defense against a contract that specified a single named twin. Every swap muddied the ledger. Every borrowed name starved the arithmetic.

And then Hazel had left. Taken the job in the city, kept her name clean and separate, built a life where no one confused her with anyone. And Wren had stayed near the farm, visiting Edith, answering the phone, being the twin who remained legible. Who remained *findable*.

*Oh, Wren.*

The last entries were recent. Edith's hand had shrunk to a cramped, ancient scrawl.

*2021 — Wren came to stay for the winter. He stood at the fence line every dusk. She saw him. She waved at him, God help her, she WAVED, and he bowed. I told her never to wave. I told her never to smile at anything she couldn't name.*

*2023 — She asked me what the ledger was. I lied. She is smarter than I was. She found the guest room.*

*Last entry — March — He has chosen. He says the account has run too long and confusion is no longer credit. He says the twin who kept the name will settle it. I begged. I offered everything. He said, kindly, the way you'd refuse a child asking for candy before dinner: "You misunderstand, Edith. I am not taking. I am collecting what was promised."*

The entry ended there. No closing line. Just the pen pressed hard enough to tear, trailing off the ruled line, and then blankness, and then the back cover.

Hazel sat down. Her legs simply declined further service, and she sat in Edith's kitchen chair with a hundred-year-old debt open in front of her, and she did the math she'd been avoiding since the funeral home.

*The twin who kept the name.*

Wren had kept the name. Wren had been Wren, publicly, legally, all her life—except. Except the death certificate. Except the casket label. Except the funeral director's embarrassed silence and Maren's refusal to look at the paperwork. The certificate bore *Hazel's* name.

Which meant that somewhere in the last months, in some filing cabinet or hospital form or signature on a release, the names had crossed one final time. Wren had died holding Hazel's name in her mouth—or Hazel's name had died wearing Wren's body—and the ledger's arithmetic, whatever it actually was, had finally gotten a number it liked.

Or worse. Far worse, in a register Hazel couldn't yet articulate: maybe the swap at the end hadn't been an accident at all. Maybe Wren, who found the guest room, who found the ledger, who was smarter than Edith—maybe Wren had done it *on purpose*. Had signed herself into her sister's name the way you throw yourself on a grenade. Had spent her last act making sure the thing that came collecting took the right body and the wrong name, hoping the mismatch would jam the machine.

It hadn't jammed it. But it might have bought something. Time. A season. One funeral's worth of grace.

Hazel pressed both palms flat against the ledger pages, over the column of smiles, over a century of small bright payments, and felt the paper warm slightly under her hands, or imagined she did.

"You're not getting her," she said aloud, to the kitchen, to the book, to whatever kept its accounts in the walls. "Whatever you think you collected—you did the arithmetic wrong."

The house said nothing. Houses that answer are worse than houses that don't, and this one only breathed, the old timbers ticking as the morning warmed, patient as a creditor.

Hazel took out her camera and photographed the ledger cover, spine, clasp. Then she went to the junk drawer, found tape and a freezer bag, sealed the ledger inside it, and hid it in the one place she already knew the house couldn't touch: strapped flat against her own body, under her clothes, against her skin. If the photographs could rearrange themselves, fine. Let the house try to rearrange a document taped to the creditor's chest.

She was washing her hands at the sink—ink and camphor and that sweet dead-flower smell—when the phone rang.

The landline. The farmhouse landline, plugged into the wall since 1974, the number listed nowhere, the phone that had rung twice yesterday during the funeral and stopped when she approached.

She looked at it. It rang. Caller ID glowed amber: **VOSS, R.**

Hazel dried her hands slowly, watching the screen, and remembered the marginal note she'd glimpsed in Edith's younger handwriting, tucked sideways in the 1950s pages like a recipe: *Never give him your name first. He must ask. Make him ask.*

The phone rang a fourth time. A fifth.

On the sixth ring, smiling without meaning to—she caught herself doing it, horrified, and stopped—Hazel picked up the receiver and said nothing at all.
