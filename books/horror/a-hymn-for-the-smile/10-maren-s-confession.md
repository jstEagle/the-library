## 10. Maren's Confession

Maren had promised herself she would never come back to this house after dark, and here she was at nine in the evening, three sheets to the wind, standing on the porch with a bottle of rye in one hand and her dead sister's spare key in the other, hating herself for both.

The key still turned. That was the worst part. Forty years and the lock hadn't rusted, hadn't stuck, hadn't done anything so ordinary as fail. It opened the way it had always opened—smoothly, eagerly, like a mouth.

Inside, the hallway light was on. Hazel was awake. Of course Hazel was awake; Maren doubted the girl had slept since she'd arrived, since the funeral, since the moment she'd seen that casket card with her own name printed on it in careful serif type. Maren had seen the card too. She'd stood at the back of the funeral home with her coat already on, watching her niece's face go through the stages—confusion, then arithmetic, then something colder underneath—and she had thought: *she sees it now. God help her, she sees it.*

Maren set the bottle on the kitchen table before she took off her coat, because if she put the coat down first she might lose her nerve, and the nerve was the only thing keeping her vertical.

Hazel appeared in the doorway in a flannel shirt two sizes too big—Wren's, probably—and looked at the bottle, then at her aunt, and said nothing. That was another thing about the Eriksen women. They could fill a room with silence until it pressed against your eardrums like water.

"I brought my own," Maren said, tapping the bottle. "I'm not going to drink yours."

"I don't have any."

"You do. Bottom cabinet, behind the flour. Mom kept a bottle there for sixty years and never touched it. I used to think it was for guests." Maren laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, wet at the edges. "It wasn't for guests."

Hazel pulled out a chair. Sat down across from her. Waited.

That patience was new. The Hazel Maren remembered from Thanksgivings and funerals past had been all edges, quick to bristle, quicker to leave. But grief had planed her down somehow, or maybe whatever lived in this house had, and what was left sitting across the table was someone who understood that some stories can't be hurried.

"Say it," Hazel said quietly. "Whatever you drove out here to say. Say it while you've still got the courage in you. It burns off fast."

"It does," Maren agreed. "It really does." She unscrewed the cap. Her hands were shaking—not the pleasant tremor of drink but the other kind, the kind that starts in the sternum. "I need you to understand something first. I am not a good person. I want that on the record before anything else comes out of my mouth. Whatever you decide about me after tonight, decide it knowing that."

"I already decided about you years ago," Hazel said. "You left me and Wren alone with her. With Grandma. Every summer. You knew something was wrong in this house and you shipped us here anyway because it was free childcare."

The words landed where they were aimed. Maren took a long pull of rye and let them sit.

"Yes," she said. "I did. And I told myself it was fine because nothing ever happened to you girls. Nothing *visible*. And I told myself that was the same as nothing at all." She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. "So. You found the ledger."

It wasn't a question. Hazel's face did the small involuntary thing—the flicker toward the ceiling, toward the upstairs bedroom—that confirmed everything.

"Under the floorboards. Under her bed."

"Under the floorboards," Maren echoed. "Three generations. Dates, names, payments. And that last column." She couldn't say the word yet. She drank instead, and the rye went down like a lit match, and she thought: *now or never, Mare. Now or you'll drive away and never come back and this girl will die in this house the way Wren died, not knowing, and you will have done it twice.*

"The column says *smiles*," Hazel prompted.

"It does." Maren set the bottle down with exaggerated care, the way drunks do when they're trying to prove they're not drunk. "And I'm going to tell you what that means, and you're going to want to throw me out, and I'd deserve it, but I need you to hear all of it first. All of it. Can you do that? Can you just—let me finish?"

Hazel nodded once.

* * *

Maren started, as her mother had started, with 1968.

"You know Grandma almost died giving birth to your mother. The twins' mother—my sister Cora. Placental something-or-other, they didn't have the words for it then, they just said she was bleeding out and the doctor lived forty minutes away and it was February and the roads were ice." Maren's voice had gone flat, recitative. This was a story she'd been told so many times it had worn grooves in her, and she spoke along the grooves. "Grandma was twenty-six years old, lying on that bed upstairs, and she made a bargain. Not with the devil—forget everything you've read about devils, they're all wrong, they're all too dramatic. She made it with what was already here. What had always been here. Something old that lives in the land under this house, or in the house itself, or—I don't know. Nobody knows. It doesn't explain itself. It *negotiates*."

"And the price," Hazel said. Not a question either. They were both speaking in statements tonight, laying facts on the table like cards.

"A life granted for a life owed. That's how she wrote it. But it's never that clean. It granted her life, and Cora's life, and it waited. It's very patient. It waited eleven years until—" Maren stopped. Took a breath that shuddered going in. "Until the twins were born."

"Wren and me."

"No." Maren shook her head slowly. "Not you and Wren. Before you. There were twins before you. 1979. My cousins—well. On paper they never existed. Grandma was pregnant that spring, everyone said she was too old, and by fall there was no baby and no pregnancy and nobody talked about it, and I was fourteen and I asked my mother and she slapped me across the face so hard I saw stars. That was the last time anyone in this family said the word *twins* out loud until you two were born."

The clock in the hall ticked. Maren noticed, distantly, that it sounded slower than clocks should sound. Everything in this house ran a half-beat behind the world, like a film with the sync drifting.

"When you and Wren were born, I was thirty-one. I had Bethany already—she was four." Maren's hand found the bottle again. "And I want you to know that I loved her. I loved her more than breathing. That's the part people get wrong about these stories. They think the monster takes children from mothers who don't love them enough. It's the opposite. It wants the ones who are loved. Love is—it's currency, Hazel. It's the whole denomination. A child nobody wanted isn't worth anything to it."

Hazel had gone very still. The kind of stillness Maren recognized from photographs in the ledger—from the descriptions her grandmother had given once, late, drunk, weeping into her hands: *it likes them quiet, it likes them listening.*

"What did you do?" Hazel asked. Softly. Almost kindly. That kindness was worse than anger.

"In 1989, Bethany was fourteen. Your mother's twins—you and Wren—were seven, and Grandma had started having her spells, her confusions, and there was talk—there was always talk—that the debt might pass down, that it might come due out of order, that the thing might take interest early." Maren heard her own voice speed up, the way it did when she approached this part, the way a car speeds up approaching an accident it can't look away from. "And I thought—if it's going to take someone, let it take from me. Let it be mine. I'll pay the principal myself and the rest of you go free. That's what I told myself. That it was noble. That I was volunteering."

She stopped. The kitchen was so quiet she could hear the house settling around them, or not settling—*breathing*, the walls moving a fraction of an inch, in and out, slow as sleep.

"But it refused her," Hazel said.

Maren laughed—a horrible sound, a bark with nothing behind it. "It refused her. I did everything right. I followed every instruction in the ledger margins, I wrote the offer in my own hand, I signed it, I offered up my own daughter, my beautiful girl, freely given, and it—" Her voice cracked clean through. "It sent her back. That's the part. It didn't just refuse. It *sent her back*. She walked in our front door at six in the morning, barefoot, in her nightgown, in November, and she was fine, physically fine, not a mark on her, and she smiled at me—"

Maren's hands were flat on the table now, pressing down, as if the table might buckle.

"—and it was the wrong smile. It was a smile wearing my daughter's face. And she sat down at our table and ate her breakfast and told me, in a voice like a customer service representative, that the account was not in arrears, that the terms specified *twins born under the wrong star*, and that a single child, however generously offered, constituted an incomplete payment and would be returned to sender. And then she blinked, and she was Bethany again, and she didn't remember any of it. Not the three days she was gone. Not the walk home. Nothing."

"Oh my God," Hazel whispered.

"She slept for two days when it happened. Then she woke up hungry and asked why I was crying." Maren picked up the bottle. Put it down without drinking. Her stomach had passed beyond alcohol into some purer territory of nausea. "I moved us to Duluth that spring. Four hundred miles. I changed the locks, I burned the letters, I never let Bethany visit, never let her near this county, and when she got married I paid for the wedding and made sure it was nowhere within a day's drive. People called me cold. My own mother called me a coward to my face at Christmas, 1991, in front of everyone." Maren smiled, and there was nothing in it. "My mother. Who fed it for fifty years. Called *me* the coward."

"You were protecting her."

"I was protecting myself," Maren snapped, and then, immediately, deflating: "Both. Both things are true. That's the trap of it, Hazel. Every person in this family who ever did the math did it selfishly and dressed it up as sacrifice. Grandma called it a bargain. My mother called it tradition. I called it protection. And Wren—" Her voice broke on the name and she let it. "Wren called it what? Did she ever figure it out? Is that why she came back?"

Hazel was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice was carefully level, the levelness of someone holding something heavy with straight arms.

"She left me a voicemail. I haven't been able to listen to all of it yet."

"Don't," Maren said, too fast, too loud. Then, forcing it down: "Don't listen to it in this house. Whatever else you do. Take the phone outside. Past the fence line. Promise me."

"Why?"

"Because it listens through the walls, and Wren's voice is—" Maren stopped herself. Shook her head. "Because I said so. Because I've earned exactly one piece of advice worth taking and that's it. Please."

Hazel studied her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and Maren felt something in her chest unclench a single degree.

* * *

The rye was half gone. Maren didn't remember drinking most of it, which was probably for the best, because the story she still had to tell required her to be as close to sober as she ever got.

"There's one more thing," she said. "The reason I actually came. The reason I broke my own rule about dark." She turned the bottle slowly on the tabletop, watching the label rotate. "When it sent Bethany back. When it spoke through her. There was a moment—at the end, before she blinked back into herself—where it looked at me with her eyes and it said something I've never told another living soul. Not my husband. Not my priest. Nobody."

Hazel leaned forward slightly.

"It said: *the offer is noted. The offeror may amend.*" Maren's throat worked. "Amend. Like a contract clause. Like it was—inviting me. Telling me the door wasn't closed, just locked, and I held a key. For thirty-five years I've told myself I imagined it. Drunk, grieving, hysterical, whatever. But I didn't imagine it. And I've thought about it every single day since. Every day, Hazel. Do you understand what that means? That for thirty-five years, part of me has known there was a version of this where I ended it—ended the debt, closed the account, freed this whole rotten family—and I never once tried to find out what *amend* meant. I ran. I always run."

The kitchen light buzzed overhead. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked—the guest room, the room that was never a guest room—and Maren watched Hazel's eyes track the sound without surprise, without fear even, just a grim sort of bookkeeping, and thought: *she's already further along than any of us ever got.*

"Hazel. Listen to me. If it offered *me* an amendment, it will offer *you* one. That's how it works, that's the whole shape of it—it doesn't just collect, it negotiates, it loves the negotiation, it feeds on the choosing. And when it makes its offer—and it will make it beautifully, reasonably, in a voice you'll want to trust—remember what I'm telling you now. Remember that I sat in this exact chair and chose to run, and my running bought thirty-five years and cost one twin, and the bill has come due anyway. Running is just paying later with interest."

"Then what do I do?" Hazel asked. And there it was—the question, the real one, stripped of all the armor. Maren looked at her grand-niece, this sharp, stubborn, terrified young woman sitting in her grandmother's kitchen at midnight with a ghost's inheritance on her shoulders, and felt something crack open in her chest that wasn't despair for once.

"You do what none of us had the guts to do," Maren said. "You stop treating it like a monster and start treating it like what it actually is. It's a counterparty. It has terms. Terms can be read. Terms can be—" she swallowed— "*amended*. Read everything. Grandma's papers, the church archive, the ledger, all of it. It binds by what's written, not what's said—I know that much, Bethany's voice told me that much. So write, girl. Write like your life depends on it. Because it does."

Hazel held her gaze. Outside, the wind moved through the dead cornfield with a sound like paper turning.

"Thank you," Hazel said finally. "For coming back. For telling me."

"I'm not forgiven," Maren said. It wasn't a question.

"You shipped two little girls to this house every summer because it was free childcare," Hazel said. "You fed your own daughter to it on paper. And then you drove four hundred miles in the dark, drunk, to warn me anyway." She reached across the table and covered Maren's trembling hand with her own steady one. "Yeah. You're forgiven. Go home, Aunt Maren. Sleep. Don't come back until it's over—one way or the other."

Maren stood on legs that barely held her, gathered her coat, and made it as far as the hallway before Hazel's voice stopped her.

"One more thing. You said it sent Bethany back smiling. Wearing her face." Hazel's expression, in the dim hall light, was something Maren had never seen on a living person before—grief and scholarship fused into one hard bright thing. "Last night, my reflection smiled at me. And it was wearing Wren's."

Maren stood frozen in the doorway, the cold night air pouring in around her ankles, and heard herself say, in a voice gone thin as thread:

"Then it's already started collecting. And Hazel—whatever you're planning to write—write it *tonight*."
