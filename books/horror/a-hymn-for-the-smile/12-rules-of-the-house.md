## 12. Rules of the House

The ledger's margins were a graveyard of handwriting.

Grandma's script dominated—small, upright, the penmanship of a woman who'd learned to write on slate. But there were other hands beneath hers, older ones, faded to brown ghosts in the paper. And in the bottom corner of the first page, pressed so hard the nib had nearly torn through, someone had written three lines and boxed them with a ruler:

*Do not answer to your name.*
*Do not accept gifts.*
*Do not look away from the smile.*

No explanation. No exceptions listed. Just the rules, the way you'd write *don't touch the stove* on a note pinned above it—knowledge bought once and never wanted twice.

Hazel sat at the kitchen table with the ledger open, her camera beside it, a pot of coffee going cold. Outside, Tuesday had turned into Wednesday without ceremony. Voss had left at dusk with a tip of his hat and a promise to return—"for the appraisal," he'd said, smiling with exactly the right number of teeth—and the house had exhaled around her like something settling back into sleep.

She didn't have until dawn tomorrow. She might not even have that long. So she would do what she had always done when the world stopped making sense: she would document it. Test it. Write it down.

Her whole life, people had called the photographing a quirk. A coping mechanism, one therapist said gently, after Wren. But it had never been coping. It was verification. If she could prove a thing had happened—if she could hold its evidence in her hand—then it couldn't be revised behind her back. The world was full of people who would tell you your memory was wrong. Paper didn't lie.

Except here, apparently, paper lied constantly.

She turned to a fresh page in her own notebook—the cheap spiral one she'd brought for funeral logistics—and wrote at the top: *RULES. TESTED.* Then, underneath: *Rule 1: Do not answer to your name.*

* * *

The problem with not answering to your name was that everything in this house wanted to use it.

It started small. At 9:14 by the kitchen clock, the floorboard at the top of the stairs creaked, and from somewhere in the walls—not the pipes, she'd checked the pipes—a voice said, soft as dust falling:

"Hazel."

Not Voss's voice. Not anyone's voice. It sounded like her own voice played back at the wrong speed, or like Wren's voice remembered incorrectly by someone who hadn't heard it in years.

Hazel's whole body went rigid. Every instinct she owned leaned toward answering—that reflexive *what?* that lived in the spine, older than language. Someone says your name; you turn. That's what names were *for*.

She bit down on it. Kept her eyes on the ledger. Counted ten breaths.

"Hazel?" Closer now. From the hallway outside the kitchen door. "Hazel, I'm cold."

Wren's voice. Actually Wren's voice this time, or close enough that grief grabbed Hazel by the throat before suspicion could. Her sister had been dead nine days. Her sister had died in this house. And dead sisters did not get cold, because dead sisters were not—

"Answer me," the voice said, and there was something wrong in it now, a hollowness under the warmth, like a speaker playing a recording of affection. "You used to answer me. You used to come when I called. Even when we weren't speaking. Especially then."

*I know,* Hazel thought, eyes burning. *I know I did.*

She picked up her pen instead. Wrote: *9:16. Voice mimics Wren. Requests response by name. No response given.* Her hand shook so badly the letters came out jagged, but they came out.

The silence stretched. Then the voice changed tactics, and this was the part she'd remember later, the part that made her understand the rule wasn't superstition—it was law.

"Hazel Eriksen," it said, formal now, each syllable placed like a card on a table. "Daughter of Iris. Granddaughter of Astrid. Born second by four minutes and first by every measure that mattered." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down between courses. "If you are not Hazel Eriksen, then who is buried in the churchyard? Who is in the box with the wrong name? Answer, and I will tell you."

And God help her, she almost did. Because that was the question, wasn't it—the one that had been sitting in her chest since the funeral home, since the casket label, since the death certificate with her own name printed in official ink. She wanted the answer more than she wanted air. Whoever spoke through the walls knew. It had to know. It was the only party in this entire rotten arrangement who had read the original contract.

But answering meant claiming the name. And the margin said don't.

She stood up, walked to the sink, and ran the water loud enough to drown out anything else the house wanted to say. When she came back, the hallway was empty and ordinary, and the notebook page had one more line on it, in handwriting that was not hers:

*So stubborn. Like her.*

She photographed the page. In the image, the extra line read: *Good girl.*

* * *

Rule two cost her more, because rule two arrived wearing kindness.

At noon, she found a plate on the kitchen table where no plate had been. Slices of apple cake on her grandmother's good china—the pattern with the blue cornflowers, the set that only came out for Christmas. A fork laid neatly beside it. Steam still rising faintly off the cake, which was impossible, because the oven had been cold since Maren left and there was no flour in the pantry and no apples anywhere in the house.

A note sat propped against the plate, written in grandma's hand: *For Hazel. You've lost weight. Eat.*

Hazel stood over it for a long time.

This was the cruelty of it—she saw that now, saw it with a scholar's clarity even as her stomach cramped with genuine hunger. She hadn't eaten properly in days. Funeral food, gas station food, nothing. And here was apple cake on the good china, warm, made by hands that had been making it for her since she was six years old, when Grandma Astrid would let her lick the spatula while Wren got the bowl because Wren was the polite one and Hazel was the greedy one, and Grandma had laughed and said greedy girls grow up strong.

The fork gleamed. The steam curled. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled, patient as interest accruing.

*Don't accept gifts.*

Was a gift still a gift if you took a bite and paid for it? Was it a gift if the giver was dead? Was it—

Stop. She was negotiating. That was how it worked, she realized; that was the whole architecture of the thing. It didn't break down doors. It waited for you to argue yourself across the line, one reasonable exception at a time, until you'd accepted so many small kindnesses that you owed a large one without ever signing anything.

Hazel took out her phone and photographed the plate. Then she opened the trash can, lifted the china carefully—she wasn't going to break Grandma's dishes, whatever else happened—and scraped the cake into the garbage. The cake went in heavy and wet, heavier than cake should be, and for half a second as it fell she saw that it wasn't cake all the way through. The interior was dark and fibrous, like packed earth, like something that had grown rather than been baked.

She washed the plate. Dried it. Put it back in the cabinet with the others.

When she closed the cabinet door, the kitchen smelled of apples, everywhere, thick and sweet, and the smell did not leave for six hours. She wrote it all down: *12:03. Gift offered via deceased relative's handwriting. Refused. Cost: hunger, plus the knowledge that refusal is noticed.* She hesitated, then added: *Also cost: I wanted it. Log that too. Wanting is probably the actual trap.*

Underneath, in that other handwriting, small and almost fond: *Noted.*

* * *

The third rule was the worst, because the third rule required her to find the smile.

She found it at 4 p.m., in the parlor window.

She'd been carrying boxes of Grandma's things toward the door—Maren had asked her to sort the linens, an absurd domestic errand under the circumstances, but sorting gave her hands work and her hands needed work—and she passed the front window and there it was. Outside. Standing on the lawn in the flat gray light, maybe twenty feet away.

It looked like Mr. Voss. Same gray suit, same courteous posture, hands folded in front of him. But it wasn't Voss, because Voss had left, and because the face was wrong in the way a mask is wrong—features present, expression assembled, but nothing behind it moving. And it was smiling. It had been smiling, she understood immediately, for a very long time. The smile had the worn quality of something held for hours, muscles locked, lips drawn back and fixed, showing teeth that were slightly too even, slightly too many.

Every nerve in her body screamed the same instruction: *look away. Look away now.*

That was the test. That was the whole test. The margin said don't look away, and every animal instinct she possessed said look away, run, hide, put a wall between your face and that thing, and she stood in the parlor holding a box of pillowcases and understood that this was the moment the rules were actually about. Not the easy refusals. This. The moment where obedience to the written word meant overriding the oldest commandment in the body.

She set down the box. Slowly, so slowly, the way you move near a dog you don't trust.

Then she looked at it. Directly. Eyes open, unblinking past the burn.

The smile widened. Not much. A centimeter of additional teeth, a fractional lift at the corners, the way a salesman recalibrates when he realizes the mark isn't walking past. Its head tilted, birdlike, curious. And in the flat light she could see that its eyes—Voss's mild gray eyes—were not reflecting the lawn. They reflected nothing. They were windows painted onto a building nobody lived in.

Her eyes streamed. Her vision blurred. Blinking, she decided, was not looking away. She blinked when she absolutely had to, fast, and kept her gaze nailed to that face, and the staring contest went on for one minute, then five, then ten, her legs trembling, her heart slamming, tears cutting cold tracks down her cheeks, and the thing on the lawn never moved, never blinked at all, never once varied except to keep widening by increments so small she could only measure them in photographs.

Because that was her weapon, and she finally understood why she'd been given it. She raised her phone with shaking hands and took a picture. Then another. Then another, every thirty seconds, timestamped, documented, a deposition conducted in shutter clicks.

At minute fourteen, the thing on the lawn inclined its head—an acknowledgment, almost respectful, the way one professional recognizes another's competence—and stepped backward into the shade of the big maple, and the shade took it, and the lawn was empty.

Hazel's legs gave out. She sat down hard on the parlor floor among the pillowcases and cried—not elegantly, not briefly—and when she was done she wiped her face, retrieved her notebook, and wrote:

*4:00–4:14 p.m. Smile observed, exterior, duration 14 min. Maintained eye contact per Rule 3. Cost: unknown yet. Possibly my nervous system. Possibly more. It acknowledged me. Note: acknowledgment implies I am now a party it deals with, not merely prey. Keep looking at it. Looking may be the only leverage I have.*

She flipped back through the ledger afterward, hunting marginalia, cross-referencing, and found a note in the brown ghost-hand from generations back, squeezed sideways along the edge of a payment record: *it cannot take what is witnessed. witnessed things must be accounted. account for everything.*

Account for everything. She looked at her camera, at the notebook filling page by page, at the ledger with its columns of dates and names and smiles, and felt something shift in her chest—not hope, nothing so generous, but its austere cousin: method. It had rules. Rules could be learned. Learned rules could be gamed. She was the granddaughter of a woman who'd negotiated with a monster across sixty years of ledger entries, and maybe the inheritance wasn't only the debt. Maybe the inheritance was also the literacy.

She ate half a can of cold soup standing at the counter, refusing on principle to enjoy it. She checked the locks. She carried the camera everywhere, even to the bathroom, even to bed, where she lay listening to the house tick and settle around her like a large animal digesting.

At some hour past midnight, drifting, she heard footsteps on the stairs. Slow. Unhurried. They passed her door without pausing and continued to the end of the hall—to the guest room, the room with the chair facing the door—and stopped.

Then, through the wall, clear and conversational, Voss's pleasant voice said:

"You've been very thorough, Miss Eriksen. I do admire thoroughness." A pause, the sound of a man settling comfortably into a chair. "We'll want to discuss terms soon. Do sleep well."

Hazel lay in the dark, heart hammering, and reached for her notebook one more time.

*1:40 a.m. It knows I know the rules. Negotiation phase beginning.*

She fell asleep with the pen still in her hand, and dreamed of mirrors—rows of them, every wall, every surface, all of them showing her face, and all of them smiling.
