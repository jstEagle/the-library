## 3. Children Who Count Themselves Carefully

The rain came in off the moor that Thursday like something with an errand, driving sideways against the schoolroom windows until the panes wept in long diagonal threads. Tamsin had given up trying to teach fractions to eight children who kept glancing at the ninth desk—the empty one by the radiator—and so he set them a page of sums and let the room settle into the soft industry of pencil on paper.

He used the quiet to study them.

It was a habit from his old life, from the comprehensive outside Leeds where he'd learned to read a classroom the way sailors read weather. Posture told you who hadn't slept. Fidgeting told you who was hungry. And the way a child held their hands—open or closed, still or busy—told you almost everything else.

These children held their hands very still.

All eight of them. Even little Bram Halloway, who couldn't have been more than seven and should by rights have been swinging his legs under his chair, sat with his palms flat on the desk as though the wood might otherwise carry him off. They wrote their sums neatly. They looked up when he looked at them, and they looked down again immediately, and not one of them whispered, or passed notes, or did any of the hundred small rebellions that made a classroom breathe.

They were counting themselves, he realized. The way you count coins you're afraid of losing. Or the way you count the people left in a lifeboat.

His chalk hovered over the board. *Three-quarters of twenty-four,* he'd written, and beneath it, without quite deciding to, he added: *and how many are missing?*

He wiped it away before anyone could see.

* * *

Wren stayed behind after the bell.

Not the school bell—that one rang fine, a small hand-pulled thing in the corridor that Odell's caretaker cranked with visible reluctance—but the end-of-lesson shuffle, coats and satchels and the stampede for the door. Eight children filed out into the grey afternoon. Nine children remained: Tamsin at his desk, and Wren Ashcombe standing at the coat pegs with her arms through her sleeves but her coat unbuttoned, watching him with the flat, patient attention of a cat deciding whether the thing on the floor is dead enough to eat.

"Wren," he said. "Everything all right?"

"You looked at the register again." She said it without accusation, the way she might report that it was raining. "Third time today."

Had he? He supposed he had. The register lived in his top drawer now instead of the headmaster's office—he'd asked Odell for his own copy, and Odell had gone a strange colour and produced one within the hour, which itself was information—and he'd taken to consulting it between lessons like a man checking a wound.

"Eleven names," Tamsin said. "Eight desks. I'm a teacher, Wren. Attendance is rather my business."

"Nobody asks about attendance here." She buttoned her coat now, slowly, one brass button at a time. Her fingers were steady. That was the thing he kept noticing about her—she was perhaps eleven, small for it, with a cowlick of dark hair that refused every comb in Hollowmere, and her hands never shook. "You shouldn't either. It's not a rule exactly. It's more like—" she searched, "—like not tapping on the glass at the aquarium."

"Because something might notice?"

"Because it's rude," Wren said, "to the things in the water."

The radiator ticked. Outside, the rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist that made the churchyard look like a photograph left out too long. Tamsin came around the desk and leaned against its edge, deliberately casual, the posture he'd perfected over nine years of coaxing frightened children toward difficult sentences.

"Then help me be polite about it," he said. "You don't have to tell me anything. But if there were a reason a teacher ought to know why three seats sit empty—if it were something that touched the children in this room—I'd want to hear it from someone brave enough to say it plainly. And I think you're the bravest person in this building."

"That's not bravery," Wren said. "That's just being the oldest."

She said it the way other children said *I'm tall for my age*, and something in Tamsin's chest went cold and stayed cold.

* * *

She didn't leave. He hadn't expected her to. She perched on the windowsill instead, coat finally buttoned, boots swinging a few inches above the floor, and watched the mist eat the far hedge while she talked—or while she answered, rather, because Tamsin had learned already that Wren didn't talk so much as release information, carefully rationed, like a woman paying out rope over a cliff edge.

"The first ones went before my mum was born," she said. "There were four missing then. Now it's three."

"Missing," Tamsin repeated. "Or gone?"

"Gone." She considered the word, turned it over. "Missing is when nobody knows where. We know where. Sort of. They went to sing."

"To sing."

"The Visitor likes singing." She said *the Visitor* the way she'd say *the dentist*—a fact of the calendar, unpleasant but scheduled. "That's what my gran called it. Other families have other names. The Founders' Guest. The Quiet Tithe. Old Man Merrow just calls It *the arrangement*, like it's a lease." A ghost of a smile crossed her face and vanished. "Gran said when she was a girl you could hear them on the hill some nights. All the ones that went before. Singing rounds. Gran knew the tune but she wouldn't ever sing it, not even in the bath with the door shut."

Tamsin found his voice had gone somewhere and had to fetch it back. "Wren. Are you telling me these children—your classmates, the ones on my register—they left? Walked out? Their parents let them—"

"They don't get let," Wren said. "They get *taken turns*. Everyone takes turns eventually. That's the whole point of turns." She hopped off the sill and stood in front of him, and up close her eyes were a pale grey-green, the colour of the moor in fog, and absolutely level. "You take your turn, and the village gets through the winter, and the crops come, and nobody starves, and the wells stay sweet. My gran's family survived the bad year because of a turn. So did Bram's. So did everybody's. That's why nobody says anything. Saying something is like spitting in the soup that keeps you alive."

"And whose turn is it now?" The question left him before he could weigh it. "Whose turn is next?"

For the first time, something moved behind Wren's eyes—not fear exactly. Fear would have been easier. It was closer to arithmetic.

"Not yet," she said. "We don't know till they draw. But you count, Mr. Quintana. Everybody counts. You count the years since your family last gave, you count how many kids you've got, you count whether you've been rude to anybody who matters." She glanced, very briefly, toward the window, toward the hill beyond the churchyard where the belfry stood empty and wrong against the sky. "My family's been careful for two generations. Careful like counting stitches. But careful only stretches so far. Turns come round."

"Takes turns," Tamsin said slowly, "with *children*. Eleven-year-olds. Seven-year-olds." His voice had dropped without his permission. "And the whole village stands by and lets it happen. Every year. For a hundred years."

"It's not letting," Wren said, with the terrible patience of the instructed correcting the ignorant. "It's *paying*. There's a difference. Letting is when you could stop it and don't. Paying is when stopping costs more than you've got." She picked up her satchel. "You'll understand by winter. New teachers always do. Last one cried in the stock cupboard, Miss Ferrier told me, and then she stopped asking questions, and then she stayed thirty years and died in the cottage by the mill and everyone said what a good teacher she was."

"I'm not going to stop asking questions, Wren."

"No," she agreed, at the door. She turned back, and for one moment the matter-of-fact mask slipped, and underneath it Tamsin saw the child—the whole child, the one who still slept with a light on, the one who had rehearsed this conversation in her head a dozen times before daring to have it—and his heart broke cleanly, like ice giving way. "I know. That's why I told you. Somebody has to know the words, even if knowing doesn't help. Gran always said the worst part isn't the Visitor. The worst part is that we taught ourselves to count so carefully we forgot how to shout."

* * *

He locked the school himself that evening, though the caretaker normally did it, and stood a long time in the empty room after the children's ghosts of noise had faded.

Eight chairs pushed in. Three pulled slightly out, the way Odell's staff left them, as if the room expected its absent pupils back at any hour. On the wall above the coat pegs hung a faded chart, *OUR CLASS FAMILY*, with paper apples bearing each child's name—and three hooks bare, three apples long since taken down, though the pinholes remained, tiny dark mouths in the plaster.

Tamsin took the register from his drawer and laid it open under the desk lamp. Eleven names. He read them aloud, quietly, one by one, the way you'd read a roll call for soldiers who hadn't come home—and when he reached the third crossed-out name, Merrin Dole, aged nine, he heard Wren's voice in his head, calm as a timetable:

*Everyone takes turns eventually.*

No. Not everyone. He straightened, and the lamplight caught his own reflection in the dark window—a thin man, a tired man, a man who had already run once from a scandal and knew precisely how heavy a secret could get when you carried it alone.

"Not everyone," he said aloud to the empty room. "Not this time."

Outside, far off across the wet fields, something that was not a bell began, very faintly, to toll—and Tamsin Quintana, rationalist, runaway, new-made protector of eight careful children, reached for his coat and his torch and started, for the second time in his life, walking directly toward the thing he feared most.
