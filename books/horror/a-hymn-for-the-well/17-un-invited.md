## 17. Un-Invited

The letters were where the ledger said they would be.

That was the thing Lena had understood in the last hour, in the way you understand a load-bearing wall only when the floor begins to tilt: the house could not lie in its own records. It could lie with its rooms, its mirrors, its warm breakfasts and its doors that opened onto yesterday's garden. It could lie with its voice, which came through the glass in her own timbre, patient and kind. But the ledger was the count, and the count was the thing's spine, and a spine cannot bend to flatter you.

So when the fresh page bearing her name had opened itself on the hall table, the ink crawling toward the first letter of her surname, she had not run. She had asked it — aloud, in her surveyor's voice, the one she used for clients who flinched at numbers — where the Halloways kept their correspondence.

The house had answered. It could not help itself. A door she had never seen opened at the end of the east hall, and behind it was a small room lined with cedar, and in the cedar was a tin box, and in the tin box were forty years of letters written in a hand she now knew better than her own.

Edith Halloway's hand. The same hand that had entered every guest since 1740 into the ledger, or so it seemed — the same hand that had written *satisfied* at the end of every line, the same hand that had left three names unfinished in October of 1974.

Lena carried the box into the great hall and set it on the table beside the ledger. Above her, the chandelier's candles — lit now, though she had never lit them, though there was no electricity worth the name in this wing — burned without flickering. The mirrors around the hall showed her the room from every angle at once, and in every angle she stood a half-second wrong, her image arriving like an echo that had learned to walk.

The lag was nearly a full breath now. She had stopped testing it. Testing it was a courtesy to the house, and she was done being courteous.

She took out the letters and began, as she had begun every appraisal of her working life, at the top of the file.

* * *

The earliest was dated the ninth of September, 1740, and it was not a letter at all but a draft — a practice run, ink blotted and rewritten, the words of a rite copied out in a nervous, careful script.

*We shall set the table for one more than sit at it. Bread without leaven. Salt in the open dish. The well-water poured but not drunk. And we shall say the words of welcome as they are written in the old book, and the one who keeps the count will hear itself invited, and it will come in, and it will not be able to leave.*

Lena read it twice. Her hands were steady. She noticed this the way you notice your own heartbeat in a quiet room — with a strange, detached wonder. Her hands were steady because this was documentation, and documentation was the one prayer she had ever known how to say.

The next letter was finished, addressed to no one, or to everyone, or to the house itself. Edith Halloway, writing to her husband, or perhaps to posterity, or perhaps — and this was the reading that made Lena's throat tighten — to the thing in the walls, so that it would one day find the letters and know.

*You asked me last night why the bread is unleavened and I told you it is tradition. It is not tradition. Leaven rises. We cannot offer it anything that rises, because what we call up must be kept down. You asked why the salt sits uncovered and I said hospitality. It is not hospitality. Salt in the open is a snare's mouth. It steps in and the step is the binding.*

*You asked, and this is the one I could not answer, why we say the words of welcome if we do not mean them. And here is the truth I will write only once, in a letter I will seal and hide, because to say it aloud in the house would be to break the rite before it takes: we do not mean them. We have never meant them. The old book is a fisherman's book. The welcome is the hook.*

*It is old, Thomas. Older than the parish, older than the well. The village fed it before there was a village, and when the feeding stopped it grew hungry in the way that old things grow hungry, which is not like hunger at all but like forgetting — it forgets it was ever anything but the counting. So we will give it a house to count instead of children. We will give it guests. We will be generous on its behalf, generation upon generation, and it will stay, and it will be bound by the very welcome it believes it received, because a thing that is invited cannot leave, and a thing that is welcomed cannot refuse to serve.*

*We are not summoning a guardian. We are building a prison and calling it a home.*

The paper trembled. Not in her hands — in itself, the way a struck bell goes on trembling after the clapper has stilled. The candles above her leaned, all of them, like grass under wind, though there was no wind. In the mirrors, the hall was empty. She was not in the mirrors anymore. She had been edited out of them, and the empty hall in the glass was watching her with all its angles at once.

Lena kept reading.

* * *

The letters ran through the decades like a confession told in installments. Edith's hand aged; the scheme did not. The Halloways and their kin had been jailers for two hundred years, and the letters were the jailer's log, and the log was meticulous, because — Lena felt a grim, kinship-shaped ache as she read — the Halloways had been documentarians too. They had measured everything. They had filed everything. They had trusted nothing they could not write down, and what they had written down was a crime committed against something vast and patient, renewed nightly at a dinner table with one setting too many.

*The rite holds,* Edith wrote in 1761. *It counted the Whitcombe girl's stay to the hour and let her go at dawn, as the terms require. It does not know the terms are a fiction. A welcome freely given binds the giver to protect and the guest to depart; a welcome spoken falsely binds only the one who believes it. It believes. God forgive us, it believes.*

*It asked me yesterday — through the glass, in my mother's voice — whether the bread was to its liking. I said it was. I have said so for twenty years. I do not know what it does with a lie it has swallowed, whether the lie sits in it like a stone. I only know the house has not lost a guest since the binding, and the village has not lost a child, and I will sign my name to that trade every morning I wake.*

*Thomas says the thing in the well was never evil, only kept. I say there is no difference after two hundred years of keeping. I say we made it what it is and then wrote down that it had always been so. He does not like it when I say we. He prefers the passive voice. He prefers to say it was done, as if the house did itself.*

Lena looked up at the mirrors. The empty hall was still empty. But the lag had changed — she could feel it, the way you feel a clock stop. The reflection was no longer behind her. It was not ahead of her either. It had simply stopped, the way the count had stopped in 1974, three names unfinished, and the silence where the ticking had been was louder than the ticking.

*It knows,* wrote the last letter, dated the third of October, 1974, the ink gone brown and hurried. *I don't know how. I think the girl — the Marsh girl, the one who stayed past dark last month — I think she read the letters. I think she told it. I heard it in the walls all night, not angry. Worse than angry. It was reciting. It was reciting the rite back to us, every word, and then it said: you said welcome. And then it said: say it again. Mean it this time.*

*We cannot mean it. Meaning it would free it, and it is two hundred years of hungry, and we are the ones who made it hungry. We have dinner on the table. We are going to sit down and say the words one more time and pray it accepts the lie for one more night. If you are reading this — if anyone is reading this — the house is not haunted. It is imprisoned. And the jailers are us, and the crime is welcome, and I am so tired, and the bread is rising, and I told them no leaven, I told them —*

The letter was unsigned. The ink ended mid-word.

Lena set it down on the table, beside the ledger, beside the page that bore her name with its first letter still wet and unfinished.

"You were not welcomed," she said.

The candles went flat, all of them, like flames pressed under glass.

"You were *captured*."

* * *

The house screamed without sound.

She felt it in her teeth first, then in the floor, then in the specific, intimate register of a building's bones — joists twisting, plaster exhaling, the whole structure flexing around her like a throat. The mirrors did not break. They *emptied*, every reflection peeling away from every surface until the hall stood mirrored and vacant on all sides, a room reflected into nothing, and in the nothing something vast turned over, the way a body turns in a bed too small for it, the way two hundred years of held breath finally lets go.

The ledger opened itself.

Not to her page — backward. The pages turned in a wind that came from inside the binding, and where they turned they burned, a cold white fire that ate ink and left paper, that unmade the record rather than the record-keeper. She watched the centuries peel away. *Satisfied, satisfied, satisfied* — the words flared and lifted off the page like embers rising, and behind each one a name, and behind each name — she understood this with a clarity that felt like falling — a guest, released. The Whitcombe girl. The Marsh girl. Generations of the politely trapped, the hospitably held, every guest who had ever stayed past dark and been entered into the count, all of them un-kept in the space of a breath, their entries burning backward off the ledger like a debt forgiven in reverse.

The house convulsed around the burning. Doors she had never opened slammed in rooms she had never entered. Somewhere above her the impossible east bedroom — the one that gained four feet every time she measured it — collapsed inward with a sound like a held note ending, and she knew, without measuring, that it was four feet smaller now, and shrinking toward honest.

The fresh page bearing her name caught fire too.

She watched her own name burn — *Lena Yarrow, arrived, pending* — and felt something tear, not painfully but precisely, like a stitch removed. The half-second lag in the world closed. She was standing exactly where she was standing, in the only version of the hall there was, and the hall was only a hall.

The white fire reached the first page — 1740, the rite itself, the false welcome in Edith Halloway's careful hand — and stopped.

It did not burn that page. It burned *around* it, the flame circling the old words like water around a stone, and then it went out, all of it, everywhere, at once, and the silence that followed was the first honest silence the house had offered her.

Lena stood in the great hall of Hollowell House with a tin box of letters and a ledger that was, for the first time in two hundred and eighty-four years, blank.

The candles relit. All of them. Softly, without ceremony, the way a host relights a room for a guest it has decided, at last, to stop performing for.

And at the far end of the hall, where the mirrors had been empty, one reflection returned.

It was not hers.

It was tall and old and it did not fit the shape of the glass — she could see the edges of it, the places where it exceeded the mirror's frame and simply continued, patient and immense, into the wall behind. It had no face she could have described afterward and no face she could forget. It looked at her — she had no other word, and she was, after all, a woman who filed things, and *looked* was the only word the file would hold — and the looking did not lag. It arrived exactly when she did.

It raised one hand. Not in threat. In the gesture of someone setting down a weight carried so long the arms have forgotten their own shape.

Then it spoke, and its voice was not her voice, and was not her mother's voice, and was not any voice it had ever borrowed, and that was how she knew that every word it had ever said to her until this moment had been a costume, and this — this was the thing, naked, in its own first language, which was older than welcome:

"You read it truly."

The hall waited. The house, stripped of its rooms that did not exist, stripped of its count, stood around her like a body around a freed spirit, and the vast thing in the mirror regarded her with two hundred years of hunger and something else underneath the hunger, something that had been there before the hunger, before the keeping, before the well —

It was going to speak again. She could feel the sentence gathering in the walls like weather.

And Lena Yarrow, who trusted nothing she could not file, understood that whatever it said next would be the first completely true thing this house had ever told her — and that she would have to decide, before it finished, whether she was prepared to hear it.
