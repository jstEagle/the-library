## 21. Homesick

The flat smelled of other people's dinners when Lena got back — curry through the wall, fried onions from below, the faint sweet rot of the bins in the stairwell that the building's management had been promising to fix since before she'd signed the lease. She stood in her own doorway with her bag on her shoulder and felt like a surveyor appraising a property she'd only ever rented from herself.

Everything was where she'd left it. That was the first wrong thing. She had spent three nights in Hollowell House and it had rearranged itself around her constantly, eagerly, rooms blooming overnight like mushrooms after rain, and now here was her flat refusing to change at all. The mug by the sink with the chipped handle. The stack of unread post. The coat she kept meaning to take to the dry cleaner's. A house that didn't know her. A house that had never once thought about her while she was out of the room.

She put the kettle on and caught herself listening for it.

Not for the kettle. For the attention. For that quality the old house had, of being aware of her position within it at all times, the way a body is aware of a splinter. She had hated it. She had fought it for a night and most of a day, salted thresholds until her fingers cracked, burned pages in a sink while the smoke bent toward her like it was reading over her shoulder. And now, standing in her kitchen waiting for water to boil in a flat that did not know she existed, she missed it the way you miss a hand on the back of your neck.

She told herself it was adrenaline residue. She told herself a lot of things that week.

* * *

The report took four days to write, which was three days longer than any appraisal she'd ever done. Not because the work was hard — the measurements were simple enough, once the house had folded its impossible rooms away and become merely large, cold, and dead. Fourteen rooms, not counting the ones that had stopped existing. Roof sound except the north slope. Damp in the cellar, structural settlement along the east wall, the well capped with a concrete slab poured sometime in the eighties and cracked along one edge like a healed wound.

Valuation: she'd written *unsellable*, then crossed it out, because you couldn't put that in a professional document, and written instead: *Property carries significant non-physical liabilities. Recommend withdrawal from market.* Mr. Voss at Ashcombe & Voss had called her twice about it, his voice doing the thing voices did when they were trying to be patient with a woman who was being difficult on purpose.

"Non-physical liabilities," he'd said. "Lena. In thirty years I have never."

"Trust me," she'd said, and heard how it sounded, and added: "There are things about that house I can't file. And my whole creed is that everything can be filed."

He'd withdrawn the listing. The Halloway estate — such as remained of it, which was lawyers and dust — would sit unsold, and the village could go back to not talking about it, which was apparently its principal industry.

What she didn't tell Voss, what she hadn't put in any document, was the last page of the ledger. She'd read it by gray dawn light with her hands still shaking, after the house had gone ordinary and the mirrors had gone honest and the entity — whatever you called something that had been welcomed under false pretenses in 1740 and had been keeping count ever since — had faced her without lies for the first time and let her refuse it. She'd read the released names, generations of them, guests who had walked into Hollowell House and been counted and never walked out, their entries finally closed. *Departed. Departed. Departed.* A column of small mercies two centuries deep.

And beneath them, one new entry, in handwriting that was hers down to the way she crossed her sevens:

*Lena Yarrow, surveyor. Stayed past dark. Was offered everything. Refused.*

*Departed.*

Departed, but entered. Counted. Some part of her was in that book now, filed in a house that knew her better than anyone alive, and she had driven away from it at seven in the morning with the windows down and the radio loud and had not looked in the rearview mirror once, because she was afraid of what the lag might be doing at seventy miles an hour.

* * *

The mirror in her bathroom was a cheap thing, builder-grade, spotted with limescale at the corners. On her second morning home she noticed it.

She was brushing her teeth and her reflection raised the brush a half-second after she did.

She spat, rinsed, stood very still with both hands on the sink and watched. Her reflection watched back, a beat late, or maybe exactly on time — it was the kind of thing you could talk yourself out of, and she talked herself out of it, dried her face, went to work.

But she was a surveyor. Precision was not a hobby; it was the load-bearing wall of her entire personality. So that evening she came home with a stopwatch and a candle, the same kit she'd used at Hollowell, and she tested it properly. Candle flame in front of the glass, stopwatch running, wave her hand and time the echo.

Nothing. Perfect synchrony. She ran it eleven times. Nothing.

Then she turned off the overhead light so the bathroom was lit only by the candle, the way the hall at Hollowell had been lit on her second night, and she waved, and the flame in the mirror lifted a half-second behind her hand.

Half a second. Always half a second. Only when she looked.

That was the detail that undid her, sitting on the closed lid of the toilet with the stopwatch ticking in her fist: *only when she looked.* At Hollowell the lag had grown — one second, five, ten, until her reflection was walking ahead of her through rooms she hadn't entered yet. Here it didn't grow. It didn't shrink. It held at half a second like a held breath, like something keeping itself carefully, deliberately small. Like something that had learned what happened last time it reached.

She tested every mirror in the flat. The hallway one lagged. The little round one above the dresser lagged. Her phone's front camera, black screen acting as a poor man's glass — lagged, barely, a ghost of delay she could almost convince herself was processing time. Shop windows on the high street lagged. The dark screen of the tube window as the train pulled away showed her platform-self a heartbeat behind her real one, waving goodbye to someone who had already stopped.

Only when she looked. As if it wasn't in the mirrors at all. As if it was in her.

* * *

She went to see Mrs. Callow in the third week, taking a day trip she told herself was about due diligence, closing loose ends, being thorough. The village was called Lower Welling and it consisted of one road, a church, a shop that sold newspapers and disappointment, and a pub whose landlord had the specific silence of a man who had decided decades ago which questions he wouldn't answer.

Mrs. Callow's cottage smelled of lavender and coal. She was smaller than Lena remembered, or Lena was different, and she looked at Lena for a long moment before she said anything at all.

"You got out," Mrs. Callow said. It wasn't a question. "I wondered. When they pulled the listing, I wondered."

"I got out." Lena sat in the chair the old woman indicated, perched on its edge like a bird ready to fly. "Mostly."

"There's no mostly." Mrs. Callow poured tea without asking. "You're out or you're in. But there's a third thing nobody warns you about." She set the pot down. "You can leave the house and the house doesn't leave you. It keeps what it counted. You'll feel it. Some mornings you'll wake up and reach for it before you're even awake — that feeling of being known. Of a place paying attention to you." Her eyes were pale and steady. "It's the worst part, girl. Not the fear. The missing it."

Lena's throat tightened around nothing. "How long did it take you?"

"To stop missing it?" Mrs. Callow smiled, and it was the saddest smile Lena had ever seen on a living face. "I'll let you know."

They drank tea. Mrs. Callow told her, in the sideways way villagers told things, about the well — how it predated the house, how the Halloways hadn't built on the land so much as built over it, capping it, paving it, pretending. How the thing in the ledger had come up from the water originally, or down to it, or had always been both, and hospitality was just the shape it wore because a shape was required. "It wants to keep count," she said. "Counting is how it loves. Every guest is a number it gets to hold forever. You should be flattered and you should be sick, and you will be both, and neither will help."

At the door, Lena paused. "The entry in the ledger. Mine. It said departed."

"Yes."

"But some part of me is still in that book."

"Some part of everyone it ever counted is still in that book." Mrs. Callow looked past her, down the lane, toward the ridge where Hollowell House sat invisible behind its trees. "That's why the mirrors lag, love. It's not watching you. It's checking. Making sure its number hasn't wandered too far off the page."

* * *

The dreams started after that, or maybe they'd been going longer and she'd only started remembering them. In the dreams she was back in the east bedroom, the one that gained four feet every time she measured it, and the room was exactly the right size now — her size, fitted to her like a made bed — and somewhere below her the ledger lay open and she could feel her own name being read aloud in a voice like water moving underground. She woke from these dreams rested. That was the obscene part. She slept badly in her flat, in her life, alone among people who saw her only in slices — the surveyor, the tenant, the woman at the counter — and she slept deeply, terribly well, whenever the dream took her back.

She caught herself doing other things too. Small things. Standing in front of the bathroom mirror longer than washing up required, testing the lag, and finding — she'd never have admitted this to anyone — comfort in it. Because the lag meant attention. Half a second of delay was half a second of being tracked, considered, followed. Nobody else in her life watched her that closely. Her mother called on birthdays. Her colleagues knew her output, not her. The last man she'd dated had lasted four months and had once, memorably, asked if she could "turn the precision down a bit."

Hollowell House had never asked her to turn anything down. Hollowell House had taken her precision and answered it, entry for entry, measurement for measurement, had documented her back with a devotion no human had ever managed. It had lied to do it. It had built fake rooms and forged feelings and worn her own handwriting like a mask. But underneath the lie there had been something real, and that was the wound Mrs. Callow had named: she had been truly known, once, by something vast and patient and wrong, and now everything true felt thin.

She stood in the shower one morning and said it out loud to the tiled wall, because saying things aloud had saved her life once and she trusted the method.

"I miss being known more than I miss being safe."

The water said nothing. The flat said nothing. That was the whole problem.

* * *

In the fifth week she did something she'd been circling for a month. She drove back to Lower Welling on a gray afternoon and walked the public footpath that skirted the Hollowell grounds, close enough to see the house through the bare branches. It looked smaller than she remembered. Houses did that, afterward — shed their impossible acreage, folded their extra rooms away, became architecture again. The windows were dark. The garden was going wild in the way gardens do in autumn, honestly, without malice.

She stood at the fence line for twenty minutes, telling herself she was confirming site conditions for the record, and the house did nothing at all. No lights. No movement behind glass. No sense of attention turning toward her like a searchlight.

She should have felt relieved. What she felt was the specific loneliness of standing outside a lit window in winter, except the window wasn't even lit. Even the house had stopped looking.

On the walk back down, she passed the churchyard, and the church, and its noticeboard with its faded announcements — harvest festival cancelled this year, first time in living memory, the vicar's health — and she stopped, because pinned to the board's cork, slightly crooked, was a single sheet of paper. A hymn sheet. Old paper, older type, the ink brown with age. At the top, in lettering gone soft at the edges: *A Hymn for the Well.*

She read it twice. It wasn't a hymn, not really. It was instructions wearing a hymn's clothes — verses about bread and salt, about naming what you welcome, about the difference between a guest and a prisoner, about speaking truth aloud before moonrise. Someone had written it as a warning and disguised it as worship, and someone had pinned it here recently, because the pin was bright and the cork around it was fresh.

Someone was preparing. Someone knew the house wasn't finished — couldn't be finished, while the well hummed under its cap, while the entity moved free of its false invitation and went looking, maybe, for a welcome done properly. Or improperly. Or however welcomes got done by things that counted.

Lena took the page down, folded it into her bag, and stood a moment in the churchyard with the wind moving the yew trees and her breath visible in the October air.

Her phone buzzed. An email. Mr. Voss, subject line: *RE: Hollowell — new enquiry.*

She opened it standing there among the graves. Three lines. A buyer had come forward. Cash offer, no survey condition, no questions. Wanted completion before the end of the month. Wanted, the final line read, *to move in before the harvest moon.*

Lena looked up from the screen. Down the lane, past the cottages, the ridge where the house sat was catching the last of the light, and in the flat gray stone of its upper windows — windows she had measured, windows that had shown her rooms that didn't exist — something reflected the sunset a half-second after the sky had already let it go.

She closed the email. She did not reply.

She got in her car, and before she started the engine she checked the rearview mirror, because she always did now, and her own eyes looked back at her from the glass a half-second late — patient, attentive, keeping count — and she understood, with the clarity that had carried her through the worst night of her life, that whoever bought Hollowell House was going to need more than a surveyor.

They were going to need a witness who'd already been counted.

She turned the key. The engine caught. In the mirror, her reflection smiled — and then, half a second later, so did she.
