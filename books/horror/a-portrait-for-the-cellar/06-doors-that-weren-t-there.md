## 6. Doors That Weren't There

The count broke on a Tuesday.

Xanthe knew it before her feet touched the floor, the way you know a fever before the thermometer confirms it. She lay in the grey half-light of dawn and listened to the house breathe, and something in its rhythm was wrong—off by one, like a heartbeat with an extra systole hiding between the beats.

She got up. She counted.

Eleven doors on the ground floor. There had always been eleven. She'd walked them her first week at Hollowmere, fingertips trailing each panel of oak, saying the numbers under her breath the way she said them everywhere: *one* through the pantry door, *two* into the morning room, *three* the servants' passage that smelled of cold ash and older dust. Eleven doors, fourteen windows on the east face, nine light fixtures she could reach without a ladder. The numbers were load-bearing. They held up the whole architecture of her sanity, and she had built that architecture over eleven years and forty-one patients, brick by counted brick, because the alternative was to listen too closely to what the dying were counting toward.

She walked the hall. *One.* Pantry. *Two.* Morning room. *Three*—

She stopped.

Between the morning room and the servants' passage, where yesterday there had been nothing but wallpaper—faded roses, water-stained at the cornice—there was a door.

It wasn't new. That was the thing that made her stomach drop through the floorboards. It wasn't new the way a carpenter's work is new, with bright wood and sharp edges. It was old. The paint was crazed into fine grey rivers, the brass handle worn smooth in the middle by decades—no, generations—of hands. It looked as though it had always been there, and the only evidence it hadn't was Xanthe's own ledger, kept in her own hand, in her own careful print: *Ground floor, 11 doors,* dated her third night, when the mirrors had still been honest.

*Twelve,* she said aloud, and her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice in a stranger's throat.

She did not open it. She had rules about doors that appeared overnight, even if she'd never written those rules down. Instead she finished the circuit, and found two more—one at the end of the upstairs corridor where the wall had been blank, one tucked behind the linen press like a shy child—and by the time she reached the kitchen her count stood at fifteen doors and her hands were shaking so badly she burned the toast without noticing until the smoke curled up black against the window.

Edith was already at the table, wrapped in her shawl, watching her with those pale, unblinking eyes.

"You've seen them," Edith said. Not a question.

"The house has grown three doors since yesterday." Xanthe set down the ruined toast. Her voice was steady. She'd had practice being steady; it was the one skill hospice work taught better than any other. "I'd like to know how."

"Would you?" Edith smiled, and there was no warmth in it, only a terrible tiredness. "No, you wouldn't, dear. You'd like to be told how, by someone else, so it stays their problem. I've watched three nurses ask that same question and then pack their bags before they heard the answer." She poured tea neither of them would drink. "You're still here, though. Four days now. That's longer than most."

"Mrs. Vane—"

"Edith." The old woman's hand trembled around the teapot, and Xanthe saw, for the first time, how thin it had gotten. How translucent. "We're past Mrs. Vane, I think. You've been counting my doors."

It wasn't an accusation. It was worse—it was recognition. Two women who both kept tallies against the dark, sitting across a kitchen table from each other, each pretending not to see the other's arithmetic.

"I count everything," Xanthe admitted. "Doors. Lights. Stairs. It's—" *A superstition. A coping mechanism. A symptom,* the agency psychologist had written in her file years ago, before Xanthe learned to stop being interesting. "It helps."

"It won't," Edith said simply. "Not here. The house doesn't mind being counted, you see. It minds being *known*. And every door it grows is a door it wants known." She looked at Xanthen over the rim of her cup, and her eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "Don't go looking for what's behind them. Promise me."

Xanthe promised, because it was easier than telling the truth, which was that a promise made to a dying woman was made to someone with one foot already out the world's door—and Xanthe had learned long ago that promises to the dying were the first things the dark collected.

* * *

She lasted six hours.

It wasn't weakness. She told herself that later, standing in the cellar dark with her heart slamming against her ribs like something caged. It wasn't weakness; it was the count. Fifteen doors, and she could feel them the way you feel a missing tooth—the absence of certainty, the gap where the number used to sit flush against reality. Every hour she didn't account for them, they accounted for her instead. She could almost hear the walls doing sums.

At four in the afternoon, Edith went down for her nap—her sleeps had grown long and shallow, more rehearsal than rest—and Xanthe stood in the front hall with her ledger open and her pen uncapped, and lied to herself one last time: *just the count. Just to make the number right again.*

The twelfth door opened onto stairs going down.

Of course it did. Cellars went down. But these stairs were wrong in a way her body understood before her mind caught up—they descended in a spiral, tight and turning, and the air rising out of them was warm. Not stale-warm, not boiler-warm. Warm like breath. Like the exhalation of something very large sleeping just below, and the whole house was its ribcage, and the doors were the spaces between its ribs.

She took the candle from the hall table. She told herself it was for the dark. She knew, in the place where she kept the truths she didn't say aloud, that it was because flame made reflections, and reflections had been lying to her all week, and she wanted—needed—to catch one in the act.

The stairs turned seven times. She counted them. Seven turns, thirty-one steps, and the temperature climbing with each one until she was sweating in the cool October house, until the candleflame leaned toward the bottom of the stairwell as if pulled by the breathing.

The cellar was not a cellar.

Cellars were stone and shadow and shelved preserves. This was a room, low-ceilinged and dry, and it was hung with portraits.

They covered every wall. Generations of Vanes, painted in the heavy oils of their respective centuries—a man in a Puritan collar, a woman with a high powdered coiffure, children in mourning dress holding miniature coffins, the family's whole long procession staring down with the flat patience of the painted dead. Their frames were gilt and worm-eaten and identical in one respect only: every single face was turned slightly, a degree or two, toward the center of the room.

Toward the far wall.

Toward the largest portrait, hung alone above a mantel of black marble, and Xanthe knew before she raised the candle whose face it was, because she had seen that jawline reproduced in Edith, that stubborn mouth, those pale eyes that watched and watched and never blinked enough. A woman in a grey dress the color of famine winters. Her name was painted small beneath her: *MARGARET VANE, 1947.*

And below Margaret Vane, centered on the mantel, hung an empty frame.

It was the size of a face. Just a face—shoulders cropped away, background bare, the oval opening showing only the black marble wall behind it. It was smaller than the others, humbler, positioned like a servant's portrait among gentry. But it was finished. Gilt-edged. Wired for hanging. Waiting.

Xanthe stepped closer, because some part of her brain still believed in inspection, in assessment, in the professional fiction that understanding a thing reduced its power. The frame's proportions were exact. She knew faces; she had watched hundreds of them change and fail and finally go slack. The oval was sized for a woman perhaps thirty-five. Fair hair. A long, tired face that had learned to hold itself very still.

Her own measurements. Her own face.

The candle shook. In the black marble beneath the empty frame, her reflection wavered—and lagged. She lifted the candle; a half-second later, the reflection lifted hers. She turned; the reflection followed late, and in that stolen second, in the gap between her movement and its echo, the reflection was smiling.

She dropped the candle. It guttered on the stone and died, and in the sudden dark the portraits rustled—not moving, she told herself, *not moving*, just settling, just old canvas breathing—and somewhere above her, through the floors of the growing house, she heard Edith's bell ring once, twice, three times, urgent as a bird striking glass.

* * *

Edith met her at the top of the cellar stairs, one hand braced on the doorframe, breathing hard, her cardigan thrown over her nightdress. For a moment they just looked at each other—the nurse with candle smoke on her fingers, the patient gray-faced with fury or fear or both.

"You promised," Edith said.

"Yes."

"And you went anyway."

"Yes."

Something moved across the old woman's face—not anger. Something rawer. "Good," she whispered, and then she laughed, a cracked, awful sound, and sat down right there on the top step, gathering her shawl around her like armor. "God help me, but it's good. Sit down, Xanthe. No—here. Beside me. If we're going to do this, we'll do it properly."

Xanthe sat. The stairwell breathed warm air up around them.

"My grandmother signed something," Edith said, without preamble, staring down into the dark Xanthe had just climbed out of. "Winter of '47. The snow came in November and didn't stop till March, and the roads were gone, and they starved up here, all of them—my grandmother, her sister, the baby. By February they were eating the candles. By March my grandmother couldn't get out of bed." Her voice was flat, recitative, a story worn smooth from being told silently to herself ten thousand times. "And something in the mirrors offered to keep them alive. All it asked was an invitation. Signed. Blood and ink, grandmother wrote in her diary—she left it in the library, in a box, and I read it when I was nineteen and thought it was a madwoman's fairy tale." She laughed again, softly. "It keeps them alive. It keeps *this house* alive. And every generation, when the signer dies, it needs a new signature. A living hand. Someone who belongs here."

"Edith—"

"The lawyers come Friday," Edith said. "Dawn. To sign the house over, end the Vane line, let the whole rotten thing fall down at last. And I have spent three weeks trying to die *before* they arrive, because I finally understand what my grandmother understood too late." She turned, and took Xanthe's hand—her grip shockingly strong, desperate, the grip of a drowning woman. "It doesn't want my signature, dear. I'm dying either way; a dying hand signs nothing it can be bound by. It wants *yours*. It grew you a door. It cut you a frame." Her thumb pressed hard against Xanthe's knuckles. "It has been measuring your face since the night you arrived."

The cellar breathed beneath them. Upstairs, faintly, in the hall mirror Xanthe could not see but knew was there, her reflection was already descending stairs she hadn't climbed yet.

"Then tell me everything," Xanthe said. "All of it. Tonight. Before Friday."

Edith looked at her for a long moment—and nodded, once, like a woman accepting terms.

"Then we'll need the brandy," she said, "and the diary. And you'll want to sit close, dear. Because after midnight—" her eyes flicked to the darkened hallway, to the twelve doors that were now fifteen, "—it answers back."
