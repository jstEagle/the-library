## 10. Sunrise, Unsigned

The count reached nine at half past five.

Xanthe heard it from the cellar stairs, where she and Edith had spent the last three hours holding hands in the dark beneath the cracked portrait. The voice came through the floorboards, through the stone, through the marrow of the house itself—thin and patient, the way dying men counted, the way walls had counted all week. Nine. Eight. But underneath it now, woven through it like a second thread, was Edith's own voice, hoarse and steady, counting forward.

"One," Edith whispered. "Two. Three."

She had started her own count an hour ago, after the portrait split down the middle and the lag collapsed and every mirror in Hollowmere went suddenly, mercifully honest. Xanthe had expected silence after that. She hadn't expected Edith to begin counting *up*—but of course. Of course. The house had always counted backwards toward something. Edith had decided, somewhere in that long dark, that she would count toward something instead.

"Four," Edith said. "Five."

Her hand was light in Xanthe's. Paper over bone. But warm. That was the thing Xanthe would keep, later, when everything else had gone to lawyers and demolition crews: the warmth of it. Nineteen years a hospice nurse, and she had held a hundred dying hands, and this was the first one that felt less like losing something and more like being trusted with it.

Above them, the backwards count faltered. Seven. Seven again. A stutter, like a record skipping. Then six, quieter, as if the voice were moving away—out through the front hall, out through the mirrors, out into whatever grey country received things that had been invited in and then uninvited by grief too honest to counterfeit.

"Six," Edith echoed, smiling without opening her eyes. "We're matching, dear. Did you notice? We're counting together."

"I noticed." Xanthe's voice came out wrecked. She didn't try to fix it. That was the whole lesson of the night, wasn't it—the not-fixing, the not-smoothing-over, the letting grief sit in the open where nothing could wear its face. "Seven."

"Seven," Edith agreed. "Eight."

The sky beyond the cellar door's high window had begun to change. Not dawn yet, but the promise of it—a thinning of the black, a bruise going gold at the edge. Xanthe watched it while they counted. Nine. Ten. Twenty. Somewhere past thirty Edith's numbers slowed, spaced further apart, each one arriving like a guest who has traveled far and is glad to set down their bag.

At fifty-one, the solicitors' cars would be pulling up the drive. Xanthe could already imagine them: Mr. Ashworth with his leather folder, the junior associate with his thermos, the papers with their impossible clause—*the resident's designee shall co-sign*—waiting for a signature no living person should give. Let them come. Let them find two women in a cellar, one dead and one grieving, and a house full of honest mirrors. There was nothing in this place left for them to collect but dust and daylight.

"Sixty-two," said Edith.

"Sixty-three," said Xanthe.

They alternated now, passing the numbers back and forth like a ball, like a plate at supper, like anything shared between people who have decided, late and against all odds, to be family. Sixty-eight. Seventy-one. The gold at the window widened. The backwards voice was gone entirely; Xanthe checked the small mirror in her pocket—Edith's dressing-table glass, palm-sized, silver-backed, salvaged from the cellar shelf—and saw only her own face looking back, tired and tear-streaked and exactly on time.

"Ninety-six," Edith whispered.

Her breathing had changed. Xanthe knew the change the way she knew her own pulse—the lengthening between breaths, the tide going out. Nineteen years, and she had never once been able to stop it, only witness it. Witnessing, she understood now, had been the whole job all along. Not the counts, not the lights, not the careful invisibility. Just this: *I am here. You are seen. You do not go alone.*

"Ninety-seven," Xanthe said, because Edith's lips had stopped moving, though her eyes were open now, fixed on the ceiling, or on something above the ceiling, or on nothing at all except the far side of morning.

"Ninety-eight," Xanthe said.

Edith's fingers tightened once around hers. Deliberate. A signature of a different kind—the kind no lawyer drafts, no mirror reflects, no house can hold.

"Ninety-nine," Xanthe said.

And then, in a voice so small it might have been the house itself speaking, or the woman, or both, Edith Vane finished it herself:

"Zero."

* * *

The sun cleared the treeline at 6:14 a.m., and Xanthe sat with Edith's hand until it cooled, which took longer than she expected and less than she wanted.

She did not call anyone immediately. There was no one to call, really—not until the lawyers arrived, and arrive they did, at 6:40, Mr. Ashworth's grey Audi crunching up a drive still silvered with frost. He knocked. She let him in herself, standing in the doorway with the morning behind her, and something in her face made him stop on the threshold and remove his hat.

"She passed at dawn," Xanthe said. "Peacefully. She was counting."

Ashworth looked past her, into the hall, at the great mirror there. He looked at it a long moment—at his own reflection, arriving exactly when he arrived—and whatever he'd been prepared to say about co-signatories and designees died somewhere behind his teeth.

"The papers," he managed finally.

"Are upstairs on the desk. Unsigned. All of them." She stepped aside to let him pass, then didn't. "There's a codicil in the drawer beneath them. Mrs. Vane dictated it to me Tuesday night. It leaves Hollowmere to a charitable trust—demolition and land restoration, wildflower meadow, the lot. Her handwriting is on the instruction letter. My signature is on it as witness, dated before any of you arrived with your clause nobody remembers drafting."

Ashworth blinked. "That's not—I don't recall any—"

"No," Xanthe agreed. "You wouldn't."

He signed what needed signing in the front hall, under the honest mirror, and if his pen hesitated once over the line marked *designee*, he crossed it out himself and wrote *void — provision lapsed* in his neat solicitor's hand, and did not look up at the glass while he did it.

* * *

It took four days for the trust's surveyors to arrive and eleven for the demolition permit to post in the village window. Xanthe stayed through all of it. She told herself it was for the paperwork, and partly it was, but mostly it was because Edith deserved to be counted out properly—every room catalogued, every light noted, every door accounted for. The house had grown doors in the dark week; now, in the daylight, they were gone again, sealed back into plaster as if ashamed. Xanthe walked the halls each evening with her tally book and found the numbers true. Forty-one doors. Thirty-six lights. One house, dying honestly at last.

On the last morning, she went down to the cellar one final time.

The cracked portrait had been taken away by the trust's conservators—there was talk of a museum, talk of burning it; Xanthe voted for burning—but the shelf beneath its hook still held the small things: a hairbrush, a tin of buttons, a candle stub, and the little silver-backed mirror she had pocketed that dawn and then, strangely, brought back.

She stood over it a long while.

Every instinct she'd built across nineteen years said *leave it*. Mirrors had lied to her, hunted her, worn the faces of everyone she'd failed. She should walk out of Hollowmere with nothing made of glass, nothing that could hold a second she hadn't lived yet.

But she picked it up anyway.

Not as a wound. As a reminder. That grief spoken aloud was stronger than grief hidden. That counting could be a way of staying rather than a way of hiding. That a woman who had spent her whole life being unnoticed had been noticed, in the end, by someone who mattered—and had held her hand down to zero.

In the little mirror, her face looked back at her, right on time, eyes red-rimmed and clear. Behind her reflection, the cellar wall showed only cellar wall. She turned the glass over. On the back, scratched faintly into the silver, in a hand she recognized from the 1947 diary: *E.V., who counts forward.*

"Hadn't seen that before," Xanthe said aloud, to no one, to Edith, to the honest empty air. "Good."

She put it in her coat pocket, where it rode light as a stone from a riverbed, and climbed the stairs.

* * *

The front door of Hollowmere House had always been the first thing she counted on arrival and the last on leaving. Door forty-one. She stood before it now with her single bag packed, the morning bright and hard as new glass outside, frost smoking off the lawn, wildflower seed already sitting in a truck at the gate waiting for the walls to come down.

Behind her, the hall was bare. The great mirror had been crated for removal an hour ago, and the wallpaper where it hung showed a paler rectangle, a ghost of hanging, nothing more.

Xanthe took out the tally book, found today's page, and wrote, in her small precise hand:

*Doors: forty-one. Lights: thirty-six, all dark, all at rest. Patients lost: everyone, eventually. Patients honored: all of them, by name, out loud, forever.*

Then, beneath it, because invisibility was a habit she intended to break one entry at a time:

*Xanthe Galloway was here. She stayed. She grieved. She signed nothing.*

She closed the book, opened the door, and stepped through into full daylight, counting—one step, two, three—all the way down the drive, all the way out, forward.

Behind her, in the pocket of her coat, the little mirror caught the sun and threw it back, a single bright point, like someone waving.
