## 1. The Wrong Grave

The bus from Ashford let Lior off at the bottom of Harbour Hill at 4:17 in the afternoon, and Greywater came up to meet him the way it always had — slowly, then all at once, like something surfacing.

He hadn't been back in nine years. He'd told himself it was work, and then distance, and then the simple physics of a life that had accreted elsewhere: a flat in Leeds with a radiator that clanked, a job designing museum wayfinding systems, a girlfriend who had lasted fourteen months and left him with a better vocabulary for his own avoidance. But standing there now with Noa's ashes in a canvas bag over his shoulder and the wind coming off the channel smelling of iron and old rope, he understood that he had simply never found a reason strong enough. Grief, it turned out, was one.

The town looked worse than he remembered, which was saying something. Greywater had been dying since before either of them was born — a fishing port that lost its fish, a resort that never quite became one — but decay had a tempo, and this felt accelerated. The sea wall along the front had buckled into a low zigzag, like a sentence someone had given up on halfway through. Half the shops on Fore Street were shuttered, their windows papered over with faded posters for events years past: a regatta in 2016, a Christmas market, a jumble sale. The chippy was still open. The shopkeeper's — Hollis & Son, General Provisions — still had its striped awning, though the stripes had gone the color of weak tea.

Lior walked up through the town with the bag of ashes against his back, feeling them shift with each step, a soft granular weight that no amount of adjusting could make comfortable. He'd carried them from the crematorium in Ashford himself. The funeral director had offered to ship them. Lior had said no without knowing why, and now, climbing the hill with his sister on his shoulders, he thought: *because she'd have hated the post.*

Noa would have hated all of this, actually. She'd have hated the bus, the town, the wind, the whole grim pageant of returning. She'd have made a joke about it — something quick and sideways, delivered while looking at her phone — and then she'd have gone quiet, because underneath the joke she'd have been doing what she always did, which was noticing everything. That was the difference between them. He catalogued; she perceived. He filed the world away; she read it.

And now she was two pounds of ash in a bag, and he was reading the world alone.

The cemetery sat above the town on a shelf of land where the church had once stood before the sea took it — everyone in Greywater said "the church" the way you'd say "the war," a single definite catastrophe belonging to everyone and no one. All that remained up here was the graveyard itself, terraced into the slope, its older stones leaning seaward as if craning to watch the water that had eaten their parish. The newer section, where the Voss family plot lived, was flatter, tidier, fenced with chain-link that the salt air had chewed to lace.

Their grandmother Edith was buried at the near end: EDITH MARIE VOSS, 1931–2019, BELOVED MOTHER AND KEEPER. Lior had never asked what she'd kept. He'd assumed it was a family joke, or a typo nobody corrected.

He found the plot by counting stones, the way you find your seat in a dark cinema. Grandma, then the gap where their parents' stone would eventually go — both alive, mercifully, retired to Spain and communicating primarily through group-chat complaints about the heat — and then the space that had been reserved for as long as Lior could remember, the third slot, the one Edith had pointed out once with her cane when Lior was eleven: *that one's spoken for, love, don't you go putting flowers on it yet.*

That was where the funeral director had said the interment would be. That was where the small gathering would stand tomorrow at ten, and say the words, and put Noa in the ground.

Except there was already a headstone in the third slot.

Lior stopped walking. The wind moved through the grass behind him with a sound like a long exhale.

It wasn't just any headstone. It was *old* — genuinely, unmistakably old, the kind of old that took decades of weather to write. The granite had gone grey-green with lichen, crusts of it blooming across the face like a map of somewhere else. The carved letters were softened, their edges rounded, the serums of the serif worn to suggestions. Rain had streaked down from the top edge in dark mineral tears that had themselves weathered. A chip had come off the lower left corner, and the exposed interior stone had dulled to match the rest, which meant the chip was old too. Moss had colonized the base where the stone met the earth, thick and established, the kind of moss that took years to get confident.

He stood there for a long time, doing the thing he did, the cataloguing thing, because the cataloguing thing was how he kept the world from tipping over. Granite. Likely imported, same as the others in this row. Weathering consistent with — what? Thirty years? Forty? The lichen alone argued for decades; lichen grew at the speed of geology. Somebody had cut this stone, carved it, set it in the earth, and then *left it alone* for most of Lior's lifetime.

Then he made himself read it.

NOA ELISE VOSS
1994 – 2024
SHE SANG US HOME

His knees did something unreliable. He went down onto one of them in the wet grass without deciding to, the canvas bag sliding off his shoulder into his lap, and he put his hand flat against the stone because there was nothing else to do with his hands.

*SHE SANG US HOME.* Noa hadn't sung since school. Not properly, not where anyone could hear — she'd hum sometimes, absently, when she was cooking or thinking, a tuneless little loop of notes that drove every flatmate she ever had quietly insane. Lior had teased her about it their whole lives. *You're the only person I know who hums in a minor key while making toast.* And she'd laugh and say she couldn't help it, it was the house, she'd grown up humming it, grandma's house hummed at night and you just sort of absorbed it, like damp.

But she didn't sing. She'd told him once, drunk on half a bottle of prosecco at his thirtieth, that she'd promised someone she never would. He'd laughed and asked who, and she'd said, *you wouldn't know them,* and changed the subject so smoothly he'd spent three years occasionally wondering if he'd imagined the conversation.

Now she was dead — three weeks dead, killed by an aneurysm so sudden and total that the doctors used words like "merciful" and meant it — and there was a headstone in her slot that had been standing in the weather longer than some of the houses on Fore Street. With an epitaph about singing. In a town she'd visited maybe four times in her adult life.

Lior stayed down on one knee until his leg went numb, running his thumb through the carved letters. NOA. The N was nearly smooth. Under his fingertip the lichen had a faint smell, dry and mineral, like the inside of very old books.

There were explanations. Of course there were. His mind, loyal and bureaucratic, began generating them immediately, presenting them for review like a clerk who doesn't want to consider the alternative. The family had ordered the stone early — people did that, pre-need, Edith had probably arranged it years ago, wanted everything sorted — and the mason had cut it and — no, that didn't explain the weathering, you couldn't fake thirty years of lichen, and even if you could, why would you, and anyway Edith was dead four years and nobody in this family planned anything that far ahead except funerals, apparently —

— apparently.

He sat back on his heels. The light was going amber and long across the terraces of graves, and the older stones above him leaned toward the water, and somewhere below in the town a dog started barking and stopped.

Three weeks. Noa had died three weeks ago. The ashes had taken time to release — paperwork, the coroner, the crematorium's queue — which was why the interment was only happening now, tomorrow morning. Which meant that whoever placed this stone had done it knowing she was dead before Lior knew. Before anyone outside the hospital knew. Before the family group chat had produced its forty-one messages of shock and the flower arrangements and his mother's voice on the phone saying *aneurysm, they said it was instant, she wouldn't have known.*

Someone in Greywater had known first. Someone had cut a stone — or pulled one from storage, or — no. You couldn't pull this from storage. This stone had *aged*. This stone had been sitting in salt rain while Noa was alive and renting flats and sending him voice memos about her terrible coworkers, and it had been getting greener and softer-edged the whole time, and that was not possible, and yet.

And yet here was the other thing, the thing he almost didn't look at, the thing he made himself look at last, crouched in the grass with the shadows going blue between the graves:

The dates fit the stone. Not the other way around. It wasn't that someone had guessed wrong and carved 2024 hopefully. The carving was worn evenly, all of it, the name and the dates alike, as if the whole inscription had been true for exactly as long as the stone had stood. As if the stone had never known a version of events where Noa Voss died any year but this one.

As if the stone had been waiting for the date the way a stage waits for the actor.

Lior got up. His knee cracked. He picked up the bag of ashes and held it against his chest with both arms, the way you hold a sleeping child you don't want to wake, and he looked down the hill at Greywater — the buckled sea wall, the tea-colored awning, the chimney smoke going up straight in the windless cold, the drowned church's foundations visible as a dark lattice at low tide out past the harbour arm — and he felt the grief in him change state. It didn't lessen. Nothing was going to lessen it. But it curdled, the way milk curdles: same substance, new structure, something you could no longer drink.

Because here was the thought he'd been circling, the one the clerk in his head kept refusing to file:

*She called me from here.*

Not from Leeds, not from her flat. Three weeks ago — no. Longer. Five weeks ago, six, right at the beginning of November, Noa had left him a voicemail at 11:40 on a weeknight, and he'd been in a meeting and meant to call back and hadn't, because that was the kind of brother he'd been lately, the kind who let calls become voicemails become texts become *sorry, mad week, ring you Sunday.* Her message had been short and strange and slightly breathless, the sound of wind behind her, and he'd played it twice and then filed it under *Noa being Noa* and let it sit in his phone like an unexploded shell.

*I'm at Grandma's,* she'd said. *I know, I know, don't start. There's something here I need to — Lior, there's a chair. In the photos. I keep counting and it keeps being there. Call me back, okay? I mean it. Call me back.*

He hadn't. Six days later she was dead.

And now he was standing over a grave that had been ready for her since before she was born, in a town that had known, holding the phone number of a grief that had started as a question and was hardening into something with edges.

The streetlights came on down in the town, all at once, sodium-orange, and the lit windows of Greywater looked back up at him — dozens of them, small and warm and yellow, and he had the sudden unshakable sense that behind more of those windows than could be statistically likely, people were looking back. Not at the view. At him. At the man on the hill with the ashes.

*Small town,* he told himself. *Funeral tomorrow. Everyone knows.*

Everyone knows. Yes. That was exactly the problem.

He turned back to the stone one last time. The light had almost gone, and in the last of it the lichen on the granite made patterns that might have been deliberate, might have been nothing: whorls and rings spreading out from the carved letters like ripples from a dropped note. SHE SANG US HOME. Us. Not *we*, not *she is at peace* — *us*. Whoever had ordered this stone had counted themselves among the sung-home. A congregation. A choir, if you liked.

Noa had promised someone she'd never sing again.

Lior shouldered the bag and walked down out of the cemetery as the full dark came, taking the coast road toward Edith's house on Chapel Lane — the house with the extra rooms, though he didn't know that yet, the house with the photographs, though he didn't know that either. The key was where it had always been, under the loose brick by the drainpipe, wrapped in a plastic bag gone brittle with age. The door opened onto cold air and the smell of a shut-up house and, faintly, from somewhere inside — from deeper than any room should be —

someone humming.

A minor key. Unhurried. Patient as tide.

Lior stood in the doorway with his hand on the latch, the ashes heavy against his hip, and did not go in for a long moment. Then he stepped inside, and closed the door behind him, and turned on every light in the hall.

The humming did not stop.
