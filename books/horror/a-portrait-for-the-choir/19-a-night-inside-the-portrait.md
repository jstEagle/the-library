## 19. A Night Inside the Portrait

He should not have slept in the attic room.

That was the first thought he had afterward, and it was also the last thought he had before — the thought he carried to the edge of sleep like a stone to the lip of a well, held there, and then let go. Grandma's journal lay open on his chest. Rule three: *never let it learn your face.* He'd read it four times. He'd told himself he understood it.

But the attic was warm, warmer than the rest of the house, which had been cold for fifty years in a way that had nothing to do with weather, and the sailcloth bundle sat against the far wall where he'd left it, breathing its slow painted breaths, and somewhere between one blink and the next the warmth folded around him like a hand and took him under.

The dream did not begin so much as arrive, the way fog arrives on a coast — no border, just a moment when you realize you can no longer see the shore you were standing on.

* * *

He was standing on a floor of dark water that bore his weight without rippling. Above him hung a sky the color of varnish, amber and deepening toward brown at the edges, and he understood with a lurch of recognition that he was looking up at the inside of the painting's frame. The gilt edge caught light that came from nowhere. He was inside the picture. Not watching it. Standing in it.

A table stretched away from him across the water-floor, long as a church aisle, set for a meal that had been waiting a long time. Candles burned in holders of green glass, their flames perfectly still, neither guttering nor warming. Six chairs occupied the near end of the table. A seventh stood open at the head, pulled back at an angle of welcome, its seat worn pale in the shape of someone who had sat there often, long ago.

And the six figures were there.

They were seated exactly as they appeared in the portrait upstairs — Maren young, her hair still dark, hands folded; Tobias upright and thirty-something, jaw set like a man mid-argument; Ilsa with her chin lifted; two others he knew only from the attic, faces he'd studied by flashlight: a heavyset man with kind eyes, a thin woman whose mouth turned down at one corner as if she'd heard bad news she refused to repeat. And the sixth, at the far end, half-turned away, was the organist. He could tell by the hands. Even in paint, even here, those hands rested on the table like they missed a keyboard.

They were all breathing. That was the thing he hadn't been ready for. Upstairs, through sailcloth, the breathing had been faint, a rumor. Here it filled the space, six chests rising and falling in slow unison, and the sound of it was almost tender, almost like the house itself inhaling.

"Hello, Lior."

Noa's voice.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, the way voices do in dreams, but it was unmistakably hers — the slight rasp on the second word, the way she said his name like she was handing him something. His whole body turned toward it before his mind agreed to move.

She wasn't visible. The open chair at the head of the table was empty. But the air above it had begun, very slightly, to condense — the way breath fogs glass, the way Noa's shape had condensed above Hollis's font, if Hollis's font existed yet, if any of this was happening in time rather than only in the amber dark.

"You found the journal," the voice said. "You always read the instructions. You used to read the instructions on cereal boxes out loud at breakfast until I threw toast at you."

"Noa." His voice came out cracked, younger than he was. "Is that you?"

"It's what's left that sounds like me." A pause, and then, gentler, with her old honesty: "I won't lie to you in here. That's the one rule I get to keep."

"What is this place?"

"The part of the painting that isn't paint." The candle flames leaned, all together, though there was no draft. "You've been asking everyone what was taken in 1974. Nobody told you the true answer. They took a picture of a table. And a table wants people sitting at it. That's all it has ever wanted. That's all it has ever *done*."

The figures at the table did not turn to look at him. That frightened him more than if they had. Their eyes were fixed on the middle distance, on some point past the frame, and their breathing went on, patient as tide.

"They're not trapped," Noa's voice said, reading him. "I know how it looks. But they're not screaming in there, Lior. They're *resting*. That's the bargain nobody explains properly. The years it lends you, it holds the tired parts. The ache. The nights you couldn't sleep. It carries them so you don't have to. And then one day the ledger comes due and it asks for the loan back with interest, and the interest is—" the voice wavered, steadied— "the interest is everything it kept safe for you. All at once."

"Ilsa."

"Ilsa," the voice agreed. "She's sleeping now. Really sleeping. When she wakes she'll be ninety-one years old in a single morning, and every night she didn't spend being afraid will land on her like snow. She knows. She sang anyway. They all sang anyway."

"Why are you showing me this?"

Silence. The candles burned. Somewhere beyond the gilt edge of the sky, water moved — he could hear it now, a low tidal hush, the drowned chapel's memory of drowning.

"Because you're going to be asked to sit down," Noa's voice said. "Not tonight. Maybe not for weeks. But it will happen, and it will be offered kindly, by something wearing my voice or my face or my laugh, and I need you to have already decided. Deciding in advance is the only weapon you have in here."

"I'm not sitting."

"You say that now."

"*Noa.*"

"Say it again," the voice said softly. "Say my name again. It's the only thing I get to keep that's actually mine."

He said it. He said it until his throat hurt, walking the length of the table, past the six breathing figures who would not look at him, past the green-glass candles, to the seventh chair. Up close he could see it was made of the same wood as the chair in the photographs — the chair that had appeared in every family picture since 1974, empty, waiting, slowly filling. This was that chair. Of course it was. The photographs weren't haunted. They were *correspondence*. The chair in the pictures was this chair, seen from the other side of the frame, and every year it appeared a little closer to occupied because somewhere, in here, the seat was learning the shape of the person it wanted.

The shape it wanted now, worn pale into the wood, was small. Narrow shoulders. A way of sitting with one foot tucked under.

His stomach dropped.

"That's not mine," he said. "That's not my seat."

"No," the voice said. "It's mine. It was always going to be mine. I came here first, Lior. Before the voicemail. Before the funeral. I sat in it once, just once, to see. And the chair remembers everyone who ever sits in it, and now it has my shape, and when the debt comes due it will call me by it." A pause. "You're standing where I stood. Isn't that strange? We keep ending up in each other's places. Twin things do that."

He looked down at the water-floor and saw, reflected faintly in its dark surface, not his own face but hers — Noa as she'd been at nineteen, at twenty-five, at the last birthday they'd shared, layered like transparencies, all of them smiling at him from below.

"Don't look too long," the voice warned, and there was something new in it, something that sounded less like his sister and more like the house. "Rule three."

"I know the rules."

"Do you? Say them."

"Never sing after dark. Never count the rooms twice. Never let it learn your face."

"And why the last one?"

He hesitated.

"Because a face is an invitation," the voice said. "The chair doesn't take bodies. It takes *likenesses*. It sits in your photograph, it wears your profile in the paint, and then one day the likeness gets hungry enough to walk out of the frame and the body stays behind wondering why it feels lighter. That's what happened to the organist's congregation. That's what's happening to this town, slowly, the way rot happens to a pier — nobody notices until the boards give."

The six figures breathed. The candles burned. And the chair at the head of the table turned, very slowly, without touching the floor, until it faced him.

"Sit," said Noa's voice — and then, immediately, overlapping it, fighting it, "*don't* sit, Lior, don't—" and the two registers tangled like frequencies out of phase, his sister and the thing using his sister, both speaking through the same amber air, and he understood suddenly that this was the trap's true architecture: not force, never force, just longing with the volume turned up until you couldn't hear which voice was which.

"I need to know one thing," he said, and his own voice surprised him by holding steady. "One true thing. If you're really in there — if any of you is really in there — tell me something about us that the painting couldn't know. Something we never wrote down. Never photographed. Never said in front of anyone."

The tangle of voices stopped.

The silence went on so long he began to count his heartbeats, and then, distantly, he realized counting was dangerous, counting rooms, counting anything, and he made himself stop.

Then Noa's voice came back — alone, he was almost sure, almost certain, the rasp on the second syllable clean and unaccompanied — and it said:

"The summer we were eleven. The storm knocked the power out for three days and we buried a shoebox in Grandma's garden behind the hydrangeas, and you made me swear never to tell, and I never have, not to anyone, not even you. It's still there. You never dug it up because digging it up would mean admitting you remembered, and you were too proud." A breath. "There's a tooth of mine in it. And a note that says if either of us dies, the other one has to dig it up and read it out loud at the grave."

His eyes were burning. In the dream he could feel tears the way you feel weather through a window — real, but on the other side of something.

"So dig it up," the voice said. "Read it out loud. And then come back here and tell me what it says, and if you can tell me, I'll believe you're still you. And if you can't—" the voice gentled into something unbearable, something that sounded like forgiveness given in advance— "if you can't, then whatever answers won't be me, and you'll know, and you'll run, and I will not blame you. I want you to hear that. Whatever happens. I will not blame you."

The chair faced him. The seat waited, worn to her shape.

"Sit," said the house.

"Don't," said Noa.

He reached out — he would never know, afterward, whether he meant to push the chair away or pull it closer — and his fingers touched the wood.

* * *

He woke on the attic floor with the journal creased into his cheek and dawn coming gray through the sealed window, and his right hand ached as if it had gripped something hard all night.

There was a splinter in his palm. Old wood, dark, smelling faintly of varnish and salt.

He sat up too fast and the attic tilted. The sailcloth bundle against the wall was breathing — he could see it now in daylight, the faint rise and fall, faster than last night, agitated, like a sleeper disturbed. And beneath the sailcloth's edge, where the wrapping had shifted, he could see the canvas.

Seven chairs at the table now.

Six occupied. One empty, angled outward, facing the viewer. Facing him.

And the wood of that seventh chair, in the paint, bore a mark that had not been there yesterday: a small pale wear-spot on the seat, the size of a hand.

His hand.

Lior got up, wrapped the splinter in a page torn from the journal — evidence, he told himself, though evidence for whom, for what court, he couldn't say — and went downstairs on legs that didn't trust the stairs. In the kitchen he ran the tap and drank three glasses of water and did not look at the window over the sink, where the glass was fogged from the inside despite the cold.

In the fog, faintly, someone had drawn a smiley face. The way Noa used to draw them on steamed-up car windows, crooked, one eye bigger than the other.

He wiped it away with his sleeve and made himself say it out loud, to the empty kitchen, to establish the fact of his own voice: "I'm not sitting down."

The house said nothing. But upstairs, faintly, through two floors, the humming started again — and this time he recognized the tune. It was the hymn half the town had mouthed at Ilsa's funeral. The counter-hymn. Ruth's grandmother's hymn.

The house was learning it too.

He grabbed his coat. There was a name he'd written in the margin of the journal three days ago and done nothing about — *Ruth Vane, granddaughter, lives past the cannery, ask at the shop* — and he had run out of nights he was willing to spend alone with a painting that breathed. If anyone alive knew how to fight a song, it was the blood of the woman who'd sung against the flood.

Outside, the tide was coming in over the low street, early, hours ahead of the tables, and the sea was the color of varnish.
