## 32. Breaking Into the Drowned Church

The tide tables said we had four hours and eleven minutes.

Tobias had underlined the number twice in pencil, then written *do not trust the sea to be honest* beneath it, which was the most Tobias thing I'd ever seen a ninety-year-old man do with his last good handwriting. He'd wanted to come. Maren had told him no in the voice of someone nailing a coffin shut, and he'd taken it the way old soldiers take orders they know are right — badly, and in silence.

So it was six of us standing at the seawall at 3:40 in the morning: me, Ruth, Maren, and three of the choir — Petra, who sang alto and had already forgotten her mother's maiden name; Dev, who hadn't spoken above a whisper since rehearsal started costing him pieces; and young Callum, nineteen, who'd joined the choir two years ago and had no business being anywhere near this debt except that he'd sung three verses of the counter-hymn and the portrait knew his face now too.

Nobody had made him come. That was the worst part. He'd volunteered, and when Maren tried to refuse him he'd said, "You're all going to forget why this matters. Someone should remember who wasn't there for any of it." Nineteen years old and he'd understood the arithmetic better than the rest of us.

The chapel didn't look like much from the seawall. A black hump offshore where the channel bent, maybe three hundred yards out, its bell tower the only part that cleared the water even at low tide — a broken tooth against the grey sky. The town had let the causeway rot in '74 and never rebuilt it. Officially, St. Brendan's-by-the-Sea had been "lost to erosion." Unofficially, everyone in Greywater had known for fifty years that the church drowned on purpose, or as close to on purpose as a building gets, and that the water was less a grave than a lock.

"The crypt entrance is on the landward side," Ruth said. She had her grandfather's notation rolled in a waterproof tube slung across her back like a rifle, and she'd dressed for the water in a fisherman's slicker over a wetsuit she'd clearly owned longer than I'd been alive. "There's a stair that runs down from what used to be the vestry. My grandfather's journal says the crypt floods last and drains first. The builders knew what they were doing."

"Builders in 1740," Maren said. "Building a church they intended to drown someday."

"They built it to be drowned *safely*," Ruth corrected. "That's not the same thing, but it's something."

I pulled my gloves tighter. The cold coming off the channel had teeth in it. Somewhere behind us, up the hill, the shopkeeper's windows were dark, and I found myself checking them anyway. Hollis had taken the chapel basement two nights ago — locked the iron door, chained it, and started whatever rite he'd pieced together from half a ledger and a whole lot of grief. We couldn't get to him from inside. The choir's own door was sealed against them; whatever Hollis had done with the salt and the thresholds, it held. Which left the way in that nobody had used since before the flood.

Which left going down through the dead.

"You don't have to carry it," Maren said to me, for the fourth time. She stood very straight in the wind, and I noticed she kept touching her coat pocket, where the ledger rode against her ribs. Since she'd lost 1974 — since she'd sung verse after verse and let the hymn eat the founding sin right out of her head — she carried the book everywhere, like a prosthetic memory. She couldn't tell you anymore why she felt guilty. She just knew the weight of it, the way an amputee knows the itch.

"I do, though," I said. "Kin of the debtor. Your rules, not mine."

"Our rules," she agreed quietly. "Yes."

Ruth looked between us and said nothing. She'd made her terms clear and hadn't softened them: the choir sings, the choir pays, and if Lior Voss thought he could stand outside this thing clean, he was fooling himself. But she'd come anyway. Whatever she thought of these people, her grandfather had sung against the flood until the water took the organ and then took him, and I think carrying his notation back into that church was the closest thing to a resurrection she'd ever get.

"Low tide's at 4:15," Dev whispered. "We go now or we swim."

We went.

* * *

The crypt door was exactly where Ruth's journal promised, behind a curtain of bladderwrack and fifty years of barnacle, a granite arch with a lintel carved in letters worn nearly smooth. Ruth put her palm flat against the stone and read by touch.

"*Memento mori,*" she said. "And underneath — smaller — *sed non semper.* Remember death. But not always."

"Somebody had a sense of humor," Petra said.

"Somebody had a theology," Ruth said. "It's worse."

The door itself was oak gone soft as bread. Callum put his shoulder into it and it gave with a sound like a rib cracking, exhaling air that had been shut in the dark since before any of us were born. It smelled the way my grandmother's house smelled in the rooms that weren't on the blueprints — salt and candle smoke and something older underneath, something like a cellar where apples have rotted for generations. Like sweetness that gave up.

I went first. Kin of the debtor. Nobody argued.

Our headlamps cut cones through the black, and the black was thick enough to have texture. The stair descended in tight spirals, each step dished in the middle by feet that had stopped walking on it two centuries ago. Water ran down the walls in threads, catching our light, moving wrong — not dripping so much as *climbing*, slow as syrup, upward along the stone toward the sea. I watched one thread for three steps and decided I hadn't seen it. There was a limit to what I could metabolize before dawn.

"Talk," Ruth said behind me. Her voice bounced strange in the spiral. "Keep talking. Places like this fill silence with suggestions."

So we talked. Petra recited the alto line of the counter-hymn under her breath, not singing it — never singing it, not down here, not after dark, rule two of grandma's journal — just fingering the notes on her knee like a pianist. Dev named the streets of Greywater in alphabetical order. Callum listed every dog he'd ever owned. I talked about Noa. Not the sharp memories — those I was saving, hoarding, rationing like the last of a medicine I'd soon need to spend — but the safe ones. How she chewed pens. How she lost arguments on purpose when she was tired. How she'd once convinced a substitute teacher that our school had a second gymnasium, underground, and kept the lie alive for a full semester.

"She would've hated this," Callum said.

"She'd have loved it," I said. "She'd have hated how much she loved it."

The stair ended at a landing, and the landing ended at a door, and the door was iron, and the iron was wet on the *inside* of the corridor, though the corridor was dry. Rust had eaten it into lace around the hinges. Maren pushed past me and set her hands on it.

"This is the undercroft," she said. "Below the nave. When the tide's out, there's air above the waterline in the main hall — a few feet. Enough to breathe if you keep your head down and don't panic." She paused. "My grandmother described it to me. She helped build the seal on the other side, in '74. She said—" Maren stopped. Frowned. Reached for something that wasn't there anymore, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth. "She said something about it. I had it yesterday."

"It's all right," I said.

"It isn't," she said, "but it's necessary," and she shouldered the iron door, and the rust-lace gave, and the sea breathed out at us.

* * *

The nave of St. Brendan's-by-the-Sea was a cathedral turned aquarium. Our lights couldn't find the ceiling; they found water instead, black and still as poured glass, filling the upper reaches of the space where the roof must have been. The floor was exposed — flagstones furred with weed, a foot of brine pooled in the low places — and the pillars rose out of it like the legs of drowned giants, each one wrapped to shoulder height in kelp that hung motionless, waiting. Along both walls, the stained glass survived in fragments, and when our beams touched it the colors came up out of the dark like things surfacing: a blue here, a wound-red there, saints with their faces dissolved by salt, their halos intact.

Nobody spoke. Ruth had been right about the silence. In a place like this, silence wasn't empty. It was occupied.

We moved single file along the north wall, following Ruth's grandfather's directions — *keep to the stones that ring true*, and she tapped each flagstone with a boat hook before she trusted it, a dull knock for solid rock, a hollow slosh for the voids where the floor had given way to the channels the builders had cut. Tap. Knock. Tap. Slosh. Around us. The water in the pools trembled without wind.

Halfway down the nave, Dev stopped whispering his street names.

I turned. His light was fixed on the far wall, on the space behind the altar, and I followed it and understood.

The wall was there. And the wall was bare.

Fifty years underwater, and the plaster behind the altar was pale and clean, a rectangle of untouched stone the size of a large painting, outlined by a margin of grime like a bruise around a healed wound. Everything else in this church wore the sea. The sea had never touched that spot. Or — I corrected myself, stomach turning — the sea had never been allowed to.

"It knows we're looking at it," Petra said softly.

"It doesn't know anything," I said. "It's paint."

"Lior." Maren's voice was gentle and terrible. "You've seen it breathe."

I had. Six faces in sailcloth-wrapped oil, chests rising and falling by a millimeter, breaths spaced minutes apart, patient as geology. I'd put my hand near the canvas once and felt warmth come off it like off a sleeping animal. *It's paint* was a sentence I'd been saying to myself for weeks, and every day it cost more to say.

"Keep moving," Ruth said. "Crypt's below the tower. Come on."

We came on. And as we crossed the crossing — the exact center of the nave, under the invisible vault — the water above us moved.

Not much. A shiver across the surface, high in the dark, spreading outward in a perfect ring, as if something enormous down in the flooded rafters had turned over in its sleep. Kelp swayed on pillars where there was no current. Far above, at the limit of my light, something pale drifted past the broken roof-line, unhurried, and did not come back.

Callum grabbed my sleeve. I let him. We were all holding onto somebody.

* * *

The crypt stairs went down again, below the waterline, into a low barrel-vaulted chamber where the air tasted of iron and iodine. Sarcophagi lined the walls — local gentry, long dead, their effigies worn faceless — and in the middle of the floor, ringed by a channel of standing brine, was the font.

Or what had been the font. It had been a baptismal basin once, granite, plain as a feeding trough. Now the basin was dry, and the channel around it was full, and someone had spent the last two days making the geometry mean something. Salt lay in lines on the flags — careful, deliberate, a diagram in mineral — and candles burned at five points of it, their flames burning *downward*, thin blue tongues pointing at the stone, and I decided, firmly, not to think about that either.

Hollis's work. He'd come in through some way we hadn't found, or the chapel had let him in because grief is a key the lock was always cut for. The sailcloth was gone from where the choir had hidden it — no. I stopped myself. The portrait wasn't here. Tobias had confirmed it that morning, white-faced: the attic in grandma's house stood empty, the wrapping folded neat as a flag on the floor. Hollis had it. He'd taken it home first, done whatever preparation his father's drowned ghost demanded, and then brought it here to finish it.

But the rite wasn't finished. The candles were fresh. The salt lines were crisp-edged. And on the font's rim, laid out with terrible care, was a photograph.

I knew it before I reached it. Of course I did. It had hung in grandma's hallway my entire childhood: Noa and me at eight years old, gap-toothed, on the beach at Greywater, squinting into a sun that had probably never been that warm. Except that in this version — in the version Hollis had carried down here and set on the stone like an offering or a hostage — the empty chair was in the picture. It stood on the sand beside us, wooden, patient. And sitting in it, faint as breath on glass, was a shape with Noa's profile.

"He's close," Maren said. She crouched at the edge of the salt diagram without crossing it, reading it the way Ruth read stone. "This is binding-work. Old stuff. He's not trying to make the portrait give Noa back." Her hand hovered over the lines. "He's trying to make the portrait *be* Noa. Fuse her into it. Give it a face it can wear, a voice it can use, and then it belongs to him. A person in a frame he controls." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious. "He thinks he's saving her. Lior. He genuinely thinks—"

"I know what he thinks." My voice came out flat. "I got the offer too."

The photograph sat on the font. The shape in the chair sat in the chair. And I want to be honest, because this chapter deserves honesty: for about four seconds, I considered leaving it exactly where it was. Walking away. Letting Hollis bind her, fuse her, keep her — a Noa-shaped thing humming in oil and salt, a sister I could visit, a voice I could talk to, real enough to answer. Four seconds. Long enough to feel the whole weight of it, the whole temptation, warm as bathwater.

Then I remembered her voicemail. *It's sitting in your chair now.* Not *help me.* Not *come back.* Fear in her voice. Whoever was in the photographs, whoever was filling the chairs, it wasn't Noa resting. It was Noa caught. And Hollis, with all his love and all his ruin, was about to weld the cage shut forever and call it mercy.

"We take it back," I said. "Tonight. Before the tide turns and before he comes back for the next stage."

"And if he's already here?" Dev whispered.

The question had barely landed when the water in the channel around the font began to move — slowly, deliberately, counterclockwise, a drain spinning in reverse. The candle flames leaned east, all five together, toward the tower stairs. And from somewhere above us, muffled by forty feet of black water and stone, came a sound none of us had heard in this place yet, and all of us recognized instantly.

Somebody was singing.

A single voice, male, cracked with age and salt, working its way down through the flooded nave — not the counter-hymn, nothing holy, something with a grind and a hunger to it, a rhytidote, a wrenching song, the song of a man pulling on a rope with both hands.

Hollis was in the church. Hollis was above us. And the tide, Tobias had warned us, does not always keep its promises.

Ruth unslung the notation tube from her back and held it against her chest like a shield, or a weapon, or a promise.

"Then we'd better get up there," she said, "before he finishes the verse."
