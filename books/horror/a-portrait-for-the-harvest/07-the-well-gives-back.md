## 7. The Well Gives Back

The well stood behind the house like a mouth that had forgotten how to close.

Emrys had known it was there since childhood—every child who visited Hollowmere knew the well, the way they knew the loose step and the smell of the cellar—but he had never once looked into it. Children didn't. Something in the bones of them understood that the well was not a hole but a held breath, and you did not ask a house to exhale.

He stood at its lip now in the grey hour before dawn, Aldous's truck still idling in the lane behind him, his father's voice still ringing in his skull like a struck pipe.

*Burn it down, Emrys. Burn it and walk away and never say her name again.*

But the frost on the kitchen window had spelled *ALDOUS* in letters a foot high, and when Emrys had called him—half hoping for denial, half hoping for anything—the silence on the line had lasted eleven seconds. He'd counted. Counting was what he did. Eleven seconds of a man breathing through his teeth, and then the click, and then the dial tone humming like a hymn sung by someone who had forgotten all the words.

Aldous had come anyway. Of course he had. He'd arrived at three in the morning with a can of petrol in one hand and a look on his face Emrys had never seen before: not anger, not fear, but the naked pleading of a man watching his own reflection begin to speak.

Now his father sat in the truck with the engine running and the doors locked, as if glass and steel could be counted the way Emrys counted lights.

The well's stone lip was furred with moss gone black at the edges. A wooden cover lay beside it, rotted soft as bread, and across its face someone had carved a single word long ago, the grooves packed with decades of grime:

*MAREN.*

Not a warning. A marker. The way you marked a grave.

"Son." Aldous's voice came thin through the closed window. "There is nothing down there worth finding. I promise you that. Whatever it's showing you, it's lying."

"It isn't showing me anything," Emrys said. "It's just letting me see."

He fetched the rope from the shed—the good hemp rope he used for hauling traps out of crawlspaces—and tested it against the iron ring set into the wellhead, an ring polished bright, brighter than any other metal on the property, as if hands had gripped it often. As if hands had been lowered down it often. Or raised up.

His flashlight beam went down and down and found no bottom, only a softness to the dark, like velvet, like soil that had been waiting a long time to be asked about something.

*She recorded a harvest visitor,* the diary had said. *No one else would acknowledge him. He came for a debt, and the debt was Papa's, and Maren saw everything.* And then the entries stopped mid-sentence, October 1974, the ink trailing off the page in a line like a pulse going flat.

Emrys tied the rope around his waist with a bowline his fingers tied without instruction. His body knew knots. His body knew dark places. His whole forgettable life had been preparation for descending into holes where other people's secrets lived.

"Emrys." His father was out of the truck now, standing at the edge of the yard, and his voice cracked open like river ice. "Please. I am asking you as your father. I have never asked you for anything."

That was true. That was horribly true. In fifty-one years Aldous Ravensworth had never asked his son for help, or company, or forgiveness, or even attention. He had paid for things. He had sent cards with money folded inside instead of words. And now here he was in the frost-grey dawn, begging, and the begging itself was a confession larger than any sentence he could have spoken.

"You asked me to stay blind," Emrys said quietly. "You've been asking me that my whole life. Every time you changed the subject. Every time you looked through me at dinner. You were practicing."

He swung his legs over the lip.

The cold rose up to meet him like water, though there was no water. It climbed his boots, his knees, his chest, tasting of iron and old apples and something sweeter underneath, something like the inside of a locked pantry. He paid out the rope hand over hand, and the light shrank above him to a coin, and the walls of the well slid past—stone, mortar, stone—and then, at the depth where the daylight gave out entirely, the walls changed.

They were scratched.

Thousands upon thousands of fine scratches, overlapping, layered, some shallow and some gouged deep enough to hold shadow. Not claw marks. Emrys knew claw marks the way priests know scripture. These were fingernail marks. These were the marks of hands that had climbed this wall for years, patiently, hopelessly, the way ivy climbs, because stopping was not something living hands could do.

He stopped counting them. Some numbers are mercies withheld.

His feet touched bottom.

The floor of the well was soft earth, and it gave beneath his boots like a mattress, like a bed recently slept in, and the smell hit him—not decay, not exactly, but the sweet-dry smell of attics and harvest lofts, of corn husks and paper and time. He turned in a slow circle, and the beam of his light found what it found.

Bones first. Small-boned, fine, arranged with terrible neatness against the far wall, seated upright in the posture of someone waiting to be introduced. A dress had gone to lace around them, a pattern of small flowers he recognized from the photographs—Maren at the harvest table, 1973, laughing at something outside the frame. Around the neck, resting on the collarbone where a throat had been, hung a locket on a chain gone green with age.

And beside the bones, placed carefully, deliberately, the way a child places a toy where it will be found—

One shoe.

Small. Brown leather. A buckle strap. The kind of shoe a boy wears when he is four years old, when he is visiting his grandmother for the harvest, when he wanders away from the adults because the adults are busy arguing in voices that frighten him, when he follows his aunt Maren out to the well because she is the only one who ever looks directly at him, ever says his name like it matters, ever *sees* him—

Emrys picked it up.

It fit his memory the way a key fits a lock. He remembered the buckle. He remembered the strap that pinched. He remembered crying when it was lost, and being told not to fuss, and the strange blankness in his father's face when he said *where is Aunt Maren*, and how quickly everyone agreed that Maren had gone away, that families do that, that little boys forget.

He had never forgotten. He had only been made to believe he had. The forgetting had been installed in him like insulation, packed into every year since, and now, holding his own small shoe in a dead woman's grave, he felt it all come loose at once and fall through him like rot through a floor.

"I was here," he whispered. "I saw."

The well breathed.

There was no other word for it. The darkness drew in around him and then released, a slow exhalation that stirred the dust off Maren's bones and lifted the fine hairs on his arms, and it carried a scent like the last warm day of October, and it carried—faintly, unmistakably—a sound. Not a voice. A sigh of relief so vast it might have been the house itself, the whole structure of Hollowmere settling on its foundations the way a dog settles after standing guard too long.

Above him, faintly, came the creak of hinges.

One door. Then another. Then another, each one distinct, each one familiar, and Emrys counted them without meaning to, the ritual rising in him like a reflex—front, back, pantry, cellar, the cupboard under the stairs, the little door to the coal chute—one, two, three, four, five, six, seven.

Every door he had counted shut stood open.

He climbed. The rope burned his palms and he did not care; the scratches on the wall slid past his face and he did not look at them again, though he felt them looking at him, felt the whole history of those hands pressed flat against the stone on either side of his ascent like a congregation. The coin of light widened. The cold loosened its grip on his ankles, his knees, his chest, reluctantly, the way sleepers release a blanket.

He came over the lip into a morning gone gold.

Frost covered the entire yard—not patches, not patterns, but a uniform white sheet, except where it wasn't. In the frost, in letters ten feet tall, written across the garden from the well to the fence as if by a finger the size of weather:

*THANK YOU.*

And beneath it, smaller, almost shy:

*NOW HIM.*

Aldous stood at the edge of the writing. He had come out of the truck at some point—he must have, he must have heard the doors opening, heard the house exhale—and now he stood very still with the petrol can dangling from his hand, staring at the frost, at the word *HIM*, and his face had gone the colour of the frost itself.

"She's free," Emrys said. His voice came out scraped raw. He climbed fully out of the well and stood, swaying, the locket in one fist and the small brown shoe in the other. "Whatever she was owed—it isn't finished, Dad. It's not the locket. It never was the locket."

Aldous's eyes went to the shoe. To the small brown shoe in his grown son's hand, and something behind his face collapsed inward, storey by storey, like a house being demolished from the attic down.

"No," he said. "No, no—you were asleep. You were *asleep*, I checked, I made sure—" He stopped. His mouth kept working after the words ran out.

"You made sure," Emrys repeated. The words tasted like well-water. "You made sure a four-year-old was asleep, and then you and Grandfather carried her out here, and I woke up, didn't I. I followed her. I watched. And she—" his voice broke, and he let it, because breaking was the point, breaking was the whole price of admission— "she hid me somewhere. She hid my shoe so no one would know a child had seen. She protected me while you buried her."

The frost letters began, slowly, to melt. But the air did not lighten. If anything it thickened, gathered, the way air gathers before a storm that has decided which tree it will strike. Behind the house, behind the gold windows, something vast shifted its weight.

Aldous dropped the petrol can and turned for the truck.

He got three steps. Then the frost took his feet—not violently, not dramatically; the white simply climbed his boots the way it had climbed the windows, patient and alphabetic, spelling something up his legs that Emrys could not read and did not want to. Aldous looked back at his son with an expression stripped of everything but the truth.

"The feast," Emrys said. "Sunday. The whole village will be there."

His father's lips moved. Whether it was *please* or *don't* or *her name*, even he seemed no longer sure.

Emrys closed his fist around the locket until he felt its shape print itself into his palm, and began to walk toward the house to wash the well off his hands.

Behind him, softly, every open door in Hollowmere began, one by one, to close.
