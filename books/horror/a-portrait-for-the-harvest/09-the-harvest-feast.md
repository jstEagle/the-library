## 9. The Harvest Feast

The locket weighed almost nothing. That was the first thing Emrys understood as he walked the mile from the house into the village, the little brass oval cupped in his palm like a sleeping bird. He had expected it to drag at him, to pull his arm down the way guilt was supposed to, but it was lighter than a key, lighter than a coin, and somehow that lightness was worse. Fifty years of silence, and it fit in a child's fist.

The frost had not left his father's hands. Aldous sat in the kitchen of the house on Hollow Lane with his fingers wrapped in tea towels Emrys had soaked in warm water, and he had not spoken since the well, except once, when Emrys had told him what he meant to do.

*They'll take the farm,* his father had said. *They'll take the name. Everything your grandmother built.*

*She built it on Maren,* Emrys had answered, and his father had turned his ruined hands over in his lap and looked at them as though they belonged to a stranger, which, Emrys supposed, in a way they now did.

The village green had been dressed since morning. Garlands of wheat and rowan berries looped from the maypole to the churchyard wall, and long trestle tables ran the length of the green under strings of yellow bulbs that buzzed like trapped wasps in the cold. The harvest feast was the one night a year when Hollow Vale stopped pretending it wasn't watching itself — every family out in the open, every grudge and marriage and debt displayed over pies and cider like silverware.

Emrys stood at the edge of it for a long time, at the border where the lamplight gave out and the dark fields began, and he felt the old instinct rise in him like water in a cellar: *be forgettable, be furniture, be the man nobody remembers was ever in the room.* Fifty years of Ravensworth survival lived in that instinct. His father had survived by it. His grandmother had survived by it. Maren had not survived it.

The locket was warm in his hand. It should not have been warm. The night was cold enough to see his breath, and the brass should have been a piece of ice against his skin, but it held the heat of him, patient, the way the house held its heat, the way the extra chair held its place in every photograph, waiting.

*They are all here,* said the voice that was not a voice, the one he had stopped pretending was the wind. It came from behind him, from the direction of the house, from the direction of everywhere. *Every throat that swallowed the silence. Go and open yours.*

He crossed the green.

* * *

The first faces that turned to him did so the way faces turn to weather — a flicker of notice, then away. He was the Ravensworth boy, the one who'd gone off and come back strange, the pest man, the quiet one. Mrs. Calloway from the post office gave him a nod over her cider. Old Tam Weller, who had been a boy of nineteen in the autumn of 1974, was telling a story with his hands and did not break rhythm at all.

Emrys stood at the end of the nearest trestle table and put the locket down on the boards between a dish of damson jam and a basket of apples, and the sound it made was very small, and the sound it made was also the loudest thing on the green, because the music stopped.

Not gradually. Not with a fumble or a laugh. The fiddler simply set his bow down, mid-phrase, as though he had finished, and the buzz of the yellow bulbs deepened into something like a held breath, and one by one the faces came around to Emrys the second time, and stayed.

"I have something to say," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "It won't be pleasant. I'd ask you to let me finish, but I know you won't be able to stop listening. None of us ever could."

Tam Weller's hands had gone still in the air.

"My aunt was Maren Ravensworth," Emrys said. "My grandmother Edith's daughter. And in the autumn of 1974, she disappeared, and this village agreed — without ever once saying it aloud — to forget her. You let the house go quiet. You let the room stay empty. You let my family keep the chair."

He heard the word land. *Chair.* He saw it move through them like a draught under a door — the oldest ones first, Tam Weller and the Priors and old Mrs. Hedgerow, whose faces went the color of the frost on their windshields — and then the younger ones, who had grown up in a village with a hole in it and never been told the hole's name.

"There was a man," Emrys went on. "A visitor, at the house, that harvest. He came to collect a debt. Not money. My father — Aldous Ravensworth — had done something, years before, in the fields beyond the quarry, something that killed a man, and the visitor had come to make the family pay. Maren saw him. Maren heard everything. She wrote it in her diary, every word, because she was nineteen years old and she believed that the truth was a thing you wrote down."

The yellow bulbs dimmed, all together, as though a great body had passed between them and the sky.

"And my father couldn't let that stand. And my grandmother couldn't let that stand. And this village — *your* fathers and mothers, *your* aunts and uncles, some of you sitting here tonight —" his voice cracked, and he let it, "— looked at the empty chair and said nothing, year after year, harvest after harvest, because the silence was cheaper than the truth. She didn't run away. She didn't go to London, she didn't drown in the canal. She went into the dry well behind the house, and she never came out, and what was left of her has been down there for fifty years holding a locket with her own name in it because no one else would."

He picked up the locket. It was warm. It was so warm now it nearly burned, and he opened it, and held it up so the bulbs caught the two small portraits inside — Edith, young, and a face no one on the green had seen in half a century, a girl with dark hair and a crooked smile, nineteen forever.

"Her name," Emrys said, "was Maren Ravensworth. Say it. I'm asking you. I'm not asking you to forgive my family — God knows we haven't earned it. I'm asking you to say her name, tonight, out loud, in the open air, where the frost can hear it. Because something has been keeping her house warm for fifty years, and it is not a ghost, and it is not a rat, and I have finally understood what it wants. It doesn't want the locket. It never wanted the locket. It wants the silence *broken*. It feeds on what we don't say, and we have been feeding it since 1974, and I am done feeding it."

The green was absolutely still. Somewhere beyond the churchyard, in the dark fields, the wind had stopped — not gentled, not lulled, *stopped*, the way a listener stops breathing.

And behind Emrys, at his back, the air began to gather.

He did not turn. He knew better than to turn. He felt it the way you feel a storm front come over a hill — a pressure, a density, a cold that had intention in it. The temperature of the whole green dropped in a single breath. The cider in the cups grew skins of ice. Frost bloomed up the trestle table legs in ferns and feathers, and every window in the houses that ringed the green went white at once, and in that whiteness, Emrys knew without looking, letters were forming. He did not need to read them. He had read them every morning for a week. *GUILT. ALDOUS. MAREN. SPEAK.*

"Say her name," he said again, and now his voice was not steady at all, and that was right too, because this was not a steady thing. "Please. For fifty years she has been the extra chair. Let her be the name."

Tam Weller stood up. His chair went over backwards and he didn't catch it. He was crying without any noise, an old man's silent weeping, and his mouth worked twice before anything came out.

"Maren," he said. "Maren Ravensworth. She taught me to skip stones on the canal. I never — I *knew*, I always knew something — Maren. Maren."

And the green broke open like ice on a spring river. Mrs. Calloway said it. Old Mrs. Hedgerow said it, both hands pressed to her mouth. The Priors said it, and their grown children said it, and the children too young to understand said it because everyone else was saying it, a ragged chorus rising off the trestle tables, *Maren, Maren Ravensworth, Maren*, and with every voice the pressure at Emrys's back changed — the cold did not lift but it *turned*, it turned like a great animal turning toward a sound it had waited fifty years to hear, and the frost on the windows began to spell something new, something none of them could see but all of them felt, and Emrys, with tears freezing in the creases of his face, understood that it was not an accusation anymore.

It was a thank-you.

The locket in his hand grew lighter still, and then lighter than nothing, and he looked down and it was fading — not tarnishing, not dissolving, but *arriving*, going somewhere it had been owed for half a century, and in the last instant before it left his palm he felt it pulse once, like a heart remembering its rhythm, and then his hand was empty and the air smelled, impossibly, of cut wheat and woodsmoke and a girl's hair.

The bulbs came back up. The fiddler, weeping, lifted his bow and did not play.

And Emrys felt the presence behind him lean down close, closer than weather, closer than a shoulder, and the not-voice came one final time, and it was gentle now, gentle the way a house is gentle when it has finally gotten what it wanted, and it said:

*One debt remains. The one who kept the lights on. Come home, Emrys. The chair is set for two.*

He looked down the mile of dark road, toward the house on Hollow Lane, where every window — every window he had ever counted, every door he had ever locked — now blazed with warm yellow light, like a house setting its table.

Behind him, the village was still saying her name.

He began to walk.
