## 4. The Excavation Crew

The ground at Hollowmere lied, and Odile Ferro had been digging long enough to know when a lie was being told to her face.

She stood at the lip of the trench with her tea going cold in one hand and the site plan flapping against her knee in the other, and she looked down at where Danny's mini-digger had bitten into the lawn and struck something that should not have been there. The plan said garden. The plan said topsoil over clay over gravel, six feet of nothing worth a man's wages before they hit the old culvert line running toward the ha-ha. The plan had been drawn from county records by a man in an office who had never put a spade in this particular earth.

The ground said otherwise.

"Again," she called down, and Danny worked the bucket back into the wound he'd opened, and the teeth scraped along stone with a sound like a fingernail drawn the length of a coffin lid.

It wasn't the sound that bothered her. She'd heard stone under turf a hundred times — old walls, forgotten footings, a farmer's folly sunk into a field somewhere and left for the worms. What bothered her was the shape of it. From up here, looking down into the cut, she could see the curve of it running away north-south, dead straight, too regular for any ruin. And it was *clean*. There was no root mat clinging to it, no soil welded on in forty years of wet and frost. The clay fell away from that stone like water off a duck's back, like the earth itself had agreed not to touch it.

"Basement," said Danny, when she climbed down. He said it the way men say things they want to be true so they can go home on time.

"That's no basement." She crouched, ran her thumb along the exposed edge. Granite, or something wearing granite's clothes, and cold enough that her skin wanted to stick to it. "Basements don't run a hundred feet without a corner."

"So it's a wall."

"It's a wall where there's no house." She straightened up and looked back at Hollowmere itself, sitting on its rise behind its yew hedge with the grey morning stacked against its windows. The house looked at her the way houses do, which is to say it didn't, which is to say she felt it doing it anyway. "Job says drainage. Job says find the outfall, lay the new run, tie into the main. Job does not say excavate a mystery."

Danny grinned. He was twenty-six and thought everything was a story he'd get to tell in the pub. "Mystery pays time-and-a-half if you bill it as unforeseen conditions."

"Bill it as whatever you like. Get me ten feet exposed by lunch."

She climbed out of the trench and did not look back at the stone, because looking felt like something you shouldn't do twice.

* * *

By Thursday they had thirty feet of it exposed, and Odile had stopped calling it anything at all, even in her head.

Thirty feet of dressed granite, four feet below the lawn, running arrow-straight from the yew hedge toward the ha-ha, and then — this was the part that made her sit down on the spoil heap with her hard hat off and her hands flat on her knees — it turned. A clean right angle, cut sharp as a carpenter's mark, and after the turn it kept going, parallel to the house's east front, close enough that if the house had a cellar it would share a wall with whatever this was.

A structure. Under the garden. As big as the house above it, or near enough, and no record of it anywhere. She'd pulled the deeds through the county archive's online catalogue on Wednesday night, sitting in her van in the layby with a bag of chips going soft beside her, and the records were thin in a way that made her professional skin crawl. Not missing — thin. Like a book with pages pressed together. The house was there, dated 1847, the Ashcombe name all through it, but the plans attached to the later filings showed the grounds as empty as a cleared table. No outbuildings beyond the stable block. No structures under the lawn.

Stone doesn't lie about how long it's been buried. But paper lies all the time, and Odile had built a whole life on trusting paper, because paper was what separated a job from a gamble, a wage from a whim. Her father had poured concrete for forty years and told her once, when she'd got her first level and her first transit: *Numbers don't care whose feelings are hurt. That's why you trust them.* She'd carried that like a tool in her kit ever since. Numbers didn't care. Numbers kept you honest and safe and employed.

The numbers here said there was a building under the garden that had never been built.

"Could be a folly," offered Priya, who was their best hand and too sensible to offer follies. "Rich family, 1840s, they did stupid things with money."

"Follies show up in estate papers. Rich families *liked* showing off their stupid things." Odile turned her flask in her hands. Above them, at the drawing-room window, a curtain moved. Old lady Ashcombe, watching. Or nobody. "And follies aren't warm."

Priya looked at her. "Say again?"

"Warm." Odile heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used for bad news on jobs. "Danny brushed the soil off that corner section yesterday afternoon. I put my palm on it this morning to check for frost damage before we load it. Frost damage is my department; I've got more experience of frozen stone than I've got of most things." She paused. The wind moved the yews and made no other sound anywhere. "That stone was body-warm. Not sun-warm — it was shaded, and the air's four degrees. Body-warm. Like putting your hand on the flank of a horse."

Priya said nothing for a while. Then, because she was Priya: "Ground source heat. Old system, some Victorian experiment, pipes running—"

"Pipes don't make stone feel like *skin*, Priya."

"No," Priya admitted. "They don't."

They sat with that. Down in the trench, Tam was working alone with the shovel, clearing the last of the clay off the turn in the wall, and his movements had a quality Odile had noticed for three days now and hadn't named: too steady. No wasted motion, no fidgeting, none of the seventeen-year-old restlessness that usually meant she had to ride him every twenty minutes just to keep him on task. He dug like a metronome. He dug like something was keeping time inside him.

"Tam!" she called. "Break. Come up and eat something."

He stopped. He looked at the stone first — laid his free hand flat on it, briefly, the way you'd touch someone's arm to say *wait* — and then he climbed the ladder and came and sat between her and Priya and ate his sandwiches like a normal boy, and Odile told herself the shiver that went up her neck was the wind.

* * *

The sleepwalking started on Sunday night, though Odile didn't learn of it until Monday, and by then it had happened twice.

She learned of it because Tam's mum rang her at half six in the morning, and the woman's voice had the specific cracked steadiness of a parent who has decided to be calm and is spending everything she has to do it. He'd woken at two in the morning standing in the back garden in his pyjamas, barefoot on frost, facing west. Facing, though neither of them knew it yet, toward Hollowmere. His eyes open. Saying something under his breath, over and over, in a rhythm more than words. When she shook him he came back confused and freezing and swore he'd gone to bed at eleven.

Second time, Monday night — except Odile found out about it Tuesday morning from Tam himself, who came to her at the vans before the others arrived, hunched in his hi-vis with his hands jammed in his pockets, and said, "Boss, can I tell you something you're not allowed to laugh at?"

"I never laugh at anything before eight," Odile said. "Go on."

He told her. Last night, same thing, except this time his mum had followed him out and watched him walk to the gate and stand there with his hand on the latch, and when she asked him where he was going he answered — asleep, eyes shut, feet blue on the flags — *"To finish it."*

"And then?" Odile asked, because Tam had stopped.

"Then I let go of the gate and walked back to bed." He swallowed. "I don't remember any of it. Mum's got it on her phone, she filmed the second night, she thought — I dunno what she thought. You can see me talking. Boss, I don't know what I'm saying. It's not English. Or it is, but—" He rubbed his face with both hands. "I've never sleptwalked in my life. Never. And it's only since we hit the stone."

Odile looked at him for a long moment. Seventeen years old, ears still too big for his head, a boy she'd taken on because his uncle vouched for him and because every crew needs a kid to teach, and she could see he was frightened in the deep way, the way you get frightened when your own body stops being yours.

"Right," she said, and heard herself reach for the register she trusted, the one with numbers in it. "Here's what happens. You see your GP this week — I'll pay the hours, put it through as a wellness visit, I don't care. Sleep study if the GP says so. And until then you're on days only, no early starts, and you tell me the minute anything else happens. Anything. You hear voices, you dream funny, you so much as stub your toe sideways, I want to know."

"It's the house, isn't it," Tam said. Not a question. "Everyone thinks it. Danny jokes about it but he locks his van door now. Priya won't take the trench shift alone. Nobody's saying it, but it's the house."

"The house is a building, Tam. Buildings are timber and brick and damp. They don't want things."

He looked at her with those too-earnest young eyes, and what he said next she would turn over for many nights afterward, turning it like a stone in the hand, looking for the seam.

"You touched it too," he said. "The stone. I saw you. You left your hand on it longer than you needed to."

She had no answer for that, so she gave him the answer she had instead, which was work: "Trench, nine o'clock, and don't be late."

* * *

That evening she stayed late. She told herself it was for the paperwork — the variation order for the unforeseen structure, which she had to draft whether or not she believed in it, because the client's solicitor would want it itemized and itemization was the closest thing her trade had to prayer. The light went long and amber across the lawn, and the spoil heaps went blue-shadowed, and the exposed granite drank the last of the sun and gave nothing back.

She walked the trench alone, slow, with her torch, reading the wall the way she'd read a thousand walls. Dressed blocks, each one taller than her forearm, fitted without mortar she could find — no, not without mortar. Without *visible* joints. The courses met so finely she couldn't slide a blade into them, and she tried, kneeling in the clay with her knife, and the knife tip skated away off stone that should have had a seam and didn't.

At the turn, where the wall bent toward the house, the clay had fallen away overnight — frost-lift, she told herself, though the nights hadn't been cold enough — and exposed a stretch lower down than they'd yet reached. And set into that stretch, flush with the courses, was a panel. Door-sized. Door-proportioned, anyway: the eye read *door* before the mind objected, the way the eye reads a face in a knot of wood.

There was no handle. There was no hinge visible. There was no gap around its edges, no shadow-line, nothing to suggest it opened or had ever opened. It was simply a rectangle of the same granite, slightly smoother than its neighbours, the way a cheek is smoother than a forehead.

Odile crouched before it with her torch beam steady on it, and her breath hung white in the cold, and she became aware that she was holding her own breath, and made herself breathe.

"Unforeseen conditions," she said aloud, to hear a human voice, her own, flat and practical and hers. "Variation number one. One door, subterranean, non-functional. Bill it as decorative masonry."

The stone was warm. She knew it would be before she touched it, and she touched it anyway, laid her whole palm flat against the smooth rectangle, and the warmth came through her glove like a hand answering a hand.

And beneath her palm — faint, rhythmic, patient as tide — she felt it move. Not opening. Nothing so kind. A pressure, from the far side, applied and released. Applied and released. The way a thing knocks when it has knocked a long time and learned no one is coming, and knocks anyway.

Odile Ferro took her hand off the stone, stood up, and walked back to her van at exactly the pace of a woman who was not running, and sat in the driver's seat, and wrote in the site diary, in her square forewoman's hand: *Day 9. Structure confirmed, extent unknown. Recommend structural survey prior to further excavation.* All true. All numbers. All the paper armor she had.

Then she sat in the dark with the engine off and admitted the other thing, the thing with no column in any form she'd ever filed: that tomorrow morning she would come back. That she had already decided. That the knocking had sounded less like a threat than like a request, and that she — forty-one years old, practical as a spirit level, raised on her father's gospel of numbers that don't care — had stood in a trench in the dark with her hand on a buried door and felt, unmistakably, absurdly, *wanted*.

Her phone buzzed against the dash. Tam's mum, forwarding the video she'd mentioned, *so you know I'm not making it up.*

Odile watched it twice. A boy in pyjamas crossing frost-white grass, unhurried, certain, his lips moving. She turned the volume all the way up and held the phone close, and under the wind she caught it — the rhythm of him, the repeated syllables, rising and falling like a knock on wood.

Like a knock. In the exact rhythm of the thing she'd felt through the stone.

She started the van. Behind her, up on its rise, Hollowmere House stood black against the last grey of the sky, and in one upstairs window a light came on — quietly, without anyone having seemed to pass it — and stayed on, the way a porch light stays on for someone expected home.
