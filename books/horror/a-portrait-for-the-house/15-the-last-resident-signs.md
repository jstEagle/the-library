## 15. The Last Resident Signs

The solicitor arrived on a Tuesday, in a grey car that seemed embarrassed to be at the end of such a long lane.

Silas watched from the study window as Mr. Pell—small, precise, smelling of wet wool and carbon paper—stepped out onto the gravel and looked up at Hollowmere House the way men look at properties they intend to reduce to figures. He had done it himself a hundred times. He knew the posture: chin tucked, eyes moving in efficient diagonals, the whole building translated in real time into columns and clauses. It was the look of a man who believed a house could be finished by paperwork.

Silas no longer believed that. He was not sure he believed anything anymore, except what his own hand had written in a ledger older than his profession.

"Mr. Yarrow." Pell extended a hand in the doorway, briefcase held like a shield. "Pell and Marrow, conveyancing. Miss Ashcombe's buyers are eager to close. Three days, if all goes smoothly."

Three days. The words landed in Silas's chest with the weight of a surveyor's plumb line dropped down a stairwell.

"Nothing about this house goes smoothly," he said, and was surprised at how much like a warning it sounded, and how little he cared to soften it.

* * *

They convened in the morning room because Verity could no longer manage the stairs without an hour's negotiation and a quarter hour of rest for every flight. She sat in her chair by the window wrapped in a shawl that had once been wine-dark and was now the colour of old bruises, and Silas saw immediately—before Pell opened his case, before the first document was laid on the table—that she had spent more of herself since he'd last seen her.

It was in her hands first. They lay in her lap like things set down and forgotten. Then in her eyes, which had always been quick, cataloguing, Ashcombe-quick, and which now moved slowly, as though each thing they landed on had to be identified from a shrinking inventory. She smiled when she saw him, and the smile was entirely herself, warm and wry and undiminished, and that was somehow worse. Everything around the smile had been spent. She had paid and paid and paid, page after page of the founders' ritual read aloud in a voice growing thinner, and what remained of her was gathered into that smile like the last light gathered into a lamp before the oil gives out.

"Mr. Pell," she said. "You've brought the papers."

"I have, Miss Ashcombe. Completion is set for Friday noon, assuming—" He glanced at Silas, at the windows, at the water stain on the ceiling that had not been there yesterday and which, Silas noticed, was shaped less like water damage than like a hand. "Assuming nothing unusual arises."

"Something unusual has been arising in this house since 1847," Verity said pleasantly. "But do go on."

Pell went on. He was good at his work; Silas could hear it in the clean architecture of his sentences, the way each clause locked into the next. Contract of sale. Title. The trust instrument Verity had insisted upon drafting instead—a transfer, not a sale, four names where there might have been one buyer. Pell had raised objections; the objections had been overruled by the simple fact that the seller could do as she liked with what was hers, provided what was hers could be proven to exist, which was proving to be the day's central difficulty.

Because here was the thing Silas had learned in the county offices, in the parish registers, in the deed itself: Hollowmere House was becoming difficult to document. The title search had returned pages that were blank where ownership should have been recorded. The parish register for the relevant years showed baptisms and burials for every family in the district except the Ashcombes, whose entries existed only as faint indentations, as though the ink had been called back. And the deed—the physical deed, which Pell now unrolled on the morning room table with the reverence of a man handling scripture—bore the signature spaces of every previous owner, and every one of them was empty.

"Not unsigned," Pell said, tapping the paper with a pencil, as though the fault were in the pencil. "Unsigned *by anyone living*. There are marks here. Impressions. As if signatures were made and then—" he searched for the professional term and failed to find one "—withdrawn."

"The house keeps its people," Verity said. "Even their names. Especially their names."

Pell looked at her the way one looks at a client who has begun saying alarming things in a pleasant voice. Then he looked at Silas, man to man, professional to professional, seeking the sanity of a colleague.

"You've surveyed the property," Pell said. "Your report will accompany the file. You can confirm the boundaries are as described?"

And there it was. The question Silas had been dreading for days, arriving dressed in ordinary clothes.

He thought of the corridor on the second floor that had returned a different length on each of his first seven walks. He thought of the ledger in the library, open on its stand, in which his own handwriting appeared in entries dated 1849. He thought of the square footage that exceeded the footprint by exactly the dimensions of a sealed room beneath the garden, behind a door that knocked at dusk. He thought of his heartbeat, logged in a fair round hand identical to his own, between an entry about a christening gown and an entry about a death in the east field.

"Yes," Silas said.

Verity's eyes came up to his. She said nothing. She did not need to. He heard her anyway, in the voice she used when reading the ritual aloud, patient and sorrowful: *You may still walk away clean, Mr. Yarrow. That is what neutrality is for.*

"No," Silas said.

Pell's pencil stopped.

"No," Silas said again, and found that his voice had gone quiet and steady, the way it went when he was reading measurements he trusted. "I can't confirm the boundaries, because the boundaries are not settled. I can't certify the square footage, because the house is larger inside than out, and the difference is exactly the size of a room this family sealed shut with someone inside it. I can't sign a report that pretends otherwise. I have tried to be neutral about this house for three weeks, Mr. Pell, and I am telling you, as one professional to another, that neutrality here is not safety. It is vacancy. And vacant things are what get kept."

The morning room was very quiet. Rain had started against the windows, soft and unhurried, the way rain fell at Hollowmere—as though the house had asked for it.

Pell closed his case halfway, then thought better of it, then closed it fully, then opened it again to retrieve a pen he did not need. Men like Pell did not flee; they rescheduled. "I'll need to consult my principals," he said. "Friday stands unless instructed otherwise. Miss Ashcombe—the documents remain with you. Do not sign anything until completion."

"I have signed many things in this house," Verity said gently. "Most of them were not asked for."

* * *

After Pell's car had ground away down the lane, Silas sat with Verity in the failing light. The fire had been lit against the damp, though the chimney drew strangely, sending smoke curling back into the room in shapes that almost resolved into letters before breaking apart. Odile was somewhere in the house—he could hear her boots on the stairs, checking doors, checking Tam, who slept now in the blue room and no longer walked in his sleep but talked in it, softly, in a girl's voice from 1848. Tam's word. Constance's word. The binding word that would crack the seal from the inside, if they lived long enough and were brave enough to use it.

"He offered you the door," Verity said. It was not a question. "Pell. A clean exit. Finish your appraisal, file your report, take your fee, and be a man who once surveyed a strange old house."

"He did."

"And you refused him in front of me, which was either kindness or cruelty, and knowing you, Mr. Yarrow, you are not certain which."

"It was honesty," Silas said. "I'm told it's a kind of cruelty that respects people."

Verity laughed—a small sound, quickly spent, like a match struck in wind—and then she was quiet for a long time, watching the smoke write and unwrite itself against the ceiling.

"I have perhaps forty memories left," she said at last, in the tone other people used for discussing bank balances. "I counted last night. It's become a habit, counting. My mother's face went on Thursday. I kept the sound of her voice a little longer, but it went too, sometime in the small hours, and I woke knowing only that something had been taken and that I had agreed to it. My wedding day went three weeks ago—I remember that there was a wedding, and that I was happy, and that the dress was blue, and everything else is a shape where a day used to be." She turned her hands over in her lap, studying them as if they belonged to the transaction too. "I don't regret it. I want you to understand that. Every memory I spend buys back a page of Elias's instructions, and every page brings us closer to finishing what my family began and abandoned and lied about for a hundred and seventy years. But I tell you the arithmetic, Silas, because you are the one who does arithmetic, and because you deserve to know what you would be signing yourself into."

"And what is that?"

"A house that takes payment in the only coin that matters," Verity said. "And gives back—" she paused, choosing, "—gives back being known. Completely. Which is either the most terrible thing in the world or the only thing in the world worth having. I've never decided. Perhaps that's why I'm the last Ashcombe."

Silas looked at his hands. They were steady. He noticed that they were steady and understood why: because some part of him had already decided, had decided weeks ago, perhaps in the corridor that changed its length, perhaps the moment his handwriting appeared in a book he had never touched. The decision had simply been waiting for his signature the way the deed waited for its owners' names—withdrew, but not absent.

"Tell me what happens if I walk away," he said. "Truly. No comfort."

Verity was silent so long that the fire cracked twice.

"If you walk away," she said, "the sale completes on Friday. The house passes to strangers who will not finish the ritual, because strangers never do; they will live in it, or leave it, and the keeping will continue, and Elias will knock at that door until the end of the world, and everyone this house has ever held will go on being held. Tam will carry Constance's word in his mouth for the rest of his childhood, and perhaps longer. Odile's name stays in the ledger whether she reads it or not—it has been there since the day I hired her crew; the house chose her before any of us chose anything. And you—" she looked at him with terrible gentleness, "—you will drive back to your lodgings with your instruments and your immaculate reports, and you will be the most thoroughly documented invisible man in England, and one night, years from now, you will open a survey file and find your handwriting in the margins, describing rooms you never measured, and you will understand that the house never let you go either. It doesn't need to keep you, Silas. It only needs you to remain unfinished. Unfinished things belong to whoever holds the pen."

The rain thickened against the glass. Somewhere below them, deep beneath the garden, faint as a pulse through a wrist, came the knocking. Dusk-knocking. In the rhythm of the marginalia. Patient as arithmetic.

"And if I sign?" Silas asked. "If I write myself into the ledger—fully, the way the ritual demands, everything given willingly? What guarantee—"

"None," Verity said. "There is no guarantee. That is rather the point. The founders wanted consent, not collateral. Elias built a door that only opens from the inside, and then his brother and sister built a wall around it, and the whole wretched beautiful machine runs on one fuel: someone choosing, without assurance, to belong." Her voice frayed on the last word, and she pressed her lips together until it steadied. "I cannot promise you release. I can promise you that you will be seen—all of you, the orphanhood and the diligence and the loneliness you wore like a grey suit for forty years—and that the house will hold all of it gently, if we finish the sentence. If we don't, it will hold all of it forever. Those are the terms. I'm sorry they're not better. My family wrote them in grief and amended them in fear, and there was never time to make them kind."

Silas stood. His legs felt like another man's legs, borrowed, unsteady—but his hands were still steady, and he had learned in thirty years of surveying that the hands know before the head does.

"Then I'll need the evening," he said. "To prepare the entry. An accounting of Silas Yarrow. If I'm going to sign myself over to a house, I intend to do it with the same rigour I'd bring to any appraisal." He managed something that was nearly a smile. "Complete figures. Nothing omitted. Nothing reconciled by guesswork."

Verity reached up and took his hand—her grip feather-light, her eyes bright with something that was not quite tears and not quite triumph. "Mr. Yarrow," she said, "in a hundred and seventy years, you are the first person to ask the house for time to be thorough."

* * *

He worked past midnight in the library, by lamplight, with the ledger open before him and a fresh pen and the good paper he saved for final reports. Outside, the rain went on. Below, the knocking kept its rhythm—three and a pause, three and a pause, like a heart deciding.

On the blank page that waited for him, in his own unmistakable hand, a single line had already appeared, faintly, like breath on cold glass:

*An accounting of Silas Yarrow, surveyor — commenced this night, at his own request.*

Beneath it, space. Room enough for everything.

Silas dipped his pen, and did not yet write, and did not yet sleep, and listened to the knocking count down the three days like a patient, hopeful clock.
