## 4. Countersign

The hallway had become a gullet.

Xanthe ran with her shoulder against the wallpaper, which pulsed warm and damp under her jacket like the lining of something's cheek. Behind her, the stairs she'd descended were no longer stairs — they had folded into a slope of softwood, slick with sap, and down that slope came the sound of the house swallowing its own architecture, room by room, drawing everything toward the center where the closing table waited.

She could hear it thinking. That was the worst part. Not words, not anymore, but a pressure behind her eyes, patient as interest accruing: *sign, sign, sign.* Her name. It wanted her name the way a lung wants air.

The dining room door stood open at the end of the hall, and through it she could see the table — her grandmother's good table, the one with the burn ring from a kettle in 1987, laid out now with papers, a pen, two chairs. One for the house. One for her.

The extra chair.

It sat at the table's head, pulled out at an angle of invitation, and on its seat lay a folder of documents so fresh the ink still smelled of iron. Above them, on the wall, hung the portrait of the first founder — Aldous Brightwater, 1823, or whatever he'd called himself when the house needed him to be someone. His painted eyes tracked her down the hall. His painted mouth was almost smiling.

Almost. Because she had torn down her own portrait yesterday, and the house had been screaming ever since in a register below hearing.

*The ritual requires a signature,* she thought, sliding into the dining room, slamming the door, dragging the sideboard against it. The wood groaned but held. *Not a specific signer.*

She'd understood it an hour ago, crouched in the cellar among the founders' ledger, reading the renewal clauses by flashlight while the walls breathed around her. Every clause said *the Resident shall sign.* Not Xanthe Kavanagh. Not any name. The house couldn't read; the house could only count. A signature was a signature was a signature. It had never occurred to the house across two hundred years that anyone would sign anything other than their own name — because everyone wanted to live, and living meant giving yourself away in installments.

But her grandmother was dead. And you cannot hold a dead woman to a contract.

You can, however, invoke her hand.

Xanthe picked up the pen. It was warm, like something recently held.

*Don't,* said the pressure behind her eyes, and for the first time it wasn't patient. The door shuddered against the sideboard. Sap wept from the ceiling in long amber ropes. Somewhere above her, the house was peeling back its own floors to look down at her, all its rooms becoming mouths.

"Her name," Xanthe said aloud, and her voice sounded steadier than she felt. "Marguerite Anne Kavanagh. You already accepted her. She signed in '74, in '98, in 2011. She died mid-renewal — your mistake, your gap in the ledger." She uncapped the pen. "I'm just completing her paperwork."

She wrote *Marguerite Anne Kavanagh* in her best imitation of the hand she'd spent three days studying on the old deeds — the slight forward lean, the looped *g*, the way the final *h* trailed off like a woman too tired to finish her own name.

The house went silent.

Then every door in it opened at once.

Not violently. Politely. The way a bank opens its doors when the debt is settled. The pressure behind her eyes dissolved into something almost like confusion, and in that confusion Xanthe crossed the room, took the founder's portrait off the wall — Aldous Brightwater smiling his almost-smile, oil paint over whatever he'd actually been — and carried it to the fireplace, where her grandmother's ashes had been scattered two years ago because no one else in the family would take them.

The lighter was in her jacket pocket. She'd carried it since chapter one of this nightmare, telling herself it was for the pilot light.

The portrait caught slowly, the way old things do. Aldous's face blistered first, the smile going last, and as the canvas blackened the whole house exhaled — a long, descending sigh that Xanthe felt in her teeth, in the floorboards, in the fillings of her molars. Two hundred years of borrowed time burning off in minutes.

She stepped back and watched the house die.

It aged the way a fruit rots in time-lapse. Wallpaper yellowed, browned, curled, sloughed. Plaster powdered. The beams overhead grayed and split, sagging into the geometry of honest decay — no more impossible rooms, no more interior larger than exterior, just a structure finally obeying physics after two centuries of refusing to. Sap hardened into plain resin. The throat-hallway collapsed into an ordinary corridor with ordinary rot, smelling of wet plaster and mouse droppings instead of hunger.

The chair at the head of the table stayed upright until the very end. Then its legs went soft, and it sank into itself like a knee buckling, and it was only wood again.

* * *

Outside, the morning was insultingly normal. Frost on the lawn. A crow somewhere. Xanthe sat on the surveyor's case at the end of the gravel drive and let herself shake for a full minute before she made herself stop.

She had inherited nothing. That was the point. The deed, when she checked it on her phone through the county records, listed the property as lapsed estate, unclaimed, scheduled for demolition. No signature held. No name bound. Marguerite Anne Kavanagh's ghost owed nothing, and neither did Xanthe Kavanagh, who walked away owning exactly what she'd arrived with: her own name, her laser measure, her lighter, and a memory she suspected she'd be paying off in nightmares for years.

The house didn't fall so much as settle, over the following hour, into the ruin it should have been all along. From the road she watched part of the roof go in, softly, like a sigh finishing itself.

Before she left, she took one photograph. Habit. Documentation. Twenty years of surveying had taught her to record the final condition of every property.

She didn't look at it until she was in the car, engine running, heat finally reaching her hands.

In the photograph, the dining room stood open to the sky through the fallen roof, the ruined table visible amid the rubble. And beside the table, untouched by the collapse, unburned, unbuckled, upright on ground where nothing else was upright —

— the chair.

Empty. Facing the camera. Patient.

Not hers anymore. But waiting, the way furniture waits, the way houses wait, for whoever comes next — some future appraiser, some estranged granddaughter, some stranger with a clipboard and a rational explanation for everything.

Xanthe deleted the photo.

The phone saved it anyway.
