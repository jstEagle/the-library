## 2. Household of One

The registrar's office smelled of lamp oil and old paper, the way every official building in Greywater seemed to smell, as though the town had decided sometime in the nineteenth century that this was the correct atmosphere for civic life and simply never revisited the decision.

Maren Kolve sat behind a desk that was too large for her and did not appear to mind. She was perhaps fifty, with gray hair cut short and practical as a fence post, and she wore the expression of a woman who had spent thirty years telling people things they didn't want to hear and had long since stopped apologizing for it.

"Probate will take six weeks at minimum," she said, sliding a folder across the desk. "Longer if there are complications."

"There won't be complications," Theo said. "It's one house and whatever was in his bank account, which I'm guessing wasn't much, because Elias believed money was a moral failing."

Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might have been considering an offer.

"You knew him well?"

"Well enough to know he'd have hated this whole process. The forms. The stamps." Theo looked down at the folder, at the official seal embossed in blue wax. "He used to return junk mail with corrections."

"Then you'll understand when I say this next part." She folded her hands on the desk. "You're staying at the house on Vinter Lane."

It wasn't quite a question, so Theo didn't answer it.

"I'd advise against it," Maren said. "There's a perfectly good inn by the harbor. The Meridian. Clean rooms, decent coffee, and Mrs. Aldous changes the sheets more often than strictly necessary because she worries about damp. Sell the house as-is. Take the offer sight unseen. There are buyers in this town who'd purchase it without walking through the door."

"That seems like a strange way to buy a house."

"It's a strange town." She said it flatly, without self-pity, the way you might report the weather or the tide tables. "Some properties should be sold as-is, Mr. Iversen. Some rooms should be sold as-is. Your brother's house is one of them."

Theo studied her. In the two days since he'd arrived — since the funeral home, since the casket, since the hands that were not his brother's hands — he had learned to watch people in Greywater the way you watch water for signs of something underneath. Most of them gave nothing away. The town had a collective face, smooth and pleasant and slightly wrong, like a portrait painted by someone who'd only ever had the subject described to them.

Maren Kolve was the first person who had looked at him with anything resembling concern.

"Why?" he asked. "What's in the house?"

"Nothing," she said. And then, after a pause so brief it could have been accidental: "That's rather the point of houses. They hold what we put in them, and then they keep holding it after we've stopped wanting it held."

"That's very poetic for a registrar."

"I contain multitudes." She pushed the folder another inch toward him. "Sign where the tabs are. The inn is four hundred kroner a night and includes breakfast. I'd book now; the festival crowds arrive next week."

Theo signed where the tabs were. He had no intention of booking the inn, but he saw no reason to tell her that. He was learning that in Greywater, information was currency, and he was newly poor.

* * *

Elias's house stood at the end of Vinter Lane where the lane gave up pretending to be a street and became a footpath, then a suggestion, then a tangle of beach grass running down toward the grey line of the sea. It was a narrow, two-story thing, salt-bleached blue, with a porch that listed slightly to starboard like a ship that had grown tired of sailing and decided to stay.

Theo had visited twice in the last decade. Both times, Elias had met him at the door with coffee already made, as though he'd been standing there waiting, which knowing Elias he probably had been. His brother had never owned a television, never owned a car, never owned anything that required a subscription. He owned books, a kettle, a telescope with a cracked lens he refused to replace because, he said, the crack made the stars look honest.

Now Theo stood in the doorway with a key that felt heavier than keys should, and no one made him coffee.

Inside, the house smelled of dust and paper and, faintly, of Elias himself — that particular smell of black tea and pencil shavings and the cheap soap he bought in bulk because brand loyalty was, in his view, a form of surrender. Theo stood in the front hall for a long moment, breathing it in, letting it hurt.

"Grief is just love with nowhere to go," their mother used to say, and Theo had always thought it was sentimental nonsense. Standing in his dead brother's hallway, he revised his opinion: it wasn't sentimental. It was accurate, and accuracy could be its own kind of cruelty.

He started with the kitchen, because kitchens are honest rooms. Two mugs in the drying rack. A jar of instant coffee, nearly empty — Elias drank tea, so the coffee was for guests, and Theo felt an unexpected stab of tenderness at the thought of his brother keeping guest coffee the way other people kept guest towels. A calendar on the wall, still turned to the current month, mostly blank except for small penciled notations on scattered dates. Not appointments. Theo leaned close. They were initials. *R.V. — 14th. S.O. — 21st. T.B. — 3rd.*

Interviews, maybe. Meetings. Elias had been a journalist once, briefly, before journalism proved insufficiently obsessed with truth for his tastes, and he'd fallen back into the family trade of being difficult in private.

The living room held the books, hundreds of them, shelved in no order Theo could discern until he pulled one out at random — a history of Norwegian coastal settlements — and found the shelf behind it lined with notebooks.

Dozens of them. Identical black cloth covers, the kind sold in stationery shops to students and poets and people with more discipline than either. Each spine bore a date range in Elias's small, upright hand.

Theo carried an armful to the kitchen table, made himself the guest coffee — it seemed only right — and began to read.

* * *

The early notebooks were what he expected. Elias's mind on paper: digressive, precise, occasionally funny in a dry way that made Theo laugh out loud alone in a dead man's kitchen, which was an experience he did not recommend. Notes on local history. Complaints about the ferry schedule. A three-page argument with a librarian about the misclassification of a maritime atlas.

But somewhere around the fourth notebook — dated eighteen months ago — the tone changed.

The entries got shorter. Denser. Dates. Names. And beside each name, a column of figures, and above the column, a single word:

**owed.**

*Ragnhild Voss, d. 1911. No burial record. Owed: 4 months' wages, withheld.*

*Tomas Brandt (no relation), d. 1888. Listed as 'departed.' No departure record. Owed: unknown.*

*Ingrid Solheim, d. 1904, age 9. Cause of record: fever. No physician's certificate exists. Owed: a name in the ground.*

Theo turned pages. The pattern held. Elias had spent his last year compiling a list of the town's dead — not the dead in the churchyard with their handsome stones, but the other kind. The unrecorded. People who appeared in no ledger, occupied no grave, left no trace except in these notebooks, resurrected in pencil by a man who believed that erasure was the only unforgivable sin.

And beside every entry, that column. **Owed.**

Not owed *by* anyone, Theo noticed. Not owed *to* anyone. Just: owed. As though debt were a weather condition. As though it hung over certain names like fog.

He worked through the afternoon and into the evening, forgetting to eat, forgetting the tea going cold beside him. Outside, the light went from silver to pewter to nothing, and he got up only to switch on lamps, moving through his brother's house like a man wading through deepening water.

Near midnight, he found the entry that stopped him cold.

It was dated eleven days before Elias died. The handwriting was different — faster, pressed harder, the pencil nearly tearing the page.

*M. Kolve showed me the charter today. Or part of it. She shouldn't have. She knows she shouldn't have. She did it anyway, which means she wants it ended as much as I do, she just can't say so out loud. Nobody here can say anything out loud. That's the first rule of Greywater, though nobody wrote it down. Of course nobody wrote it down. Writing things down is how I got here.*

*The bloodline clause is real. Every generation, a named line. The founders call it tithe. The word they use in the actual document is 'maintenance,' which is somehow worse. You maintain a road. You maintain a furnace.*

*I have all the names now. Two hundred years of them. If I do this right, no one else gets collected. If I do this wrong—*

The entry stopped there. The next page was torn out.

Theo sat in the dark kitchen with the notebook open in front of him and realized his hands were shaking.

Collected. His brother had written *collected*, eleven days before he died, and now Elias lay in a casket in the Hartvig Funeral Home wearing his own face over a stranger's hands, and the town registrar had told him to sell the house without looking inside it.

He should leave. Any reasonable person would leave. Go back to the inn, book a room from Mrs. Aldous, eat her unnecessary breakfasts, sign the probate papers, and let Greywater keep its secrets in the manner to which it had become accustomed.

Instead, Theo got up and checked the locks on the doors — all three of them, including the little bolt on the kitchen door that Elias had installed himself, crookedly, because he was a brilliant man and a hopeless carpenter — and then he went upstairs to find the rest of the notebooks.

* * *

The bedroom was exactly as Elias had left it, which was to say aggressively tidy except for the desk, which looked like a paper mill had experienced a religious awakening. And there, on the nightstand, a small digital recorder, its charging light blinking green and patient in the dark.

A sticky note was attached to it. Elias's handwriting, but hurried:

*For Theo, if you're reading this, which means I've run out of time to explain properly. Don't listen to these alone at night. — E.*

Theo picked up the recorder. Turned it over. The memory card slot was empty, but a shoebox on the closet shelf held a row of labeled cards, dozens of them, each marked with a date.

His brother had recorded himself sleeping. Every night, apparently, for the last year. Theo couldn't imagine why — some experiment, some theory, some thread in the great tangled weave of whatever Elias had been pulling at. His brother had always been like this: fixated, forensic, incapable of leaving a loose end alone even when the loose end was clearly attached to something with teeth.

*Don't listen to these alone at night.*

Well. Theo set the recorder down and looked around the bedroom — at the cracked telescope in the corner, at the books stacked on the floor, at the window with its view of the sea going black under a moonless sky — and felt the loneliness settle over him like a coat he hadn't asked to wear.

He was thirty-four years old, and his twin brother was dead, and everyone in this town knew something they weren't saying, and the registrar had practically begged him to leave, and the funeral director had flinched when Theo mentioned the hands.

He wasn't leaving. Whatever Elias had found, whoever had taken him — Theo was going to sit in this house, in his brother's chair, at his brother's table, and read every last page until the column marked *owed* made sense.

Because here was the thing Theo understood now, standing in the household of one: the debt ran both directions. Elias had spent his last year writing down the names of people no one would remember. The least his brother could do — the least Theo could do — was make sure somebody finished the ledger.

He took the shoebox downstairs, set it on the kitchen table beside the notebooks, and poured himself the last of the guest coffee.

Outside, the sea made its slow, patient sound against the shore. Like breathing, he thought. Like something waiting between breaths.

On the table, the recorder's charging light blinked green, steady as a pulse.

Tomorrow, he'd start on the recordings. Tonight, he had two hundred years of names to learn.
