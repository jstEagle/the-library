## 14. Rehearsal

They met in Sef's workshop because it was the only room in Greywater where Theo didn't feel watched.

That wasn't entirely true — the workshop had been burned two nights ago, and the smell of char still lived in the rafters like a tenant who refused to leave. But Sef had swept up, nailed a tarp over the worst of the window, and set three mismatched chairs around a workbench scarred by forty years of honest labor. It was the kind of room that had been used too hard to be afraid of anything.

"Sit," she said. "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stand in the doorway like a man deciding whether to get on a boat." She was already laying out papers: photocopies, since the originals were ash or stolen. "Boat's leaving either way. Sit."

Theo sat. Maren Kolve took the third chair, which creaked under her with the complaint of furniture that knew her weight and resented it. She had brought the charter — the original covenant, wrapped in oilcloth like something infectious. Maybe it was.

"Right," Maren said, all business, because that was how she survived things. "Elias's plan. Or what we can reconstruct of it. I want to say upfront that I've read this document four hundred times in thirty years and never once saw what he saw, so if we're wrong about any of this, the fault is partly mine for being a coward with good reading comprehension."

"That's very generous," Theo said.

"I'm a registrar. Generosity is itemized."

* * *

The charter lay open between them, its ink browned to the color of old tea. Theo had read it twice now, and both times his eyes had snagged on the same passage near the end — the one written in a different hand, cramped and hurried, as though the writer feared being interrupted.

*Should the keepers' consent be withdrawn, and should a bloodline stand forth to name the kept thing and itemize what has been given it, then the bargain is voided and the well must render up its holdings to the surety.*

"It's a trial," Theo said, for maybe the sixth time, because saying it out loud was the only way to make it stay real. "The original ritual. Before they corrupted it. It was never meant to be feeding — it was meant to be *judging*. They just found judging harder than feeding, so they stopped doing it."

"Two hundred years ago," Sef said. She turned a page with a fingertip, careful, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "My grandmother's people came after. The ones they shipped in. By then the trial part was already gone — just stories. The story said there used to be a *question* asked at the equinox, and then one year there wasn't, and after that the fish came back fat and nobody asked why."

"And Elias found this clause how?" Maren asked. "I've had this document under lock my whole career."

"He said you have to read it like a lawyer trying to break a contract, not a historian trying to understand one." Theo smiled despite himself. "He said I'd know what he meant. He used to say I argued with waiters for sport."

"You do argue with waiters for sport," Sef said.

"I once sent back a soup on purely structural grounds."

Maren made a note. Theo genuinely could not tell whether she was joking.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the shape of the thing assembled itself slowly, the way a photograph develops — first shadows, then outlines, then details you wished would stay blurred.

The ritual required four elements. A name. An accounting. A withdrawal of consent from every keeper family. And a bloodline — someone descended from the founding stock, standing in the chamber as both accuser and surety. The accuser named the crime. The surety accepted the debt.

"The problem," Maren said, tapping the page, "is the last part. *Surety.* It means if the verdict fails, the debt doesn't evaporate. It transfers. Everything the well holds passes into the person standing surety. Every name. Every — holding." She chose the word carefully, and Theo heard what she didn't say: *every collected brother.*

"So if I flinch," Theo said, "if I get it wrong—"

"Then you don't die. That's the trap of it. Dying would be clean." Maren's voice was flat, registrar-flat, the voice she used for bad news delivered on schedule. "You'd hold it. All of it. Whatever the thing below has been keeping for two centuries, plus whatever it takes from you at the moment of failure. You'd become the well's next keeper, except you'd be the only one, and nothing would ever feed you."

Sef reached across the bench and put her hand flat on the charter, covering the clause. "There's another way," she said. "Nobody's making you—"

"Yes they are," Theo said. "Three someones. You two, and a dead man who left me his notebooks and his house and a column labeled *owed*." He looked at the hand on the paper. "Move your hand, Sef. I need to see it."

She moved it. He looked at it a long time.

"I keep waiting," he said, "for the part where I change my mind. Like there's supposed to be a moment where a reasonable man stands up and walks out and catches the ferry. And it keeps not coming. I sat in that cellar chair last week — the blank one, with his coat on it — and I felt more at home than I've felt since he died. That's not courage. That's just the shape of the hole matching the shape of the job."

"Grief makes terrible career counseling," Maren said.

"Sure. But it makes excellent motivation."

* * *

They rehearsed.

It had been Sef's idea, and Theo had resisted it — *rehearsing* sounded like community theater, like folding chairs and a woman with a pitch pipe — but she'd been right, the way quiet people are usually right. If he was going to stand in front of a sealed well and conduct a two-hundred-year-old trial, he was not going to do it reading off cue cards for the first time while something ancient breathed through the floorboards.

So they rehearsed in the workshop, with the tarp window going amber behind them as evening came down.

Maren played the entity. This had been decided without discussion; she simply began doing it, in a voice she produced somewhere deep in her registrar's chest, a voice like a filing cabinet full of wet paper.

"*Who names me?*"

"Theo Iversen," Theo said. "Son of Astrid Iversen, daughter of Henrik, son of the line that signed first. Bloodline of the keepers. I name you the Kept Thing Below the Meetinghouse. I name you what they made you."

"Slower," Sef said from her corner, where she'd appointed herself director. "Not slower like you're scared. Slower like you're *certain*. A judge isn't slow because he's afraid. He's slow because the words have to land."

Theo tried again. "*I name you—*"

"You're rushing the second sentence."

"How can you tell I'm rushing a sentence I haven't said yet?"

"Because your shoulders did it first." Sef came over and put two fingers on his shoulder blade, pressing down. "Here. This is where you carry it. Every time you speed up, it climbs. Let it sit heavy. Heavy is fine. Heavy is honest."

He let it sit heavy. He said the naming again, and this time the words came out of him like stones dropped into deep water, and the workshop went very quiet, and even Maren-as-the-entity forgot her line.

"That," Sef said softly. "That one."

Then the accounting. This was the longest part, and the worst. Two centuries of the fed, itemized. Maren had spent three days cross-referencing the founders' own records against Elias's notebooks against the counter-ledger fragments Sef's people had hidden in floorboards and hymnals and one memorable instance inside a taxidermied gull. The list ran to ninety-one names. Some had dates. Most had only descriptions — *the girl from the relief ship, 1841; the man who owed Halvard's grandfather money, 1888; the three who went down to the cellar for a festival errand, 1952.*

Ninety-one. Theo read them aloud, all of them, twice, until he could do it without his voice breaking on the children, and then once more when it broke anyway, because Sef said the breaking was allowed, the breaking was even good, as long as he kept reading.

"The accuser who flinched in 1893," Maren said quietly, when he'd finished the fourth pass, "was a man named Anders Voss. No relation to you — the name died out. But Ines Brandt's family kept his transcript, and according to the story, he got through sixty names before he stopped. Just stopped. Mid-sentence. And the well—" She stopped herself.

"Took him," Theo said.

"Collected him. As interest." She closed her folder. "Which brings us to the consent withdrawals. Five families. Each one must be spoken aloud, by name, by the bloodline, renouncing their claim. Halvard Brandt will be standing in that room, Theo. So will the others. They will not say yes. They will say everything else."

"Then I'll speak it for them. The clause says withdrawn, not surrendered. Withdrawal doesn't need their cooperation. It needs their names, and I have their names." He tapped the charter. "It needs one more thing, though. Say it plainly, Maren. You've been circling it all day like a gull over a fish head."

Maren folded her hands. For a moment she looked every year of her age and several she hadn't earned yet.

"It needs the surety to mean it," she said. "Not perform meaning it. Not recite meaning it. The 1893 transcript says the rite 'tastes the heart.' Elias wrote the same thing in the margin of his notebook — you can't fake the wanting. You have to actually be willing to hold every debt if the verdict fails. Willing, in the marrow. Because the moment you stand up there and lie about it, the well will know, and the well does not respect perjury."

The workshop was silent except for the tick of the stove cooling.

"I'm willing," Theo said.

"You can't know that yet."

"No," he agreed. "But I know this much. Everyone in this town wants something *from* that thing below. Prosperity, safety, their grandfather's fishing luck. I'm the only person in two hundred years who walks in there wanting nothing except for it to be over and my brother to be free. If willingness is the price of admission, I'm the richest man in Greywater."

Sef laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered, but real. "You argue with waiters for sport," she said, "and you talk like that. Elias never stood a chance of being the interesting twin."

* * *

They ran it twice more, end to end. Naming, accounting, withdrawal, verdict. By the second run Theo's voice had stopped shaking on the children's names, mostly, and Maren's entity-voice had developed an unsettling habit of answering slightly before he finished asking, which everyone agreed to never mention again.

Near midnight they stopped, and Sef made coffee on the woodstove, and the three of them sat drinking it out of chipped mugs while the fire settled.

"Costs," Maren said. She said it the way she opened every ledger. "Let's count them honestly, because no one else in this town ever has. One: if the verdict fails, you hold everything. Two: if it succeeds — and here's the part the charter buries in the passive voice — the surety renders up a holding of equal weight to what he asks released. Symmetry. The well doesn't give; it trades."

"Elias figured that out," Theo said. "It's why he wrote to me. His last letter — the one that arrived after the funeral — he said, *whatever they offer you, the price is always the same shape: it takes the thing you'd least trade.*" He looked into his mug. "For me that's not complicated. There's exactly one thing I'd least trade."

"Your memories of him," Sef said. Not a question.

"Every one. The well doesn't do partial payments." He said it steadily, because he'd been saying it to himself for days, wearing a groove in it, making it walkable. "If this works, I walk out of that cellar having judged a monster and freed my brother, and I won't remember I had a brother. I'll remember a house I inherited and a funeral I attended and a stranger's face in a coffin. That's the counted cost. I've counted it. I'm paying it."

Maren studied him for a long moment. "You could still take Halvard's offer. Money, comfort, Elias supposedly returned. A lie with excellent dental coverage."

"A lie that talks in his voice but doesn't know what he knew." Theo shook his head. "The thing in the recordings quoted his childhood back at me last week. Got every detail right. And then it called our mother *kind*, and Elias would have chewed glass before saying that sentence sober. Comfort can lie. I learned that from a tape recorder." He set down the mug. "No. We finish the rehearsal. We do it right. And whatever it costs, it costs *once*, cleanly, instead of forever, dirty."

Sef reached over and squeezed his wrist, brief and hard, the way dockworkers say goodbye.

"Then tomorrow we do the last piece," she said. "The one thing we haven't got."

"The consent of the fifth family," Maren said. "Brandt. Without a Brandt's claim visibly broken — without one of them *renouncing* — the withdrawal reads as theft, not verdict. The charter's clear. We need a founder to turn."

Theo laughed, tired and hollow. "Wonderful. Where do we find a Brandt with a conscience?"

The knock came at the tarp-covered window then — three soft raps, hesitant, the knock of someone who had never knocked on this side of town before and hoped very much not to be recognized for it.

Through the gap under the tarp, lamplight caught the hem of a coat that cost more than Sef's entire workshop.

A woman's voice, low and urgent, pitched to carry only through the gap:

"My name is Ines Brandt. I think I have something your brother was looking for."
