## 15. The Founder's Doubt

Ines Brandt had spent thirty-one years learning to set a table as though the world were stable.

It was, she had come to understand, the family trade. Not shipping. Not the cannery shares, not the timber rights, not the tidy annuities that arrived each quarter like weather. The trade was *composure* — the ability to pour coffee while a man across from you described something impossible in a level voice, and to say, "More sugar?" as though he'd told you about his garden.

Her father was the finest practitioner alive. Halvard Brandt could discuss feeding schedules for a thing beneath a meetinghouse with the same warmth he brought to pruning roses, and he could do it while buttering toast.

Ines could not.

She stood now at her kitchen window — the good kitchen, the one facing the harbor, because her father believed you should always be able to see what your money floated on — and watched Theo Iversen walk the seawall below. He walked it every morning since the equinox notice went up on the meetinghouse door. Same coat. Same pace. A man rehearsing something, though she doubted he knew that's what he was doing. She recognized rehearsal when she saw it. Her whole childhood had been one long dress rehearsal for a performance she'd never agreed to give.

"You're up early," her father said behind her, and she did not jump, because jumping was also not permitted.

"I couldn't sleep."

"Equinox nerves." He said it kindly. He said everything kindly; that was the trick of him. "They pass. They passed for your grandmother, and hers before her. It's the house settling."

"The house," Ines said, "is two hundred years old and sits on top of a well full of names."

Halvard smiled at the window, at the harbor, at the morning generally. "And yet the herring came back this spring. Best run in a decade. You want to know why? Because we keep our accounts current." He patted her shoulder on his way to the sideboard, and his hand was light, almost affectionate, and it felt exactly like being stamped. "The Iversen boy will sit the chair. He wants his brother back more than he wants his principles. They always do, at the end. Grief is very persuasive arithmetic."

He left for the cannery office at seven-forty, as he did every day of his ordered life, and Ines stood in the kitchen until eight o'clock, which was the hour she had decided on three nights ago, and then she took down the tin of loose tea from the third shelf — the tin nobody drank from, because it held no tea — and put on her coat.

* * *

The 1893 transcript lived in a lockbox under the floor of her father's study, beneath a loose board her great-grandfather had hidden it under and her grandfather had nearly found twice and her father had never once looked for, because Halvard Brandt did not go looking for things that might indict him. That was the difference between them, Ines thought, prying the board up with a letter opener: her father managed his shame by refusing to acknowledge its existence, and she had spent her life drowning in hers precisely because she acknowledged everything.

She had known about the box since she was nine.

That was the year she'd been sent to fetch her father's reading glasses and heard him downstairs talking to Maren Kolve's mother about "the renewal language," and, being a curious child with an unsupervised half hour, she'd gone exploring. Children always find the loose boards. It's practically a law of nature. She had lifted the transcript out, read the first page standing in the cold study with her heart going like a trapped bird, and put it back, and said nothing, for twenty-two years.

Twenty-two years of saying nothing. She could have filled a well of her own with all the things she hadn't said.

Now she wrapped the box in a wool scarf, tucked it into her market basket under the parsnips, and walked out past the housekeeper with a cheerful "I'm after vegetables," which was even true, in the way that a man who robs a bank is technically after money.

* * *

Elias Iversen's house sat where the town thinned into scrub pine, and it looked wrong without him in it — or rather, it looked right, which was worse. Elias had kept his house the way unrecorded people kept their lives: spare, watchful, nothing displayed that couldn't be denied. Ines had been here once, a year ago, to bring him a jar of her plum preserves, which had been less a gift than a reconnaissance mission, and he had known it instantly and let her in anyway, because that was the kind of man he'd been. He'd eaten a spoonful of preserves standing at his own counter, considered them seriously, and said, "Your plums are honest. Your family isn't. But the plums are doing their best."

She had laughed until she cried, and then just cried a little, and he'd pretended not to notice, and that was the last real conversation she'd had with anyone in Greywater.

Theo answered the door looking like a man who had been sleeping in shifts against his own thoughts. Up close, the twin resemblance was a strange ache — Elias's face wearing a different grief, the way a house wears new paint over old water damage.

"Ines Brandt," she said. "You don't know me."

"I know the name."

"Everyone knows the name. That's rather the problem." She held up the basket. "I've brought parsnips and a confession. The parsnips are a pretext. May I come in?"

He studied her for a long moment — and she let him, because she owed him the inspection. Then he stepped aside.

The kitchen smelled of burnt coffee and paper. Notebooks were stacked on the table in careful towers, and beside them, a small recorder with a worn label in Elias's handwriting: *NIGHTS — DO NOT ERASE.* Ines looked at it too long, and Theo noticed her noticing.

"You've heard them," she said. "The recordings."

"How do you know about the recordings?"

"Because my family has been listening for them for two hundred years." She set the basket down and pulled out the scarf-wrapped box instead of any vegetable. "Not yours specifically. Any voice that comes up out of the quiet. We call it the Listening Season, the weeks before a renewal. My grandmother used to say you could hear the well clearing its throat." She unwrapped the box. "I'm here because I'm done being one of the people who keeps things under floorboards."

* * *

She made him read it in silence, which felt right. Some documents deserve the dignity of not being narrated over.

The 1893 transcript was typescript over carbon copies, brittle as autumn leaves, and it began the way a court record begins, because that is what it was — the original form of the ritual, the trial, before her ancestors sanded it down into a feeding schedule. *In re: The Occupant of the Well Beneath the Greywater Meetinghouse. Accuser: Anders Brandt, of the bloodline, standing as surety.*

Theo read for a long time. His finger stopped moving down the page around the fourth session, and Ines watched his face do what faces do when they learn the shape of a trap.

"They tried it," he said finally. "A verdict. Before."

"1893. My great-great-grandfather Anders. He was a deacon and a true believer — in the town, in the covenant, in the idea that if the thing below could be *named properly*, legally, it could be judged and starved and Greywater could be free." Ines sat down across from him, because her legs had opinions. "He got as far as the itemization. Two hundred pages of debts. Names of the fed. And then—"

She turned to the final page herself, because she had read it so many times she could have recited it, but some things you should look at directly. The last entry was not typescript. It was Anders Brandt's hand, shaking, the ink dragged where the pen had pressed too hard:

*It showed me my daughter. It has her voice exactly. It asked me whether I was certain, and I hesitated, and the hesitation was consent. God forgive me. The chair is warm. I cannot get up. I do not wish to get up. That is the worst of it — I do not wish to—*

The sentence never ended. There was a stain on the corner of the page that Ines had decided, decades ago, not to speculate about.

"The accuser flinched," she said. "One hesitation. That's all it needed. The rite requires certainty — total, sustained, all the way through the verdict. Anders loved his daughter more than he hated the thing, and the thing knew it, and it used her voice, and he wavered, and it took him. Consumed him as interest on his own doubt." She laughed, briefly, horribly. "My family's fortune dates from that winter. We were paid for him. We've been paid ever since. Every generation, someone in my line signs the renewal, and every generation we tell ourselves it's mercy — that feeding it keeps it calm, that starving it would make it hungry enough to take the whole town. My father believes it completely. That's the most frightening thing about him. He's not lying. He's *sincere*."

Theo hadn't moved. "Why give me this? Your father will know it's gone."

"He'll know eventually. Probably by tonight. He notices everything except himself." She pushed the box an inch toward Theo, as if it might otherwise change its mind. "I'm giving it to you because Elias interviewed me eleven months ago, and I lied to him. He sat where you're sitting and asked me, gently, whether the founders kept records of the unrecorded dead, and I said I didn't know. I knew. I'd known since I was nine years old. He thanked me anyway. He said, 'When you're ready, the door will still be there.' And then he died trying to do alone what should have taken all of us, and I attended his funeral and watched five families perform grief like a civic duty, and I thought: *the door is still there, Ines. It has been there for twenty-two years.*"

Her voice cracked on the last of it, and she let it. Composure was the family trade. She was resigning.

"Elias was kind to you," Theo said quietly.

"He was kind to everyone the town counted as nothing. That was his whole method, I think. He understood that the ledger of the unrecorded was the only evidence that mattered, and the only way to recover it was to treat the unrecorded as if they'd never been erased." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike. "There's a woman out past the cannery flats — Sef. He spent his last months with her people. If you haven't found her yet, find her. And there's a clause in the charter — Maren Kolve will know it, she's the registrar, and God help her, she hates what she keeps. The verdict clause. It's never been invoked successfully, but it exists. Anders proved it can fail. Which means it can also hold."

"Anders hesitated," Theo said slowly, "because it offered him something he wanted."

"It will offer you something too." Ines met his eyes and made herself say the rest plainly, because plainness was the only inheritance she had worth passing on. "It knows what you want. It's been listening to you sleep, Theo. Whatever it offers you — your brother, his voice, his memories, his laugh — understand that it can produce all of it perfectly. That's not hope. That's bait. The only question the ritual actually asks is whether you can hear your heart's desire speaking in the dark and still read the charges aloud."

The recorder on the table sat between them, patient as its subject.

"And if I flinch?" Theo asked.

"Then you become another page in my family's collection, and I become the woman who handed you the knife." She stood, and her knees complained, and she ignored them. "So don't flinch. I've carried Anders for a hundred and thirty years without ever meeting him. Don't make me carry you too."

* * *

At the door, Theo stopped her with a question she'd been dreading.

"Why now? Twenty-two years, and you come the week before the equinox?"

Ines considered lying, found she'd lost the knack somewhere between the parsnips and the transcript, and told the truth.

"Because my father offered you the fortune Tuesday night, and you didn't take it, and he came home *pleased*. He said, 'He'll crack by Friday, they always do,' and he poured himself a whiskey, and I watched him celebrate the fact that a grieving man was going to be devoured, and I realized I was looking at my future. Forty years from now that's me at the sideboard, pouring, sincere, certain." She pulled her coat tight against the sea wind. "Also — and this is the smaller reason, but it's mine — I liked your brother. He ate my preserves and insulted my family and it was the first honest conversation I'd had in a decade. Somebody owes him. It may as well be the person whose name is on the debt."

She was halfway down the steps when he called after her.

"Ines. The thing in the recordings — it's been using Elias's voice. Answering questions I didn't ask. Do you know what that means? What it's doing?"

She turned. Below her, the harbor lay bright and innocent, herring boats going out on a prosperity that had never once been free.

"It means it's rehearsing," she said. "For you. The way my family rehearses composure. It's practicing being your brother so that when the moment comes, the hesitation will feel like love." She gripped the rail. "Whatever it says to you next — and it will say something soon, it's running out of time same as we are — remember that the real Elias never once asked you to doubt yourself. That's how you'll know the difference."

She left him standing in the doorway with the transcript in his hands, and she walked home the long way, past the meetinghouse, and she did not lower her eyes as she passed it, and it was the smallest rebellion in the history of Greywater, and it was hers.

Behind her, in a house above a sealed well, a recorder waited, and something in the static waited with it, and the equinox was four days out.

Three, if you counted honestly.

Her father had taught her to count honestly. It was the one lesson she intended to keep.
