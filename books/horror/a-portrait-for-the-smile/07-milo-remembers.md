## 7. Milo Remembers

The thing about being the only one who remembered was that it made you quiet.

Milo had learned this the way he learned most things—by watching what happened when he didn't keep his mouth shut. In kindergarten, he'd told Mrs. Alvarez that his sister used to sit next to him at breakfast and steal the marshmallows out of his cereal, and Mrs. Alvarez had smiled the way adults smile when they think a child is being precious, and she'd said, *You mean your imaginary friend, sweetheart?* And his mother, who was picking him up that day because his father worked late, had gone very still in the doorway of the classroom, and her stillness had frightened him more than any scolding ever could have.

After that, he kept it folded up inside himself, small and secret, like a moth in a matchbox. He could feel it fluttering sometimes—at night, mostly, or in the car when they passed the turnoff for Larkspur Road—but he didn't let it out. He'd learned that letting it out made his mother's face do something terrible. Not angry. Worse than angry. *Empty*, like a room where the furniture had been carried out but the rectangles on the wallpaper were still there, pale ghosts of where things used to stand.

Her name was Edda.

No—that wasn't right either. Edda was the name from school, the name Miss Vasquez's roster didn't have, the name that had made his stomach drop through the floor when he heard it spoken aloud in a bright classroom on a Tuesday morning. His sister's name—the real one, the one that lived in the matchbox with the moth—was different. It was soft at the front and dark at the back, like a stone you found in a creek bed. He could feel the shape of it in his mouth even now. He just couldn't make the sound anymore. Somewhere in the last four years, the sound had gone out of it, the way heat goes out of a stone after sundown.

He still remembered everything else. That was the cruel arithmetic of it: the name went first, but everything else stayed. The gap in her teeth. The way she could whistle through her fingers before he could. How she'd dared him to touch the electric fence at Farmer Hollis's place and then cried harder than he did when he actually did it. The smell of her hair—rain and shampoo, the cheap kind with the cartoon mermaid on the bottle. Her socks, always mismatched, always on purpose, because she said matching was for people who were afraid of their own feet.

Nobody else in the world knew those things. When you were the only one carrying them, they got heavy in a way that had nothing to do with weight.

* * *

Miss Vasquez stayed after class on Thursday, erasing the board with slow strokes, and Milo pretended to be organizing his desk so he wouldn't have to walk past her and pretend not to look at her face. But teachers always caught you pretending. That was their whole job, practically.

"Milo." She crouched down by his desk instead of standing over him, which he appreciated. Adults who stood over you wanted obedience. Adults who crouched wanted conversation. "Can I ask you something? And you don't have to answer if you don't want to."

He shrugged, which meant yes.

"Tuesday, when I was taking roll. You said a name I don't have on my list."

His heart did the thing it did on the fire escape at recess, when he leaned too far over the rail. "I say a lot of stuff."

"You said *Edda.*"

The moth beat its wings against the matchbox. Milo looked at his hands. There was pencil graphite in the crease of his left thumb, a silver seam, and he traced it twice before he trusted his voice.

"You wrote it down," he said finally. "On your list. Under the desks part."

"I wrote down that I count twenty-nine desks and thirty names," Miss Vasquez said. "That's all. Just numbers. Nobody else thought it was strange, Milo. Not the principal, not the other kids. Just me. And then you said that name like you were waiting for someone to say it back."

He should have lied. He was good at lying about this; he'd had practice. *I was joking. I made it up. Kids do that.* He opened his mouth to deploy the lie, and then he looked up at her face—really looked—and saw something he'd never seen in an adult's face before. She wasn't smiling the precious-smile. She wasn't wearing the empty-room look his mother wore. She looked, he realized, exactly how he felt. Like someone holding a moth in a matchbox and listening to it flutter.

"My sister sat for a portrait once," Milo said.

* * *

He told her in pieces, the way you hand someone broken glass—carefully, flat side first, so nobody bleeds.

It was three years ago, maybe four; time worked strangely around the memory, stretching and compressing like taffy. There had been a flyer stapled to the telephone pole outside the grocery store, and his mother had stopped to read it, and then she'd gotten that look she got when she thought something was a good idea, which was a dangerous look, in retrospect. A free family portrait. Some photographer doing a promotion, working out of an old house on the edge of town. His mother loved photographs. Their hallway at home was a gallery of them—Milo gap-toothed on the first day of school, Milo asleep on the dog, Milo, Milo, Milo.

They'd walked to the house together, all three of them. He remembered the fog sitting low that day, thick as wool, and how the house seemed to rise out of it like the top half of something whose bottom half had drowned. He remembered the photographer—an old woman, small and neat, with hands that smelled like lavender and something underneath the lavender, something mineral and cold, like well water. He remembered that she'd called his sister by name before anyone introduced her, and his mother had laughed and said word must get around, and the old woman had smiled and said, oh, names get around to me especially.

And he remembered the chair.

There was a wooden chair in the corner of the studio, old and dark, the kind with spindles and a woven seat, and the old woman had asked his sister—asked *Edda*, though that wasn't her name, though the name he couldn't say was hers—to sit in it. Just her. Just for one alone, she'd said, every child gets one solo portrait, it's tradition. His mother had hesitated, and Edda hadn't. Edda had never hesitated about anything in her life. She'd climbed into the chair and swung her mismatched socks and grinned her gap-toothed grin and said *take a good one, I'm famous.*

The flash went off. And Milo, standing by the door with his mother, had felt something pass through the room—not wind, nothing touched his skin, but something passed through anyway, the way a sound passes through a wall. Like the whole house had swallowed.

"That's when she started fading?" Miss Vasquez asked quietly. They were still in the classroom. Outside the windows, the fog was doing its afternoon work, erasing the playground fence, the basketball hoop, the world beyond.

"Not right away," Milo said. "It was like... you know when you leave a popsicle on the counter? It doesn't disappear. It just gets less. First it was little things. Her teacher marked her absent when she was sitting right there. Then my mom set two plates at dinner and then one plate, and when I asked, she said, *one plate for what, silly?* And then—" He stopped. The matchbox was shaking. "Then one morning her bed was just a bed. And Mom was making pancakes and humming, and I came downstairs and said, where's—" 

He couldn't finish. He'd never been able to finish. The sentence broke in the same place every time, right where the sound of the name should go, and the silence where the sound should be was the loudest thing in his life.

Miss Vasquez didn't tell him it was okay. She didn't tell him he was imagining things, or that grief did strange things, or any of the sentences adults kept loaded like tranquilizer darts. She pulled the chair from the neighboring desk around to face him and sat in it, knees almost touching his, and she said:

"Show me."

* * *

She drove him home in her car, which smelled like coffee and old paper, and his mother wasn't due back from the diner until six, so they had the house to themselves. Milo took her down the hallway first, to the gallery of photographs, and showed her the gaps without showing her the gaps—because there weren't gaps, that was the whole horrible trick of it. The wall was full. The wall had always been full. Milo asleep on the dog. Milo missing a tooth. Milo in a Halloween costume shaped like a shark. Frame after frame after frame, and not one of them held anything but him.

"She should be here," he said, pointing at the shark costume picture. "This was her costume first. I wore it the year after because she grew out of it. She's supposed to be in the picture from the year before, standing behind me, making the fin bite my head. Mom has it in a box somewhere, except she doesn't, because the box only has me in it now."

Miss Vasquez stood very close to the wall, closer than people usually looked at pictures, and her breath fogged the glass over the shark photo faintly. "Milo," she said, and her voice had gone strange and careful, "when you look at these frames. Do you ever see... marks? Underneath?"

He frowned. "Marks?"

"Like a shadow of something that isn't there. An outline. A rectangle slightly darker than the wall."

He looked. He'd looked a thousand times, but he looked again, really looked, the way she was looking—and there. Behind the shark photo, where the frame met the wall, the paint was a shade deeper, a faint rectangle of less-faded color, like the wall remembered something hanging there that no longer hung. And below the sleeping-on-the-dog picture, another one. And beside the door, a patch of wallpaper where the sun hadn't reached evenly, a pale ghost-shape the size of a frame that wasn't there.

His throat closed. "Mom says we used to have more pictures up. She says she took some down when she repainted." His voice cracked on *repainted*. "But she repainted before I was born. I checked. I asked Grandma. Grandma said the wall's been that color since 1998."

Miss Vasquez was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "My grandmother had a house full of photographs like this. With a chair in them." And the way she said *my grandmother* told Milo, without her explaining anything, that she was carrying a matchbox too. Maybe more than one. Maybe hers had a whole drawer of moths in it.

They went to his mother's room. The albums lived on the top shelf of her closet, in a row, spines labeled in her round handwriting: *MILO — BABY. MILO — 1ST GRADE. MILO — SUMMER.* Every album. Every spine. His name, his name, his name, like a chant, like a wall built brick by brick against something.

He opened the oldest one on his mother's bed while Miss Vasquez sat beside him, and they turned the pages together. Here he was, brand new, red-faced and furious in the hospital blanket. Here he was at the beach, eating sand. Here he was—Milo turned a page and stopped, because there, in the corner of a birthday picture, half cut off by whoever held the camera, was an arm. A small arm, a kid's arm, reaching into the frame toward the cake, wearing a watch made of braided embroidery thread.

He knew that watch. He'd helped braid it. Purple and green, because purple and green were the colors of—

Of the stone in the creek bed. Of the name with the soft front and the dark back.

"There," he whispered, touching the arm. "That's her. That's her arm, that's her watch, I made that watch." He flipped backward, forward, hunting. On the page before, a blur at the edge of a picnic scene, a smear of motion that might have been a kid running through the shot. Three pages later, a birthday hat floating at the frame's edge with nothing under it—or nothing the camera would admit to. Fragments. Edges. The album was like a photograph of a person seen through frosted glass: you could prove something had been there, but you couldn't make out the face.

Miss Vasquez took out her phone and photographed each one. The arm. The blur. The hat. The pale rectangles on his hallway wall. "Why?" Milo asked. "What good does a picture of a picture do?"

"Because," she said, "everything in this town wants you to think you're the only one who counts. And I am telling you right now, Milo Reyes"—she said his whole name, both barrels, the way you say a name when you want it to stick—"you are not the only one. I count too. And whatever took your sister took students from my classroom, and it took people from my family, and I think it's been taking from this town since before either of us was born." She paused, and her thumb moved over the photo of the small arm in its braided watch. "We're going to get her back. Or we're going to make sure everybody remembers she was here. One of those. Probably both."

Nobody had ever said *we* to him about Edda before. Not once in three years. His mother said *you need to let this go, baby.* His father said *buddy, some kids make up siblings.* His grandmother had died before it happened, or maybe after—he honestly couldn't remember which, and that bothered him in a low, constant way, like a draft under a door.

But this woman—who'd known him eleven days, who'd counted desks on her first morning and written down the wrong number on purpose—this woman said *we* like she meant it, and Milo felt something in his chest unlock that had been locked so long he'd forgotten it was a lock.

"Her name starts soft," he said suddenly. The words came out before he decided to say them. "Her name. It starts soft and ends dark. Like a rock from the creek. I can't—I can't say it anymore. But I can feel the shape of it. Is that crazy? Does that help at all?"

Miss Vasquez's eyes had gone bright. "It helps," she said. "Soft start, dark end. I'll hold onto that. You hold onto the socks and the whistling and the watch. We split it up. That way neither of us is carrying all of it alone."

He nodded, and then, because he was nine-going-on-ten and had learned the hard way what hope cost, he said the thing he'd never said out loud: "If it comes for me next—if I start getting less—you'll say my name? Even if I stop knowing yours? Even if I forget to listen?"

She took his face in her hands, gently, the way you hold something you've decided not to lose.

"Milo," she said. "Milo. Milo. I will say it so many times this town will be sick of it."

Outside, the fog pressed against the window, patient as ever. And in his backpack, forgotten since Tuesday, Milo's class photo lay curled at the edges—and if either of them had looked at it that evening, they might have noticed that in the back row, at the far right, the empty space beside his shoulder had begun, very faintly, to take the shape of a girl with mismatched socks.

But neither of them looked. Not yet.

There was still time. There was almost still time.
