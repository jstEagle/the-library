## 9. Rules of the House

Wendell Okafor had been counting doors at Hollow Creek Elementary for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had never once told a teacher why.

He'd come close, a few times. There was a music teacher in the nineties, a soft-spoken man named Aldous who noticed that the supply closet on the second floor had three hinges when it should have had two, and who stood in the hallway after dark with his keys jingling in his hand like he wanted to ask something. Wendell had poured him a cup of coffee from the janitor's pot—burnt, always burnt, but hot—and the two of them had sat on the little folding chairs outside the gym while the radiators ticked. And then Aldous had said, *You ever get the feeling this building is holding its breath?* and Wendell had said, *Every night,* and Aldous had laughed, and that was the end of it. Aldous transferred to Pinewood in the spring. He sent a postcard. The postcard was the last anyone heard from him, and even the postcard had begun to feel strange after a while, like a receipt for something purchased long ago by someone else.

So Wendell had learned to keep his mouth shut and his ledger open.

The ledger lived in the bottom drawer of his custodial desk, beneath a false bottom made from an old clipboard, beneath that a layer of floor wax cans nobody would ever move. It was a composition book, the marbled black-and-white kind, its spine cracked white with use. Thirty-one years of nights. Every door in the building, listed by wing and number, and beside each one a tally mark for every night Wendell had walked the halls and laid his palm flat against the wood and felt, with the flat of his hand, whether the door was *settled*. Not locked. Locked was a matter of bolts and latches, and locks were the building's business, not his. Settled was different. Settled meant the door agreed with itself about where it was.

Most nights they all agreed. Some nights—and these were the nights that kept him keeping count—one of them would be restless under his palm, humming faintly like a struck tuning fork, and if he didn't stand there and speak to it, quietly, the way you'd speak to a spooked horse, it would be somewhere else by morning. A classroom door appearing where a storage door should be. A hallway that ran two doors longer than the blueprint allowed. He wrote those down too, in pencil, because pencil could be erased and some things you didn't want to commit to ink.

Miss Vasquez had been at Hollow Creek four weeks when she started noticing things, and Wendell knew it the way you know weather coming—not from any one sign but from the pressure behind your ears. She counted her students. He'd seen her doing it through the little wire-glass window in her classroom door, her lips moving, her finger drifting across the room like a conductor's baton. Nobody else counted. That was the whole trouble with Hollow Creek, as far as Wendell could tell: nobody else counted, and so nobody else noticed what the counting was for.

On a Thursday evening in late October, with fog lying against the windows like something listening, she found him in the boiler room.

* * *

He heard her on the stairs before he saw her—the particular careful step of someone descending into a place they don't trust. His flashlight lay on the workbench beside the ledger, and he turned it face-down without hurrying, an old reflex, though some part of him understood that hiding it now was like hiding a broom from another person who also swept.

"Mr. Okafor?" Her voice came down the concrete throat of the stairwell. "I saw your light."

"Boiler's temperamental," he called back. "I sit with it sometimes. Keeps it honest."

She came down into the yellow light. She looked tired in a way that wasn't about sleep—tired the way a rope is tired, fraying somewhere in the middle where nobody checks. She was carrying a manila folder, and he recognized it before she opened it, because he had carried that same folder himself, twenty-nine years ago, down these same stairs, to show a woman who was not a teacher and not quite anything else. Edith Vasquez. Orsen's grandmother. The folder was full of class photographs, decades of them, and every photograph had one more blurred face than the year before, and the faces weren't blurred like bad film. They were blurred like breath on glass.

"I need to know," Miss Vasquez said, "if I'm losing my mind. And if I'm not—" She stopped. Looked at him straight, which most people couldn't do down here, in the boiler light, where the shadows moved wrong. "You've been here thirty years. You must have seen it."

Wendell wiped his hands on his rag, slowly, buying time the way old men do. Then he pulled open the drawer, lifted the wax cans, slid out the false bottom, and set the composition book on the workbench between them.

"This is a ledger," he said. "Of doors."

She looked at it. She was smart; he could see her deciding not to laugh, deciding instead to read his face, and whatever she found there made her go still.

"Thirty-one years," he said. "Every night, I walk the building and I put my hand on every door, and I make sure it knows where it is. Because this building likes to be whole, Miss Vasquez. It likes it so much it'll make up parts it doesn't have. Doors that go nowhere. Hallways that grow overnight like fingernails. And if you don't count them—if you don't stand there and say, no, you're the door to Room 4, and there are eleven doors on this hall and not twelve—it starts believing its own arithmetic." He tapped the ledger. "Counting isn't a habit. It's maintenance."

"And the children?" she asked quietly. "Is that maintenance too?"

He looked at the folder in her hands. He thought about Milo Fenn, whom he'd watched fading all month the way frost fades off a windshield—there and then less there and then only there if you already knew to look. He thought about how the boy had started eating lunch standing next to Wendell's cart, unasked, just to be near somebody whose eyes tracked him.

"Sit down," Wendell said. "This takes telling right, or it doesn't take at all."

* * *

There were two folding chairs against the wall, and they sat, and the boiler ticked and breathed, and Wendell folded his hands over his knees the way he did when he was about to say something he'd rehearsed alone for three decades.

"Edith Vasquez told me the rules in 1987," he said. "Your grandmother. I'd been here two years. My wife, Alma, worked the front office—attendance clerk. And one October we lost a boy. Second grade. Daniel Pruitt. One day he was in every photograph and every memory, and the next day his mother was setting one plate at dinner and asking me, polite as anything, why I kept saying a name she didn't know." Wendell's voice stayed level, but his hands had gone tight around each other. "I went to Edith because Alma's own mother had gone the same way, years before, and Edith was the only person in this town who didn't look at me like I was grieving something that never existed. She invited me to her kitchen. Made coffee. And she told me the rules of the house."

"The house being—"

"Everything. The school. Your grandmother's place. This whole town inside the fog line. It's all one house, far as the thing cares." He held up one finger. "Rule one. It doesn't steal anybody. That's the first thing you have to understand, because it's the thing that makes it worse instead of better. It *collects*. And it can only collect what's been left out. Unclaimed property, Edith called it. People the world has stopped actively remembering. Not hated, not mourned—just... unpicked up. A child whose parents are too tired to look at them straight. An old man whose friends have all died. A wife whose husband got busy." His jaw worked. "Attention is a rope, Miss Vasquez. You throw it to somebody every day, or the current takes them. And the current here has a name, and it sits in a chair."

Orsen's face did something complicated. "The chair in the photographs."

"You've seen it." It wasn't a question. "Then you know it's real, and I don't have to convince you, which is a mercy, because convincing people is the one job I've failed at for thirty-one years." He raised a second finger. "Rule two. Keep them named. Not written down—Edith was very clear about that. Written is not the same as remembered. A roster is a cage with the door painted on. You have to say the names. Out loud. To their faces. Every day, like feeding. *Good morning, Milo. Good morning, Edda.* Names spoken are anchors. Names filed are bait."

"That's why you talk to the doors."

"That's why I talk to everything in this building that'll hold still for it." Third finger. "Rule three. Keep them seen. Eye contact, Miss Vasquez. Real looking, not glancing. The thing can't take what somebody's actively watching. That's why it works slow, works at the edges, works on the ones standing in the backs of rooms. And rule four—" his voice dropped, and the boiler seemed to quiet to hear it, "—keep the lights on. All of them. Especially at night. Light is just attention the sun makes, Edith said. A lit room is a claimed room. You leave a corner dark long enough, the house starts furnishing it."

Orsen sat forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment she looked so much like her grandmother that Wendell's chest hurt. "And if you break the rules? If you miss a day?"

"Then you pay attention to what goes missing." He let that sit. "That's how I learned rule five, which isn't really a rule, it's just the truth underneath all of them: it never comes for the ones who are loved out loud. It comes for the lapses. The gaps. The half-second where you're looking at your phone instead of the road." He finally looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and calm, all three at once, the way they'd been for thirty years. "In March of 1988, I was doing inventory in the gym. Forty minutes. Alma was in the office forty feet away, and I hadn't said her name since breakfast, because we'd been married nineteen years and you stop saying it, you start thinking *she knows.* Everybody thinks *she knows.*"

The boiler ticked. Somewhere above them, a door settled with a sound like a sigh.

"When I came back," Wendell said, "her coat was on the hook. Her purse was in the drawer. Her coffee cup was still warm. And Principal Reyes asked me kindly to clear out her desk, because the office needed the space, and there was no record of any Alma Okafor having worked at Hollow Creek Elementary. No file. No emergency contact card. Nothing in the yearbooks—I checked, later, all of them. Just a faint smudge in the 1979 staff photo, like somebody had breathed on the glass." He unfolded his hands at last, and they shook, and he let them. "One lapse. Forty minutes. Nineteen years, and one lapse."

Orsen reached over and put her hand on his forearm, and he let it stay there, because it was warm and human and because, God help him, he'd forgotten what that felt like.

"Why tell me now?" she asked. "After all this time?"

"Because you count," he said simply. "Out of everyone in this town, you count. And because your grandmother left a debt in this house, and debts don't die with the debtor—they wait in the furniture for family." He nodded at the folder of photographs. "How many faded faces?"

"One more every year. And the newest ones—" she hesitated, "—they're not blurring anymore. They're thinning. Like watercolor left in the rain."

Wendell closed his eyes. When he opened them, he stood, took the ledger off the bench, and pressed it into her hands, which was the hardest thing he'd done since he'd cleared out Alma's desk.

"Then the count's coming due again," he said. "It always comes due when a Vasquez starts seeing the chair. Edith told me that too, at the end—said the thing keeps books, and books want balancing, and our family signed something once that made us the pen." He walked her toward the stairs, and paused at the bottom step, and said the last of it quietly, the way you tell someone the location of a landmine:

"Don't write anything down twice, Miss Vasquez. Once is remembering. Twice is filing. And everything filed in this town belongs to it eventually." He glanced back at the boiler, at the small brave orange eye of it burning in the dark. "Go home. Say your people's names tonight, out loud, even the empty rooms. And tomorrow—"

But he never finished the sentence, because upstairs, faint through the floor, a bell rang in the empty school. One short ring. The kind a bell makes when something brushes past it in a hallway where nobody is walking.

Wendell looked at the ceiling, and then at Orsen, and his face went gray as ash.

"It's early," he said. "It's never been early before."
