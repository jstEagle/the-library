## 20. After the Count

The fog had learned to be ordinary again.

Milo noticed it first from the bus window—the way it sat low over Hollow Creek in the mornings now, gray and harmless, like breath on glass instead of like a held one. Before, the fog had seemed to *wait*. It had pooled against the school's windows and pressed there, patient as a creditor. Now it just drifted through the streets the way fog was supposed to, thinning by nine o'clock, gone entirely by recess.

He counted the kids getting off at the corner stop. Four of them. Him, Priya Nair, the Delgado twins. He counted them twice, because counting twice had become a thing his hands did without asking his brain, the way some people cracked their knuckles or chewed their pencils. Priya caught him doing it and didn't tease him. None of them teased each other about it anymore. The four of them had a whole silent language built out of small redundancies—counting twice, saying names before they said anything else, touching a shoulder to confirm a shoulder was there.

"Morning, Milo," Priya said, using his name first, the way they all did now. "Nice shirt."

"Thanks, Priya." He touched her sleeve, light, barely a brush. She was solid. Warm. *There.*

Three weeks since the night in the corridors. Three weeks since the town line, since the van door, since—

Milo's mind did what it always did when it reached that part. It slid sideways, like a foot on wet tile. He knew the shape of what happened. He knew there had been a ledger, and a count, and that Mr. Wendell had stood at the edge of Hollow Creek with his ring of keys and his notebook full of numbers, and that he had turned around and walked back into the fog with his shoulders straight and his flashlight beam bobbing ahead of him like a lantern in a story. Milo remembered that part perfectly. He remembered it the way you remember a dream you've decided to keep—vivid, complete, and somehow weightless, as if remembering it cost nothing, which felt wrong, which felt like the memory itself had been sanded down somewhere so it wouldn't cut him every time he picked it up.

What he couldn't hold onto was Ms. Vasquez's face in that moment. He knew she'd been crying. He knew she'd screamed Wendell's name until her voice tore. But when he reached for her face—her actual face, the specific arrangement of it—he found only a kind of bright blur, like a photograph left too long in the sun.

He'd asked Priya about it once, carefully, on the walk home.

"Do you remember what Ms. Vasquez looked like? That night?"

Priya had frowned, working at it. "Sort of. Tall-ish? Dark hair?" She'd shaken her head. "It's weird. I remember everything else."

*Everything else.* That was the phrase Milo kept coming back to. Because everything else was a lot. Everything else included the fact that they were alive, and full, and that their desks existed again—all thirty-one of them, no gaps, no names the records refused—and that Milo's mother set two plates at dinner and sometimes three, when his aunt came over, and never one, never ever one, and that when he came downstairs in the morning his mother said "Morning, Milo" and hugged him hard enough to lift his heels off the floor, every single day, like she was making up for something she couldn't name.

The school itself had gone soft and sunlit. Mrs. Okafor in the front office hummed while she laminated things. The boiler clanked but only in a normal, boiler-ish way. And the basement archive—Milo had checked, on a dare he gave himself—was just a basement archive: dusty boxes, dead moths, a smell of old paper and floor cleaner. No ledger. No class photos with faces bleeding out of them. He'd looked for twenty minutes, heart hammering, and found nothing but a trophy from 1987 and a box of holiday decorations shaped like snowmen.

Whatever had lived in the paper was gone. Paid. Closed. He knew those words belonged to the story even though he couldn't have told you where he'd learned them.

But the four of them still visited Ms. Vasquez on Saturdays. That had started two weeks ago, unbidden, an instinct none of them discussed. They simply showed up at her door on Maple Street—the grandmother's house, though Milo hadn't known that then, hadn't known whose house it was or what had been bargained in its kitchen—and she opened the door and smiled like the sun coming up over the water tower, and said, "You guys! Come in, come in, I made too many pancakes again," and that was that. A ritual nobody had proposed and nobody would ever cancel.

* * *

This Saturday, October had arrived properly overnight. The maple in Orsen Vasquez's yard had gone the color of fire, and leaves skated across the porch boards when the wind pushed them. Milo pressed the doorbell and heard, inside, the small chaos of someone who owned a dog now—a scruffy terrier mix she'd adopted from the shelter two weeks ago, named Biscuit, who came barreling at the door barking his whole heart out.

"She got a dog," Priya whispered, delighted.

"I heard. She says he doesn't let her sleep past six."

The door opened. Ms. Vasquez stood there in jeans and a flannel shirt with flour on one sleeve, hair pulled back, Biscuit weaving between her ankles. She looked—Milo searched for the word—*lighter*. Not smaller. Just less carried-down, like someone had taken sandbags out of her coat pockets.

"There's my favorite troublemakers," she said. "Get in here before the pancakes go cold. Biscuit, *off*, we talked about this—"

They filed past her, and each of them did the thing without planning it: touched her arm, her elbow, her shoulder, quick and casual, four small confirmations. She didn't seem to notice, or if she did, she read it as kids being affectionate, which maybe it also was.

The kitchen smelled like coffee and butter. Milo stopped just inside the doorway, the way he always did, and let himself look at the room.

He didn't know why the kitchen mattered to him. That was the strange part. He'd only been in this house a handful of times, all after the night he couldn't fully picture, and yet every time he entered this room something in his chest went tight and reverent, like walking into a church. Sunlight came through the window over the sink and lay across the table in a long warm plank. There were jars on the windowsill—Orsen had started keeping flowers in jam jars, cheap grocery-store carnations mostly, because she said the house liked having living things in it. She'd laughed when she said it. *The house likes it.* Nobody had questioned it. In Hollow Creek you didn't question the small superstitions anymore; you just honored them, the way you honored the fact that half the town now left a porch light burning all night, and that the hardware store had sold out of timers twice.

"Milo, you want chocolate chips in yours or are you still pretending to be a purist?"

"Purist," he said, and she snorted and flipped the pancake, and the ordinary miracle of it—of being known, of being expected, of being *fed*—settled over him like a blanket.

They ate at the table, all five of them plus the dog angling for scraps under it. Orsen asked about school. Priya reported that Mr. Calloway had assigned a five-page essay and deserved to be reported to The Hague. The twins argued about whether the new kid, Marcus, had a crush on Priya, and Priya threatened them with syrup. Orsen laughed until she had to put her fork down.

And underneath all of it, Milo watched her.

She was good. That was the thing that both relieved him and quietly broke his heart. She was genuinely, thoroughly good—happy, even, in the uncomplicated way of someone who has woken up from a long illness with no memory of the fever. She remembered them. She remembered their names, their seats, their handwriting, that Milo bit his nails during tests and that Priya drew horses in the margins of everything. She remembered teaching them. What she didn't remember was the rest—the corridors rearranging themselves, the litany of names spoken down hallways that multiplied like a hall of mirrors, the photograph albums, the chair. When the twins had once asked her, shyly, why she'd fought for them—*we heard you did something, back in October, nobody talks about it but we heard*—she'd gone quiet and looked out the window for a long moment.

"I honestly don't know," she'd said finally, and smiled, and it had been true. "I think... I think somebody taught me how to count. I can't remember who. But I've never forgotten how."

Milo had excused himself to the bathroom that day and run the tap so no one would hear him cry.

Somebody taught me how to count. He knew who. He could see him still, plain as morning: big knuckles, keys on a carabiner, a notebook with a rubber band around it, a voice like gravel poured slow. *You count what matters, kid, and you say it out loud, because a number in your head is just a wish. A number out loud is a promise.* Wendell had taught him that in a boiler room that probably didn't exist anymore, over a ledger that definitely didn't exist anymore, and Milo carried the lesson like a stone in his pocket, smooth and permanent, with no idea how it had gotten there.

And Orsen—she'd lost more than that. Milo understood it in fragments, the way you understand a movie you walked into halfway through. Whatever price had been paid to close the count, she had paid it out of herself. There was a hole in her the exact shape of a person, and she didn't know it, and everyone who might have told her was either gone or sworn to silence by their own half-memory. Rosa Vasquez had moved to her sister's place in Sacramento in September; Milo's mother had sent a casserole. Nobody spoke of her much. When they did, they trailed off, the way people trail off near a grave.

"You okay, Milo?" Orsen was looking at him, spatula in hand, head tilted. "You went somewhere."

"Just thinking about the essay," he lied, smoothly, twelve years old and already fluent in mercy. "Five pages is a lot."

"It really is. Want me to look at your outline? I used to be good at outlines." She paused, frowned faintly, as if the sentence had a splinter in it. "*Used to be.* Weird thing to say. I'm still a teacher."

"You're still a teacher," Milo confirmed, and meant a hundred other things by it.

* * *

After breakfast they ended up in the living room, because Biscuit stole a sock and led them on a parade through the house, and because Orsen had a record player now and wanted opinions on whether it was too loud for the neighbors. The four kids sprawled on the furniture while a scratchy jazz record spun, and Milo drifted, the way he always drifted, toward the mantel.

That was where the photographs lived.

Most of them were new—Orsen with the dog, Orsen with the kids from the Fourth of July barbecue, a blurry one of the whole staff at the harvest fair. But at the end of the row, in a plain black frame, stood a photograph Milo had noticed on his last visit and hadn't been able to stop thinking about since.

It was old. Faded at the edges, colors gone soft as watercolor. A garden, blazing green. A woman in the middle of it, laughing—really laughing, head tipped back, one hand raised mid-gesture, caught in the middle of some joke the camera never heard. She wore a cardigan the color of moss. There was no date written on the back. There was no chair anywhere in the frame, no empty seat, no shadow waiting at the edge of the grass. Just the woman, and the garden, and the laugh.

Milo stared at it the way he stared at math problems that almost made sense.

"That one?" Orsen appeared beside him, holding two mugs of coffee. She followed his eyes to the frame. Her expression did something complicated—fondness layered over puzzlement layered over something older and quieter, a homesickness with no home attached. "I don't know who she is. Isn't that strange? I found it in a box when I was cleaning out the house. Everyone else in these albums I can name—cousins, neighbors, my mom when she was young. But not her." She touched the top of the frame lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "I don't know why, but I couldn't put her away in a drawer. It felt—" She stopped, searching. "Rude? Like putting someone outside in the rain."

Milo's throat was tight. He looked at the laughing woman—at the cardigan, the color of moss, the color of a garment he had seen once, worn by someone who should not have been wearing it, in a school gymnasium full of parents whose faces wouldn't hold—and he said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't break the beautiful, fragile thing standing next to him. Some prices you paid all at once. This one she was paying in installments, forever, without knowing the currency.

"She looks nice," he managed.

"She looks like someone who laughed a lot," Orsen agreed. "I hope I would've liked her." Then she shook it off, brightening, because that was what she did now—she moved toward the light, always, like a plant. "Okay! Opinions. Is the record player too loud? Be honest. Mrs. Petrelli next door already gives me a look about the dog."

From the couch, Priya called, "Mrs. Petrelli loves the dog, she told my mom," and the moment dissolved into the ordinary noise of an ordinary Saturday, and Milo let it carry him along.

But before they left, at the door, with the afternoon going gold and the maple dropping its last stubborn leaves, Milo did the thing he did every visit. He turned back on the porch and said, "Bye, Ms. Vasquez. Thanks for breakfast."

"Anytime, Milo." She crouched to scratch Biscuit's ears, then looked up at him, and for one second—one flicker, like a bulb deciding whether to come on—something moved behind her eyes. Something vast and sad and grateful, with no story attached to it. "Hey. You take care of yourself, okay? You count what matters."

The words landed in Milo's chest like a bell struck.

"I will," he said, voice only slightly unsteady. "You too."

He walked down the steps to where Priya and the twins waited on the sidewalk, and the four of them fell into formation without discussing it, and behind them the house glowed warm in the late light, every lamp on inside although the sun hadn't set—left burning out of a habit she couldn't explain, in a house that liked having living things in it.

Milo glanced back once more. Through the window he could see Orsen standing at the mantel again, looking at the laughing stranger in the garden, her hand resting on the frame.

And on the table beside her, half-hidden under a stack of mail, he saw the corner of something old and leather-bound, dark as a closed eye, sitting very still—as if it were waiting, patiently, to be found.
