## 1. Dead Air

The clock above the transmitter read 1:47 a.m., and Rafael Kavanagh had already counted the doors twice.

There were four of them in the WLMR studio building. The front entrance, with its bell that hadn't worked since the Clinton administration. The fire exit at the end of the hallway, painted shut sometime before he was born and never repainted since. The door to the equipment room, which stuck unless you lifted the handle while pushing, a trick he'd learned his first week on air eleven years ago. And the studio door itself — the one with the little square window, the one that stayed locked from the inside during his shift because Hollowmere after midnight was not a place you wanted surprises walking in on you.

Four doors. He counted them the way other men checked their wallets or touched their keys. It wasn't fear, exactly. It was more like knowing where the exits were in a dream.

The ON AIR sign buzzed its familiar amber hum. Outside the window, Main Street lay under a skin of frost so thin it looked like the town had been dusted with sugar. The streetlights made orange pools at intervals, and between the pools there was nothing but dark, and in the dark, if you looked long enough — and Rafael had learned not to look long enough — the shapes of parked cars seemed to rearrange themselves slightly, like sleepers turning over.

"—and that was 'Moon River,' for Doris out on Fenwick Road," he said into the microphone, his voice dropping into the register he thought of as his radio voice, warm as a hand on a shoulder. "You're listening to WLMR, 1340 on your dial, the overnight hours here in beautiful Hollowmere, where the coffee is terrible and the company is better. Lines are open. If you're up, I'm up."

He let the board breathe for a moment, feeding the silence out over the airwaves like bread to birds. That was the trick of overnight radio nobody taught you: the dead air between words mattered more than the words. People driving trucks through the dark, people lying awake beside sleeping spouses, people sitting alone in kitchens with the lights off — they didn't call because they wanted to hear a voice. They called because they wanted to be heard by one.

The first line blinked red.

"Caller, you're live with Rafael. Who's this?"

A pause. A breath that crackled faintly, the way old phone lines did when they ran through wet ground.

"It's Eleanor," the woman said. "Eleanor Voss. Out past the quarry road."

"Evening, Eleanor. Or morning, I suppose. You sound like you've been crying."

"I have." Another breath. "I wanted to tell somebody about the service. Nobody's awake to tell."

Rafael leaned back in his chair, the vinyl sighing beneath him. He knew Eleanor Voss the way he knew half his overnight regulars — a voice, a set of grievances, a geography. She called maybe once a month, always about her garden, always about her late husband's roses that kept coming back no matter how she neglected them, as if the flowers had inherited some stubbornness of their own.

"How was it?" he asked gently. "The service."

"Small," she said. "Small and cold. The church heater's been broken since October and Pastor Lindqvist said a few words about the resurrection that I don't think anybody believed, least of all me. My sister came down from Carver Falls. She wore green, can you imagine. Green to a funeral. And the casket—" Her voice caught, smoothed itself with visible effort. "The casket was white. I picked it myself, months ago. I always said I'd want white, like fresh snow, and my sister remembered, thank God. Somebody has to remember what you asked for."

"That sounds lovely, Eleanor. White suits you."

"You think so?" There was something almost girlish in it, a pleasure that sat strangely against the grief. "I never liked the idea of being buried in black. Everybody else gets to wear the black. Let me have the white."

Rafael laughed softly, a host's laugh, calibrated to carry comfort without condescension. "You'll be the best-dressed woman in the cemetery."

The laugh she gave back was wrong.

He couldn't have said why at first. It was pitched correctly, timed correctly, warm at the edges. But it went on two beats too long, and underneath it there was a sound like wind moving through a house where all the windows were closed, and when it stopped, the silence afterward had weight, the way a room has weight when someone has just left it.

"Rafael," she said. "Can I ask you something?"

"Anything."

"Do you think it hurts? After. Do you think there's an after where things still hurt, or does it stop? Does it finally stop?"

The question settled over him like cold water. He'd taken a thousand calls in eleven years — lonely ones, drunk ones, grieving ones, one memorable man who phoned every Tuesday for a year to report on his feud with a raccoon — and he had a reflex for all of them. But this one slipped past every reflex he owned.

"Eleanor," he said carefully, "is this about Harold?"

"No, honey. This is about me."

* * *

Afterward, he could never reconstruct the order of things — whether he put her on hold first or hung up entirely, whether he stood or remained seated, whether the studio had gone cold around him or whether that was memory inventing drama after the fact. What he remembered cleanly, with terrible clarity, was the obituary page of the *Hollowmere Courier*, still sitting on the desk where he'd left it Tuesday evening, folded to the local section because there was rarely anything else worth reading.

ELEANOR VOSS, 74, of Hollowmere, passed peacefully at home on Sunday, October 12th. Beloved wife of the late Harold Voss. Services held Tuesday at First Lutheran. In lieu of flowers, the family asks donations be made to the Hollowmere Volunteer Fire Department.

Tuesday. Two days ago. He had read it without reading it, the way you read everything in a small town, names sliding past like scenery. Services held Tuesday. Small and cold. The church heater's been broken since October.

His hands were steady when he picked up the phone. That surprised him. He dialed the number listed for the family — the sister in Carver Falls, presumably — and listened to it ring four times, five, six, before a woman answered with a voice sanded raw by weeping and interrupted sleep.

"Hello?"

"Ma'am, I'm sorry to call so late. This is Rafael Kavanagh, from the radio station. I—"

"Oh." The word came out flat, exhausted. "You're the third one tonight. The flowers were delivered, sir. Whatever company you're with, the arrangement was received Tuesday morning, before the service. You can stop calling now."

She hung up. Rafael sat holding the receiver against his ear long after the dial tone had replaced her, listening to that steady tone the way you might listen to a heartbeat, grateful for anything in the world that was still doing what it was supposed to do.

* * *

He went back on the air at 2:31. He knew the exact time because he announced it, because announcing things was what he did, because the alternative was to sit in the amber glow of the ON AIR sign and think about the fact that a dead woman had just described her own funeral to him with the fond precision of a bride reviewing her wedding photographs.

The white casket. The sister in green. The broken heater.

Nobody had told her those things. Nobody could have told her those things, except someone who had been there, and even then — even a living mourner wouldn't have known about the casket choice made months ago, wouldn't have described the pastor's sermon with that particular note of disappointment, *words about the resurrection that I don't think anybody believed.*

Unless she'd been there. Unless she'd been lying in the white casket with her hands folded, listening through the lid.

Rafael pressed his palms flat against the mixing board until the cold metal bit into his skin. Think. There was a version of this that made sense. There was always a version that made sense; his grandfather used to say that about everything, that the world was full of explanations if you were willing to accept a smaller world to fit them in. A prank caller. Someone who'd known Eleanor, who'd attended the service, playing a game with the overnight host. Cruel, but possible. A recording — though WLMR's lines weren't recorded inbound, he'd have to check that, he should check that. Grief madness. Sleep-talking. A wrong number that happened to be a masterpiece of coincidence.

The second line blinked red.

He stared at it for a long time. Long enough that the light seemed to pulse, patient, unhurried. Dead air stretched out behind him on the broadcast, four seconds, six, eight — an eternity in radio terms, enough for listeners to start wondering if the station had died in the night.

He took the call.

"You're live with Rafael."

"Is this the show?" A man's voice, older, gravelly, with the flattened vowels of someone who'd spent his life outdoors. "The night show?"

"It's the night show."

"They told me at the funeral home you'd be on. Well — they didn't tell me. I heard it. Through the wall. They had the radio on in the office while they, you know. While they did the paperwork on me." A dry chuckle. "Walt Prentiss. Out on Route 9. Heart, they think. Found me Tuesday morning in the truck."

Rafael closed his eyes. "Mr. Prentiss—"

"Walt."

"Walt. What can I do for you tonight?"

Another pause, and in it Rafael heard something he would spend the rest of this story trying to unhear: the sound of a man getting comfortable. Settling in. The small domestic noises of someone pulling a chair closer to a table, preparing to talk awhile.

"Just wanted somebody to know," Walt said, "that the truck's still sitting in the dooryard, and my daughter keeps meaning to move it, and she can't. She walks out there every day and stands next to it and comes back inside. I watch her do it. I wish she'd just sell the damn thing."

"I'm sure she will, Walt. When she's ready."

"That's what I keep telling her. Can't hear me, but I keep telling her." The chuckle again, warmer now, almost contented. "Say, you ever wonder about that? About hearing? Whether it goes both ways?"

Rafael looked at the four doors. Front entrance. Fire exit. Equipment room. Studio door, locked from the inside.

"Every night," he said. "That's kind of the job."

* * *

By four o'clock he had taken nine calls.

He wrote them down because writing them down made them into data, and data could be survived where experience could not. On the back of a stale pizza coupon he logged each one in his small, careful handwriting — the handwriting of a boy who'd won penmanship awards in a school that had since been condemned:

**1. Eleanor Voss — funeral, Tuesday. White casket. Sister in green.**
**2. Walt Prentiss — heart, found Tuesday AM, Route 9. Truck in dooryard.**
**3. Denny Kubiak — ? (wouldn't say how). Asked about the quarry.**
**4. Ruth Ann Meechum — hospice, Monday. Wanted the hymn played again.**
**5–9. [see below]**

Callers three through nine blurred together in a way that frightened him more than any single call had. Denny Kubiak had asked, twice, whether the water in the old quarry froze all the way to the bottom in winter, and when Rafael said he didn't know, Denny had said, *I do now,* and laughed, and the laugh had that wind-through-closed-windows sound underneath it. Ruth Ann Meechum had requested "In the Garden" a second time and then, before hanging up, added in a small voice: *Tell my son the checkbook's in the freezer. He'll need it come the first.* Caller six had only breathed. Callers seven and eight had talked about the weather in October of 1974 — specifically, about how cold it had been the night of the twelfth, how early the frost had come that year, how the ground had been too hard to dig properly — and Rafael had assumed they were old men rambling until caller nine, a woman whose name he never got, said quietly, *Ask your grandfather what they buried in the hurry,* and hung up.

His grandfather had been dead for nineteen years.

Rafael sat with the pizza coupon in his shaking hand and made himself look at the pattern the way you make yourself look at an X-ray. Nine callers. Every one of them dead — he verified four of them against the obituaries and the scanner traffic Maren Oduya logged at dispatch, and the other five he simply believed, because belief had become the path of least resistance somewhere around caller four. All dead within the last week. All calling a radio show at two and three in the morning to chat about funerals and freezers and the frost of October 1974.

And one of them had mentioned his grandfather.

He turned the microphone on. The red light woke. Somewhere out in the dark, in kitchens and cabs and sleepless bedrooms, hundreds of people — living people, he had to hold on to that, mostly living people — were waiting to hear whether the voice would come back.

"That was 'Always on My Mind,'" he said, and his voice held, which felt like a small miracle he hadn't earned. "It's 4:17 in Hollowmere. Coldest night of the season so far, they're saying. Frost advisories all week." He paused, and heard himself add, before he could stop it: "If you're up, I'm up. Lines are open."

Both lines lit immediately. Red, red. Like eyes opening.

Rafael counted the doors again — front entrance, fire exit, equipment room, studio door — and reached for the first call, telling himself what he would keep telling himself for days yet: that there was a version of this that made sense, that the world was full of explanations if you were willing to accept a smaller world to fit them in.

Outside the studio window, the frost on Main Street had begun, very faintly, in the shape of letters, to write itself across the glass.
