## 16. The Nineteenth Caller

The frost had stopped at his collarbone.

Rafael noticed it first thing each morning now, standing in front of the bathroom mirror with the tap running hot and useless against the cold in the pipes. The pattern ended just below his throat like a high-collared shirt made of winter, its edges precise as engraving. Below that line his skin was still warm, still his. Above it he was a document someone else was writing.

He had learned not to touch it. Touching it felt like reading over a stranger's shoulder.

The station smelled of burnt coffee and old carpet, the way it always had, and for the first time in weeks the phones were quiet. Since the broadcast — since he'd read the suppressed report aloud, name after name after name, his grandfather's among them, his own blood spoken into the dark like a confession extracted under lamplight — the callers had come one by one to say *received*, and then they had stopped coming at all. Dead air, but honest dead air now. He found he could sit inside it without counting the doors.

Maren was at her desk in the corner, logging nothing. She'd kept the log out of habit, the way sailors keep watches in calm seas. Her pen hovered over a blank line.

"Quiet night," she said, not looking up.

"Enjoy it."

"I don't trust it." She clicked the pen twice. "Silence from the living I understand. Silence from them—" She didn't finish. Neither of them liked saying *the dead* anymore, as if naming them might renegotiate terms.

Rafael pulled the fader down on the bed track and leaned toward the mic out of forty years of inherited instinct — his father's instinct, though he hadn't known until recently whose instinct anything of his really was. "You're listening to WLMR, Hollowmere's voice in the dark. It's 1:47, the frost is holding, and the line is open. If you've got something to say, say it slow. We've got all night, and lately, so do I."

He meant it as a joke. It came out sounding like an obituary.

The board lit before he finished the sentence.

Line three. He watched it pulse the way it always did — no ring, just light, patient as a held breath. Maren's pen stopped moving. Across the room their eyes met, and he saw her do the arithmetic she always did: check the obituaries, check the morgue log, check the list. Nineteen names. Nineteen voices. All received. All silent since.

"Go ahead, caller," Rafael said. "You're on the air."

Static first. Not the soft oceanic static of the other calls — this was sharper, closer, like breath against a mouthpiece. Then a voice, and it was wrong in a way he couldn't immediately name, the way a room is wrong when a picture has been moved half an inch.

"You shouldn't have said my name."

Rafael sat very still. "I'm sorry — who is this?"

"You know who." A woman's voice. Old — genuinely old, not the ageless age of the others, but worn, cracked at the edges, a voice that had smoked or shouted or simply lived a long hard time. "You read it off the report. Page four. You said *Delia Marsh, infant, presumed deceased.* You said it to the whole county."

Maren was already moving, pulling files, her fingers flying through the stack she'd built over weeks. Rafael heard paper tearing somewhere in her urgency.

"Ma'am," he said carefully, "if you're calling about Delia Marsh—"

"*I am* Delia Marsh."

The studio seemed to tilt. Rafael gripped the edge of the console, feeling the cold in his fingertips where they pressed the laminate — the frost conducting itself up through the desk, or maybe just his own pulse misbehaving.

"That's not possible," he said, and hated how small it sounded.

A laugh came down the line, dry as paper burning. "That's what everyone said in 1974 too. That's rather the point."

* * *

She talked for eleven minutes before he thought to ask her to prove it, and by then she had proven it six times over.

She knew the party. October 12th, 1974, the harvest gathering at the Kavanagh house — nineteen children in the cellar while the adults drank upstairs, because that was what the adults did, they put the children below and the music above and called it safety. She knew the fire started in the kitchen, not the cellar. She knew the cellar door locked from the outside, and she knew — her voice went flat and terrible here — that it was opened once, briefly, and that choices were made about who came up the stairs and who stayed down.

"I was four," she said. "I remember the coat. Someone put a coat over me, a child's winter coat, blue with a torn hood, and pushed me behind the coal chute. And then the door shut again." A pause, long enough for Rafael to hear her breathing, wet and human and alive. "Nineteen went down. Eighteen stayed. Everyone in this town has spent fifty years believing I was the eighteenth. Your broadcast told them otherwise."

Rafael's mouth had gone dry. "The records—"

"The records say what your grandfather paid them to say. A death certificate for a child nobody ever saw buried. Do you know what that bought me?" Her voice climbed, and Maren looked up sharply, hand hovering near the dump button, and Rafael shook his head — *no, let her talk, let her say all of it.* "It bought me a life. A real one. My mother carried me out of this town two days later with a suitcase and a new name, and we never came back, and nobody ever looked for us, because why would you look for a dead girl? I grew up in Ohio. I taught fourth grade for thirty-one years. I have a garden. I have a granddaughter. For fifty years I have been the safest person in America, because I did not exist."

"And now," Rafael said quietly.

"And now every Prentiss and Whitlock and Kavanagh sympathizer in the county knows there's a living witness. Knows there's a Marsh woman who remembers the door opening. You read the names, Mr. Kavanagh. You read *my* name. You gave me back to them." The fury in her voice wasn't loud anymore; it had gone past loud into something colder, and Rafael recognized the temperature. It was the temperature of the thing writing on his skin. "I listened to your show for years, you know. From Ohio. Late nights grading papers, I'd stream the station. I liked your voice. Isn't that funny? I liked the voice of the family that left me behind a coal chute."

"We didn't know," Rafael said. "I didn't know any of it until—"

"Nobody ever knows. That's the family business, isn't it? Not knowing." She exhaled, and the sound of it crackled the line. "There were men at my house last week. Before your broadcast even. Two of them, county plates, asking my neighbor if anyone from Hollowmere had ever been in touch. Asking about property records. Asking about *me*. I told myself it was coincidence. Old women tell themselves things. But then you said my name on the air, and yesterday there was a car parked across from the garden center for four hours, and I am done telling myself things."

Maren slid a note across the console: *Cross-checking Marsh — no death cert filed publicly. Nothing in county index. She's right. It was buried.*

Rafael closed his eyes. Behind them he saw the ledger, the torn page, the space where a twentieth name should have been. His father's name. His father, who survived because someone else paid his share. He had believed, until this moment, that the someone else was a stranger. Now he understood the arithmetic had been crueler than that. The ledger hadn't been missing a name. It had been missing *two* — one written out, one never written in at all.

"Delia," he said. "Come to the station. Come on the record. I'll put you live, I'll put you anywhere you want to be seen. Visibility is protection now — the whole town knows what happened, the families are ruined, there's nothing left for anyone to protect by hurting you—"

"You think ruin ends things?" She laughed again, and there was something almost pitying in it. "Mr. Kavanagh, I've been alive for fifty-four years, which is longer than most of the people in your little graveyard drama, so let me teach you something the dead can't: shame doesn't end men. It just changes what they're willing to do. You took away the thing they were ashamed of and replaced it with a witness. Which one do you think sleeps worse?"

The line hissed. Somewhere in the static Rafael thought he heard — faintly, beneath her breathing — another sound. A skipping rhyme, sung very far away, the way you hear children through the walls of a school. *One for sorrow, two for the door...*

"Do you hear that?" he asked.

Her breathing changed. "Every night since your broadcast," she whispered. "Every night, under my window. I thought I'd imagined it fifty years ago. I know I haven't imagined it this week." A pause. "They're not angry at me, Mr. Kavanagh. That's the worst part. I waited my whole life to be angry at them and they're not angry at all. They're *waiting*. Like I'm late. Like I was supposed to come back and I keep not coming back."

"What do they want?"

"I don't know. But I know what I want." Her voice steadied, and when it came again it was the teacher's voice, the one that had managed thirty-one years of fourth-graders, patient and immovable. "I want the rest of it. Not the report — I've read the report, my lawyer read the report, it says what happened and not *why*. I want to know what was taken. Not who. What. Because whatever it was, it's the reason eighteen children stayed behind a locked door while grown men stood in a yard and let them, and it's the reason your family kept a ledger like a grocery bill, and it's the reason I've been hearing a nursery rhyme under my window in Ohio, which should be impossible, and yet here we are."

Rafael looked down at his chest. Through his shirt, faintly, he could see the pattern had moved. An inch, maybe less — new lines threading up toward his throat, fine as etching, and threaded within them, legible if he tilted his head, a word he hadn't seen before:

*TAKEN.*

"Delia," he said slowly, "I think I know where the answer is."

"Then go get it," she said. "And Mr. Kavanagh — next time you want to save somebody, ask them first."

The line went dead. Not faded — cut, clean, like a door closing.

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. The ON AIR sign buzzed its red buzz. Outside the studio windows, Hollowmere lay under a sky the color of unwashed wool, and the flood channel excavation lights burned orange in the distance, illuminating nothing.

Maren broke the silence first. "I ran her name while she talked. There's a Delia Marsh, seventy last month, Columbus, Ohio. Registered voter. Pension records. A photograph." She turned her laptop toward him, and there she was — a broad-faced woman with white hair and reading glasses on a chain, standing in front of tomato plants, caught mid-laugh by some grandchild's camera. Alive. Specific. Real in a way none of the callers had ever been.

"She's the only one," Maren said. "Of all of them, she's the only one who got out." She scrolled. "And Rafael — the property inquiry from last week? County records show it was filed by a shell company. I traced it two layers deep before it went dark. The second layer belongs to the same trust that paid for the concrete permit."

"Prentiss."

"Prentiss's money, if not Prentiss's hands. They're not done. Ruin made them quiet, not finished." She closed the laptop. "She's right, you know. About what she asked. We have the who. We have the how. We don't have the *what*. And until we do, everything else is just — weather."

Rafael stood. The frost shifted with him, a garment he hadn't chosen, and in the reflection of the dark studio glass he could see the new word sitting over his heart like a name tag at a conference of ghosts.

His grandfather's house was waiting. The boarded windows, the cellar stairwell, the ledger with its torn page. And below all of it, behind a steel door sealed from the inside, whatever it was that nineteen children had been traded for — whatever it was that had let grown men stand in a yard and count, and choose, and lock.

He picked up his coat. On the way out, he paused at the door and looked back at Maren.

"If I'm not back by dawn—"

"I know where you keep the spare key," she said. "And I know how to work the board. Go."

The night air hit his face like water. Somewhere far off — under the town, under the channel, under fifty years of concrete and silence — he could almost hear it: a skipping rhyme, patient as frost, keeping time.

*One for sorrow, two for the door...*

He got in the truck and drove toward the house that remembered him, carrying a dead girl's question like a lantern: *What was taken?*

The frost, he noticed as he drove, had begun to spell the answer. One letter at a time. And it wanted him home to finish the sentence.
