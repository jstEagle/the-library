## 3. Wren on the Porch

The light was going out of the day the way water goes out of a sink — not all at once but in a slow spiral, gray draining into darker gray, until the sky over Hollowmere House was the color of old pewter and the bare trees stood against it like ink strokes on wet paper.

Astrid had spent four hours inside. Four hours of measuring rooms that refused to be measured, of writing figures in her ledger book and then looking down to find them smudged or wrong or, in one case, replaced entirely by a column of numbers she had never written at all. She had found her own childhood drawings folded into a wall cavity behind the plaster of the upstairs landing — crayon houses with too many windows, a stick-figure family holding hands, and one drawing of a well with something rising out of it that six-year-old Astrid had rendered, with unerring instinct, as a scribble so dense it looked like a hole burned through the paper.

She had put the drawings in her satchel. She did not know why. It felt like evidence. It felt like contraband. It felt like bringing home bones.

Now she needed air, and daylight, though there was very little of either left. She let herself out the front door — the lock turning behind her with a sound like a swallowed word — and stepped onto the porch, and stopped.

Someone was sitting on the porch steps.

* * *

The porch light was burning above them. It had been burning when she arrived, hours ago, though the house had been empty for thirty years and the electricity should have been cut off before her mother's funeral. She had noted it in her report under *anomalies*, professional word, safe word, and then she had avoided looking at it all afternoon because every time she glanced up the bulb seemed to have brightened, patiently, like an eye adjusting.

The light fell full on the figure now, and Astrid's first thought — absurd, reflexive, the thought of a woman who appraised houses for a living and believed in load-bearing walls and municipal records — was: *squatter.*

Her second thought arrived a half-second later and took her knees with it.

The figure turned its head. Small face, round-cheeked still, with the particular softness of a child who has not yet learned where their jawline will end up. Hair the color of wet sand, cut in a ragged bowl that no adult hand could have managed. A sweater knitted in stripes of rust and cream, sleeves too long, cuffs frayed from being worried by small thumbs. Corduroy trousers worn pale at the knees. Bare feet, despite the cold, despite the frost already silvering the dead grass beyond the porch rail.

Wren.

Not a child dressed like Wren. Not a memory wearing a costume. Wren — exactly, perfectly, terribly Wren, down to the chipped front tooth and the freckle constellation across the nose that Astrid had once, at six years old, mapped aloud as *the Little Dipper, but sideways.*

"Thirty years," Wren said. Their voice hadn't changed either. It was high and light and slightly hoarse at the ends of sentences, the voice of a kid who talked constantly and had never once been told to hush. "You said you'd come back after supper."

Astrid's satchel slid off her shoulder and hit the porch boards. She didn't hear it land. The world had narrowed to a point, a needle's eye, and everything she was poured through it backward.

"You're not real," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "You were never real. You were — I made you up. Dr. Halvorsen said I made you up because Dad left and Mom worked nights and I needed—"

"I know what she said." Wren patted the step beside them, an old gesture, worn smooth as a river stone. Astrid's body remembered it before her mind did; her feet were already moving. She sat down on the cold boards three feet away, close enough to feel the wrongness radiating off the child like heat off a stove, far enough that she could pretend otherwise.

Frost had formed on the porch rail beside her. She watched it creep while she breathed. In. Out. The way her mother had taught her, hands pressed flat over a small frightened chest: *breathe like the tide, little bird, in and out, nothing lasts.*

"You sat right there," Wren said, pointing at the exact board beneath her. "Every evening in October. You'd wait till the streetlight came on and then you'd say, *he'll be here any minute,* and I'd say, *I'm always here, you just can't see me till the light does.*"

"The streetlight." Astrid heard herself say it from very far away. "There wasn't a streetlight. There was never a streetlight on this road. I checked, when I got older. I drove out here when I was nineteen and I sat in my car and I looked, and there is no streetlight within two miles of this house."

"No," Wren agreed comfortably. They swung their bare feet, heels knocking softly against the step. Knock-knock-knock. The rhythm of it went through Astrid like a key through a lock. "But you saw one. Every night. Right at the bend in the drive. Orange light, moths around it in summer. That was me, mostly. That was how I let you know I was coming."

"That was a hallucination."

"It was a courtesy," Wren said, with the mild dignity of a child correcting a slow classmate. "Houses don't have to be polite. This one chose to be. Mostly because of me. I told it you'd be scared otherwise."

Astrid laughed. It came out of her like something breaking — a short, cracked sound with no humor anywhere in it, and it frightened her more than anything else that had happened today, because it was the laugh of a person whose sanity has begun negotiating terms.

"This isn't happening," she said. "I'm tired. I've been in a cold house all day breathing dust and mold spores, and I skipped lunch, and grief does things to the—"

"Astrid." Wren turned and looked at her fully, and their eyes were brown, warm brown, ordinary child-brown, and they held nothing but patience, which was somehow the worst thing they could have held. "You counted the doors today."

The laughter died in her throat.

"Eleven," Wren said. "Then thirteen. Then nine. You wrote it down twice and crossed it out both times. You do that when you're scared. You've done it since you were little. Cross it out, start again, maybe the second answer will be kinder." They knocked their heels against the step. Knock-knock-knock. "The house counted back. Did you feel it? Like someone walking behind you matching your stride?"

Astrid's arms had broken out in gooseflesh beneath her coat. "How do you know that."

"Because I was listening." Wren said it simply, without apology, the way a child says *the dog ate my homework* — a fact offered up, expecting the world to accommodate it. "I'm always listening. That's most of what I am, really. Listening and waiting. I got very good at waiting. Thirty years is long, but it's shorter than some waits I've done."

"*Some waits,*" Astrid repeated. "What does that mean? What are you?"

Wren smiled, and the chipped tooth caught the porch light, and for one vertiginous moment Astrid was six years old again, sitting cross-legged on this same porch in the orange glow of a streetlight that did not exist, sharing secrets with her best friend in all the world, the only creature alive who never got bored, never left, never asked her to be anyone but exactly what she was. The joy of it rose in her like floodwater — sudden, total, drowning. Her eyes stung. Her chest ached with it. *Wren. Oh God, Wren, I missed you, I missed you every single day and I never told anyone because telling would make it worse—*

And then the joy crested, and broke, and underneath it was the cold black thing it had been hiding all along:

Children who are real grow up.

This one hadn't.

The sweater was the same sweater. The hair was the same hair. The chipped tooth had chipped the same way, on the same corner of the same step, the summer Astrid was seven — she remembered the incident, Wren tripping on the lip of the porch, blood on the boards, Astrid crying harder than Wren did. Thirty years, and not one day had touched them. The world had moved on and left this child sitting on the steps like a coat someone forgot, and the only reason a child stays a child is that they were never a child at all.

"What are you?" she whispered.

Wren's heels stopped knocking.

The dusk had thickened around them while they talked. Beyond the porch rail the yard was dissolving into blue-black shadow, and the well's stone cap was a low dark shape near the edge of the trees, and Astrid realized she had been avoiding looking at it all afternoon without ever deciding to.

"You remember the winter we met," Wren said. It wasn't quite a question. "October. You'd just moved in. You were sad all the time — quiet-sad, the kind grown-ups miss. And one night the streetlight came on and I was under it, and you weren't even surprised. Do you remember why?"

Astrid did remember. She remembered with her whole body, a shiver starting at the base of her skull. She remembered thinking, with the terrible clear logic of a six-year-old: *of course the house gave me a friend. Houses know when children are lonely. That's what houses are for.*

"I thought the house sent you," she said slowly.

"The house didn't send me." Wren picked at a thread on their cuff. Unraveling it. The cuff had been unraveling for thirty years and was no more undone than before. "The house *kept* me. There's a difference. Sending is a choice. Keeping is—" They searched for the word, frowning, and Astrid watched a child's face perform an ancient weariness, and had to grip the porch rail to keep the ground steady. "Keeping is a debt."

"Whose debt?"

Wren looked at her then — really looked, the way no six-year-old had ever looked at anyone — and behind the brown eyes something older moved, patient and vast and sorry, like a shape passing under ice.

"Yours," they said gently. "Your family's. But mostly—" the heels started knocking again, knock-knock-knock, soft as a heartbeat heard through a wall, "—mostly the town's. Astrid, I need you to listen now, because the light's almost gone and after dark I can't say certain things out loud. The house won't let me. It gets shy after dark."

"Shy," Astrid echoed. "The house gets *shy.*"

"It's polite during the day. It's honest at dusk. And it's hungry at night." Wren scooted closer, and the cold coming off them was not the cold of a child's body; it was the cold of a root cellar, of deep water, of the space beneath floorboards where nothing has ever seen the sun. "You found the drawings. Good. You need to find the rest. There's a ledger, down where it's damp. Your grandmother's name is in it. Last page. Uncrossed."

The name landed in Astrid like a stone dropped down a shaft. She waited for the sound of it hitting bottom.

It kept falling.

"Wren," she said, and her voice shook, "what happened in 1994? Why did we leave? My mother never said. She'd change the subject, she'd leave rooms, once she broke a glass just holding it when I asked about this house—"

"I know." Wren's voice had gone quiet, hoarse at the ends, the way it always went when the talking got serious. "She carried it her whole life. So did your grandmother. So will you, unless you finish it." They reached out — a small hand, fingers cold as coins — and touched the back of Astrid's wrist, and the touch went through her like winter through a window seam. "You came back to count the doors. But the house brought you back to count something else."

"What?"

Wren nodded toward the yard, toward the low dark shape at the tree line, and as the last of the daylight failed, Astrid saw — or thought she saw, or knew, in the way you know things in dreams — that the frost on the grass had grown in patterns, spiraling outward from the well in long pale fingers, like something below the ground slowly spreading its hand.

"The water's rising," Wren said. "It's been rising since the day you drove up. It knows the ledger's unfinished. It knows a name is owed." The child's face turned up to hers, and the porch light buzzed overhead, and somewhere inside the house a door closed — softly, deliberately, like a throat clearing. "You asked what I am. Ask me instead what happens at solstice if nobody pays. Then ask me fast, Astrid, because you've got eleven days, and tonight—"

The porch light flickered. Once. Twice.

"—tonight it's already getting impatient."

Behind them, through the door she had locked, Astrid heard the hallway clock — a clock she had catalogued that morning as *non-functional, mechanism seized* — begin, very faintly, to tick.
