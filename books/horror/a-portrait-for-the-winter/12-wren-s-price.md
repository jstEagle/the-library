## 12. Wren's Price

I have been in this house so long that I no longer remember the shape of the world outside it.

That is not quite true. I remember shapes — the way birches lean when the wind comes off the ridge, the particular gray of a November sky like unwashed wool, the smell of woodsmoke and wet leaves and someone else's supper drifting down a lane. I remember these things the way you remember a language you spoke as a child: perfectly, uselessly, with no one left to speak it to.

But I do not remember my name.

I remember that I had one. It sits somewhere behind my ribs like a stone at the bottom of a well — which is fitting, perhaps, because that is where names go in this house. Down. Into the water. Into the ledger. I gave mine up before Astrid was born. Before her mother was born, possibly. Time moves strangely for me; I am kept the way jam is kept, sealed and sweetened and shelved in the dark, and the seasons pass over me without ever quite touching.

Tonight the house is quiet in the way it only gets before it wants something. The floors have stopped their slow counting. The doors are holding still at twelve, which they only do when they are listening. And Astrid is sitting on the floor of the old nursery with the founders' papers spread around her knees like fallen leaves, and she has asked me the question I have been dreading for thirty years, since the first afternoon I saw her come up the drive with her mother's chin and her grandmother's stubbornness written all over her small furious face.

*What are you, Wren?*

I could lie to her. I have lied to children before — that was part of what I was for. But she is not a child anymore, and lying to her now would be like lying to the house: it would know, and it would wait, and eventually it would collect.

So I tell her the truth, and the truth tastes like well water.

* * *

"Before you," I say, "there was another."

Astrid looks up from the papers. The lamp light does something unkind to her face, hollowing her cheeks, deepening her eyes until they look like the two dark doorways at the end of the upstairs hall. She has not slept properly in three days. None of us have. The house doesn't sleep either, but it pretends, the way an old dog pretends, circling and settling and keeping one ear turned toward the cellar.

"Another child," she says. Not a question. She has read enough of the ledger by now to know how this goes.

"A girl," I say. "In 1931. There was a bad winter — the worst the town had seen since the founding. The wells froze, the cattle died, and the thing under Hollowmere began to stir because the solstice offering had been... thin. A name from a family that had already given twice. It wasn't enough. It never is, in the end. That's the arithmetic of hunger — it always rounds up."

"What happened to her?"

"She was brought down to the cellar on solstice night by her own father. He carried her. She was seven." My voice comes out flat, and I am grateful for that. Flat voices can carry terrible cargo without spilling it. "The ritual requires three acts. Naming. Offering. Sealing. Her father performed the naming — wrote her name in the ledger with his own hand, because the house will only accept a name given freely by blood. Then the offering. And then—"

I stop. In the walls, faintly, the pipes tick. The house settling its weight, or pretending to.

"And then the sealing didn't take," Astrid says quietly. "Because of what you told me. About the dead whispering through the well. They said the founders never wrote the rite down."

"They never wrote it down because writing it down makes it transferable, and a debt that can be transferred can be refused. So each generation had to carry it in their bodies, hand to hand, mouth to ear. And in 1931, something went wrong. Whether the girl fought, or the father faltered, or the thing below simply decided it wanted more than it was owed — no one living knows. The sealing failed. The well stayed open. And the house—" I spread my hands, those hands that have no reflection in the hall mirror, that leave no prints in the frost, "—the house was left with a wound. An unfinished sentence. It does not tolerate unfinished sentences, Astrid. You've seen that. It counts its doors over and over because it cannot bear for any number to be unresolved."

"So it kept her."

"It couldn't keep her *below* — the seal had failed, the offering hadn't fully passed over. But it couldn't let her go either, because a name given cannot be ungiven. So it did what houses do with things they cannot digest." I sit down across from her, folding myself onto the nursery floor, and even now, after everything, I choose the spot where the moonlight comes through the window, out of some ancient habit of wanting to be warm. "It kept her *in*. Between. Neither offered nor free. And because the house is old, and old things get lonely — because between winters, with the bargain dormant and the well still, there is nothing in these rooms but dust and waiting — it made her into a companion. Something to walk the halls. Something to count the doors alongside it. Something small enough that a lonely house might love."

The lamp flickers. Outside, the wind finds the loose shutter on the east gable and worries it, back and forth, like a tongue working a broken tooth.

"You," Astrid says.

"Me," I say. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I was going to tell you differently, when you were six. I was going to tell you I was your friend because I liked you. That part was true. But the rest — the reason I was here at all, the reason a lonely little girl on a lonely hill found a playmate no one else could see — that was the house. It put me on the porch the day after your family moved in, the way you'd put out a saucer of milk for a stray. Here, it said. Here is something soft for the child. Something to keep her happy while we decide."

"Decide what?" But I can see in her face that she already knows. She has known since the ledger. Since her grandmother's uncrossed name. Since Edith Marlowe sat across from her in a kitchen that smelled of cinnamon and cowardice and confessed to thirty years of substitutes.

"Whether you'd be the next entry," I say. "You were born into the default line, Astrid. Your family took the house's protection every winter of your childhood — the full larder, the mild fevers, the neighbor's tractor that swerved away from your father on the ice road. All of it bought and paid for. And when the time came to renew, your grandmother refused, and died trying to end it, and the debt rolled forward. Down the years. To you."

* * *

Here is what I have not told her yet. Here is the part I have been carrying like a hot coal cupped in both hands, walking toward this moment one careful step at a time for three days.

She asks it anyway, because she is her grandmother's granddaughter, and the people in this family have never once been able to leave a locked door alone.

"The ritual needs an offering," she says slowly. "Naming, offering, sealing. If I complete it — if I seal the well at solstice — what happens to you?"

The coal burns straight through my palms. I let it.

"I don't know," I say, and then, because she deserves better than that: "No. That's a coward's answer. I know. I've always known. I just hoped you'd never be brave enough to ask."

"Wren."

"If the seal takes, the cycle closes. Everything the house is holding gets resolved. Every unfinished number becomes finished." I look at my hands. They are a child's hands. They have been a child's hands for ninety years, and they will be a child's hands until the end of this, whatever the end is. "Every name in the ledger goes where names go. Including mine. Including the part of me that's still — that's still owed. I was given, Astrid. Given isn't taken back. Given is *kept*."

"No." She says it fast, the way you say it when you haven't thought it through, when it comes up from somewhere below thinking. "No. There has to be another way. We'll find another way. Edith's papers, the founders' notes, the rite the dead were whispering — maybe there's a clause, maybe—"

"There isn't a clause." I make my voice gentle, which is the cruelest thing I know how to do, because gentleness is what I was made for. "There's never been a clause. That's the whole architecture of it. The bargain is a circle, and circles don't have doors, only—"

"Don't." Her voice cracks down the middle. "Don't you dare be philosophical at me. You don't get to sit there in that body — in *her* body, in that little girl's body — and tell me you're fine with being erased. You don't get to make this easy."

"I'm not making it easy," I say. "I'm making it true. There's a difference, and it matters, because if you go into solstice night believing there's a way to save everyone, you'll hesitate at the moment you can't afford to hesitate, and the well will crest, and the thing below will come up hungry, and it will take *everything*, not just me. Not just you. The town. The valley. Whatever it can reach before dawn." I lean forward. The moonlight slides off me like water off glass. "Listen to me. Ninety years I have walked these halls. Ninety years of counting doors and dusting nothing and listening to the house breathe. Do you know what I wanted, all that time? Not freedom. I stopped wanting freedom around 1960, quietly, the way you stop missing a limb. What I wanted was for it to mean something. For the counting to end on a real number. For someone to close the book."

"Not like this."

"Especially like this." I smile, and I feel the smile fit my face the way it always has, slightly too wide, slightly too bright, the smile of a thing designed to comfort. "You were the best part of it, you know. Thirty years ago. Six years old, furious at the world, marching around this house telling it what was what. You talked to the doors. Did you know that? You told them they'd better behave or you'd have your father unscrew them off their hinges. The house *loved* you. It hasn't loved anything since 1931. When you left, it grieved. Houses grieve badly — they sulk, they rearrange, they let the cold pool in corners where children used to play. This whole return of yours, every room remembering, every drawing surfacing from the walls — that isn't the house hunting you, Astrid. That's the house showing you its scrapbook. Saying: *look. Look what we kept. Come home.*"

"That's worse," she whispers. "That's so much worse."

"Yes," I agree. "Love with a price attached usually is."

* * *

We sit in silence for a long time after that. The nursery holds us the way it held us once before, decades ago, two small figures playing at tea with cups that weren't there. I can feel the house leaning in around us, listening through every seam, and I hate it a little for that, and love it a little too, because that is the condition of being what I am: to hold two feelings in one hollow chest and have neither one win.

Finally Astrid speaks, and her voice has changed. The crack has sealed over. What's underneath is harder and colder and more like her grandmother than I think she realizes.

"Then here's what happens," she says. "I finish the ritual. All three acts. And when I get to the offering, I offer something else. My memories of this place — I've been thinking about it since the well started rising. The house wants belonging. That's what all of this is. Not blood. Not lives. Belonging. It kept you because it was lonely. It wants me because I'm the last person who ever loved it honestly. So maybe the offering doesn't have to be a life. Maybe it can be the love itself. Give it everything I remember about being happy here, and let the seal run on that."

"It won't accept it," I say. "The rules are older than the house's wants."

"Then it can starve," she says, "and I'll write my own name in the ledger instead of anyone else's, and we'll find out together what happens."

And there it is. The thing I feared more than erasure: not that she would fail, but that she would refuse the terms — refuse the entire grammar of sacrifice this house was built on — and start improvising against rules that have killed everyone who ever bent them.

"You can't replace me with yourself," I say. "That's not freeing me, that's just changing whose name is in the column."

"I'm not replacing you," Astrid says. "I'm ending the column."

The word goes through the house like a bell through water. Somewhere below us, down past the cellar stairs, past the shelf of preserves and the founders' iron-bound chest, the well gives a single soft sound — a lap of black water against old stone, patient as interest accruing. The frost on the window crawls a fraction of an inch inward, five-fingered, reaching.

And the house begins, very quietly, to change its mind about us.

The lamp gutters. In the hallway, a door that has held steady at twelve for three days swings open on darkness that wasn't there before, and the number of doors becomes thirteen again, and then eleven, and the floorboards under Astrid's knees shift like a tide going out. The nursery walls breathe inward. On the desk by the window, where she left her appraisal folder, paper whispers — pages turning themselves, the report writing itself in her own handwriting, line after line, and though I cannot read it from here I know what it says, because I have watched this house compose documents before. It is very good at appraisals. It has been appraising her since the day she arrived.

*Astrid,* I want to say. *Run.*

But I know her now, ninety years of knowing her compressed into one week, and I know she will not run. She stands up in the guttering lamplight with the founders' papers clutched to her chest and her jaw set like her grandmother's, and she looks at the walls that are closing around her like a fist, and she says, to the house, in the voice of a six-year-old who once threatened to unscrew its doors:

"We're not done talking."

The house disagrees. The hallway outside the nursery stretches, lengthens, folds back on itself, and the lamplight bends toward the cellar stairs like iron filings toward a magnet, and beneath our feet, beneath the whole shivering frame of Hollowmere House, the well begins — softly, steadily, with the sound of a held breath finally being released — to refill.
