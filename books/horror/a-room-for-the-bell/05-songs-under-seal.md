## 5. Songs Under Seal

Teo had been avoiding the Villalobos house for three days, and he was good at it. He knew all the ways a man could not be somewhere in a village the size of Corvo Alto: he could take the upper path past the olive terraces instead of the lane; he could time his trips to the bakery for the hour when Sable went to the church; he could let his dog off the lead so that any approach required an apology first. He had done all of these. The village watched him do them and said nothing, because the village understood. That was the whole problem with Corvo Alto. It always understood.

On the fourth evening, Sable found him instead.

He was mending a section of wall below the cisterns, alone, when her shadow fell across the stones. He didn't look up right away. He finished setting the stone, brushed the mortar from his hands with exaggerated care, and only then turned to face her — because you couldn't run from Sable Villalobos. You never could have, even as children, even when she was small enough to fit inside a flour sack and stubborn enough to climb out of one.

"You've been hiding from me," she said.

"I've been working."

"Teo."

He looked at her properly then. She had her grandmother's jaw — that same hard line under the softness — and she was holding a folder of photographs against her chest like a shield, which meant she'd been at them again, which meant the chair had moved again, which meant everything was worse than yesterday and would be worse tomorrow.

"Come inside," he said. "Not here. Walls talk here."

"That's exactly what I'm afraid of," she said, but she followed him down.

* * *

His house sat at the crook of the lower lane, the last one before the fields gave up and the scrub took over. It had been his mother's house, and before that his grandmother's, and the rooms still held their shapes around the absences: the chair by the window where no one sat, the second cup in the cupboard nobody used, the coat hook by the door kept empty the way other houses kept a shrine. Teo lit the lamp, put water on for coffee he had no intention of drinking, and waited for her to start.

She didn't make him wait long. She spread the photographs on his table like a hand of cards.

"Forty years," she said. "I pulled every picture I could find in my grandmother's house, plus what Father Anselmo let me copy from the parish albums. Same kitchen. Same table. And there's a chair, Teo — a chair that isn't in the earliest ones, and then it is, and then it's closer to the head of the table every decade. Closer every *year*, if I read the light right. I came here to record a lament. Nobody sings a funeral song twice unless they're hiding something about it. And now there's furniture growing in my family photos like mold." She flattened her palm on the table. "Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me it's two different chairs. Tell me anything."

The kettle began its low complaint toward boiling. Teo watched it and thought about how many times in his life he had stood at this exact distance from the truth — close enough to feel its heat, far enough to pretend it was weather.

"You should burn them," he said.

"The photos?"

"All of them. Every picture in that house. Take them into the yard and burn them and don't look while they go." He heard his own voice and hated how steady it was, how practiced. "Then pack your recorder and take the morning bus, and don't come back, and when people ask you about Corvo Alto, say the wine is bad and the beds are lumpy and the folk music is thin. Say anything. Lie well. It's the kindest thing anyone does here."

Sable stared at him. In the lamplight her eyes were almost black, and he could see the archivist in them doing arithmetic — weighing his face, his tone, the thirty years of shared summers and stolen figs against the shape of what he was telling her.

"You're not lying," she said slowly. "That's the worst part. You actually believe what you're saying."

"I don't believe it. Belief is for outsiders. I *know* it, the way you know the taste of your own mouth." He got up, poured the coffee he didn't want, set the cup in front of her anyway because his mother had raised him to do that much. His mother. The word opened under him like a floorboard.

"Teo." Her voice gentled, which was more dangerous than the questions. "What happened to your mother?"

* * *

Everyone in the village had a version of the year Teo turned nine, and none of the versions were spoken aloud. They lived in the pauses between sentences, in the way conversation bent around certain months like water around a stone. His mother had gone to market in Vila Real and not come back — that was the official shape of it, worn smooth by repetition until it felt almost true. A woman leaving. Women left. It happened.

But Teo had been nine, and nine-year-olds see everything and are believed in nothing.

He remembered the week before. The way the older women had stopped speaking when he entered a room mid-sentence. The way his mother had measured salt into jars — actual jars, sealed with wax, laid along every windowsill and threshold like small white sentries. He remembered that she had cried once, quietly, while kneading bread, without changing the rhythm of her hands, as if grief were just another ingredient. He remembered asking her what was wrong and her saying, *Nothing, meu amor, eat your soup,* and he remembered that the soup tasted of rosemary and something else underneath, something mineral and cold, like coins held too long in the mouth.

And he remembered the night itself. Not her leaving — he'd slept through whatever that was — but the morning after. The silence of the house. The wax seals on the windowsills broken outward, every one, as if something had walked the perimeter of the room touching each jar in turn, checking the locks, finding them sufficient, and then simply — choosing differently. The chair by the window pushed back from the table at an angle no living person leaves a chair. Facing the door. As if she had stood there a long time, looking out, before she stopped being anywhere at all.

The village had closed around the absence like water. No search party worth the name. No priest saying masses for the missing — Anselmo had said one, Teo learned later, but privately, in the tower, with the door barred. The council had come to the house with a document about guardianship already written, needing only his father's signature, and his father — hollowed, drinking, half-mad with a grief he wasn't permitted to name — had signed. Within a month the household was listed in the parish rolls as reduced. Within a year, if you asked anyone what had become of Rosa Ferreira, they'd look at you with mild, honest confusion, the confusion of people who have agreed very carefully on a story, and say: *Didn't she leave?*

"They sang for her," Teo said. He hadn't meant to say it yet. It came out anyway, the way blood comes through a bandage. "At the wake for old Marta — did you notice? They sang the lament once, and then the oldest woman, Dona Ilídia, made a motion with her hand, like cutting a thread, and everyone stopped. That's not tradition. That's protocol. One verse is a warning. It says: it has taken, it may take again, keep your doors shut and your names unspoken. But the song has a second part. My grandmother knew it. Dona Ilídia knows it. Half the women over sixty know it, the way you know a prayer in a language you don't speak." He wrapped both hands around his own untouched cup. "They sing it once to warn each other. They never sing it twice, because sung twice — completed — it stops being a warning. It becomes an invitation. A welcome. Sung whole, at the right hour, in the right house, it tells the Bell: *this door is open. This name is given.*"

Sable had gone very still. "The Bell," she repeated. "You said that like it's capitalized. Like it's a name."

"It's the only name it gets. Saying more gives it more." He laughed, short and joyless. "You're the folklorist. You've read the comparative literature — the bell that tolls for a household, the death-knock, the fetch, the church-grim. Every culture has some version of the thing that announces a taking. Ours doesn't announce. Ours *collects*. Once a generation, one household. It comes when it's invited, and it can only be invited by song, and the song is the only thing in this village older than the church."

"And my grandmother—"

"Kept you ignorant." He met her eyes and didn't flinch, though it cost him. "Yes. On purpose. Ask yourself why a woman who loved you would leave you a house full of answers and no key to any of them. Ask yourself why she never once let you visit after you turned twelve. Ask yourself why she wrote to you every month for twenty years and never once mentioned the lament, the registry, the chair — any of it." He leaned forward. "There's a reason, Sable. There's always a reason. Protection looks exactly like a lie from the inside."

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the scrub with a sound like dry paper turning.

"My mother," he said, because now that the seal was cracked the rest wanted out, all of it, the whole pressure of thirty years. "The year I turned nine. The ledger — you haven't found the ledger yet, so I'll tell you what's in it. Households. Dates. One name per generation, written in advance, the way you'd write a debt into a book. And next to each name, when it's paid, the entry goes black. Burned? Inked? I don't know. I've never seen it. Nobody under sixty has seen it and admitted it. But my mother's name was in it. Rosa Ferreira, the Ferreira household, the year 1994." His voice caught and he let it. He was tired of being steady. "She knew. That's why the salt, that's why the crying, that's why she faced the door. She had months to prepare and nowhere to run, because running doesn't work — it takes the household, Sable, whoever's under the roof, and if the roof empties it follows the blood. She stayed so it would only be her. She stayed so it wouldn't be me."

Sable's hand had come to rest near his on the table, not quite touching. "Teo—"

"Don't." He drew back. "Don't comfort me. Comfort is what we use instead of truth in this village. We comfort each other all the way to the grave." He stood, walked to the window, looked out at the dark coming down over the fields. When he spoke again his voice was flat, archival, the voice of a man reading someone else's file. "Here's what you need to understand about us. About all of them — the council, Anselmo, Dona Ilídia, every gray head in Corvo Alto. We're not monsters. Monsters would be simpler. We're survivors of a thing that only spares villages that feed it, and we've been feeding it for three hundred years, one name per generation, and every one of us who lives does so because someone else's name was written down. My mother. Your great-uncle Joaquim, probably. Someone's daughter in 1911. The whole village stands on a foundation of names, and the price of standing is silence, and the silence is the second payment, and we pay it every day, gladly, because the alternative—" He turned back to her. "The alternative is that it doesn't stay fed. And then it doesn't take one household."

The lamp flickered. Neither of them moved.

"So when you came back with your recorder," Teo said, "asking for the lament — asking for a *second take* — do you understand what that sounded like? To them? Do you understand what you asked for at Marta's wake, standing there with your microphone like a knife?" He shook his head. "Half the village thought you were the Bell's herald. The other half thought you were just a fool. They're still deciding."

"I asked for a folk song," Sable whispered. "I asked for a *song*."

"You asked for the one sentence this village has spent three centuries not finishing." He came back to the table, gathered her photographs, squared their edges, and held them out to her — not returning them, exactly. Surrendering the argument. "Burn them or don't. Leave tomorrow or don't. But hear me, because I'm the only one who'll say it plainly: whatever you find in your grandmother's house, however bad it gets — the answer will not be the one you came for. The truth here doesn't free anyone. It just tells you whose name is next."

She took the photographs. At the door she paused, silhouetted against the last of the light, and asked the question he'd been dreading since she arrived — the one he could see she'd been carrying behind her teeth for days, patient as an egg tooth.

"Teo. If there's a ledger with names in it. Whose name is on the last page?"

He looked at her — at her grandmother's jaw, at the folder pressed to her chest, at the whole doomed architecture of her homecoming — and found he could not lie to her. It was the one kindness left.

"Go home, Sable," he said. "Look in the cedar chest under your grandmother's bed. And when you find it — and you will find it tonight, because you're her granddaughter and you don't know how to stop — come back here before dark tomorrow. Because after you read that last page, you're going to need someone in this village who's on your side."

She left. Teo stood in the doorway until her footsteps faded on the lane, then went inside and did what his mother had taught him, what the village had taught him, what he would teach no one: he took down the wax-sealed jar from the cupboard, checked that the seal held, and set it on the windowsill facing the road to her house.

All night, he told himself. Just until she reads it. Just until she understands what she is.

The jar sat in the dark, white and patient as a small moon, and did not break.
