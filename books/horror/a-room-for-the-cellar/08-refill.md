## 8. Refill

The lights went out at 1:47 a.m., all of them at once — the board, the ON AIR sign, the little green pilot eye of the console that had glowed without interruption for eleven years — and then they came back on the way a held breath comes back: too fast, too full, as if the dark had been poured back into the room from a great height.

Theo's hand was already on the fader. He didn't remember moving it. The record he'd been playing — a slow, rain-soaked ballad he used to fill the hour when callers thinned out — kept spinning, its needle riding the run-out groove with a soft rhythmic tick, tick, tick, like something counting.

He looked up through the studio glass at the window facing Delmore Street. Every streetlight down the block was stuttering. Not failing — *stuttering*, bright and dim and bright again in a pattern too regular to be electrical trouble, a pulse, a rhythm, the whole town blinking like one enormous eye trying to wake up.

"Okay," he said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud was what he did, it was the whole architecture of his life. "Okay."

The phone lines lit up. All six of them. At once.

In eleven years of overnight radio, Theo Quintana had never seen all six lines light simultaneously. He'd seen two, maybe three during the ice storm of '09, when half the county was awake and frightened and wanted a voice in the room with them. Six was not an audience. Six was a congregation.

He let them ring. He watched them ring. And he understood, in the wordless way you understand a change in air pressure before a storm, that answering was a decision with weight to it, that the ringing itself was a kind of question, and that whatever he did next would be remembered by more people than were currently alive.

He answered line one.

"KDLR, this is Theo. You're on the air."

The voice that came through was Mrs. Ada Renner's, and it was not Mrs. Ada Renner's. Ada Renner had called his show every Thursday night for years to complain about the music and request Patsy Cline, and her voice had been dry and particular and faintly amused, the voice of a woman who found the world disappointing but forgivable. This voice was flat. Even. It came out of the speaker like water finding level.

*"Renner, Ada Louise. Nineteen thirty-one. Crossed the eleventh of March."*

"Ada?" Theo said. His professional mask was still on — he could feel it there, the warm broadcaster baritone, the calm — but underneath it his hands had gone cold around the fader. "Ada, if you can hear me, you sound different tonight. You want to tell me where you're calling from?"

*"Crossed the eleventh of March,"* she said again, patiently, the way you'd repeat directions to someone who wasn't listening. *"Cause listed: heart. Attended by: daughter, son-in-law. Interred Fairview, plot forty-one. Renner, Ada Louise."*

And then line two clicked over automatically — the board doing something he hadn't told it to do, the console routing calls the way a throat routes breath — and a man's voice joined hers, mid-recitation, harmonized with it almost:

*"Okafor, Daniel Bright. Nineteen fifty-eight. Crossed the ninth of March. Cause listed:—"*

"Stop," Theo said quietly. "Folks, I know who you are. I've known since Tuesday. You don't have to do it like this."

But they did have to do it like this. That was the thing he understood even as he said it. Last week the dead had called him as themselves — confused, gentle, asking him to keep the lights on, each of them still carrying some residue of the person they'd been, the way a bell carries the shape of the note after it stops ringing. Whatever had come up out of the ground tonight, whatever the flickering lights were feeding, had wrung them out. They weren't callers anymore. They were recitations.

Line three. Line four. Line five. Each one a name, a date, a cause, a plot number. He recognized every name. That was the worst part — not that they were strangers, but that they were his listeners, his insomniacs, his truckers and nurses and widowed schoolteachers, and now they were being read aloud like entries, like inventory, their whole lives compressed to a crossed-out line in a ledger he had seen only once, in his grandmother's attic, and had told himself he'd imagined.

*Each crossed out on a date matching a local death.* He had read that ledger three days ago and put it back in the trunk and driven home and kissed Marisol and made coffee and done the show, and he had thought, I will deal with this tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow. Now the ledger was reading itself back to him through the mouths of the crossed-out, live on 580 AM, and every insomniac in the county was hearing it.

He should cut the mic. He knew he should cut the mic. Some deep animal part of him was screaming it — end the broadcast, go dark, unplug the transmitter, stop being audible, because audible was visible and visible was *available* —

But stopping felt like opening a door.

That was the knowledge that had settled into him over these past weeks, unearned and absolute, the way you know not to look over your shoulder on a dark road. As long as he was talking, as long as the signal was going out, as long as there was a voice in the room filling the silence, the silence couldn't fill itself with anything else. Dead air wasn't empty. Dead air was a vacancy, and vacancies got filled. His grandmother had known it. Whoever she'd made her bargain with had counted on it. A room for the cellar; a voice for the well. Keep the lights on. Keep the line open. Keep talking until sunrise.

So Theo Quintana did the only professional thing left in him. He leaned into the microphone, and he smiled — they couldn't see it, but a smile changes the voice, everyone in radio knows that — and he said:

"You're listening to KDLR, the overnight shift. It's ten minutes to two, and we've got a full board tonight, friends. Sounds like everybody wants to talk. So let's talk."

* * *

For the first hour, he tried to make it a show.

It was the strangest broadcast of his career, and he had once spent four hours interviewing a man who believed the reservoir was shaped like a keyhole on purpose. He took the recitations and wrapped them in patter — *that's a name I haven't said on air in years, Ada, I hope wherever you're calling from they've got Patsy Cline* — and between names he played records, long slow ones, songs about rivers and rest, buying himself ninety seconds at a time. The dead didn't seem to mind. They waited through the music with the patience of furniture, and when the song ended they picked up exactly where they'd left off, the next name, the next date, the next plot.

And the names kept coming. That was the other thing. The ledger in his grandmother's trunk had held perhaps sixty entries across seventy years. But the callers weren't limited to it. Line six gave him a name he'd never heard — *Vasquez, Elena Marisol* — no relation, a woman from the east side who'd died in January, before any of this started, before the calls, before the counting of doors. The recitation had widened. The well wasn't just reading its old accounts. It was taking new ones.

Or — and this thought arrived with the cold specificity of a knife laid flat against skin — it was reading the town. All of it. Every name that had ever been given to it, freely or otherwise, in seventy years of doorkeepers and bargains and quiet arrangements made in kitchens like Marisol's mother's kitchen. The ledger was just the page Theo had seen. The book went deeper.

At 2:40, the lights flickered again — harder this time, the whole studio dropping to black for a full second, the console dying under his hands, the transmitter light winking out — and in that second of true darkness Theo heard, very clearly, beneath the hum of the building, a sound like water rising in a deep stone throat. Not rushing. Rising. Patient. A well refilling.

Then the power returned, everything surging back brighter than before, and the voices on the phone lines changed pitch — all six at once, dropping half a step, like a choir modulating to a darker key — and they began to recite faster.

*"Hollis, Ruth Anne. Crossed the fourth of November, nineteen sixty-one—"*

*"Pemberton, Elias James. Crossed the second of June, nineteen seventy—"*

Theo's own blood stopped moving for a moment.

Elias Pemberton. His grandfather. Dead nineteen years before Theo was born, a man who existed for him only as a photograph on the stairs and a name on a deed. Crossed out. In the ledger. In his grandmother's handwriting, presumably, or in whatever hand had written before hers.

*"Pemberton, Corinne Mae. Crossed the— "*

His grandmother. Recited by the dead, on his own show, while he sat at the microphone with his mouth open and nothing coming out of it. For three seconds — three seconds of dead air, three seconds of vacancy — Theo Quintana said nothing at all.

The studio got colder. Not metaphorically. His breath fogged in the light of the ON AIR sign, and somewhere behind the racks, in the wiring, in the walls, something shifted its weight.

He talked. He made himself talk. It came out ragged, nothing like the warm baritone, but it came out: "—and that, folks, is a name from my own family, which goes to show you nobody's exempt from the census tonight, am I right? Stick around, we've got weather on the half hour and more of your calls after this." He cued a record with hands that shook so badly he missed the start groove twice. The song came on. The cold receded, grudgingly, like a tide deciding to be merciful.

He understood then, fully, what the performance was. Not entertainment. Not distraction. Performance was the wall. Every word he spoke was a brick. The show wasn't a show anymore; it was a door being held shut with his voice, and the moment he stopped — really stopped, stopped meaning it, stopped filling the room — the door would swing inward, and the thing that had been invited into his grandmother's house decades ago would walk through the doorway it had never once had to open for itself.

*It has never opened a door that wasn't offered.* He hadn't heard the second voice say those words yet — that conversation was still ahead of him, sleeping in his own tape hiss — but he knew it anyway, the way the town knew the shape of the reservoir. Everything that happened in this house happened by invitation. Even this. Even the recitation. Somewhere in the terms of the bargain, in the fine print written in flood-water ink, there was a clause about the lights staying on, and his grandmother had signed it, and the signature had passed down like the deed, like the house, like the twelfth door he refused to count again.

* * *

By 3:30, the listeners had started calling in.

Not the dead ones. The living. Line one blinked with a real caller — he could tell immediately, the breath, the hesitation, the small human stutters the recitations never had — a man out past Miller Road, whispering that every light on his property had blinked at the same time, in rhythm, and that his dog wouldn't stop looking at the well cover in his yard. Then a woman from the flats, crying quietly, saying her kitchen radio had turned itself on at two a.m. and was playing his show, except she didn't own a radio that received KDLR, the set was her dead husband's and it had never worked.

Then a teenager, voice cracking, no whisper at all, loud and terrified and gloriously alive: "Dude, dude, are you hearing what they're saying? Those are death notices. My grandma listens to you, she has the scanner app, she woke my mom up, the whole street's awake, is this a prank? Tell me it's a prank."

And Theo, mask firmly in place, voice steady as a table, said: "I wish I could tell you that, friend. What I can tell you is that if your lights are flickering, stay inside, keep a lamp burning, and don't answer any knock you're not expecting. That's good advice most nights. Tonight it's very good advice."

Half the town would call it a stunt by morning. He could already hear the morning-drive guys doing bits about it, the Facebook posts, the *remember when Theo Quintana faked a ghost broadcast*. Let them. Ridicule was a kind of darkness too, but it was a human darkness, a safe one, and every person laughing at him tomorrow was a person who hadn't heard the second voice, hadn't stood in front of the bricked alcove in his cellar and felt it humming like a held breath against his palm. The stunt story was a mercy. He'd take it.

But a handful — a handful would know. A handful had called in tonight, whispering, in voices gone strange and careful, saying things like *I hear it too, at night, when the house is quiet, it answers questions I haven't asked yet,* and *my mother told me never to count the doors and last night I counted the doors,* and one old man who simply said, in the cadence of a man laying down a heavy tool after decades of use, *"Tell the Delgado girl the account's come due,"* and hung up before Theo could ask what account, whose due, which Delgado girl.

Marisol. He hadn't called her. She was asleep at home — or lying awake, which was worse — and he had not called her, because he did not know how to say any of this out loud to the one person whose face he loved, and because some cowardly, practical corner of him believed that as long as she didn't know, she was outside the circle of the lamplight, and things in the dark could only take what stood in the light.

He knew it wasn't true. He'd known since the alcove. Nothing in that cellar needed his permission to reach her. But the lie was load-bearing, and he kept it up another hour, the way he kept the show up, the way he kept everything up.

* * *

At 4:15, the recitations stopped.

All six lines went quiet between words — mid-name, actually; a woman's voice had gotten as far as *"Delgado, Rosa Elena, crossed the—"* and simply ceased, like a needle lifted. The silence that followed was enormous. It pressed on the windows. Theo sat in it with his hand hovering over the fader, broadcasting nothing but the soft spin of vinyl and his own breathing, and he realized the breathing was the only sound he'd heard all night that belonged entirely to him.

Then, from line four — the line that had been silent longest — a voice came through that was different. Smaller. Human. Shaky with the effort of being a self.

*"Theo."*

"Ada?" he said. "Ada Renner?"

A pause. A gathering. *"It's hard. Holding the — holding myself. It's like standing in a river. But I'm here a minute. Listen to me."*

"I'm listening, Ada. Everybody's listening."

*"The well fills at dawn. That's what tonight was. It doesn't take, understand, it — it counts. It counts what it's owed, and at sunrise the count comes due, and whatever name is sitting uncrossed when the water reaches the top—"* Her voice frayed, thinned, began to flatten back toward the recitation cadence, and he heard her fight it, heard her drag herself back like a swimmer dragging herself toward shore. *"Your name is next, Theo. It's been next for a while. Somebody wrote it down a long time ago and it's been waiting, patient as anything, and the water's coming up, and when it crests—"*

The line dissolved into static. Into the flat voice. *"Renner, Ada Louise,"* it began again, serene, and Theo reached over with a steady hand he did not feel and killed the line gently, the way you'd close a door on someone sleeping.

4:22 a.m. Ninety-eight minutes to dawn.

He looked at the console, at the ON AIR sign burning its red vigil, at the window where the streetlights of Delmore Street pulsed in their slow, tidal rhythm, and he understood that the show was over in every way that mattered and that he could not, must not, stop broadcasting. The performance was survival. The performance was the wall. He would sit here and fill the air with his voice until the sun came up or he came apart, whichever happened first, and either way the microphone would be hot.

He leaned in.

"This is Theo Quintana, KDLR, twenty minutes to five," he said, and his voice was wrecked and human and completely, stubbornly awake. "Stay with me, folks. We're gonna be here till sunrise. All of us together. Nobody spends this night alone."

On line two, softly, patiently, something that used to be Daniel Okafor began to recite.

And in the doorway of the studio — though he had locked that door himself at midnight, though the hallway beyond it was dark and empty and had been all night — Theo heard, distinctly, the sound of a second set of footsteps stopping beside his chair.

He did not look. He kept talking.
