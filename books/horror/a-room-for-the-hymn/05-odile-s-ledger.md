## 5. Odile's Ledger

Odile arrived with the rain and did not knock.

Bram found her at the kitchen table of the farmhouse at six in the morning, still in her travel coat, a canvas satchel open before her like something surgically opened. She had let herself in with the key hidden under the third porch board—the same key their grandmother had sworn no one else knew about—and she had already made tea neither of them would drink.

"You look terrible," Bram said, because it was true and because they had never learned any other way to speak to her.

"You look like your mother," Odile said, which was worse. She turned the satchel toward them. "Sit down, Bram. I need to show you what we are."

The book she drew out was not beautiful. That was the first shock of it—Bram had expected something grand for all this weight, some grimoire bound in calfskin and iron, and instead it was a ledger, the kind a feed store might keep, its cover cracked to gray suede, its corners soft as old bread. The word *Brightwater* was inked across the front in a hand that had pressed hard enough to emboss the paper beneath.

"What is it?"

"Accounts," Odile said. "Owed and paid."

* * *

She opened it to the first page and turned it so the morning window light fell across the columns, and Bram read, and the room went cold around them in a way that had nothing to do with the draft under the door.

The entries were deaths. Every death in the family, going back past the founding of Hollowmere itself, recorded in a cramped, patient script that changed hands every few pages and never changed its discipline. A name. A date. And beside each one, in a narrower column, a single word or phrase: *sung forward. paid in full.* Or, less often, in a shakier hand: *partial. balance carried.*

"There were two hundred and eleven of us," Odile said. "Give or take. Every Brightwater who died inside the county was sung out at their deathbed. The hymn, forward, start to finish, by whoever kept the ledger. It wasn't mourning. It was maintenance."

"Maintenance." Bram heard their own voice go flat. "You're telling me our family sang to a door."

"I'm telling you our family sang to keep a door *shut*, and there is a difference so wide you could bury a town in it." Odile's finger came down on an entry near the middle of the book, where the handwriting had begun to deteriorate, letters leaning like tired men. "Read the marginalia. Grandmother made me memorize it when I was nine. I thought she was senile. I thought—" She stopped, jaw working. "I thought a lot of things at nine."

Bram read. The margins were crowded with notes in three or four generations of hands, arguments conducted across decades in ink:

*It does not want worship. It wants voices. Feed it the dying and it sleeps.*

*Never sing it alone. Never sing it whole unless a death requires it. It learns from every verse; what it hears once it keeps.*

*Aunt Verity sang it drunk at Midwinter, 1911. The well froze from the bottom up. Do not be Verity.*

And then, near the end, in a hand Bram recognized with a lurch—they had seen it on recipe cards, on birthday cards, on the salt canisters in the pantry—a note written large and pressed deep:

*The line is thinning. Each singer gives more than a song. My mother lost her mother's face. I have lost the sound of my own name sung by anyone who loved me. When I am gone, let it sleep hungry rather than wake fed. Let the last verse stay unsung. Forgive us. We were afraid.*

Bram looked up. "Whose writing is that?"

"Your grandmother's," Odile said. "The last entry in the ledger is hers." She turned pages until she reached it, and Bram saw that the final record was not a death at all. It was dated forty-one years ago, and it read, in full:

*Sealed the door from within. Maren stays with it. The hymn ends unfinished. God forgive the coward's arithmetic.*

"Maren," Bram said slowly. "Maren Brightwater. She was in the church records. The singer who disappeared in 1984."

"She didn't disappear." Odile closed the ledger with both hands, as if it might breathe. "She walked into the passage behind the seal and barred it behind herself. From the inside, Bram. Think about what that means. She didn't lock it in. She locked herself in *with* it. Because the binding only holds if someone sings against it from that side, forever, and she was the last of us strong enough—or stupid enough—to do it."

"And now the crew broke the door."

"Now the crew broke the door," Odile agreed. "And whatever Maren has been holding shut for forty years has had four decades to learn her voice, and through her voice, ours."

* * *

Bram stood at the sink afterward, washing cups that were already clean, because their hands needed occupation the way other people's lungs needed air. Ten years of hospice work had taught them a hundred small rituals for steadying themselves, and they ran through all of them now without effect.

"You knew," they said finally. "Before the excavator. Before any of this. You came to the funeral and told me to sell the house and never come back, and I thought you were just being Odile."

"I was being kind, in the only dialect our family speaks." Odile hadn't moved from the table. She looked smaller in the farmhouse kitchen than she ever had anywhere else, as though the house remembered her and was pressing down. "I told you to leave because distance thins it. The counting reaches farther every generation—that's in the margins too, if you read the dates. Our grandmother heard it in three rooms. Your mother heard it across the whole farm. You hear it through hospital walls. Do you understand what that curve means, Bram? It isn't that we're getting closer to it. It's that it's getting closer to *us*. We're not its jailers anymore. We're its larder."

"The patients count backward," Bram said. "Every one of them, in their last days. I told myself it was coincidence for ten years."

"It's rehearsal," Odile said. "It's learning the hymn from the only people who still carry pieces of it in their blood. The dying give it up easiest—it's already halfway out of them. And when it has enough of the song, it won't need the dying anymore. It will come looking for a whole voice. An heir. Someone who can sing the final verse, the one Maren never taught it, the one our family refused to finish." Her eyes came up, and they were their grandmother's eyes exactly, and Bram hated her for it. "There are two of us left, Bram. Two Brightwaters who can hear it. And I am fifty-eight years old with a heart that my doctors tell me, in their gentle way, is a matter of months."

The cup slipped in Bram's wet hand. They caught it against the basin hard enough to bruise.

"You're sick."

"Dying, technically. There's a difference and it's mostly paperwork." Odile smiled, and it was the worst thing Bram had ever seen her do, because it was warm. "Which makes me the next entry in the ledger. And it makes you the one holding the pen."

"No." Bram set the cup down with exaggerated care, the way they set down everything when they were frightened, precision as armor. "No. I'm not doing this. I'm not singing anything. I don't even know the hymn."

"You know it better than you think. You've heard it backward your whole life; it lives in you inverted, like a negative. That's why the counting stops around you—did you notice? In the ward, when you sit with them, the numbers slow?" Odile rose, stiff, and came to stand close, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "You're not just an heir. You're a counterweight. The thing can't finish its song while you're listening, because part of you is always singing against it. Grandmother called it the spare part. The last bolt in the door."

"Spare part." Bram laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "That's what I am. Not a person. A *part*."

"You're my niece. My nephew. Whichever you've decided this year—you change it like weather and I love you either way." Odile took their face in both hands, and her palms smelled of rain and old paper. "You're the only family I have left, and I am asking you to hate me for what I'm about to say, because if you hate me you'll remember it. When I die, the thing will come for you. Not the counting. *It.* And you will have one choice: learn the final verse and sing it forward, and pay what Maren couldn't ask anyone else to pay—or run, and let it finish the hymn with whatever voice it finds, and Hollowmere goes the way of the choirs."

"The choirs?"

Odile's hands dropped. Something moved behind her face—not fear exactly. Shame.

"That," she said, "is the other thing I came to tell you. And you're not going to believe it until you see the church yourself."

Outside, the rain thickened. And far beneath the floorboards, beneath the clay and the limestone and the sealed stone door, so faint that Bram could pretend—for another hour, another day—that they had imagined it, something began to hum a melody they almost knew, patient as water, waiting for the ledger's next line.
