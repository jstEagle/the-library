## 10. A Room for the Hymn

The last verse had no words. That was the first thing Bram understood, standing at the threshold with the door open and the dark leaning out of it like a tide. The hymn had never been language. It was arrangement—voices placed one against another the way stones are placed in an arch, each one bearing the weight of the rest. The thing below had spent a century learning how to sing alone, and a song sung alone is only a sound. What it needed, what it had always needed, was a room built of voices.

Bram gave it one.

They opened their mouth and the first note came out steady, and the corridor behind them—every patient, every nurse, every soul the backwards hymn had caught mid-breath—went still. The thing sang along. Of course it did. It sang along the way ivy climbs, wrapping each phrase before Bram could finish it, dragging the melody backward through their chest, and with every note it pulled, something left.

Not pain. That was the terrible mercy of it. It felt like setting down bags after a long walk. The smell of woodsmoke in the Brightwater farmhouse kitchen—gone. The sound of Odile laughing, a dry rustle like paper—gone. Grandmother's hands, flour-dusted, pressing a salt line into the sill—gone, gone, gone, each memory lifted out whole and carried down into the dark, folded into the thing's growing choir, made into another stone in its arch.

Bram sang anyway.

They had learned the verse from the dying, and the dying had learned it from the walls, and the walls had learned it from the Brightwaters who built the seal and abandoned it unfinished. Ten verses of keeping. One verse of ending. The final verse was a door that only closed from the inside, and the singer had to be the last thing through.

Halfway. The thing's voice was inside their voice now, harmonizing, trying to bend the phrase retrograde, trying to sing the ending backward into a beginning. Bram felt it working at the architecture of the verse like a locksmith, patient, ancient, so tired of being alone. *Come down*, the harmony said, in the wordless way harmony says things. *Come be kept. We have her. We have all of them. You will never be lonely again.*

And that was the trap, and Bram saw it clearly, and it nearly worked. Because the memories were going, and what remained was not emptiness but a kind of terrible clarity: they knew they were loved. They could not remember a single face, but the knowledge of being loved had been poured into them so deeply, by so many hands, that it had become structural. It was the beam the whole house stood on. The thing below could take the faces, the voices, the names. It could not take the fact.

*I was loved,* Bram sang, in the only language left. *I chose this. Those are mine. You can't have those.*

The last note of the last verse was a single held tone, low, almost beneath hearing, and Bram gave it everything that remained—not the memories, those were already gone, but the wanting, the long lonely decade of night shifts and whispered comforts, the whole shape of a life spent listening to the dying so that no one would have to die unheard. They gave it that. The note went down through the stone floor, through the excavation, through the door that had been sealed from the inside by a Brightwater woman a hundred years ago, and it found her, and it found all of them, and it closed the arch.

The door shut with a sound like a book closing.

Then—silence.

Not the held-breath silence of the counting years. Not the silence of something waiting. Ordinary silence, the silence of stone and soil and a town asleep, and Bram stood alone in the excavation with their ears ringing and their hands shaking and no idea, anymore, why they were crying.

---

They found Bram at dawn, sitting on the lip of the pit with their coat around their shoulders, watching the light come up gray and clean over the scaffolding. The foreman said later that Bram looked like a person waking from surgery—present, polite, and searching.

"Are you hurt?" the foreman asked.

"No," Bram said, and found it was true. "No, I don't think anything's hurt. I just—" They stopped. There had been someone. Someone who came, someone with a ledger, someone who— There had been someone, and the shape of the absence was so specific it ached, like the phantom pain of a limb. "I think I lost someone. I don't know who."

The foreman looked at them strangely and helped them up the ladder, and neither of them spoke again.

---

The hospice was quiet in a way it had never been quiet. Bram walked the ward that morning and felt the difference in their body before they understood it in their mind: no counting, no hum under the floorboards, no backwards melody threading through the walls like water through limestone. The building was just a building. The dying were just dying.

Mrs. Abernathy in room four went at eleven, in her sleep, with the window cracked to the spring air. Mr. Voss went that afternoon with his daughter's hand in his, and his daughter said, bewildered and grateful, "It was so peaceful. It's never peaceful." One by one, through the week, the ward emptied itself the way wards are supposed to—gently, in order, without accompaniment. Bram sat with each of them. Held their hands. Said the things hospice workers say. And when each one died, Bram felt the same phantom ache, the same specific absence—*this one taught me something, this one mattered, I know they mattered*—with nothing behind it but the knowing.

The grief was intact. Only the beloveds were gone.

Dr. Ferro found them in the break room on Thursday, staring at the bulletin board covered in photos from the summer picnic. Faces smiled out of the glossy paper, and Bram studied them like a traveler studying a map of a country they'd visited in a dream.

"Bram. You've been different since the excavation. Should I be worried?"

"I'm fine." Bram touched the edge of a photo—a woman with white hair and a fierce mouth, someone's arm around her shoulders. "Do you know who this is?"

"That's Odile. Your aunt. She was here for—Bram, she was here for weeks. She died Tuesday night. You sat with her. You *sang* to her, they told me, the night the— the night everything happened." Dr. Ferro's voice gentled. "You don't remember her at all?"

Bram looked at the photograph a long time. The fierce mouth. The paper-rustle quality of the smile, though they couldn't have said why that phrase came to them. "I know I loved her," Bram said finally. "I know she loved me. I know she gave something so that I could—" They stopped. The sentence had no end they could reach. "I know it the way you know a load-bearing wall is there. You can't see it, but the house stands."

Dr. Ferro said, quietly, "That's a terrible price."

"It was mine to pay." And Bram found, to their own surprise, that they were not bitter. The anger they should have felt—at the Brightwaters, at the cowardice, at the inheritance—was itself a memory they no longer possessed. What remained was the choice, and the choice had been made freely, and a freely made gift does not curdle into resentment. It just costs. It just keeps costing, quietly, every morning, every face in the corridor that Bram almost recognized and could not place.

"I'm staying," Bram said. "The night shift. I want the nights."

"You could go anywhere. Start over."

"Everything I am is here." Bram said it simply, because it was simply true. The memories were gone, but the shape they'd left behind was Bram's—ten years of listening, a decade of small kindnesses, the competence that came from choosing, night after night, to sit with the dying. The thing below had taken the faces. It had not taken the person the faces had made.

---

On Bram's first night back, they made their rounds the old way. Tea for Mr. Calloway, who liked it too sweet. The window cracked for Mrs. Abernathy's empty room, out of habit, then closed again, out of grief for a woman Bram knew they had loved. At the end of the corridor, Bram stopped at the door of the new patient—a transfer, arrived that afternoon, an old man with hollow eyes who had not spoken since admission.

He was counting.

*Four. Three. Two.*

Bram went cold from the scalp down. They stood in the doorway with their heart hammering, listening for the hum beneath it, the backwards melody, the thing that patient and hungry beneath the floor—

The old man opened his eyes. Looked at Bram. And smiled, small and tired and human.

"Sorry," he rasped. "Bad habit. My wife—she used to count my pills out at night. Four, three, two, one, sleep. I count down to sleep now. Silly." He closed his eyes. "One."

Bram stood there a long moment, hand pressed flat against the doorframe, feeling the ordinary stone, the ordinary silence, the ordinary night of an ordinary town that had forgotten how to sing and was finally, blessedly, allowed to forget.

They finished the rounds. They sat with the dying. They did the work.

And at 3 a.m., in the break room, Bram took out a pen and a fresh notebook—Odile's brand, they'd somehow known to buy that brand—and wrote on the first page, in careful letters, the only inheritance worth keeping:

*I was loved. I don't remember by whom. I chose this, and I would choose it again. If you're reading this, and you hear something counting in the walls—*

Bram paused. Listened. The hospice breathed around them, silent, stone-silent, safe.

*—then sing. Sing forward, all the way to the end, and don't stop for anything that offers to sing it for you. Some doors only close from the inside. Make sure it's your voice holding them shut, and not your silence letting them open.*

They closed the notebook, and went back down the corridor to the rooms of the dying, and the night was long, and it was only a night, and Bram stayed.
