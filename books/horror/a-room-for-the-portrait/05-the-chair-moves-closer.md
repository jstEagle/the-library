## 5. The Chair Moves Closer

Theo did not sleep. That was the thing he would remember later — not the photographs themselves, not yet, but the fact of lying in the narrow bed in the back room of the farmhouse, listening to the pipes tick and settle, and thinking: *I'll sort it in the morning. It'll make sense in the morning.* As if daylight were a solvent. As if the world were a stain that only looked like a shape.

By six he had given up on the fiction of rest and gone down to the kitchen, where he made tea he didn't drink and laid the photographs out on the table like a hand of cards he was afraid to look at.

There were eleven of them. He'd pulled them from the third estate box the night before, the one marked in his aunt Marjorie's careful hand: *FAMILY — KEEP*. He had told himself he was cataloguing. He was good at cataloguing; it was half his job. Infestations were, in the end, a paperwork problem — you documented, you measured, you filed, and somewhere in the filing the fear got smaller. That was the whole architecture of his sanity: if you could write it down, it couldn't grow.

He arranged the photographs left to right, oldest to newest, the way he'd arrange invoices.

The 1912 wedding portrait came first. Great-Grandfather Edmund Hartwell, stiff in his collar, a bride whose name Theo had never learned, both of them staring past the camera with the grim endurance of people who considered photography a medical procedure. Behind them, the south wall of the farmhouse, whitewashed. The orchard gate.

And there, at the edge of the frame, close enough to the couple that it seemed to have elbowed its way into the composition — a wooden chair.

Plain. Ladder-backed. Empty.

Theo had noticed it last night and filed it, because that was what he did. Old photographs had clutter in them. Someone had left a chair out; someone had been too poor or too indifferent to move it. He'd written *chair, extraneous* on his inventory sheet in his small, square handwriting, and he had gone to bed, and he had lain awake.

Now, in the grey light, he picked up the second photograph. 1923. A christening, by the look of it — a baby swaddled in lace, held by a woman with Edmund's same unhappiness in her jaw. Taken on the front step this time, the door behind them. And there, at the edge of the group, the same chair. Ladder-backed. The same worn pale patch on the left armrest where a hand would rest.

He set it down. Picked up the third. 1931, harvest, a row of Hartwells squinting in front of a hay cart — and the chair, standing on the packed earth at the end of the row, close enough that the nearest man's elbow almost touched it.

Theo sat very still.

He was not a fanciful man. Three generations of Hartwells had made their living crawling under floors and into roof spaces, and the trade taught you a specific discipline: what you saw, you saw; what you didn't see, you didn't invent. Rats were rats. Squirrels were squirrels. The thing in the pantry ceiling was grease and handprints, and handprints had hands behind them, and hands had bodies, and bodies could be found, blocked out, and billed for.

But chairs did not walk.

He laid out the rest in a line across the kitchen table, and the kitchen went very quiet around him, the way rooms do when they are watching.

1946. The chair, on the lawn, in front of a cluster of returning soldiers.

1954. The chair, beside a birthday table, close enough that its shadow fell across the cake.

1963. Closer.

1971. Closer still — Theo could see the grain of the wood now, the pale patch on the armrest, a scratch low on the front leg.

1987. The year the room was sealed. The chair stood directly behind the family, close enough that its backrest rose above their shoulders, close enough that if any one of them had leaned back—

Theo's tea went cold in its mug. He didn't notice.

* * *

He was still sitting there at nine, surrounded by ninety years of Hartwells and one persistent chair, when the Landau pulled into the yard and Priya got out.

She was Marjorie's daughter's daughter — cousin once removed, or twice; Theo had never got the arithmetic straight, and Priya teased him for it. Twenty-six, a postgraduate in archival studies at the university, home for the summer to help clear the estate, and the only member of the family who had reacted to the news of the handprints with *fascination* rather than the sign of the cross.

She came in carrying a document tube and a bag of pasties, and stopped dead in the doorway.

"Blimey," she said. "You've been busy."

"Priya." Theo's voice came out rusted. He cleared his throat. "I need you to look at something. And I need you to tell me I'm wrong."

"That's a hell of a briefing." She set down the tube, dropped into the chair opposite — and then paused, hand on its back, and looked at the table again, and Theo watched her count the photographs the way he had counted them. "Eleven photos," she said slowly. "And in every single one—"

"There's a chair."

"—there's a chair." She pulled out the chair — the real one, the kitchen one — and sat, and leaned over the spread, and for a long moment said nothing at all. Theo watched her eyes move. Left to right. 1912 to 1987. Left to right again.

"Same chair," she said at last. It wasn't a question.

"The armrest patch. The scratch on the leg. Look at 1954 and 1971 side by side." He slid them together. "Same wear. Same damage. Fifty-seven years apart, and the same scratch."

"Wooden chairs last," Priya said, but her voice had the careful flatness of someone building a wall out of words and knowing it won't hold. "Heirloom pieces get passed down. It could have been kept in a barn and brought out for—"

"Priya. It's in *every* photo. Weddings, christenings, harvests, funerals — look, here, 1968, Great-Uncle Albert's funeral, they're all in black at the churchyard gate and there's the *chair*, standing on the grass like it came for the service." He tapped the photograph. "Nobody brings a chair to a funeral. Nobody brings a chair to *every photograph for seventy-five years*. And nobody — *nobody* — leaves it empty in every frame. Not one person sitting in it. Not one person even touching it. Look how they stand around it. Look at the gaps."

Priya looked. Theo watched her look, and saw the moment it landed, because she went pale in a way that no argument could have produced.

The gaps. In every photograph, the Hartwells arranged themselves with a small, instinctive clearance around the chair, the way people stand near a sleeping dog. No one's hand on its back. No one's foot overlapping its shadow. Ninety years of family members, dead and scattered and forgotten, and every single one of them had known — in the wordless way families know things — not to touch it.

"They knew," Priya whispered. "Whoever these people were, they *knew*. It's not clutter to them. It's—" She stopped.

"It's in the will," Theo said. "I went through the estate papers. Edmund Hartwell, 1934. There's a line about household goods, and one item listed separately. *'The chair, which is not to be moved, sat in, sold, or burned, but left always where it stands.'* No bequest. No explanation. Just — leave it alone."

"Where it *stands*," Priya repeated. "Not where it *is*." She looked up at him. "Theo. Where does it stand *now*?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. That was the question that had been sitting in Theo's chest since six in the morning, heavier than the photographs, heavier than the handprints on the pantry ceiling. Because he had walked this house his whole life. He had played in these rooms as a boy, crawled under these floors as an apprentice, and he had never — not once — seen a ladder-backed chair with a pale patch on its left armrest. Not in the kitchen, not in the parlour, not in the barn, not in the attic where Marjorie's sealed room waited behind its painted-over door.

The chair was in every photograph the family had ever taken.

And it was in no room of the house at all.

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the work was the only thing holding either of them up.

Priya was methodical in a way Theo recognized and trusted — the same discipline, different instrument. She photographed each picture with her phone, cropped, enlarged, compared. She built a timeline on the back of an old pest-control invoice, dates and locations in her small looping hand beside his square print.

"Look at the *distance*," she said, around noon, and her voice had changed.

Theo came around the table. She had the 1912 and the 1987 side by side.

"In 1912, the chair's at the edge of the frame. Half cut off, even. Like it just happened to be there." She moved her finger. "1923, a step closer. 1931, closer. It's never *in* the centre — it's always at the edge of the group, always at the margin, but the margin keeps—" She stopped, and he saw her decide to say it. "The margin keeps shrinking."

Theo looked. She was right. He had felt it all morning without letting the thought form, the way you feel a draught before you admit the window's open. In the early photographs the chair was furniture that had wandered into shot. By the 1950s it was a member of the group. By 1987 it stood directly behind the family, near enough to touch, near enough that its empty seat hovered just behind the shoulders of the youngest child — a girl of six, dark-haired, squinting at the sun.

Marjorie. 1987. The year she sealed the room.

"The chair is getting closer," Theo said. He said it the way he'd say *there's an active wasp nest in the eaves* — flat, procedural, because the alternative to flat and procedural was not saying it at all. "Across seventy-five years. In a series of still photographs. It is getting *closer*."

"Or the camera is," Priya said. Then: "No. No, I checked — the framing changes, the groups change, some are taken from the front of the house and some from the orchard, and the chair is always with the *people*, not the place. It's not standing in a spot they happen to stand near." She looked up at him, and her eyes were bright and frightened and alive in a way that Theo found, absurdly, comforting. "It's standing near *them*. It's following the family. And it's closing the distance."

"Chairs don't walk, Priya."

"I know chairs don't walk." She laughed, one short syllable with no humour in it. "I'm an archivist, Theo. I spend my life with dead people's paper. I believe in provenance and I believe in ink and I believe that most hauntings are damp and most ghosts are draughts. And I am telling you that this chair is closer in every photograph than it was in the one before it, and I have measured it, and I will show you my measurements, and you can check my maths, because I would *love* to be wrong." She pushed the invoice across the table. "Check my maths."

He checked her maths. She was not wrong.

* * *

The afternoon light came slanting through the kitchen window, turning the dust above the table to slow gold, and they kept working because stopping was unthinkable. Priya made tea this time and they both drank it. Somewhere in the walls a pipe knocked twice and they both flinched and pretended not to.

"It's like a long exposure," Priya said quietly. "You know — when you photograph something moving with a slow shutter, and it smears across the frame? A hundred photos of one long movement. We're looking at seventy-five years of a single gesture." She turned the 1987 photograph face-up again. "A chair, walking toward a family. Slowly. So slowly nobody ever—"

"Nobody ever took two photos in the same decade and compared them." Theo rubbed his eyes. "Why would they? You don't notice a thing that moves an inch every ten years. It's like—" He stopped.

"Like watching your nails grow," Priya said.

"Like watching anything you don't want to see."

They sat with that. Outside, a rook called from the orchard, and the sound was enormous in the silence.

"Theo." Priya's voice had dropped. "The last photo is 1987. Marjorie sealed the room in 1987. And there's been no family photograph since. Nobody's taken one. Don't you think that's—" She turned her phone toward him. She'd found it in the second box an hour ago: a snapshot, colour-faded, of the whole family on the front step, 1987, summer. Marjorie, seventeen, arms folded, wearing the expression of someone who has recently seen something and told no one. And behind her, close enough that its backrest nearly brushed her hair—

"Where is it now?" Theo said. "That's the question you asked. Where does it stand now. Because it's not in any room I've ever been in, and I've been in all of them, and—" He stopped again. Because his eye had gone, without his permission, to the far end of the kitchen. To the door. The sealed door at the end of the hall, the one Marjorie had stood in front of two days ago with her hand flat against it like a woman holding back a tide, the one he was contractually, professionally, *ancestrally* forbidden to open.

And he thought: *the pantry prints stop at that door. They climb the wall like a ladder and they stop, exactly, at the sealed door. Like something reaching it. Like something standing at it.*

Like something waiting to be let back in to where it used to sit.

"The chair's been walking toward the house for a century," he said. "And the house locked a door on it in 1987, and now there are handprints on the pantry ceiling like rungs of a ladder, and they stop at that door." He looked at Priya. "It's not trying to get out, Priya. It never was."

Priya had gone very still.

"It's been trying to get back *in*."

The rook called again. Neither of them moved. On the table between them, the 1987 photograph lay face-up in the slanting light, and the empty chair stood just behind seventeen-year-old Marjorie's shoulder, close enough to touch her hair, patient as furniture, patient as a debt, closer than it had ever been in ninety years of Hartwell photographs — and still closing.

Theo's phone rang. The screen said *AUNT MARJORIE*, and for no reason he could have written on any report, in any box, on any form in the whole sane paperwork world, his hands went cold before he answered it.

"Theo," she said, the moment he picked up. Her voice was strange — tight and low, the voice of someone who has been driving and turned around twice. "Have you been going through the photographs?"

Theo looked at Priya. Priya looked at Theo.

"Aunt Marjorie," he said carefully, "we need to talk. Not on the phone. There are things in these pictures that—"

"I *know* what's in the pictures." For the first time in his life, he heard his aunt sound old. "That's why I put them in the box, Theo. That's why I wrote *keep* on it and put it in the back of the attic and never opened it again for thirty-seven years." A breath, unsteady. "You need to stop looking at them. And you need to come to the kitchen, both of you, sit down, and let me tell you about the well."

Theo's mouth went dry. "The—"

"The well under the house," Marjorie said. "And what my grandmother bargained with it. And why the chair is in every photograph." Another breath. "And why, Theo — God help us — it's so much closer now than it was in mine."
