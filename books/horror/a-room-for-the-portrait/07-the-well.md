## 7. The Well

The hatch had been under the stairs for as long as anyone could remember, which was exactly the kind of thing Theo Hartwell had spent thirty-eight years not thinking about.

He'd grown up in this house — summers, at least, until the family stopped coming — and the hatch had been a fixture of his childhood the way the draughty windows and the smell of coal dust in the pantry were fixtures: permanent, unremarkable, never opened. His grandmother had kept a cedar chest against it for decades, heavy enough that moving it would have required a decision, and no one in the Hartwell family made decisions about that corner of the house. It simply wasn't discussed, the way Great-Grandmother Agnes's portrait wasn't discussed, the way the sealed room upstairs wasn't discussed. The Hartwells were champions of the undiscussed. They'd turned avoidance into an inheritance more reliable than the property itself.

Now the cedar chest sat shoved against the far wall, dragged there — judging by the furrows ploughed through forty years of dust — sometime in the last two days. Theo crouched in front of the hatch with his headlamp on and his crowbar across his knees and told himself, in the flat professional voice he used for wasp nests and dead rats under floorboards, that he was only doing what any pest controller would do. Something was making noise in the walls. Noise meant access points. Access points meant cavities. Cavities meant you checked every one of them, even the ones nobody wanted checked.

*Especially* the ones nobody wanted checked. That was where things nested.

The hatch was oak, black with age, set into the flagstone floor like a lid on something being kept down rather than something being stored. There was no handle. There hadn't needed to be; whoever had built it had fitted an iron ring flush to the wood, and someone after them had filled the ring's mounting holes with lead, poured in and smoothed over, so that the ring itself remained but was now purely decorative — a handle that could not be gripped without first digging out the metal around it.

Theo looked at that lead for a long moment.

Somebody had sealed this deliberately. Not casually, not as a matter of tidiness, but with intent — molten lead poured by lamplight into screw-holes, smoothed with a thumb before it set. That was work done in fear or in certainty, and either way it was work done by a Hartwell, because this was a Hartwell house and no stranger had ever been invited past its gate.

"Right," he said aloud, because saying things aloud helped, and because the silence of the house had developed a quality lately that made him want to fill it. "Right."

He dug the lead out with the claw of his hammer. It came away in grey curls, soft as wax, and beneath it the iron ring was bright — unnaturally bright, unsullied by a century of rust, as if the air below the hatch had been keeping it clean. Or as if hands had been polishing it.

He didn't let himself finish that thought. He hooked the crowbar through the ring, braced his boots against the flagstones, and pulled.

The hatch didn't fight him. That was somehow worse. It lifted smoothly, silently, on hinges that should have been seized solid, exhaling a breath of air from below that smelled of nothing at all — not damp, not rot, not stone-dust, just a clean absence of smell, like the inside of a jar. The headlamp beam went down into darkness and found stone steps, narrow, spiralling, worn concave in their centres by generations of feet.

Feet. Plural. Generations.

Theo sat back on his heels and took out his phone and checked, pointlessly, that he had signal. Two bars. He texted Priya: *found a cellar under the stairs. going down.* She replied inside a minute — she'd been at the parish office all morning, pulling records — with a thumbs-up and then, thirty seconds later: *text me when you're back up. not if. when.*

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he clipped his headlamp tight, took the crowbar, and went down.

* * *

The steps turned twice and delivered him into a round chamber maybe twelve feet across, its ceiling low enough that he had to stoop. The walls were coursed fieldstone, dry as bone. And in the centre of the floor, where any sane builder would have put nothing at all, was the well.

It was a proper old thing — a low circular wall of fitted stone, waist-high, its rim worn smooth and glossy in places, rough in others. No cover. No grate. A mouth, open and waiting, four feet across.

His headlamp threw the beam down into it and found — nothing. Not darkness exactly, but a depth beyond the light's authority, a blackness that swallowed the beam a few feet down and gave back nothing. He picked up a pebble from the floor, weighed it, and dropped it in.

No splash. No click of stone on stone. Nothing.

He waited ten seconds. Fifteen. The pebble did not arrive anywhere.

"Okay," Theo said quietly. "Okay."

Because here was the thing — here was the thing his rational mind grabbed hold of like a man grabbing a rail in rough water: wells had bottoms. Wells were holes in the ground, and holes ended. If a pebble dropped into a shaft made no sound, the shaft was either bottomless or muffled or — his mind offered the options with the calm of a checklist — or something was wrong with the acoustics, or the well was deeper than any well had a right to be under a farmhouse cellar, or—

Or the well was full of something that didn't echo.

He walked the chamber's circumference instead, grateful for the task, running his gloved hand along the stonework the way he'd run it along a hundred cellar walls looking for entry points. The stones were sound. Dry. No mortar crumble, no rodent runs, no insect frass, no droppings of anything — and that itself was wrong, he realised, wrong in a way his professional brain registered before his conscious mind did. Cellars had life in them. Spiders at minimum. Beetles. The small commerce of dark places. This room was sterile as a showroom.

Then his hand crossed a seam in the stone and caught on something sharp, and he stopped, and raised the lamp, and stopped breathing.

Tally marks.

They covered the inner face of the well wall — the wall you'd see only if you leaned over the rim and looked down into the shaft itself. Groups of five, four strokes and a diagonal slash across them, scratched into the stone in rows, column after column, band after band, rising from the visible darkness up to within inches of the rim. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Some were deep and confident, cut by something hard. Some were shallow, wavering, ragged-edged —

— ragged-edged in a way he recognised, because he'd spent two days photographing handprints on a pantry ceiling, and he knew what human nails did to soft surfaces when the hand using them had no tool and no choice.

Theo leaned over the rim, slowly, the way you lean over a bridge railing above a drop, and put the light directly onto the marks.

Up close they were worse. They weren't uniform. They weren't one campaign of counting; they were dozens, hundreds, layered over one another, some scored through older ones, some pressed into grooves already there. And they climbed. Below the reach of his light the marks continued down into the black, and he understood with a lurch that whoever — whatever — had made them had made them *descending*, scratching tallies into the shaft wall on the way down, and that the deepest ones he could see were still fresh enough to show pale stone dust in their cuts.

Fresh. As in recent. As in — he made himself think it — as in since Tuesday.

He straightened up too fast and cracked his head on the low ceiling, and the pain was almost a relief, something physical and explicable, something he could file. He stood rubbing his scalp and ran the checklist, because the checklist was all he had:

Rodents don't count. Insects don't count. Water damage doesn't produce tally marks. Settling doesn't produce tally marks. Hoaxers do, sometimes — but the hatch had been lead-sealed under a chest no one had moved in living memory, and the dust on that chest had lain undisturbed for decades, and he had personally photographed it before Marjorie's arrival, and there had been no footprints in that dust except his own from yesterday, and—

And the tally marks at the bottom of the shaft were newer than the ones at the top.

Something was counting down there. Now. Recently. Repeatedly.

His phone buzzed in his pocket and he flinched hard enough to nearly drop the crowbar. Priya: *you ok?*

*Fine,* he typed. *Found a well. Dry. Old. Lots of tally marks on the inside.*

Three dots appeared, vanished, appeared. *tally marks like counting?*

*Like counting.*

*Counting what?*

He looked at the rim, at the smooth-worn lip of stone, at the places where the gloss of wear was deepest — and saw, finally, what he'd been refusing to see since he'd come down the stairs. The wear on the rim wasn't random. It was two patches, opposite each other, polished bright as river pebbles. Places where hands had gripped, over and over, for years, for generations. Places where someone had leaned over this mouth and looked down.

Or places where someone had been held over it.

*I don't know yet,* he sent. And then, because lying to Priya felt worse than the dark did: *I think I'm going to look.*

Don't, she wrote. Then: wait. Then: give me 20 mins im getting in the car.

Twenty minutes. He could wait twenty minutes. He told himself he would wait twenty minutes, and he meant it, and then he made the mistake of turning back toward the stairs, because in turning he caught the edge of the well in his peripheral vision and the blackness of it seemed to have changed — seemed closer, though the rim hadn't moved, seemed to have *risen*, though that was impossible, though stone does not rise, though—

There was a smell now. Faint. Under the clean emptiness. Wet stone, and under the wet stone something organic and cold, like the air off a lake at night, like the breath of a place that holds water and everything water has taken.

The well was not dry. He understood that suddenly, wholly, the way you understand a fall has begun only after it starts. The well was not dry; the well was *waiting*. It had been emptied, the way a glass is emptied, the way a ledger is cleared — and it stood there open-mouthed in the dark under the stairs of his family's house, patient as arithmetic, waiting to be refilled.

And the tally marks on its throat were the count of what it expected.

Theo stood very still. Somewhere above him, faintly, through the floorboards, he heard the house settle — or shift, or breathe — and he thought about the pantry ceiling, the greasy rungs of handprints climbing toward the sealed door, and he thought: *they weren't climbing up to get out.*

*They were climbing down to count.*

"No," he said aloud, and his voice sounded thin and young in the round stone room. "No, I'm not doing this. I'm not becoming one of those people. There is a physical explanation. There is always a physical explanation."

There was a physical explanation for the pebble. There was a physical explanation for the tally marks. There was a physical explanation for the chair.

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, and he had no idea where it came from, and that frightened him more than anything else in the last three days — because he hadn't thought the word *chair*. He hadn't seen a chair. But the knowledge was suddenly in his head, cool and certain, the way you know the next note of a song you've never heard: *there is a chair at the bottom of the well.*

He looked over the rim.

He didn't decide to. Later he would examine that moment again and again — lying awake, staring at ceilings, in the weeks when he still slept — and he would never find the decision in it. His body simply leaned, the way iron leans to a magnet, and the headlamp beam swung down into the shaft, and the blackness parted for it this time, generously, like a curtain drawn back for a guest.

The shaft went down perhaps twenty feet. Its walls were the tally-marked stone, the scratches continuing into the depths until the light lost them. And at the bottom, sitting on bare earth that should have held a century of fallen debris and had none — not a leaf, not a pebble, not the one he'd dropped — was a wooden kitchen chair.

Old. Straight-backed. The plain pine kind that farmhouses bought by the dozen and wore out by the pair. Its seat was worn glossy in the middle. Its arms — it had arms, oddly, wide flat arms — were worn glossy too, in two long patches, as if hands had rested there often, or as if wrists had lain across them often, and Theo's mind supplied the second image whether he wanted it or not.

The chair faced the ladder of light. Faced upward. Faced him.

It was empty.

It was empty, and it was the most occupied object Theo Hartwell had ever seen in his life. Emptiness poured off it. It sat at the bottom of a hole in the earth beneath his family's house, twenty feet below a floor his grandmother had swept, and it waited with the specific, dreadful patience of a seat kept for someone who is late — and every tally mark on the walls around it, thousands upon thousands of them, scratched by fingernails in the dark, said the same thing in the same dumb language:

*Not yet. Not yet. Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.*

Theo's phone buzzed against his hip. He didn't look at it. He couldn't have read it; language had temporarily left him. He stood gripping the rim of the well with both hands — standing, he realised dimly, exactly where the stone was worn smoothest, his hands fitting the polish as if the polish had been measured for him — and he watched the chair at the bottom of the well, and the chair did not move, and the dark around it did not move, and nothing happened at all, and it was the worst moment of his life.

Then, from the bottom of the shaft, from the earth itself, from everywhere and nowhere, came a sound.

Not a voice. Not yet. A sound like a page turning. Like a ledger opening. Like a great dry book being thumbed to a marked place, unhurried, somewhere below the chair, below the earth, below everything — and then a pause, a listening pause, the pause of something checking a name against a list.

The headlamp flickered once.

Theo took his hands off the rim. He walked backward to the stairs without turning around, because some animal part of him understood that turning around was a permission of some kind, and he climbed the spiral steps with the crowbar gripped in both fists like a rosary, and he did not stop until he was in the kitchen with the hatch shut and the cedar chest hauled back over it and his own weight planted on top of the chest, shaking, texting Priya with thumbs that wouldn't behave:

*get here fast. bring everything you've got on agnes. dates, records, all of it.*

Her reply came in seconds: *why? what did you find?*

He stared at the message for a long time. Above him, through the ceiling, faint and patient, the house ticked and settled around its secret.

*there's a chair,* he typed. *and i think it's mine.*
