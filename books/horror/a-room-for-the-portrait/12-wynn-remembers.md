## 12. Wynn Remembers

Wynn Hartwell had spent the better part of ninety-one years learning how to let go of things, and by now he had let go of nearly all of them. Names went first — not his own, that one clung like a burr, but other people's, the names of postmen and vicars and the man who used to deliver coal. Then the years went, sliding off him like water off waxed canvas, so that 1962 and 1994 sat side by side in his memory with no wall between them, and he could walk from one into the other without noticing he'd crossed a threshold.

But some things did not let go of *him*.

He woke in the back bedroom of the farmhouse — the room he'd been born in, though for three days he had believed it was a hospital ward, and before that a railway carriage, and before that the long grass behind the church where he'd hidden from his father's belt at seven years old — and he knew, with the terrible suddenness of a lamp being lit, exactly where he was and exactly what year it was and exactly what was about to happen.

The clarity came like that sometimes. A pane of clean glass in a filthy window. And Wynn had learned, over these last foggy years, that when the glass cleared you looked through it fast, because it would not stay clear long.

He lay still under the eiderdown and listened to the house.

It was counting.

Not aloud — not yet — but underneath everything, under the tick of the cooling radiators and the wind worrying the loose slate above his window, there was a rhythm, patient as a pulse: *one, two, three, four.* He could feel it in the bed frame. He could feel it in his own chest, keeping time beside his heart like an unwelcome guest walking in step.

*Four,* said the house. *Four Hartwells under my roof.*

And somewhere below, faint but unmistakable, the sound of a fingernail — or something very like a fingernail — scoring stone.

Wynn closed his eyes. "Oh, Agnes," he said softly, to the ceiling, to the walls, to the well beneath them all. "You're at it again."

* * *

They had told him, before he came — Marjorie had driven down from Carlisle to collect him from the home, and she'd been careful with her words the way their family was always careful, wrapping the truth in tea towels of euphemism. *We need everyone together, Uncle Wynn. There are things happening at the farm. Theo's found marks on the walls. You'll be comfortable, we'll look after you.*

She hadn't said the word *counting*. She hadn't needed to. The word had been sitting in Wynn's head for eighty years, waiting, like a coat hung on a hook for someone who never came home.

He got up slowly, one hand on the iron bedstead, and dressed himself properly — collar buttoned, cardigan straightened, shoes polished with the edge of his trouser leg because he couldn't find the tin. If the house was going to count him, it could count a Hartwell who looked respectable. His mother had raised him that way, insofar as his mother had raised him at all.

His mother.

The thought of her opened the glass pane wider, and Wynn stepped through it, and suddenly he was standing in 1938, and he was eleven years old, and the kitchen smelled of boiled mutton and coal smoke, and his mother was feeding a photograph into the grate.

* * *

Her name had been Edith. Edith Hartwell, née Sowerby, a thin woman with hands like winter branches and a way of looking at doors as though they might say something. Wynn remembered her mostly in fragments now — the smell of lavender water, the hymn she hummed while wringing sheets — but that evening in November 1938 he remembered whole, every second of it, because it was the only time in his childhood he had ever seen his mother afraid.

There had been a photograph of her taken at the chapel anniversary, her and nine other women arranged on the steps with their hats on. Someone — Aunt Ruthie, probably, who was forever arranging people into rows — had given her a copy, framed, and it had stood on the mantelpiece between the clock and the china dog for two years.

On that November evening, Wynn had come downstairs for a drink of water and found his mother standing before the mantel with the photograph in both hands, staring at it.

"Mam?"

"Go back up, love."

"What's wrong with—"

"*Go back up.*"

But he hadn't gone back up. He was eleven, and stubborn, and he stood in the doorway and watched his mother turn the photograph toward the firelight, and he saw — even then, even at eleven — what she was looking at.

Behind her, in the photograph, at the end of the row of chapel women, there was a chair.

A wooden chair, plain-backed, standing on the chapel steps where no chair should be, empty, angled slightly toward the camera. Toward her. And it was closer than anything else in the picture — closer than the women, closer than the steps' edge — as though it had walked forward out of the crowd to stand at her shoulder.

"It weren't there," his mother said, not turning round. Her voice was flat and quiet, the voice she used for telling him his father wasn't coming home again. "I've looked at this picture a hundred times, Wynn. It weren't there."

"Mam, maybe somebody moved—"

"There's nobody *on* the steps but us ten." She turned then, and her face in the firelight was grey as porridge. "And it's nearer than it was in your gran's wedding picture. I checked. I've had them all out on the table since Tuesday. It's nearer in every one."

Wynn had not understood. He was eleven; he understood fires and belts and hunger and the names of cows. But he understood fear, and his mother's fear came off her like heat off a stove.

Then she pulled the photograph from its frame, held it over the grate, and burned it.

He remembered the details with horrible precision: how the corner caught and curled brown before it blackened; how his mother held on until the flame licked her fingers, because she wouldn't put it down half-burned, wouldn't leave any piece of herself intact enough to count; how she stirred the ashes with the poker and ground them small; how she washed her hands afterward, twice, up to the elbows, at the sink, saying nothing.

"Why did you do that, Mam?"

She dried her hands on her apron and looked at him for a long moment, and whatever she decided in that moment — whether to tell him or keep the burden another forty years — was settled by the sound that came then, faintly, from under the floor. From the cellar. From further down than the cellar.

A scratching. Rhythmic. Patient.

His mother went white. She crossed the kitchen, took Wynn by the shoulders, and knelt so their faces were level, and she spoke fast and low, the words tumbling out like coins from a torn pocket:

"The well counts us, Wynn. It counts the blood. Great-Grandmother Agnes made a bargain down there before you were born and the bargain isn't finished, and when the well wants filling it takes a tally of everyone left alive with Hartwell blood, and the one it counts twice has to sit. Has to sit in her chair, in her picture, until the next filling. Your auntie Nora counted twice in 'twenty-nine. She sits now. Nobody says her name at Christmas and you'll know why." Her grip tightened, fingers biting through his shirt. "So you never let it count you twice. Do you hear me? Once is living. Twice is hers. If you see the chair come close in a picture — if you hear the scratching — you burn the picture, you get out of this house, and you don't come back till the well's full and sleeping. Promise me."

"I promise," Wynn had said, eleven years old, promising anything to make her face less grey.

"And if I'm ever counted—" Her voice cracked, just once, the only crack in all those years. "If I'm ever counted twice, Wynn, you won't look for me. Whatever you hear in the walls calling my name, you won't answer it. Swear it."

He swore. Children swear easily. It is one of the cruelties built into them.

Eight months later his mother was dead — drowned, said the coroner, in the well, though the well had been dry as a sermon for thirty years, and though a woman of fifty does not climb down a shaft in her nightdress, and though her body was found seated at the bottom, upright, composed, hands folded in her lap, as though she had been waiting there a long while for someone to find her.

Wynn had not looked for her. He had kept that promise. It was the only one of the two he'd managed to keep.

* * *

The glass pane began to fog at the edges. Wynn felt it going — the year 1938 softening, the kitchen dissolving, his mother's face blurring into the general grey sea. He gripped the bedstead.

"No," he said aloud, to his own mind. "Not yet. I'm not finished."

Because that was the thing about the clarity: you could fight for it. The doctors had never believed that — they thought confusion was weather, something that happened *to* you — but Wynn knew better. The fog was a tide, and tides could be argued with, at least for a little while, at least by a man who had spent his whole life holding on to things.

He stood at the window. Below, in the yard, he could see Marjorie's car and Theo's van, and Theo himself crossing the gravel with a clipboard under his arm, moving like a man doing sums he didn't like the answers to. Good lad. Third generation of them, and the same walk — the Hartwell walk, chin down, shoulders set against the world's nonsense. Wynn's father had walked like that. It had never once saved any of them.

Down the hall, he could hear voices: Marjorie, low and urgent; Priya, the cousin-by-marriage girl, quick and bright, asking questions in the way young people asked questions, as though answers were a right rather than a mercy. They were working it out, the pair of them. Working it out eighty years too late, but late was better than never.

They didn't know the deadline. That was the thing. He could hear it in their voices — urgency, yes, dread, yes, but no *clock*. They thought they had time. They were measuring the danger in days and weeks, the way you measure a leaky roof.

Wynn knew better. The well kept a calendar older than any of them, and it kept it faithfully, and it filled at the turning of the year — at midwinter, at the solstice, when the dark was deepest and the door between the living and the counted stood widest. Agnes's bargain had been struck at midwinter. Nora had been taken at midwinter, 1929. His mother had gone into the ground — or out of it — in June, but that had been a surrender, not a taking; the well hadn't wanted her yet, and she had known it, and the knowing had been the worst part, the waiting part, the part that wore her down to grey porridge.

Midwinter. Three weeks away. Perhaps less.

And the portrait must have its sitter by then. That was the arithmetic his mother had taught him in one burning November minute: the well refills, and the refill must be paid, and the payment is a body in the chair — a Hartwell, counted twice, painted in, kept. If no sitter came willingly, the well would take its tithe the other way. All of the bloodline. Every last one. His mother had said that too, in the end, near the end, when the fog had already started gathering around her edges and she spoke in lists: *the well or the wall, the chair or the grave, one or all, Wynn, one or all.*

He pushed open his bedroom door and went out into the corridor, one hand trailing the wallpaper — the paper his mother had chosen, roses on cream, faded now to ghosts of roses — and made his slow way toward the voices.

* * *

They were in the front room. Marjorie stood by the fireplace with her arms folded, wearing the face she wore at funerals, and Priya sat cross-legged on the floor surrounded by photographs laid out like a hand of cards, and the sight of them — the empty wooden chair recurring across nine decades, closer each time, marching patiently toward the camera — made something lurch in Wynn's chest that was half grief and half relief. So they'd seen it. Thank God. Thank God they'd seen it. Half the horror of his long life had been seeing it alone.

"You shouldn't have them spread out like that," he said, from the doorway.

Priya startled. Marjorie turned. "Uncle Wynn — you should be resting—"

"They can see you." He came into the room, slowly, lowering himself into the armchair by the window with the care of a man descending a ladder. "The chair. It knows when it's being looked at. My mother worked that out. She never let a photograph of herself stay in this house more than a week. Burned every one. Said a picture was a door and the chair came through doors." He nodded at the spread on the floor. "You've laid out a dozen doors."

Priya gathered the photographs quickly, stacking them face-down, and Wynn noted with distant approval that she did it without asking why. Sharp girl. The family had married well, once, in that direction.

"Wynn." Marjorie crouched by his chair, and her funeral face crumpled into something younger and more frightened. "You're lucid. Oh, thank God. How long do we have you?"

"As long as I hold on." He patted her hand. Her bones felt small under the skin. When had Marjorie got old? She'd been a girl sealing up rooms yesterday. "Listen to me, Marjorie. Both of you. The glass is clear right now and it won't be tomorrow, maybe not for days, so you'll listen while I can speak plain."

"We're listening," Priya said.

"The well fills at midwinter." He said it plainly, the way you'd say *the milk comes Tuesdays*. "The solstice. Longest night. That's when the bargain comes due — that's when it counted Nora, that's when it tried for me in 'seventy-nine, though your grandmother burned my picture in time, God rest her, she learned from Mam. Three weeks. Less. And by that night the portrait must have its sitter, or it doesn't take one." He paused, and made himself say the rest, the hard rest, the list. "It takes all. One or all. That's the terms. Agnes signed them and we've been paying the interest ever since."

Silence in the room. The fire ticked. Somewhere far below — so far below it might have been imagination, except that Wynn had stopped believing in that particular imagination decades ago — something scratched.

Marjorie's face had gone the grey of his mother's, all those years ago. "Three weeks," she whispered. "Theo thinks — we thought — months, maybe. Time to research, to find a way—"

"You haven't got months. You've got until the longest night." Wynn leaned forward, and took his great-niece's hand, and fixed her with eyes that were, for this one clear hour, the sharpest things in the house. "And there's one thing more, and you'll not like it, and you'll do it anyway. The house counts who's under its roof. Every Hartwell under this roof is a name on its tongue. So you get them out — Des, Cass, the boy, all of them — you get them over that gate and you keep them out. Because I remember what happens to families that stay past the counting." His voice dropped, and the fog pressed at the edges of the glass, and he hurried, stumbling a little over the words. "I remember the winter of 'twenty-nine. I was two. But they tell it — they told it — how the house got warm. How the doors all stood open. How it smelled of Sunday dinner in every room, and nobody had cooked. It *invites*, Marjorie. It makes itself comfortable. That's how you'll know the counting's begun in earnest — not the scratching, not the names. The welcome."

He sat back, breathing hard. The effort had cost him; he could feel the tide pulling at the glass, 1938 and 2024 beginning to swim together, his mother's kitchen bleeding rose-patterned into the front room. But he had one more thing, the most important thing, and he gripped the arm of the chair and held on.

"One more," he managed. "The portrait. Whatever you do — however bad it gets — nobody looks at it alone. And nobody," his hand shot out and caught Marjorie's wrist, surprising them both with its strength, "nobody opens that sealed room thinking they're the one who can bear it. That's how it chooses. It waits for the one who thinks they deserve it. Forty years you've carried that door on your back, girl. Don't you dare carry yourself through it."

Marjorie stared at him. Something moved behind her face — guilt, old and load-bearing, shifting at last under weight it had carried since 1987.

"How do you know I sealed it?" she said quietly.

"I don't," said Wynn, as the fog rolled in over the glass and his mother's voice began mixing with Marjorie's, November bleeding into December, the well and the wall, the chair or the grave. "But you've got her look. Agnes's look. The look of someone keeping a room shut." His eyes drifted, unfocused now, toward the ceiling, toward the upper floors, toward the sealed door at the end of the landing that he had walked past every day of his childhood without once letting his gaze rest on it. "One or all," he murmured, sinking. "One or all. Mind the clock, Marjorie. Midwinter's coming, and the house has started setting places."

He did not remember saying the last part. But Priya wrote it down, word for word, and later — when the extra place appeared at the dinner table, laid with care, set for Theo — she would show him the page, and Wynn, deep in his fog, would smile at it the way a man smiles at a prophecy fulfilled: sadly, and from a very long way off.
