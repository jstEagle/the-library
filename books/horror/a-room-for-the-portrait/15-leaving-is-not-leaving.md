## 15. Leaving Is Not Leaving

Marjorie Hartwell had packed the car the way her mother had taught her to pack for a funeral: everything essential in one bag, nothing sentimental, and a clean handkerchief folded on top. She had done it in the dark of four a.m., by the light of the kitchen's single working bulb, moving through the house on the balls of her feet like a girl sneaking home past curfew. The others were asleep — Theo on the sofa with his boots still on, Priya curled in the armchair over an open ledger, Cass and Des upstairs in rooms that had been theirs as children and had, apparently, been waiting for them all these years.

She did not tell them she was going. That was the coward's arithmetic, and she knew it, and she did it anyway: if she left first, she could send back help from somewhere the roads worked properly. If she asked permission, someone would talk her out of it, or worse, someone would insist on coming, and then there would be more Hartwells in the car than the house would know what to do with. She had spent forty years learning that the only safe number of Hartwells near this farm was zero.

The keys were in her coat pocket where they always were. Her hand closed around them and the metal was warm, which she told herself was because she'd been holding them since midnight, turning them over like worry-beads, though she hadn't been. She'd put them down on the hall table at eleven. She was almost sure she'd put them down.

Outside, the frost had come early and hard, silvering the yard into something almost beautiful. Almost. The farmhouse stood behind her with its windows dark except one — the sealed room's window, high up under the eaves, where no bulb had burned since 1987 and where, tonight, a faint amber glow pressed against the glass like a thumb against skin.

Marjorie did not look at it. She had gotten very good, over forty years, at not looking.

She got the Rover started on the third try, which was normal; the Rover started on the third try in any weather, any decade, as reliably as a bad knee predicting rain. She let it idle while she buckled her seatbelt, checked the mirrors, adjusted the rearview so that the house slid out of frame entirely. There. Just a road now, and frost, and the grey beginning of dawn pooling in the east like watercolor.

The gate was open. She noted that and filed it away without deciding what it meant. She hadn't opened it. Neither had anyone else, presumably, unless Des had gone out for a smoke, but Des had quit in March and lied about it ever since, and liars don't get up before seven. The gate stood wide against the hedge, and beyond it the lane ran straight and pale toward the crossroads, and beyond the crossroads the world — properly the world, with petrol stations and traffic lights and people who had never heard the word *Hartwell* spoken indoors.

She drove through the gate and felt the house come off her shoulders like a wet coat being lifted away. It lasted perhaps two hundred yards. Then the coat settled back on, heavier.

*You'll be back,* said something that was not quite a voice. It didn't use words, exactly. It used certainty. Marjorie had grown up with that certainty the way other children grew up with church bells — ambient, structural, impossible to argue with. She pressed the accelerator harder and the certainty got quieter, and she took that for victory, because at fifty-eight she still occasionally mistook volume for truth.

The lane unspooled ahead of her. Frost-smoked hedges. A pheasant exploding upward from the verge, absurd and golden in the headlights. The crossroads sign: CHURCHYARD LANE, 1½ MILES. She knew that sign. She'd known it since she was small enough to ride in the footwell of her father's van, watching it swing past on trips to Sunday service. St. Cuthbert's, where every Hartwell for six generations lay under slate stones with their names cut deep enough to survive the weather, which the family had always taken for piety and which Marjorie, lately, had begun to suspect was something more like insurance.

She turned right at the crossroads, away from the churchyard, toward the A-road. Toward the motorway. Toward anywhere.

The lane curved. Lanes curve; that is what lanes do. But this curve had a quality she couldn't name at first, and it took another half mile of driving before she understood what was wrong: the curve bent *back*. Not sharply, not obviously — just a few degrees too far, the way a sentence bends when the speaker has decided where it will end regardless of grammar. The hedges on her right began to repeat themselves. Not identically — she gave herself credit for noticing the differences, a broken post here, a gap where a badger trail crossed — but the differences repeated too. The same broken post. The same badger gap. The same pheasant, or its twin, detonating out of the same stretch of verge.

Marjorie slowed the car and looked at the odometer.

Four miles since the gate. The churchyard was a mile and a half from the farm. Four miles was the distance to St. Cuthbert's and back, if you went down, touched the lychgate the way children dared each other to, and came home again without stopping.

"No," she said aloud, to the car, to the frost, to whatever was listening. "No, you don't."

She turned the Rover around. It was not easy; the lane had narrowed without seeming to narrow, the hedges leaning in like interested bystanders, and the three-point turn required five points and then seven. When she finally faced the way she'd come, the crossroads sign was visible ahead of her: CHURCHYARD LANE, 1½ MILES.

She had not passed it. She had driven four miles and not passed it.

Her hands were doing something independent of her now, gripping the wheel, knuckles pale. She drove toward the sign. It approached at the correct speed, swung past on her left, and she turned left this time, hard, onto the A-road, and the A-road was there, blessedly, gloriously there — wide tarmac, white lines, a lorry rumbling past in the opposite direction trailing diesel breath. Relief hit her like warm water. She laughed, one short bark of it, embarrassing even alone.

Then the lorry's taillights receded, and kept receding, and did not diminish, and after a full minute of watching them she understood that they were not getting further away because they were not actually moving away at all. They were fixed. A picture of taillights, painted at exactly the depth of ordinary perspective, holding perfectly still while her speedometer climbed from forty to sixty.

She pulled over. Put the handbrake on. Sat.

The engine ticked. Somewhere a bird made a sound that was almost, but not quite, a song thrush — the rhythm right, the notes wrong, a thrush rendered by someone who had only ever had it described to them.

"Right," Marjorie said. Her voice sounded very loud and very human and she clung to it. "Right. Think."

Thinking was her gift, when she could get it to work. She had been the one who kept the family accounts, who caught the solicitor's error in Uncle Albert's estate, who had once, at fourteen, proved to the entire parish council that the tithe map was mislaid by forty feet. Numbers didn't lie. Distances didn't lie. Whatever this was, it operated through distances, and things that operate through rules can be measured, and things that can be measured can be beaten.

She reset the trip meter to zero. Drove on.

The A-road carried her for six miles of perfect normality — junctions, a roundabout, a Little Chef whose lights glowed with the flat cheerfulness of genuine commerce. She nearly wept at the Little Chef. Then the road dipped between two hills, and came up out of the dip, and the sign at the crest read: HARTWELL FARM LANE, ¼ MILE.

The trip meter read eight miles. Round trip to the churchyard and back: eight miles exactly. She had never measured it before in her life. She would have sworn on any Bible you liked that the churchyard was closer than that, four miles at most. But the odometer disagreed, and the odometer had apparently been keeping its own accounts for some time.

She tried the motorway next. She drove forty minutes west with the radio on, loud, tuned to a phone-in program about parking disputes, filling the car with other people's petty voices until the cab felt inhabited by the ordinary world. When the motorway signs for Birmingham appeared she took the exit, followed the ring road, watched the city glow ahead of her like a promise — and came around a bend beneath a railway bridge and out the other side onto a single-track lane with frost on the hedges and a sign that read CHURCHYARD LANE, 1½ MILES.

The radio was still playing the phone-in. The same caller. The same complaint about the same disabled bay outside the same chemist. She switched it off and the silence came in like floodwater.

She tried walking.

It seemed important, afterward, that she had tried. She parked the Rover at the crossroads — the real crossroads, or the version of it currently being offered — and set off on foot down Churchyard Lane itself, on the theory that the thing was bound to the vehicle somehow, to engines, to the idea of leaving at speed. Walking was slower. Walking was humbler. Perhaps the house's attention would slide off something so small.

The lane was beautiful in the pre-dawn. Frost furred every twig. Her breath hung in ribbons. And the walk was fine, genuinely fine, for a mile and a half, until the lychgate of St. Cuthbert's rose ahead of her, black oak and lichen, exactly as she remembered it from a hundred childhood Sundays — and she stopped, because she had not walked a mile and a half. She had walked perhaps four hundred yards. The churchyard had come to meet her.

Through the lychgate she could see the stones, ranks of them, silvered with frost. And nearest the gate, one plot stood different from the rest: freshly turned earth, dark and soft, with no stone yet, tended recently — she could see the marks of fingers in the soil, small prints, greasy-dark, pressed into the frozen ground like seals in wax.

Marjorie did not go in. Something in her, some animal ledger older than thought, advised against it with total clarity. She stood at the lychgate and looked at the turned earth and understood, in the way one understands things at five in the morning after a night without sleep, that the earth was waiting for someone, and that the grave had been dug to a family measurement.

When she turned around, the Rover was gone.

Not stolen — *gone*, along with the crossroads, the hedges, the lane itself. She stood instead at the bottom of the farm track, the gravel under her sensible shoes, the farmhouse rising ahead of her against the lightening sky, its front door standing open with the warmth of the hall spilling out yellow across the frost. From inside came the smell of frying bacon and toast, and the murmur of voices — Theo's low baritone, Priya's quick questions, Cass laughing at something. Breakfast sounds. Homecoming sounds.

The door had been locked behind her. She had locked it herself; she always locked it; locking doors against that house was a ritual she'd performed since childhood, half superstition, half spite. Now it stood open, patient, hospitable.

*Half-measures,* said the certainty, gently, almost kindly. *You cannot leave by half-measures. Either everyone goes, or no one does.*

And beneath the kindness, unmistakable now, the other thing — the thing she'd heard once before, in 1987, as a girl of eighteen with a torch in her hand and a door handle under her palm: the sound of a count being kept. Not names yet. Not yet. Just the sense of a tally, patient as water finding its level, noting that ten Hartwells had slept under this roof last night and only nine remained in its beds this morning, and that the tenth was standing on the gravel having failed, twice, to be elsewhere.

Marjorie walked up the track. What else was there? The frost crunched under her like old bone. At the threshold she paused, one hand on the doorframe, and looked back down the lane — the true lane, the ordinary lane, running straight and innocent toward the crossroads and the world.

A car was coming along it. Small, dark, moving fast, kicking up frost-smoke. As it drew nearer she saw the driver: Des. Her brother Des, who had fled to the attic in shame two nights ago and slipped out before dawn yesterday, who had sworn he'd rather die than spend another night under that roof — his face set forward, jaw tight, hands strangling the wheel, driving *toward* the house with the helpless velocity of a man returning to a fire he'd sworn he'd escaped.

The Rover's odometer, when she checked it later, parked in the yard where it had apparently been all along, read eight miles.

Round trip to the churchyard and back.

Des killed the engine, got out, and held up a plastic document wallet like a man presenting credentials at a border. His hands were shaking.

"Marge," he said. "I found something. About Agnes. You need to — we need to—" He stopped, swallowed, looked back the way he'd come, at the empty lane, and Marjorie watched him realize, with dawning horror, that he could not have said how he'd arrived, or why, or what had turned him around on whatever road he'd been fleeing down. "I was driving away," he said quietly. "I want that on the record. I was driving *away*."

"I know," Marjorie said. "So was I."

Above them, faint in the grey morning, the window of the sealed room glowed amber, and inside the house, down in the cellar, in the dry dark of the well, something scratched a fresh mark into the stone — unhurried, precise, keeping count.
