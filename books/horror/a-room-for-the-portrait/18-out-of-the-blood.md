## 18. Out of the Blood

Cass watched her boyfriend through the kitchen window the way you watch someone defuse a bomb — except the bomb was a farm gate, and the bomb was her family, and the bomb was the slow, patient arithmetic of a house that had decided what it was owed.

His name was Joel. He'd arrived two days ago with a duffel bag and a grin, expecting a rustic weekend at his girlfriend's family's ancestral pile, and instead had walked into a household where the walls sweated in cold rooms, where an old man spoke to a portrait like an old friend, and where Cass's uncle Des had a bedroom door that nobody opened anymore. He'd taken it better than most. He'd taken it better than Cass, honestly, who had spent the first night of the solstice countdown crying in the bathroom and listening to the taps run by themselves, gently, like something washing its hands.

"It's a theory," Priya had said that morning, spreading her notes across the kitchen table with the careful, apologetic precision of someone laying out a surgical plan. "The boyfriend test. He's not a Hartwell. Not by blood, not by name, not by anything. If the house counts blood — and everything in the journal says it does — then the gate should open for him the same way it opens for the postman."

"And if it doesn't?" Cass had asked.

Priya hadn't answered. She'd just looked at the window, at the grey December light lying flat on the yard, and Cass had understood that nobody wanted to say it out loud: if the gate didn't open for Joel, then the house had already counted him too, and the counting was bigger than any of them had guessed.

Now Joel stood at the gate with his keys in his hand and his breath steaming, and he turned back and gave Cass a thumbs-up, because Joel's entire emotional register was some variation of a thumbs-up, and that was one of the ten thousand reasons she loved him.

"Whenever you're ready," Theo called from the porch, notebook open, pen ready. He'd insisted on documenting it. Even now. Even with the well half full of something that wasn't water and the voice in the walls reciting their names in their own voices, Theo needed a record. Cass was beginning to understand that the notebook was how he prayed.

Joel pushed the gate.

It swung open on its hinge, easy, ordinary, the small complaining creak of rusted metal that every gate in England made. He stepped through. He turned around on the far side of the line, standing on the verge where the lane began, and he was — outside. Just outside. Standing in the world, on the road, under the sky, with nothing between him and anywhere else in the country except a long walk and a phone signal.

He jogged down the lane to where his van was parked, and the engine turned over, and the headlights came on, and he drove away, and Cass watched the taillights shrink down the road and around the bend and gone.

Gone. The word hit her like a physical thing.

"He's out," Theo said quietly. He wrote it down. *J. Okafor, non-blood, exits via front gate, 11:42 a.m., no resistance.* His pen hovered. "He's out," he said again, like he was testing whether the sentence held weight.

Cass came out onto the porch. The cold hit her face and she stood at the top of the steps and looked at the gate — the same gate, the same rusted latch, the same lane — and something in her chest was doing two completely opposite things at once.

Relief, first. Enormous, staggering relief. *The rule is real. The rule has a shape. The rule can be used.* Priya was already back at the table, already writing, already turning the fact over like a coin to see what was stamped on the other side. If the house counted blood and not residency, then the curse had a border. And a border meant a map. And a map meant a way out.

And underneath the relief, arriving a half-second later, the other thing.

She looked at the gate and her legs wouldn't go.

* * *

She tried anyway. Of course she tried. Priya stood at the porch rail with her stopwatch — she'd brought a stopwatch, because Priya believed in measuring things even when the things were nightmares — and Cass walked down the path with her coat buttoned to her chin and her hands in her pockets and told herself, the whole way, that it was just a gate, it was just a gate, it was just a gate her great-grandfather had painted green in 1961.

The latch was cold. She lifted it.

Nothing stopped her. That was the worst part, she thought later. Nothing ever stopped any of them. The gate swung open exactly as wide as it had swung for Joel, on exactly the same creak, and the lane lay there, and the sky lay over the lane, and it all looked exactly as open as it had looked for him.

She stepped through.

The cold came up through her boots like the ground itself had an opinion. She took one step, two, three, four — she counted them, because counting was the only thing she could still trust — and at the fifth step she made the mistake of looking back at the house.

The farmhouse sat in its yard the way a spider sits in the exact center of a web. Not menacing. Patient. Its windows caught the low sun and held it, and for a moment — she would swear this later, would swear it on anything — the windows were the color of the well water. Black, still, deep.

She turned back to the lane and walked. Ten steps. Twenty. The lane was straight and she walked it straight and at forty steps she stopped, because ahead of her, where the lane should have bent down toward the crossroads, there was the farmhouse gate.

The same gate. The same green paint, flaking in the same places. The latch hanging open exactly as she'd left it.

Behind her, down the lane she'd just walked, she could hear Joel's van, or a van, turning the corner by the crossroads. The sound came from the wrong direction.

Cass stood at the gate with her hand on the cold latch and did not cry, because she'd used up all her crying on the first night, and what was left was something drier and worse. She walked back up the path and into the kitchen and sat down at the table where Priya had laid out the journal and the parish records and the deed, and she said, in a voice she didn't recognize as her own:

"It's blood. It's actually blood. He walked out like it was nothing and I couldn't even get to the crossroads."

"We know," Priya said gently. "We know, Cass."

"No, you don't." She heard her own voice crack and kept going anyway, because it was going to crack whether she let it or not. "Joel is in a van on the M5 right now. He's going to stop at a services and buy a terrible sandwich and text me about it. And I'm going to be here, being *counted*. Do you understand? He gets to leave. He gets to just — leave. Because he had the incredible good luck to not be born into this family."

Nobody said anything. Uncle Wynn was in the corner chair by the window, wrapped in a blanket, and his eyes were open and clear in the way they'd been clear more and more often lately, the lucidity coming to him in patches like sun through moving cloud.

"You're angry at the wrong thing," Wynn said.

Cass looked at him. "Am I?"

"You're angry because you can't leave and he can." Wynn's voice was papery but steady. "But think about what it means, girl. Think about what it just told you. It doesn't want Joel. It doesn't want the postman, the vicar, the developer, the whole rest of the world. It wants us. Only us. It has wanted only us for a hundred years." He pulled the blanket closer. "That's not just a cage, Cass. That's information."

* * *

The information sat on the kitchen table all afternoon while the light died early the way December light does, and the family gathered around it in ones and twos, the way they'd started gathering without anyone deciding to.

Theo had pinned a sheet of paper to the wall. At the top he'd written *THE RULE* in his blocky, report-writing capitals, and beneath it:

*The house counts blood, not residency.*
*Non-blood persons enter and exit freely (verified: J. Okafor, 11:42 a.m.).*
*Hartwells cannot pass the gate (verified: C. Hartwell, 11:51 a.m.).*
*Therefore: the curse is inherited. Therefore: the curse has a lineage. Therefore: the lineage can be traced.*

"Therefore," Priya said, "we need to know exactly what it thinks it's counting."

She had the journal open in front of her, the one from the lead-lined box, and beside it the deed, and beside that the photocopied parish register pages. Cass had watched her work for days now, watched her move through the documents with the patience of someone defusing something, and she'd started to understand that Priya read old paper the way Theo read a house — looking for what was wrong, what didn't match, what had been touched by hands that shouldn't have been there.

"Agnes Hartwell," Priya said. "Great-great-grandmother. Declared dead in 1919. Signature on the deed in 1921. We've been over that. But here's what I keep snagging on." She turned the deed toward them and put her finger on a line. "This. *'Agnes Hartwell, widow, of Hartwell Farm, in consideration of natural love and affection, conveys the said property to...' * — and then the name. And the name is Edward Hartwell. Agnes's brother."

"So?" Des said from the doorway. He'd been in the doorway a lot since the fire attempt. He looked like a man who'd been counted and knew it.

"So widows convey to children. To nephews sometimes, if there are no children. But look at the phrasing — *natural love and affection* — that's the standard formula, fine, but look at the date. 1921. Two years after she 'died.' And look at this—" she flipped to the register copy. "Agnes Hartwell, buried at St. Aldhelm's, March 1919. Except the grave's empty. We've seen it. So she didn't die, or she didn't stay dead, or 'Agnes Hartwell' is a name that stopped meaning what we think it means somewhere around 1919." She looked around the table. "The house counts blood. Agnes's blood. But here's my question, and I need someone to say it out loud before I do: how much of Agnes's blood is actually in this room?"

The silence had a texture. The fire ticked in the grate. Upstairs, faintly, the voice in the walls had started its evening recitation — *Marjorie. Desmond. Wynn. Cassandra. Theodore.* — unhurried, patient, a bell with no clapper.

"None," Marjorie said.

Everyone turned. She was standing by the dresser with a teacup she hadn't drunk from, and her face was the face of a woman setting down something she'd carried for forty years, except Cass had learned that Marjorie set things down and then picked them up again, over and over, because some things only stay put when the whole family holds them down together.

"None," Marjorie said again. "That's the thing my mother told me and I didn't listen. Agnes never had children. Never married — well. There was a bargain instead of a wedding, my mother said. The well instead of a husband." Her mouth twisted. "I thought it was a story to keep girls off the cellar stairs. Everything was a story to keep girls off the cellar stairs. But if she never had children—"

"Then we're not her blood," Cass said slowly. The idea moved into her like cold air under a door. "Then the house is counting—"

"A lie," Priya said. "A hundred-year-old bookkeeping error. The deed says Edward inherited the farm, and everyone after is Edward's line. But somewhere — in the ledger the house keeps, in whatever the well actually reads — the family got filed under the wrong name. Under *her* name." She spread her hands. "We're being collected for a debt that was never ours."

Wynn laughed. It was a small, dry sound, and it surprised everyone, including him.

"A clerical error," he said. "A hundred years of it. My mother burned her own photograph over this. Des has a tally on his door. And it's a *filing mistake*." He laughed again, weaker. "God help us. God help the poor thing. Imagine being that old and that patient and wrong."

But Cass wasn't laughing. Cass was looking at the gate through the kitchen window, at the latch hanging open, at the lane beyond it where Joel's taillights had gone and not come back, and she was doing the arithmetic that nobody else seemed to be doing yet.

If the house counted blood, and they were not the blood —

Then why, she thought, could she not walk out the gate?

The question must have shown on her face, because Priya followed her eyes to the window and stopped moving. Theo stopped writing. Even the voice in the walls seemed, for a moment, to pause between names.

"Because it doesn't know that yet," Marjorie said quietly. "Or because it doesn't care. Or because—" she set down the untouched teacup, and her hand was not quite steady, "—because a lie that's been fed for a hundred years doesn't stop being hungry just because you show it the receipts. We have to *prove* it. To the house. To the well. To whatever keeps the ledger. We have to correct the record in a language it accepts."

"And what language does a well accept?" Des asked from the doorway.

Nobody answered him. Outside, the light had gone completely, and the gate stood open on the dark, and the lane beyond it was empty in both directions, and Cass sat with her hands flat on the table and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grief, that Joel was eating a terrible sandwich somewhere on the M5, and she was glad, and she was furious, and she was ashamed of being glad, and all three of those things were going to have to live in her together until this was over.

Her phone buzzed. Joel: *Made it to services. They were out of meal deals, which honestly feels like a personal attack. You ok?*

She looked at the message for a long time. Then she typed: *Working on it. There's a loophole. Stay somewhere warm.*

She put the phone face-down on the table, and upstairs the voice in the walls began the count again, and this time — Cass was almost sure, though she would not say it aloud, not yet, not with everyone finally, finally hopeful — this time it ended on a name it had never used before.

It ended on hers. Twice.
