## 19. Des's Bargain

Des Hartwell had never been a brave man, and he had stopped pretending otherwise somewhere around his second divorce. Bravery was for people who hadn't learned how the world actually worked — that everything, in the end, came down to who was willing to walk away first and fastest. He'd walked away from marriages, from jobs, from a mortgage in Reading that still gave him nightmares of a different flavor than the current kind. Walking away was his one talent.

So it was a bitter irony, sitting at the kitchen table of a house that wouldn't let him leave, to realize he was about to do the one thing he'd never done: volunteer to stay.

The tally on his bedroom door had appeared two nights ago. One clean stroke, cut into the paint as if with a fingernail, though when he ran his thumb over it the groove was smooth as glass, older than the door itself. He'd slept — if you could call it sleep — with the duvet pulled over his head like a boy, listening to the walls recite names in a voice like dry gravel shifting. *Marjorie. Wynn. Cass. Priya. Theo.* And then, always last, always softest, almost tender: *Desmond.*

It knew him. That was the worst of it. Not Desmond Hartwell, not Des from the estate agency brochures, but *Desmond*, the name his mother had used only when he was in trouble, the name that meant *come here and be ashamed of yourself*. The house said it the way she used to say it.

He looked across the kitchen table at Theo, who was hunched over Agnes's journal with a pencil behind his ear like a man doing his taxes at the end of the world. Priya had her laptop open, cross-referencing parish records against something handwritten. Marjorie stood at the window even though there was nothing to see but fog pressing against the glass like a face waiting to be let in.

"I've been thinking," Des said.

Theo didn't look up. "Dangerous."

"Go on, then. Laugh." Des turned his mug in a slow circle. The tea had gone cold hours ago; everything in this house went cold unless you watched it. "I've been thinking about what it wants. What the well wants. It wants a sitter, right? One body in the chair by solstice, and everyone else walks. That's the deal Agnes made. That's the arithmetic."

Marjorie turned from the window. "Des—"

"Let me finish. I've done the sums." He laughed, and hated the sound of it, high and thin. "I've literally done the sums. It's my job. You put one asset in and you protect the rest of the portfolio. That's not heroism, that's just good bookkeeping."

"You're talking about putting yourself in a chair," Priya said quietly. She'd closed the laptop. "In a painting. Forever."

"I'm talking about a trade." He made himself say it plainly, because if he dressed it up he'd lose his nerve. "Me for them. My name off the list of the living, theirs kept safe. If the house will take it."

The silence that followed had texture. The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere above them, in the sealed room, floorboards settled with a sound like someone shifting their weight.

"The house doesn't negotiate," Marjorie said. Her voice was flat, and Des recognized the tone — it was the voice she'd used at their mother's funeral, the voice that held things down so they couldn't get up and bite anyone. "It's not a person, Des. It's a debt. Debts don't haggle."

"Everything negotiates," Des said. "I've sold houses to people who swore they'd never sell. I've talked banks out of repossession twice. There's always a number. There's always a version of yes." He pushed back his chair and stood, and his legs were shaking, and he was grateful no one seemed to notice, or kind enough to pretend not to. "I just have to find it."

"Des." Theo had finally looked up. His brother's face was grey with exhaustion, pencil smudge on his cheekbone, and underneath the exhaustion was something Des hadn't seen since they were boys — fear for him. Actual fear, aimed at Des, as if Des were worth being afraid for. "Don't."

"Somebody has to be first to try," Des said. "And I'm the most expendable Hartwell in this house. No kids. No one waiting. Half the family can't stand me and the other half is being polite about it." He managed a grin. It felt like wearing someone else's teeth. "That's not self-pity, that's market analysis."

He walked out before any of them could answer, because he knew if he stayed he would hear an argument he wanted to lose, and he could not afford to lose this one.

* * *

The portrait hung in the hall where it had reappeared after Des burned it in the yard — unmarked, unsmoked, its frame colder than the air around it. Des had done that burning himself, doused it in white spirit, stood back and watched it go up like a scream. And in the morning it had been here again, hanging straight and patient, and his bedroom door had carried its single fresh-cut tally, and he had understood with the clarity of the damned that half-measures were simply another way of paying interest.

Now he stood in front of it alone, at three in the morning, with a torch in one hand and his heart going like a fist on a locked door.

Agnes Hartwell looked out of the canvas at him. She had always looked at him, he realized now — not past, not through, but *at*, the way a shopkeeper watches a customer who keeps picking things up and putting them down. She sat in her high-backed chair with her hands folded, and beside her, painted with terrible care, stood the empty chair. Smaller. Waiting. Its seat caught the painted light like a held breath.

Since Wynn's night — since the house had tried on the old man's cardigan and found it wanting — the empty chair had been empty again. But Des could feel it the way you felt a queue forming behind you in a quiet shop. Something was coming to fill it. Something was choosing.

"All right," Des said aloud. His voice cracked on the second word and he started again. "All right. Agnes. Mrs. Hartwell. Great-Grandmother, if we're being formal, though God knows how that works when you're — whatever you are."

The portrait said nothing. Of course it didn't. But the hall got quieter, the way a room gets quieter when someone stops talking to listen, and Des's skin crawled from his wrists to his collar.

"Here's my offer." He stepped closer. The cold coming off the canvas was wrong, was *directional*, flowing toward him like water finding a drain. "You want a Hartwell in that chair. Fine. Take me. Take Desmond Peter Hartwell, born 1968, currently residing — well, wherever you like, you're keeping me here anyway." His laugh came out broken. "I'll sit. I'll sit tonight, tomorrow, whenever you want. I won't fight it. In exchange—" and here his voice did something he didn't authorize, dropped low and fierce, "—in exchange you clear the ledger. Every name. Marjorie, Wynn, Theo, Cass, Priya. All of them off your books. They walk out that gate and the roads stay straight and this house never counts them again. That's the trade. One for all. It's better arithmetic than you've been running for a hundred years, and I know you people love your arithmetic."

He was shaking now, properly shaking, sweat rolling cold between his shoulder blades despite the directional chill. He made himself hold the portrait's gaze. Her eyes were the color of pond ice, and they were not kind, but they were *attentive*, and attention was the first currency of any negotiation.

"That's it," he whispered. "That's my best offer. Take it or—"

Or what? He had nothing else. They both knew it.

The house considered.

He felt it considering. There was no other word. The temperature in the hall dropped until his breath showed, and beneath his feet, through the floorboards, through the cellar hatch he'd helped pry open a week ago, came a sound like a great slow page turning. Down in the well, he understood without knowing how he understood, the tally marks were pausing — the scratching that had become constant, nail after nail after name, had stopped mid-stroke. The whole house hung suspended, weighing him. Weighing his offer the way a bank weighed collateral, coldly, completely, finding out exactly what he was worth.

*Please,* Des thought, and was disgusted to find tears standing in his eyes. *Please. Let this be the one thing I do right.*

Then the house answered.

It didn't use words. It used the well. From below came a single sound — not the scratch of a tally, but a knock, deep and resonant, stone on stone, like a clerk stamping a document DENIED. The cold reversed itself, rushing away from Des back toward the portrait, and in the canvas Agnes's folded hands shifted — he saw it, he would swear on anything he had left that he saw it — one finger lifting, unhurried, and pointing.

Not at him.

Past him. Through him. Down the hall, toward the stairs, toward the sealed room at the end of the landing where the door gaped open now, where the smell of wet paint lived.

The finger pointed at the room. At the seal that had been broken. At the threshold some fool had crossed.

"No," Des said. "No, no — it wasn't me. I didn't open it. I burned the bloody thing, I tried to *end* it—"

The finger did not move.

Down in the well, the tallies resumed, faster than before, eager, and Des heard — or imagined he heard, and would spend the rest of however long he had failing to decide which — a voice threaded through the scratching. Not the reciting voice from the walls. A different voice. Older. Dry as paper, patient as arithmetic, amused the way a spider might be amused.

*The one who opened the room. The debt follows the hand, not the heart.*

"It wasn't me!" Des shouted it at the portrait, at the house, at the fog pressed against every window. "Ask her! Ask Marjorie! She was a child, it wasn't her fault either, it was forty years ago—"

But even as he said it he understood, with the sick lurch of a man reading the small print after signing, that fault had nothing to do with it. This was not justice. This was bookkeeping. The hand that broke the seal owed the debt, and Marjorie's hand had broken it, and the house had waited thirty-eight years for the count to come due, and it did not care about children or intentions or brothers offering themselves up out of guilt. It cared about entries in a ledger. It cared about *whose* hand.

Which meant every hour Des spent trying to bargain was an hour the house spent getting closer to Marjorie.

He backed away from the portrait. Agnes's finger lowered, slowly, folding back into her hands, and her expression — he'd have testified to this too, later, in whatever court would take the evidence — her expression was almost gentle. Almost pitying. The look you gave a man who'd offered you a counterfeit note and genuinely hadn't known it was fake.

Des turned and ran. Up the stairs, past the sealed room without looking at it, along the corridor to the narrow door under the eaves, the attic stair he'd found as a boy and used as a hideout when the grown-ups' voices got sharp. He took the steps three at a time, hauled the door shut behind him, and dragged the old trunk across it with hands that barely worked.

Then he sat down in the dark among the boxes of photographs, in the dust and the cold, and put his head in his arms, and shook.

He had offered everything he had. Everything. His name, his forever, the only currency he'd ever really possessed — and the house had looked at it and found it counterfeit, because Des Hartwell had spent his whole life walking away, and walking-away men's sacrifices don't clear. The house knew. The house had read him like a credit report.

And worse — far worse, the thought arriving now and settling into him like a splinter working toward the heart — the house hadn't even been tempted. Because it already had what it wanted. It had known since 1987 whose hand broke the seal. It had been waiting, patient as a lien, for Marjorie to come downstairs on her own.

Unless someone got to her first.

Des lifted his head in the dark. Below him, muffled through the floorboards, he heard the front door open and close, heard footsteps cross the yard, heard the old Land Rover's engine turn over and catch.

Marjorie was driving somewhere. Alone. At four in the morning.

He was on his feet before he knew it, hauling the trunk away from the attic door, taking the stairs in the dark, and the shame of what he'd just tried — the shame of offering himself and meaning it and being refused for it — rode on his back all the way down, because none of it mattered now. None of it mattered except reaching the yard before the taillights reached the gate.

Because Des Hartwell knew, better than anyone alive, what happened to people who drove away from this house alone.

They came back. Or the road brought them back. Or the house came out to meet them.

And Marjorie's name was next on the list.
