## 20. The Portrait Changes

Theo did not sleep so much as he was set down in the dark and picked up again at dawn, like a parcel left on a doorstep by someone who didn't want to be seen delivering it.

He had taken the spare room at the far end of the hall — the one that smelled of camphor and old plaster, the one furthest from the sealed door — and he had told himself, lying on a mattress that sagged toward the middle like a tired animal, that the voice he'd heard the night before had been pipes. Old houses settled. Old houses talked. He was a Hartwell; he'd grown up with this house's vocabulary of knocks and groans, and none of it had ever meant anything.

But pipes didn't recite names. Pipes didn't pause between them, unhurried, patient, the way a man pauses between items on a list he has read ten thousand times and will read ten thousand more. *Marjorie Ellen Hartwell.* A bell struck somewhere behind the plaster. *Desmond Paul Hartwell.* Another bell. The names hadn't stopped when he'd pulled the duvet over his head. They hadn't stopped when he'd put his earbuds in and played a podcast about the history of concrete at full volume. They had simply waited beneath the concrete-voice, the way a river waits under ice, and when the podcast ended at 3:14 a.m. — he knew because he checked — the recitation picked up exactly where it had left off, mid-name, as though it had been holding the syllable for him.

*Cass—*

He had not stayed to hear the rest. He had gone downstairs, made tea he didn't drink, and sat in the kitchen until the windows went grey, watching the garden wall for movement that never came but felt perpetually imminent, like a held breath.

At half past seven, Marjorie came down in her dressing gown, took one look at him, and said nothing. She put the kettle on. That was the whole conversation. Some things were too large for words before eight o'clock.

It was Priya who found it.

She came down at nine with her laptop under her arm and her hair still wet from the bathroom, and she stopped dead in the hallway between the stairs and the kitchen, the way you stop when you've walked into a spider's web — not hurt, just suddenly aware that something has been built across your path in the night.

"Theo," she said. Just his name. But she said it the way people say *fire.*

* * *

The seal on the sitting room door was broken. Not torn, not forced — broken neatly, the strips of wood Marjorie had nailed across it in 1987 pried away and stacked against the wall in an orderly pile, nails laid on top in a tidy row, points all facing the same direction. Theo's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. He hadn't done it. Priya hadn't. Marjorie had been in the kitchen the whole time, within sight.

The door stood open six inches.

Inside, the room was exactly as a sealed room should be: dust sheeted furniture, grey light through curtained windows, air thick enough to chew. And on the far wall, above the cold fireplace, hung the portrait.

Great-Grandmother Agnes Hartwell sat in it the way royalty sits in postage stamps — spine straight, hands folded, mouth set in the thin line of a woman who has signed something and does not intend to discuss it. Her dress was high-collared black. Her eyes were pale, almost colourless, and they did not look at the viewer so much as through the viewer, at something standing slightly behind and to the left, which Theo found himself unwilling to turn around and check for.

And beside her — where yesterday, according to every account, there had been only painted wallpaper and wainscoting — stood the chair.

A wooden kitchen chair, plain-backed, its seat worn pale by decades of sitting. It was rendered in the same oils, the same palette, the same careful hand. It stood at a slight angle to Agnes, facing outward, facing the room, facing whoever came through the door.

And it was not empty.

Theo's first thought, absurdly, was that someone had put a real coat over a real chair in front of the painting — some prank of Des's, some test. Because what sat in the painted chair was unmistakably a figure wearing a work coat: dark green canvas, the heavy kind, with reinforced elbows and a zip that caught on the second tooth. His coat. The one hanging on the hook by the back door right now — he made Priya go and check, and she came back white-faced and confirmed it was still there, still empty, still smelling of rodent repellent and diesel.

But the painted coat was empty too. There was no body in it. No arms filled the sleeves, no chest rounded the front. The coat sat on the chair the way coats sit on chairs when their owner has stepped out of the room — except that the collar stood up slightly, expectant, and the shoulders held a shape that was waiting rather than vacant.

There was no head. Where a face should have been, above the coat's collar, the paint was smooth flesh-tone, featureless, unfinished — like a sculpture a sculptor had abandoned at the stage where the clay says *person* without yet saying *who.*

"It wasn't there last week," Marjorie said from the doorway. She would not cross the threshold. Her knuckles were white on the frame. "I looked at it through the gap in the boards, after we broke the seal. I looked for a long time. There was no chair. There was never a chair in the painting. Agnes stands alone in that portrait, Theo. She always has."

"Then when did it change?"

"That's not the question." Marjorie's voice was very quiet. "The question is what it's waiting for."

Priya raised her phone and photographed it. The flash stuttered. On the screen, the image came out wrong — the chair slightly larger than it should be, closer to the camera than the perspective allowed, the way the chairs in the photographs had always been. Too close. Always slightly too close.

"We need to document this," Priya said, and her voice shook only a little. "Every day. Same time, same angle, same lighting. If it's changing, I want a record of how fast."

"Why?" Theo asked.

"Because," Priya said, looking at the faceless figure in the work coat, at the collar waiting for a head, "because I think it's counting up to something. Everything in this house counts, Theo. I don't think this is any different."

* * *

He told himself he wouldn't go back in. He had a job to run — two call-outs booked, a wasp nest in Kettering, a restaurant with mice — and he clung to the routine of it like a man clinging to driftwood. He drove out to Kettering and poisoned a wasp nest and gave a woman in a dressing gown a receipt, and the normalcy of it was so violent, so total, that for forty minutes he almost believed the farmhouse was a bad dream he'd eventually wake from into a life where portraits stayed painted and voices stayed in radios.

Then he came back, because there was nowhere else to go, and the house was still there, and the portrait was still there, and the figure in the chair was still there.

But it was different.

Not obviously. Not at first glance. He stood in the doorway with Priya beside him and compared it to the photographs on her phone, and it took him a full minute to find what had changed, and when he found it he wished he hadn't.

The hands.

Yesterday the coat's sleeves had ended in empty cuffs, dark openings, nothing. Today there were hands resting in the figure's lap — painted hands, large-knuckled, with short bitten nails and a faded scar across the left index finger where a rat had gone through glove and flesh in one bite when Theo was nineteen.

His scar. His bitten nails. His father's hands, which had become his hands without his noticing, the way faces do.

"It's working downward," Priya whispered. "Or upward. Building from the edges in. Like a photograph developing."

That night Theo slept in his van in the farmyard, engine off, wrapped in a sleeping bag rated for temperatures he was not experiencing, and it was somehow worse than the spare room, because through the windscreen he could see the upstairs window of the sitting room, and the curtain did not quite close, and the pale rectangle of the portrait showed through the gap like a lit screen, and he lay there until three a.m. telling himself that paintings cannot watch, paintings cannot watch, paintings cannot watch, before finally turning the van around so the windscreen faced the hedge.

The voice in the walls reached him even out there. Fainter, muffled by brick and distance, but rhythmic, patient, a tide going out and coming in. He counted the intervals between names. Eleven seconds. Always eleven seconds. He fell asleep doing the arithmetic of how many names could be spoken between now and the solstice, and woke at dawn with the answer written on the inside of his eyelids and no memory of writing it.

* * *

On the third morning, the figure had a jawline.

On the fourth, hair — dark, cut short, receding at the temples in the exact pattern Theo saw in mirrors and avoided photographing.

On the fifth morning, Theo stood in the sitting room alone — he had begun coming in alone, early, before anyone else was awake, because some part of him had decided that if this thing was going to keep happening, he would rather meet it first, on his terms, like checking a trap line — and the figure had ears now, and the beginning of a nose, and the coat had gained details that made his throat close: the burn mark on the left pocket from a flare he'd used in a loft in Corby; the frayed cuff he kept meaning to sew; the pen in the breast pocket, his pen, the good one, the one currently in his shirt pocket downstairs.

The paint around the figure was wet. Not glistening — wet, actually wet, the surface tacky under the fingertip he extended before he could stop himself, and when he drew the finger back a thread of oil paint came with it, slow and thick as honey, the colour of skin.

He wiped his hand on his jeans and the smear wouldn't come off. It stayed on his knuckle all day, faintly warm, like a touch that had decided to stay.

"You shouldn't go in there alone anymore," Marjorie said that evening. She was washing dishes that didn't need washing. The tap ran and ran. "When your grandfather was dying — my father-in-law — in his last month he used to sit in that room. Before we sealed it. He'd sit in the chair by the window and look at the portrait and talk to it. Not to Agnes. To the space next to Agnes. He'd say, 'Not yet, not yet, I'm not finished.' And we thought it was the morphine." She turned off the tap. The silence afterward was enormous. "He died on the twenty-first of December. Solstice morning. And when we went to lay him out, his hands—" She stopped. Started again. "His hands were stained to the wrist. Black. Like ink. Like he'd been reaching into something."

Nobody spoke. From upstairs, faintly, came the sound of the walls settling — or reciting — and everyone at the table pretended it was settling.

* * *

On the seventh morning, the figure had a mouth.

It was closed, neutral, unremarkable — Theo's own mouth, the one Cass once said made him look like he was permanently about to ask for paperwork. But its existence changed everything, because a mouth implies speech, implies breath, implies a face assembling itself around the machinery of being alive, and Theo stood in the cold room looking at his own painted lips and understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own death certificate, that the painting was not depicting him.

It was *becoming* him. Detail by detail, morning by morning, the way frost becomes a window. And when it was finished — when the last brushstroke of him dried into permanence — the original would be surplus. Redundant. A rough draft.

He thought about Des's wrists, and the ink-dark marks like restraints. He thought about Wynn's mother burning her photograph, and what it must have cost her, and why she'd known to do it. He thought about the tally marks in the well, fresh-cut each dawn, matching the number of Hartwells under the roof, and how the number had not changed since they'd all arrived, as if the well were satisfied, as if the well were *waiting.*

He got Priya. He got Marjorie. He made them stand in the doorway and look, because he could not carry it alone anymore, and because a witness is a kind of anchor.

"That's you," Priya said. Not a question.

"That's me."

"How long?"

"Seven days. It started the night after the first count." He did the arithmetic aloud, because saying numbers made them smaller. "Solstice is in twelve days. If it keeps this pace—"

"It'll be finished before the solstice," Marjorie said. Her voice had gone strange and calm, the calm of a woman standing on a platform watching a train come in that she has been expecting for forty years. "It'll be finished, and then it won't need to wait for the solstice at all. The solstice isn't the deadline, Theo. It's the delivery date."

Behind them, in the portrait, in the silence, the painted mouth moved.

None of them saw it move. All three of them would swear afterward that they were watching the figure's face at that exact moment, and none of them saw it. But the room's air pressure changed — a soft displacement, like a sigh let out through cloth — and on Priya's phone, which was still recording timelapse frames every thirty seconds, the final frame of that minute showed the figure's lips parted.

Just parted. As if drawing breath.

As if, somewhere deep in the drying oil and the patient pigment, seven mornings of Theo Hartwell had practiced, and were nearly ready, and wanted very much to try out the voice.

That night, the recitation in the walls paused eleven seconds longer than usual between names — and in the gap, very softly, in a voice Theo recognized the way you recognize your own footsteps, something began to hum.

It was the tune he whistled on call-outs. The one his father taught him.
