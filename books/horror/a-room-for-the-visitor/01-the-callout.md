## 1. The Callout

The complaint came in on a Tuesday, which Jude Ishikawa considered a good sign. Nothing that arrived on a Monday was ever simple—Mondays were for landlords panicking about weekend damage, for tenants who'd spent three days convincing themselves the scratching was mice and failing. Tuesdays meant someone had thought it over. Tuesdays meant paperwork.

Jude liked paperwork.

The work order sat on the passenger seat of the van, printed in triplicate because the county system was twenty years out of date and Jude had never once been sorry about it. *Complainant: A. Voss. Address: Marrow House, 11 Fenwick Lane. Nature of complaint: noises within walls, persistent. Duration: unspecified.* The handwriting of whoever had transcribed the call was cramped and impatient, but the address itself had been written in a different hand—older, careful, each letter formed with a draftsman's precision. Someone at the office had looked up the property and copied it from a record rather than from the caller's mouth.

That was unusual. Jude noted it in the margin of the work order, because noting things was what Jude did. Noted it the way they noted everything: date, time, weather (overcast, 61 degrees, wind from the southwest), odometer reading, the exact minute they pulled away from the depot. Forty-one years old and still keeping field logs like a first-year apprentice, because the log was the ritual and the ritual was the armor. If you wrote it down, it existed. If it existed, it could be understood. If it could be understood, it could not hurt you.

This was the creed. Jude had never examined it closely enough to notice it was also a prayer.

Fenwick Lane ended where the town's ambition did—a gravel shoulder, a rusted guardrail, and beyond it the long flat fields that developers had been circling for a decade like patient sharks. Marrow House stood at the end of the lane behind a hedge gone feral, two stories of gray clapboard with a slate roof the color of wet stone. It was not derelict. That was the first thing Jude noticed, and the noticing snagged somewhere under the sternum like a fishhook. Derelict houses were honest. This one was *maintained*—gutters clean, steps swept, paint touched up in places with a slightly wrong shade of white so the repairs showed like scars. Someone loved this house badly, or dutifully, or both.

The gate latch was brass, polished by hands. Jude put on gloves before touching it anyway, logged the time—9:47 a.m.—and walked up the path through a corridor of overgrown boxwood that smelled like cat piss and church.

The woman who answered the door was small and upright and eighty-something, dressed as if expecting a bank manager: cardigan buttoned to the throat, hair set and sprayed into permanence. Her eyes did the thing Jude had learned to read across thousands of callouts—the flick down to the van logo on Jude's shirt, the flick back up, the quick arithmetic of *is this person going to believe me.*

"Voss," she said. "Ada Voss. You're late."

"Ishikawa." Jude offered the laminated ID, then the clipboard with the work order, then a pen for the signature line, in that order, because sequence mattered. "Nine forty-seven arrival, logged. You reported noises in the walls."

"I reported noises *within* the walls," Ada Voss said. "There's a difference. Come in. Wipe your feet."

* * *

Inside, Marrow House smelled of beeswax and cold ash and, beneath both, something mineral and old, like the air in a cave that had never fully decided to be indoors. The hallway ran deep and narrow, papered in a pattern of faded birds, and lined with doors. At the far end, light came through a window in a way that made Jude think of aquariums.

Jude worked the way Jude always worked: perimeter first. They walked the ground floor slowly, tapping baseboards with a knuckle, listening, logging. Kitchen: dry, no droppings, no grease trails. Under the stairs: dust undisturbed except for one clean rectangle where something had recently been removed—a box, maybe, or a photograph that had hung there a long time. Living room: a cold fireplace, furniture arranged around it like an audience, and a wall of framed photographs, dozens of them, all slightly askew by exactly the same degree, as if adjusted by the same compulsive hand.

"You'll want the east parlor," Ada said from the doorway. She hadn't followed; she'd waited, arms folded, watching Jude work with the expression of someone supervising a repair they already knew the outcome of. "That's where it is."

"It?"

"The noise. And the rest of it." She turned and walked without waiting to see if Jude followed, which no one ever did, which everyone always did.

The east parlor's north wall was wrong. Jude saw it from the doorway and stopped, pen hovering over the log. The plaster had been patched—recently, competently—but the patch was a different texture than the surrounding wall, smoother, and it bulged very slightly, the way skin does over a splinter the body can't push out. Around the edges of the patch, the older wallpaper had been cut back in a neat border, exposing a band of bare plaster six inches wide.

And in that band of bare plaster were fingerprints.

Not smudges. Not stains. *Prints*—pressed into the plaster itself, as though the wall had once been soft clay and something had gripped it from inside and held on. Five fingers each, elongated, the tips tapering to points that had dug in deep enough to leave crescent craters. There were four sets of them, spaced along the exposed band like rungs, ascending toward the ceiling. As if something had climbed the inside of the wall. As if something had paused there, gripping, and pressed hard enough to leave its signature in stone-soft gypsum, and then continued up.

Jude stood very still.

The professional part of the mind—the part that had catalogued eleven thousand rodent entry points, bat colonies, carpenter ant galleries—began its inventory automatically. Plaster sets in hours. These prints were in cured plaster, in a wall sealed since before the patch went on. Pressure required would exceed anything a trapped animal could exert. The finger spacing was too wide for a raccoon, too long for a human child, too—

Stop. Log it. Photograph it.

Jude got the camera bag from the hall and worked methodically, the way that steadied them: wide shot establishing the wall, medium shot of the print band, close-ups of each individual print with the scale card placed beside them, oblique lighting raked across the surface to raise the depth of the impressions. Shutter clicks sounded enormous in the quiet house. Between shots, Jude wrote. *Four sets impressions, plaster, interior face of sealed cavity wall, E parlor N wall. Depth approx 3–4 mm. Digit count five. No corresponding damage to exterior surfaces. No entry point identified.*

No entry point. That was the sentence the pen hesitated over. Everything gets in somehow. Mice need a gap the width of a pencil. Bats need a crack. Even water needs a seam. But this wall was sealed, patched, painted—and the prints were on the *inside* of it, pressed outward, the way you'd press your fingers against a window from the other side.

Jude photographed the hesitation too—not literally, but they felt it enter the record anyway, felt the log's tone shift from survey to testimony. *Impressions appear to have been made from within the wall cavity, pressing outward.*

They knelt and put their ear to the patch. Cold plaster against the cartilage, breath held, eyes closed. The house ticked and settled around them the way houses do, wood contracting, pipes cooling. And underneath all of it—so faint it might have been their own pulse conducted through the floorboards—something shifted. Once. Slowly. The way a sleeper turns over.

Jude sat back on their heels and found Ada Voss standing in the doorway again, watching.

"Well?" she said.

"There are prints in the plaster," Jude said. "Behind the seal. I'll need to open a section of the wall to inspect the cavity."

"No," Ada said.

"It's standard procedure. I can't treat what I can't see."

"I know what's in the cavity," Ada Voss said, and her voice didn't shake, which was somehow worse than if it had. "I've known for fifty years. You're not here to open the wall. You're here to write down that you heard it too."

She left the doorway before Jude could answer, her footsteps receding down the hall, unhurried, a woman who had all the time in the world and knew exactly how much of it was left.

Jude knelt alone in the east parlor with the camera warm in their hands and the log open on their knee. The creed rose up in them like a reflex: *write it down, make it real, make it safe.* So they wrote. They wrote until the page was full, describing the prints in language so precise it became a kind of incantation—*tapering distal tips, crescent deformation, uniform spacing suggesting deliberate ascent*—and when the page was full they started a second page, and only when the second page was half full did they admit what they were doing.

They weren't documenting an infestation.

They were holding onto the edge of themselves by the pen.

Because here was the thing Jude had not written down, the thing that sat in their chest now like a swallowed stone: when they'd put their ear to the wall, in that half-second before the shifting sound, they had heard something else. Faint. Rhythmic. Under the plaster, under the lath, under fifty years of silence.

Counting.

Something in the wall had been counting, in a slow dry whisper, and it had stopped—at the exact moment Jude's ear touched the plaster—at the number that sounded, unmistakably, like *nine forty-seven.*

The minute Jude had logged their arrival at the gate.

Jude closed the log. Their hands were steady—they checked, deliberately, and they were steady—and that steadiness frightened them more than shaking would have. Because the creed said observation keeps you safe, and Jude had built forty-one years on that creed, and standing in the east parlor of Marrow House they understood, for the first time, in the wordless way you understand a floor has gone soft beneath you:

Observation is a door. And something on the other side of this wall had just watched them back, and taken note, and filed them away.

In the hallway, as Jude packed the camera, the light through the far window flickered—once, twice—like a blink. Jude logged the time.

9:58 a.m.

Behind the north wall of the east parlor, softly, patiently, something began counting again.
