## 1. The Counting Ward

The night nurse's station at Marrowfen Hospice smelled of lavender oil and old radiators, and by half past ten it had settled into the particular hush that Zephyrine Crane had come to think of as the ward's true voice — not the beeping of monitors or the squeak of trolley wheels down the corridor, but the silence underneath all of it, the silence the dying made together, like a congregation.

Zephyrine sat with the logbook open and a cup of tea going cold at their elbow, and listened to Mr. Aldous Vane count.

*Eighty-nine. Eighty-eight.*

He did it in his sleep, mostly. That was how it always began, in Zephyrine's experience: a murmur at first, barely louder than breath, a man counting down from some private number only he had been given. Aldous Vane had started three nights ago at ninety, and he had counted faithfully ever since, through morphine dreams and lucid hours alike, his voice thin and papery but perfectly steady, as though he were reading from a document pinned somewhere behind his closed eyes.

*Eighty-seven. Eighty-six.*

Zephyrine wrote the time in the margin of the chart. 10:41 p.m. Number: eighty-six.

Twenty years of this. Twenty years of nights in wards like this one — first in Leeds, then York, then here, back to Marrowfen because the town took people back whether they wanted returning or not — and in all that time Zephyrine had never once asked the question that sat at the bottom of every shift like a stone in a well. *What is he counting to?*

You didn't ask. Asking was how you lost the ability to do the work. Zephyrine had watched a new nurse, years ago, a bright girl called Priya with henna on her hands, ask a dying man what he was counting, gently, kindly, kneeling beside him with her notebook ready. He had opened his eyes and looked at her with an expression of such pure, unguarded terror that she'd handed in her notice before the week was out. Not because he'd answered. Because he hadn't. Because whatever lived behind the counting was not for the living to see, and he had known, in that moment, that she could see him seeing it.

So you didn't ask. You logged. You kept the tea warm and the lips moist and the sheets turned, and you wrote down the numbers, and you never said aloud what the numbers meant, even though you knew. Even though you had known since your first year, since Mrs. Halloway in bed four who had counted down from forty-one over six days and died, precisely, at zero, at 4:17 in the morning, with Zephyrine's hand in hers and no monitor alarm sounding, because her heart had simply arrived where it was going.

The counting was never wrong.

That was the sentence Zephyrine did not think, most nights, in the way that a person does not look directly at the sun. It was there anyway. It was there now, in the lavender-and-radiator hush, while Aldous Vane counted his way down through the eighties like a man descending stairs he could not see the bottom of.

*Eighty-five.*

* * *

Aldous Vane had been a schoolmaster for thirty-eight years. Everyone in Marrowfen over forty had been caned by him or loved by him, sometimes both, and the distinction had mostly depended on whether you did your sums. Now he lay in the corner room with the good window — the one that caught the morning light off the reservoir — and his body had become a ledger of its own failures: the heart murmuring its irregular arithmetic, the lungs filling like slow sponges, the legs gone to paper beneath the blanket.

He was ninety-one years old. He had begun counting at ninety.

Zephyrine had noticed that, of course. They noticed everything; noticing was the whole profession, the part they didn't teach you, the part you either had or you drowned without. Ninety years of life, ninety steps down. Coincidence. It was always coincidence. There was a whole shelf in Zephyrine's mind, dusted daily, devoted to coincidences.

At eleven o'clock Zephyrine went in with the water glass and the swab, and found him awake.

Awake was rarer now. In the lucid hours his eyes were still schoolmaster's eyes — pale blue, quick, faintly disappointed in the state of the world — and they found Zephyrine in the doorway the way they'd once found a boy passing notes in the third row.

"Nurse Crane." His voice was a dry leaf. "Come to check my homework?"

"Always, Mr. Vane." Zephyrine raised the head of the bed two clicks, held the straw to his mouth. He drank like a bird, small precise sips, watching them over the rim of the glass.

"You'll want to write this down," he said.

"I've got my book."

"No." A faint smile moved the papery skin around his mouth. "I mean tonight's figure. I'm eighty-four as of—" he glanced at the wall clock with the gravity of a man consulting an instrument "—four minutes past. You'll want it exact."

Zephyrine's hand did not shake. Twenty years had trained the shaking out of their hands, or buried it deep enough that it only surfaced later, at home, in the dark, in the small hours when the cottage creaked and the streetlamps outside buzzed and went out one by one up the lane. Their hand did not shake. They wrote: *11:04 — 84.*

"You keep very good records," Aldous observed.

"It's the job."

"Mm." He closed his eyes. Opened them again. Something moved behind the schoolmaster's calm, something that had perhaps been waiting all evening for the water and the swabbing to be done with. "Nurse Crane. May I ask you something unprofessional?"

"People ask me unprofessional things all day. It balances out."

"When I reach the end of it—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully, choosing words the way he'd once chosen them for reports, each one load-bearing. "When I get near the bottom. Will you be sitting with me? Not for the forms. For the—"

"For the company," Zephyrine said.

"For the company," he agreed, relieved, as if Zephyrine had translated a difficult phrase for him. "It's quieter with someone there. Did you know that? Everyone thinks it must be worse, having a witness. It isn't. It's quieter."

"I know," Zephyrine said. "I'll be here."

He nodded then, satisfied, and let his eyes close, and within four minutes his breathing lengthened into sleep, and within nine the counting resumed, soft as rain on a far roof.

*Eighty-three. Eighty-two.*

Zephyrine sat in the visitor's chair by the window until the tea at the station went fully cold, and did the thing they always did, the ritual so worn it had become almost thoughtless, which was the point of rituals: they estimated. Rate of descent, roughly one number per ninety minutes overnight, faster in the mornings when the body's own tides ran high, slower when the morphine deepened. Aldous was strong for ninety-one; he'd go slower than average. Call it a number every hundred minutes, with the usual acceleration in the last dozen, when the counting always hurried, as if the stairs grew steeper near the bottom.

Eighty-two remaining. Somewhere between five and six days. Death arriving, if the pattern held, in the early hours of Thursday — call it 3 a.m., give or take the width of a hymn.

Zephyrine wrote the estimate in the back of the logbook, in pencil, in the column reserved for it, the column no one else had ever seen because the back pages were Zephyrine's alone and had been through four logbooks and twenty years. The column had no heading. It had never needed one.

Then they checked the estimate against the actuals, the long ledger of the dead, and felt the familiar cold pass through the room without disturbing so much as the curtain.

Mrs. Halloway, 41, exact. Mr. Petch, 12, exact — twelve hours from first count to last breath, gone so fast they'd barely gotten the family called. Old Sorrel Nye, who had counted down from three hundred and eleven across the whole of a winter, exact to the hour, and Zephyrine had sat with her for the final fortnight nearly every night, listening to a woman count her way down from a year, and had told themselves every night that it was a coincidence, and had believed it the way you believe a rope bridge holds: by not looking down.

Two hundred and six names in the back of the books. Two hundred and six estimates. Two hundred and six arrivals, exact.

The counting was never wrong.

Zephyrine capped the pen, squared the logbook, and carried the cold tea to the sluice, and did not think about any of it, professionally, thoroughly, with the discipline of two decades' practice.

* * *

The ward slept. Zephyrine made rounds at midnight and again at two, touching shoulders, adjusting blankets, checking the drip rates. In bed two, Mrs. Fennimore — not dying yet, not counting, just sleeping the drugged sleep of the chronically ill — murmured something about her hens. In bed five, young Danny Roke, thirty-one, the motorway accident, counted too, but he counted upward, which was different, which was hope, and Zephyrine logged his numbers in the ordinary columns like everyone else's vitals and let the back page stay blank for him. Upward-counters sometimes came down. Downward-counters never went back up. That was another thing nobody said.

At 3 a.m., the hour that belonged to itself, Zephyrine sat with Aldous Vane because they had promised, though the promise had been for the end and the end was days off. But the 3 a.m. ward had a quality the other hours lacked — a held-breath quality, the building listening to itself — and Zephyrine had learned long ago that the counting sounded different at 3 a.m. Thinner. Closer. As if the numbers were being read from just the other side of the wall.

*Seventy-nine,* said Aldous Vane, asleep, serene, his hands folded on the blanket like a man done with the day's marking.

Zephyrine sat in the dark and let the ward breathe around them and thought about nothing, expertly.

Except that thinking about nothing, at 3 a.m., has a floor, and if you sit still enough you fall through it. And Zephyrine fell, as they did most nights, just a little way — down to the edge of the question, the stone at the bottom of the well, and stood on it a moment in the dark of their own skull, feeling its cold come up through their shoes.

*What are they counting to?*

Not *what are they counting down from.* From was easy. From was human. A life measured backward, a mind tidying itself toward the door, the brain doing what brains do. Any textbook would hold that.

But *to.* To implied a destination. To implied that somewhere at the bottom of every downward count there was a zero waiting with its hat off, punctual, patient, and that the dying were not wandering toward death but *travelling* toward it, on schedule, by timetable, and that someone — something — had printed the timetable.

And the other question, the one underneath, the one Zephyrine had never once let rise all the way to the surface in twenty years, the one they drowned nightly and it came back nightly, patient as tide:

*Why does it start at the number of your years? Who tells it where to begin?*

Aldous Vane began at ninety. He was ninety-one. Off by one — but then, he'd been born in January, and it was June, and if you rounded down to completed years, as ledgers do—

Zephyrine stood up abruptly. Checked his drip. Checked his pulse, needlessly, fingers at the papery wrist, feeling the heart keep its own erratic time, indifferent to the other count, the real one, running underneath everything like water under ice.

*Seventy-eight,* said Aldous Vane, kindly, in his sleep, to no one, to the room, to whatever waited at the bottom of the stairs.

"Goodnight, Mr. Vane," Zephyrine said aloud, because saying things aloud at 3 a.m. was how you climbed back up out of the well, and walked the corridor back to the station with their footsteps deliberately loud.

* * *

Shift ended at seven. By then Aldous had reached seventy-six, right on the curve, and Zephyrine had amended the estimate in pencil: Thursday, between two and four in the morning. They wrote the handover note — comfortable, settled, family aware — and signed it, and stood a moment at the corner-room door looking in at the old man sleeping his way downstairs, morning light coming gold off the reservoir and lying across the blanket like something laid there by hand.

"Thursday," Zephyrine murmured. "Early. I'll be here."

He didn't stir. But the counting, for just a moment — and Zephyrine would spend the walk home telling themselves they had imagined it, would spend longer than the walk, would spend, in fact, the rest of what remained of their easy nights — the counting paused. One beat. Two. As if the counter had heard, and noted, and approved.

Then: *Seventy-five,* and the morning went on.

Outside, Marrowfen was waking the way it always woke in June — gulls arguing over the reservoir, bread smell from the bakery on Fen Lane, dew smoking off the churchyard grass. Zephyrine walked home through it with the tiredness sitting on their shoulders like a coat, past the post office, past the green where the duck pond needed clearing, past the lane that branched up toward the old chapel on its hummock above the village.

They did not look at the chapel. Nobody looked at the chapel. It had been roofless since before Zephyrine was born, a grey ribcage of a building on the hill, and the village had arranged itself around not-looking-at-it with the same seamless, generations-deep competence with which Zephyrine arranged itself around the back pages of the logbook. Salt on the sills. Windows shuttered at dusk. No one out after the streetlamps died. All of it so old and so ordinary that it had stopped being superstition and become simply *how things were done here*, like blacking the grate or standing for the anthem.

Zephyrine had lived in Marrowfen for eleven years and had never once wondered why the streetlamps went out at midnight instead of dawn, why the ironmonger sold salt in sacks, why Edith Crane — their grandmother, dead these two winters — had kept salt in every room of the cottage like flour, like sugar, a jar on every sill, refilled monthly, unexplained.

These were questions for people who hadn't learned, yet, what questions cost.

Zephyrine let themselves into the cottage, put the kettle on, and stood at the sink looking out at the garden while the water heated. On the windowsill, in the morning light, sat grandmother Edith's last jar of salt, half full, its lid dusty.

Tonight, Zephyrine would learn, the parish register in the vestry of St. Brannoc's held a record of a bell — cast 1743, recast 1820, melted for scrap in the spring of 1911, its bronze weighed and paid for and carted away to York, its mounting beam sawn out and burned. Tonight, at 11:47, that bell would ring.

But that was hours away yet, and the kettle was boiling, and Aldous Vane was at seventy-five, and the counting, as everyone in Marrowfen knew without knowing, was never wrong.
