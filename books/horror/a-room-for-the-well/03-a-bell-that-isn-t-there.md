## 3. A Bell That Isn't There

The dream was the usual one — Mr. Vane's voice counting down through water, *forty-one, forty, thirty-nine* — when the bell woke Zephyrine for true.

One toll.

It came through the cottage walls like a fist through wet paper, low and round and impossibly near, though the nearest church with a working bell was St. Aldhelm's in Fenmarsh, eleven miles east over the causeway. Zephyrine lay in the dark with their hand pressed flat to their sternum and waited for it to happen again, the way you wait for thunder to resolve into a lorry on the ring road, a door slammed by wind, anything with an honest explanation attached.

Nothing followed. The silence after the toll had a texture to it, dense as felt, and inside that silence Zephyrine could hear their own blood moving and, faintly, from across the lane, the creak of the Postlethwaites' gate swinging on its hinge — which it never did at night, because no one in Marrowfen touched a gate after dark.

*Eleven forty-seven,* said the clock on the mantel, in its small apologetic voice, when Zephyrine finally got up to look.

They stood at the window with the curtain held back two inches, the way they'd learned to hold it — enough to see, not enough to be seen seeing — and looked out at the village. The streetlamps along Chapel Row had died at ten o'clock as they always did; the parish council had been promising new wiring since before Zephyrine moved into Edith's cottage, and everyone understood the promise was a fiction maintained for the benefit of outsiders, of whom there were none. Beyond the lamps' dead reach, Marrowfen went about its oldest habit: it hid.

But tonight the hiding had changed shape. In the window of number six, where old Mrs. Osgood lived, a candle flame stood up straight and golden. Across the green, the Hollis house showed a second candle, then a third, lit in a row along a windowsill like little passengers waiting for a boat. Down by the reservoir, a light moved behind the Ketterleys' frosted glass — slow, deliberate, room to room — and Zephyrine understood, with the particular cold that comes up through floorboards, that every household in the village was doing the same thing at the same moment, without a telephone call, without a knock, without a word.

Lighting candles. Saying nothing.

Zephyrine had lived here four years now, back in Edith's cottage after Leeds and the hospice rotation and the long gray years of not coming home, and in four years they had perfected the art of not noticing. It was a professional skill, really. Twenty years of nursing the dying taught you which questions fed the fear and which starved it, and Zephyrine had gotten very good at starving things. When patients counted backwards in their sleep — and they did, so many of them, always backwards, always toward zero, always landing on the exact minute of their going — Zephyrine logged the numbers in a plain notebook and told themselves it was pattern recognition, a curiosity of the dying brain, a thing with a neurological name they hadn't bothered to look up because looking up would mean taking seriously.

When the village put salt along its sills every Michaelmas, Zephyrine called it tradition. When nobody walked past the roofless chapel after sundown — taking the long way around by the mill path rather than cut across the chapel yard, even the teenagers, even drunk — Zephyrine called it superstition, and superstition was harmless, and besides, the chapel yard was muddy.

When Edith, in her last lucid week, had gripped Zephyrine's wrist hard enough to bruise and said, *Don't answer it if it ever calls you by name*, Zephyrine had called it the morphine talking.

The candle flames across the green burned steady in windless air. Nobody's curtains twitched. Nobody stepped outside to ask if anyone else had heard it. That was the part that made Zephyrine's mouth go dry: not the bell, but the response to the bell, choreographed and silent, a congregation performing a rite whose words had been lost on purpose.

Zephyrine let the curtain fall and did what they always did with the unexplainable. They made tea.

* * *

The kettle's noise filled the kitchen and made the silence outside feel louder. Zephyrine sat at Edith's old table with the steam rising and tried to assemble the night into something manageable. A single bell toll at 11:47 p.m. There were explanations. A car backfiring on the causeway — but the sound had been round, bronze, sustained, with that hum underneath it that metal gives when it's been struck and is deciding how long to grieve. A trick of the wind through the chimney — but there had been no wind. Half-asleep invention — except Zephyrine had been awake, truly awake, and the toll had come from the direction of the chapel, and it had seemed to arrive not through the ears exactly but through the floor, up the chair legs, into the teeth.

And there was another explanation, the one Zephyrine had been not-thinking for twenty years, the one shaped like all those dying voices counting down to a minute that always proved exact.

They washed the cup they hadn't drunk from. They went back to bed and lay listening until dawn grayed the curtains, and the bell did not toll again, and sleep did not come either.

* * *

In the morning, Marrowfen performed its masterpiece of collective amnesia. The candles were gone from the windowsills. Mrs. Osgood was sweeping her step and wished Zephyrine good morning in her cracked, warm voice, the voice that had once sounded out syllables over young Zephyrine's shoulder while the other children already read whole pages. The Postlethwaites' gate hung closed. At the shop, Denzil Hollis rang up milk and bread and spoke about the weather with the fervor of a man reciting scripture, and when Zephyrine paused at the counter and said, carefully, "Quiet night," Denzil's eyes came up and held theirs for one full second — a look Zephyrine had seen a hundred times in the hospice, on the faces of relatives who knew the prognosis and were begging silently not to be asked to say it aloud.

"Aye," Denzil said. "Quiet."

That was all. But his hand shook slightly as he made change, and he watched Zephyrine leave through the shop glass, and Zephyrine felt the weight of that look between their shoulders all the way up the high street.

So Zephyrine did the only reasonable thing left. They went to the records.

* * *

St. Aldhelm's in Fenmarsh kept the Marrowfen registers — had kept them since the Marrowfen parish was consolidated in the fifties and its church deconsecrated, though everyone still called the roofless ruin "the chapel" the way you'd keep calling a dead man by his name. The vicar there, a soft, sweating man named Ilex who had taken the combined benefice and visibly regretted it, let Zephyrine into the vestry archive with the relief of someone handed an excuse to make tea.

"Family history?" he asked.

"Something like that." Zephyrine took the box marked MARROWFEN — PARISH GOODS & INVENTORY and carried it to the table under the vestry's one high window. Dust rose off the cardboard in a slow gold column. Somewhere in the church above, a pigeon shifted its weight on a beam.

The inventory books were fat ledgers bound in green buckram gone gray at the corners, their spines crackling like frost when opened. Zephyrine worked backward through the decades — 1919, 1915, 1912 — reading entries in a series of curate hands: *one altar cloth, linen, darned; six hymnals; the stove, cast iron, defective.* And then 1911, and a hand that pressed harder into the page than any before or after it:

*October the 9th, 1911. This day the great bell of Marrowfen chapel, cast Anno Domini 1742, weight eleven hundredweight and a quarter, was taken down and conveyed to the foundry at Thurlby and there melted for scrap, the proceeds (four pounds, two shillings) being applied to the relief fund for the fever widows. Let no bell hang in Marrowfen again.*

Let no bell hang in Marrowfen again.

Zephyrine read the line three times. Around them the vestry held its breath. The pigeon above shifted again, and its claws ticked on the beam like a small patient clock.

There it was, then. The bell was gone. Had been gone for sixty-odd years — melted down, sold by the pound, poured into whatever the war-shadowed foundries of 1911 were pouring metal into. There was no bell in Marrowfen. There had been no bell in Marrowfen within living memory, unless you counted Mrs. Osgood's memory, and Mrs. Osgood had been a child in 1911, and children remember bells the way they remember giants.

Zephyrine copied the entry into their notebook anyway, in their flat nurse's handwriting, beneath the columns of numbers they'd been logging for twenty years. *Vane, Aldous — began count at 90, died at 0, 6:12 a.m., exact.* Then, below it: *Bell removed 1911. Melted. No bell.*

The ink looked absurd sitting there among the death counts, like a joke told at a funeral. Zephyrine closed the notebook and sat for a while in the dust-colored light, feeling the first real crack open in the masonry of twenty years' denial — not a dramatic collapse, just a hairline, just a cold draft through a wall that had always been warm.

Because here was the thing Zephyrine had built their whole peace upon: everything strange in Marrowfen could be filed under tradition, and everything strange at the bedsides could be filed under neurology, and the two files never needed to touch. Salt on sills: tradition. Dying men counting to zero: neurology. But last night the files had touched. Last night something had rung in a village with no bell, and the village had answered with candles, and the answering had been the frightening part — because you don't light candles for a backfire. You light candles for a visitation. You light candles the way you'd set a place at a table for someone you owe money to.

"Found what you were after?" Ilex asked from the doorway, teacups rattling on a tray. He was looking at the open ledger, at the entry, and his face had done something complicated and then smoothed itself flat, the way faces do in Marrowfen.

"Just family history," Zephyrine said.

"Mm." He set the tray down and didn't pour. He stood with his hands hanging at his sides like a man who has forgotten what hands are for. "You know," he said, to the ledger rather than to Zephyrine, "when they consolidated the parishes, my predecessor told me there were boxes in this archive I was never to open, and boxes I was never to *not* open, and he never told me which was which." A small laugh escaped him, entirely without humor. "I've often thought that was the most honest instruction anyone's given me in this job."

He poured the tea after all, badly, overflowing one cup, and talked about the roof of the Fenmarsh nave until Zephyrine left.

* * *

The walk home took longer than it should have, because Zephyrine took the mill path around, avoiding the chapel yard without admitting to themselves that they were avoiding it — and then stopped, halfway along the muddy detour, furious at their own feet, and turned and cut directly across the grass toward the ruin.

The chapel stood open to the sky, roofless these ninety years, its walls furred with lichen, its windows empty sockets of glass-less tracery. Grass grew in the aisles. A rook nested in the tower stump. In daylight it was nothing — a picturesque decay, the kind of ruin tourists photographed in better-kept villages. Zephyrine walked up the nave through the knee-high grass and stood where the altar had been and listened.

Wind in the grass. Rooks arguing somewhere beyond the yew. The distant pump of a tractor on the fen. Ordinary sounds, all of them, each one arriving honestly through the ears.

And yet. Zephyrine laid a palm against the inner face of the east wall, and the stone was warm. Not sun-warm — the wall faced north, in shadow since noon — but warm the way a body is warm, with that suggestion of circulation beneath, of warmth generated rather than stored. Zephyrine snatched their hand back and stood breathing too fast, telling themselves about heat retention in massed masonry, thermal lag, geology, anything.

Beneath their feet, faintly, through the soles of their shoes, the ground seemed — very slightly, very slowly — to rise and fall.

Zephyrine did not stay to verify it. They walked home fast, taking the direct route this time, past Mrs. Osgood's cottage, where the old woman was watering her roses and called out, "You'll want your salt renewed soon, love. Michaelmas comes early some years."

It was June.

* * *

Night came down on Marrowfen the way it always did, in layers — first the birds going quiet all at once, as if a conductor had cut them off, then the streetlamps buzzing alight and dying on schedule at ten, then the shutters, the bolts, the salt lines renewed or not according to each household's private arithmetic of belief. Zephyrine sat up with Edith's tea service and the parish notebook and forced themselves to do the thing they had spent twenty years declining to do: think it through.

Twenty years of numbers. Ninety-counting Vane, dead at zero at 6:12. The woman in Leeds with the brain tumor who'd counted down from sixty for nine nights and gone at zero at 4:03. The old soldier who counted in French. The child — Zephyrine flinched away from the child — who had counted from such a small number, only seven, seven nights of *seven, six, five*, and Zephyrine had held the small hot hand and smiled and said *you're doing wonderfully* and hated themselves for the word *doing*, as if counting were a task, an errand, as if the child were running an errand for something.

Always exact. Every one. Twenty years of exactness, filed under coincidence, because the alternative filing was unthinkable.

And now a bell with no throat had tolled once, at 11:47, and a village had lit its candles in the dark and said nothing, and a stone wall had been warm, and the ground had breathed.

Zephyrine wrote in the notebook, under the inventory transcription, one line: *What is it counting toward?*

Then they crossed the line out. Then they wrote it again.

Across the green, in the window of number six, a candle bloomed — early tonight, hours before any toll, hours before anything had happened at all. Then the Hollis house. Then the Ketterleys'. One by one the windows of Marrowfen kindled their small obedient flames, and Zephyrine sat in the dark of Edith's kitchen watching them, unlit, unwilling, while the clock crawled toward midnight and the village held its breath around them.

At 11:47, the bell tolled twice.

Two notes, round and bronze and grief-heavy, rolling up out of the earth itself — Zephyrine would swear afterward they felt it come up through the floor, through the chair, through the bones of their own pelvis, as if the sound were being struck somewhere far below the chapel, below the foundations, below the fen — and the second note had not quite finished dying before every candle in Marrowfen guttered at once, all together, like a held breath released.

Zephyrine sat very still in the ringing dark.

Tomorrow, they told themselves, I will go and see Edith's things. The boxes in the attic I never unpacked. Whatever she left, whatever she wrote, whatever she gripped my wrist trying to say.

The candle in Mrs. Osgood's window relit itself. Or was relit. From this distance, in this dark, Zephyrine couldn't tell which — and understood, with a cold that started in the teeth and worked inward, that not knowing the difference was the whole of Marrowfen's condition, and had been for a very long time, and that they had been born into the middle of a sentence and were only now learning to hear the words.

Somewhere beneath the village, patient as water, something waited to see whether the third night would bring three tolls.

Zephyrine went to the attic stairs and put their hand on the rail, and climbed.
