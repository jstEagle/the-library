## 10. The Neighbor at the Window

The salt held. That was the first thing Zephyrine noticed when they woke — not the hour, not the cold pressing through the window glass like a palm laid flat against the pane, but the line of salt on the sill, undisturbed, a white seam of ordinary arithmetic in the dark. Edith's marginalia had been specific: *coarse salt, unbroken line, renewed each dusk.* Zephyrine had followed it the way they followed a doctor's orders, half faith and half habit, and now they lay in bed listening to their own heart and thinking: *it held, it held, it held* — a counting of their own, though theirs ran upward.

The clock on the mantel said 2:51. The cottage was the kind of cold that meant something had opened somewhere — a door left ajar in another room, a window unlatched in a house that had no reason to be generous with its warmth. Zephyrine knew cold. Twenty years of night shifts taught you the difference between a draft and a presence, between weather and intention. This was intention. It moved through the rooms like a slow tide, pooling at the foot of the bed, rising up the blanket's edge.

They did not get up. Not yet. There was a discipline to fear, if you'd practiced long enough — and Zephyrine had practiced twenty years of standing still while people died, of keeping their hands steady while numbers fell out of other mouths like coins from a torn pocket. So they lay still, and they breathed, and they waited for the thing in the dark to show itself, because it always showed itself eventually. The dying always told you what was coming. You only had to listen.

At 2:57, three minutes before the hour Mrs. Osgood would later be said to have appeared — though *appeared* was such a gentle word for it — Zephyrine heard gravel shift outside the front of the cottage. Not footsteps. Gravel does not crunch under feet; it grinds, it announces. This was softer than that. It was the sound of weight being placed down carefully, the way you set down something breakable, or something you intend to pick back up.

Then came the voice.

"Forty-one."

Zephyrine's whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the room. They knew that voice. They had known it since they were five years old, sitting cross-legged on a braided rug while an old woman traced letters in a sand tray and said, *the cat sat, love, can you find the cat?* Tam Osgood had taught half of Marrowfen its letters, its psalms, its manners. Her voice was the sound of the village's childhood — dry, warm, faintly amused, as if everything children did was slightly funnier than it was important.

"Forty," said the voice at the window. "Thirty-nine."

It was coming from the front bedroom window. Zephyrine's window. The one above the sink, the one that looked out over the lane toward the roofless chapel, the one whose sill wore its line of salt like a stitched wound.

They got up. They don't remember deciding to. The body has its own triage, its own protocols, and some part of them — the nurse part, the part that had never once refused a call bell no matter the hour — simply stood and crossed the cold floorboards and stood at the curtain with one hand raised to pull it back.

*Don't*, said some older, wiser part of them, the part that had spent two decades not asking what the counting was for.

But the voice was Tam's voice. And there are limits to what discipline can do against love.

Zephyrine pulled the curtain aside.

* * *

The moon was up and three-quarters full, and it gave the lane the flat silver light of an X-ray — everything visible, nothing alive. Frost furred the hedges although it was June. The streetlamp at the corner had died hours ago, the way the lamps always died now, all together, like a held breath released. And pressed against the window — not standing near it, not looking in, but *pressed against it*, forehead and palms and chest flattened to the glass as if the window were the surface of a pond and she had risen slowly from beneath it — was Mrs. Tam Osgood.

She was in her nightgown. The thin cotton one with the faded cornflowers, the one Zephyrine had helped wash last spring when her hands got too bad for the mangle. Her bare feet did not quite touch the ground. That was the first wrongness, and Zephyrine's eye — trained by twenty years of watching bodies fail — snagged on it and could not let go. She hung against the glass the way laundry hangs, supported by nothing, her toes pointing downward, six inches of dark air between her soles and the frost-silvered stones.

And she was smiling. That was the second wrongness. Tam Osgood had a face built for wryness, for the small skeptical twist she gave the world over her spectacles; she had never in her life worn an open smile, and she wore one now, wide and serene and perfectly symmetrical, and it did not fit her face the way a smile is supposed to fit. It fit the way a mask fits. It lay *on* her.

Because her face was smooth.

Zephyrine's breath fogged the glass and they wiped it with their sleeve without meaning to, needing the image clear, needing to be sure. But they were sure. The lines were gone. Every line. The crow's-feet, the deep furrows between the brows, the soft collapse of jowls, the creases around the mouth that decades of speaking had carved — all of it smoothed away, the skin taut and pale and poreless as the surface of a candle that has been lit and blown out too many times. She looked like a face remembered by someone who had never looked closely. She looked like a description of Tam Osgood rather than Tam Osgood herself. The features were all present and correctly arranged — the strong nose, the thin lips, the eyes behind spectacles she was somehow still wearing — but the texture of a life was missing. Something had taken her face off, and put it back on inside out, and the wearing of it was the horror: the way the smile pulled at skin that had forgotten how, the way the eyes crinkled where crow's-feet used to be, mimicking an expression the flesh no longer had the architecture to hold.

"Thirty-eight," said Tam Osgood's voice, muffled slightly by the glass, and perfectly, horribly clear.

Her lips moved with the words. Of course they did. Whatever stood at the window was not a recording; it was a performance, given in costume, by something that had learned the role from the inside.

"Thirty-seven." A pause, delicate, conversational. "You've salted your sill, Zeph. Edith would be proud. Edith was *always* proud of you, though she showed it poorly. Do you remember the summer of the wasps' nest? You were nine. She wouldn't let anyone smoke it out until you'd gone in to supper. She said the wasps had as much right to their house as we had to ours." The smooth face tilted, and the smile widened by a degree that the muscles of a living face could not have managed. "I remember that. I remember *everything*. That's the trouble, love. That's the whole trouble."

Zephyrine's hand found the windowsill. The salt bit into their palm, a small bright pain, and they were grateful for it. Pain was a number you could hold onto.

"Mrs. Osgood," they said, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "Tam. If you can hear me — go home. Go home, Tam."

The smile didn't flicker. But behind the spectacles, the eyes — Tam's eyes, grey and quick, or the memory of them rendered in something glossier — tracked sideways, then back, like a person reading.

"Oh, I'm *at* home, dear. That's the joke of it. That's the cruel, clever joke of it." The voice dropped, confiding, warm, the voice she'd used over tea and seed cake when she wanted to tell you something scandalous about the flower show. "It doesn't take us out of ourselves, you know. Everyone assumes that. Everyone thinks being taken means going *somewhere else*. But it's the opposite. It comes *in*. It puts us on like a coat and it finds all our pockets full already. Everything I ever knew is still in here, Zeph. Your name. Your grandmother's name. The words to every hymn I ever bawled out at forty children. The smell of the schoolroom ink. The way your mother cried at her wedding and pretended it was the wind." The eyes glistened. Whether it was grief or imitation of grief, Zephyrine could not tell, and that inability was its own wound. "It keeps us because it likes the taste of us. Memories are how it eats. And it is *so hungry*, love. It has been so hungry since 1911."

"Thirty-six," the voice added, almost as an afterthought, resuming the count the way a clock resumes ticking after someone has spoken over it.

Zephyrine pressed their hand flat against the inside of the glass. It was a stupid gesture, a nurse's gesture, the gesture they made through isolation-glass doors, the gesture that said *I'm here, you're not alone.* Their palm met the cold where Tam's forehead rested, separated by four millimetres of glass and a hundred years of silence kept.

"Who took you?" they asked. "Was it the bell? Did the bell—"

"The bell doesn't take anyone." For just a moment the serenity cracked, and underneath it was something old and tired and frightened, and Zephyrine would have sworn on any book you liked that it was genuinely Tam — the real woman, surfacing through whatever wore her, like a drowning person breaking water for one gasp. "The bell only *counts*. It counts what's owed. It's the tallyman's finger tapping the ledger. It's the debt that takes us, Zeph, the debt your family signed — oh, don't look like that, Edith signed it out of love, they all signed it out of love, that's the filthiest trick of it, love is the currency—" The gasp went under. The smoothness closed over the face again like pond ice healing. When the voice resumed it was pleasant, unhurried, a teacher's voice at the end of a lesson. "Thirty-five. You should write this down, dear. You were always my best pupil. Thirty-four. Nine names a generation. One circle. One keeper. Thirty-three. Ask yourself why the fever stopped in 1911 and never came back, not once, not in Marrowfen, while every village around lost its children by the dozen. Ask what was paid. Thirty-two. Ask who's paying now."

Each number landed like a stone dropped down a well — Zephyrine heard it fall a long way before it struck anything.

"You're counting down," they said. "What happens at zero?"

The thing wearing Tam Osgood regarded them with infinite patience, infinite fondness, and no humanity whatsoever.

"Why, the same thing that always happens at zero, dear. Collection." The smile stretched. Somewhere beneath the skin of her throat, visibly, something swallowed. "Though I'll tell you a secret, since it's you, since I taught you your letters, since I held your hand crossing the ford when you were six and terrified of the water — I'll tell you what none of the others understood yet." The eyes came very close to the glass, and the moonlight filled them, and they were empty the way a well is empty: deep, cold, and echoing. "Zero isn't the end of the counting. Zero is the beginning of the *taking*. The tolls mark the days. The counts mark the mouths. And when the roster fills — nine, Zeph, it wants nine, it has always wanted nine — then the bell rings the tenth time, and the tenth time isn't a number at all. It's a *name*. It's been practicing yours."

The frost on the outside of the glass spread outward from where her forehead touched it, ferning across the pane in white feathers, and through the growing lace of ice Zephyrine watched Tam Osgood's mouth form the next number.

"Thirty-one."

"Stop," Zephyrine whispered. "Please. Tam. Stop counting."

Something happened then that Zephyrine would spend the rest of the night turning over, the way the tongue returns to a broken tooth. The smooth face stilled. The smile remained, but the eyes changed — and for perhaps five seconds, five seconds hanging in the frozen air like drops about to fall, the thing at the window was not performing. The eyes welled. The mouth trembled around the shape of a word that was not a number, and the word, silent, formed clearly against the glass, letter by letter, the way Tam Osgood had once formed letters in a sand tray for a five-year-old:

*D-O-N-'-T L-E-T I-T F-I-N-I-S-H M-E.*

Then the water broke over her again, and she was serene, and she was smiling, and she began, softly, sweetly, in the voice that had taught a village to read, to sing:

"Thirty. Twenty-nine. Twenty-eight. Twenty-seven..."

She did not turn away. She rose — there was no other word for it, she simply ascended, nightgown drifting, feet trailing, the way smoke rises up a flue — lifting past the top of the window frame, past the eaves, into the silver dark, still counting, the numbers falling gently down through the frost-laced glass like petals from a tree that had died standing up.

"...twenty-six... twenty-five... twenty-four..."

Zephyrine stood at the window until the voice faded below hearing, which took far longer than physics should have allowed, and then stood there longer, hand still pressed to the ice-feathered pane, feeling the cold of it work into the bones of the palm.

* * *

Dawn came grey and late, the way it had come all week, the sun a smudge of brass behind cloud like a coin at the bottom of a dirty well. Zephyrine had not slept. They had made tea they did not drink and sat at the kitchen table with Edith's ledger open in front of them, and at some point — they could not say when — they had begun writing, on the back of a page of Edith's careful columns, everything the thing at the window had said. *Nine names a generation. One circle. One keeper. The roster fills. The tenth toll is a name.* Their handwriting grew smaller and harder as it went, pressing through the paper, as if the words might escape the page if they weren't pinned there.

When the light was strong enough, they walked up the lane to Tam Osgood's cottage.

The door stood open. Inside, the kettle was cold on a cold hob, and her spectacles — the real ones, the ones with the mended left arm wrapped in thread — lay folded on the table beside a cup of tea gone grey with standing, untouched, as if she had stepped out a moment ago and might step back in and complain about the state of it. On the floor by her chair, arranged with terrible neatness, lay a ring of small wet stones.

Zephyrine did not touch them. They stood in the doorway of the woman who had taught them to read, in the smell of candle smoke and something underneath it — green, stagnant, ancient — the smell of pond water, and they understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the stones were a signature, a receipt, a little model of a chapel built by something that wanted them to know exactly where Tam Osgood had gone.

Nine names a generation. One circle.

Zephyrine walked home fast, the ledger clutched to their chest, and did not stop until they were inside with the door barred and the salt renewed. Then they sat down, turned to the page of circled names — generation after generation, one name ringed in Edith's fading ink — and read them properly for the first time, aloud, in the empty kitchen, their voice shaking.

Every circled name was a Crane.

And beside the last one, dated 1973, in handwriting gone thin and hurried, Edith had written a single line that Zephyrine had read a dozen times before and never understood until this morning, with the frost still melting on the glass and Tam's voice still counting somewhere behind the walls of the world:

*Hold the door from the inside, and do not sleep till sunup. Six hours. No more, no less. God forgive me — I couldn't manage it, but perhaps the child will.*
