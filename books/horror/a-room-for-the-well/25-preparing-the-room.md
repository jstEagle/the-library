## 25. Preparing the Room

The loft above the chapel had not been swept in seventy-six years.

Zephyrine knew the number the way they now knew all numbers — by their weight, their terrible exactness. Grandfather Crane had opened his door at hour six in 1949, and no one had climbed up since. The dust lay thick as felt, grey and soft, holding the shapes of things long gone: a chair's four legs printed like fossils, a rectangle where a Bible had lain face-down, and — this was the one that stopped Zephyrine's broom mid-stroke — a small oval pressed deep into the floorboards near the window, as if someone had knelt there for a very long time, or lain there, curled, waiting for something to be over.

They swept anyway. It seemed important that the room be clean. Whatever else the next night was going to be, it would not be squalid. Zephyrine had spent twenty years making death tidy — straightening blankets, wetting lips, turning faces toward windows so the last light fell kindly. This was the same work. That was the thought they kept returning to, the one that let their hands keep moving: *this is the same work, only the patient is yourself.*

* * *

They came each morning with a basket, and each morning the walk from the cottage to the chapel grew stranger. Marrowfen in daylight had always been plain — limestone and slate, hedgerows going to seed, the reservoir flat and silver beyond the ridge like a coin someone had dropped and couldn't pick up. Now it looked rehearsed. Curtains twitched in patterns too regular to be curiosity. Salt lines had appeared overnight on doorsteps Zephyrine hadn't fortified, copied from Edith's marginalia or from some older memory, the grains poured in careful unbroken rings. People nodded to each other across the lane and did not stop. Conversations happened in doorways, brief as birdsong, and everyone glanced at the chapel roofline the way you glance at a bruise — to check whether it has spread.

Nobody said the word *volunteer*. They didn't have to. After the schoolhouse vote, after the whole room of frightened faces had turned like compass needles toward the one Crane still standing, the word lived in the air of the village the way damp lives in stone. Zephyrine walked through it daily. It settled on their shoulders. They declined to brush it off.

Let them think what they liked. Let Father Ilex wring his hands and call it martyrdom, let old Cobb the farrier mutter that Cranes had always been either saints or fools and never could tell which. Zephyrine had made their decision somewhere between the well-bricks warm as a sleeping animal and the phone call to Leeds where they'd managed only weather — and decisions, once truly made, stopped feeling like courage. They felt like chores. There was a room to prepare. So they prepared it.

* * *

The water first. A jug, stoneware, blue-banded, bought from the hardware shop where Mr. Pell rang it up without comment and then, as Zephyrine turned to go, said quietly: "My mother sat with your grandmother, the winter she kept her vigil. Said Edith Crane asked for two things beforehand. A comb, and someone to remember her middle name."

"Did she get them?"

"She got the comb." Pell looked down at the till. "Nobody could recall the name. She laughed about it after. Said it was the first thing the well took, and it took it gently, like picking a thread." He slid the jug across the counter. "No charge. And Zeph — take more water than you think. Six hours is longer than six hours, in there."

Zephyrine took three jugs. They set them in the loft's northeast corner, away from the window, where frost wouldn't reach them first if it came again. June frost. They'd woken twice now to ferns of ice feathering the inside of the cottage glass, blooming and melting by seven as though embarrassed. The entity was hurrying; the tolls were coming wrong, off-schedule, eight where nine should stand, dawn where midnight belonged. Time in Marrowfen had begun to fray at the edges like old cloth. Which meant the vigil might not wait for the proper debt-night. Which meant the room needed to be ready *now*, tonight, every night, the way a labouring woman's bag sits packed by the door for weeks.

Zephyrine knew about waiting for births. They were learning about waiting for everything else.

* * *

The ledger went on the sill beneath the east window, where the first light would find it. Edith's handwriting covered its pages in that upright, unhurried script — salt receipts, nail counts, the names of the nine struck through generation after generation, and the final page with its floor plan inked so carefully, each wall measured, the little cell labeled in her hand: *the room for the well.*

Zephyrine had read it eleven times. They read it a twelfth while sitting cross-legged on the swept boards, dust motes turning gold around them, and found things the eleventh reading had missed. In the margin beside the floor plan, small as an apology: *Bring nothing you cannot bear to lose, and lose it before you arrive.* And beneath that, fainter, the pencil nearly ghosted: *It will offer you back whatever hurts most. Refusing is the whole of the work.*

So Zephyrine spent an afternoon losing things in advance. Not destroying — Edith hadn't said destroy — but setting apart, carrying each precious thing out to the cottage garden and burying the ache of it somewhere the well-thing's long fingers couldn't follow. They gave Nell's childhood letters to Mrs. Danby two lanes over, wrapped in brown paper, with instructions to hold them until called for. They left the photograph album — the real one, the heavy leather one with Martin in it, Martin laughing on the pier at Whitby with gulls wheeling behind him like punctuation — locked in the hospice office drawer, under the rota sheets where no uncanny thing would think to look. Ten years dead and his face already going soft at the edges when Zephyrine reached for it; they could not afford to feed that softness to something hungry. Better it blur on its own honest terms than be sharpened into bait.

What remained, they carried up the loft ladder in a biscuit tin.

Three photographs, chosen coldly, chosen as provisions rather than treasures. Nell at eight, gap-toothed on a garden wall, because a face that young carried no hooks in it. The hospice staff, Christmas past, all of them squinting against the flash, because those were people who would still exist tomorrow regardless of what happened in this room, and tomorrow was an anchor. And one of Edith — young Edith, taken before any of it, standing outside this very chapel in a coat too big for her, squinting into a sun seventy years gone. Zephyrine studied her a long time. The jaw was theirs. The way she held her chin slightly lifted, as if listening for something just above the horizon — that was theirs too. *You negotiated,* Zephyrine thought at the photograph. *You amended the terms and died believing I might manage what grandfather couldn't. I haven't decided whether to thank you or be furious. Perhaps both. Perhaps both is what inheritance means.*

They stood the photograph facing the bed. Then, remembering the marginalia — *nothing you cannot bear to lose* — they turned it face-down, and found that this too was bearable, and understood that this was the entire discipline in miniature: love, handled carefully, edges taped.

* * *

The bar took the better part of a day.

Edith's plan called for iron — a length of it driven through the door itself, sliding into sockets sunk in the frame, so that the door could not be opened from without even if the hinges surrendered, even if the wood forgot it was wood. Zephyrine bought the iron from Cobb, who forged it fresh without being asked why, hammer falling in the blue evening light of his forge with a steadiness that said he knew exactly why and would not insult either of them by naming it. "Six-foot," he said, wrapping it in sacking. "Overlong. Cut it down or leave it proud. Proud's better, I'd say. Something that sticks out past the edge of a thing looks like it means business."

Drilling the holes was the worst work Zephyrine had done in years, and they had held hands through every manner of leaving. The loft door was oak, older than the parish register, dense as bone, and the brace-and-bit fought them for every inch. Sweat ran into their eyes. Splinters worked under the skin of their palms and stayed there, small bright grievances. Twice they stopped, arms shaking, forehead resting against the cool wood of the door, and both times they became aware of a silence below them — the undercroft silence, the bricked-well silence — attentive, patient, *listening to the carpentry*. The thing beneath had waited since 1911. It could watch a woman drill holes in a door and feel, presumably, whatever it felt instead of amusement.

"Go on and watch," Zephyrine said aloud, surprising themselves. Their voice sounded strange in the empty loft, human and small. "Watch me make it harder for you. Watch me measure the sockets twice. This is what preparation looks like from the inside."

The bricks below, as far as anyone knew, said nothing. But that afternoon the temperature in the loft dropped four degrees, and stayed dropped, and Zephyrine worked the rest of the day in their coat.

By dusk the bar sat proud of the door's edge, black iron through old oak, sliding smooth into its sockets with a sound like a word being finished. Zephyrine tested it five times. Slid home, the door became less a door than a section of wall that had briefly entertained other ideas. They stood looking at it, palms burning, splinters throbbing, and felt something they were careful not to name in case naming it invited correction: satisfaction. Grim, domestic, hard-won. The satisfaction of a made bed, a filled pantry, a latched gate before weather.

Love expressed as provisioning. That was all any of this was, in the end. Twenty years of nursing had taught them that nobody can be saved from dying, but everybody can be made ready for it — and readiness was a language, and it said *you matter* louder than any word. So they provisioned themselves the way they'd provisioned a hundred dying rooms. Water. Light. A chair by the window. Someone's face turned toward the sun.

* * *

The mirror was forbidden. They kept it anyway.

Edith's marginalia was unambiguous on the point — *no glass, no reflection, the well sees through silvered surfaces and shows you what it sees* — and Zephyrine had obeyed it for the mirror in the cottage bathroom, covering it with a towel these three nights, washing by touch and memory of their own face. But on the fourth morning, gathering provisions, they stood in the charity shop on Fore Street and looked at a small oval mirror in a tarnished frame, price tag fifty pence, and heard their own voice say: *I'll want to know what I look like. At the end. However it ends. I'll want to have seen my own face recently, with my own eyes, while they were still mine.*

That wasn't defiance. Or it was, but the honest kind — the kind that knows the rule and breaks it knowingly, paying the toll of it in advance. Zephyrine had watched patients refuse morphine for exactly this reason. *I want to feel what's happening to me. It's mine, even the bad of it. Especially the bad of it.*

They hung the mirror inside the wardrobe door, so it could be shut away, so that looking at it would always be a choice made deliberately, door opened, glass uncovered, eyes met. Some doors close from this side too. Some open from this side as well. The distinction was going to matter, in the hours when everything blurred.

* * *

On the sixth evening the room was done, and Zephyrine stood in the middle of it taking inventory the way they'd taught new nurses to take inventory: aloud, slow, touching each thing.

Water, three jugs, northeast corner. Ledger, east sill, photograph face-down beside it. Blanket — Edith's own, moth-holed, smelling of cedar and decades, brought from the cottage chest because if the blood remembered anything useful it would remember being wrapped in this. Candle, twelve of them, beeswax, because paraffin spat and tallow stank and if Zephyrine was going to sit a six-hour vigil in a haunted loft they would do it by decent light. The comb Mr. Pell's mother had mentioned — Zephyrine had bought one, absurdly, a tortoiseshell thing, and put it in the tin without examining why until now: because Edith had asked for one, and some requests you honor retroactively, across death, just to be kind. A clock — wind-up, loud-ticking, because the vigil's whole architecture was hours, and Zephyrine wanted to hear each one arrive and depart like stations on a line. The iron bar, seated. The mirror, shuttered. The chair, angled to catch sunrise through the east window, because the terms ended at sunup and Zephyrine intended to meet the terms looking directly at them.

It was, they realized, standing there in the candle-smelling dusk, a sickroom. A beautiful, doomed, lovingly appointed sickroom, and the patient was Zephyrine Crane, and the nurse was also Zephyrine Crane, and for the first time in twenty years the counting at the center of the room would be their own.

They sat down in the chair and let that land. Let it hurt properly, once, cleanly, the way you let a stitch be pulled. Then they got up, trimmed the candle wicks, checked the bar, drank half a cup of the water to confirm it was good, and climbed down the ladder into the ordinary dark.

* * *

The walk home was quiet in the new way Marrowfen had learned to be quiet — not peaceful but held, like a breath. The streetlamps died at ten as they always did. Zephyrine counted the lit windows on the way: four. Two nights ago it had been nine.

At the cottage door they paused. Frost had come early again, silvering the hedge, and the air smelled of pond water — that smell, Osgood's smell, the signature the enemy left instead of bodies. Zephyrine stood very still and listened to the village breathe, and beneath the breathing, far off toward the chapel, heard — or believed they heard, which by now amounted to the same — a single low tone, sustained, like a bell deciding.

Not tolling. Deciding.

Nine villagers had stood in the square yesterday, counting in unison, serene as churchgoers. Tam among them, Tam who had taught Zephyrine to read, her mouth moving through numbers with a smile that belonged to something wearing her. The roster was complete. The arithmetic was finished. All that remained between Marrowfen and collection was one barred door, one tired nurse, and however many hours the thing beneath chose to grant.

Zephyrine went inside, salted the threshold, and wound the spare clock — the one they'd keep by their own bed, ticking toward whatever midnight the bell finally chose.

Outside, at the edge of hearing, the tone resolved itself, sweet and dreadful, into the first stroke of nine.
