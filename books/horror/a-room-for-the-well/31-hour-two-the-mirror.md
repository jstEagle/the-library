## 31. Hour Two: The Mirror

The lamp burned with a steadiness Zephyrine did not trust.

Everything else in the room had begun to move — or seemed to; it was difficult, by then, to draw the line between what the eye reported and what the ear suggested. The bell's voice had not stopped. It had passed through tolling into something else entirely, a continuous brazen note that was less a sound than a pressure, the way deep water presses on the eardrum, the way a held breath presses on the ribs. Zephyrine had been sitting with it for the better part of an hour now, and had learned that the only way to bear it was to stop calling it a sound. It was weather. It was the room itself, resonating.

Hour two. The small brass alarm clock — wound tight, its tick deliberately set against the bell's drone — read 1:14 in the morning.

Zephyrine sat on the chair facing the barred door, hands flat on their knees, and recited names in the silence behind the teeth. *Marion Pettibone. Harold Voss. The little Ferrier girl, Ada, who liked her hair braided in two and not one. Mr. Aldous Vane, who counted to ninety and always landed exactly.* Twenty years of patients, filed and kept. The names were ballast. The names were stones in a pocket on a river crossing.

The room around them was small and deliberately plain: the sloped ceiling of the chapel loft, one narrow window with the shutter drawn, a cot they would not lie down on, the water jug, the ledger, the photographs in a tin. And, propped against the wall where Zephyrine had placed it against all sense, the mirror.

Edith's marginalia had been explicit. *Never a glass in the room. It does not show you; it shows it you.* Zephyrine had read that line four times, had felt the old nurse's stubbornness in it — the same tone Zephyrine used with families who wanted to hide the morphine — and had brought the mirror anyway.

Because a room with no mirror is a room where you cannot check your own face. And Zephyrine had spent twenty years watching faces for the change that comes before the end, and could not bear, on this of all nights, to be unwatched.

That was the honest reason. There was another, less honest, that they were not yet ready to say aloud, even inside: that a thing which shows you what *it* sees might also show you what it wants. And Zephyrine had spent two decades not asking what the counting was for, and had survived by not asking, and some stubborn splinter of them — the same splinter that had walked into the undercroft, that had answered the well in Edith's voice with their own name — still believed that looking was a kind of armor.

The bell held its note. The clock ticked. Zephyrine recited.

*Vane, Aldous. Osgood, Tam — no. Not Tam. Not yet. Strike her. Strike her and grieve later.* The grief kept trying to get in. It came the way cold comes under a door, along the floor, at ankle height, and Zephyrine kept stepping away from it. *Hollis, Peter. Crane, Edith. Crane, Edith. Crane—*

The lamp guttered.

Not the way lamps gutter when there is a draught — the shutter was fastened, the seams stuffed with wool. It guttered the way a voice gutters when the speaker forgets, for a moment, what they were saying. The flame shrank to a blue bead, hung there, and then stood up again, steady, as if nothing had happened. The room was half a shade darker afterward, though. Or the air was. Zephyrine could not have said which.

They looked at the clock. 1:19. Five minutes had gone somewhere without their permission.

*Names,* they told themselves. *Stay in the names.*

*Voss, Harold. Pettibone, Marion. The twins at the reservoir — no, don't. Don't count them. Don't think about the water, don't think about how the counting didn't stop even under—*

The mirror moved.

Not fell. Not slid. *Moved* — a small adjustment, the way a sleeper shifts, the way a cat settles its weight when it decides to keep watching you. It had been propped at a slight angle against the wall; it was now propped at a slightly different one, facing the chair more directly. Zephyrine was almost certain. Almost. The almost was the worst part, because certainty could be acted on and almost could only be sat with, and sitting with things was what the whole night was made of.

They did not look at it. They looked at the door instead — the good oak door, barred from within with the iron-shod beam Edith's plan had specified, the nails driven deep, the bar seated in its brackets with a carpenter's care Zephyrine had not known they possessed until they were driving the last of them. Six hours. Hold the door from inside. Do not sleep. Do not speak. Do not *remember aloud* — that phrase had been underlined twice in Edith's hand, and Zephyrine had thought, in the daylight of preparation, that they understood it.

They were beginning, in the dark, to understand it differently.

Because remembering aloud did not only mean speaking. It meant letting the memory come up the throat of you, into the face, into the eyes. It meant *showing* a memory to the room. And the room, Zephyrine was learning, was a throat too.

The bell's note deepened. Or the floor deepened under it. Zephyrine felt the vibration change character — less in the ears now, more in the sternum, in the fluid of the spine, a note that was being played on the building itself, on the bones of the chapel and the bones of the hill it stood on, and under the hill, under the undercroft, under the bricked and breathing well, the thing that had been alone since 1911 turned over in its sleep and listened.

*It's listening for the remembering,* Zephyrine thought. *It can't take what I don't hold up to it.*

They thought of the patients. Of the way the dying, in the last hours, would sometimes turn their faces toward a corner of the ceiling and speak — names, mostly, or fragments, or that counting — and how Zephyrine had always read it as the mind loosening its moorings. Now they understood it as a room filling with listeners. The dying were not confused. The dying were being *attended*.

*Pettibone, Marion. Hollis, Peter. Vane, Aldous.* The names were getting heavier, like wet rope. Each one came up trailing its whole weight of scene and scent and hour: Marion Pettibone's hands, liver-spotted and fine, folding a paper crane she never finished; Harold Voss asking, at the very end, in a voice of perfect clarity, *is my mother in the room?* and Zephyrine saying *yes* because it was kinder, because the room had been full of something, even then, and perhaps it had been kinder and true.

*Don't show them,* Zephyrine warned themselves. *Hold them flat. Hold them like papers in wind.*

The mirror moved again.

This time there was no almost about it. The glass shifted against the plaster with a small dry sound — the sound of a frame finding its footing — and settled facing the chair, facing Zephyrine, squarely, the way a person turns to face you when they have decided to stop pretending to look out the window.

Zephyrine's whole body went to iron. Do not look at it. Look at the clock. 1:26. Look at the door. Look at the bar. Count the nails — twelve in the bar brackets, you drove them, you know them, count them — one, two, three—

The glass caught the lamplight and held it wrong. That was the thing about mirrors, Zephyrine thought with a strange, floating clarity; that was the thing no one said. A mirror is a door that has been painted shut, and the paint is your own face, and the paint is always slightly thinner than you believe.

In the glass, the lamplight did not flicker with the flame. It burned still. And around its stillness, the reflection of the room was — changing.

Zephyrine looked. God help them, they looked.

The room in the mirror was the same room. Same sloped ceiling, same cot, same jug, same barred door. But the walls were wrong. The walls were *covered*. Every inch of plaster, floor to eaves, was inscribed with marks — small, dense, regular marks, ranked in columns, in thousands of columns, tens of thousands, the way a prison wall is notched and the way no prison wall has ever been notched, because no prison has stood long enough. Counted days. Counted hours. Counted *counts* — Zephyrine understood that with a certainty that arrived whole and cold: each mark was a number someone had reached, a zero someone had landed on, a backward journey someone had completed under this roof or under the hill beneath it, and the walls of the room as the other one saw it were papered with the arithmetic of everything it had ever collected.

And the walls were not flat.

In the mirror, the plaster of the walls bulged — gently, here and there, the way a wall bulges behind wallpaper when the plaster beneath has given way — and each bulge had a shape, and each shape, as Zephyrine's eye traveled the glass with the horrible traction of a dream, resolved into a face. Pressed into the plaster from the other side. The way a face presses into a window from outside. The way a face presses into fabric.

They were all different and they were all patient. An old man with a farmer's weathered creases, his features smoothed as if the plaster had taken him softly. A young woman whose mouth was open on a word she had been saying for a long time. A man in a vicar's collar — no. Not Ilex. Older. A vicar from another century, his collar a white smudge. And near the door, where the light fell best, a woman with her hair in a coil at the nape and her chin lifted at an angle that Zephyrine's body recognized before their mind consented to.

Edith.

Her face was pressed into the wall of the room as the thing saw it, and she was not screaming, and she was not serene. She was *attentive*. She was looking, in the mirror, at Zephyrine — or the wall she was in was looking — with the expression of a woman standing at a platform watching a train she has put someone on, wanting, desperately, to be sure they have their ticket.

Zephyrine's breath went in and did not come out.

The face in the plaster moved. Not much. The lips — Edith's lips, the lips Zephyrine had kissed goodnight at six years old, flour-dusted, from the bakery run — the lips parted, and the wall around them flexed like skin around a speaking mouth, and though no sound crossed out of the mirror, Zephyrine's mind supplied the word with terrible fluency, because the shape of it was the shape of a name, and the name was *Zephyrine*, and it was being said the way Edith had always said it: all four syllables, unhurried, the *ph* soft as breath, a name pronounced by someone who had chosen it and loved it and was now, from inside a wall, asking—

*Don't.*

The word rose in Zephyrine's throat with its own momentum. *Don't* — the whole sentence behind it, *don't, Grandmother, don't, I'm here, I'm holding it, tell me what to do* — and Zephyrine felt the sentence gather like a sneeze, like a sob, like water finding the crack, felt the jaw begin the small treacherous loosening that precedes speech, and understood in one white flash what Edith's underlined phrase had meant all along.

*Do not remember aloud.*

Because the moment they spoke to her — the moment they answered her — the memory would come up whole. The bakery. The flour. The smell of yeast and coal smoke and the winter she'd walked them home under one umbrella and told them nothing, told them *nothing*, all those years, carrying it alone the way a keeper carries a door. It would all come up the throat and into the face and the room would *take it*, the room would drink it out of the air, and Zephyrine would be left with a wall where a grandmother had been, and the thing beneath the chapel would have Edith's voice — the real one, the living one, the one it had only ever been able to counterfeit — and it would never need to counterfeit anything again.

Zephyrine bit down.

They bit down on the inside of the cheek, hard, and the pain came up bright and clean and animal, and they rode it, eyes streaming, jaw locked, the sentence dying in their mouth in pieces — *don't, Grand—* — and swallowed. Swallowed it the way you swallow a fever. The taste of blood, faint and coppery, and beneath the blood the ghost of the word they had not said.

In the mirror, Edith's pressed face closed its mouth.

The expression did not change to disappointment. That was the unbearable part. It changed to something like *approval* — the look of a teacher when the child finally holds the pencil right — and then the whole wall of faces in the glass shifted, all together, a slow ripple passing through the plaster like wind through wheat, and every face turned, all at once, toward the door.

Toward the barred door. From the inside of the mirror, they all looked at the door as if it were the interesting thing, as if the door were what they were all waiting behind, as if Zephyrine had it backwards — as if it were not the room that was walled with the kept, but the kept who were walled in with the room, and the door was the only way anyone had ever—

*No.*

Zephyrine stood. The chair scraped. The sound was enormous in the bell's held breath.

They crossed to the mirror in four steps, took it by the frame with both hands — the wood was cold, cold as the well bricks, cold as pond water — and turned it face to the wall. Then, because Edith's marginalia had not covered this and Zephyrine's own hands decided on their own authority, they dragged the cot across it, and the tin of photographs on top of that, and stood with their palms flat against the cot's frame, breathing, until the shaking slowed.

Behind them, the room was only a room. Lamp, wall, shuttered window, door, bar. The bell held its note. The clock said 1:38.

*Hour two,* Zephyrine thought. *Two down. It saves its best for the hours when the body wants to sleep. It knows the body. It has been learning bodies since 1911.*

They went back to the chair. They did not sit in it so much as lower themselves into it, joint by joint, the way you lower a patient who has fainted. The cheek throbbed where the teeth had gone in. Good. Pain was a name too. *Pain, mine, now, this hour* — it anchored.

*Names.* They started over, from the beginning, from the very first patient, the old man on the Fen Road in their first year, whose name they had to dig for — *Garrett. Ambrose Garrett. Ninety-one, the winter of the bad chest* — and the digging was good, the digging was work, and work was a wall of its own.

The lamp stood steady. The cot stood against the mirror. The bell held its long brazen breath, and beneath it — so far beneath that Zephyrine might have invented it, and could no longer be sure of the difference — something turned over again, and listened, and waited with the patience of a thing that had waited through a hundred years of keepers and had learned, at last, exactly where the seams in a person run.

The clock ticked toward 1:50.

And from below — faint, through the floorboards, through the stone, through the hill — came the sound of the nine in the square, counting in unison, and for the first time their numbers were not descending.

They were counting *up*.
