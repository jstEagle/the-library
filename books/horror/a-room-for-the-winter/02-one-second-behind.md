## 2. One Second Behind

The lag did not frighten him at first. That was the thing Coral would remember afterward, when he understood what he had been living inside: the mirrors had been gentle with him, the way a trap is gentle, the way snow is gentle coming down over a snare until the weight of it closes.

On his second morning in the Ravensworth house he stood before the tall glass in the front hall, buttoning his coat, and noticed that his reflection's hand moved a half-beat after his own. He tested it the way a folklorist tests anything — patiently, repeatedly, with notes. He raised his right arm; the mirror raised its left a moment late. He blinked; the reflected eyes closed on a delay so slight it might have been his imagination, except that his imagination had never once been this precise. He wrote in his field journal: *Hall mirror, delay approx. one second. Cause: temperature? Old silvering? Suggests double-paned construction or —* and there he stopped, because there was no honest explanation to suggest next, and he was too good a scholar to write down a lie just to have a sentence end.

He told himself it was the cold. Old houses breathed; old glass warped; old mercury sank and pooled and dragged. He repeated this to himself like a charm, which was fitting, because by then he had begun to understand that everything in Hollowmere was a charm of one kind or another.

* * *

By the third day the delay had become anticipation.

He was in the kitchen, heating water for tea, when the clouded pane of the old cabinet mirror — a small thing, foxed with age, meant more for decoration than use — showed the pantry door swinging open. He watched it happen in the glass: the door moving inward, darkness beyond it, the hem of a coat stirring at the threshold. Then he turned, and the actual door was shut, still, silent. The kettle ticked as it warmed. Nothing moved in the house at all.

Coral set down his cup and walked to the pantry and opened the door himself, deliberately, so that the room and the reflection would agree again. Inside were jars of preserves gone dark with age, a broom, a smell of dust and something sweeter underneath, like apples long past rotting into memory. He stood in the doorway a long time, feeling the house attend to him. That was the only word for it. *Attend.*

That evening the stairwell mirror showed him a figure on the landing above — a shape in old-fashioned clothes, standing where the light from the window fell short, hands folded, head inclined as if listening to something below. Coral spun around. The landing was empty. When he looked back, the figure was still there in the glass, and it took another full second before the mirror remembered to be empty too.

He did not sleep well. He slept, though, and that seemed worse somehow — that his body could settle into this house's rhythms so easily, as if some part of him had always known them.

* * *

In daylight he did what he knew how to do: he worked.

The study on the ground floor held the house's papers — decades of them, boxed and shelved by his great-grandfather Edmund Ravensworth with an archivist's care that Coral recognized as kinship. Correspondence. Deeds. Receipts for lamp oil and salt pork going back past the turn of the century. And ledgers — many ledgers, bound in green cloth, their spines dated in a small copperplate hand.

He began with the earliest and read forward, taking notes, building the ordinary skeleton of a family's life: harvest accounts, wages paid to a gardener named Tull who appears for forty years without ever aging in the sums, purchases of black wool by the yard in suspiciously regular quantities. It was absorbing work. It was *safe* work. For whole hours he forgot the mirrors, and the house let him forget, patient as winter.

It was in the ledger dated 1891 that he found the first column that didn't balance.

Under the heading **WINTER GUESTS**, in Edmund's careful hand:

*Mr. Alistair Finch, surveyor — stayed on.*
*Mrs. Ida Prewitt, schoolmistress — stayed on.*
*A gentleman passing through, name unknown — stayed on.*

Three names. Three entries. And beside each, struck through with a single firm line, the way one cancels a debt paid in full.

Coral sat very still. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled with a sound like a sigh.

He pulled the next ledger. 1892: two guests, stayed on, struck through. 1893: one. There were gaps — years with no entries at all, and those gaps frightened him more than the entries, because he could not tell if they meant no guests came, or that the record-keeper had grown tired of pretending it was bookkeeping. In one margin, beside the year 1904, Edmund had written a single line in pencil, faint as breath:

*The house does not ask twice. Better we choose than it chooses.*

Coral read it four times. His own handwriting, three generations removed, looked back at him from the page — the same narrow letters, the same habit of pressing too hard on the downstrokes. He had spent his career collecting other people's songs and stories, cataloguing the ways communities explain themselves to themselves, and he knew — had always known, in the professional part of his mind — that behind every gentle custom there was usually something with teeth. He had simply never expected to find the teeth in his own mouth.

Because that was the thing he could not look away from, the thing that made his hands go cold on the ledger's edge: Edmund Ravensworth had not been a victim of whatever Hollowmere did each winter. He had been its *clerk*. Its steward. The lists were tidy. The cancellations were neat. This was a man balancing accounts, and the account was human beings, and the family name on the spine of every ledger was his own.

*Stayed on.* As if they'd extended their visit. As if they'd liked the snow.

He thought of the carol the singers had refused to repeat, the way the color had drained from old Tam Wicker's face when Coral asked for a second verse. He thought of the welcome too warm for a stranger, the pies left on his step, Maren Hale's eyes sliding away whenever he mentioned how long he planned to stay. He had catalogued all of it as folklore, filed it under *hospitality customs, alpine regions, comparative*. Now the file tore open in his hands and everything spilled out in its true order: they had not been welcoming him. They had been *feeding* him — fattening the days with kindness so the house would find him tender.

And the Ravensworths had built the table everyone ate at.

He got up on unsteady legs and went to the hall mirror, needing to see his own face, needing proof he was still a man and not an entry in a ledger. His reflection met him there — but late, as always, arriving a heartbeat after the fact like news from a distant country. And in that heartbeat of difference, the glass showed him something else: himself, standing exactly where he stood, but with his coat still on and snow melting on his shoulders, as though he had just come in from outside — as though the mirror were showing him a version of this evening that had not happened yet, a Coral returning home through the snow to a house that was waiting for him.

Then the reflection caught up, and it was only him, pale and rumpled, holding a ledger against his chest like a shield.

Outside, the light was failing early, the way it did here, the day folding up like a letter. And down the lane, through the study window, Coral saw figures coming along the packed snow toward his gate — lanterns bobbing, voices carrying thin and bright across the cold, several of his new friends come calling with a basket, singing something under their breath that he could not yet make out.

He knew, with the certainty of a man reading his great-grandfather's handwriting, that tonight they would ask him to sing.
