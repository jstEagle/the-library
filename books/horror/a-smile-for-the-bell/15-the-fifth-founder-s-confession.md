## 15. The Fifth Founder's Confession

She came out of the mirror the way smoke comes out of a snuffed candle — reluctantly, and only because something had asked her to.

Emrys was sitting on the cellar floor with their back against the cold stone of the ritual wall, the ledger open across their knees, when the air in front of them thickened. Not darkened. Thickened, like water going to syrup. And then there was a woman standing in the middle of the cellar, wearing a dress two hundred years out of fashion and an expression Emrys recognized from their own bathroom mirror: the look of someone who has counted every exit in a room and found none of them good.

"You're not her," the woman said.

"No," Emrys said. "I'm not."

The ghost — and it was a ghost, no point pretending otherwise; she had the translucency of old glass and the stillness of furniture — tilted her head. Her face was the smudged one from the portrait, but here, in the place where the house kept its memories, the smudge had been wiped away. She was perhaps forty. She had ink stains on her fingers. Her eyes were the color of the bell above them, that deep bronze that wanted to be struck.

"Then you're the one they've sent," she said. "Or the one it's caught. Which?"

"Both, maybe." Emrys closed the ledger carefully, keeping a thumb in the page. "You're Ada Bellharrow."

"I was." She looked around the cellar as if seeing it for the first time in a long while, and Emrys understood that she probably was. The loop didn't let its prisoners wander. "This room is smaller than I remember. Everything here is smaller than I remember. Grief does that. It shrinks the rooms."

* * *

Her name, in life, had been Ada Bellharrow, and she had been the youngest of the five founders, and she had run.

That much Sable's research had gotten right. What the research hadn't gotten right — what no document could have gotten right, because the truth had never been written down anywhere except inside a woman's head and then inside a house's walls — was why.

"I want you to understand," Ada said, pacing now, her feet making no sound on the flagstones, "that I did not flee out of fear. Everyone who came after assumed it. My brothers wrote letters about my frailty. My sister wrote letters about my nerves. Marguerite Voss's great-great-great-grandmother wrote a letter about my cowardice that is still in your historian's archive, folded into a box marked *Miscellaneous Estate Papers*, and I have read it over her shoulder more times than I can count." A thin smile. "It is a strange thing, being a rumor for two centuries."

"The rite needed a keeper," Emrys said. "Someone living. Someone willing."

"Willing." Ada stopped pacing. "Yes. That word is doing so much work in that sentence that it ought to be paid wages."

She sat down across from Emrys, mirroring their posture without seeming to notice she'd done it, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them. For a moment she looked less like a founder and more like a child locked in a cellar — and Emrys felt that recognition land somewhere old and bruised, in the place where a six-year-old had once sat in the dark counting the sound of their own breathing because counting was the only thing that made the dark have edges.

"Tell me what it actually was," Emrys said. "Not the ledger version. Yours."

Ada was quiet for a long time. In the silence, Emrys could hear the house breathing — that slow tidal movement in the walls that had started three nights ago and hadn't stopped since. Above them, faint through four floors of stone and timber, the bell hummed. It never stopped humming anymore. It was waiting to be told what it was for.

"The Keeping was meant to be a mercy," Ada said at last. "I need you to believe that, because everything that followed depends on it. Bellharrow sat on something hungry. You've felt it — don't shake your head at me, you've felt it, it's been feeling at the edges of you since the day you walked in with your little case of tools and your habit of touching every doorframe twice." Emrys's hand stilled against the doorframe they were, in fact, touching. Ada almost smiled. "It doesn't matter what it was. A hunger older than the land's name. It ate the first village that stood here. It would have eaten everything after. So five foolish, brilliant people decided to give it a keeper instead — someone bound into the house's bones, holding the door shut from the inside, forever. The bell was the lock. The keeper was the hand on the rope. One ring to seal it, one soul to hold it."

"And you were supposed to be the one."

"I volunteered." She said it flatly, daring Emrys to pity her. "I was the youngest. I was the least attached. That was the logic. Elias had a wife. Marguerite's line had the estate. I had nothing but a head full of Latin and a talent for being overlooked. It seemed tidy." Her hands tightened on her knees. "And then, at the last moment, with the circle drawn and the words half-spoken and the bell already leaning toward me like a dog toward its master, I understood something none of us had understood in all our years of preparation."

"What?"

"That willingness can't be borrowed." She looked up. "The rite didn't just need someone willing. It needed someone who chose it *knowing*. Fully knowing. And I knew — in that last second, I finally knew — that I wasn't choosing it. I was escaping into it. I was so tired of being unloved and unnoticed that eternal servitude sounded like belonging. That isn't willingness. That's despair wearing willingness's coat. And the house — the thing we were binding — it would have known. It feeds on exactly that. I would have sealed the door with myself as the hinge, and the hinge would have rotted, and everything we built would have come apart in a century instead of holding forever."

"So you ran."

"So I ran." Her voice cracked on the second word, two hundred years of crack compressed into one syllable. "Mid-rite. I broke the circle with my own foot, which is the single most blasphemous act ever committed in this county, and I ran up those stairs, and behind me I heard my brother Elias scream my name, and then I heard the bell fall silent, and then I heard nothing ever again, because the house took all four of them into itself unfinished — half-bound, half-hungry, stuck in the moment I broke. Stuck *here*." She gestured at the walls, at the whole vertical weight of Bellharrow above them. "Every hour since has been that hour, circling. They pace the halls looking for me to come back and finish it. And the hunger underneath — it's been patient. It's been widening the mirrors. Pulling the present backward. Because it knows that eventually someone will come down these stairs and read that ledger and think they've found a way out."

Emrys's mouth was dry. "There is a way out. Isn't there? You came to tell me there's a way out."

"There is a way out," Ada said. "And it will cost exactly what completing the rite costs. That's the joke. That's the beautiful, ancient joke."

* * *

She drew it in the dust of the cellar floor with one translucent finger, and the lines stayed — the house let her write here; this was her chapter of it.

"The Keeping binds a keeper to the bell, and the bell seals the door, and the door holds the hunger. Unmaking it means opening the door — briefly, deliberately, in full knowledge — and letting the hunger pass through and out, back to whatever dark it crawled from. But a door that opens must be held open by someone. And a door that closes must be held closed by someone. There is no version of this, complete or unmake, where nobody holds the door." She looked up. "Do you understand? Mercy was never free. Someone pays. The only question the house has ever asked anyone who stands where you're standing is: *will it be you, and will you mean it?*"

"And if no one answers?"

"Then it keeps asking. It widens the mirrors until the whole world is this hour. It has time. It is made of time." Ada brushed the dust from her hands, though there was nothing on them. "My siblings thought I robbed them of their triumph. The house thinks I robbed it of its meal. I have spent two hundred years knowing that both of them are wrong and neither of them will ever be told so. That is what running bought me. Not freedom. Just a different cell, with a better view."

Emrys sat with that. Around them the cellar held its breath — or rather, the house held its breath, because the cellar was only stone, and stone had never breathed in its life.

"Sable Fenwick," Emrys said. "The historian. She's still outside. Still real. If I do this—"

"If you do this, she walks out carrying the truth, and the truth stays alive, and the hunger goes where it should have gone in 1804, and four shades get to stop pacing." Ada's voice softened into something that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite hope. "And you become the house. Not eaten — *become*. You'll feel every draft. You'll know every door. You'll open the door for whoever needs it opened, and keep it shut against whatever needs it kept shut, and no one will ever thank you, and no one will ever know, and you will belong here more completely than any living thing has ever belonged anywhere."

"That sounds like the trap."

"It is absolutely the trap," Ada agreed. "That's what I'm telling you. It was always the trap. The founders built a beautiful trap and called it duty, and I refused the trap and called it mercy, and the house has been waiting two hundred years for someone who would walk into it with their eyes open and call it a choice." She leaned forward, and her bronze eyes held Emrys's, and for a moment Emrys saw reflected in them every door they had ever counted, every light they had ever left burning, every cellar they had ever been locked in and every cellar they had ever feared becoming. "So I will ask you the question I couldn't honestly answer for myself. Not yet. Take it upstairs. Sleep on it, if this house lets anything sleep. But answer it true, because the bell will know a lie."

She rose. The air began to take her back, syrup-thick, drawing her toward the mirror on the far wall where the reflected cellar waited with its frozen copy of this exact conversation.

"Ada," Emrys said. "Why me? Why now? Two hundred years — why did it wait for a pest controller?"

Ada paused at the edge of the glass. When she turned, her expression was the saddest thing Emrys had seen in a house full of horrors.

"Because it didn't wait for a pest controller," she said. "It waited for someone who counts doors because doors were once counted against them. Someone who leaves lights on because they know what the dark does. Someone who has spent their whole life building rituals to keep enclosed spaces safe — for everyone but themselves." The mirror surface rippled around her like water accepting a stone. "The house didn't choose you, Emrys Lockridge. You were always coming here. It simply finally sent for you."

And she was gone, and the cellar was empty except for the ledger, the dust-drawn diagram, and the low bronze hum of a bell two hundred years tired of waiting.

Emrys sat alone in the dark for a long time. Then they took out their torch, clicked it on, and began — slowly, methodically, doorframe by doorframe — to climb toward the tower.

Above them, faint and patient, the bell hummed a note that sounded almost like a name.
