## 18. Climax: Ring the Changes

The stairs to the tower had not existed an hour ago.

That was the thing about Bellharrow in its final minutes — it stopped pretending. The house was done with suggestion, done with the slow theatrical lag of mirrors and the polite wrongness of extra doors. Now it simply built what it needed: a staircase of black oak rising through a shaft that had been solid wall since 1804, each step warm underfoot like the flank of something breathing.

Emrys climbed with their hand on the rope rail and counted.

Forty-one steps. They'd counted forty-one on the way up from the cellar, when the stairs were still a rumor in the plaster. Counting again now, because counting was the last tool they owned that still worked, and because Sable was three steps behind them breathing hard, and because if they stopped counting they would start thinking about what waited at the top.

"Say it again," Sable said. "The terms."

"There are no terms." Emrys didn't turn around. "There's a bell and there's me. One of us rings the other."

"That's not — you can't just *decide*—"

"I decided about the cellar light when I was nine," Emrys said. "Left it on every night for six years so I'd know the dark hadn't followed me up. You want to argue about whether deciding is allowed, do it while we're walking."

Sable shut up. Good. Emrys liked her better when she was listening than when she was being brave; her bravery had a way of arriving at exactly the wrong moment, like now, like the way she kept touching the ledger strapped under her coat as if paper could stop what was coming.

Below them, the house shuddered. Not an earthquake-shudder — a swallowing one. Through the stairwell's open slats Emrys could see down into the great hall, where the mirror over the mantel had given up reflecting entirely and now showed only the loop: the cellar, the circle of salt gone grey, five shadows standing at five points, and the sixth point empty. The interrupted moment, playing on a screen of silvered glass. Somewhere below that, Voss's hired men were in the building — Emrys had heard the front doors go in ten minutes ago, heard two voices, then one voice, then the particular wet quiet of a house finishing a meal it had been cooking for two centuries.

The lag was collapsing. That was the worst part. All this time the mirrors had run behind, one second, four seconds, ten — the present being dragged backward toward 1804 like a tide going out. But tides turn. The gap between now and then was closing at both ends, and when it closed all the way, everything inside these walls would be pressed flat into the moment the ritual failed, forever, a fly in amber the size of a manor.

Unless someone stood at the sixth point. Unless someone rang.

*Someone must hold the door,* Ada had said, in the bedroom that remembered her flight. *Mercy was never free. I could not pay it. Perhaps you can.*

Emrys reached the top.

The bell chamber was round, open to the night on four sides through arches hung with ivy gone woody and black. The bell itself hung from a beam of oak older than the manor — iron, pitted, unmarked by any foundry stamp, as if it hadn't been made but grown. The rope was new. That was the detail that sat wrong in Emrys's chest: after two hundred years of dust, the rope was hemp-bright and freshly whipped at the end, coiled neat as a hangman's noose on the floor beside the wheel.

Waiting to be used. It had always been waiting.

Beyond the arches, the grounds moved wrong. The lawn rippled like water. The drive was a river of moonlight flowing uphill toward the house. And the sky — the sky had seams in it, faint vertical lines, like wallpaper lifting at its edges, and behind the seams was not stars but the cellar. The salt circle. The five shadows, waiting for their sixth.

"It's beautiful," Sable said quietly, coming up beside them. She meant it. That was the trouble with historians; they looked at the end of the world and saw provenance.

"It's a mouth," Emrys said. "We're standing in it."

They crossed to the wheel. Up close the bell hummed without being struck, a note below hearing, felt in the teeth and the backs of the knees. Emrys put one hand on the wheel's rim and the wood knew them instantly, warmed to their palm like a handshake, and they snatched their hand back before the grip could become mutual.

"Not yet," they told it.

"You're really going to do it." Sable wasn't asking. She'd worked it out on the stairs, Emrys realized — she'd probably worked it out in the cellar, days ago, and had been spending the intervening time constructing arguments, and here at the top she found she had none left that fit the shape of the room.

"The rite needs a willing keeper bound to the bell," Emrys said. "Ada couldn't finish it because binding someone unwilling makes a prison, and she wouldn't build a prison. So she ran, and the rite hung open for two hundred years, and the house got hungry instead of whole." They turned to face her. "I'm willing. That's the whole trick. That's the entire ceremony. Two hundred years of ritual and it comes down to one person saying yes with nobody making them."

"Then let me say it first."

"No."

"Emrys—"

"*No.*" It came out harder than intended, and the bell answered it, a low toll-less thrum that rattled the ivy. Emrys breathed. Counted the arches. Four. Counted Sable's hands, white-knuckled on the strap of the ledger bag. Two. "Listen to me. You're the only person alive who knows what happened here. Ada's confession is in your head and the ledger is in your bag. If I ring and we're both bound, the truth dies in this tower and Voss's family wins by default — they get their empty deed and their clean story and in fifty years some other lawyer is feeding keepers to some other house. Somebody has to walk out of here carrying it. That's not negotiable. That's load-bearing."

"So you've decided my role too."

"Somebody has to," Emrys said. "It's kind of my whole condition."

Sable laughed — a cracked, furious, grieving sound — and Emrys understood it cost her more than screaming would have. Then her face changed, went scholarly and sly, and Emrys recognized the expression because they'd seen it in the cellar over the ledger: the look of a woman who has found a loophole.

"The binding takes the one who rings," she said. "You said. The hand on the wheel, the voice that consents. Fine. But the rite needs a sixth point in the circle, and the circle is downstairs, and the bell is up here, which means the binding runs through the house — through every mirror, every door, every seam. Which means—" she was already moving, backing toward the eastern arch, "—which means if I'm standing in a reflection when it rings, I'm part of the circuit, and maybe the bell can't tell which of us is the keeper until it's done choosing—"

"Sable. *Don't.*"

She stepped backward through the arch.

For half a second she was silhouetted against the seamed sky, and then the air took her the way water takes a stone — no drama, just absence — and the space where she'd been filled with a slow ripple of moonlight.

Emrys stood alone in the bell chamber with their heart going like a trapped bird.

The eastern arch wasn't an arch anymore. It was a mirror — full-height, silvered, framed in the same black oak as the staircase — and in it Sable was already walking away down a reflected version of the tower stairs, moving with the strange doubled smoothness of things inside glass. The reflected chamber was identical except for one detail: in the mirror, the bell rope lay coiled on the floor, and no one stood at the wheel.

She was going down to the circle. To stand at the sixth point herself. To be standing in the house's bones when the bell rang, gambling that proximity would outrank consent.

Emrys did the arithmetic in about a second and a half, and hated every number in it.

Because the horrible thing was that Sable might be right. The rite was old and hungry and imprecise; it had fumbled its one attempt in 1804; it might well take whichever body was closest to the circle's heart. And Emrys could not follow her into the mirror — the reflections only opened outward now, the house pulling everything in, nothing out — and even if they could, chasing her would just deliver two keepers to one altar.

So they did the other thing. The thing their whole life had been training them for without knowing it.

Emrys counted the exits.

One: the western arch, open to the collapsing lawn. Two: the stairwell, down into a house that was eating the present. Three: the mirror, which was not an exit at all but a throat. Four—

Four. There was no fourth. There never was. That was the joke of their whole life, the private punchline: count the doors, leave the lights on, and the dark still gets in, because the fourth door was always a lie you told yourself at bedtime.

But the house believed in the fourth door. The house had spent two centuries learning to be a place, and places have rules, and Emrys had spent two days teaching it their rules. Every light burning. Every door counted. Eleven doors at dusk, twelve by midnight — and the twelfth had been warm, and it had breathed, and it had wanted to be found.

The house loved rules. It was starving for them. A rite interrupted is a rule half-spoken, and this whole catastrophe was a house trying to finish a sentence.

So Emrys gave it a sentence to finish.

They turned to the mirror, where Sable's reflected figure was nearing the bottom of the reflected stairs, and they spoke clearly, the way you speak to a client explaining an invoice, the way you speak to a house you intend to manage:

"Bellharrow. Terms of service." Their voice cracked across the chamber and the bell drank it. "The keeper is the one who rings. The one who consents. Those are Ada's words, written in her hand, witnessed by four founders who are standing in your cellar right now waiting for you to get it right this time. You don't get to choose by proximity. You don't get to choose by hunger. You choose by consent or you wait another two hundred years, and I know — I *know* — how long two hundred years feels."

In the mirror, Sable had stopped on the reflected stairs. She was looking up, and though sound didn't cross, Emrys saw her mouth move — arguing, of course, always arguing — and then saw her face change as the reflected chamber obeyed. The reflected rope lifted from the reflected floor. By itself. Coiling upright like a charmed snake, then settling back down, slack, refused.

The house had heard. The house had chosen to hear. Consent or nothing.

Sable stood very still for a long moment. Then she did something Emrys would think about for the rest of a very long rest-of-a-life: she unslung the ledger bag, held it up toward the mirror's surface — toward Emrys — and mouthed, distinctly, *proof*, and set it down on the reflected step.

On the real side, at the base of the real eastern arch, the bag materialized out of a fold of moonlight, landing soft as a dropped coat. The mirror passed things inward. Apparently, tonight, at the seam of everything, it could pass things outward too — small things, true things, things freely given.

Emrys picked it up. The ledger inside was warm.

"Get out," they said to the mirror, and their voice broke on the second word, and they let it. "Get out and write it down and don't you dare publish. Keep it like a grave. And Sable —" the reflected figure had turned, was climbing back up toward the reflected arch, face wet and jaw set, "—when you visit, bring flowers. I always wanted flowers. Nobody ever thinks of it for pest controllers."

She reached the surface. For one instant her palm met theirs through the glass, cold on one side, warm on the other, and Emrys felt the exact width of the world between them — a quarter inch of silver and two hundred years.

Then Emrys stepped back, and pointed at the floor behind her, and said the last commandment they had left:

"Door."

Behind Sable, in the reflected tower, a door appeared in a wall that had never had one. Plain, panelled, ordinary — the twelfth door, the warm one, the one that breathed. It swung inward onto not-stairs, onto not-night, onto the plain grey corridor of somewhere else, somewhere outside the seams, and Emrys watched Sable understand it, watch her hesitate — of course she hesitated, God help them both — and then Emrys put every ounce of the voice they used on clients who wouldn't leave the attic, the voice that ended arguments:

"*Out.*"

Sable Fenwick walked through the door, and the door closed, and the mirror became only a mirror, showing a bell chamber with one person in it.

Emrys set the ledger down gently by the arch, where she'd find it or send someone who would. Then they turned to the wheel.

The house was almost done. Through the arches the lawn had stopped pretending to be land; the sky-seams had widened to gaps; and far below, through the stairwell, came a sound like a great intake of breath drawn through every chimney at once. In the great hall mirror, the five shadows had turned their faces upward. Waiting. Two hundred years of waiting, and it came down to a pest controller with a rope.

Emrys thought about the cellar. Nine years old, locked in, counting the door hinges in the dark because counting was a candle you could carry in your head. They had spent their whole life managing small spaces, keeping the lights on, keeping the tally — everyone had called it damage, and it had never once felt like damage, it had felt like *work*, like a job nobody else would take.

Nobody else would take this one either.

"Yeah," Emrys said, to the bell, to the house, to the five faces below, to the girl in the cellar. "I'll take the callout."

They gripped the wheel. It knew them. It had always known them.

And Emrys Lockridge pulled.

The bell swung, gathered the whole falling night into its mouth, and rang —

* * *

One note. It went out through the arches and down through the stones and sideways through every mirror in the manor at once, and everywhere it passed, the lag closed — not collapsed, *closed*, the way a wound closes, the present and 1804 meeting not as a collision but as a seam being sewn. In the great hall the five shadows raised their arms. In the cellar the salt circle blazed white. The sixth point filled — not with a body, but with a voice, Emrys's voice, ringing in the iron, saying *yes, willingly, witness it* — and the interrupted sentence finished itself at last, two hundred years late and exactly on time.

The bell rang a second time, though no hand touched it, and the sky-seams sealed. The third ring was softer, almost tender, and it sounded less like a warning than like a door being locked from the inside by someone who planned to stay.

When the sound finally faded, the bell chamber was quiet, and ordinary, and cold, and Emrys was still standing at the wheel with their hands on the wood.

Their hands, they noticed, were slightly transparent at the edges. Like lamplight through vellum.

Down in the drive, small and human and alive, a figure with a canvas bag was running for the gate, and Emrys felt her the way you feel a bird land on a wire — a tiny warmth on the outermost edge of a self that now extended, apparently, to the property line.

"Well," Emrys said aloud, and their voice came from the bell, and the walls, and the ivy, and their own still-solid middle, all at once. "Let's see about those lights."

Every lamp in Bellharrow lit at once — every bulb, every sconce, every candle-end in the 1804 bedroom — and stayed lit.

Somewhere below, in the hall, the front door unlocked itself, and swung open, and waited.

It was, Emrys decided, a decent start.
