## 6. Marisol's Rules

Wren woke on her second morning at Hollowmere to the smell of pancakes she had not made.

This was alarming for several reasons, not least of which was that the kitchen stove hadn't worked when she'd tested it the night before — she'd turned every knob and gotten nothing but a hollow *clunk*, like the house swallowing. And yet here was the smell, buttery and golden, drifting up the back stairs like an apology.

She found her father in the kitchen, standing over a griddle that was, impossibly, glowing orange.

"Cyrus."

"Don't," he said, without turning around. "I know. I checked it twice last night. It didn't work twice."

"Maybe it just needed to hear you say please."

He gave her a look over his shoulder — the old look, the one from before everything went sideways between them, the one that said *you're being ridiculous* while secretly enjoying that she was. "Pancakes?"

"Sure." She sat down at the table, which wobbled, then stopped wobbling as if embarrassed to be caught. "Any luck with the measurements?"

"I'm not talking about the measurements."

"You're always talking about the measurements."

"Because they're *wrong*, Wren." He flipped a pancake with more force than the pancake deserved. "The east hallway is four feet longer than it was yesterday. I measured it at noon and again at dusk. Four feet. The house is either settling or lying, and houses don't settle sideways."

Wren didn't tell him about her own discovery from the night before — the cellar stairs that went down farther than the house went up, the chalk tallies in her own seven-year-old handwriting. She'd spent half the night staring at the ceiling trying to make it into something explainable. Sleepwalking. A fever dream. Some very committed vandalism by a previous child with identical penmanship.

She'd almost managed it, too. Right up until she'd come downstairs and smelled pancakes.

There was a knock at the front door. Three small knocks, spaced carefully, like someone who had practiced knocking politely.

Cyrus frowned. "Expecting anyone?"

"No." But Wren was already moving, because some part of her — the part that had never fully grown up, the part that still kept a spare room in her head for things that shouldn't fit anywhere else — knew exactly who it was.

Marisol stood on the porch in the yellow raincoat, hands folded behind her back, rocking slightly on her heels. The morning fog hadn't burned off yet, and she stood in it like she was made of the same material — soft-edged, faintly luminous, entirely unbothered.

"You came back," Marisol said. Then, correcting herself with visible effort: "You came back *again*. That's better grammar. Auntie Vera says I have to work on my tenses."

"Marisol." Wren's voice came out strange, half laugh, half something older and sadder. Behind her, she heard her father set down the spatula.

"The man who measures things is making breakfast," Marisol observed, peering past Wren's hip. "That's new. He used to just walk around with the tape measure going *tsk, tsk, tsk*. He has a very tsk-y face."

"He can hear you."

"I know." Marisol smiled, and there was something in the smile that Wren's adult mind snagged on like a fingernail on a loose thread — something patient, something *old* wearing a seven-year-old's face the way you'd wear a coat that no longer quite fit. "That's why I knocked. Knocking is polite. Houses remember who's polite."

"Wren," Cyrus said from the kitchen doorway, dish towel in hand, voice carefully level in the way he got when he was anything but. "Who is this child? Where are her parents?"

"She doesn't have parents, Dad."

"That's not funny."

"It's not a joke." Wren turned back to Marisol. "Can we talk? Just us?"

Marisol considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. "He gets one pancake," she said finally. "The round one. The others are mine."

* * *

They sat on the porch steps, the two of them, Wren with her knees drawn up and Marisol with her boots swinging — boots that did not quite touch the boards, Wren noticed now, though there was nothing ghostly about it; it was simply that Marisol was small, and had apparently been small for a very long time.

The fog was lifting off the lawn in slow rags. Somewhere inside the house, a door closed softly, though Wren had left them all open to air the rooms out.

"You have questions," Marisol said. "Your face does the question thing. It scrunches. It always scrunched. Even when you were little and asking me why the wallpaper grew."

"The wallpaper *grew*?"

"On Tuesdays. You thought everyone's wallpaper grew on Tuesdays." Marisol kicked her feet. "You should ask your real question first. The big one. Get it out of the way so we can eat."

Wren took a breath. "What are you?"

Marisol looked at her for a long moment. The fog moved behind her and did not move through her, which answered one question and raised six more.

"I'm your friend," she said. "You made me up. Mostly." She held up a small hand before Wren could speak. "But I was here first. That's the part that matters, and it's the part I'm not allowed to tell you all of, so don't ask it sideways, because I'll get confused and say something wrong and then the house will hear me and get *ideas*." She said *ideas* the way other children said *vegetables*.

"Allowed by who?"

"By the rules." Marisol hopped off the step and landed facing Wren, suddenly formal, suddenly precise, a schoolmarm in a raincoat. "And since you're going to be stubborn and stay — I can see it in your scrunchy face, you're staying — then you need the rules. All of them. Because last time you were here, you were little, and little people get forgiven. Big people get *kept*."

The word landed cold, despite the warming morning.

"Rule one," Marisol said, holding up one finger. "Never sing the fourth verse."

"What fourth verse?"

"See, this is the problem with rules. Everyone asks what they're *about* instead of just doing them." She sighed, deeply, the sigh of someone who had given this lecture many times across many decades. "There's a song. The house likes it. Your family sings it. There are four verses. You will only ever hear three of them, because the fourth one isn't written down anywhere, and if you ever find it, or figure it out, or start humming something and realize it's *going somewhere you don't recognize* — you stop. Mid-note. You bite it. Like biting a marble."

"That's oddly specific."

"I've had a long time to be specific." Finger two. "Rule two: never answer the cellar. If you hear voices from under the floor — singing, calling, saying your name, saying *anything* — you do not answer. Not even to say 'no.' Especially not to say 'no.' 'No' is an answer. Silence is the only thing down there that can't be turned into a thread."

"A thread?"

"They pull threads." Marisol's boots had gone still. "Threads become ropes. Ropes become invitations. And once you've been invited properly, it's rude not to come down." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all; they were the eyes of someone describing a machine whose gears she'd seen up close.

Wren thought of the chalk tallies in the cellar, marching down the stone in her own childish hand. Counting doors. Or counting something else.

"And rule three?" she asked quietly.

Marisol's whole demeanor changed. She climbed back onto the step beside Wren, close, shoulder to shoulder, and when she spoke again her voice was smaller — genuinely small, a child's voice, scared in the ordinary human way.

"Never let the house count you."

"Count me how?"

"Any way. Doors, footsteps, your name on paper, your face in mirrors. If you notice it counting, you break the count. Walk backward. Say a different number out loud. Hum — no, don't hum, humming is nearly singing—" She pressed her palms against her eyes. "Just *interrupt* it. The house counts the way you breathe, Wren. It doesn't mean to. But if it ever finishes a count with you at the end of it, you belong to the tally. And the house keeps its tallies."

The porch boards were warm under Wren's hands. The morning was bright and ordinary and utterly failed to make any of this feel less true.

"Marisol." She chose the words carefully. "What happens if someone breaks the rules?"

And here was the thing Wren would think about later, in the dark, in the weeks after: the pause. The long, terrible pause, during which Marisol's mouth opened and worked and produced nothing — not refusal, not secrecy, but genuine *blankness*, like a music box with the mechanism removed. Her face went smooth and slightly wrong, a doll's face between expressions.

"I can't say," she said at last, and her voice had gone thin as paper. "I've tried. There's a hole where the answer goes. I fall into it every time and climb back out and my knees hurt." She looked up, and her eyes were wet. "I don't know what happens. That's the worst part. I've watched it happen and I *still don't know what happens*, because knowing got taken out of me, and whatever's down there got taken out of *them* too—"

She stopped herself, jaw snapping shut like a trap.

"*Them,*" Wren repeated.

Marisol stood up abruptly, raincoat rustling, and pointed at the kitchen window with tremendous authority. "The pancakes are burning. Rule four: pancakes burn when you're not watching them. This is the most important rule."

"Marisol—"

"*Pancakes,*" said Marisol, and marched inside.

* * *

Her father had saved the round one, which Wren chose not to read too much into. They ate at the wobbly table, and Marisol ate four pancakes with the focused devotion of someone for whom breakfast was clearly the high point of whatever century she was having, and Cyrus watched her over the rim of his coffee with the expression of a man running calculations he didn't like the results of.

"So," he said, in his surveyor's voice, the reasonable one, the one that had talked banks and county clerks into all manner of things. "Marisol. You live... nearby?"

"Nowhere," Marisol said cheerfully, syrup on her chin.

"Where's nowhere, exactly?"

"Everywhere the house isn't. It's very roomy." She licked her thumb. "You measure everything, don't you. I remember. You measured the porch when Wren was little and then you measured it again and it was different and you got that line between your eyebrows." She reached across the table and, before Cyrus could flinch, pressed one small finger precisely into the crease above his nose. "It goes *here*. It's deeper now. You've been worried a long time."

Cyrus went very still.

"Dad," Wren said carefully, "when I told you about her — the imaginary friend, from when we stayed here — you said you remembered. You said I used to talk to the empty porch."

"I said you talked to *yourself* on the empty porch," Cyrus corrected, but his voice had lost its footing. "Kids do that. It's developmentally normal. There were studies."

"There were no studies," Marisol said kindly. "There was you, listening at the window, deciding I wasn't real because that was easier. That's okay. Grown-ups have to decide things or they can't sleep." She patted his hand, which was trembling slightly, and pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of mercy. "You're a good father. You came back to a house you hate because your daughter might come looking for you. The house noticed. The house notices *everything*, so be boring. Boring things get left alone."

"That's not comforting," Cyrus said.

"It's not supposed to be comforting. It's supposed to be *useful*." Marisol gathered the last pancake onto her plate with surgical precision. "Comfort is what the house offers. That's rule five, actually, the secret one, the one I probably shouldn't say." She leaned forward, and the morning light through the window seemed to bend slightly toward her, attentive. "If the house ever gives you exactly what you want — the room you missed, the person you lost, the childhood you actually needed instead of the one you got — that's not kindness. That's bait. The house is *lonely*, Wren. Lonely things are the most generous things in the world right up until they close their hands."

She said it lightly, licking syrup off her thumb, and the horror of it arrived a full second late, the way thunder arrives after lightning.

Then she hopped down from her chair. "I have to go. The house gets jealous when I'm happy too long. It thinks happiness is a door left open." At the doorway she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at Wren with an expression far too old for the face wearing it.

"You asked what happens if the rules break," she said. "I couldn't answer. But I'll tell you what I *can* say, the sideways way, the way that doesn't fall in the hole." Her voice dropped. "Everyone who ever broke a rule is still here. Nobody who broke a rule ever *left*. Do the arithmetic on that, Wren. You're better at sums than I am. You always were."

And she was gone — not out the door, but *from* the doorway, the way a note stops being in a bell, and the kitchen felt suddenly larger and colder and honest about it.

Cyrus set down his fork. "Wren."

"I know."

"That child said the house *notices everything.*"

"I know, Dad."

"And she said—" He stopped. Ran a hand through his hair, a gesture she'd known since childhood, the gesture that preceded his worst discoveries. "Last night, after you went up, I heard footsteps on the stairs. Mine. One beat behind mine. I counted the doors on this floor to calm myself down." His jaw tightened. "Eleven at midnight. Thirteen when I finished."

Wren's blood went slow and heavy.

Thirteen. *Never let the house count you.*

"We need to see the study," she said, standing, because her father's face had gone the color of the fog outside, and because somewhere beneath the floorboards — faint, patient, threaded through the joists like roots — she could have sworn she heard the first thin notes of a song beginning.
