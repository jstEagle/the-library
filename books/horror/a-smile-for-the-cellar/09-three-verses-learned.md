## 9. Three Verses, Learned

Cyrus Crane had spent thirty-one years believing that a man could outwalk his obligations if he simply kept moving and never looked at the paperwork.

He was, by profession, a man of paperwork. He understood better than anyone how a debt worked: it did not chase you, it did not shout, it sat in a drawer with your name on it and waited with the patience of ink. Hollowmere was a debt. He had known it since the second night, when the lullaby came up through the floorboards — three verses, sung thin as tissue paper by voices that had no business still having throats — and he had recognized the melody the way you recognize your own handwriting on a check you don't remember signing.

His aunt Ede had sung it to him here, summers, when he was small enough to be carried up to bed. He'd forgotten everything else about her — her face had gone soft in his memory like a photograph left in the sun — but not the song. Songs are filed differently. Songs are kept somewhere the house can reach.

He sat at the study desk at eleven o'clock at night with the register open in front of him and his own name drying on the last line, and he made the decision the way he made all his decisions: quietly, with a pencil, in the margins.

*Wren leaves Friday,* he wrote. *I stay.*

It was, he told himself, a surveyor's logic. A structure with an outstanding obligation requires a responsible party. The house wanted an heir; he was the nearest heir; therefore the matter was simple, administrative, almost boring. He was very good at making things boring. It was how he survived them.

What he did not write down — because some things should never be given the dignity of ink — was that Wren had arrived on his porch three days ago with two suitcases and a hope she tried to hide behind sarcasm, and that he had spent twenty years teaching her, by example, that fathers leave. He would not teach her one more lesson like that. Even if the final lesson cost him the cellar.

* * *

The house began teaching him before dawn.

He woke at four-something to find the walls humming. Not creaking — houses creak, it's their way of clearing their throats — but humming, low and resonant, the note coming out of the plaster itself as though the whole building were a struck bell that had been ringing for a century and had only now gotten around to admitting it.

Cyrus lay in bed and listened, and his professional instincts, God help him, took notes.

*Load-bearing hum. Structural. The melody is in the joists.*

It was the first verse. He knew it immediately, the way you know a face under a hat. The same tune Aunt Ede had sung, except the house's version was slower, and underneath it there were other voices — far off, muffled, like a choir rehearsing through several floors of carpet. The cellar singers. Keeping time. Waiting for him to catch up.

"All right," Cyrus said to the ceiling. His voice came out steadier than he felt, which was the only kind of steady he'd ever been any good at. "All right. I'm listening."

The pipes answered.

That was the thing he would remember afterward, sitting in whatever waiting room the afterlife turned out to keep: the plumbing sang harmony. When he ran the tap that morning, the water came out accompanied — a second line woven under the first verse, rising from somewhere deep in the stack, warm and patient. He stood at the kitchen sink with his hands under the running water and laughed, once, a short ugly laugh with nothing funny in it, because the alternative was to sit down on the kitchen floor and not get up.

"You're eager," he told the drain.

The drain gurgled something that was unmistakably, structurally, *verse two.*

* * *

He learned it the way he learned everything: by measuring.

Verse one lived in the east wall of the parlor. He established this by walking the room with his tape measure while humming, and noting that when he hit the correct intervals, the tape seemed to extend willingly — forty feet of parlor in a house whose exterior footprint allowed for twenty-six — whereas when he hummed wrong, the room snapped back to its honest dimensions like a dog shaking off rain. The house rewarded accuracy. It was the first entity he'd ever surveyed that shared his values.

"Forty feet," he muttered, reeling the tape back in. "You're a liar and a show-off."

The wall hummed, pleased with itself.

Verse two was in the staircase. Of course it was. He discovered this on Wednesday afternoon, climbing from the ground floor to the landing, when he realized he'd been singing under his breath without deciding to — the steps setting the tempo, each riser a beat, the banister buzzing against his palm like a tuning fork. Twelve steps up. On the thirteenth step, which did not exist and had never existed in any of his surveys, the melody paused expectantly.

Cyrus stood on the nonexistent step and felt it hold his weight.

"I know," he said. "I know what I'm doing."

He wasn't sure that was true. But he climbed the rest of the way, and behind him, faintly, the thirteenth step sighed and let itself be counted.

Verse three took two days, because verse three was in the cellar door, and Cyrus was not ready to stand in front of the cellar door and sing.

He practiced the first two verses instead, walking the halls at dusk with his coat collar up, humming into the cold. The house warmed around him as he did it. There was no other word for it. The radiators — dead since before he arrived, he'd noted them in his preliminary report, *heating system non-functional, recommend full replacement* — ticked and breathed and gave off heat. The lamps brightened as he passed. On Thursday evening he found fresh flowers in the parlor vase, white and waxy, grown from nowhere, and he stood looking at them for a long moment with his hands in his pockets.

"This is how you got her, isn't it," he said quietly. "Aunt Ede. This is how you got all of them. You're not frightening. You're *comfortable.*"

The fire in the grate — unlit, unlit for thirty years — crackled agreeably.

And that was the horror of it, he thought, watching flames he had not lit dance in a hearth that should have been cold: the house didn't menace. It *hosted.* Every wrong measurement, every impossible door, every footstep one beat behind his own in the walls — none of it was an attack. It was hospitality. It was a host pulling out chairs. *Sit here. Stay here. Belong here.* His aunt's smile in the old photographs, the one he'd always found unsettling and never known why — he understood it now. She hadn't smiled because she was happy. She'd smiled because she was *welcome*, and there is a kind of welcome that does not let go.

He was going to walk into that welcome on purpose, so that his daughter wouldn't have to.

He found, examining the feeling, that it fit him like an old coat. That frightened him more than the cellar did.

* * *

Friday morning, Wren caught him at it.

He was in the kitchen, washing dishes he didn't need to wash — the house had begun cleaning itself around him, another courtesy he chose not to log — and he was humming verse two without noticing, tapping the rhythm on a soup pot, when he heard her coffee mug stop halfway to her mouth.

"Dad." Her voice was careful, the way it had been careful all week, both of them handling each other like borrowed china. "What's that song?"

"Nothing. Old thing." He set the pot down too hard. "My aunt used to sing it."

"It's the one from the floorboards." She set the mug down. "The one the — the singers do. You're singing *with* them."

"They're short a baritone," Cyrus said, and reached for humor the way other men reached for the light switch. It had served him well for twenty years of missed birthdays and forwarded mail. "Look, Wren—"

"Don't." She said it fast, and then said it again, slower, and the carefulness cracked right down the middle. "Don't do the thing where you decide something enormous and tell me about it afterward in a reasonable voice. Marisol says—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. Marisol's rules. Never answer the cellar. Never let the house count you. And the third rule, the one the little girl in the yellow raincoat had repeated until her voice wore thin: *never learn the whole song.*

"Wren." He dried his hands. He turned around and gave her the truth, plain, because she deserved at least one plain thing from him. "There's a line in the family register downstairs with my name on it. The ink's already dry. This house has been holding my seat for thirty years, and the only question is whether I sit down in it or whether it finds a way to pull you into it instead. You've been counted. I've seen it — your reflection lagging, your footsteps arriving early. It's starting on you because I've been hiding in the guest room pretending to be a bank employee."

"So we *leave.* We get in the car and we drive until the measurements behave."

"And it follows its heir." He said it gently. "You know it does. You've seen the register. Nobody ever got out of this family by driving fast."

She stared at him, and he watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic he'd done, the same cold columns — and he hated the house for making his daughter good at sums like these.

"There's a fourth verse," she said finally. "Marisol won't say what it does. But everyone down there is waiting for it. What happens if you finish the song, Dad? What actually happens?"

"I go below," he said. "And I hold the door from the inside. And you grow old somewhere with normal hallways, and you get to be angry at me for ordinary reasons, like missing Christmas." He managed a smile. "I've earned a few decades of that."

"That's not — Dad, that's not a plan, that's a *eulogy* with extra steps—"

"It's a survey report," he said. "Findings and recommendation. And my finding is that you leave Friday, and my recommendation is that you let me do one job properly."

She left the kitchen. From the hallway came the sound of her footsteps — arriving half a beat before she did, the house already counting her — and Cyrus stood alone among the clean dishes and the warm air and the flowers that grew from nothing, and the pipes beneath the floor hummed verse three to him, coaxing, patient, kind.

"Not yet," he told them. "But soon."

They harmonized anyway. They had all the time in the world, and they knew it, and they were glad to wait.

* * *

He went down to the cellar door on Saturday night, because Saturday night was when Aunt Ede used to sing it, and if he was going to inherit her debt he would at least inherit her schedule.

The door stood at the end of the kitchen corridor, oak, iron-banded, older than every other door in the house by a century at least. The chalk tallies climbed the wall beside it in a child's hand — his daughter's hand, twenty years younger, counting doors that weren't there. He put his palm flat against the oak. It was warm. Everything was warm now. The house had turned its heat toward him like a face toward the sun.

He sang verse one to the door.

The cellar answered — dozens of voices, paper-thin, rising through the wood, taking up the melody where he left it and handing it back. Verse two. He gave them verse two. The iron bands on the door hummed in sympathy, and somewhere above him the whole house leaned in, rafters and plaster and pipe, a congregation bending toward its organist.

Then verse three. He'd only heard it twice, both times through the floor, and he stumbled on the turn of it — and the voices below caught him, bore the phrase for him, set him gently back on his feet. They *wanted* him to learn it. They had been waiting, some of them, longer than the house had had electricity, and here at last was a living throat standing at the door, willing, nearly ready.

When the last note settled, the silence afterward was the fullest silence Cyrus had ever stood inside. Full the way a held breath is full.

"Three verses," he said to the door. His voice shook only a little, and only at the end. "I've got three verses."

Through the oak, soft as a child at a keyhole, the singers hummed the first two notes of the fourth — the two notes that were all anyone seemed to know, the two notes that opened a door with no handle on this side — and stopped, and waited, the way they had always waited, for someone to give them the rest.

Upstairs, faintly, he heard Wren's bedroom door open. Heard her cross the hall. Heard her stop at the top of the cellar stairs, one beat ahead of her own footsteps, and say his name into the dark:

"Dad. Come quick. I found the music box — and there's writing inside the lid."
