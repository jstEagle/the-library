## 12. Marisol's Origin

Wren found the photograph on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the days her mother used to say nothing good ever happened, and Wren had inherited the superstition along with her crooked smile and her habit of humming when she was nervous.

She was in the study looking for — she would have said, if anyone asked — a pen. That was true in the way most lies are true. She was also looking for anything that explained the register she'd seen three nights ago, the one with her father's name drying into the last blank line like it had always been there, waiting for him the way a chair waits for someone who is expected.

The pen was on the desk. The photograph was underneath it, face-down, which no honest photograph ever is.

She turned it over and the room got very quiet, the way rooms do when they're listening.

It was a family portrait, sepia going soft at the edges, the kind taken by a traveling photographer who set up his backdrop in the parlor and charged by the head. There were six Cranes in it. A tall man with a beard like a storm front. A woman whose hands were folded so tightly in her lap that Wren could feel the tension across a hundred years. Two older children, stiff as fence posts.

And at the end of the row, holding the woman's hand, a small girl in a yellow coat.

The coat wasn't rain-yellow. It was the yellow of old paper, of things kept too long in sunlight, but Wren knew it. She had known that coat the way you know your own handwriting. She had zipped it up a thousand times in a game where Marisol pretended to be cold even though Marisol was never, ever cold.

The girl in the photograph was not smiling. She was looking directly at the camera with an expression Wren recognized from mirrors — the look of a child who has already learned that the adults around her are counting something, and that she is what's being counted.

On the back, in faded pencil: *Marisol Crane, b. 1849.*

Wren sat down on the study floor without deciding to. Her legs made the decision for her, the way legs do when the rest of you has stopped being reliable.

*Crane.*

Not Marisol-from-next-door. Not Marisol-the-imaginary, who had appeared out of the specific nowhere that seven-year-olds keep in reserve. Marisol Crane. Born 1849. Which meant that when Wren was seven, standing on this porch in her own yellow raincoat, the girl beside her had been a hundred and forty years past the age of yellow coats.

Which meant Wren hadn't invented her after all.

Which meant — and this was the thought that made the floor feel less solid than floors should be — which meant everything Wren had built her childhood out of had been real, and she'd spent twenty years apologizing to therapists for it.

* * *

She found Marisol on the landing of the east stairs, sitting with her knees drawn up, watching dust motes perform their slow ballet in the light from the window. This was Marisol's favorite occupation. Wren had forgotten that. Dust-watching. Marisol used to say the dust was the house thinking out loud.

"You were real," Wren said. No preamble. She'd learned from her father that preambles were just fear wearing a nicer coat.

Marisol didn't turn around. "You found the picture."

"Marisol Crane. Born 1849." Wren sat down on the step below her, so they were almost eye to eye, the way they used to be when Wren was small enough for this arrangement to work. "You let me believe I made you up."

"You did make me up." Now Marisol turned, and there was something in her face Wren had never seen there before — something older than the face itself, like a grandmother's expression wearing a child's skin. "That's the whole trick of it. The house can't call to people. People don't come to houses that sing under the floor. But a little girl can make a friend. And a little girl grows up, and has little girls of her own, and tells them about the friend she made here once." Marisol's hands folded in her lap, tightly, exactly like the woman in the photograph. "I was your friend so you'd come back."

The words landed in Wren's chest and just stayed there, heavy, unabsorbed, like swallowing a stone.

"The house needed a lure," Wren said slowly. Testing each word before she committed her weight to it. "Like a fishing lure."

"I'm the shiny part," Marisol agreed. Her voice was very steady. Steadier than a seven-year-old's voice should be able to be. "The first heir was my papa. He wrote the first three verses, and then he went below, and Mama couldn't finish the song because she wasn't born a Crane, only married one, and the song knows the difference. So the house kept me. It keeps whoever refuses." She shrugged, a small precise shrug. "Refusing isn't leaving. Nobody told us that part until it was too late."

"How long?"

"A hundred and sixty-one years." Marisol said it the way other children said their age, with a slight pride, and then the pride fell off her face like something dropped. "I counted at first. Then I stopped counting and started watching dust instead. Dust doesn't ask you anything."

Wren reached out and took her hand. Small. Cold in the way that photographs are cold, not the way hands are. "Why didn't you tell me? When I was little. Why let me think—"

"Because a friend you invented can't hurt you." Marisol looked down at their joined hands. "A friend who's real, who's a hundred years old and lives in a house that eats families — that friend is a thing you run from. I needed you to come back. Not now. Someday. With your own little girl someday, or your father, or anybody with Crane blood, because that's how it works, that's how it's always worked, and—" Her voice cracked, and for one moment she sounded exactly, entirely, devastatingly seven. "Because you were nice to me. You were the only person in a hundred years who was nice to me first, before you knew what I was for. I wanted to keep that. Just for a while. Just mine."

Wren's eyes were burning. She laughed instead of crying, because that was the family way, apparently, and said, "You know this is the worst friendship reveal in history. Most people just get ghosted. I got a ghost who was never a ghost."

"I don't know what 'ghosted' means but it sounds like something the house would do."

"It does, actually. Yeah." Wren wiped her face with her sleeve. "Okay. Okay. So you refused the fourth verse. My great-aunt sang it — she went below, we heard her last night. What happens if nobody sings it? Ever? If the line just... stops?"

Marisol was quiet for a long time. Outside, wind moved through the dead garden, making the bare branches tick against the windows like fingernails, politely, patiently.

"Then the house gets hungry," she said finally. "And hungry houses don't wait for invitations. They count faster. They open doors that shouldn't be doors." She looked up, and her eyes were the exact gray of the sky over Hollowmere. "Wren. How many doors did your daddy count today?"

"Eleven. He counts every morning. Eleven, he says, like it's a prayer."

"What about last night?"

Wren opened her mouth. Closed it. Last night, coming back from the cellar with the music box, she had passed the hall closet, except it hadn't been the hall closet, it had been a door she'd never seen, and she'd been too tired and too frightened to mention it.

"Twelve," she whispered.

Marisol nodded, unsurprised, unsurprising, and Wren understood suddenly that Marisol had known this was coming the way you know weather. Had maybe always known, through a hundred and sixty-one years of dust-watching, that eventually a Wren would sit on these stairs and find a photograph and the counting would start again.

"There's one more thing," Marisol said. "About why the house keeps me. It's not only the lure."

"Then what?"

"The house doesn't remember how to be a home anymore. It remembers me. I'm the last part of it that was ever loved without owing anything." Marisol smiled, and it was the smile from all those summers, the gap-toothed sunshine smile, and it broke Wren's heart clean in half because now she could see the effort in it, the hundred and sixty-one years of practice behind it. "So it keeps me the way you keep a photograph. Except photographs don't get lonely."

* * *

They went down to the kitchen together, because grief is easier with tea in it, even tea made on a stove that lit reluctantly and smelled faintly of the cellar. Wren put the kettle on and Marisol sat on the counter, which had been against the rules once, and swung her feet, which had never been against any rules because some things are universal.

"So the register," Wren said. "Every Crane. All of them went below."

"All but the refusers. There've been four of us. Me, and a boy named Aldous in 1911, and twin sisters in 1953 who refused together, which the house hated, and..." Marisol trailed off.

"And?"

"And your aunt. Your father's aunt. She refused for thirty years, Wren. Thirty years she lived here and wouldn't sing the fourth verse, and the house was patient, the house is so patient, and then one winter she got sick, and sick people will trade anything for more time, and she traded." Marisol picked at a chip in the countertop. "We heard her singing it. Down there. All night. She got the verses wrong on purpose, at the end. Trying to break it from inside. The house doesn't like that."

Wren poured the water. Watched the steam climb. Thought about her father, who had started humming three days ago without noticing, who had caught himself twice and gone pale both times, who had stood in the hallway this morning with his lips moving around syllables like a man rehearsing a speech he hoped he'd never give.

"She's still down there," Wren said. "Your papa. Aldous. The twins. Aunt Josephine. All of them."

"Mm-hm."

"And they're waiting for someone to finish the verse so they can stop."

"They're waiting for someone to finish the verse so they can stop," Marisol confirmed, "and the house is waiting for someone to finish the verse so it can keep them, and everybody's waiting, and that's the whole house, really. A big room full of waiting." She hopped off the counter. "Your tea's steeping wrong. You always let it go bitter when you're sad."

"You remember that?"

"I remember everything about you. That's the curse of being kept. You keep other things too." Marisol paused at the kitchen door, small and yellow and impossibly old, and for a second her outline seemed to flicker, like a candle considering going out. "Wren. When you figure out what to do — and you will, you were always the one who figured things out, I just watched dust — when you figure it out, don't do it for the house's reasons. The house will offer you a deal. It's very good at deals. It learned them from the Cranes."

"What kind of deal?"

But Marisol was already moving toward the stairs, and behind her, faint and rising, came a sound that stopped Wren's blood mid-circulation: her father's voice, upstairs in the study, low and careful, singing the first verse of a lullaby he claimed he'd never learned.

He hit every note.

The kettle screamed, and downstairs, beneath the floor, beneath the shallow dirt and old stone, something began — softly, gratefully, in many thin voices — to hum along.
