## 14. Thirteen Doors

Wren had started keeping her own tally on the back of a receipt from a gas station two hundred miles away, because if the house was going to count things, she wanted to count them right back. It felt like a small act of rebellion, the way flipping off a traffic camera feels like rebellion — technically true, practically useless, and weirdly satisfying.

Eleven doors. That was what the house was supposed to have. Cyrus had said so on their first night, tapping his surveyor's notes with the particular satisfaction of a man who has found an error in someone else's arithmetic. Eleven doors, and he'd measured every one of them twice, because measuring things twice was how Cyrus Crane loved the world.

By the third morning there were twelve.

She found the twelfth door between the pantry and the back stairs, in a stretch of wall she had walked past a dozen times without noticing anything amiss. It was painted the same faded green as the rest of the woodwork, and it fit the wall the way a word fits into a sentence that has been waiting for it. When she put her hand flat against it, the wood was warm.

"You're not supposed to be here," she told it.

The door did not answer, which she appreciated. She was getting tired of things answering.

* * *

Marisol sat on the kitchen counter with her yellow raincoat pooled around her like melted butter, swinging legs that hadn't grown in twenty years, watching Wren make tea she wouldn't drink. Marisol never drank anything. Wren had stopped finding this sad around day four and started finding it practical — one less cup to wash.

"Twelve," Marisol said, before Wren could say anything.

"How do you know?"

"I can hear it." Marisol tapped her temple. "It's like when you know your mom's awake downstairs even though you can't see her. The house gets a different kind of quiet." She frowned at her own knees. "Wren. Twelve is bad. Thirteen is worse."

"What happens at thirteen?"

Marisol's mouth opened. Closed. She looked, for a moment, like a child trying to repeat a swear word she'd been told never to say, testing its weight behind her teeth. Then she shook her head hard enough that her dark hair swung. "I'm not allowed to count that high."

"You counted to a hundred last Tuesday. You were very smug about it."

"That's different." She slid off the counter and landed with no sound at all, which Wren still hadn't gotten used to. Raincoats should thump. "Counting doors is different. Counting doors is letting it count you back." She took Wren's hand, small fingers cold as milk bottles. "Don't touch the new door, okay? Don't knock. Don't lean on it. Don't even think about it too loud."

"Can you think quietly?"

"No," Marisol said seriously. "That's why I don't do it much."

* * *

Of course Wren touched the new door. Not that day — she had some respect for the rules, or at least for the fear underneath them — but that evening, passing through the kitchen with an armful of firewood, she caught herself leaning against the wall beside it while she shifted her grip, shoulder blades brushing the green paint, and the wood beneath her was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, and something on the other side of the wall leaned gently back.

She dropped the firewood. Logs scattered across the flagstones like startled animals. From the study, her father called, "Everything all right?" in the voice of a man who had learned that week that 'everything all right' was a question with no good answers in this house.

"Fine," she called back, and stood there with her heart going like a trapped bird, and the door stayed shut, and somehow that was worse than if it had opened. A door that opens is honest. A door that waits is a promise being made about you without you.

That night the lullaby came up through the floorboards again, three verses, sung by voices thin as tissue paper, and Wren lay in bed and hummed along under her breath without deciding to, and only realized she was doing it when Marisol appeared at the foot of her bed — Marisol could appear anywhere now; the house had stopped pretending to keep her out — with her hands clamped over her ears and her face crumpled like wet paper.

"Don't learn it," Marisol whispered. "Please don't learn it. Your dad's already learning it and now you are too and I can't— I'm not allowed to tell you what happens."

"Then don't tell me," Wren said. "Show me."

Marisol looked at her for a long moment. Twenty years old and seven years old at once, and tired in a way no child should know how to look. "You won't like it."

"I haven't liked anything since Tuesday."

* * *

What Marisol showed her was the hallway.

They walked it together at midnight, barefoot, Wren holding a candle stub because the house's electricity had developed opinions about which rooms deserved light. The hallway ran from the front stairs toward the east wing, and Wren knew its length the way you know the length of your own arm. Fourteen steps past the linen closet, turn at the window seat.

At step fourteen, the hallway continued.

Not dramatically. That was the horrible part. There was no thunderclap, no groaning of boards. The wall that had always been there simply wasn't, and where it had been, the hallway went on — same runner carpet, same wainscoting, same wallpaper patterned with faded birds that might have been swallows or might have been something with longer wings. Another door stood ahead of them. Green paint. Brass handle worn bright by hands.

"Thirteen," Marisol whispered.

And then, because the house apparently believed in symmetry, Wren turned and saw that behind them, where the hallway had ended against the west wall for as long as anyone alive could remember, there was another door. Also green. Also waiting.

Fourteen steps forward. Fourteen steps back. And between them, the house breathing.

"It does this when it's almost ready," Marisol said. Her hand found Wren's. "When it's almost done counting."

"Done counting what?"

Marisol didn't answer. But she didn't let go of Wren's hand either, and they walked forward together, because Wren Crane had inherited her father's fatal conviction that a room you haven't seen is a room that's lying to you.

* * *

The first door opened onto the cellar stairs.

Of course it did. Down and down, farther than the house was tall, into the dark where the chalk tallies climbed the walls in a child's handwriting — her handwriting, aged seven, tallying doors that hadn't existed yet, because the house had shown them to her early, the way you show a child photographs of relatives they'll meet someday. Cold air rose up the stairwell carrying dust and candle smoke and, faintly, sweetly, the sound of many mouths moving in unison. Not singing yet. Breathing in time.

Wren closed that door with both hands, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping thing.

The second door opened onto her childhood.

* * *

It was her bedroom. Not a copy of it — the house didn't do copies, the house did originals, kept fresh — but the actual room, the one from the summer she was seven, the summer her father had brought her to Aunt Odile's house while her mother was in the hospital, the summer that had ended with a car ride home and a silence between father and daughter that had lasted, in various forms, twenty years.

The wallpaper was blue with white stars. Her mother had painted stars on a ceiling once, in the house they'd left. Wren had cried for that ceiling for a year.

Her small bed stood against the wall, turned down. Not just made — turned down, the blanket folded back in a neat triangle, the pillow plumped, the way you prepare a bed for someone you've been expecting. On the nightstand: a glass of water, still sweating with cold. A book, spine cracked at her favorite page. And a jar of lemon drops, the kind her mother bought from the shop by the hospital, the kind nobody sold anymore, full and gleaming and impossibly patient.

On the floor, arranged in a careful circle, sat her toys. Mr. Buttons the rabbit, whose ear she'd chewed ragged. The tin horse with the chipped leg. All of them exactly as she'd left them, except clean, except mended, except waiting.

"Oh," Wren said. It came out very small.

"I tried to warn you," Marisol said miserably, from somewhere near her elbow.

"This is—" Wren stepped inside. The room smelled like it should smell, like crayons and rain and the lavender sachet Aunt Odile kept in the linens. "This is the summer I—"

"The summer you were happy here." Marisol's voice was gentle. "Before everything got heavy. The house kept it. The house keeps everything, Wren. That's the whole problem."

Wren sat down on the edge of the small bed, and the mattress received her with a sigh of springs, and her feet didn't quite reach the floor.

That was the moment she understood she was in trouble. Because her feet didn't reach the floor. She was twenty-eight years old, five foot six, and sitting on a child's bed in a preserved room, and her feet dangled like a seven-year-old's, and it felt — God help her, it felt *good*. It felt like being held. Like being the exact size the world expected her to be. Somewhere below her, faintly, the singers breathed in time, and the house breathed with them, and the breath moved through the little room like a tide moving through a shell, and Wren thought, with the calm clarity of someone already halfway underwater: *I could stay here. This is the room where my mother was still alive at the edges of the day. This is the room where my father still carried me upstairs when I fell asleep on the sofa. Everything I actually wanted is in this room, and the house knows it, because the house was listening the whole time.*

"Wren." Marisol tugged her sleeve. "Your feet."

Wren looked down. Looked at her own hands — smaller. Smooth. Seven years old, holding a glass of water that sweated cold against her palm.

She set the glass down very carefully and stood up, and the room resisted her standing the way deep water resists a swimmer rising, a soft pressure, a reluctance, a *stay, stay, we kept it all for you* — and then she was through the doorway and in the midnight hallway, twenty-eight again, heart slamming, hands shaking, adult-sized and furious about it.

Marisol pulled the door shut. It closed without a sound, which was somehow the loudest thing Wren had ever heard.

* * *

They sat on the window seat at the end of the hall until the shaking stopped, sharing the candle stub between them like conspirators.

"It offered me my feet not reaching the floor," Wren said finally. "That was its big move. That's what it's got."

"It's not nothing," Marisol said. "It's the best thing it has. It kept your summer perfect. It's been keeping it perfect for twenty years, waiting." She hugged her knees. "It doesn't understand that people grow. That's its whole sickness. It wants everyone the size it remembers."

Downstairs, a door opened and closed. Footsteps on the stairs — her father's tread, heavier than it used to be, slower, and Wren noticed with a lurch that she could hear it *wrong*: half a beat ahead of where it should be, arriving before him, the house counting his footsteps for him now, keeping his rhythm the way it kept her summers.

Cyrus appeared at the top of the stairs holding his own candle, and stopped when he saw them, and Wren watched him take in the scene — his daughter and the impossible child, the candle, the hallway that was visibly, measurably longer than it had been that morning — and she watched him do the thing he always did, the thing that was both his greatest flaw and his most stubborn grace: he reached for his notebook.

"Dad," she said quietly. "How many doors did you count today?"

He didn't pretend not to understand. He flipped the notebook open, and the candlelight showed her the columns of figures, the obsessive neatness of his hand, and the newest entry, written in ink that still shone wet:

**13.**

"Thirteen," Cyrus said. "As of six o'clock this evening." He looked down the hallway, at the two green doors standing at either end of it like bookends, like parentheses, like arms. "And Wren — the extra two aren't new construction. I measured. They were always in the walls. Every measurement I've taken since I got here, every wrong number — the house wasn't failing to add up." He closed the notebook slowly. "It was adding up to this."

Behind the door at the far end of the hall, faint and sweet and terribly patient, the singers below drew a breath — all of them together, one long indrawn breath, like a choir before the downbeat — and held it.

Held it.

Waiting to see who would give them the note.
