## 11. Ruthie's Verse

The name came to her the way names always did at KRVL—through the dead.

It was three in the morning, two nights after the Listeners had spoken their pieces on air, and Odile sat in the studio with the board lights washing her hands green, transcribing what she'd recorded. She had learned not to trust her memory with them anymore. Memory was a door too, and things walked through it when she wasn't looking.

*Voss,* the alto voice had said, clear as a struck glass amid the chorus of the others. *Ruthie Voss. Second from the left. I never got my verse.*

Odile had played the tape back eleven times. On the twelfth, she stopped hearing the voice and started hearing the girl inside it—young, frightened, patient in the way only the dead are patient—and she reached for the phone book of a city that no longer kept one, and then for the county records instead, and then, when those failed her, for Father Ansel Brandt, who answered his telephone on the first ring as though he had been sitting beside it for fifty years waiting for exactly this call.

"Ruthie Voss," he repeated, and Odile heard something in his voice that she had not heard before: relief. Terrible, guilty relief. "You found her name."

"She gave it to me."

"Yes." A long breath, papery and slow. "She would. Ruthie was always the responsible one. Even at seventeen." Another breath. "If you want her music, you'll want her sister. Miriam. Miriam Voss-Hale. She lives out past the reservoir—the blue house with the iron rooster on the fence post. You can't miss it. Nobody else ever built out there."

"Why not?"

A silence long enough that Odile checked the connection.

"Because Miriam asked them not to," Brandt said, "and in 1974, people still believed a woman like Miriam when she said a thing."

* * *

The drive took her forty minutes through country that grew progressively less certain of itself—subdivision giving way to farmland giving way to scrub pine and rusted fence line, the road narrowing until her headlights seemed to be the only thing holding the dark back. The reservoir appeared on her left as a black absence, flat and vast, and she caught herself counting the doors of the houses she passed—one, two, three—a habit so old it had become a form of breathing.

Her mother had taught her that. *Thresholds remember,* Vivian Crane used to say, kneeling in doorways with small Odile beside her, touching the frame the way you'd touch a scar. *Every doorway keeps an account of everyone who ever crossed it. So be polite going through. Say thank you to the wood.* Odile had thought it was a game then. She had spent the last months of her mother's life understanding it was a survival manual.

The blue house sat alone where the road gave up, paint gone gray-blue like old denim, and on the fence post before it stood an iron rooster, weathered to a green-black patina, its beak open in a crow that had been silent for half a century. Every window was lit. That was the first strange thing. The second was that all the curtains were drawn behind the light, so the house glowed like a lantern with its shutter closed—giving off brightness but showing nothing.

Odile parked and sat with her hands on the wheel.

She had rehearsed this. In the car, over the last ten miles, she had rehearsed it: *My name is Odile Crane, I host the graveyard shift, I think your sister has been trying to reach me.* But every version sounded insane, and she had learned in the last weeks that insanity was not a persuasion strategy. It was simply the truth wearing its worst clothes.

She got out. The night smelled of water and cold iron. As she crossed the yard, she noticed the ground itself: a wide circle around the house, maybe thirty feet across, where nothing grew. Not dead grass—no grass at all, just packed pale earth, swept clean, deliberate. Someone had maintained that bare ring for years. Decades.

The door opened before she knocked.

The woman standing there was small and straight-backed, somewhere north of eighty, with white hair pinned in a knot and eyes the color of wet slate. She wore a cardigan buttoned to the throat despite the warmth of the house spilling out around her. She looked at Odile for a long moment, and Odile watched recognition move across her face like weather—not surprise, not confusion, but grief arriving on schedule.

"You have her chin," Miriam Voss-Hale said. "Vivian Crane's chin. I'd know it anywhere. I sang next to that chin for six years." She stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before you let the night in with you. And wipe your feet. I mean that more than it sounds."

* * *

The interior of the house was warm and close and smelled of cedar and old paper. Every surface held lamps—dozens of them, all burning, on tables and shelves and windowsills, little brass lamps and ceramic lamps and one enormous hurricane lamp on the kitchen table like a centerpiece. Odile counted without meaning to. Thirty-one. Thirty-two if you counted the nightlight glowing in the hallway.

"You're wondering about the lights," Miriam said, pouring tea Odile hadn't asked for into cups that had clearly been waiting. "Lights are cheap. Darkness is expensive. You learn the exchange rate, you live alone as long as I have." She set a cup in front of Odile and sat down across from her, folding her hands. "Ansel called me yesterday. First time in nine years. He said a girl would come asking after Ruthie's music, and he said I should give it to her, and I told him Ansel Brandt could go straight to the hell he helped build." Her slate eyes didn't blink. "So convince me why I shouldn't feel the same about you."

"I don't know if I can," Odile said. It came out honest, which surprised her. "I think—I think your sister is one of the voices calling my radio station. I think she's been calling it since before I understood who was calling. I think she wants to finish a song she never got to finish, and I think—" She stopped. Started again. "I think my mother ran out on something in 1974, and everything since then has been the interest accruing on that debt. And I think Ruthie paid in her place."

Miriam was quiet for a long time. The lamps hummed. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe ticked like a patient clock.

"Ruthie was the youngest," she said finally. "Seventeen. Alto two. She used to practice in the hayloft because the acoustics were good and because Ma couldn't stand hearing the same eight bars over and over." A ghost of a smile touched the old woman's mouth and was gone. "The night of the fire, I was supposed to be there. I had a cold. Ruthie teased me about it all week—*you'll miss the broadcast, Miri, we're going to be famous.*" Her hands tightened around her own cup. "I've had that cold for fifty years now. Never really shaken it."

"The sheet music," Odile said gently. "Brandt says you have it."

"I have everything of hers." Miriam rose, joints cracking like kindling, and went to a cabinet against the far wall—an ordinary oak cabinet, except that it was bolted shut, four bolts, one at each corner, the metal bright with use while the wood around it had gone soft and dark with handling. From a drawer she took a key ring and unlocked the bolts one by one, and Odile noticed that her hands shook, and that they shook in a practiced way, the tremor of someone who had done a frightening thing many hundreds of times and never gotten used to it.

Inside the cabinet: a shoebox, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed string. And beneath it, filling the rest of the space, row upon row of hymnals—dozens of them, different editions, different printings, collected across decades. Odile leaned closer and saw that in every single one, the same pages had been torn out.

"You bought every copy you could find," Odile said slowly.

"Every printing of the Aldermoor hymnal, 1961 through 1977. Then the reprints. Then anything with the hymn in it." Miriam set the shoebox on the table between them but did not untie it. "Page 114. *The Deep Responds.* It was a local piece—some organist wrote it for the chapel in the twenties, never published wide. Four verses. After the fire, the diocese pulled it from circulation, and I made sure of the rest." She looked up, and her eyes were terrible and steady. "Do you know why, girl? Do you know what happens when that song gets sung?"

"It calls something," Odile said. "That's what you believe."

"That's what I *know*." For the first time, Miriam's composure cracked, and underneath it Odile saw not madness but exhaustion—the bone-deep fatigue of a lifelong vigil. "Three days after the fire, I found Ruthie's folder in the rubble line. Her music. I brought it home and I put it on the piano, because I was eighteen and stupid and grieving, and I wanted to hear her one more time. I played the opening bars. Just the opening. Eight notes." Her voice dropped. "And the house answered."

"Answered how?"

"Harmonized." The word fell into the room like a stone into still water. "Something under the floor, under the ground, under whatever is under the ground—it picked up the line and carried it, low, like men singing in a cistern. And then every door in this house opened at once. Every one. And the lights all went out at the same instant, and in the dark I heard footsteps coming up from the cellar, slow, polite, unhurried, like a choir filing in to take their places." She pressed her palm flat against the tabletop as though steadying herself against a swell. "I got the candles lit. I got the folder locked up. The footsteps stopped at the top of the cellar stairs. They stayed there till dawn. And then they went back down, and I nailed the cellar door shut, and I have kept that music locked and that song unsung for fifty years, and in fifty years nothing in this house has opened that I didn't open myself."

Odile's skin had gone cold. "The bare ring outside. The dirt."

"Salt and ash, renewed every season. Things cross a threshold easier over living ground." Miriam almost smiled. "Your mother taught me that trick, actually. Did you know that? Vivian came here once, years ago. Must have been twenty years back. She stood in my kitchen and looked at the cabinet and cried, and she told me to keep doing what I was doing, and she said—" the old woman's voice changed, softened, became an imitation of another voice, tender and broken— "*keep it locked, Miriam, and someday my daughter will come, and God forgive me, you'll have to let her take it.*"

The tea had gone cold in Odile's cup. She stared at it.

"She knew I'd come."

"Vivian Crane knew everything and said nothing, that was her whole religion." Miriam's hand came down over the shoebox, protective even now. "So here is my question, and think carefully, because I am an old woman and I have burned a great many bridges to keep this box shut. If I give you this music—if I let the song back into the world—what stops it from happening again? What stops it from singing itself? What stops it from taking *you*?"

"Nothing," Odile said. "Nothing stops it. That's rather the point."

Miriam studied her for a long, long moment. Outside, wind moved across the reservoir, and the iron rooster on the fence post creaked on its stake, opening and closing its silent beak.

* * *

They untied the string together, which Miriam insisted on, saying that some things should not be done by one pair of hands alone. Inside the oilcloth, the folder was schoolroom cardboard gone soft as cloth, and inside the folder lay Ruthie Voss's sheet music—hand-copied, in a young girl's careful pencil, the notation precise and pretty, decorated in the margins with small doodled birds.

Page 114. All four verses.

Odile turned to the fourth verse and felt the air in the room change—felt the thirty-two lamps lean, almost imperceptibly, toward the page, flames bending as if in a draft that wasn't there. The torn-out verse. The completion. The words her mother had ripped from every hymnal within reach, written here in a dead girl's pencil:

*What was given, given whole,*
*What was taken, taken sweet,*
*Sing the answer, sing the call,*
*Till the singer and the sung shall meet.*

Below the final line, in smaller letters, a note in the same young hand: *Solo line goes to V.C. — she says the answering part feels like falling.*

Odile's throat closed. Her mother. Seventeen years older than Ruthie and already marked for the solo, already feeling the shape of the fall.

"She wrote that the morning of the fire," Miriam said quietly. "I know because she showed me at breakfast. She was so proud. She said when the broadcast aired, everyone would hear her verse." The old woman closed her eyes. "Everyone heard it. That was the problem."

Then Miriam did something Odile did not expect. She pushed the folder across the table—all the way, deliberately, until it rested against Odile's hands—and then she covered it with both of her own, pinning it there, and looked up with her wet-slate eyes full of fifty years of held breath.

"Take it," she said. "Take it and finish what they couldn't. But I'm asking you one thing, girl, and I need you to swear it on your mother's grave, because I will know if you lie—your chin is her chin, and I will know." Her grip tightened on the folder, on Odile's fingers beneath it. "When it's done—when the debt is paid or the door is shut or whatever it is you mean to do—burn it. Burn the music. Don't lock it away like I did, don't leave it for some other fool daughter to find in fifty years. Sing it once, sing it true, and then let it end. Promise me the song dies with the thing it feeds."

"I promise," Odile said.

"And one more." Miriam's voice dropped to nearly nothing. "If you see her—if Ruthie speaks to you, if she asks you anything—tell her Miri kept the birds. Tell her I drew birds on everything for years after, trying to remember how hers looked. Tell her the hayloft acoustics were never as good as she thought. Tell her—" and here the old woman's composure went entirely to pieces, quietly, the way old structures fail, all at once and without sound— "tell her I'm sorry I had the cold."

Odile put her free hand over Miriam's knotted fingers. "I'll tell her."

The lamps flared, all thirty-two at once, bright enough to throw their shadows hard against the walls—and in the instant of that flare, Odile would swear afterward, she heard it: faint, beneath the floor, beneath the earth, a low harmonic sliding into place like a key finding its lock. Something, somewhere below the reservoir and the roots and the rock, had just been told that the song was coming home.

Miriam heard it too. She went white. But she didn't take the folder back.

"Go," she whispered. "Go now, before it gets brave. And girl—" Odile paused at the door, the folder pressed to her chest, and Miriam Voss-Hale stood among her thirty-two burning lamps looking suddenly very small and very old and very much alone. "When you meet the thing under the chapel—when it offers you the deal it offered them—remember that your mother didn't slip out because she was a coward. She slipped out because she was pregnant with you. Whatever you decide at that door, decide it knowing you were the reason she ran."

The door closed behind Odile with the softness of a held breath released.

She drove back toward the city with the folder on the passenger seat, and she did not turn on the radio, and she did not need to. Halfway home, at the point where the road bent along the black water of the reservoir, the car's speakers clicked on by themselves—volume low, courteous, almost gentle—and a voice she knew began to hum the opening bars of *The Deep Responds*, keeping her company through the dark.

It was Ruthie's voice. And it was practicing.
