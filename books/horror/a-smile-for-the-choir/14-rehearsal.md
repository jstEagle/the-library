## 14. Rehearsal

The manuscript smelled like a drawer that had been closed since the Nixon administration—camphor, dust, and beneath it something sweeter, like flowers left too long in water. Odile laid Ruthie Voss's sheet music flat on the studio table under the gooseneck lamp, smoothing the curled corners with both hands, the way you'd calm a frightened animal.

She had taken the long way home from Margaret Voss's house, the manuscript in a manila envelope on the passenger seat like something radioactive. Twice she'd pulled over and just looked at it. Twice she'd thought about the old woman's voice—*burn it, burn it, I begged my mother to burn it and she wouldn't, and look what it cost*—and twice she'd started the car again anyway, because the alternative was a city full of sleeping listeners with something low and hungry humming underneath their dreams.

The station was hers at this hour. Marcus had gone home at eleven, or said he had; she'd heard him in the parking lot afterward, sitting in his car with the engine off, and she'd pretended not to notice. Since the night his mouth had betrayed him—since she'd found him in the stairwell, jaw working silently around a melody he couldn't name, tears running into his collar—he'd stopped calling her crazy. That was almost worse. Fear had made him gentle, and gentleness from Marcus was like snow in July: beautiful, and a sign something was deeply wrong.

She locked the outer doors. Counted them: three. Counted the lights: the board, the ON AIR sign, the lamp. Her mother's arithmetic, performed nightly, the way other people said their prayers. *Thresholds remember*, her mother used to say, and Odile had thought it was just one of those things mothers said, like *wear a coat*, until the dead started calling and every threshold in her life began to feel like a mouth.

She set the manuscript on the mixing desk and looked at it for a long time before she touched it again.

* * *

The hymn had no name. That was the first thing she understood. Where the title should have been, the paper was blank—not worn blank, not faded, but blank in the way a tooth socket is blank. The ink of the rest of the page sat around the absence like a crowd around an empty chair. Ruthie Voss had written out the whole thing in a careful alto's hand, pencil for the harmony line, ink for the melody, and at the top, where a title belonged, nothing. As if the song had refused to be named. As if naming it would be an invitation.

The notation was old-fashioned, four-part harmony, the kind her mother had sight-read at the kitchen table while Odile did homework and pretended not to listen. The first three verses were complete. The fourth—the final verse—was written for two voices only, and not in the ordinary way. The lines interleaved, one voice starting a phrase as the other finished it, the melody handed back and forth like something breakable, like a child passed over a fence in a fire. Canon, the old woman had called it. Call and answer. You could not sing it alone. The music itself would not allow it. Where the second voice entered, the first voice's line simply stopped, mid-phrase, mid-word sometimes, waiting. The page was full of pauses shaped like open doors.

Odile sat with that for a long time.

Then she reached for her mother's tapes.

* * *

The storage unit had given up eleven reels in all, and Odile had listened to only fragments—the way you test a stove with your hand before committing to the heat. Her mother's voice lived in those reels the way a face lives in a mirror: so familiar it stopped being a voice at all and became a room she'd grown up in. Tonight, in the dead station, with the manuscript open in front of her, she threaded the first reel with hands that were not quite steady.

The tape hissed. Then her mother, young—nineteen, maybe twenty, the voice still carrying its childhood at the bottom of it—sang.

It was the hymn.

Odile's whole body went cold and then hot. She had expected fragments, practice runs, maybe a different piece entirely. But the reel held the hymn, verse by verse, sung slowly, deliberately, in a small room with hard walls—a bathroom, maybe, or a stairwell—recorded close to the microphone, almost whispered at the top of the phrases. Not a performance. A lesson. A woman teaching a song to someone who wasn't in the room yet.

To someone who wasn't born yet.

"Verse one," her mother said between takes, and her speaking voice did something to Odile's chest that singing never had. It was the voice from a hundred phone calls home, from *did you lock the door, Odile, count them twice*. "Again. Slower this time. Don't push the high part. It isn't a climb, it's a hand reaching down."

Odile stopped the tape. Sat in the dark with the reels stacked beside her like small, patient animals.

*She knew.* Not suspected, not feared—knew, the way you know a load-bearing wall. Her mother had recorded these lessons knowing her daughter would someday need them, the way you leave a will, the way you teach a child to swim before the flood. And the note in the hymnal—*Never sing along*—suddenly read differently. Not *never sing this song*. *Never sing along.* Because the song required two, and to sing along with the wrong voice was to answer the wrong call.

Her mother hadn't been keeping the song from her. She'd been keeping it *for* her, in the only vault she trusted: a storage unit, a shoebox of reels, a daughter raised on thresholds and counted lights.

Odile pressed play again and let her mother teach her.

* * *

She worked the way she did everything at KRVL—alone, in the small hours, with the ON AIR sign dark and the transmitter humming its low note through the floor like a sleeping animal's breath. She sang quietly, under her mother's recorded voice, matching pitch, matching breath. The first verse was simple, almost plain, a melody that moved the way a procession moves. The second added a descant that made the back of her neck prickle. The third was where the harmony turned strange—where the four parts stopped moving together and began, very slightly, to disagree, the way family members disagree at a funeral, politely, in the same key.

And the fourth verse stopped her cold every time, because it stopped itself. The manuscript's first voice would rise to the top of a phrase and hang there, an open interval, a question mark made of sound, and Odile's throat would close around the answer because the answer wasn't hers. It was written for the second voice, the answering line, and the answering line was not in Ruthie Voss's manuscript at all.

It took her until nearly three in the morning to understand why.

She was splicing by then—she'd dragged out the old editing block from the equipment cage, the razor blade and the grease pencil, tools older than she was, and she'd been marking the reels for the passages she needed: her mother singing the answering phrases, scattered across eleven reels like beads from a broken necklace. It was on the seventh reel, between two takes of the third verse, that her mother stopped singing and simply talked, the way a person talks when they're alone and tired and the recorder is the only witness.

"The last verse needs two," her mother said. "It was always two. That's the whole trick of it, I think. That's why It wants a choir and not a congregation. One voice is a person. Two voices in canon is a *promise*—one made and one kept, back and forth, forever if you do it right." A pause, tape hiss like distant surf. "It bound Itself with a promise once. That's what Father Brandt says. It doesn't understand songs that keep going after the singers stop. It's starving for an ending."

Another pause. Longer. And then, so quietly Odile leaned into the speaker:

"If you're hearing this, Odile. If it's you. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, baby. I tried to end it in '74 and I couldn't, and I've been paying for that in installments ever since, and I always knew the balance would come to you." Her mother's breath caught, steadied. "The answering line is yours to give. But listen to me. *Listen to me.* When you sing it—when you give it—don't go through the door to do it. Sing from your side. Make It come to the song. Doors that open for a voice on the threshold—"

The tape ended. Not faded—ended, cut clean, the splice visible even in the dark, a physical scar in the brown ribbon.

Odile sat with her hands flat on the desk, feeling her own pulse, counting it the way she counted lights and doors. *Doors that open for a voice on the threshold—* and then nothing. Her mother had been cut off mid-warning, or had cut herself off, and either way the sentence hung in the air of the studio like a note that refused to resolve.

*Don't go through the door.*

But the door under the chapel opened inward only for someone willing to stay. That was the whole arithmetic of it. That was the deal as old as the fire. A song for a singer. A completion for a keeper.

Unless her mother had found another way. Unless the splice she'd been building—call and answer, Ruthie's verse and her mother's tape braided across fifty years—was itself the other way. Two voices, one living and one recorded, one on each side of everything. A canon sung through a wall.

Or unless her mother had simply run out of tape, and of time, and the warning had died in the machine with everything else.

Odile got up and checked the doors. Three. Checked the lights. Three. The repetition didn't calm her tonight, but she did it anyway, because her mother had taught her to, and because tonight it felt less like a ritual and more like a message being sent down a very long wire.

* * *

By four she had the answering line assembled. It was ugly work, the splices—her mother's voice jumping between takes, between years, the room tone shifting under her like tide—but the melody held. The answering line was a descending figure, mostly, patient, always arriving just after the call, always a little lower, the way an answer is lower than a question, the way a hand extended downward is lower than the hand that reaches for it. Spliced end to end it made a strange, breathing music: her mother at nineteen, at twenty-five, at thirty, at forty, answering a call that hadn't been sung yet.

Answering *her*.

Because that was the last thing Odile understood, sitting in the blue hour with the assembled tape threaded and ready, and it settled over her not like dread but like something warmer and much more terrible.

The final verse required two voices. Call and answer. And when she sang Ruthie's line in this studio, alone, at the top of each phrase, hanging on the open interval—her mother's recorded voice would answer. Had already answered. Had been answering, on these reels, for fifty years, waiting in the magnetic dark for the call to arrive.

Her mother had built a duet across death. Had spent decades of nights in small rooms with a microphone, cutting the answering line into pieces and hiding the pieces where only her daughter would find them, so that when the debt came due—*and I always knew the balance would come to you*—Odile would not have to stand at that door alone. Would not have to stand at it at all, if her mother's plan held. Would only have to sing, and be answered, the way a child in the dark calls out and a voice comes back: *I'm here. Count the doors. I'm here.*

Odile put her forehead down on the desk, on the cold metal beside the faders, and cried the way she hadn't since the funeral—briefly, silently, with the discipline of someone who has learned that grief is a door too, and you don't leave it open.

Then she wiped her face, sat up, and ran the verse again. Call. Answer. Call. Answer. Her voice and the ghost of her mother's, stitching themselves together in the dead air of a dead station, and it was beautiful, and that was the problem, because she could feel—she could *feel*, the way you feel weather changing through a window—how much the thing below wanted to hear it. How the transmitter's hum under the floor had shifted, tonight, into something almost like attention. How every perfect phrase was a hand reaching down into the dark, and something down there was rising toward it, patient, grateful, and *hungry*.

At 5:47, as the sky outside the studio's one window went the color of dishwater, the phone rang.

The board showed no caller ID. It never did anymore.

Odile looked at it for four rings. Five. She knew the rules. She'd written the rules. But the rules had been bending all month, and the last caller who'd broken them gently had mentioned her own funeral the way you mention the weather, and the callers since had stopped being people at all.

She picked up.

For a moment: nothing. The breathing-in-unison she knew, the tide-sound of many lungs. Then a single voice separated from the chorus, young, female, an alto's voice, and it did not demand and did not plead. It said, in a tone of terrible kindness:

"That was lovely, Odile. She'd be proud." A pause, and Odile heard—she would swear on anything, on the counted doors, on her mother's grave—she heard the choir smile. "You don't need to come to the door to finish it. That's true, what she told you. But you should know the other half of it."

The line hissed. Somewhere under the hiss, the harmonic turned over like something waking.

"The door doesn't only open for the one who stays," the alto said. "It opens for the one who *calls*. And you've been calling all night."

The dial tone came, soft as a held breath released.

Odile sat in the graying light, the manuscript open, the spliced reel turning slowly to its end, and understood that the rehearsal was over. Whatever she did next would be the performance, and the audience had already taken their seats, and they were very, very patient, and they had been waiting since 1974 for the last verse of a song that could only be sung by a daughter standing at a door—singing, or staying, or both.

She reached out and stopped the tape.

Outside, far below the city, far below the pit and the concrete and the counted days, something began—very quietly, in a voice like her mother's—to sing the call.
