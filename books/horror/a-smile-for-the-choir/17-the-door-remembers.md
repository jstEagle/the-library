## 17. The Door Remembers

The pit had been waiting for her all week. Odile could feel it the way you feel a phone ringing in another room—not a sound, exactly, but an insistence, a pressure behind the sternum that no amount of coffee or dead air could quiet.

She told herself she was going for Marcus's sake, to see if the concrete trucks were really coming Friday. She told herself it was professional curiosity, that KRVL still had listeners who deserved to know why their parking garage smelled like hymns. She told herself a lot of things at three in the morning, and most of them were lies she'd gotten good at living inside.

The chain-link around the excavation site had been cut and re-tied so many times it looked like lace. Odile slipped through the gap behind the generator trailer, her boots finding the mud ruts left by the excavator, and stood at the edge of the pit with her flashlight off, letting her eyes adjust.

Below her, maybe twenty feet down, the door stood in its frame of older stone, exposed now on three sides where the crew had dug away everything that had hidden it for fifty years. The floodlights had been shut off since the city pulled the crew—someone had remembered, or someone had been made to remember, that leaving lights burning over an open hole invited things up out of it—but the moon was high and white and generous tonight, and it fell into the pit like water into a cup.

The door was warm. She knew this from the foreman's interview, from the way he'd said *warm* twice, as if once hadn't been enough to make anyone believe him. Stone didn't stay warm through a November night. Stone didn't hum, either, but Odile could hear it now, standing at the rim—a note below hearing, felt more than heard, rising through the soles of her boots and settling into her teeth.

She climbed down.

The ladder rungs were cold and slick with condensation, and halfway down she stopped, because the humming changed. It wasn't louder. It was *attentive*. The difference between a room empty of people and a room containing one person holding their breath.

"Hello," Odile said, because she was her mother's daughter and her mother had taught her that thresholds remember being greeted politely. "I'm not here to bother you."

The humming did not change. But something in it shifted—she couldn't have said what—like a listener leaning forward.

Her flashlight found the door.

It was black stone, or stone gone black, and it filled the archway completely, fitted so precisely into the jambs that she couldn't slide a fingernail between them. There was no handle. No hinges visible from this side. No lock, no keyhole, nothing that suggested it had ever been meant to open from outside.

And carved into its face, dense as rain on a window, were marks.

Odile raised the light.

Tally marks. Groups of four vertical strokes crossed by a fifth, then a space, then four more, thousands of them, covering the door from top to bottom like a skin of scratches. They weren't uniform. The ones near the top were deep and deliberate, gouged by something patient. Lower down they grew shallower, hurried, some overlapping, some abandoned mid-stroke—and near the bottom, where the stone met the floor, they stopped entirely, in a cluster of half-finished fours, as though the hand making them had simply

stopped.

*Counting days,* she thought. *Then counting years.*

She pressed her palm flat against the door before she could talk herself out of it.

Warm. Not feverish, not burning—the warmth of a body under blankets, of bread just out of the oven, of a cheek you might kiss. And beneath her hand, faint but unmistakable, came a rhythm. Not the hum anymore. A pulse. Slow, patient, enormous, like something sleeping very deeply and very close to the surface.

"I know what you are," she whispered. "You're a count. You're somebody keeping track of how long."

The pulse quickened. Just slightly. Just enough.

Odile took her hand back and played the light across the tally marks again, slower this time, reading them the way you'd read braille if you only knew one word. Four strokes, a cross-stroke, a space. Days. Then somewhere in the middle of the door the spacing changed—the groups got larger, separated by deeper grooves, five days, ten, whole months compressed into single desperate clusters. Years passing in a few square inches of stone.

And then, near the bottom right corner, the marks grew small again. Careful again. As if whoever—or whatever—was carving them had found a second wind, or a second purpose, and wanted these last ones to be legible.

They stopped at a group of four with the fifth stroke begun but never finished.

Beneath it, scratched shallow and crooked, was a date.

Odile read it twice. Her breath came out in the cold air and hung there, and the pit held its silence around her like a hand cupped over a candle flame.

**MARCH 3RD**

March third. Her mother had died on March third, eleven years ago, in a hospital room that smelled like lilies and antiseptic, while Odile sat in the hallway because she couldn't stand the sound of the oxygen machine. March third. The date her mother's voice had gone out of the world.

The date the calls had started.

*How the calls started after her mother's death.* She'd told herself it was grief, that the dead had always been calling and grief had simply tuned her ear to them. She'd built her whole ritualized night around that story—lights counted, doors checked, callers screened, rules kept. And underneath it all, all these years, the truth had been sitting in a storage unit on Delmore Avenue, in reel-to-reel boxes labeled 1974, in a hymnal with its heart torn out.

Whatever was owed hadn't died with her mother. It had *transferred*. Passed down like the shape of her hands, like her habit of counting doors, like the voice that callers on the graveyard shift sometimes mistook for a woman named Vivienne Crane.

"Oh," Odile said. It came out very small. "Oh, you've been counting me."

The pulse beneath the stone beat once, hard, and the warmth under her palm flared almost painful, and for a moment—just a moment—she heard it clearly: not a hum, not a harmonic, but a voice, low and layered, dozens of voices braided into one, speaking in the tone of someone correcting a ledger.

***"Vivienne."***

"Not Vivienne." Her own voice surprised her—steady, clear, carrying in the pit the way voices carry when the air wants them to. "Vivienne Crane is dead. I'm Odile. Odile Crane. And I'm the one who's going to finish what she started."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature and intent.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere—from the mud, from the ladder, from the transmitter in her pocket that shouldn't have been receiving anything—the choir began to sing.

Not the hymn. Not yet. Just the first line of it, hummed, patient, expectant. A tuning. An invitation. A reminder that they had waited fifty years and could wait fifty more, but that they would very much prefer not to.

Odile stood in the pit with her hand on the warm black stone and let them sing, and something in her chest that had been fear for weeks now turned over, slowly, like soil, and became something else. Something with edges. Something that could be aimed.

She thought of Ruthie Voss's sister pressing the manuscript into her hands like it was a live coal. She thought of Brandt dying above his shuttered parish, hoarding his confession like the last coin of a bankrupt man. She thought of her mother's note in the margin of the ruined hymnal—*Never sing along*—and understood it at last, not as a warning against temptation but as a mother's arithmetic: *if you never join the song, the song can never claim you.*

Well. The song had claimed her anyway. It had claimed her on March third, eleven years ago, when the debt rolled downhill and landed on a nine-year-old girl who didn't even know there was a hill.

"You kept count," Odile said to the door. "Good. Keep counting. Because I'm coming back, and when I come back, I'm bringing the rest of your song with me. Every verse. Every line. Even the one they tore out."

The humming swelled, and this time there was nothing patient in it at all.

She climbed the ladder with the taste of hymn in her mouth and the date carved in her mind, and behind her, in the dark of the pit, the tally marks seemed to shift in the moonlight—four strokes, a cross-stroke, a space—

—as though, somewhere below, something had picked up the tool again and begun, at last, to count down.
