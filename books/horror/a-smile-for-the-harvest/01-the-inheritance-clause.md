## 1. The Inheritance Clause

The letter had been in Bram's jacket pocket for eleven days, folded into eighths and softened at the creases like something worried by teeth. They hadn't read it past the second paragraph. The second paragraph was where the word *bequeathed* lived, sitting in the sentence like a bone in the throat.

*The estate of your uncle, Aldous Lockridge, having no other living kin of record—*

Bram had stopped there on the train, in the parking lot of the county records office, and twice more since. It wasn't that they couldn't read it. It was that reading it made it true, and Bram Lockridge had built a whole life out of not letting things be true until they could be measured.

They parked the hatchback where the gravel drive gave up trying to be a road, and got out with the surveying case in one hand and the laser level in the other. October air, dry and sweet with rotting apples from an orchard nobody had pruned in years. The farmhouse sat at the end of it like a held breath.

Two stories, clapboard gone silver-gray, a porch roof sagging on its left side the way an old man leans on a cane. Windows dark. Chimney intact. The barn behind it listing east. Bram stood in the weeds at the edge of the yard and did what they always did first: measured.

Ridge line roughly thirty feet. Foundation probably 1890s, maybe older—the county said the parcel was surveyed in 1887, but county records in this part of the state were a rumor wearing a suit. Roofline straight except for the porch. No visible subsidence. The house wasn't falling down. It was just old, and old things made sounds, and sounds felt like attention if you let them.

Bram did not let them.

"Structural settling," they said aloud, to the house, to the apples, to nobody. "You're a box of sticks. You creak."

The house did not answer, which Bram counted as agreement.

They'd been doing this for nine years—abandoned places, unmapped places, the places between the lines on the map. Grain elevators in Kansas. A drained reservoir in Oregon with a town still standing at the bottom, church steeple poking up through the waterline like a periscope. Bram's channel had forty thousand subscribers who loved the way they narrated decay without romance, who trusted them because Bram never pretended a ruin was haunted. *Buildings don't haunt,* their most-pinned comment read. *People do, and then they leave.*

Uncle Aldous had never watched a single video. That was one of the few facts about him Bram possessed with certainty.

The estrangement ran back three generations, or so it felt. Grandfather Elias had cut off his own brother over something no one would name—land, money, a woman, a dog; the family story changed depending on who was drunk—and Aldous had been the cut-off brother's son, which made him doubly exiled, a man born already outside the fence. Bram had met him exactly once, at fourteen, at a funeral, where Aldous had stood at the edge of the cemetery like a man waiting for a bus that never served his stop. He'd looked at Bram across the grass with an expression Bram had spent twenty years failing to file correctly. Not longing. Not guilt. Something closer to *inventory*. Like he was checking whether there was enough of Bram to matter.

Then last spring: a phone call from a lawyer Bram had never heard of, in a town Bram had driven through once at night. Aldous Lockridge, dead, alone, found in his kitchen chair by a neighbor who noticed the mail going yellow in the box. And everything he had—house, land, orchard, well, whatever was in the bank after taxes—signed over to the grandniece-or-nephew (the lawyer had stumbled delicately over this) he had met once.

Bram had told Wren about it on the phone, expecting her to laugh. Wren was the family's other survivor, cousin through the legitimate branch, the one who'd stayed near the old country and kept the reunions going and smiled at aunts until her face ached. Wren had not laughed. There had been a silence on the line long enough that Bram checked whether the call dropped.

"You should sell it," she'd finally said. "Fast. Don't even go inside. Sign the papers and sell it sight unseen."

"That's insane. I'm an explorer. You're telling me not to explore my own inheritance?"

"I'm telling you some houses are priced right for a reason." And then she'd laughed, too brightly, and turned the conversation to her garden, and Bram had filed the moment under *Wren being weird*, a folder that had existed since childhood and was, frankly, overstuffed.

Now Bram stood in the dead man's yard with surveying equipment and a letter they wouldn't finish reading, and thought: *I'll do the full documentation. Every room, every elevation, every crack. Then I list it, sell it, and buy myself two years of runway somewhere with better ruins.* Grief, if that's what this was, could be processed through a camera lens like anything else.

The front door wasn't locked. Of course it wasn't. Dead men don't need locks, and apparently neither had Aldous, lately.

Inside smelled like dust and cold ash and, underneath both, something faintly sweet—not rot exactly, more like cider pressed too far, apples gone past vinegar into something else. Bram catalogued it automatically: *ambient organic decomposition, low level, non-hazardous probable.* The entry hall ran narrow and tall, wallpaper printed with a pattern of small repeating fruit that time had faded to ghosts. A coat rack held one coat, still hanging, patient as a dog.

"Hello, house," Bram said, and their voice came back off the walls a half-beat flatter than they expected, swallowed instead of echoed. Old plaster. Heavy furnishings. Acoustics like a padded cell.

They set up in the parlor: tripod, laser level, the total station on its mount. The routine steadied them the way it always did. Numbers first, feelings later, feelings possibly never. They shot the corners of the main rooms, logged dimensions into the tablet, photographed the water stains on the ceiling of the back bedroom—a slow leak, dead pipe, nothing interesting. The kitchen still had a single plate on the table, a fork beside it, a glass with a dried ring at the bottom. Aldous had died mid-meal, or mid-life, which amounted to the same thing.

Bram stood looking at the plate longer than the plate deserved.

He'd sat here every night, presumably. Decades of nights. Eating alone in a house his own family had amputated him from, and his final act of spite—or sentiment, or whatever it was—had been to leave all of it to the one person who'd looked at him across a cemetery and walked away.

"You could've left it to the county," Bram told the chair. "Would've served you right."

The chair said nothing. The house said nothing. But the quality of the nothing had weight, the way a room has weight when someone is standing just behind the door of it. Bram felt it along the back of the neck, that animal static, the sense of a gaze arriving slightly ahead of any evidence of a gazer.

They turned. Empty hallway. Dust motes turning in the light from the kitchen window like a slow galaxy.

"Settling," Bram said again, firmer this time, and went back to work.

* * *

By late afternoon they'd covered the ground floor and half the second. The inventory was unremarkable in the way that only very old houses can be unremarkable: furniture under sheets, a sewing room frozen mid-project, a child's height chart penciled on a doorframe with names Bram didn't recognize—names from before, from the deep generations, the ones the family tree kept vague on purpose. *Elias, 1931. Marta, 1934. Silas, 1936.* Then the pencil marks stopped, mid-childhood, as if the children had simply stopped growing, or stopped being measured, or stopped.

Bram photographed the doorframe and moved on.

It was on the stairs, coming down with the tablet under one arm, that the feeling got specific. Not the diffuse watched-ness of the afternoon—something sharper. The stairwell window faced west and the low sun came through it amber and thick, and in that light Bram saw the dust on the banister.

Or rather, didn't see it.

Every surface in the house wore its gray velvet of neglect. Except the banister rail, which was clean. Polished. A hand-width band of bare wood running the length of it, gleaming faintly, as though a hand had trailed along it recently and often. As though someone had run their palm down these stairs this morning, and yesterday morning, and ten thousand mornings, and the wood had learned the touch and kept it.

Bram set the tablet down on the third step and looked at the banister for a long time.

Aldous had died in April. Six months ago. Nobody had polished anything since. But wood holds oil, holds wear—decades of a single hand following the same path would burnish it exactly like this. This was not a mystery. This was forensic trace evidence of a lonely man pacing his own house, touching the things he touched because there was nothing else alive in here to touch.

Bram picked the tablet back up. Their hand, they noticed, was colder than the house warranted.

"Okay," they said quietly. "Okay."

In the entry hall, the coat on the rack shifted. Just the furnace duct ticking below, just the differential cooling of an old structure in an October evening, just physics doing its honest work. Bram watched the coat settle and wrote in the log, in the field notes section where they recorded ambient conditions: *House makes noise. Normal.*

Underneath, in smaller type, the app's auto-suggested tag read: *occupied?*

Bram deleted it.

* * *

They ate standing at the kitchen counter—sandwich from the cooler, beer gone warm—and spread the lawyer's letter out flat, deciding that tonight, with the work done, they would be an adult about it and read the whole thing. Clause four concerned mineral rights. Clause five concerned the well. Clause six—

Clause six was short.

*The grantor further directs that the property shall not be subdivided, nor the cellar excavated, nor any portion of the premises sealed, blocked, or filled, save by the heir in person and of their own free will. This condition runs with the land.*

Bram read it twice. Lawyers loved weird boilerplate; some of them got paid by the clause. But this wasn't boilerplate. This was specific. *Nor the cellar excavated. Nor any portion sealed, blocked, or filled.* Somebody, at some point, had wanted to make sure that whoever owned this house could not wall anything off.

And somebody else—Aldous, presumably—had wanted to make sure the owner couldn't be forced to.

Bram looked toward the kitchen's back corner, where a door stood closed. Painted shut, probably. Cellar access. Every farmhouse had one, full of jars and spiders and the particular cold that comes up out of the ground like breath.

They finished the beer instead, and took their sleeping bag up to the front bedroom, the one with the good lock on the door and the window over the porch. Explorer's protocol: sleep high, sleep near exits, sleep with the gear bag packed. The house ticked and groaned around them, joists adjusting to the dropping temperature, wind finding gaps in the clapboard and worrying them. All of it legible. All of it explicable. Bram lay in the dark translating each sound as it came—*wind, settling, branch, wind*—like a man defusing bombs by naming them.

Somewhere below, faintly, came a rhythm. Slow. Regular. Not mechanical—mechanical sounds have a hollowness to them, a circuit's patience. This was wetter than that. Softer. Like something working patiently at a great distance, the way you might hear, through several floors, another person eating.

Bram lay still and listened until it stopped, and told themselves it had been the well pump cycling, except they'd shut the breaker to the well that afternoon, and knew it, and knew it.

"Tomorrow," they said to the ceiling, to the house, to the uncle whose silence had gotten so much louder since he died. "Tomorrow I survey the cellar, document everything, and find out why your lawyer didn't want me to seal it."

The house held its silence around them, attentive, almost courteous, like a host waiting for a guest to finally ask the right question.

Sleep came eventually, thin and shallow. And in the hallway outside the locked door, the mirror at the end of the corridor—the old one, the tall one, the one Bram had noted for the sale listing as *antique, minor foxing*—stood in the dark reflecting the empty hall perfectly, on time, for now.
