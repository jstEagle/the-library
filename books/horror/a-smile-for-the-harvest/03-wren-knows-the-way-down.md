## 3. Wren Knows the Way Down

Wren Lockridge arrived on the third morning, before Bram had even finished the first pot of coffee, driving a car too clean for the gravel road and carrying two boxes of cleaning supplies she had no plausible reason to own.

"I figured you'd be living in filth," she said on the porch, not quite smiling. "You always were a feral child."

"We're thirty-two, Wren."

"And you still eat standing over the sink. I've seen your stories." She pushed past him into the house with the ease of someone who had done it a hundred times, and that was the first wrong note—because as far as Bram knew, Wren hadn't set foot in this house since she was nine years old.

He'd called her from the lawyer's office three weeks ago, mostly out of obligation. *Uncle Alder's dead. I got the farm. Come see it sometime.* She'd said yes in a voice that suggested she'd rather have said anything else, and then hadn't mentioned it again until she appeared on his porch with rubber gloves and a bucket like a woman arriving to mop up a crime scene.

"Where do you want me?" she asked, already snapping the gloves on.

"The kitchen's fine. I've been working down in the cellar, mostly."

Her hands stopped moving. It was a small thing—a half-second pause, a breath held just slightly too long—but Bram had spent a career noticing how people moved through spaces, and he catalogued it the way he catalogued everything: automatically, without yet believing it meant anything.

"The cellar," she said.

"Found some interesting construction back there. Old stonework. You wouldn't know anything about that, would you? Family property."

"No," Wren said, and started scrubbing a counter that didn't need it.

* * *

She cleaned the way some people pray—with total absorption, as if the work itself were keeping something at bay. She did the kitchen, then the hall, then the front parlor, working outward from the center of the house in a pattern Bram might have found charmingly methodical if he hadn't noticed its logic. She was avoiding the cellar door. Every circuit of her cleaning bent around it, the way water bends around a stone.

By early afternoon he couldn't stand it anymore.

"You want to see what I found?"

"Not particularly."

"Wren." He kept his voice light. "It's a tunnel. Hand-cut stone, pre-1900 by the tool marks, absent from every county survey going back to the original plat maps. Do you understand what that means? Somebody dug miles of this and then made sure nobody wrote it down. That's not construction. That's concealment. This is the kind of thing urban explorers write books about."

"I know what it is," Wren said.

The rag went still in her hand.

Bram waited. The house waited with him—he'd been noticing that too, how quiet it kept itself, how the settling sounds stopped whenever someone stood still long enough to listen, as if the house were also holding its breath.

"It's a root cellar," she said finally. "Old farms have them. Granddad used it for turnips or whatever. Can we please clean the upstairs?"

"Turnips," Bram repeated. "Through a bricked archway hidden behind shelving, with tally marks carved into the walls in at least four different handwritings?"

Something happened to her face then. Not surprise—that was the thing he couldn't get past later, turning it over in his mind. He had led her to the archway expecting shock, the wide-eyed *what is this?* of a relative shown a family secret, and instead what crossed her features was recognition. Dread, specifically. The look of a person who has opened a door they specifically hoped was painted shut.

"You've been down there," she said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, each word placed like a foot on ice.

"About forty feet in. I stopped when I ran out of daylight. Wren, those tallies—they're counting *down*. Groups of marks with lines struck through them, but the numbers decrease as you go deeper. Who counts down?"

"Please reseal it."

"What?"

"The archway. Brick it back up. I'll help. I'll pay for the mortar, I'll mix it myself, we can have it done before dark." She was talking fast now, the composure cracking along seams he'd never seen before. "You haven't told anyone else about it, right? No forums, no explorer groups, nothing posted?"

"I was going to document it properly first—"

"*Bram.*" She grabbed his arm, and her grip hurt. Her eyes were wet and furious and terrified all at once, and none of it made sense, because it was a hole in the ground, it was old masonry, it was exactly the sort of thing that could be explained by any one of six boring historical processes. "Please. Please don't go back down there. Reseal it and we never talk about it again. I'll tell you everything I know about the family, I'll answer every question, I swear, just not that. Not that one thing."

And here was the fracture, though neither of them would have named it that night. Because Bram looked at his cousin—the cousin who had spent her whole life performing normalcy so convincingly that the family had almost seemed real—and saw that her terror was genuine, and thought: *genuine fear has a source.* And Wren looked at Bram and saw the exact expression their grandmother used to describe with loathing: the Lockridge curiosity, the need to know, the family trait that had put names in the ground.

"Bram," she said, softer now. "Some things in this family aren't secrets because nobody knows. They're secrets because everybody knows, and we agreed."

"Agreed to what? What could possibly be down there that an entire town agreed to forget?"

She let go of his arm. She looked toward the cellar door, and Bram watched her listen—to the house, to the silence under the floor, to something he couldn't hear—and when she looked back at him her face had closed like a fist.

"If you go down there again," she said quietly, "count the tallies yourself. Don't trust the ones near your name."

"My name isn't down there."

Wren picked up her bucket. "I'll do the windows," she said, and walked out of the kitchen, and did not come back into the room where the cellar door stood open, not once, not for the rest of the day.

* * *

He should have listened to her. He would spend a great deal of time afterward understanding exactly why he hadn't—not malice, not even recklessness, just the plain structural fact of who he was. Bram Lockridge believed that the world was legible. Every strange thing had a mechanism; every mechanism had a history; every history could be dug out and laid in the light and understood. His uncle had died leaving him a house full of locked drawers, and Bram had spent his whole life learning to pick locks. Wren was asking him to leave one shut forever, and asking it in a voice that proved there was something inside worth locking away, and no Lockridge in three hundred years had ever managed that.

So at dusk, while Wren scrubbed windows that were already clean, Bram took his headlamp down the cellar stairs.

The archway gaped where he'd broken through the brick. Cold air moved against his face, steady and directional, like breath. He stepped over the rubble and played his light across the nearest wall, and the tally marks caught the beam in their generations of handwriting—quill-cut, knife-cut, chisel-cut, modern.

He hadn't brought chalk. He counted with his eyes, following the descending numbers down the corridor, and told himself the cold on his neck was just the draft, and the prickle between his shoulder blades was just the animal awareness of being underground, and the feeling of being *read* was just his own imagination reflecting off stone.

Near the deepest mark he'd reached, the newest handwriting—faint, recent, cut by something patient—had added fresh strokes beside the last number.

And beneath the tallies, in letters small and neat and unmistakable, someone had carved a name.

BRAM.

Behind him, far up the shafts, faint as a pulse through the earth, came a sound. Rhythmic. Wet. Like chewing.
