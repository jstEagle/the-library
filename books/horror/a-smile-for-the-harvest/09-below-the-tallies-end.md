## 9. Below, the Tallies End

Bram didn't sleep. That was the honest version of it, and honesty had become a discipline, like flossing or stretching—something you did not because it felt natural but because skipping it had consequences you could feel in your body.

The mirror lag had been going on for six days now. Six days of tests, of stopwatches and phone cameras and a laser level borrowed from the truck, of standing in the upstairs hallway at 3 a.m. with their hand raised like a student who knew the answer and was terrified to be called on. The reflection always followed. Never led. A perfect copy, one second behind, patient as interest accruing.

The rational explanations had died in order of plausibility. First: fatigue, microsleeps, temporal distortion of perception under stress. Then: a trick of the house itself, some settling geometry that delayed light along an angled path—Bram had actually run the math on this, filling two notebook pages before admitting that no arrangement of mirrors could add a full second without folding the hallway through another dimension. Then: neurological. Then: neurological but worse.

What remained was the thing Bram refused to name, sitting in the corner of their mind like a guest who wouldn't leave.

So instead of sleeping, Bram went back down.

* * *

The tunnels were colder than the cellar, which was itself colder than the October night outside. The air below had a taste to it—mineral, wet stone, and underneath that something faintly sweet, like fruit going soft in a bowl nobody was watching. Bram had noticed it the first day and catalogued it as groundwater chemistry. Tonight they noticed themselves noticing it, and hated how much attention they were paying to their own senses, as if the senses were witnesses who might be bought.

Headlamp on. Rope clipped to the iron ring Wren had shown them—the ring set into the cellar floor, hidden under a hatch painted over so many times it had become part of the wood grain. Wren hadn't come back since the argument. *Reseal it,* she'd said. *Please. I'm asking you as family.* And Bram had said something clever about evidence, and watched her face do the thing where it closed like a shop pulling down its shutters.

The rope took their weight. The shaft dropped maybe twelve feet into a chamber that the county survey had never heard of, that the deed didn't mention, that existed in no record Bram could find in three archives and a very uncomfortable phone call with a retired county clerk.

Hand-cut walls. That detail still got them, even now, even afraid. Someone had cut these corridors out of limestone with hand tools, following the seams, and the work was good—good enough that after four generations of neglect there wasn't a single collapse. You don't get craftsmanship like that from people doing a job. You get it from people doing a duty.

And the tallies. The walls were covered in them, thousands upon thousands, scratched into the stone in generations of handwriting. Bram had spent two days photographing and cataloguing them, and the catalogue had produced the finding that had ended their sleep: the marks didn't count up. They counted down.

Each cluster began large—a hundred strokes, fifty—and diminished toward zero, and then a new cluster began, sometimes in a different hand, sometimes in the same hand decades apart, the way handwriting changes when the wrist ages. Down to nothing. Start again. Down to nothing. Like timers. Like someone carving off pieces of a waiting period, watching it shrink, and then resetting it and beginning again.

Bram had told themselves it was quarrying math. Tonnage estimates. Depletion curves. They'd believed it about forty percent, which was, they were coming to understand, the ceiling of what belief could be stretched to down here.

Tonight they went deeper than they'd gone before, past the junction where the corridor forked toward a cold draft that smelled of open earth, past the chamber where the tally marks grew dense as braille. Their boots found the rhythm of the walk, and the walk became a kind of thinking, and the thinking kept circling one question:

*If it's counting down, what happens at zero?*

* * *

They found the fresh marks by accident, which is to say they almost walked past them, because the headlamp beam swept the wall and swept back and something about the wall's texture snagged—not the marks themselves, at first, but the fact that the stone around them was lighter. Pale. Recently exposed.

Bram stopped. Raised the lamp.

The wall here was different from the rest of the tunnel. Cleaner. Deliberate. And carved into it, in a careful, formal script that belonged to no century in particular—letters like headstones—

**BRAM LOCKRIDGE**

Their own name. Cut deep, finished smooth, the way you'd carve a name you intended to last.

Bram stood very still. The headlamp hummed. Somewhere behind them, water moved in a seam, patient as everything else in this place.

"Okay," Bram said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "Okay. Someone knew I was coming. Uncle Alder. He knew I'd inherit, he cut it ahead of time, he—" But Alder had died suddenly, badly, alone, and the lawyer's letter had said the estate passed *immediately and without contest*, phrasing Bram had filed under *eccentric legal drafting* and never revisited. Had Alder known? Had Alder cut his heir's name into a wall underground and then died right on schedule?

Or—

Bram's light moved down from the name, and the thinking stopped.

Below the name, in the same pale, freshly cut stone, were tally marks. Fresh ones. Not generations old. Not weathered. The grooves still held dust in their bottoms, fine and white, and when Bram touched one, the edge was sharp enough to catch skin.

Four strokes. Four vertical lines, grouped the way the old clusters were grouped, counting down the way the old clusters counted down.

And beside them, older, faded, half-erased by mineral bloom: a fifth stroke, crossed out.

Bram's mouth went dry. They counted again. Four. They photographed it. Four. They checked the date stamp on the photos against the date stamp of the photos they'd taken five days ago of this same stretch of wall, which showed—Bram scrolled back, hands steady in the way of someone spending all their steadiness on their hands—showed five strokes, uncrossed, crisp.

Five days ago: five.

Now: four.

Something down here was crossing them off. One per day. And Bram did the arithmetic they would spend the rest of their life unable to un-do: if the count had started at whatever number it started at, and it was at four now, and it fell by one each night—

Four nights.

"No," Bram said. "No, that's—that's coincidence. That's erosion. That's someone else down here." Their voice came back off the stone flat and dead, absorbing sound the way the whole tunnel absorbed sound, giving nothing back. "People live near here. Kids. Vandalism."

Vandals who carved their name in formal lettering. Vandals who maintained a daily schedule. Vandals whose work appeared on a wall in a tunnel that did not exist.

Bram laughed—one short, ugly syllable—and the laugh echoed wrong, arriving back at their ears a half-beat late, and they decided firmly not to think about why that might be.

* * *

They should have left. Every instinct they trusted—the ones built from a decade of urban exploration, the instincts that said *unstable floor, bad air, turn back*—all of them were screaming the same wordless scream. Bram catalogued the screaming, named it, thanked it, and kept walking, because the alternative was to go up into the house where every reflective surface held a copy of them running one second behind, and down here at least the fear had a shape.

The corridor narrowed. The tally marks thickened until the walls were more mark than stone, and Bram noticed something they'd missed in the earlier surveys of the survey: the newest clusters weren't just counting down. They were converging. Each reset began lower than the last. A hundred. Fifty. Twenty. Ten. Whatever the counts measured, the intervals between zeros were shrinking. Something was approaching. Or something was being approached.

The air changed. Warmer by a degree, maybe two, and the sweetness stronger—not rot exactly, not fermentation, but the smell of abundance held too long, harvest heaped past its season. Bram's light found a side passage, low-ceilinged, sloping down, and from somewhere along that passage came a draft that moved against the tunnel's other currents, slow and tidal, in and out, in and out.

Breathing. The tunnel system was breathing.

Bram stood at the mouth of the side passage for a long time. Long enough for the headlamp battery indicator to blink amber. Long enough to have turned back twice over.

Then, from far below—farther than the passage, farther than anything the map in Bram's head allowed for—came a sound.

Rhythmic. Wet. Regular as a metronome set to something enormous.

It sounded like chewing.

Not fast. Not greedy. Slow and thorough and contented, the chewing of something with all the time in the world and no reason to hurry its meal, and it rose up the shafts the way the smell did, diffusing through stone that should have been proof against both. Bram pressed their back against the wall. The stone was warm. The stone was warm like skin.

Every rational structure Bram had built—the catalogs, the tests, the notebooks of alternative hypotheses—went quiet all at once, not collapsing but simply ceasing, the way a machine stops when the power is cut rather than when it breaks. What was left was very simple and very cold: *something is down there. It knows my name. It is counting my nights. It is eating.*

Denial didn't die dramatically. It just stopped being available, the way a road stops being available after a washout. You can stand at the edge all you want. There's nowhere to drive.

Bram slid down the wall until they were sitting, knees up, headlamp aimed at the floor, listening to the distant patient chewing and the tidal breath of the tunnels and their own heart working hard in the dark.

"I don't believe in you," they said finally. It came out small. "I want that on the record. Whatever record exists down here. I don't believe in you, and I am going to find out what you are anyway, and those are not contradictory."

The chewing paused.

For three seconds—Bram counted, because counting was the last tool they had—for three full seconds the deep sound stopped, and the silence had a quality of attention, of something vast turning its face upward through the stone toward a small warm shape with a light.

Then it resumed. Unhurried. Unbothered. As if it had heard the protest, found it charming, and filed it away.

Bram got up. Climbed the rope with arms that shook. Did not look back at the wall with their name on it, and then, at the top, looking back anyway through the hatch, saw in the pale stone below the four strokes a fifth groove already begun—shallow, incipient, the stone splitting along a line that hadn't been there an hour ago—as if the count couldn't wait for midnight anymore.

They sealed the hatch. Stood in the cellar breathing hard. And through the doorway, at the end of the dark hall, the great mirror caught the light from the kitchen and threw it back one second late—and in that stolen second, Bram saw the reflected figure lower its raised hand before Bram had raised theirs.

Above ground, the moon was four nights from full.

Down below, the count agreed.
