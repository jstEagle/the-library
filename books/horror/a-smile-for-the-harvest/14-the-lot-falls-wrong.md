## 14. The Lot Falls Wrong

Wren had known the token would bear her name since she was nine years old.

Not guessed. Not feared in the ordinary way a child fears thunder or the dark under the bed. Known, the way you know the shape of your own hands. She had spent thirty years building a life around that knowledge the way a river builds around a stone—never moving it, only flowing past it so smoothly that anyone watching would think the stone wasn't there at all.

So when the town elder's thick fingers unfolded the wooden slip on the kitchen table, and the lamplight caught the single word burned into it—*WREN*—she did not gasp. She did not cry out. She felt her heart settle, slow and heavy, like a door closing in a house she'd long ago finished packing.

"There it is," she said.

Bram made a sound beside her that she had never heard from them before. Not a word. Something older than words, a sound from the animal floor of a person, and Wren understood that in this one night she was watching her cousin's entire worldview come apart at the seams—and that she would have to be the one holding the needle when they tried to sew it back together, if there was time.

She didn't look at the token again. She didn't need to. She'd drawn it herself, in a dream, when she was nine. She'd woken with the splinters of it under her fingernails.

* * *

The townsmen had come at dusk, as the moon was rising wrong—full and fat and three days early, hanging over the ripened fields like a lamp someone had lit in a room where no one was home. There were eleven of them. Wren counted without meaning to. Eleven lanterns, eleven faces she had known her whole life: Mr. Aldous from the feed store who had sold her penny candy when she was small; the Hollis brothers who fixed her truck every winter and never charged full price; old Mrs. Vann, who had taught her to bake and who now stood at the back with her hands folded like a woman in church.

They had come with the ledger. Not Bram's ledger—the other one, the town's copy, bound in leather gone black with handling, its corners worn round as river stones. The elder, a man named Corliss whom Wren had always thought of as harmless, a retired schoolteacher with a hearing aid and a garden he was proud of, set it on the kitchen table with both hands, gently, the way you set down a sleeping child.

"The moon's up," Corliss said. "The fields came in overnight. You've seen them. Corn standing ripe where it was green yesterday morning. Apples heavy on the branches. The whole valley came in like it was August, and it's not August." He said it without triumph, without menace. He said it the way a man reads a weather report for a storm that has already arrived. "It's time."

Bram had planted themselves between Wren and the door. Wren noticed that—filed it away in the small warm room she kept for things Bram did that proved they loved her, a room she rarely opened because opening it made the arithmetic harder.

"Time for what, exactly?" Bram said. "Say it plainly. You want to put her in the ground."

"No," Corliss said, and he looked genuinely pained. "We want to put her *below*. There's a difference. There's always been a difference. Nobody who's gone down has been put anywhere. They've been *given*. It's an honor, son. It's the honor this family has carried for two hundred years, and the town has carried it with them."

"By staying quiet."

"By *keeping*," Mrs. Vann said from the back, and her voice was soft and old and terrible. "You keep a thing by not speaking it. You've kept it too, child. All them years you smiled at the fair and said your family was just farmers. You kept it better than any of us."

Wren felt that land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash. It never came. The well had no bottom. She had known that for thirty years too.

* * *

The lot itself was Corliss's idea, or the founders' idea wearing Corliss's voice—Wren had never been sure where the men of the town ended and the custom began, and she suspected the custom had long ago eaten the men the way ivy eats a fence. Every keeper, he explained, every keeper in two hundred years, had been chosen by lot from among the blood. Tokens in the old meal jar. One name in a dozen blanks. The hand of the eldest Lockridge drew it, so there could be no argument of favor, and the hand of the eldest townsmen read it aloud, so there could be no argument of family.

"It's fair," Corliss said. "It's been fair since before the war. Before the *last* war. It's the only fair thing in the whole arrangement, and we've kept it fair, and that's why the harvests have kept."

Bram's jaw worked. Wren watched them do the math they were built to do—the rationalist's math, probability and sample size and the physics of a wooden token in a ceramic jar—and she watched the math fail them, because there was no version of the math that explained what happened next.

Bram drew. Wren watched her cousin's hand go into the jar, watched the tokens shift and knock like teeth, watched the fingers close and withdraw and open over the table.

The token was not blank. The token said WREN, in the same burned letters, in the same old hand as all the tokens before it—she could see the family in the lettering, generations of the same strokes, the same *W* with its flared ends that appeared in the ledger, in the tallies on the tunnel walls, in the signature on the deed to the house.

"That's not possible," Bram said. "The odds—"

"Son." Corliss's voice was gentle as snowfall. "The lot has never fallen wrong. Not once. You think that's chance? We think that's *attention*. Something down there has been choosing its keepers a long time, and it chooses well, and it chooses the one who's ready." He turned his kind, tired eyes on Wren. "And it chose right. Look at her. She's not surprised."

Everyone looked at her. Eleven lanterns' worth of faces, and not one of them cruel. That was the worst of it, Wren thought. Not one of them cruel. They were all so *sorry*. Mr. Hollis had tears standing in his eyes. Mrs. Vann had begun to pray, quietly, under her breath, a planting prayer in words Wren half-recognized from a hymn she'd been forbidden to sing.

"Say something," Bram whispered to her. "Wren. Tell them no. Tell them it's not—there has to be a way, there's always a way, we haven't even tried the ritual, we don't know all the terms, maybe there's a substitution clause, maybe—"

"Bram." She said their name quietly and it stopped them like a hand on the chest. "Come here."

They came. Of course they came. They would always come. That was the whole problem, and the whole grace, of her cousin.

She took their face in both hands. Their cheeks were cold. Behind them, the townsmen waited with the patience of furniture, because they knew—she could see it in their eyes—they knew there was nothing to wait *for*. The lot had fallen. The lot always fell true.

"I need you to listen to me," she said. "Not argue. Not plan. Just listen, because I'm only going to say this once, and then I'm going to do the hardest thing I've ever done, which is tell the truth in front of witnesses."

Bram's throat moved.

"When I was nine years old," Wren said, "I dreamed I was standing in a dark room with a jar of wooden tokens, and I reached in, and I drew my own name. I woke up with splinters under my fingernails, Bram. Little black splinters, like the wood in that jar is not any wood that grows in this valley. I never told anyone. Not Grandma. Not the uncles. I hid my hands under the blanket and I picked them out one by one and I buried them in the flowerbed by the kitchen window, and the next spring the peonies came in *early*."

Bram made a small sound.

"Grandma never let me look in mirrors after dark. You know that. You thought it was superstition, one of her little rules. It wasn't. It was because she'd drawn the lot for her brother, in 1961, and the token had said *his* name, and she'd looked in the hall mirror that night to fix her hair before the men came, and the girl in the mirror had already been crying." Wren's voice did not shake. She had rehearsed this her whole life without knowing it. Every dinner where she'd changed the subject, every fair where she'd smiled, every year she'd pretended—she'd been rehearsing. "She told me once, when I was little, when she thought I was asleep. She said, *the mirror shows you what's coming, and what's coming for our family is always the same thing, and there's no use practicing your grief in glass.*"

"Wren—"

"Let me finish." She smoothed their hair back from their forehead, the way she had when they were children and Bram had scraped a knee and refused to cry. "I have spent my whole life pretending this family was normal. You spent yours pretending it didn't exist. We were doing the same thing, cousin. We were both just facing different walls. And I want you to know—I *need* you to know—that I'm not brave right now. I'm not calm because I'm strong. I'm calm because I did this crying when I was nine years old, alone, in a bed with splinters under my nails, and there is none of it left in me. It's all been spent. I'm just the shape the crying left behind."

"That's not—" Bram's voice cracked clean through. "That's not how it's supposed to work. You're supposed to get to live your life. You're supposed to—Wren, I'm the one who opened the archway. I'm the one who broke the seal, I read the ledger, I *did this*—"

"You inherited," she said. "Same as me. Same as everyone with our blood. There was never a version of this where nobody paid. There was only a version where you didn't know the price until the bill came due." She turned to the townsmen. "When?"

Corliss consulted the black ledger with the deference of a man consulting scripture. "Moon's crest at midnight. The singing starts at the farm gate at eleven. It's a short walk to the mouth."

"Then I'd like an hour," Wren said. "To pack a bag. It's a long time below."

The townsmen bowed, actually bowed, small and awkward, and filed out onto the porch to wait with their lanterns, and the kitchen was suddenly just a kitchen again, and Wren let herself lean against the counter and feel, for the first time all night, the true weight of it settle onto her shoulders.

Bram was staring at her like she'd already died.

* * *

She packed while they talked. She'd known what to bring for thirty years, in the way she'd known everything: a warm coat, because it got cold down there—she'd heard the uncles say so, before they stopped saying anything; her grandmother's recipe box, because the thing below liked stories and recipes were stories you could eat; a photograph of the two of them at the fair, age eleven and twelve, gap-toothed and sunburned, the last summer before everything went quiet.

Bram followed her from room to room, arguing. She let them argue. It was how they loved, and she wanted to be loved tonight, and there wasn't time for them to learn any other way.

"There's a substitution clause," Bram said for the fourth time. "I found it. Page forty of the town ledger, it says the lot may be answered—*answered*, Wren, that's the word—by any of the blood who speaks the founder's words first at the mouth. First. Before the named one crosses. It's right there."

"Page forty of a book written by men who fed their own children to a root so their corn would grow," Wren said, folding the photograph into her coat pocket. "And you're going to quote it at me like a lawyer."

"I'm going to *use* it like a lawyer."

"Bram." She stopped in the hallway, under the great mirror, and made the mistake of looking up.

The mirror showed the hallway a half-second late, as it had for days. But tonight it showed something else too. It showed the hallway, and the two of them in it—and it showed Wren smiling. A wide, warm, easy smile, the smile she'd worn at every fair for thirty years.

She was not smiling.

She looked at the glass and the glass looked back and she understood, with a calm that frightened her more than any scream could have, that the thing below had already seen this night. Had already worn it. Had already *practiced* her.

"It's early," she said quietly. "Not late. The old farmer was right. It's not lagging. It's *showing*."

Bram followed her gaze and went gray.

"Wren," they said, and their voice was very small, very young, the voice of the kid who'd scraped a knee and refused to cry. "Wren, I can't do this without you."

She turned from the mirror and took their face in her hands one more time, and she gave them the smile the glass had promised, the one she'd been saving her whole life, and she said:

"Then it's a good thing you've got until eleven o'clock to figure out how."

Below the floorboards, far beneath the cellar, beneath the tunnels and the tallies and the dark, something turned over in its sleep—and the peonies by the kitchen window, out in the impossible warm night, opened out of season, all at once, like hands.
