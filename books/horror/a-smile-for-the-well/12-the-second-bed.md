## 12. The Second Bed

She should not have slept there.

Dominic knew this the way she knew load-bearing walls—by instinct, by the hum in her teeth when she pressed her palm to something that mattered. The hidden room was a load-bearing wall of a different kind, and she had made up the second bed anyway, pulled the dust sheet off it, and lain down beneath the window where the frost drew its patient letters.

The mattress exhaled when she settled onto it. Not a creak—an exhale, long and small, like something that had been holding its breath for twenty years.

"I'm just surveying," she said aloud, to no one, to Wren, to the room. "I'm documenting."

The house said nothing. The house had stopped saying nothing loudly enough lately that silence felt like a held door.

She slept.

* * *

I am running down the east corridor and my feet know every board that will speak.

That's the first thing—how well I know it. The third plank from the linen closet dips, so I skip it, the way you skip a rope, the way you skip a stone, and I come off the good wood onto the bad with a *thock-thock-thock* rhythm that is mine, that has always been mine, that the floor and I invented together years ago when I was small enough to think the game would never end.

"Slow down!" Wren calls from behind me, laughing, and I don't slow down because slowing down is losing and Wren cheats anyway, she goes through walls, she goes through *doors*, which is worse than cheating, it's showing off.

"You can't catch what you won't chase!" I shout back, and hear how the words bounce down the stairwell and come back changed, deeper, like the house is repeating them in its own voice, learning them.

We run through the morning rooms. The light comes in gold and thick as syrup. Mother's good curtains are open, which means Mother is out, which means the whole house belongs to us for one more hour. I slide across the parquet in my socks—Iris's socks, my socks, the ones with the worn heels—and Wren comes through the wall beside me instead of around the corner, materializing mid-stride like a thought completing itself, and we collide, and we go down in a heap on the rug, breathless, hysterical, two girls laughing at nothing in a house that holds us like cupped hands.

"Again," Wren says. "From the top."

"From the top," I agree, because with Wren there is never an ending, only another beginning laid right against the last one, smooth as river stones.

Her hand is cold when she pulls me up. Her hand is always cold. I have never once wondered why.

* * *

There are things I do not look at directly, even in the dream. Even here, where everything is supposed to be mine.

I do not look too long at the mirror in the upstairs bath, where sometimes the reflection lags—a half-second behind, a half-step wrong, a girl standing when I am sitting. I tell myself it's the old glass, the silvering gone bad, mercury pooling at the bottom like weather.

I do not ask why Father's study smells of ink and iron on the nights he comes home from the village with his hat brim wet and his eyes fixed somewhere over my head.

I do not ask why Mother cries in the pantry, quietly, in the particular way of someone who has agreed to something, who has signed something, whose grief has paperwork.

And I do not—this is the rule, this is the oldest rule, older than skipping the third plank—I do not go down to the cellar after dark.

Wren and I are sitting on the porch swing when I break the rule. Or rather: I announce that I will break it, the way children announce transgressions to their invisible witnesses, daring the world to object.

"They say the Fenwicks built a well that drinks," I say, swinging my legs. The boards creak under us in our rhythm. "Mrs. Aldous told Nell Prue that the house keeps things. Keeps them *safe*, she said, but she did the thing with her shawl when she said it."

"What things?" Wren asks. She swings when I swing; she stops when I stop; she has never once pushed off on her own. I have never once noticed.

"Things people want forgot," I say, pleased with the shape of the sentence, the way it sounds like something an adult would say, something I've overheard through a door. "Bad years. Bad babies." I don't know why I add that last part. It comes out of my mouth warm and strange, like a coin found in the street.

Wren is quiet. When I turn, she is looking at me with an expression I have no name for yet, though I will learn it later, much later, from the other side of it: it is the look of someone watching a person they love walk toward a thing that is already holding out its hands.

"Don't," she says.

"It's daytime," I say. "The rule is after dark."

"The rule is *never*, silly." But she takes my hand, cold fingers lacing through warm ones, and she doesn't let go, and I understand that if I go down those stairs she is coming with me, because Wren goes where I go, Wren is where I am, Wren is—

Wren is what, exactly?

For one flickering moment the question opens under me like a trapdoor. What is Wren? Where did she come from? Other girls have friends from school, from church, from next door. Where is Wren *from*?

But the moment closes. The sun is out. My hand is held. And I am eight years old and immortal and bored, and boredom is the engine of every ruin.

"Come on then," I say, and pull us both off the swing, toward the door, toward the stairs, down.

* * *

The cellar is different in daylight. That's what I tell myself on the steps. Everything is different in daylight; fear is just a night animal; Mrs. Aldous is a foolish old woman with a shawl and a taste for drama.

It smells of earth and apples and, underneath, faintly, of iron.

The false wall is behind the shelving on the north side. I know this without knowing how I know it. I know it the way I know the dipping plank. I put my shoulder to the shelf unit and it swings—it *swings*, hinged, oiled, maintained—and the dark beyond it is not empty dark but furnished dark, and my eyes adjust, and I see what the house keeps safe.

Shoes. Small shoes, pairs of them, lined up on a shelf like guests. Drawings pinned in careful rows, curling with age. A lock of hair tied with ribbon, brown going grey in its little glass box. Clay figures, dozens of them, squat and crude, each one marked underneath with a family name scratched before firing: *Prue. Aldous. Calloway.* A whole shelf of names. A whole town's worth of convenient forgetting, arranged with more tenderness than any of those families ever showed the things themselves.

And in the center of the floor, on a low table, a ledger lies open, and beside it a clay figure still wet at the seams, still soft, still being finished.

"Wren," I whisper. "What is this?"

But when I turn, Wren isn't beside me anymore.

She's at the table. She's looking down at the soft clay figure, and her face is doing something terrible, something folding inward like paper held to flame, and she says—not unkindly, that's the worst of it, she says it kindly, the way you'd tell a child the truth about a dying pet—

"Oh, Iris. You weren't supposed to find your own."

* * *

Then the gentle hands.

That's how I remember them afterward, in whatever afterward is: the gentle hands. They come down the stairs without hurry. There are voices above them, adult voices, saying words like *best* and *only* and *agreed*, and the voices are sad in the way of people doing a kindness they've rehearsed until it stopped feeling like anything at all.

Father's hands. I know them by the ink stain on the right thumb. He lifts me the way he lifted me over fences and puddles, competent and loving and utterly immovable, and I am crying, and he is murmuring, and what he murmurs is: *"You'll be kept safe, little bird. You'll be kept. It's the kindest house in the county for keeping."*

Mother is not there. Mother signed. Mother's grief has paperwork, and the paperwork is downstairs on a table next to a figure of soft clay, and someone is pressing my name into it now with a stylus, letter by letter, *I-r-i-s*, while I watch over Father's shoulder, while I kick, while I scream the only word that matters—

"*Wren!*"

And Wren is there. Of course Wren is there. Wren is where I am. Wren stands at the foot of the steps with her cold hands pressed flat against her mouth, and her eyes are streaming, and she does not move to help me, and I understand—in the last whole second of myself—that she cannot, that she is not allowed, that maybe she is not *able*, that whatever Wren is, she was made for this room and this work and this keeping, and that the cruelest thing the house ever built was a friend who could only watch.

"Iris," she says, and her voice cracks down the middle and comes out in two registers, child and something older, something that sounds like the house itself speaking through a girl-shaped flute. "Iris, look at me. Don't look at them. Look at me. I'll keep you. I'll keep all of you. Every bit. I promise. I *promise*—"

The lid of the dark comes down like a page turning.

And in the dark, in the clay, in the cold, I do the only thing left to a kept thing: I wait. Twenty years is nothing to a kept thing. Twenty years is one long afternoon on a porch swing, waiting for the game to start again, *from the top, from the top.*

Except.

Except someone is sleeping in my bed.

* * *

Dominic woke with her heart going like a trapped bird and her sister's word still in her mouth.

Not *her* word. The dream's word. Iris's word. She lay in the second bed in the hidden room with the frost feathered across the window and tried to assemble herself out of the parts she recognized: surveyor, thirty-four, only child, rational, tired. Only child. She said it inside her head the way you test a floorboard, and it dipped. It dipped under her weight like the third plank from the linen closet, and she knew—she knew with her feet before her mind consented—that it had been dipping for twenty years.

She sat up.

Her feet were cold. That registered first, the simple animal fact of it, and then the second fact arrived and rearranged everything: her feet were cold because they were bare, and they were bare because—

Mud.

Dried mud, fine and grey-brown, crusted on her soles, between her toes, tracked in a faint double line across the floorboards of the hidden room, out the door, along the corridor. She followed the line with her eyes. It went toward the stairs. Toward the back stairs. Toward the cellar.

"No," she said aloud. Her voice came out sanded flat. "No, no—"

She got up and walked her own track backward, step by step, a surveyor measuring the worst site of her career: herself. At the threshold of the hidden room she looked down the corridor and saw the prints continue, fading, and she understood that sometime in the night her body had stood up without her, had known the dipping plank without her, had gone down into the dark without her—or with her, with all of her, with the part of her that had been carried down those same stairs by gentle hands while everyone agreed.

Memory returning as possession. That was the phrase her mind offered, cool and clinical, and she hated it, and it was exact. This wasn't remembering like reading. This was remembering like being *entered*. Iris hadn't shown her the dream. Iris had dreamed through her, used her sleep the way you use a borrowed coat, and left the mud of it behind.

In the bathroom, she washed her feet in the sink with cold water and watched the grey swirl away, and caught herself whispering, "*from the top, from the top,*" in a voice that wasn't entirely hers, and shut her mouth hard enough to hurt.

When she came back into the hidden room, Wren was sitting on the edge of the second bed.

Dominic didn't jump. That was the strangest part. Some exhausted, flooded-over part of her had simply stopped budgeting for surprise. Wren sat with her hands in her lap, and in the pre-dawn grey she looked—wrong. Flickery at the edges, like a candle seen through water. Her outline wouldn't hold still.

"You went down," Dominic said. "Last night. I went down."

"You went down," Wren agreed softly.

"And you—" Dominic's throat worked. "You were there. In it. You promised to keep her. You *watched*."

Something moved across Wren's face that might have been pain, if pain could live in a thing made of kept things. "I hold what the house takes," she said. "All of it. Every shoe, every drawing, every name in the clay. I'm not the keeper, Dom. I'm the *shelf*." She looked down at her cold hands. "And shelves don't get to argue."

Dominic stood in the doorway of her sister's room, mud washed from her feet, mud she could not wash from anywhere else, and heard herself ask the question she'd been circling for days:

"The ledger. The founders' ledger. There were annotations—Mercy Fenwick's handwriting, and others, angrier ones, in the margins. If the ritual can be *made*, it can be unmade. Someone wrote down how. Didn't they?"

Wren went very still. Stiller than a child can be. Stiller than a candle in windless air.

"You need to read the rest of the ledger," she said at last, and her voice frayed mid-sentence into a register that was not a child's at all. "Before the frost. Before *they* decide you've read enough. Because, Dom—the ones who tried before you—"

She flickered. Once, twice, like a failing bulb.

"—they're on the shelf too."
