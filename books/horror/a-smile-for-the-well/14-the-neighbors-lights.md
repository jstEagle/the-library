## 14. The Neighbors' Lights

The first one came at dusk, and Dominic almost mistook him for a surveyor.

She was in the front room with the Fenwick ledger open on her knees and a pencil behind her ear, transcribing Mercy's annotations onto legal pads because the old paper shed like a molting animal whenever she turned a page too fast. Headlights swept the drive. She looked up expecting the county man she'd emailed about the well easement, some tired public servant with a clipboard and a flashlight.

No car came up the drive. The headlights just stopped—out on the road, past the iron fence—and then they went off, and a shape got out of the car and stood beside it.

He didn't come to the gate. That was the first wrong thing. In twenty years of rural appraisals, Dominic had learned that people always came to the gate. They wanted to look at your clipboard, ask if you'd found anything interesting, complain about the road or the taxes or the state of the hedgerows. People were incurious about houses but ravenous about other people's business.

This man stood at the fence line with his hands in his pockets and his face tipped up toward the house, and he watched the windows.

She gave it ten minutes. Then fifteen. The light died by degrees and he didn't move, didn't check his phone, didn't shift his weight the way standing bodies eventually have to. When she finally went to the door and called out—not a greeting, just a neutral *hello* pitched to carry—he turned his head toward her voice with the smooth unhurried motion of an owl, and even across forty yards of dark lawn she could see that his face was kind.

That was the second wrong thing. He looked at her the way you look at someone sleeping.

"Evening," he said. Warm. Reasonable. "Cold one coming."

Then he got back in his car and drove away, and Dominic stood in the doorway with the ledger against her chest and her heart going like a trapped bird, telling herself: *county man, lost, turned around.* Telling herself the way she'd been telling herself things for ten years, and hearing, for the first time, how much effort the telling took. How much it cost. Like pushing a piano uphill in gloves.

Two more cars passed before midnight. Neither slowed.

* * *

By the second night there were six of them, and they had brought chairs.

That detail undid her worse than anything else had. Not torches, not crowbars, not a mob with painted signs—the banality would have been almost a relief, something she could name and fear cleanly. Instead, when she pulled back the curtain at nine o'clock, she saw folding chairs set up along the outside of the fence, spaced with the casual geometry of people waiting for a parade. A thermos passed hand to hand. Someone had a dog lying flat at their feet, chin on paws, watching the house with the same patience as its owner.

Mrs. Calloway's son was among them. Gerald. She recognized him from the archive photographs and from the single disastrous visit to his mother's room, before the nurses had gently suggested Dominic not return. He sat closest to the gate, and he wasn't watching the windows like the others. He was watching the door. Waiting for her to come out so he could be kind to her again.

"They can't cross," Wren said.

Dominic hadn't heard her arrive. That was new, and bad—Wren used to announce herself with creaks and cold spots, a courtesy of physics. Now she simply was there, standing at the window's edge, and Dominic noticed with a lurch that the girl's outline had gone soft at the shoulders, like a photograph left in the sun. Her left hand kept doing something small and repetitive, opening and closing, as if testing whether it still worked.

"You're sure?"

"The deed." Wren said it the way you'd say *the law*. "The keeping runs to the property line. What's inside stays inside, and what's outside—" she nodded at the chairs, the thermos, the patient faces "—stays outside. They made sure of that, back at the beginning. Aldous charged them extra for it."

"For what?"

"For the right to forget without having to look at what they forgot." Wren's voice did something terrible then—it flickered, mid-sentence, into another register, lower and younger and slightly breathless, a voice that said *Dommy, Dommy, look*—and then snapped back. Wren pressed her softening hand flat against the glass as if steadying herself. "Sorry. I'm sorry. It's getting harder to stay me."

"How long do I have?" Dominic asked, though she already knew. She'd been counting since the torn page. Since *before the first hard frost sets.*

Wren looked out at the fence line. "One more night after this one."

Beyond the glass, Gerald Calloway lifted his head, found her at the window, and smiled. He raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. More like a blessing. Or a warning given politely.

* * *

She tried the phone at two in the morning, because the phone was the last thing she trusted. The house had gotten into everything else—her registration, her email, the little automated apologies it sent in her voice—but the phone still showed her sister's contact, the one entry she'd never been able to explain deleting, restored now to the top of her favorites like a wound reopened. *IRIS.* Just the name. No number attached, or a number of all zeros, she'd never managed to read it twice.

There was no signal. There had been no signal for two days, which she had also explained away—dead zone, storm interference, tower maintenance—and which she now understood was simply the house holding its breath around her.

So she watched the neighbors instead, from the dark of the upstairs landing, through the gap in the curtains, and she took notes because notes were the last professional habit she had left and she clung to it like a rail in floodwater.

*6:40 p.m. — four vehicles. Seven adults. One adolescent, female, ~13, brought by grandmother, seated apart.*
*8:15 p.m. — no movement observed in 95 minutes. No conversation audible at 30 m despite apparent group setting.*
*9:02 p.m. — all subjects turned heads simultaneously toward east window. Simultaneity not explainable by stimulus. Nothing visible in east window except my reflection.*

She stopped writing after that last line and sat with it. *My reflection.* Because here was the thing she hadn't put on paper yet, the thing that had been sitting in her chest since the second night like swallowed ice:

They weren't watching to see what she did.

They were watching to see if she was still there.

She'd caught it finally, third night, in the angle of their attention. It wasn't surveillance of an enemy. It was the vigil of people checking on something precious and fragile—a candle flame, a pulse. Every hour or so, one of them would stand, walk a slow circuit of the fence, and study the windows with an expression she could only call *reassurance-seeking*. And whatever they saw seemed to satisfy them, because they'd sit back down and pass the thermos and settle in for another hour of silence.

They were making sure the forgetting was holding. Making sure she hadn't started remembering where they could see it.

Because that was the arithmetic underneath everything, the equation Mercy's annotations had laid bare: the ritual didn't run on the house alone. It ran on agreement. Twenty families had paid, and twenty families' forgetting fed the walls, and if one rememberer spoke the truth aloud—if the name crossed her lips inside the house before the frost—then every payment came due at once. Every erased thing came home. Gerald Calloway wasn't guarding her from the house.

He was guarding the town from her.

* * *

On the last full day, they began to speak to her.

Not crossing the line. Never that. But in the morning a woman Dominic half-recognized from the village shop stood at the fence and called across the lawn, pleasantly, the way you'd call to a neighbor over a garden wall.

"Lovely light this morning! Supposed to turn tonight, though. Hard freeze, they're saying." A pause, warm and expectant. "You'll want to be settled somewhere warm before it comes."

"I'm fine where I am," Dominic called back, and hated how thin her voice sounded across all that grass.

"Oh, I know, love. We all know." The woman smiled. "You've always been such a sensible girl. Only child, weren't you? Used to play alone out here for hours. Such an imagination." The smile held. The eyes above it did something else entirely. "Imagination's a comfort. Best leave it be, though, once you're grown."

Dominic went inside and locked a door that had never needed locking, and stood with her back against it, shaking.

At noon, someone left a covered dish on the fence post. Casserole, still warm, with a note in careful cursive: *Whatever you're going through, we understand. Come home. — G.C.* She looked out the window and Gerald was back in his chair, watching the door, waiting to see if she'd take it. Accepting the food would mean something. She understood that now. Everything meant something here; the whole village had become a ritual itself, a keeping-rite with folding chairs and casseroles instead of chalk and candles, and every kindness was a stitch meant to sew her mouth shut.

She left the dish on the post until dark. When she checked at midnight it was gone, taken away untouched, and somehow that felt like a verdict.

* * *

The frost came early to the windows that night, ahead of schedule, impatient.

It wrote while she watched. She'd stopped being able to look away from it—this was the invasion she couldn't lock out, the house no longer bothering with doors. Ferns of ice crawled up the downstairs mirrors, and in their branching she could read intention the way you read handwriting: loops deliberate, strokes confident. Letters first, half-formed, then abandoned. Then the drawing again, the one from before, rendered now in lines of white across the hallway mirror: two girls holding hands. One figure solid. One figure drawn in dashes, like a diagram of something missing.

Below it, pressed into the frost by no finger she could see, small and round and unmistakably a child's print, the word:

*SOON*

"It's not talking to me," Wren said from the stair. Dominic turned. The girl stood in the doorway with the hall light passing through her like she was made of the same material as the frost, and her face was doing something Dominic had never seen on it—fear. Real fear, a child's fear, unarmored. "It's never talked to me before. Twenty years, and it never once—" Her voice split down the middle, one half Wren's and one half the other voice, the breathless younger one, both saying the same words in unison like a choir of two. "—it knows it doesn't need me anymore. Once you say the name, I'm not the container. I'm just the wrapping paper."

"Wren—"

"Don't." She held up the flickering hand. "Don't make it a promise. You might break it. You should be allowed to break it." She looked at the window, at the fence line beyond, where the chairs stood empty now in the moonlight but the cars were still there, engines off, exhaust pipes gleaming with cold. Everyone had gone home to sleep. Everyone would be back at dawn. "They'll come inside tomorrow. After the frost sets. They won't be able to help it—the line only holds while the keeping holds, and you've been pulling threads out of it for a week. When it goes, nothing keeps them out. Nothing keeps anything out. Everything they buried comes up the drive at once, and they'll be so sorry, Dommy, they'll be weeping-sorry, and sorry people will do anything to make the hurting stop."

"Including stopping me."

"Including stopping you." Wren's image guttered like a struck match, and for a half-second Dominic saw through her—not the wall behind her but something else, a cellar, a shape of gentle hands—and then the girl reassembled, smaller somehow. "Mercy's rite takes time. The words take time. You'll be standing in the middle of it, eyes shut, speaking names, while the whole town walks down your stairs crying and begging and meaning every word of it."

"Then I'll need someone to hold the door."

Wren went very still. Outside, the first flakes of the coming hard frost drifted through the beam of the porch light, fat and slow and certain, and somewhere down the fence line a car door opened and closed, softly, like a knuckle rapped once on wood.

Gerald Calloway stood at the gate in the falling snow. He didn't ring, didn't call out. He just stood there in the cold with his hat in his hands, looking up at the lit window, and after a long moment he bowed his head—the posture of a man at a graveside—and Dominic understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that tomorrow they wouldn't wait for the frost to finish.

They were done watching.

They were coming in at first light.
