## 14. Midwinter Eve

The snow began at dusk on the twenty-first and did not stop.

Orsen watched it from the cottage window — not falling so much as arriving, a slow grey descent that erased the lane, the walls, the sea beyond the headland, until the world was reduced to the width of the glass and the small warm room behind it. The flakes came down without wind, without sound, in the patient way of things that intend to stay. By midnight the roads were gone. By morning the road to the coast road was gone. By noon there was no evidence anywhere in Hollowmere that the village had ever been connected to anything else.

Orsen had tried the phone twice. The first time there was no signal; the second time there was a signal, three bars of it, and a woman's voice reading out a number Orsen did not dial because they could not have said why. They set the phone face-down on the table after that, the way you turn a photograph over when it looks at you too long.

The lights stayed burning. All of them. Orsen had learned that much by now, had learned it in their body the way you learn a staircase in the dark: every bulb in the cottage lit from dusk to dawn, the oven door open a crack so the pilot glow showed, the torch left on in the loo where the window was smallest. It was wasteful. It was also, apparently, necessary. The landlady's habits had become Orsen's habits somewhere along the way without any decision being made about it, the way a guest's posture slowly becomes the host's furniture.

They counted the doors before bed. Eleven. There were always eleven, but counting them was the point, or part of the point, or perhaps only the shape the fear took when it needed something to do with its hands.

* * *

In the morning the sexton's niece came for them.

She was perhaps nineteen, wrapped in a coat too big for her, her face pink with cold and something else underneath the cold. She stood in the doorway without knocking again — she had knocked once, days ago, and Orsen had opened the door to find her already turning away as if the knock had been a formality addressed to the house rather than to anyone inside it.

"They're waiting," she said.

"Who is?"

"The village." She said it simply, the way you'd say *the tide's in*. "At the church. You're expected."

"Expected." Orsen heard their own voice go flat and careful. "For what?"

The girl looked past them into the cottage, at all those burning bulbs, and something moved across her face that might have been pity or might have been envy. "It's midwinter eve," she said, as though that answered everything, and turned back down the lane through snow that came up past her knees, leaving a line of footprints that filled in behind her almost faster than she made them, so that it seemed less like walking than like being slowly unmade.

Orsen dressed warmly. They took the recorder — an old habit, and also not a habit at all anymore but a kind of reflex, the folklorist's hand reaching for the machine the way a believer's reaches for a charm. Then they stood in the doorway a long moment, looking at the white lane, at the church tower just visible above the drifts with its empty belfry, and understood that they were not being summoned so much as collected.

They went anyway. That was the thing Orsen would remember afterward, if there was an afterward in which remembering happened: that they went without being dragged, without rope around the wrists, without anything except the girl's footprints filling in ahead of them and the dreadful calm that had been settling over them for days now like the snow itself.

* * *

The church was full.

Not full the way churches are full on feast days — not with hats and coats and children fidgeting and someone's baby starting to cry. Full the way a room is full when everyone in it has been holding still for a long time. Every pew occupied. Every face lit from below by candle flame, so that the congregation looked less like people than like carvings of people, weathered and patient, waiting for the sculptor to finish.

Candles everywhere. On the sills, on the window ledges, clustered on the altar in banks of dripping wax, and — this was the detail that caught in Orsen's chest like a fishhook — one candle set in each of the windows facing out toward the water, so that from the sea the church must look like a lantern with something alive inside it.

And rope. Coils of it stacked by the west door, neat as harvest. A length of it laid along the aisle runner, straight as a spine. Nobody was touching it. Nobody was looking at it. It sat among them the way the truth sat among them, acknowledged by everyone and named by no one.

Maren was near the front. When Orsen came down the aisle — and the aisle was long, longer than it should have been, the candles making the distance swim — Maren did not look up. Her hands were folded in her lap and her knuckles were white and her lips were moving, very slightly, and Orsen realized with a lurch that she was counting. Not doors. Something else. Her lips shaped numbers over and over, a rosary of arithmetic against whatever was coming.

The sexton stood by the font. He looked smaller than Orsen had ever seen him, shrunk into his coat, his hands resting on the stone rim as if the font were the only thing holding him upright. His eyes found Orsen's and held them, and in them Orsen saw no triumph, no malice — only the terrible tiredness of a man who has been waiting fifty years for a bill and is almost relieved to see the postman.

"You came," he said.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped." He said it like a confession. "Hoping's not the same as knowing. Nothing here is ever quite the same as knowing. That's how it gets you — it leaves you the hoping, so you'll walk the last mile yourself."

Orsen looked down the aisle, at the ropes, at the faces. "If I turned around and walked out—"

"You'd get as far as the headland," the sexton said. "Snow's deep past the quarry. And it'd be kinder not to." He paused. "Kinder on you, I mean. What's in the water doesn't chase. It waits. Chasing is for things that can lose."

"And if I don't do what you want? If I stand here and sing nothing?"

The sexton's gaze went to the east window, where the last light was failing behind the snow. "Then we wait," he said. "We're good at waiting. We've had practice. And the bell rings, and the dead come up out of the ground wearing our mothers' faces, and we keep the lights on, and we count the doors, and eventually—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Eventually somebody does what needs doing. It's always eventually, here. That's the whole of Hollowmere. Eventually."

* * *

They gave Orsen the front pew, which was the courtesy you extend to a guest, and Orsen sat in it and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that the courtesy and the trap were the same object viewed from different ends. Guest and sacrifice. There was an old word for that, they thought — some word from some older village than this one — and the fact that they couldn't retrieve it didn't matter, because the villagers could, and had, and had built their whole grey endurance on top of it.

An old man near the back began to hum.

It was low, barely more than breath, a single phrase of melody that Orsen recognized from the archive recordings — the plough-song, the one sung at the turning of the year, the one with the verse nobody would repeat. Other voices joined him, one by one, until the whole church was humming it, a sound like the building itself breathing, and Orsen sat in the middle of it with their hands flat on their knees and felt the melody move through the pew, through their ribs, looking for a voice to happen in.

They did not sing. But God, some traitor part of them wanted to. That was the horror of it, Orsen thought — not the ropes, not the candles, not even the bell. The horror was how much of you wanted to belong to the song. How the song fit your mouth like a key fits a lock it was cut for.

Maren turned then, finally, and looked at Orsen down the length of two pews. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, the eyes of someone who has cried herself below the waterline of crying. Her lips stopped moving. For a moment neither of them did anything at all, and in that moment Orsen saw everything Maren was carrying: the lullaby she'd sung once and begged to have erased; the child's name she'd said aloud in a shut kitchen with the curtains drawn; the tenderness braided through the betrayal, both of them true, both of them hers.

*I'm sorry,* her mouth said, without sound. And then, after a moment, worse: *Thank you.*

Orsen shook their head — not refusal, exactly. Not forgiveness either. Just the honest report of a body that didn't know yet what it felt.

* * *

It was fully dark outside when the bell began.

There was no warning in it this time, no single toll to mark the hour. It simply started ringing the way rain starts — all at once, everywhere, as if it had been ringing for years just beyond hearing and had finally stepped over the threshold. The sound came up through the floor stones, through the pews, through the soles of Orsen's shoes and the bones of their ankles, and it came down from the empty tower where no bell hung, and it came off the water and out of the ground and out of the spaces between the candles' flames.

Every villager flinched. None of them moved.

The humming stopped. In its place: silence, and the bell inside the silence, ringing without pause, without rhythm, without mercy — a peal with no ringer, rung by nothing, for no hour, and Orsen understood suddenly why the villagers kept their lights burning and counted their doors, because this sound made the whole world provisional. Made the walls suggestions. Made the dark a thing with weight and intent, pressing at the windows where the candles faced outward like small brave lies.

A woman three pews back began to weep — silently, terribly, her shoulders shaking while her face stayed composed, tears running off her jaw onto her folded hands. Beside her, a man put his arm around her and stared straight ahead at the altar, his lips moving. Counting. They were all counting now, Orsen realized. Counting doors, counting candles, counting the bells between one toll and the next, counting anything, because the counting was the dam and the bell was the water and everyone in this room had spent their whole life learning exactly how high the water could rise before the dam gave.

The sexton pushed himself upright from the font. He looked older than he had an hour ago, as if the bell were ringing the years out of him.

"It knows," he said, and his voice carried strangely in the ringing air, flat and clear, the way voices carry underwater. "Midwinter eve. Fifty-one years tonight since we let them carry her out. It keeps better books than we do."

He turned to Orsen. Behind him, at the end of the aisle, the west door stood open — Orsen hadn't seen anyone open it, and snow was drifting across the threshold in slow grey tongues, and beyond the door the churchyard was full of shapes that were not snowdrifts, standing among the leaning stones, patient as the patience in the pews.

"Come to the font," the sexton said gently. "It's time."

And Orsen rose — rose because the alternative was the shapes in the churchyard, rose because Maren was watching, rose because some part of them had been rising toward this moment since the day an old woman cut a singer off mid-verse and went white as the winter — and walked down the aisle between the candles and the rope, past the weeping woman and the counting men, to the font where their grandfather's crime had been paid for in advance by strangers who never learned to stop paying.

The bell rang on. It did not stop. It would not stop now, Orsen understood, until it was given a reason — and every face in Hollowmere turned toward them with the same expression, hope and shame fused into something that had no name in any language Orsen had ever recorded, as the sexton lifted the old book from the font's ledge and opened it to a page soft with handling, and the first shape came in out of the snow and stood dripping in the doorway, and the sexton began to read.
