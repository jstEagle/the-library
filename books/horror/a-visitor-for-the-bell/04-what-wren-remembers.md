## 4. What Wren Remembers

Wren lived in the walls the way a thought lives in the back of the mind — always present, rarely attended to.

The space between the plaster and the lathe was his by long habit: a corridor of dust and mouse-skeletons and old wallpaper curled like shed skin, running the whole circumference of Hollow Bell House in a loop that no surveyor's tape would ever find. He could walk it in the dark. He had walked it in the dark for twenty years, and before that for sixty, and before that for longer than he liked to count, because counting was one of the things that made him feel the seams.

He felt them now.

He sat cross-legged in the wall-cavity behind the nursery-to-be — the room the house was still growing, its bones creaking out of the east wing like a flower deciding to bloom — and he pressed his palms flat against his own forearms and tried to remember what he had been before he was stitched.

There had never been a *before*. That was the honest answer, and honesty was another thing that made the seams itch. He had been made whole and finished, the way a bell is cast whole and finished, with his purpose poured into him at the same temperature as his love. He remembered the making anyway. Not as memory, exactly. As texture. The way you remember being born by the shape of the scar the cord left.

*Stitch, stitch,* said the house, in the voice it used inside the walls, which was not a voice at all but a pressure, a loom working in the dark. *Stitch, stitch.*

He had been made from want. That much he knew the way he knew his own hands. A girl had wanted so hard and so lonely that her wanting had become a substance, thick as honey, and the founders had dipped their fingers in it and drawn him out of the air like taffy. They had given him her need for a spine. They had given him her loneliness for lungs. And then they had leaned close — three of them, their faces like faces on coins, worn smooth and noble and wrong — and they had breathed into his mouth the one instruction that mattered:

*Keep her. Keep her here. Deliver her when the bell is hungry.*

He had been nine years old the day he opened his eyes, because she was nine years old, and he could not be older or younger than the girl who dreamed him. He remembered the first thing he ever saw: Mirabel Hartwell sitting on the porch steps of a house that was already old, hugging her knees, watching the road her parents' car had gone down, waiting for it to come back. It did not come back for eleven days. By the fourth day she had stopped crying, because crying made the house seem to lean closer, interested, and she didn't like being interesting to it. By the sixth day she had started talking to the air beside her, quietly, the way children do when they have decided no one real is coming.

And the air had answered.

*I'm Wren,* he had said, because the name arrived in him fully formed, the way the purpose had, and because birds were the only things that ever landed on this porch and then, mercifully, left again.

She had looked at him — actually looked, her gray eyes going wide and wet and fierce all at once — and she had smiled, and the smile had gone through him like a nail through soft wood, fixing him to her forever. He hadn't known yet that the fixing was the design. He had thought it was luck. He had thought, with the whole simple shining surface of himself, *I am the luckiest thing ever imagined.*

The house had hummed in its foundations, satisfied. A keeper delivered. A hook set.

* * *

In the walls, Wren unfolded his legs and stood, and the wall-space stood with him, breathing around his shoulders. Dust sifted down through a crack of evening light. Below him, muffled by lath and plaster, he could hear Mirabel moving through the ground floor — the small domestic sounds of her survival ritual. A lamp clicking on. Another. Her footsteps going door to door, touching each frame the way a priest touches a lintel. Counting.

*Twelve,* she murmured, and he heard the pencil scratch in her notebook, and his chest did the thing it always did, the warm terrible squeeze, because she still counted doors, she still left lights burning, she still kept her little ceremonies against the dark — and every single one of those habits had been taught to her by him, and every single one of them had been designed by the founders, seeded into him so he would seed them into her, small rituals that made the house feel survivable, habitable, *home*. Rituals that kept a child calm in the pen.

They had called it acclimation. He had heard them say it through the walls, in the year before the last visitor, their voices dry as ledger paper. *A frightened offering runs. A settled offering walks.* Teach her to count the doors and she will trust the doors. Teach her to leave lights on and she will believe the light belongs to her. Let her love the house, and on the appointed night she will climb the tower stairs on her own feet, and we will not have to carry her, and a willing heart feeds better than a dragged one.

Willing. The word had a taste, and the taste was copper.

Wren pressed his forehead against the cool plaster and listened to Mirabel below, and hated himself in the specific way he had practiced for twenty years, which was to say: without any intention of stopping.

Because here was the seam he could not pick open, the stitch he could not cut: he loved her. Not as an assignment. Not anymore, if it had ever been only that. Somewhere between the porch steps and the hiding places and the games of raven-and-thimble in the overgrown garden, the love the founders had installed as a mechanism had become actual — had grown flesh around its gears, the way a pearl grows around grit. He could not locate the border where the design ended and he began. Maybe there wasn't one. Maybe he was nothing but design, all the way down, and the love was just the trap wearing his face.

But when she laughed — and she had laughed yesterday, on the porch, one startled bark of laughter when he'd told her the ivy was gossiping about her — something in him had turned toward her like a plant toward a window, helplessly, entirely, and whatever the founders had built him for had seemed, for one whole second, like a rumor about someone else.

Then the house had shifted under his feet, patient as tide, and reminded him what he was for.

* * *

He walked the wall-loop to keep himself company. Past the library, where the Hartwell ledger lay closed on the reading table, its clasp glinting; he could feel the book the way you feel a wasp on your sleeve. Past the kitchen, where the pipes knocked their old code — three knocks, pause, three knocks — a language the founders had taught the plumbing, though these days the pipes mostly talked to themselves. Past the front hall, where the extra door stood in the wall where no door should be, its brass handle warming slowly, patiently, like a hand waiting to be held.

At the north corner of the loop, where the walls ran thickest, the house's oldest timbers gathered, and beneath them — far beneath, down past the cellar, down past the stone footings, down in the dark that predated the house and had merely agreed to be built over — the other thing waited. Wren did not look down. Looking down was how it learned your face.

But he listened, the way he always listened, and what he heard was hunger doing arithmetic.

Sixty years since the last feeding. Sixty years since a woman named Alice Vane — no. Since a woman named *Hartwell*, they were always Hartwells, that was the point of the family, the family was the farm — had climbed the tower with her coat buttoned to the throat and her heart going like a rabbit's, and had stood in the circle, and had been *persuaded*, and the bell had rung once, forward, and the thing below had eaten, and the valley had stayed green and the town had stayed asleep and nobody had asked why the church bells all rang flat for a week afterward.

Alice had been persuaded. That was the founders' proudest word. Not forced — *persuaded*. Brought along so gently, so lovingly, that she had stepped into the circle believing it was her own idea. And the instrument of that persuasion, the soft voice at her shoulder for the years leading up to it, the friend who never aged and never left and never let her feel alone enough to flee —

Wren stopped walking.

He had never met the keeper who came before him. Keepers didn't keep. That was the joke the house played on itself: a keeper was made for one child, served one term, and when the bell was fed, the keeper was unmade — unpicked, stitch by stitch, back into the air, back into the general supply of want. He knew this the way he knew his own name. He had been told during the making, gently, the way you tell a child the dog went to live on a farm.

*You will deliver her, and then you may rest.*

Rest. He turned the word over. It had no bottom.

Twenty years ago, he had been meant to rest already. Mirabel had been the appointed one — third generation, Hartwell blood, brought to the house at nine years old the way calves are brought to barns — and the founders' descendants, the thin gray thread of them that remained, had begun the acclimation, and Wren had done everything he was made to do. He had befriended her. He had taught her the doors. He had made the house feel like hers.

And then, on the night before the new moon, with the basin scrubbed and the candles bought and the circle chalked fresh in the tower, Mirabel had come to him in the dark of the nursery with tears on her face and said, *Wren, I don't want to be here anymore,* and something in him had torn clean through, all the stitches at once.

He had hidden her. In the walls, in his own country, in the corridor no tape measure would find, while the house screamed through every pipe and the founders' thread-people tore the rooms apart looking. He had held her for two days in the dark, her head against his chest, her breath slowing from panic to sleep, and he had understood with total clarity that he was spending himself — that every hour of disobedience was a stitch coming loose, that the house could unmake him for this, would unmake him, gladly —

—and he had carried her out through the coal chute at dawn, and put her on the morning bus with stolen money in her pocket, and lied to her with the biggest lie of his existence, which was: *I'll be right behind you. I promise.*

He had watched the bus go from behind the hedge. He had not gone right behind her. He had gone back inside, because the debt had come due and gone unpaid, and someone had to stand in the gap and absorb the house's fury, and he was the only thing in the building made of forgiveness.

It had taken the house eleven years to forgive him. Eleven years of cold walls and silent pipes and the thing below gnawing at the roots of his thoughts. And then, last month, the appraisal letter had come — court-ordered, bureaucratic, innocent — and the house had begun to purr.

*She came back,* the timbers had said. *You see? You see how it ends? Everything comes back to the bell.*

* * *

Below him, the footsteps stopped. A floorboard sighed. Through the hairline crack in the plaster, Wren could see a slice of the hallway: Mirabel standing at the threshold of the east wing, her flashlight beam crawling up wallpaper that had not existed yesterday. The pattern was wisteria — purple clusters on a cream ground, climbing trellises — and Wren's stomach dropped through the floor of himself, because he knew that pattern. He had lain on that pattern. He had traced those flowers with his finger while a nine-year-old girl fell asleep beside him, telling her the vines were holding the ceiling up, promising her the house couldn't fall while the flowers were holding on.

Her childhood bedroom. Her childhood wallpaper. The house was remembering her back to herself, furnishing the trap with the softest things it owned.

Mirabel's hand came up and touched the paper, flat-palmed, the way you touch a face to check for fever. She stood there a long moment. Then she wrote something in her notebook — he couldn't read it, but he knew the posture, the tight shoulders, the professional armor going on plate by plate — and she turned away from the room, deliberately, the way you turn away from a dog you're afraid might follow you.

*Don't sleep in there,* Wren whispered against the plaster, knowing she couldn't hear him, knowing the house could. *Don't sleep in there, Mirabel, please—*

The house heard. Of course the house heard. And somewhere in the east wing, very softly, a nursery door swung open on oiled hinges, and stayed open, hospitable, patient, an invitation with no expiration date.

Wren slid down the inside of the wall until he was sitting in the dust again, his knees up, his arms wrapped around them, a boy-shaped knot of guilt in the dark. Twenty years old in every way except the ways that mattered. Built to deliver her. Loving her more than the house had ever intended, more than the founders had ever budgeted for, more than he knew how to survive.

That was the flaw in him, he thought. The defect. The founders had measured everything — the girl's bloodline, the moon's calendar, the bell's appetite — but they had not accounted for this: that a thing made of love, left alone with its purpose long enough, might start asking whether the two could both be served. Might start hoping, secretly, shamefully, the way a servant hopes, that the answer was no.

The new moon was six nights away. He could feel it coming up the stairs of time like footsteps.

He had broken the ritual once. He knew exactly what it cost, and he knew exactly what failing twice would cost, and underneath both prices lay the one the founders had never written down: if the bell went unfed this time — truly unfed, no substitute, no delay — the thing below would not stay below. The archivists in town still flinched at the house's name. The valley's oldest maps marked the hill with a symbol no one could translate. Hunger of that size did not knock. It simply arrived, and the valley would learn what the founders had died knowing: that the binding was not cruelty. It was a dam. And dams are cruel only to the ones who maintain them.

So: her, or everyone. His love, or the world it lived in. The founders' arithmetic, neat and terrible, and he was the calculator.

Wren put his face in his hands. In the walls around him, the house hummed its loom-song, stitching the night together, and far below, the hungry thing turned over in its sleep like a dog dreaming of meat.

Six nights. He had six nights to find a way to be both the keeper and the man — and tomorrow at dawn, he knew, she would come looking for answers, because she had seen the wallpaper too, and Mirabel Hartwell had never in her life left a locked question alone.

He would have to decide what to tell her.

He would have to decide fast.
