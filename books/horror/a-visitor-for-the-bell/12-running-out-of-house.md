## 12. Running Out of House

Mirabel packed the way she had learned to pack as a child: fast, quiet, and with one ear turned toward the rest of the house.

She had done it in the old days too — the nights when the arguments downstairs went from voices to glass, when her mother's boyfriend's truck growled in the drive and she'd sit on her bed with a duffel already zipped, waiting to see which way the night would break. She had thought she'd left that girl behind in a hundred rented rooms and two decades of distance. But here she was at Hollow Bell House, at half past three in the morning, folding her laser measure into its case with hands that remembered exactly how to be small and quick.

The ledger lay on the desk where she'd left it, open to the page with her name in fresh ink. She did not look at it again. Looking at it felt like signing something.

*Before the new moon,* Wren had said. *Please.* And then the house had punished him for saying it — doors sealing themselves like wounds closing, lights dying room by room until he'd stood in the dark of the east hall shouting her name in a voice that cracked down the middle like the bell above them both.

She'd found him after, sitting on the nursery floor with his knees drawn up, looking nine years old and infinitely tired. He hadn't explained. He'd only said, *I can't tell you everything. If I tell you everything it will use my mouth to do it.* And then, quieter: *But you have to go, Mirabel. You have to go before I stop being able to want you to.*

That sentence had been the thing that finally moved her. Not the ledger. Not the basin stained dark beneath the bell. That.

So: the duffel. The laptop. The car keys off the hook by the kitchen door, where they had been every morning since she arrived, patient as bait.

The house was quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

She moved through the ground floor by memory and phone-light, past the library where her ancestors' names marched down the pages like a procession, past the parlor where the wallpaper had begun, she now noticed, to bloom with tiny wisteria sprigs that had not been there in daylight. The front door stood at the end of the entrance hall, black iron hinges, oak gone silver with age. She had locked it behind her the first night out of habit. It would open. Doors opened. That was what doors were for.

Her hand closed on the latch. The door swung inward onto —

The nursery.

Not the porch. Not the gravel drive with her rental car sitting gray under the moon. The nursery, the impossible room from no blueprint, moonlight falling through windows that should have shown the east lawn but showed instead a darkness so complete it looked poured rather than merely absent. The rocking horse stood in the corner. The crib with its rail shaped like a birdcage. The wallpaper — her wallpaper, the pattern of leaping deer and hollyhocks from the apartment on Delmore Street, from the year she was eight, reproduced stitch for stitch in a house built a century before she was born.

Mirabel stood in the doorway with her hand still on the latch and let herself feel, for exactly three seconds, the full animal horror of it. Then she shut the door, turned around, and tried the front door again.

The nursery.

She backed away this time. Walked the length of the entrance hall — forty feet, she had measured it, thirty-eight point six on the day the numbers were still behaving — and tried the door at the far end, the one into the old morning room.

The nursery.

"Okay," she said aloud, because her father had taught her that talking to empty rooms kept you from screaming at them, and because right now the room was not empty; it was listening. "Okay. Fine."

* * *

She stopped trying the same door twice. That was the first rule of the night, and she wrote it on the inside of her own skull in block letters: *the house is not stupid, stop being predictable.*

Instead she mapped it. This, at least, was hers — the surveyor's discipline, the one craft the last week hadn't taken from her. She started in the kitchen, marked her position by the sink and the cold black window over it, and chose exits at random: the pantry door, the cellar hatch, the servants' stair, the back mudroom. She took left turns when instinct said right. She counted steps between landmarks and wrote the counts on her forearm in pen, a surveyor's tally running up her skin like a confession.

Pantry door: nursery.
Cellar hatch: nursery, though the stairs had gone down twelve steps first, and the nursery's ceiling was higher than it should have been, and the rocking horse had been moved six inches closer to the center of the room.
Servants' stair: eleven steps down, a landing, nine steps more, a door — nursery. The rocking horse facing the door now. Facing *her.*

By the fourth circuit she understood the shape of the thing, and understanding was worse than confusion. The house wasn't trapping her in a maze. A maze would have been almost respectful — a contest, walls against wits. This was simpler and crueler. Every path ended in the same room the way every river ends in the sea. The house was not trying to keep her from leaving.

It was trying to keep her *here*, in this room, among these objects, until she understood that she had never really left it at all.

She stood in the middle of the nursery with her pen-tallied arms wrapped around herself and looked at the wallpaper deer leaping their endless silent leap. On the shelf above the crib sat a tin bank shaped like a barn, paint worn off the roof where a child had thumbed it smooth. Her thumb had done that. She could remember doing it, the rough-sweet smell of the tin, the rattle of pennies.

"I don't live here anymore," she told the room.

The room said nothing, but the temperature dropped the way it does when someone opens a door somewhere else in a house, and Mirabel felt the argument land differently than she'd meant it. Not a declaration. An appeal. As if she were pleading her case to a judge who owned the courthouse, the lawbooks, and the word *guilty.*

* * *

It was Wren's voice that saved her from the fifth circuit — or rather, the absence of it that made her stop and listen.

He had been near all night. She'd caught fragments of him at the edges: a footfall in the hall that landed too lightly for an adult, a breath of that green, rain-on-stone smell that was somehow the smell of being seven and safe. He couldn't come to her. She understood that without being told. The house held him by whatever passed for his spine, and every step he took toward her cost him something she didn't want to price.

But now, faintly, through the nursery wall — the wall that on the blueprint was exterior brick and should have contained nothing but insulation and weather — came a sound. Scraping. A rhythmic, deliberate scrape, like a stick dragged along siding.

Then his voice, thin as paper held to light:

"Mira. The window. Your left."

There was no window on her left. There was wallpaper, and behind it, per the drawings, a chimney chase.

She crossed to the wall anyway. Put her palm flat against it. The plaster was cold, colder than the room, and under her hand she felt — or imagined, or refused to imagine otherwise — a draft. Moving air. Outside air.

"Wren." She kept her voice low. "What am I doing?"

"Opening it." A pause, and then, faster, urgent, tumbling: "Listen — it'll fight you, it fights me worse, but the sash cord's cut, I cut it, there's a pocket knife in your bag, blue handle, you've had it since—" He stopped himself. She heard him take a breath that no imaginary friend should need to take. "Blue-handled knife. Bottom of the duffel. Cut along the seam where the paint bulges. The window's been painted shut since 1974 but the wood's rotten, Mira, it'll give, it wants to give, I've been working at it from my side for—"

"Your side." Her stomach performed a slow, sick revolution. "Wren, what floor are we on?"

Silence. The particular silence of someone deciding whether to lie.

"What. Floor."

"The third," he said. "I'm on the roof slope outside the third-floor dormer. The nursery is — the house has moved the nursery, Mira. It's not where the blueprint says. It hasn't been for two days. You're three stories up and the drop below you is the side yard and I need you to trust me for about four seconds longer than feels survivable."

Mirabel looked at the wall. At the bulge in the paint, which now that she saw it she could not unsee — a long vertical swell, like something pressing outward, or a frame swelling with rot, or a window that had spent fifty years forgetting how to be a window.

Three stories. Her body, unassisted, versus three stories of night air.

"You're insane," she said. "You're a figment and you're insane."

"I'm yours," he said simply. "You made me out of the part of you that got you out of bad houses. Jumping is kind of the whole job description."

Despite everything — despite the ledger and the basin and the deer leaping their wallpaper leap — a laugh broke out of her, ragged and involuntary. She dug the knife out of the duffel. Blue handle. Of course. She'd bought it at a hardware store in a town whose name she couldn't currently remember, and Wren had known it was there, because Wren knew everything she kept at the bottom of bags, because he was stitched together out of the parts of her that watched the exits.

She set the blade in the seam and pushed.

The wood resisted, then sighed, then split along the seam with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and cold air hit her face — real air, pine and wet stone and coming dawn, air that smelled like nowhere in particular, which after a week in this house was the most beautiful smell she had ever known. The sash groaned upward in jerks, paint flaking, and then the window stood open on the night, and Mirabel leaned out into it.

Below and to the left, crouched on the slate slope of a dormer roof, was Wren.

Moonlight silvered him. He was exactly as he had always been — the cowlick, the patched knee, the grin with one tooth missing that he'd refused to grow back even after she'd pointed out that imaginary friends could have whole teeth if they wanted. But his face in the moonlight was not grinning. It was the face of someone standing in a doorway holding it open while something enormous comes up the stairs behind him.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi yourself." Her voice shook. She let it. "Talk to me about the jump."

"It's not far. Nine feet across, maybe ten. I'm — I'm solid enough to catch you. I've been solid all week, you've noticed." His hands flexed at his sides, and she saw that they were trembling, and that he was making them tremble on purpose, gripping and releasing, keeping himself ready. "You land on the slope, we roll to the valley of the roof, there's a drainpipe—"

"And if I miss?"

"Then I miss catching you," he said, "and I will spend whatever I am finding out what happens after that. But you won't miss. You jumped off the garage roof when you were eight because Danny Pruitt said you wouldn't. You cried the whole way down and you stuck the landing."

"That was four feet, Wren."

"You stuck it crying," he said. "That's the part that matters. That's the part I'm made of."

Behind her, in the nursery, the rocking horse began, very softly, to rock.

No one was touching it. Its runners whispered against the floorboards in a slow, patient rhythm, and the temperature in the room fell so fast that Mirabel's breath bloomed white in the open window, and from somewhere below — below the floor, below the cellar, below whatever the cellar was floated on — came a hum. Low. Bronze. Patient. The cracked bell, three stories and a foundation away, singing to itself in a register she felt in her molars.

The house knew. The house had been letting her run the corridors the way a cat lets a mouse exercise, and now it was done being generous.

"Mira." Wren's voice cracked sharp. "Now. It's going to close it, it closes everything, *now*—"

The window frame shuddered under her hands. Paint flakes rained past her into the dark. Somewhere in the walls, doors were slamming in sequence, floor by floor, a tide of oak and iron rising toward her, and the rational mind — the surveyor, the appraiser, the woman who filed reports in triplicate — screamed that you do not jump out of a window three stories up because a dead child's imaginary friend tells you to, that this is precisely the arithmetic of every nightmare—

And underneath the rational mind, older and quieter, the eight-year-old on the garage roof said: *you stuck it crying.*

Mirabel climbed onto the sill.

The room behind her exhaled. She felt it against her back, vast and cold and wanting, and the rocking horse rocked faster, and from the hum below rose something almost like words, almost like her name spoken by a mouth full of riverbed.

"Mira!" Wren had risen to his feet on the slope, arms out, slate skidding under his heels. "Look at me! Only at me!"

She looked at him. Cowlick. Patched knee. Missing tooth. Twenty years unchanged, because he had never been given time to change, because she had left him standing on a porch in 1997 and driven away and called it growing up.

"I'm sorry I left you," she said.

"I know," he said. "Jump anyway."

She jumped.

The air took her the way air does — indifferent, enormous, honest. For one full second she was nothing but a falling body and a hammering heart, and the yard tilted up at her, and some ancient vertigo shrieked that this was death, this was the whole of it—

His hands closed around her forearms. Solid. Warm. Impossibly, stubbornly solid, and the impact wrenched them both backward onto the slate slope, and they rolled, tangled, laughing or sobbing or both, down into the lead valley of the roof and fetched up against the ridge vent in a heap of elbows and breath.

For a moment neither of them moved. Below them the side yard waited, and beyond it the gate, and beyond the gate the road, and beyond the road a world where houses stayed put and doors led where they led.

"We're out," Mirabel panted. "We're on the roof. We're actually—"

The bell tolled.

Once. Backward — she felt it in her sternum, a note pulled in reverse, a sound like a breath drawn instead of released — and every window in the house lit at once, gold and warm and welcoming, and the front door below them swung open onto a hallway glowing like the entrance to somewhere loved.

From inside, drifting up through the beautiful light, came the smell of pancakes.

Her grandmother's recipe. Sunday mornings. The specific cinnamon-and-butter smell of a kitchen where someone was waiting for you, where you had always been expected, where you had never once been afraid.

"Oh no," Wren whispered beside her. His hand found hers and gripped it hard enough to hurt. "Mira — don't listen to it. Whatever it shows you next, whatever it smells like — it's not memory. It's menu."

The light in the hallway brightened. And up through the open window they'd escaped from, three stories below the stars, the nursery's rocking horse rocked on — faster now, eager, keeping time with the bell.
