## 13. What the House Wants

Wren had never been sick before.

He understood the concept the way he understood most things — secondhand, borrowed from Mirabel's childhood, from the summer she was seven and threw up behind the hydrangeas and cried because she thought her body had betrayed her. He had catalogued the memory carefully back then: the sour smell, the shaking hands, the strange humiliation of a self turning against itself. He had filed it under *things that happen to people who have bodies*.

He did not have a body. Not really. What he had was something the house lent him — a shape stitched out of porch-shadow and wallpaper glue and the particular quality of light that falls through dusty windows at four in the afternoon. And now that shape was heaving, doubled over on the nursery floor, retching up nothing, because there was nothing in him to come up. Only the words. The words were still in him, and they would not leave.

*They never meant to give her to it.*

The house had said that through his mouth. In his voice. With his breath, if breath was what moved his chest when he pretended to breathe for Mirabel's benefit. It had used him like a door uses a hinge — without asking, without noticing, the way you use a thing that exists to be used.

Wren spat — a gesture copied from a boy Mirabel had once watched behind the school, a boy named Denny who spat with great ceremony off the bridge — and nothing came out but a thin thread of silver light that dissolved before it hit the floorboards. The nursery watched him do it. He could feel the wallpaper's attention, the little printed birds turning their printed heads.

"I know," he told them. "I know what I am."

His voice came out wrong. Rougher than his voice should be. There was still a residue of the founders in his throat, a grit of old men's certainty, and he worked his jaw trying to grind it away. Above him, the ceiling plaster had begun, very slowly, to bloom with damp stains shaped like hands.

* * *

It had happened an hour ago, though time in Hollow Bell House was a courtesy the house extended and withdrew as it pleased. Mirabel had finally slept — truly slept, for the first time since the archivist's photograph had cracked open twenty years of sealed memory — curled in the nursery's daybed beneath a quilt the house had made for her out of patterns she'd loved at nine. Wren had sat in the rocking chair across the room, keeping watch the way he'd kept watch every night of her childhood here, and he had been so full of love and guilt that he hadn't noticed the pressure building until it was already inside his mouth.

The house didn't knock. The house had never knocked. Doors opened for it; walls made way; and when it wanted to speak, it simply reached into whatever part of itself was nearest and most pliable, and Wren had always been the most pliable thing in the building.

He had felt it take his jaw first. A settling, like a hand adjusting a mask.

And then his mouth had opened, and what came out was not one voice but many, layered like sediment — young voices and old, men's and women's, some confident, some frightened, all of them certain — the founders, speaking in chorus through the puppet they had built for exactly this purpose, and Wren had sat in the rocking chair unable even to close his eyes while his own face delivered the sermon.

*You believe we asked for sacrifice,* the voices had said, and Mirabel had stirred but not woken, because the house had made sure she wouldn't, because the message was not for her. Not yet. *You believe we bound the hungry thing below and fed it children out of cruelty or cowardice. Read the ledger again, keeper. Read the margins.*

*I read them,* Wren had tried to say, and couldn't, because his mouth belonged to them now.

*A willing heart, freely given, renewed each sixty years. That is the binding. But willingness cannot be commanded. Willingness must be grown.* The chorus had softened then, and underneath the old voices Wren heard something worse: satisfaction. *We tried taking them. The ones we took fought the bell. Their love curdled in the basin and the thing below grew restless, and restless is how valleys die. So we learned. We stopped taking visitors.*

*We started raising them.*

The word had gone through Wren like a nail through wet paper.

*Every third generation, a Hartwell child comes to this house — not dragged, keeper. Invited. Welcomed. Given a friend. Given someone who loves her perfectly, who knows her better than she knows herself, who will spend years teaching her that this place is safe, that this place is home, that there is nowhere else she'd rather be. And when the new moon comes, she walks to the tower on her own feet, holding the hand of the only creature she has ever trusted completely, and she gives herself gladly, because we have spent her whole childhood making her glad.*

*That is what you are for.*

Wren had felt Mirabel's hand in his — the ghost-memory of it, small and sticky with jam, gripping his fingers on the stairs, in the garden, in the dark of her bedroom when the shadows got too tall. Every moment of it, every one, re-read in the founders' light. Not companionship. Cultivation. He was not her friend who had failed to save her.

He was the trap's soft lining.

*She must love the house more than she fears the bell,* the chorus had said, and the youngest voice in it — a woman's, almost gentle — had added: *and you, keeper, are how she learned to love anything at all.*

Then they had left him. Just — left, the way one leaves a tool on a bench, and Wren had sat in the rocking chair watching Mirabel sleep, and the sickness had arrived all at once, doubling him over, and he had crawled away from her on hands and knees so she wouldn't wake and see him like this, and now he was here, on the nursery floor, retching up light.

* * *

When it passed — when the convulsions finally stilled into a low, continuous tremble — Wren lay on his back among the toy blocks and stared at the ceiling, where the damp stains had resolved into unmistakable shapes: five-fingered prints, dozens of them, pressing down through the plaster as if the house were resting its palms on the room from above.

"You knew," he said aloud. His voice was his own again, mostly. "Some part of you knew. When she made me."

Because that was the thing he couldn't stop turning over, the thing worse than everything the founders had said. Mirabel had imagined him. A lonely girl, nine years old, newly arrived at a house that smelled of dust and old rules, had needed a friend so badly that she'd pulled one out of the air — out of *herself* — and the house had caught that need mid-formation and poured it into a mold the founders had left waiting. He was hers. Her loneliness had built him, her love animated him, every good thing in him came from her.

But the mold was theirs.

He was a key cut by a child and fitted to a lock designed by monsters, and neither of them had known until tonight.

"Is that why she found me unchanged?" he asked the ceiling. "Twenty years, and I'm exactly as she left me, because you kept me? Because you *stored* me?" His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated that he'd copied cracking voices from somewhere too — from her, from the afternoon she'd cried over a broken bird's nest, everything in him sourced, everything in him secondhand, a collage wearing a boy's shape. "Did she ever have a single feeling about this house that wasn't planted?"

The ceiling said nothing. The house rarely answered direct questions; it preferred weather, rearrangement, the slow theatrical menace of growing rooms. But Wren felt something shift in the walls — not denial. Amusement. A long, patient amusement, like an old man smiling at a dog barking at the moon.

Rage arrived then, and it was so large and so new that Wren didn't have a source for it, no borrowed memory, no copied gesture. This was the first thing that had ever been entirely his own.

He got up. He picked up a wooden block — one of the toys the house had materialized, painted with the alphabet in Mirabel's childhood colors — and he threw it at the wall as hard as his borrowed arm allowed. It bounced off without a sound. The house absorbed impact the way it absorbed everything: politely, totally.

"You don't get her," Wren said. "Do you hear me? You've had sixty years to arrange your little garden and grow your little crop, and she walked into your rows like she was supposed to, fine, fine — but she *left*. Once. At nine years old, she looked at all of this, at *me*, and she chose the world outside, and you've spent two decades pretending that didn't happen." He was crying now, or doing the thing he did instead, light leaking from his eyes in bright threads. "She beat you once. A nine-year-old beat you. Because I helped her. *I* helped her, and you want to stand there in your walls and tell me my whole existence was the mechanism of her capture?"

The nursery's temperature dropped. The birds on the wallpaper turned their heads in unison, all of them, facing him.

And the pressure came back.

Not fully — not the whole chorus — but enough. Enough to stiffen his tongue, enough to put the founders' gravel under his words as he said, in a voice half his own:

*Careful, keeper. You are house-stuff. Everything you are, we can recall.*

"I know," Wren said, and fought the stiffness, syllable by syllable. "I've always known. You made sure I knew. That was the other half of the design, wasn't it? Not just that I'd deliver her. That I'd *know* I could be taken apart. That obedience would feel like survival." He stepped forward, into the center of the room, into the ring of watching wallpaper. "But here's what your ledger doesn't say, and what your margins don't say, and what none of your careful, patient, monstrous arithmetic accounts for."

The house waited. It was very good at waiting.

"She taught me things you didn't build." His voice steadied. "She taught me that friends keep promises even when it costs. She taught me that you don't get to decide what someone else's love is *for*. She gave me twenty years of her missing me — did you account for that? Every year she grew older and still carried me inside her head like a lit window? Your ritual needs a willing heart. Hers is willing, yes. Willing to love. Willing to stay. Willing, maybe, in the end, to walk into that tower." He smiled, and it was not a child's smile, and it was not copied from anyone. "But willing hearts can will *against* you. That's the flaw in your design, founders. You grew a heart in this house, and hearts are not loyal to their gardeners."

The pressure receded. The hands on the ceiling faded slowly, like footprints in snow. And in the silence afterward, Wren became aware of two things at once.

The first was the iron. Downstairs, in the kitchen drawer where the house kept its few honest objects, lay a length of iron rebar he had pried from the collapsed conservatory three nights ago — the one material in the entire estate the house could not reshape, could not speak through, could not unmake. He had sharpened it against the stone step of the back porch without letting himself think about what for. Now he knew. Or was beginning to know. A key or a weapon, he'd told himself. Perhaps both. Perhaps the distinction didn't matter anymore.

The second was Mirabel, standing in the nursery doorway.

She was pale, wrapped in the quilt, her hair pressed flat on one side from sleep, and her eyes were red-rimmed in a way that meant she had been awake longer than he'd thought. Long enough. How much, he couldn't tell — the house controlled what she heard as easily as it controlled what he said — but her expression was not the expression of someone who had slept peacefully through a sermon.

"How much did you hear?" he asked quietly.

Her gaze went to the ceiling, to the fading handprints, then back to him. "Enough," she said. "Enough to know that when it spoke through you—" Her voice caught. "Wren, it used your mouth. It stood in your face and explained what you're for, and you couldn't stop it, and you're standing there apologizing to *me*—"

"It's true, though." He said it fast, before the house could muffle him, before she could soften it for him. "What they said. I was made to bring you here. The friendship was real — Mirabel, the friendship was the truest thing I have — but it was *designed*. They grew me like a crop and harvested your trust, and I didn't know, and not knowing doesn't clean it. You deserve to be angry at me."

"I am angry," she said, coming into the room, and there was something in her face that stopped his breath — grief and fury braided together, but aimed past him, through him, at the walls themselves. "Not at you. At them. At this *place*." She put her hand flat against the wallpaper, against the little turned birds. "You want to know what I heard, Wren? I heard them brag. Sixty years of patience, generations of gardening, and they're so proud of themselves they couldn't wait until the new moon to gloat." Her hand curled into a fist against the wall. "Arrogance is a crack, Wren. My grandmother found one. I intend to find theirs."

Above them, faint and far, through six floors of timber and stone, the cracked bronze bell hummed — one low, warning note, like a throat clearing before a sentence the whole valley would have to hear.

The new moon was tomorrow night.

Wren looked at Mirabel, and Mirabel looked at Wren, and between them, silent and satisfied, the house began lighting candles.
