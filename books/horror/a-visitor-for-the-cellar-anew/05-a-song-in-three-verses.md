## 5. A Song in Three Verses

Ruth Okonkwo had been waiting for someone to ask.

Not the county men, who came with clipboards and left with signatures. Not the demolition crew, who came with hard hats and left with dirt under their nails and a look they couldn't name. Not even the young surveyor, not at first—though she'd watched him from her kitchen window, crossing the Hollowmere lot with his tape measure and his little notebook, walking the perimeter twice, three times, like a man circling a wound.

He'd come to her door on a Tuesday, because the records office was closed Mondays and he'd spent Monday standing in the road looking at the house the way you look at a person you're not sure is breathing. She'd made tea without being asked. She'd set out the good cups, the ones with the hairline cracks, because some conversations deserved vessels that had already survived something.

"You've been measuring it," she said.

"Professionally," Elodie said. "Yes."

"And it's been measuring back."

He didn't answer, which was an answer. Ruth nodded slowly, the way she did when the doctor told her things about her own heart that she'd already felt. She looked out the window at the house, gray against the gray sky, its windows dark in that particular way—not empty but *withheld*, like eyes closed by someone who might open them again.

"Sit down, child. This will take as long as it takes."

* * *

"My mother sang to me every night," Ruth began. "Lullabies, hymns, whatever the radio gave us. But there was one song she never finished. She'd sing two verses and then stop, mid-phrase sometimes, like she'd walked to the edge of a stair in the dark. I used to beg her for the rest. I thought it was a game. I thought she was teasing."

She turned her cup in her hands. The crack in the porcelain caught the light.

"It wasn't a game. It was a rule. The most important rule this town ever kept, and we kept it so well we forgot we were keeping anything at all."

"The Aldous family," Elodie said quietly.

Ruth looked at him properly then. He had the stillness of a man who has already read the file, who has already done the arithmetic, and is only waiting for the human part—the part that never makes it into records.

"You know the names."

"I know they vanished in 1911. I know the testimony from the neighbors is identical. Word for word, six different households." He paused. "That doesn't happen."

"No," Ruth said. "It doesn't. Unless everyone agreed beforehand on what they were allowed to remember."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, a crow landed on her fence and did not make a sound, which crows do not do, and neither of them remarked on it.

"Tell me about the song," Elodie said.

* * *

"They called it a rounding song," Ruth said. "You know what a round is? One voice starts, then another comes in on the same line, staggered, until the melody is braided out of separate throats and you can't tell where one singer ends and another begins. *Row, row, row your boat.* That kind of thing. Children learn it because it teaches you to listen—to hold your own line while the others carry theirs."

Her mouth went dry around the word *children*. She took a sip of tea.

"In 1911, the Aldouses were the only family on that road who wouldn't sell. The mill wanted the land. The town council wanted the mill. And the Aldouses—Emmett and Constance, and the two girls, Ada and little Miriam—they wanted their house, which sat exactly where the mill's foundation needed to go. So the town did what towns do when they can't buy a thing: they decided the thing was shameful instead."

"Shameful how?"

"Slowly. Carefully. A rumor here, a sermon there. The father was strange. The mother kept to herself. The girls didn't come to school dances. By spring, no one would trade with them. By summer, the church doors were locked to them—unofficially, of course, nothing written down, nothing anyone could be held to. And then someone—no one ever said who, which means everyone knew who—wrote a song about them."

Ruth's voice had gone flat, the way it goes when feeling would otherwise break something.

"A rounding song. That was the cruelty of it. Not a jeer, not shouted once and done—a *round*, meant to be sung in groups, passed mouth to mouth, taught to children as a skipping rhyme. Two verses. The first one mocked Emmett's stubbornness. The second one mourned Constance's pride, all false pity, sweet as syrup. And the whole town learned it. Shopkeepers hummed it when the Aldouses walked past. Children sang it in the schoolyard until Ada stopped coming to school at all. It followed that family everywhere, braided voices, everywhere they turned, until home was the only place the song couldn't reach."

Elodie had gone very still. "And the third verse?"

"Ah." Ruth closed her eyes. "The third verse."

* * *

"Here is what my mother told me, the one time, the only time, when I was seventeen and foolish enough to ask directly. She grabbed my wrist hard enough to leave marks and she said: *the third verse was never sung, and it must never be sung, and if you ever hear it starting inside you, you bite it back until your mouth bleeds.*"

"She knew the words?"

"Everyone knew the words. That was the horror of it. The third verse wasn't secret—it was *suppressed*. There's a difference, child. A secret is hidden; a suppression is a whole town agreeing, generation after generation, not to say a thing they all remember. The third verse was the invitation. The first two verses drove the Aldouses into their cellar. The third verse was written to call them out—or to call something out on their behalf. No one ever agreed what it summoned. Only that completing the song opened a door, and the door had to stay shut."

"But the Aldouses vanished before any of that mattered—"

"Did they?" Ruth let the question sit between them like a stone dropped in still water. "What does the record say? Vanished. Gone in the night. And the singing stopped, all at once, the same week. My grandmother said the whole town woke up one morning with sore throats and no memory of why. Six households gave identical testimony because they'd all sung themselves into the same forgetting. That's what a round does when it finishes, child. It erases the singers. They gave identical statements because they had nothing left *but* the statement."

Elodie's hand had gone to his notebook, though he hadn't written anything. Ruth watched him decide not to write, and respected him for it.

"So the town sealed the door," he said slowly. "After they drove the family into the cellar. After—" He stopped.

"After they mortared it shut from the outside while the family was still behind it?" Ruth finished for him, because someone had to say it, and she was old enough now that saying it could not kill her. "Yes. That's the shape of it. The song drove them down. The mortar kept them there. And the unfinished song keeps the door *sealed*—because a door that opens needs a key, and the key is the last verse, sung complete, by a living voice, at the threshold."

"How do you know that last part?"

"Because my mother knew it, and her mother knew it, and the knowing has come down to me like a debt." Ruth's voice cracked, just slightly, on the word *debt*. "We inherited the guilt along with the silence. Every woman in my line was taught the first two verses so she'd recognize the danger, and taught to stop before the third so she'd never cause it. We were the lock's maintenance schedule. That's what we were. Generations of women keeping a song half-sung so a buried family stays buried."

"That's monstrous."

"Yes," Ruth said simply. "It is. We told ourselves it was mercy—better the door shut than what's behind it loose. But mercy would have been digging them out in 1912 and burying them proper, with names and stones and apologies. What we did was keep them *conveniently* dead. Unfinished. Unmourned. Because mourning requires admitting what we sang them into."

* * *

The light through the kitchen window had gone the color of weak tea. Ruth realized she was tired in the way that meant more than tired—her heart doing its arrhythmic stutter, the one Dr. Prasad had warned her about, the one she'd decided not to worry over.

"There's something else," she said. "Something I've never told anyone, because telling it makes it real."

Elodie waited.

"The song isn't just remembered. It's *kept*. The house keeps it. My grandmother swore that Hollowmere holds everything that happened inside its lines—the walls took the singing the way wax takes a seal. And the house has been waiting ever since. Waiting for someone honest enough to measure it truly, because a true measurement is a kind of attention, and attention is what it needs to offer the memory up." She fixed him with a look. "You've heard the humming. Through the floorboards. Don't lie to me, child—I can see it in your face."

"I hear it," Elodie admitted. "Louder every day. And lately—" He hesitated, and Ruth saw the fear in him, real fear, the kind that comes before a confession. "Lately I catch myself humming it back. Verses I've never been taught. My own voice, harmonizing, when I haven't decided to."

Ruth's blood went cold, and beneath the cold, something older and sadder stirred—the recognition of a pattern completing itself.

"Then it's chosen you," she said. "And you have very little time. Because the house won't just offer you the song, child. It will *sing it through you*. It will use your throat to finish what our grandmothers' throats refused—and it won't care whether you want to belong to the chorus or not."

"Then tell me the third verse," Elodie said. "Now. Before it takes it from somewhere else."

"I don't have the words." Ruth raised a trembling hand before he could speak. "No one living has the words. My mother refused to pass them on—that was her gift to me, her disobedience, the one rebellion of her life. She gave me only the *rhythm*. The shape of the verse without its flesh. Dum-da-da, dum-da-da-dum, a rest where the worst word should fall. I've carried that rhythm for seventy years like a stone in my shoe."

"Then who has the words?"

Ruth looked past him, out the window, at Hollowmere House standing in the failing light. And for the first time since she'd begun, she smiled—a small, terrible smile, the smile of a woman setting down a weight she'd carried too long.

"Nobody, child. That's the point. The words died with the singers—which means the only place they exist now is in the memory the house itself is holding. You can't get the verse from me. You'll have to get it from *them*."

Outside, faint and unmistakable, rising through the ground like water through chalk, the humming began—and this time, Ruth heard it too, and this time, it was harmonizing with the rhythm she had carried all her life.
