## 7. Join the Chorus

The song woke him at 3:14 a.m., which was the first time he noticed it had learned to keep time.

Elodie lay in the surveyor's cot he'd dragged into the front parlor—no hotel in Hollowmere would take him anymore, not after the excavator story got around—and listened to his own voice humming under the floorboards. Not coming from below. Coming from *him*. His throat moved. His lips parted on a note he hadn't chosen, a low third above a melody that rose through the joists like water finding its level.

He clamped his mouth shut and the note kept going anyway, muffled behind his teeth, vibrating in the bones of his skull.

*That's not how singing works,* he told himself, with the flat professional calm he'd been using as a life raft for nine days now. *Sound requires an open resonating chamber.* He was aware of how absurd it was, litigating acoustics with a haunted house. But procedure was all he had left. Procedure was the wall between him and the thing in the cellar.

The song stopped at 3:22. Exactly eight minutes. He knew because he'd timed it every night this week, and it was always eight minutes, and the precision frightened him more than the music did. Ghosts were supposed to be sloppy. This was *scheduled*.

He got up, turned on every light in the parlor—the house tolerated the lights, mostly—and opened his field notebook to the running log.

Day 9. Interior vocalization, involuntary, duration 8:00. Subject melody consistent with fragment transcribed 10/14 (see p. 61). No sore throat. No memory of initiating. Recommend—

Recommend what? He stared at the page. His pen hovered. Every recommendation he'd ever written had ended in an action someone else could take: re-survey, re-stake, flag for counsel. There was no form for *my own mouth is being used as an instrument by a murdered family's lullaby.*

In the end he wrote: *Recommend completing appraisal immediately.*

It was a lie and they both knew it. The appraisal couldn't be completed. He'd proven that mathematically on day two, when the hallway gained four feet between passes, and every measurement since had only deepened the discrepancy. The house wasn't badly built. The house was *arguing*. And lately it had stopped arguing about square footage and started arguing about him.

* * *

Marcus Hale came at dawn, gray-faced, still wearing yesterday's clothes.

"They let me go home," he said, standing in the doorway without crossing the threshold, which was new. Marcus had crossed every threshold on this site like he owned it. "I got home. I stood in my kitchen. And then I was here." He held up his truck keys. "I don't remember driving."

"Sit down."

"I don't want to sit down. Sitting down is—" He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, harmonized, a second voice riding underneath it like a dub track. Elodie felt the hair on his arms lift. "You hear that too, right? Tell me you hear that."

"I hear it."

"It's better if you sing," Marcus said. Just like that. No preamble, no shame. He said it the way a man says *the pills help*, the way an addict says anything—flat, certain, rehearsed by repetition until it sounded like reason. "I sang a little last night. In the kitchen. Just the first verse, the one everybody knows. And it went quiet, Elodie. For the first time in a week, my head went *quiet*." His eyes were wet and shining and absolutely calm. "It only wants the rest of it. It only wants us to finish."

"The rest of it isn't ours to finish." Elodie kept his voice level, kept his hands busy—straightening the notebook, squaring the lamp. Motion against motion. "Ruth Okonkwo says the third verse was never written down. Deliberately. The town lost the words on purpose because whoever sings them opens that door."

"Then maybe it should open." Marcus stepped inside finally, and the door swung shut behind him without wind. "You've seen what's down there. A family walled up alive while the whole town stood in their yard and *sang at them*. That's not a haunting, that's a crime scene. You're a surveyor. Don't you want to file the report?"

"Of course I do."

"Then sing them out." Marcus grabbed his arm, and his grip was cold, cold as cellar stone. "One verse. Three lines, maybe four. Ruth's mother kept the rhythm—you said so yourself. We figure out the words together, we sing it, we're done. You get your honest number. I get my *head back*. Everybody goes home."

"And if the words are wrong?" Elodie pulled free. "If we guess, and the door opens onto something that isn't justice? Ruth said the song doesn't free anyone. It *calls*. Two verses shamed a family into a cellar. What does the whole song do?"

Marcus didn't answer. And in the silence, the house answered for him.

It started low, in the walls, in the floor, in the fill dirt under the slab—the first verse, the one every old-timer in Hollowmere could hum but nobody would explain. Then the second verse, sweeter, the one with the little rising turn at the end that made your chest ache for no reason you could name. And then, where the third verse should have been, there was a hole. A silence shaped exactly like a verse. A negative space with edges, waiting, and Elodie felt his mind lean toward it the way water leans toward a drain.

His mouth opened.

"No," he said aloud, and bit down on the note, and tasted copper.

But for one full second—one long, honeyed second—he had felt it. What it would be like. The harmony taking him in. He'd spent his whole life the man outside the group photo, the appraiser who measured other people's lives and went home to a rented apartment with no photographs on the wall. Parents gone, no siblings, a name on invoices and nowhere on any birthday card. And here was a house offering him the one thing he'd never had: a part in the chorus. A place where his voice wasn't just wanted but *required*, where the song literally could not end without him.

That was the trap. He understood it now, with terrible clarity. The Aldouses' neighbors hadn't sung out of cruelty. They'd sung because it felt like belonging, and the house had learned the lesson well. It didn't threaten you. It *recruited* you.

"Feel it?" Marcus whispered. He was crying now, smiling, swaying slightly on his feet. "God, it's beautiful. Why are we fighting this?"

"Because beautiful isn't the same as right." Elodie put the parlor table between them. "Because the last time this town harmonized, a family died under their own floor. Because I'm paid to tell the truth about buildings, and the truth about this one is that it's a grave, and graves don't get opened by whatever feels good."

Marcus's smile flickered. Underneath it, briefly, was the foreman Elodie had met two weeks ago—competent, skeptical, allergic to nonsense. "Elodie," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle, half his own and half the chorus. "I can't hold it off much longer. When it takes me, I'm going to walk down those cellar stairs, and I'm going to sing, and I won't know the words, and I'll make some up, and I don't know what happens then. Neither do you. So whatever you're going to do—" he jabbed a finger at the notebook, at the lights, at the whole stubborn apparatus of Elodie's procedure—"*do it today.*"

Then he turned and walked out, and the door closed gently behind him, almost tenderly, the way you'd close a door on someone sleeping.

* * *

Elodie worked.

That was the whole strategy, and he knew how thin it was, and he did it anyway. He measured. Room by room, door by door, tape hissing out and snapping back, numbers going into the book even though the numbers contradicted each other, even though the east bedroom was now furnished with furniture from a house he'd lived in at age six—a child's bed, a dresser with a wobbly leg, the exact water stain on the ceiling above the window. On the dresser sat a photograph frame, face-down. He did not turn it over. He had turned one over yesterday and seen his grandmother's face on a woman who died in 1911, and he was not doing that again.

The song ran under everything now, constant, like tinnitus with intentions. He caught himself tapping the rhythm on the tape measure housing. He caught himself breathing in the verse-rests. Once, walking the cellar stairs with his instruments, he realized he'd descended twelve steps on a staircase that had eleven yesterday, and that he'd taken them *in time*.

At noon he called Ruth Okonkwo's number from the porch, because the phone didn't work inside anymore—the house had opinions about outside voices.

Her granddaughter answered. "Grandma's asking for you," the girl said, before Elodie could speak. "She's been asking since sunrise. She says—" a pause, a hand over the receiver, the murmur of an old woman's voice, urgent and thin— "she says the house has started singing *her* the third verse in her sleep. Rhythm only, no words. She says it's testing whether she'll give it away. She says come *now*, Mr. Villalobos, before she gets confused about whose side keeping quiet is on."

Elodie looked back at the house. Every window on the second floor was dark except one, and in that window something stood, patient, the silhouette of a listener.

"I'm on my way," he said.

Behind him, faint and sweet and horribly familiar, his own voice began—without permission, without anger, almost kindly—to hum the space where the ending should go.
