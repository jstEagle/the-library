## 9. The Last Verse

They came at dawn without warning.

Elodie heard the trucks before he saw them—diesel growl rolling up the county road while the sky was still the color of wet ash. He had slept three hours in his car outside the gate, Ruth's rhythm still turning over in him like a stone in a tumbler, and when he opened his eyes the excavator was already being winched off its trailer, and Marcus Hale was standing at the fence line arguing with a man in a hard hat who kept pointing at a clipboard.

He got out of the car. The cold went through him like a surveyor's plumb line.

"Marcus."

Marcus turned. In the gray light his face looked wrong—not frightened anymore, but smoothed, as if something had sanded down everything behind his eyes except obedience. "They moved it up," he said. "County order. Something about the ground being unstable." He said it flatly, reciting. "Ground being unstable. That's what it says."

"Marcus, look at me. Did you sign that?"

"I don't remember signing it." He smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "It doesn't matter. It's happening, Elodie. You should go home. There's nothing left to measure."

"There's everything left to measure," Elodie said. "That's the whole point of me."

The foreman with the clipboard called out that they were starting on the east wing, and the excavator's engine caught with a cough and a roar, and Elodie felt it in his teeth—in his *teeth*, like the house felt it too, like the whole structure flinched. Above them, in the upper windows, every curtain moved at once though there was no wind.

Ruth had died on Tuesday. She had died holding his hand and giving him the verse the way you give someone a live coal—quickly, before it can burn through both of you—and her last words had been: *Don't sing it to be kind. Sing it to be true. The house knows the difference.*

He had not understood until this moment, watching the excavator swing its arm toward the east wing where the cellar stairs began, that the house knew the difference because the town never had.

* * *

Getting down into the cellar while men were tearing the building apart above him should have been difficult. It was not. That was the first thing he noticed—the stairwell door stood open for him, the steps swept clean of the plaster dust raining from the ceiling, and when a beam groaned overhead and a section of lath came down, it fell around him in two neat curtains, missing him by inches on either side, like a crowd parting.

The house wanted him here. The house had always wanted him here. Every shifted wall, every multiplied door, every four extra feet of hallway had been an invitation written in the only language the house had left: *come further in, come measure me honestly, come finish what they started wrong.*

The humming rose through the floor now like water through a foundation. Louder than it had ever been. And underneath it—Elodie stopped on the last step—he could hear words. Not quite words. Words worn smooth by a hundred and fourteen years of repetition, syllables rubbed down to their shapes, but there: the rounding song, both verses, sung by throats that no longer existed, in a room that no longer existed, above a door that had never once been answered.

He crossed the cellar floor. The excavation trench gaped where Marcus's crew had cut through the slab weeks ago, and at its bottom stood the stone doorway, sealed from the inside, mortar still bearing the smear-marks of small hands that had pressed it smooth from the other side. Furniture was packed against it—a chair back, the leg of a table, a child's rocking horse with one glass eye, all of it shoved through some impossible seam and mortared over, the family barricading themselves against the singing and then discovering, too late, that the singing was not coming for the door.

The singing had been coming for *them*. To wear them down. To shame them out of their own house, out of their own names, and when they would not go, to bury the shame along with them and call it peace.

Elodie climbed down into the trench. The stone was cold enough to burn. He put his palm flat against it, the way he'd put his palm against a hundred walls in this house, taking its measure.

"I'm a property surveyor," he said aloud. His voice sounded absurdly small. "I appraise what's actually there. That's all I've ever done. So let me tell you what's actually here."

The humming faltered. Just slightly. Listening.

"Aldous," he said. "A whole family. A father who built furniture—I've seen the chair. A mother who kept bees, according to the county record nobody read twice. Three children, because I counted three sets of handprints in the mortar, and I count everything, it's the one thing I'm good for. You were driven into your own cellar by people who sang at you because you were different, or poor, or strange, or simply *there*, and when you wouldn't leave they made leaving permanent. And then they lost the third verse on purpose so no one could ever undo it, and they called the silence justice."

His voice cracked on the last word. He let it.

"I can't give you back a hundred years. I can't give you the town, or the trial you deserved, or even your names in the record—though God knows I tried, I filed it, I wrote it down, I did my job." He swallowed. "But Ruth Okonkwo's mother carried the verse her whole life without ever letting it out of her mouth, and Ruth carried it the same way, and last Tuesday she gave it to me. Not the words. The shape of them. The rhythm her mother refused to sing. And a rhythm is a promise, isn't it? A rhythm is a door waiting for its words."

Above him, the excavator bit into the east wing. The whole cellar shuddered. Plaster snowed down through the trench mouth, settling on his shoulders like ash.

"So here is the appraisal," Elodie said. "Hollowmere House. Contents: one family, unaccounted for since 1911. Value: everything. Assessed honestly, for the first time."

And he began to sing.

* * *

The first verse came up out of the floor to meet him, a hundred dead voices wearing his melody like a borrowed coat, and it was terrible and beautiful and he did not stop. The second verse rose through the stone under his feet, muffled, patient, the sound of people who had been keeping time for a century so that someone, someday, could keep the measure. His own voice found the spaces between theirs and filled them.

Then the third verse.

There were no words. Ruth had been clear about that—her mother had refused the words so completely that they had rotted out of the family memory, leaving only the rhythm, four beats and a rest, four beats and a rest, the shape of a sentence never spoken. So Elodie sang the shape. He sang the empty verse the way you'd sing a held breath, the way you'd sing the pause before forgiveness, and the house—

The house went still.

Not quiet. Still. The difference between a held note and a held breath. The demolition noise above him seemed to happen to a different building. The humming stopped mid-phrase, and in the ringing absence Elodie heard, for the first time, the sound the house had been making underneath its song all along: weeping. Thin and old and patient, seeping up through the mortar like damp.

He sang the empty verse again. And again. And on the fourth repetition, his mouth began, without his deciding it, to fill the rhythm with words—not words he knew, not words he chose, but words arriving the way measurements arrive, honestly, because someone finally asked the right question:

*Lay down the song we sang to shame you.*
*Lay down the stones we laid to keep you.*
*What we buried was not sorrow.*
*What we buried was our neighbor.*
*Rise up measured. Rise up counted.*
*Rise up named. We name you now.*

The mortar did not crack. It *exhaled*—a long gray sigh of dust sliding down the stone face like a curtain drawn back after a century—and the furniture packed against the door sank into the floor as gently as things sinking into deep water, released at last from a duty they'd never volunteered for. The rocking horse went last, its one glass eye catching the light from the trench mouth, winking out.

The door swung inward.

Not bones. He had braced for bones, had told himself he must not run from bones, and instead: lamplight. Warmth. A cellar that was also a kitchen that was also a parlor that was also every room the house had ever remembered being, furnished in 1911, lit by oil lamps that burned without consuming anything, and standing in that light, five people.

Whole. Waiting. A tall man with sawdust still on his sleeves. A woman with a beekeeping veil pushed up off her face like a crown pushed back. Three children, the smallest holding the rocking horse, which was whole again, both eyes bright.

They looked at Elodie the way you look at a knock you'd stopped expecting.

"You finished it," the woman said. Her voice was hoarse with disuse and wonder. "After all this time. Someone finally finished it."

"The verse was kept," Elodie said. His voice barely worked. "By a woman who never sang it. And her daughter. They kept it safe for you."

The father stepped forward. Up close, Elodie could see that they were not ghosts exactly—they were the house's memory of them, rendered in the same stubborn material as the shifting walls and the multiplying doors, a family preserved the way amber preserves, perfectly and against all justice. "We heard the singing," the father said. "Every day. For years, and then decades, and then we stopped counting. We heard the town decide we were gone. And we waited, because the song wasn't finished, and an unfinished song is a locked door." He looked back at his family, and then at Elodie, and his eyes were kind and terrible. "You unlocked it. So we have to ask, because the house will ask it through us whether we want it to or not."

"Stay," the mother said simply. "Stay and keep singing. The house needs a voice now. It's been ours, and we're tired, and you sing beautifully, and you'd never be lonely, and—" she gestured at the lamplight, the warmth, the impossible parlor—"you'd belong somewhere. Truly belong. Isn't that what everyone wants?"

And God help him, it was. It was the exact thing. Elodie felt the pull of it like gravity—the warm rooms rearranging themselves subtly around him, already learning his childhood home's proportions, already offering him a chair he recognized from a house he hadn't thought about in twenty years. He could feel how good it would be. How easy. The chorus, forever, and no more county records, no more invoices, no more being a man who counted things alone in empty buildings.

The smallest child tugged his sleeve and held up the rocking horse. An offering.

"No," Elodie said, and the word cost him more than the singing had. He knelt to be level with the child. "No, sweetheart. Because if I stay, I stop being a witness, and a witness is the only thing you've got left. The song's finished. That means the door works both ways now." He stood, and addressed them all, his voice steadying as it went. "You're free. Not into the house—out of it. Out into whatever comes after houses. The appraisal is complete. The value is paid. Go."

The father studied him for a long moment. Then he took off his cap—sawdust drifting from it like snow that fell upward—and held out his hand.

"Villalobos," he said. "We'll remember your name, even if nothing else does."

"Wait," Elodie said. "How do you know my—"

But the lamps were going out, one by one, gently, like eyes closing, and the family was stepping backward into the dark between the flames, and the last thing he saw was the smallest child waving with the whole arm, the way children wave when they believe they'll see you tomorrow.

Then the cellar was a cellar. Cold stone, open doorway, and beyond it—not darkness, not lamplight, but a clean and ordinary emptiness, the kind of space that exists after something has finally, truly left.

Overhead, the excavator froze mid-swing. Somewhere above, a man named Marcus Hale dropped his coffee and started running for the stairs, shouting Elodie's name, because the whole house had just shuddered like a lung exhaling a century, and every window in Hollowmere had blown outward at once, softly, all together, like the building opening its hands.

Elodie climbed out of the trench and walked up the stairs into the falling dust, lighter than he had ever been, and did not yet know why the foreman's face, when it found him in the rubble-light, was the face of a man looking at a stranger.
