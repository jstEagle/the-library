## 1. The Wrong Body

Three years, and Rafael Thornbury still could not remember the mortician's name.

He remembered everything else about that day with a fidelity that felt less like memory and more like punishment. The rain that hadn't fallen—the sky had been the color of old pewter, dry and pressing down like a lid. The way the gravel crunched under forty pairs of shoes in the driveway of Vasseur & Sons Funeral Home, though the Vasseurs had been dead or dispersed for a generation and the sign was just a name on a fading board. The casket itself: pale birch, because Rosalind had once said she liked pale wood, and their mother had clung to that detail like a rope thrown into deep water.

And he remembered Mr.—Halloran? Hollis?—the mortician, a small man with damp hands and eyes that kept moving to the door as if he expected someone to come collect him. Rafael had approached him after the service, when the mourners were drifting toward their cars and Maren stood at the graveside long after everyone else had gone, her black gloves gripping the railing.

Rafael had only wanted to thank him. That was all. Thank you for your care with her. He'd rehearsed it in his head, the simple decency of it, the closure of it.

The mortician had taken his hand in both of his own—cold, strangely soft, like holding something recently drowned—and leaned in close enough that Rafael could smell the coffee on his breath and beneath it something faintly chemical, formalin-sweet.

"You should know," the man had whispered, so quietly that Rafael could pretend afterward he'd misheard, "that isn't her."

Then he had stepped back, patted Rafael's arm twice—a gesture so briskly professional it might have been reassurance—and walked away through the doors of his own establishment, and Rafael never saw him again. When he called the number listed three days later, it rang until it didn't. When he drove out to the funeral home the following week, the building stood empty, windows dark, a notice taped inside the glass about a lease available for commercial use. No record of any Halloran or Hollis. No record, when he pressed the county office, of a burial permit issued for Rosalind Thornbury at all.

Only the grave. Only the stone. ROSALIND MAREN THORNBURY, 1994–2021, BELOVED DAUGHTER AND SISTER. And beneath it, presumably—he had never asked to see, had never been able to make himself ask—a stranger.

* * *

Presently, the kitchen smelled of burnt toast and lavender, which meant Maren had tried to make breakfast again.

Rafael found her standing at the counter in her nightgown, buttering a slice of bread that was more charcoal than wheat, humming something tuneless and old. Her hands were steady—that was the strange mercy of her condition; her body remembered its routines even as her mind let go of them one room at a time.

"Mom." He took the knife from her gently. "Let me."

"I'm making breakfast for the children," she said.

The word went through him the way cold water goes through cloth. *Children.* Plural. She did that sometimes now, more often each month—slipped backward across some interior threshold into years when there had been four of them at this table, before the house had thinned down to its present rations: herself, Tomas, Rafael. Before the cellar door got its padlock. Before—

"Rafael's at school," she continued, squinting at him with mild suspicion, "and Rosalind is still sleeping. I'll take hers up after."

"Rosalind's gone, Mom." He said it the way you'd say *it's raining*, flat, factual, already braced. They had this exchange perhaps four times a week. Dr. Imre said not to correct her harshly, that the past was where she lived now and arguing with a woman's address only frightened her. But Rafael couldn't stop correcting her. Correcting her was the last duty he had left to the dead—or to whatever it was they had buried under a stranger's name.

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—not confusion exactly. Recognition, maybe, arriving late, like a train pulling into a station after everyone waiting had given up and gone home.

"Gone," she repeated. Her hand drifted to her collarbone, worrying the fabric of her nightgown. "Yes. Gone." Then, softer, almost to herself, in a voice that made the hair on Rafael's arms stand up: "It was only supposed to be one of them."

"What?"

But she had already turned back to the toast, humming, and the moment closed over like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

Tomas didn't come down for breakfast. Tomas rarely came down for breakfast anymore.

Fourteen years old, and he had folded himself into silence the way other boys folded themselves into sports or music or cruelty. He ate when Rafael put food in front of him. He answered direct questions in single syllables. At school—when Rafael could get him to go—he sat in the back row and drew, endlessly, small dense spirals in the margins of every notebook, page after page, until the teachers called home with concern and Rafael looked at the spirals and felt something in his chest turn over, because he drew spirals too. Had always drawn them. Their father used to say it was a Thornbury thing, a nervous habit, like biting nails.

Their father had also walked out into the orchard eleven years ago and never walked back, but that wasn't the kind of inheritance anyone discussed.

Rafael carried a tray up to Tomas's room at nine. The boy was awake—of course he was awake; Rafael suspected he barely slept at all—sitting cross-legged on his bed with his headphones on, though when Rafael touched his shoulder he could feel no music vibrating through the band. Just the plastic, warm against his palm. An empty shell held against his ears, the way another child might hold a stuffed animal.

"I made eggs," Rafael said. "Scrambled. Like—" He stopped himself before saying *like Rosalind used to make them*. He always stopped himself. In this house, her name had become a kind of load-bearing wall; you learned not to lean on it.

Tomas pulled off the dead headphones. His eyes went to the tray, then to the window, then—briefly, involuntarily—to the floor. To the boards beneath the rug, and beyond them, somewhere below, the padlocked door.

"You hear it too," Tomas said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite a sentence addressed to Rafael; it came out flat and observational, like a weather report. "Last night. Under my room. Not scratching. Walking."

"The pipes settle," Rafael said. "Old house. The water table shifts in autumn and the footings—"

"It was footsteps, Rafe." Tomas's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. There was no fear in it, no plea for comfort. Just a fourteen-year-old stating facts to an older brother who he had already, quietly, given up on convincing. "Thirteen of them. Then they stopped right over my bed, and then there was a sound like someone counting on their fingers."

Rafael set the tray down. His hands were perfectly steady. He noticed that about himself sometimes, with a distant clinical interest—that in the moments when the wrongness pressed closest, his body went calm and competent, as if some part of him had decided long ago that hysteria was a luxury the household could not afford.

"Tomas. Listen to me." He crouched so they were eye level. "There is nothing down there. I've checked the cellar a hundred times. The door's locked, the walls are stone, and the only things living under this house are spiders and bad plumbing. What you heard was the house cooling down. Houses talk at night. This one especially."

Tomas looked at him for a long time. And what was in his face wasn't disbelief—it was pity. Pity, from the younger brother toward the older, and Rafael understood suddenly that Tomas had watched him perform this same reasonable speech so many times that the performance itself had become part of the boy's evidence. *He has to explain it away,* the look said. *That's how scared he is.*

"Okay," Tomas said finally, and picked up his fork.

Rafael left the tray and stood a moment in the doorway. On the desk, among the spiral-filled notebooks, lay a pair of foam earplugs, still in their orange packaging, and beside them a cheap digital alarm clock turned to face the wall.

* * *

That evening he cooked dinner for three and set the table for three and did not look at the fourth chair, the one pushed against the wall by the window, which had been empty for three years and which none of them ever moved, though Maren dusted it daily with a devotion she no longer extended to much else.

Maren came down at six, dressed, hair combed, lucid in the way the evenings sometimes granted her—a brief return, like light coming back to a room after a cloud passes. She ate half her portion, asked intelligent questions about the roof estimate Rafael had gotten, and then, over the last of her tea, said:

"You were at the funeral today."

Rafael's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"No, Mom. That was three years ago."

"Three years." She nodded slowly, accepting this, and then her eyes came up and fixed on him with terrible clarity, the dementia receding like a tide going out and leaving everything exposed. "Three years, and you never told me what the man said to you. At the church. I saw his mouth move against your ear. I've been waiting three years for you to tell me, and you won't, and I know why."

Rafael's heart was doing something arrhythmic. "Mom—"

"He whispered it to you because it was true," she said, "and truth needs whispering in this family. We've always known that. My mother knew it. Her mother before her." She set down her cup with a small precise click, and her hands, for the first time all day, were trembling. "You want to ask me something, Rafael. You've wanted to ask me for three years. So ask me while I can still answer. Ask me tonight, because tomorrow morning I'll be making breakfast for children who aren't here, and you'll have missed your window, and you'll hate yourself a little more than you already do."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, dusk was coming down over the orchard, and somewhere in the walls—or beneath the floor, or behind the padlocked door, or nowhere at all except in the architecture of his own grief—a draft moved through the house, cold and deliberate, raising the fine hairs on his neck. It smelled, faintly, of well water and cut flowers.

"Mom," Rafael said carefully. "What did Dad mean when he said the house required—"

But Maren's clarity was already draining away; he could see it go, watch the tide come back in over her face, smoothing features, erasing text. She smiled at him, sweet and vacant, and patted his hand.

"You're such a good boy," she said. "So like your sister."

She gathered her cup and saucer, shuffled to the doorway, paused there with her back to him.

"Happy anniversary," she said, to Rafael or to the house or to no one, and climbed the stairs.

Rafael sat alone at the table with three dirty plates and one clean chair, listening to the footsteps overhead—her footsteps, slow and shuffling, twelve stairs up to the landing, he counted them automatically, he always counted them, twelve, twelve, twelve—and then, from somewhere below the kitchen floor, faint and patient and unmistakable, a thirteenth step.

He did not move for a long time.

Then he rose, washed the dishes, locked nothing—for in this house nothing was ever locked except the cellar door, and that lock had never once been tested—and went to his study, where he opened the bottom drawer and took out the small voice recorder he'd bought that afternoon from the pharmacy in town, along with Maren's refills and a pack of Tomas's earplugs he pretended he hadn't noticed his brother needing.

He wasn't sure yet what he intended to do with it. He only knew that he had spent three years explaining away every sound, every draft, every thirteenth step, and that the explanations were running out the way Maren's mind was running out, and that soon—soon—there would be nothing left between him and the raw fact underneath.

He set the recorder on his nightstand, pressed record, and turned off the lamp.

In the dark, the house breathed around him. And from far below—so far below it might have been imagination, might have been plumbing, might have been the earth itself settling over whatever they had buried in a stranger's place—came a sound he had never heard in thirty-one years of sleeping in this house.

Someone, politely, knocking on the underside of the floorboards.
