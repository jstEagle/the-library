## 6. The Voice Asks Back

For three days after the recording, Rafael did not sleep.

He told himself it was insomnia, the ordinary kind, the kind that came with grief and mortgage payments and a mother who asked him twice an hour whether Rosalind had called. He drank coffee until his hands trembled and sat in the kitchen with the lights on and listened to the house breathe around him—the tick of the water heater, the settling groan of joists, the faint arterial whisper of air moving through the floorboards as though the house itself had circulation, as though something below pumped warmth up through its veins.

He did not play the recording again. He didn't need to. It lived behind his eyes now, fully transcribed by memory: *his* voice, fluent and easy in sleep, asking questions he'd never asked awake—and the second voice answering, dry and patient as dust settling, knowing things only Rosalind could have known. The name of the game they invented at nine. The words she whispered into the gap between their beds when the thunder came. *You're the loud one. I'm the quiet one. That's how they'll always tell us apart.*

Except now he wasn't sure which one that made him.

On the third night, at 11:40, he set the recorder on the kitchen table, pressed record, and spoke to the cellar for the first time.

His voice came out smaller than he intended. "I know you're there."

Nothing. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs Maren's door creaked as she turned in bed, and Tomas's silence pressed down through the ceiling like weather.

"I know you're there," he said again, louder, and hated the way his voice cracked on *there*, the way it sounded like a boy calling into a well. "I've heard you. On this." He tapped the recorder. "You talked to me. You answered me. So you can hear me now, and I want—I need—to know what you are."

The draft rose through the floorboards then, sudden and deliberate, cold against his ankles like a hand testing the water. The padlock on the cellar door was two rooms away and he heard it anyway—a single soft clank, metal shifting against metal, unmistakable even through walls, the way you can always hear your own name across a crowded room.

Rafael's mouth went dry. He sat down because standing suddenly seemed like a decision he might not survive.

"Was it you," he said, "who took her? Three years ago. Was it you in the casket?"

The cold deepened. Not moving—*arriving*, layer by layer, the way frost forms on glass, and he understood with animal certainty that the thing below was considering his questions, turning them over the way Maren turned over her pills each morning, counting them out.

Then the recorder clicked.

He hadn't touched it. The red light was burning, and from its small speaker came a sound like breath drawn through cloth, and then the voice—not from the floorboards, not from the cellar, but from the recorder itself, intimate and close as a whisper against his ear:

*"Which one are you?"*

Rafael's whole body went cold past shivering. "What?"

*"Which twin,"* the voice said, patient, almost gentle, the tone of a clerk confirming an account number. *"Are you Rafael? Or are you Rosalind?"*

"That's—" He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, thin and high. "That's insane. You know that's insane. I'm Rafael. I'm the one who's been living here taking care of her—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward Maren, toward everything, "—for three years while you sat under my kitchen."

A pause. The refrigerator hummed. In the pause Rafael heard the house listening.

*"Say it again,"* the voice said.

"I'm Rafael Thornbury. Born March ninth, thirty-one years ago, seven minutes after my sister. I have a scar on my left knee from falling off the Hendricks' fence. I hate beets. My mother's name is Maren and my brother is Tomas and my sister is dead, and I buried her, and I'm the one who's left. That's who I am."

Silence. Then—softly, so softly he almost missed it—the recorder laughed.

It was not a cruel laugh. That was the worst part. It was warm, almost fond, the laugh of someone hearing an old joke retold by a new teller, and it lasted only a moment before the voice said:

*"That's what the other one said too."*

The kitchen tilted. Or Rafael did. He gripped the edge of the table and the wood bit cold into his palms and he stared at the little machine with its burning red eye and felt the floor of his own history go soft beneath him.

"What other one," he whispered. "There is no other one. She's dead. I buried her."

*"Did you,"* the voice said, and it wasn't quite a question. It was the way you'd say *did you now* to a child insisting the moon followed the car. *"Seven minutes. That's what you were told. Do you remember being born, Rafael? Do you remember the seven minutes? Or do you remember being told?"*

"I don't—nobody remembers being born—"

*"No,"* it agreed. *"Nobody does. Everything you are began with a story someone else told you. A name. A birth order. A scar."* Another breath through cloth. *"I have been in this house longer than your stories, Rafael Thornbury. I have watched your mother count out her pills and your brother press plugs into his ears and you sit where you're sitting now, and I will tell you something true, because true things are all I trade in: I do not know which one you are either."*

"You're lying."

*"Am I?"* The voice was unbothered. *"Your sister knew the thunder game. Who taught me the thunder game, Rafael? Think carefully. Who answered my questions last Tuesday night, in a voice I have known since before you could talk? Whose cadence do I hear when you speak in your sleep?"*

"I don't speak in your—"

*"You speak in mine,"* it said. *"Or she speaks in yours. From down here, after forty years, the difference has become academic."*

Rafael stood up. His legs held, barely. "My sister went into the ground three years ago. I saw her. I identified her body. Whatever you are, whatever game this is—"

*"You identified a stranger,"* the voice said mildly. *"Your mortician told you so himself. And still you buried her. Still you grieved her. Still you signed the papers with your own hand."* A pause, delicate as a knife laid down between courses. *"Tell me, Rafael—if you could not recognize your sister's face when it lay in front of you, what makes you so certain you would recognize your own?"*

The recorder clicked off.

Not gradually—it simply stopped, the red light dying, and the cold withdrew from the kitchen like a tide going out, leaving Rafael alone in the humming ordinary dark with his heart slamming and his reflection ghosted faint in the black window over the sink.

He played the tape back four times. It was all there, every word, his own voice cracking and pleading and the dry patient voice dismantling him question by question. On the fifth playback he noticed something he'd missed: at 2:14 in the recording, underneath his own denial—*there is no other one*—there was a second voice speaking simultaneously, low, half-buried, saying the same words a half-second ahead of him. Like an echo that arrived first. Like a man learning his lines from someone reading over his shoulder.

He stopped the tape with a shaking finger.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Then another. Footsteps crossing the hall above him—slow, unhurried, thirteen of them, ending directly over the spot where he sat, though the hallway ended twelve steps in and everyone in the house knew it, everyone had always known it, the way you know your own name.

Rafael looked up at the ceiling.

The footsteps did not come again. But neither did they leave. They simply stopped above him and stayed stopped, a weight of attention pressing down through the plaster, and Rafael understood that the question had not ended. It had only moved floors.

He gathered the recorder, the tapes, the notebook where he'd begun keeping the medication schedule in his careful accountant's hand, and carried them to his room, where he locked the door—breaking House Rule One for the first time in his life—and sat on his bed with his back against the headboard until dawn grayed the windows.

Just before sunrise, half-dreaming, he heard it one more time. Not from the recorder. Not from the cellar. From inside his own chest, in a voice dry and patient and terribly familiar, asking the only question that had ever mattered in this house:

*Which one are you?*

And for one long, lucid moment before sleep finally took him, Rafael Thornbury realized he no longer knew how to answer.

* * *

He woke at noon to sunlight and the smell of burnt toast, and for a few blessed minutes the night seemed survivable, explainable, a hallucination of exhaustion. Then he came downstairs and found Tomas standing at the kitchen counter, pale as milk, holding the recorder in both hands like something rescued from a fire.

"You left it out," Tomas said. His voice hadn't been used much in three years and it came out rusty, careful. "It was running."

"Tomas—"

"It talked to me." His brother's eyes were wet and wide and absolutely steady. "While you slept. It asked me to count the stairs."
