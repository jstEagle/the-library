## 11. Sleepwalking Interview

I didn't plan to listen to the whole thing.

That's the part I keep returning to, the part I want to make myself believe: that I pressed play intending to scrub through, to sample, to confirm what I already suspected and then stop. Ten minutes. Fifteen at most. Enough to know whether the second voice was real or whether my recorder had developed some fault—a crosstalk, a bleed from a neighbor's radio, anything with a physical explanation I could hold onto like a railing in the dark.

But the recording is four hours and eleven minutes long, and I listened to every second of it in one sitting, sitting on the edge of my bed with the volume low so Maren wouldn't hear through the wall, my hand pressed flat against my own sternum as if to confirm that the chest it rested on belonged to me.

The recorder sits on my nightstand now. I haven't deleted the file. I don't know why. Maybe because deleting it would feel like agreeing to forget, and after last night I no longer trust what I might agree to while I'm asleep.

Here is what happened, as best as I can reconstruct it. Then here is what the machine heard. I need both versions on the page, side by side, because only together do they make a shape—and the shape is worse than either alone.

* * *

I set the recorder going at 11:40 p.m., after my pills, after checking the padlock on the cellar door for the third time that evening. The house was quiet in the way it has learned to be quiet—Maren's door closed, Tomas's earplugs in, the draft under the kitchen floorboards breathing its slow cold breath like something sleeping on its side. I remember lying down. I remember the ceiling. I remember thinking, with a clarity that now seems obscene, *if there's someone down there, maybe it will talk to me too.*

Then nothing. Sleep, black and seamless, the kind I haven't had in weeks.

I woke at 6:15 a.m. in my bed, covers tucked neatly to my chin—which I never do; I sleep like a man fighting his own bedding—and my throat was dry in a specific way, the way it gets after talking for a long time. My jaw ached. There was a faint taste of dust in my mouth, as if I'd been speaking into a cellar's worth of still air.

And my feet were cold. Not bedroom-cold. Stone-cold. The particular cold of concrete through sock fabric.

I told myself I'd gotten up for water and forgotten. I have gotten up for water and forgotten before. That has always been enough. It has been enough for three years, and I built a life on top of it the way you build a house on a sinkhole, telling yourself the cracks are settling.

Then I pressed play.

* * *

What follows is the transcript. I've typed it out by hand rather than trusting myself to paraphrase, because paraphrase is a form of mercy and I don't deserve mercy from this document. Timestamps are from the recorder. Where the voices overlap I've marked it. Where I couldn't bear to type what was said, I typed it anyway.

**[00:12] RAFAEL (asleep):** —and then she said we could split it, but I said no, one of us should have the whole thing.

**[00:14]** *(silence, eleven seconds)*

**[00:25] SECOND VOICE:** And why did you say no?

The second voice—I've described it before, to Odette, to the empty kitchen, to no one who could help me. Dry. Patient. The voice of someone reading aloud in an empty library, unhurried, certain the book isn't going anywhere. But hearing it again, knowing now how long it stayed, I notice something new: it sounds *interested*. Not hungry. Interested, the way an examiner is interested. It had all night, and it intended to use it.

**[00:31] RAFAEL (asleep):** Because she'd have felt bad taking it. She always felt bad about things that weren't her fault.

**[00:34] SECOND VOICE:** Whose fault were they?

**[00:36] RAFAEL (asleep):** Nobody's. That's what I kept telling her.

I sat up straight at that. Because I remembered the conversation—or I remembered *a* conversation, a night when Rosalind was sixteen and crying over something she wouldn't name, and I told her it wasn't her fault, and she said whose was it then, and I said nobody's, and she looked at me like I'd handed her something fragile and told her not to drop it.

Except. Except the details were wrong. In my memory of that night, *she* was the one crying about the thing she wouldn't name, and *I* was the one saying nobody's fault. In the recording, the positions are reversed. Asleep, I described a conversation where Rosalind comforted me. Where I was the one carrying guilt that belonged to no one.

I told myself I misremembered. Memory is unreliable. Everyone knows this. It's the first thing you learn and the last thing you can use.

The voice knew better than to let it go.

**[00:52] SECOND VOICE:** Let's begin properly. State your name.

**[00:54] RAFAEL (asleep):** Rafael Thornbury.

**[00:56] SECOND VOICE:** Full name.

**[00:58] RAFAEL (asleep):** Rafael Edvard Thornbury. Born March ninth. This house. Twelve minutes past midnight, Mama says, though the hospital clock said eleven fifty-eight, and she always said the hospital was right because hospitals are right about time even when they're wrong about everything else.

**[01:09] SECOND VOICE:** Confident. Good. And your sister?

**[01:11] RAFAEL (asleep):** Rosalind. Older by twelve minutes.

My hands stopped moving on the transcript. I had to read that line three times.

Rosalind was younger. Twelve minutes younger. I was born first; it was the one fact of our origin story, the joke Mama told at every dinner party, the way Rafael and Rosalind sorted themselves into every game and every grievance—he's the older brother, she's the baby, twelve minutes and a lifetime apart. I have built entire years on being the elder twin. When she died—when the stranger in the casket died wearing her name—one of the small, shameful comforts I clung to was that I had outlived my little sister, that I had failed to protect her but at least I'd gone first into the world and last out of it.

Asleep, I said she was older. Asleep, I said it without hesitation, without correction, in the flat confident tone of a man stating his own address.

**[01:16] SECOND VOICE:** Older by twelve minutes. Are you certain?

**[01:19] RAFAEL (asleep):** Of course. She never let me forget it. She said she got the first look at the world and I got the leftovers.

**[01:24] SECOND VOICE:** And did she? Get the first look?

**[01:27] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(pause)* She said so.

**[01:29] SECOND VOICE:** You're hesitating.

**[01:31] RAFAEL (asleep):** I'm not hesitating. I'm remembering.

**[01:33] SECOND VOICE:** Those are different things. Take your time. We have until morning.

*We have until morning.* I wrote those words down and my pen went through the paper. It wasn't a threat. That's what makes it unbearable—it wasn't a threat, it was scheduling. The voice had budgeted my sleep the way Mama budgets her medication, and it intended to spend every hour.

* * *

**[01:47] SECOND VOICE:** Tell me about age eighteen.

**[01:49] RAFAEL (asleep):** Which part?

**[01:50] SECOND VOICE:** All of it. Start wherever you like. Start with the summer.

**[01:55] RAFAEL (asleep):** The summer I turned eighteen I worked at the orchard on Vasseur Road. Picking. My hands smelled like apple all season; I couldn't get it out. Rosa hated it. She said I smelled like a fruit cellar.

*A fruit cellar.* He said it casually, asleep, the way you say things you've said a hundred times. And I sat there in the gray light of six-forty in the morning knowing two things with absolute certainty: first, that I never worked at any orchard. Second, that Rosalind did. The summer she was eighteen—the summer they turned eighteen, our eighteenth—she picked apples on Vasseur Road, and she came home smelling of them, and she complained about it, and I teased her, and at some point she said the words *fruit cellar* and laughed and shivered at the same time, and I remember that laugh, I remember it—

I remember it from the outside. From across a kitchen table. Watching her say it.

In the recording, I was the one who lived it.

**[02:03] SECOND VOICE:** And the accident that autumn?

**[02:05] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(long pause)*

**[02:11] SECOND VOICE:** Take your time.

**[02:19] RAFAEL (asleep):** The ladder. I fell off the ladder. Third rung from the top, the whole rack of ladders went, and I broke my wrist, and Rosa drove me to the clinic with the radio off because she knew I couldn't stand noise when I was scared. She held my good hand the whole way. She said, "You're fine, you're fine," but she was the one shaking.

I broke my wrist when I was seventeen. Left wrist, bicycle, ice on the bridge road. I have the scar. I ran my thumb over the scar while the recording played, needing the proof of my own body, and the scar was there, and it meant nothing, because scars don't testify. Scars just stay.

And the rest of it—the radio off, the held hand, the shaking—that was hers. That was Rosalind's broken wrist, Rosalind's clinic trip, except in her version she'd been alone, because I was away that month, I was—

Where was I? I reached for it and the month came back wrong, soft-edged, a photograph left in the sun. I was away. I think. Wasn't I?

**[02:44] SECOND VOICE:** Who held your hand?

**[02:46] RAFAEL (asleep):** Rosa.

**[02:47] SECOND VOICE:** And who drove?

**[02:48] RAFAEL (asleep):** Rosa.

**[02:49] SECOND VOICE:** Both of you were in the truck.

**[02:51] RAFAEL (asleep):** Yes.

**[02:52] SECOND VOICE:** Then who was driving?

**[03:01] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(no answer)*

Eleven seconds of silence. On the tape, you can hear breathing—mine, slow and even, untroubled. Whatever was happening behind my sleeping face, it wasn't struggle. It was more like a man standing in front of a filing cabinet, opening drawers, finding them rearranged.

**[03:12] SECOND VOICE:** Shall I tell you who was driving?

**[03:15] RAFAEL (asleep):** Rosa was driving.

**[03:17] SECOND VOICE:** You said that. You've said it twice now, and each time a little faster. Repetition isn't recall, Rafael. Recall doesn't need to convince itself.

**[03:26] RAFAEL (asleep):** What do you mean?

**[03:28] SECOND VOICE:** I mean that you answer beautifully. Every question, fluently, warmly, with detail. Your childhood, your sister, your hurts, your summers. You are a very complete account of a person. There's only one difficulty.

**[03:41] RAFAEL (asleep):** What difficulty?

**[03:43] SECOND VOICE:** Half of it belongs to someone else.

* * *

I stopped the tape there the first time. I actually got up, walked to the bathroom, put cold water on my face, and stood looking at myself in the mirror doing the thing you do—checking the pupils, checking the symmetry, confirming the face is yours, as if a mirror has ever once in human history lied convincingly.

Then I came back and pressed play, because Odette's voice was in my head: *It takes being, boy.* And if something was auditing mine, I needed to hear the audit.

* * *

**[03:58] SECOND VOICE:** Let's try simpler things. What color is your mother's kitchen?

**[04:01] RAFAEL (asleep):** Yellow. Faded yellow. The paint's peeling above the window where the sun hits.

Correct. True. Mine.

**[04:06] SECOND VOICE:** What does she call you when she's half-asleep and mixing up her children?

**[04:10] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(pause)* She calls us both "little bird."

**[04:13] SECOND VOICE:** Us. Or her?

**[04:15] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(longer pause)* Her. She calls her little bird. She calls me Rafael.

**[04:20] SECOND VOICE:** And which one are you?

**[04:22] RAFAEL (asleep):** Rafael.

**[04:23] SECOND VOICE:** Say it slower.

**[04:26] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(pause)* Rafael.

**[04:28] SECOND VOICE:** Slower still. Like you're checking it against something.

**[04:35] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(unintelligible)*

**[04:37] SECOND VOICE:** What was that?

**[04:39] RAFAEL (asleep):** I said it fits. It fits, it's just—it fits like a coat I've been wearing so long I forgot I bought it.

**[04:46] SECOND VOICE:** Did you buy it?

**[04:48] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(no answer)*

**[04:53] SECOND VOICE:** We'll come back to that.

* * *

The interview went on for hours. It moved through everything—first day of school, the shape of my father's hands, the well behind the garden, the winter the pipes froze, the funeral. Especially the funeral. The voice circled the funeral four separate times from four directions, and each time, asleep, I gave an account that was *almost* right and exactly wrong in one load-bearing detail.

I said I'd worn a black tie. I wore gray. I said it rained. It was bright, brutally bright, the kind of day where the light feels like an accusation. I said Mama gripped my arm. She gripped Tomas's. I stood apart, because I couldn't be touched that day, because the mortician had whispered to me twenty minutes earlier—*Mr. Thornbury, I'm so sorry, this is not your sister*—and I had smiled and nodded and helped carry the casket anyway, and the weight of it had been wrong, too light, the weight of a stranger, and I have carried that wrongness ever since instead of the casket.

Asleep, I told the voice: *She looked peaceful. She looked like herself.*

Awake, I know the face in the casket looked like no one. That was the horror of it. Not that it looked wrong. That it looked like *nothing*, like a rough draft of a face, like something made by a craftsman working from a description over the phone.

**[3:12:40] SECOND VOICE:** Describe her face at the funeral.

**[3:12:47] RAFAEL (asleep):** Peaceful. Pale. Her hair was pinned the way she liked, off her forehead.

**[3:12:55] SECOND VOICE:** And her eyes?

**[3:12:58] RAFAEL (asleep):** Closed.

**[3:13:00] SECOND VOICE:** What color were they when they were open?

**[3:13:06] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(pause)* Brown.

**[3:13:09] SECOND VOICE:** Are you sure?

**[3:13:12] RAFAEL (asleep):** Hazel. Hazel-brown. They changed in different light.

**[3:13:17] SECOND VOICE:** Which is it? Brown, or hazel?

**[3:13:22] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(whispering)* Why are you asking me this?

**[3:13:26] SECOND VOICE:** Because I want to know whether you mourned her or whether you *are* what's left of her mourning itself. Because I want to know what you'll say when I ask the only question that matters. Because, Rafael, I have been keeping accounts in this house for forty years, and I am very good at finding the entry that doesn't balance.

**[3:13:52] RAFAEL (asleep):** What entry?

**[3:13:54] SECOND VOICE:** Yours.

* * *

Near dawn, near the end, the voice changed tactics. It got gentle. That was the worst part—not the interrogation but the tenderness afterward, the bedside manner of it, as though it had gotten what it needed and could afford kindness now.

**[4:02:18] SECOND VOICE:** You've done well. Thank you for your honesty.

**[4:02:22] RAFAEL (asleep):** Was I honest?

**[4:02:24] SECOND VOICE:** Completely. That's the tragedy of it. You never once lied to me. Do you know how rare that is? Most people lie in their sleep. They defend themselves. You just... opened. Like a house with no doors locked.

**[4:02:41] RAFAEL (asleep):** Is that bad?

**[4:02:43] SECOND VOICE:** It's convenient. For both of us. Now—one last question, and then you can rest, truly rest, until morning. Are you ready?

**[4:02:55] RAFAEL (asleep):** Yes.

**[4:02:57] SECOND VOICE:** The night your sister went down into the cellar—willingly, you understand, she walked down those stairs on her own feet, and she closed the door behind her, and she asked you not to follow—what did she say to you?

**[4:03:14] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(silence)*

**[4:03:30] SECOND VOICE:** You were there. I know you were there. One of you was holding the flashlight.

**[4:03:36] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(very quietly)* She said, "Whichever one of us it is—we take care of each other. Promise me that's the part you keep."

**[4:03:47] SECOND VOICE:** Beautiful. And did you promise?

**[4:03:50] RAFAEL (asleep):** Yes.

**[4:03:51] SECOND VOICE:** And which one of you was it? Which one of you did she say that to?

**[4:04:02] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(sixteen seconds of silence; on the tape, the sound of breath catching, once, like a hiccup of grief)*

**[4:04:18] RAFAEL (asleep):** Me. She said it to me.

**[4:04:21] SECOND VOICE:** Ah. There it is.

**[4:04:23] RAFAEL (asleep):** There's what?

**[4:04:25] SECOND VOICE:** The hesitation. Four seconds late, and pitched up at the end, the way answers go when they're being chosen rather than remembered. You've done it all night, Rafael. Every true thing about yourself, instant and fluent. Every true thing about *her*, four seconds late. You carry her memories like coins in the wrong pockets. You keep reaching for yours and finding hers, and you've been doing it so long you've stopped noticing the weight.

**[4:04:59] RAFAEL (asleep):** Then what am I?

**[4:05:03] SECOND VOICE:** Oh, that's not my question to answer. That's yours. Mine is much smaller. Mine is just arithmetic. The count comes soon, and I need to know how many of you there are. So tell me, carefully, honestly, the way you've been honest all night:

**[4:05:24] SECOND VOICE:** If I count the people in this house—if I walk the rooms and tally what's living under this roof—how many names should I write down?

**[4:05:36] RAFAEL (asleep):** Three. Mama, Tomas, me.

**[4:05:40] SECOND VOICE:** And the fourth bedroom?

**[4:05:43] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(no answer)*

**[4:05:50] SECOND VOICE:** The room with the hairbrush still in it. The ribbon on the dresser. The bed made up fresh, every week, by someone who doesn't remember making it. Should I count that room?

**[4:06:04] RAFAEL (asleep):** Don't count that room.

**[4:06:07] SECOND VOICE:** Why not?

**[4:06:10] RAFAEL (asleep):** *(crying now, quietly, the way you cry in dreams, without changing your breathing)* Because I don't know what's in it yet.

**[4:06:17] SECOND VOICE:** No. But you will. Sleep now, Rafael. Or whoever you are. Morning's nearly here, and mornings belong to you. For now.

* * *

The recording ends at 4:11:32. A minute later, on the tape, there's the sound of footsteps—my footsteps—crossing the floor, descending stairs. Thirteen of them. Then a long stretch of nothing, forty minutes of room tone, the house breathing, and beneath it, so faint I've had to listen with headphones at full volume to confirm it's really there, a sound like a chair being drawn out from a table below the floor. And a voice, almost lost in the static, humming something I almost recognize.

Then my footsteps again, ascending. Thirteen. My door. My bed. Done.

I woke at 6:15 with cold feet and a dry throat and a jaw that ached from answering questions.

* * *

It's noon now. Tomas is at school. Maren is sleeping in her chair with the television murmuring soap operas to her, and I've checked the cellar padlock twice since breakfast, and it holds, and it means nothing, because last night proved the lock was never the point. The door was never the point. The thing in the cellar doesn't need the door. It has me. It talks to me through my own mouth while I sleep, and I answer it *honestly*, and apparently I've been answering it for longer than one night, because the voice knew things about my sleep the way a regular knows his usual table.

Here is what I cannot stop turning over, the pebble in the shoe of my mind:

Sleeping, I said Rosalind was older. Sleeping, I worked at the orchard. Sleeping, I broke my wrist falling off a ladder and was driven to the clinic by my sister. Sleeping, I remembered her life from the inside, fluently, warmly, in perfect detail—and my own life only in outline, four seconds late, chosen rather than recalled.

There are two explanations. I've been holding one in each hand all day like the pans of a scale.

The first: my grief has rotted my memory. Three years of living with a ghost-shaped absence, and my mind has filled the vacancy with her, borrowed her history to keep her alive, until the borrowing became indistinguishable from having lived it. Grief does strange things. Doctors would nod at this explanation. I want it to be true the way you want a rope to hold.

The second explanation is the one the voice would prefer, and it is this: that the memories aren't borrowed at all. That they're *residual*. That somewhere in this house, in this family, in the ledger Mama keeps locked in her room, there is an entry marked forty years ago—*one of two, payable upon majority*—and that the payment was made, and that what came back up those cellar stairs three years ago, what ate dinner with us and buried the stranger and slept in my bed and answered questions in the dark, was never entirely sure which twin it was.

That I am not a man haunted by his sister's death.

That I am what's left after a debt was collected, walking around in the remainder, wearing a name like a coat I don't remember buying.

I can't live in that sentence. But I can't evict it either. So tonight I'm going to do the only thing left that feels like motion instead of waiting: I'm going into Mama's room while she sleeps, and I'm going to find that ledger, and I'm going to read every entry, forty years of blood types and hair and years of memory, down to the final line.

Because the voice asked me one question I haven't been able to shake, and it wasn't *what are you*. It was smaller than that. It was arithmetic.

*How many names should I write down?*

Three, I told it. Mama, Tomas, me.

And then it asked about the fourth bedroom. And I said, *don't count that room.*

But I've been standing outside Rosalind's door for an hour now, and my hand is on the knob, and I understand suddenly, with total clarity, why the bed in there is always freshly made.

Someone in this house has been keeping it ready.

And I no longer trust that it's me.
