## 14. Which One Came Home

I did not sleep after Odette Vasseur's door closed behind me. I sat in the car in her driveway for a long time with the engine ticking itself cool, watching her curtains, waiting for her silhouette to cross the window the way it had crossed it my whole childhood—slow, deliberate, a woman who moved like she was always being watched. The silhouette never came. The house sat there with its lights on and its windows dark and its porch light burning for no one, and I understood, in the way you understand things at two in the morning, that I was the last person alive who would ever knock on that door.

*It takes being,* she had said. *Whoever it keeps, everyone forgets they existed.*

I drove home with the windows down because the car felt too small for what was in my head.

* * *

The house was quiet in the way it had learned to be quiet—Tomas awake behind his door pretending to sleep, Maren medicated into her shallow evening sea, the cellar breathing its cold up through the floorboards like a thing asleep with one eye open. I went to my study, the room I had claimed the week after the funeral because it was the farthest from the kitchen and its draft, and I did the thing I had been refusing to do for three years.

I opened the boxes from the funeral.

They had been in the closet since the week after we buried her, taped shut with the same gray duct tape I'd used to seal every box of Rosalind's things. I had told myself it was grief. Grief keeps you from opening boxes. Grief makes the tape hard to cut. But grief, I was learning, is a very convenient word, and I had been hiding behind it the way a debtor hides from a bill collector—except the collector had a key to the house and a voice like dry paper and had been asking me, night after night, in my own sleeping mouth, *which one are you.*

The tape came away with a sound like a small scream.

Inside the first box: the funeral home's folder. Cream cardstock, the Verrone & Sons crest embossed in gold, my name printed on the tab in a secretary's careful hand—*THORNBURY, R.* I had never looked past the first page. I had signed what needed signing, paid what needed paying, and folded the folder shut like closing a wound.

I laid the contents out on the desk under the lamp, one document at a time, the way you'd lay out cards you already knew were going to lose.

Death certificate. I had read this one before, or I'd read enough of it. *ROSAIND MAREN THORNBURY.* Cause of death listed as *undetermined, pending*, and then, in smaller type, the amendment that had come eight months later: *cardiac arrhythmia, presumed.* Presumed. Even the state hadn't been sure what killed her, because whatever was in that casket had not died of anything. It had simply stopped, the way a coat stops when you take it off the hanger.

Then the mortician's worksheet. The one Verrone himself had filled out by hand, the one he'd been holding when he pulled me aside in the receiving line with his breath like coffee and formaldehyde and said, *Mr. Thornbury, I need you to look at her, because that is not the young woman your mother described to me.*

I had looked. That was the thing I had never told anyone, the thing I had buried so deep even the recordings hadn't found it. I had gone back into the viewing room alone, after the service, after everyone left, and I had stood over the casket and looked at her face for a full minute.

And I had thought: *I don't know you.*

Not *this isn't my sister.* That would have been clean. That would have been a horror I could name, a stranger's corpse in my sister's casket, an outrage I could take to lawyers and newspapers. What I had felt was worse. I had looked at the dead face—waxen, composed, wearing my sister's features the way an actor wears them, correctly, even beautifully—and I had felt *nothing recognize itself.* The way you can look at your own handwriting and briefly not believe you wrote it. I had told myself it was shock. I had told myself grief does that, scrambles the face, unmoors the beloved from the features. I had told myself so many things for three years that I had built a whole architecture of telling-myself, and now, holding the mortician's worksheet with his shaky pencil note in the margin—*height discrepancy? family states 5'6", measures 5'4"*—I felt that architecture come down all at once, floor by floor, like the footsteps in Tomas's stories, one at a time, closer.

I had measured Rosalind against myself a hundred times growing up. Twins do that. We were the same height at fourteen. Then she stopped, at five-four, and I kept going, and she used to stand on the second stair to look me in the eye and say it wasn't fair that she'd stopped growing and I hadn't, as if we were one plant that had been divided into two pots.

The woman in the casket was five-four.

My sister was five-four.

And still, in that viewing room, I had not known her.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Around four, when the house was at its coldest and the floorboards had given up their last creak, I went down to the kitchen and made coffee I wouldn't drink and sat with my laptop open, and I did the thing you do when the past starts lying to you: I went looking for records.

The county hospital had digitized its birth records a decade ago. I'd never had reason to look. You don't look up your own birth; it's the one document you never doubt, the foundation under all the other documents. I typed in the search fields with hands that felt like they belonged to someone else—another person's hands, another person's name—and the page loaded with the small, indifferent speed of bureaucracy.

*THORNBURY, R.* Two results.

I opened the first. *Female infant, 6 lbs 2 oz, 11:42 p.m., March 3.* Mother: Maren Elin Thornbury. Father: Edmund Thornbury, deceased. The name field was filled in by hand in the old scan, blue ink gone brown at the edges: *Rosalind Maren.*

I opened the second.

*Male infant, 6 lbs 9 oz, 11:58 p.m., March 3.* Same mother. Same father. And in the name field, in the same blue ink, a single word crossed out with one clean line, and beneath it, pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*Rafael.*

The crossed-out word was not legible. The scan was old, the ink had bloomed, the clerk had written fast. I enlarged it. I enlarged it again until the letters broke apart into gray continents. I turned the screen brightness all the way up and put my face close enough to feel the monitor's heat on my skin, and the crossed-out word stayed a smudge, a ghost of a name, four letters maybe, five, starting with a letter I could not determine.

But the death record was legible. There was a third entry, filed the next morning, 6:15 a.m., March 4. *Male infant, Thornbury. Deceased. Cause: respiratory failure, 6 hours.* No name in the name field. Just a dash, where a life too short to name had been.

*Male infant. Deceased.*

I sat very still in the kitchen while the cold came up through the floorboards and moved around my ankles like a cat.

There were two readings of this. That was the trap of it, the elegant, horrible trap. Reading one: I had had a twin brother who died at six hours old, nameless, and my parents, in their grief or their superstition or their desperation, had given me—no. Wait. That didn't work, because I was the male infant born at 11:58, and the male infant born at 11:58 was dead by morning.

Reading two: the male infant born at 11:58 was Rosalind.

I got up and paced. I made myself say it out loud, to the empty kitchen, because saying things out loud had become the only way I trusted my own mind anymore: "The records could be wrong. Records get mixed up. Hospitals swap paperwork. It was 1987, everything was handwritten."

But the ink. The ink had been pressed hard enough to emboss the page, that name, *Rafael*, written over a smudged-out something, written by someone who needed it to be true. And the death record, filed six hours after the birth, with no name, no funeral, no grave I had ever heard of, no marker anywhere in the family plot—just a dash and a cause and the bureaucratic smell of a thing quietly closed.

I thought of the casket. Five-four. A face I did not know.

I thought of Odette's voice, papery and certain: *It doesn't take bodies, boy. It takes being.*

And underneath that, older, colder, the thing Maren had said in her lucid window, the confession that had seemed like the whole of it at the time: *I traded the second-born's shadow. Only one would be taken. Rosalind went down willingly.*

The second-born. Rosalind had been born first—11:42, six minutes before the male child. Which meant the second-born, the one whose shadow was owed, the one the thing in the well had a claim on, was the one born at 11:58.

The one who died at six hours old, according to a hospital in a county town that had never once been wrong about anything else.

Or the one who never died at all, and was given a dead boy's name to wear, and grew up five-six and left-handed and afraid of the dark, and is sitting in this kitchen at dawn with coffee he cannot drink, unable to remember being born.

* * *

I went up to my room and did not go to my desk. I went to the closet, to the shoebox on the top shelf that I had not opened since I was nineteen, the box of things from before—before the estate, before the funeral, before the recordings, back when the family had been merely poor instead of indebted. Baby pictures. Maren had kept a handful from the hospital, from the years after, and I took them out now and spread them on the bed in the gray first light.

The hospital photo: two infants in a bassinet, bundled, faces turned toward each other. I had always been told it was Rosalind and me. I looked at it now with the eyes of a man auditing his own existence. Two infants. One in pink knit, one in blue. You could not tell anything else from it. You could not tell which one was which. You could not tell if either one was anyone at all.

The next photo was dated eight months later. One child, on a quilt, propped against a pillow. Rosalind, I had always been told. But the child in the photo was wearing blue, and the child in the photo had a small dark birthmark at the hairline, left side.

I put my hand to my own hairline. Left side. I had carried that mark my whole life and never once thought about it, the way you never think about your own pulse until someone makes you listen for it.

There was no photo of two toddlers. There was no photo of the twins' first birthday with two cakes or one cake and two high chairs. There was a gap, eight months to age three, and then a boy at a kitchen table with a birthday candle, alone, and a girl's voice off-camera—Maren's voice, laughing, saying something I couldn't hear—and I had spent my whole life assuming the girl was behind the camera.

But Maren was behind the camera. Maren was always behind the camera. So whose voice was that?

I sat on the edge of the bed while the light came up slow and colorless through the curtains, and I did the arithmetic I had been refusing to do. If the male child born at 11:58 died, then the child in the blue knit, the child with the birthmark, the child at the birthday table—was Rosalind. And the child in the pink knit, the one born at 11:42, the firstborn, the one whose shadow was never owed—was the one who went into the ground three years ago wearing my sister's face, five-four, unknown to me at a distance of two feet.

Which meant the funeral had been for Rosalind. Which meant Rosalind had died. Which meant I was the survivor, and the records were a clerical error, and the crossed-out name was nothing, a smudge, an inkblot, a test.

Or.

Or the thing in the cellar had told the truth in the only language it had, the language of questions: *which twin are you*—asked not because it didn't know, but because it wanted to hear me say it. Because my answer was the merchandise. Because identity, Odette had said, was the currency, and a name given to a dead child in blue ink pressed hard enough to emboss the page was the largest denomination this family had ever printed.

I thought about the recordings. I thought about sleep-me, fluent and confident, describing the summer at the lake when we were seventeen—the lake where Rosalind learned to dive, the lake where *she* cut her foot on the rocks. Sleep-me had described the cut. Sleep-me had described the shape of the scar. I had listened to that playback and felt only horror at the voice answering him. I had not let myself notice the other thing, the thing I noticed now with the morning light full on the bed and the photographs spread out like evidence at a trial: that I had described the scar in the first person. *I have a scar on my left foot from the rocks at Calloway Lake.*

I looked down at my left foot. Pale, bony, unremarkable. Unscarred.

I have never been to Calloway Lake.

I got up so fast the photographs slid off the bed. In the bathroom, I turned on the light and gripped the sink and looked at my face—the face I had worn for thirty-six years, the face that was and was not my father's, the face the mirror had been showing me every morning of my life without ever once being asked to prove anything. I looked at it the way I had looked at the face in the casket. From two feet. With nothing to recognize.

*I don't know you,* I thought, and the thought had a voice, and the voice was dry and patient, and it was not mine.

The house was silent. Tomas was asleep at last, or pretending. Maren was in her gray sea. The padlock on the cellar door was holding, the way it had held for three years, the way a dam holds, the way a held breath holds.

I understood then what the count was really counting. Not bodies. Not footsteps. It was counting the distance between a person and their own name, and mine had been narrowing my whole life—every unscarred foot, every smudged inkblot, every morning the mirror took half a second too long to hand me back my face. The entity had never needed to break in. It had been waiting, all these years, with the hospitality of a creditor, for me to arrive at the question myself. Because a debt is easiest to collect when the debtor volunteers the amount.

I went back into the bedroom and picked up the hospital photo, the two infants in the bassinet, and I held it under the lamp and looked at the pink bundle and the blue bundle and I said, out loud, in a voice that came out steady and strange, like someone else's:

"Which one came home?"

The floorboards answered. Not with footsteps. With a sound I had never heard them make before, though I would know it anywhere, because I had heard it on the recordings, night after night, in the pauses between my sleeping questions.

The sound of dry paper, turning a page.

Somewhere below, patient as interest accruing, the thing in the cellar-parlor was waiting for my answer. And for the first time in thirty-six years, I did not know what it was.
