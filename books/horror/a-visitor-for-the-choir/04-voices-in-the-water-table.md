## 4. Voices in the Water Table

The first night, Gideon told themselves it was pipes.

The house they'd rented—the only one in Hollowmere that would take a boarder, and even then at a rate the landlady seemed almost ashamed to charge—was old enough to have opinions about weather. Copper plumbing from some postwar renovation, iron drains older than that, and beneath it all the water table, high and restless this close to the valley floor, running through gravel and clay like blood through tissue. Houses made sounds. Gideon knew this. They had lived alone in enough of them.

So when the sound came up through the floorboards at 2:14 in the morning—a single held tone, low and patient, somewhere between a hum and a vowel—Gideon lay in bed and catalogued explanations the way their sister used to catalogue constellations, one by one, until the sky was tamed.

Pipes. Settling joists. Wind finding the chimney's throat.

Then the tone resolved into a note, and the note into a phrase, and the phrase was four bars of something in a minor key that Gideon did not know and yet knew *absolutely*, the way you know your own name spoken by a stranger on a train platform—and every explanation they had assembled collapsed at once, quietly, like a chair losing a leg.

Because Wren used to hum that. Not that song—Gideon couldn't have written down a note of it—but that *way*. The way she'd hold a pitch past the point where breathing demanded release, stubborn, showing off for no one, the way she hummed when she was thinking, when she was happy, when she was eleven years old and certain the world would organize itself around her eventually. It wasn't the melody that undid them. It was the *habit* inside the melody. The posture of her attention, preserved in sound.

Gideon sat up in the dark. The boards were cold under their bare feet. They pressed their palm flat against the floor as if checking a fever, and the humming came up through the wood grain into their bones, faint as a pulse in a wrist, and stopped the moment they touched it.

Silence. Then, from further away—from the direction of the kitchen, or below the kitchen, or below whatever was below the kitchen—it began again, one room over, patient, unhurried, like something that had all the time in the world because it had already taken three years of it.

"Wren?" Gideon said aloud, and hated how small their voice sounded, how much it wanted.

The humming did not answer. But it did not stop, either. And somehow that was worse—or better; Gideon genuinely could not tell anymore which—they lay back down and listened to their dead sister sing under the floorboards until dawn came gray through the curtains, and they realized their hand was still pressed to the floor, and that they had not slept, and that they were smiling.

That was the part they would remember later with the most shame. The smiling.

* * *

In the morning they did what any reasonable person raised on rationalism and grief counseling would do.

They went to the hardware store and bought a recorder.

Not the kind you play—the kind you use. A handheld digital unit with two microphones and a display that drew the sound as waves, red peaks on black, a seismograph for the air. The kid behind the counter rang it up without comment, though he looked at Gideon's face—the three days of unshaved grief, the eyes gone hollow and overbright—and said, "You're the one from the funeral."

"I'm the one from the funeral."

"Lot of people buying recorders lately," the kid said, and then seemed to hear himself, and went pink, and busied himself with the register. "Daley's crew. And Mr. Corliss's people. Everybody wants to document everything now." He slid the box across. "Battery compartment sticks a little. You have to—" He demonstrated with his thumbnail. His hands were shaking very slightly, Gideon noticed. Everyone's hands in Hollowmere shook slightly, like the whole town lived on a fault line no map acknowledged.

Gideon walked home through streets where curtains twitched in the pattern of avoidance they'd learned in chapter after chapter of this town's behavior. Mrs. Halloway's window, though—Mrs. Halloway's curtain hung still, and behind it, Gideon could feel rather than see, someone was watching them with an expression they couldn't parse. Concern, maybe. Or appraisal. The look you give a horse you're deciding whether to put down.

They set the recorder up that night on the bedroom floor, dead center, gain turned high, and lay in bed watching the red waveform draw its flatline across the little screen like a hospital monitor for a patient who hadn't been admitted yet.

At 2:14—it was 2:14 again, exactly, and Gideon noted the precision with a chill that felt almost professional—the line jumped.

The wave bloomed. Low frequencies first, felt more than heard, a pressure change in the room like the house inhaling. Then the tone. Then the phrase. Then, underneath the phrase, so quiet Gideon wouldn't find it until later, until they played the recording back through headphones at 3 a.m. with the lights on—

Underneath the phrase, there were other voices.

* * *

They spent the next day listening.

This is what Gideon Pemberton did for a living, insofar as they did anything for a living: they listened. Three novels of quiet literary dread had been built out of sitting in rooms with a notebook, transcribing the silences between what people said. They knew how to listen to a recording the way a jeweler knows how to look at a stone—slowly, under good light, turning it, looking for the flaw that proves it real.

The flaw that proved this real took them six hours to find, and when they found it they had to sit down on the kitchen floor with their back against the cold stove.

It was in the harmonies.

The main voice—Wren's voice, or the shape of Wren's voice, worn like a coat—sang the melody, four bars, rest, four bars. Simple. Almost childlike. But beneath it, in the recording, in the space below audibility where the microphones reached but human ears gave up, there were parts. Not echoes. Parts. A second voice holding a third above the melody. A third voice moving in contrary motion against both. A fourth, so low it registered less as sound than as a disturbance, a shiver in the waveform like something enormous turning over in shallow water.

Four-part harmony. Written, arranged, *rehearsed* harmony—voice-leading with intention, resolutions prepared and delayed according to rules that predated recording, predated notation, possibly predated the chapel whose foundation all of this seemed to rise from. No single throat could make these sounds. No two throats. This was a choir. This was a choir singing under the floorboards of a rented house in a drowned valley, at 2:14 in the morning, with perfect discipline, and one of the voices—the third one, the alto line, warm and slightly ahead of the beat the way certain singers always are—was Wren's.

Gideon played it forty times. On the forty-first time they caught themselves conducting. Their right hand had risen off their knee without permission and was tracing the phrases in the air, cueing entrances for voices that needed no cueing, and they looked at their own hand like it belonged to a stranger, and put the recorder away, and did not sleep, and the next night at 2:14 they got up and followed the sound.

* * *

The well stood at the back of the property, a stone throat capped with a wooden lid gone soft with rot, and the landlord had told them on day one never to open it, in the tone people use for instructions they've given many times and don't want to examine. Gideon knelt beside it in the wet grass in their coat over their pajamas, recorder in hand, and lifted the lid.

The smell rose first: cold stone, standing water, and something older underneath, mineral and sweet, like the air in a cave that has never been entered. Then the sound. Down the shaft, far below the reach of any flashlight beam, the choir was singing, and here it was louder, fuller, the harmonies stacking and interlocking with the patience of geology, and Gideon leaned over the dark and felt the song come up around them like water rising in a locked room.

They should have dropped the lid and run. Some part of them—the part that had insisted on viewing the body, the part that kept asking questions in a town that answered doors instead—registered this clearly. Run. Find Amos Pike, the deaf tenor Halloway had mentioned once in a slip; find anyone whose ears this thing couldn't reach. Do not stand over an open well at 3 a.m. listening to your dead sister sing alto.

But the alto line climbed, just then, toward a suspension—a note held against the harmony, dissonant, aching, unresolved—and Gideon knew with total certainty how it would resolve, knew the resolution the way they knew Wren's laugh, and when it resolved exactly as known, exactly as *her*, something in their chest tore along a seam that had been tearing since the funeral home, since the wrong face in the coffin, since the phone call three years ago, and they thought:

*She's alive. She's down there. She's been down there the whole time and she's singing and if I can just—*

The thought was not theirs. They understood that even as they thought it, the way you understand a dream is a dream while still believing every word of it. The thought had been *given* to them, placed in them gently, the way you slip a letter under a door, and it fit the lock of their grief so precisely that distinguishing it from their own wanting was like distinguishing blood from dye in water.

The melody paused. In the pause, in the well-dark, a voice said—not sang, said, close now, closer than the bottom of a shaft could account for, close as the skin of their own ear:

*"Gideon."*

Their own name, in Wren's mouth. The specific pronunciation, the softening of the second syllable she'd done since childhood, the private joke of it, *Gid-e-on, like the Bible but sleepier.*

Gideon's knees hit the wet grass. The recorder was still running. Later, playing it back, they would find that the entire encounter lasted ninety seconds, and that in the last ten, beneath their own ragged breathing, the choir resumed—four bars, rest, four bars—and that the alto line, on the final bar, bent upward at the end. A question mark. An invitation. *Come finish the phrase.*

They closed the lid. They sat in the grass until the sky went the color of dishwater, shaking, and the terrible thing, the thing they would carry like a stone in the shoe for days afterward, was that their first coherent thought upon being able to think at all was not *what is that thing* but:

*She asked me to come down.*

And their second thought, arriving like frost across a window, was:

*Wren never left Hollowmere. Nobody leaves Hollowmere. That's why nobody will look at me. They all know where everyone goes.*

* * *

By noon they had filled nine pages of the notebook. The rational mind, denied sleep, does not shut down; it gets stranger, sharper, drawing diagrams. Gideon sat at the kitchen table with coffee going cold and mapped it out: the dates (Halloway's choir stopped in 1998; three members vanished; Daley's breach happened eleven days ago; the singing started four nights ago), the locations (floorboards, drainpipes, the well—all water-adjacent, all connected, the whole town sitting on the same saturated gravel, the same water table, one connected dark beneath every floor in Hollowmere), and the times (2:14, 2:14, 2:14—no, not a time, they realized suddenly, pen stopping. A duration. Something measured from a fixed event. Something that had happened at a specific hour on a specific night, and everything since was an echo counting backward from it).

They didn't know what the event was. But they knew someone who might.

The hymnal page was in their jacket pocket, where it had lived since Halloway pressed it into their hands on the porch—torn from a choir book, yellowed, a single verse of music in old four-part arrangement, and at the top, in pencil, in a hand that shook the way everyone's hands in Hollowmere shook, a note Gideon had read a dozen times without understanding:

*We do not sing past the fourth verse. Whatever happens, whoever asks, however much it begs — we do not sing past the fourth verse.*

Begs. Gideon had assumed it meant the congregation begging for another hymn. Sitting in the kitchen with nine pages of notes and the memory of their name spoken up out of a well, they understood otherwise.

The singing started again at 2:14 that night, and the night after, and each time it found a new route into the house—the bathroom drain, the gap behind the baseboard, once, impossibly, the teakettle, which sat cold and empty on the stove and hummed a perfect B-flat until Gideon threw it into the yard. Each time, the alto line waited for them. Each time, at the end of the fourth bar, it bent upward. Asking.

And each time, Gideon noticed, the asking got a little easier to want to answer. The thought arrived faster now, needed less disguise. *Come down. Come finish it. She's waiting.* By the fifth night Gideon had begun sleeping with earplugs and the recorder running, and it was the recorder they trusted, the machine's blind honest microphones, and it was lying awake listening to the playback through headphones—listening to the impossible harmonies stack themselves in the dark under the world—that they finally heard the thing they'd been too close to hear before.

The choir wasn't singing *to* them.

It was singing *short*. Every night, four bars, and then a silence shaped exactly like the place where a fifth verse would go—a silence with edges, a negative space, a held breath two hundred years long. The song wasn't finished. The song had never been allowed to finish. And everything that had crawled up through the water table into the pipes and wells and floorboards of Hollowmere, wearing the voices of the missing, offering the grieving their dead—it was all of it, every note, one long unfinished sentence, waiting for someone to say the last word.

On the sixth morning, Gideon walked to Mrs. Halloway's house through fog so thick the village existed only as suggestions, and knocked, and when the door opened they held up the torn page and said, "Tell me about the fifth verse."

Old Mrs. Halloway looked at them for a long moment. Behind her, in the dim hall, Gideon could see a piano, and on the piano, arranged in a neat row like patients awaiting diagnosis, five cassette recorders of various vintages, all of them running, all of them recording nothing but the quiet.

"You've been hearing it," Halloway said. Not a question. Her eyes went to Gideon's mouth, oddly—as if checking whether the lips were cracked from singing—and then to the fog beyond, and she stepped aside to let them in, and pulled the door shut fast, as if the fog itself might follow.

"Come in," she said. "And God forgive me, but I'm glad it's finally you. Because it was always going to be a Pemberton."
