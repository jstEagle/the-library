## 12. It Learns Our Names

The first one was the postman.

Gideon saw it happen from Halloway's front window on a Tuesday morning so ordinary it felt obscene—sunlight on wet grass, a dog trotting loose down Mire Lane, and old Tom Bracken walking his route with his canvas bag slung over his shoulder. He stopped at the Ketterling place, and instead of slotting mail through the door he stood there a moment, head tilted, like a man listening to something under the ground. Then he knocked. Mrs. Ketterling answered, and Gideon watched her face change—not with alarm but with a slow, terrible softening, the way a face changes when someone says your name in just the right voice.

Tom Bracken's mouth moved. It wasn't his mouth doing it. The jaw worked slightly wrong, half a beat behind the words, as though the voice were arriving from somewhere else and the body was only trying to keep up.

Gideon couldn't hear what was said through the glass. They didn't need to. They knew that cadence. They had grown up inside that cadence, falling asleep to it through a plaster wall.

*Wren.*

Mrs. Ketterling stepped aside and let him in.

The postman came out eleven minutes later, adjusted his bag, and continued his route. When Gideon ran out barefoot onto the pavement, he tipped his cap pleasantly.

"Morning," he said. His own voice, flat and postal. Whatever had used him had folded itself away like a letter back into its envelope.

"Tom. What did you say to her?"

"Bill for the water rates." He frowned at Gideon's bare feet. "You all right, Pemberton? You look like you've seen a ghost."

"No," Gideon said. "Not yet."

* * *

By Wednesday, everyone in Hollowmere had heard their name spoken from somewhere they couldn't explain.

It happened in daylight now, which was somehow worse than the night singing. The nights had been deniable—a dream, a pipe, the wind off the water table. But the daylight voices came out of ordinary mouths, in ordinary kitchens, and left people standing with dishwater running cold over their hands.

Daley told Gideon about it at the site office, gray-faced, a thermos of coffee untouched in front of him.

"My wife," he said. "Tuesday evening. She was peeling potatoes and she said—and I want you to understand she said this *cheerfully*, like she was telling me about a coupon—" His hands made a shape in the air, then failed. "She said, 'Danny, your father called today.' My father's been dead nine years. And then she said, 'He wants to know when you're coming to visit.'"

"What did you do?"

"I said, 'What did he sound like?'" Daley laughed, and it was the worst laugh Gideon had ever heard, dry and cracking like ice over deep water. "That's what I did. I asked what my dead father *sounded like*. And she said—" He stopped. Pressed his knuckles against his mouth. "She said, 'Like himself. Like himself again.'"

He looked up. "And here's the thing, Pemberton. Part of me—the part that signed on to dig a hole in the ground for a paycheck—that part knows what's happening. But there's another part of me that's been standing outside my own kitchen door every night since, listening, wondering if I should go in."

"Have you?"

"Not yet." He stared at the thermos. "But I will. That's what I can't get past. Not *might*. *Will.* I can feel it in me like gravity."

* * *

Halloway found Gideon at the chapel ruins Thursday morning, where they'd been sitting among the fallen masonry with the torn hymnal page, cross-referencing it against the archive copies until the letters swam. She sat down beside them without ceremony, her coat smelling of mothballs and camphor, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

"It got to Amos's neighbor girl," she said finally. "Sylvie Marsh. Nineteen. She's been deaf since meningitis at six—no, wait, that's Amos. Sylvie hears fine." She corrected herself wearily; the town's afflictions had begun to blur together. "Anyway. Something stood at the end of her bed Tuesday night wearing her grandmother's cardigan and offered to bring her mother back. Her mother ran off to Leeds when Sylvie was four. Never wrote. And Sylvie—God help her—Sylvie said yes. Said yes out loud, in her bedroom, alone. Now she hums. All day. Won't stop. Won't eat unless you put the plate in front of her twice."

"Halloway." Gideon turned the hymnal page toward her. "It's not haunting them. Look at the pattern. It's not random. It's learning them. Each one gets exactly the thing they want most. That's not mimicry anymore. That's *negotiation*."

"I know what it is." Halloway's hands were folded tight in her lap, knuckles bloodless. "I sang alto to that thing for thirty years, child. I know its manners better than I know my late husband's."

"Then tell me what it wants."

"The same thing it's always wanted." She looked out at the breach in the chapel floor, where the company had strung orange plastic fencing that flapped in the wind like shed skin. "To be sung to. To be *answered*. For two hundred years we gave it the first four verses and never the fifth, and it lay there hungry and patient, because patience is the one thing it has more of than anything alive. But now the choir's broken, the seal's cracked, and it's learned something new." Her jaw tightened. "It's learned that it doesn't need us to sing to it. It can sing *through* us. Every person who says yes is another throat."

"And if enough people say yes?"

"Then it finishes its own verse." Halloway stood, joints cracking, and looked down at Gideon with an expression that was almost tender and entirely terrified. "And Hollowmere becomes the last note."

* * *

Friday, it came for Petey Oakes.

Petey was twenty-three, the youngest on Daley's crew, and he'd been sleeping on his mother's porch swing since the night at the breach because he couldn't bear the sound of the house being quiet around him. His mother, Dot Oakes, told Ruth Ellery about it at the pharmacy, and Ruth told Gideon by phone in a voice stripped down to wire.

"He sits out there all day," Ruth said. "And he talks. Not to anyone. To the *lawn*, Gid. And the lawn answers—I heard it myself, I went over there, and I stood at the fence and I heard—" A breath, ragged. "I heard my son."

Gideon closed their eyes. "Marcus has been gone six years, Ruth."

"I know how long my son has been gone." The wire snapped; beneath it was grief so old it had fossilized into something harder than rage. "I know it better than I know my own hands. And I still stood at that fence for forty minutes listening to a dead boy's voice come out of a humming man's mouth, asking me how my arthritis was doing, asking me if I'd kept his football trophies, asking me—" Her voice broke clean through. "Asking me to sing him the song I used to sing him at bedtime. Which is the fifth verse, Gideon. Did you know that? Did you know the lullaby I sang Marcus was the same one?"

Gideon hadn't known. The floor of the world tilted a degree.

"Ruth. Listen to me. Don't go back to that fence."

Silence on the line. Long enough that Gideon checked whether the call had dropped.

"Gid," Ruth said at last, very quietly, "it knows things about me that no thing under the ground could know. Things I never told a living soul. So either it's reading our graves, or it's reading something else. And I don't know which possibility frightens me more."

The line went dead. Gideon stood holding the phone long after, watching the light fail over the drowned fields.

* * *

Saturday night, it came for Gideon.

They should have expected it. They were the last unclaimed thing in town worth claiming, and the thing below had been patient with them, circling, letting the others go first like a fisherman testing hooks. But knowing didn't help. Nothing could have helped.

They woke at 3 a.m. in Halloway's spare room to the sound of the piano downstairs playing three notes. Softly. Over and over. The opening figure of the fourth verse—the one Wren used to practice with two fingers while pretending she was too cool for choir.

Gideon went down the stairs the way you wade into cold water, knowing, dreading, unable to stop.

Wren was sitting at the piano bench.

Not the thing from the coffin—that stranger's ruined face, that wrongness. This was Wren as Gideon carried her: nineteen forever, dark hair cut short the week before she disappeared, wearing the flannel shirt she'd stolen from Gideon's closet and never returned. Her hands rested on the keys without pressing them. The lamplight went through her a little, the way light goes through smoke deciding whether to be solid.

"You always came down when I played that," Wren said. Her voice was perfect. That was the horror of it—it was *perfect*, more Wren than memory, memory polished into something better than the original. "Even when you pretended you were getting water. You always stood on the third stair so the board wouldn't creak. I counted, Gid. Forty-one times."

"Wren died," Gideon said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. "Three years ago. Or she left. Either way, whatever is sitting at that piano, it isn't her."

"Aren't you going to check?" Wren smiled, and it was her smile, the crooked one, the one that meant trouble or tenderness, usually both. "That's the difference between you and me, Gid. You'll stand on the stairs forever. I always just walked in."

"The thing below doesn't know these things about me. It can't. You did."

"Maybe I told it." She turned on the bench, and the lamplight caught her eyes and did something wrong in them, a depth where there shouldn't be depth. "Maybe I've been down there three years, Gid, and I've had nothing to do but talk. Maybe everything it knows about this town, it knows because I told it. Because talking was the only thing keeping me—" Her face flickered, just once, like a candle in a draft, and underneath the flicker was something vast and patient and starving, and then Wren was back, and she was crying, and God help them, the tears were right too. "Come finish the song with me. That's all. One verse. You lead, the way Grandfather was supposed to. You and me, like when we were kids. And then nobody has to be hungry anymore. Not it. Not me. Not you."

Gideon's hand found the doorframe. Held it.

Every temptation they'd catalogued in others—Daley's father, Sylvie's mother, Ruth's son—had been secondhand. This was not. This was the exact weight of their own grief sitting at a piano, offering to be lifted, and some traitorous chamber of their heart was already composing the yes.

"You're not her," Gideon whispered. "But you're using what's real of her. Aren't you. There's a piece of her down there, and it's wearing it like a coat."

Wren's smile faded into something older than the village.

"Everyone's a coat to something, Gideon," she said, in a voice that was suddenly layered, suddenly plural, suddenly coming from the walls and the well and the drainpipes and the space between Gideon's own heartbeat. "*Sing.*"

The lamp burst. The piano sounded all its strings at once, a chord like a struck bell underwater, and Gideon was alone in the dark with their hands shaking and their sister's name caught in their throat like a bone.

* * *

Sunday morning, Hollowmere had stopped pretending.

Gideon walked the length of the high street and counted the doors that opened before they knocked. The Ketterlings' curtains hung open for the first time in years. Old Mr. Fenn stood in his garden in his dressing gown, facing the reservoir, humming. Two women from the parish council sat on a bench sharing a flask of tea, and as Gideon passed, both of them turned their heads in perfect unison, and both of them smiled, and neither of them blinked until Gideon was past.

At the corner by the war memorial, Ruth Ellery was waiting.

She looked rested. That was the detail that stopped Gideon's feet—everyone else in town wore the thing's attention like a bruise, gray and sagging, but Ruth looked *rested*, her hair done, her good coat on, her chin lifted the way it used to lift before choir performances.

"Gid," she said warmly. "Walk with me."

"Ruth—"

"I've been thinking about what you said. About not going back to the fence." She took Gideon's arm, and her grip was strong, sure, terribly calm. "And you were right. The fence is the wrong place. Too public. Too much wind." She steered them gently toward the lane that led down to the chapel, toward the breach, toward the orange fencing flapping like shed skin. "But I've been thinking about Marcus, and about the verse, and I realized something you and Halloway and poor deaf Amos have all missed."

The reservoir water, visible between the houses, had gone dark overnight. Not murky—dark, black-green, still as poured ink, though there was no wind to blame and no cloud above to blame either.

"What's that?" Gideon asked, though they already knew, and their heart was already breaking.

Ruth smiled, and it was the smile of a woman who had set down something enormously heavy after carrying it for six years.

"Somebody has to sing first," she said. "And I remember all five verses."

Down the lane, at the edge of the black water, the chapel breach exhaled—a long, low breath of air that smelled of stone and silt and something sweetly, patiently waiting—and Ruth Ellery's hand tightened on Gideon's arm, drawing them forward, whether they willed it or no.
