## 13. Ruth Sings Along

Ruth Ellery had not slept in the house since the night her son died in it, but she had never stopped sleeping beside him. That was the trick of grief nobody warned you about: the body you buried kept its own bed inside you, warm and occupied, and every morning you woke around it like water closing over a stone.

Daniel would have been thirty-one this October. She still bought the card. She still wrote it out—*Proud of you, always*—and sealed it in an envelope she addressed to a boy who had been dead for nine years, and then she burned it in the sink and ran the tap until the smoke stopped smelling like him. It was a ritual, not madness. She'd checked with a doctor once, early on, and the doctor had said *grief work*, and Ruth had thought: you have no idea what I'm working with.

So when the voice came to her, it did not come as a stranger's.

It came on the third night after Corliss's men locked the road, when the town had gone quiet in that particular way quiet goes when everyone is pretending not to listen. Ruth was sitting at her kitchen table with Daniel's photograph and a cup of tea gone cold, and the drainpipe outside the window began to sing.

Not Wren Pemberton's voice. Not Petey Oakes's humming, which by now half the town could hear through their walls at all hours, patient as plumbing. This was a boy's voice. Unbroken, fourteen years old forever, singing a note Ruth had last heard through a closed bathroom door while he washed up for supper, some nonsense song he'd made up about a dog and a bicycle.

The cup slipped. Tea spread across the table and found the photograph and soaked into it, and Ruth did not move to save it, because the voice was doing something no recording, no memory, no dream had ever managed.

It was singing slightly flat. The way Daniel sang. The way no one else in the history of sound had ever sung.

"Mom," the drainpipe said, between verses, conversational, close, "it's cold down here."

Ruth Ellery had spent nine years learning every shape her grief could take—the rage, the bargaining, the long gray plateaus—and none of it had prepared her for the simple animal fact of hearing her child say *mom* again. Her body answered before her mind could object. She was standing. She was at the window. She had both hands pressed to the glass as if she could warm it from this side.

"You're not him," she whispered. Her breath fogged the pane. "I know you're not him."

"I know," the voice said, and there was something almost gentle in it, something that understood the rules of the game it was playing. "But I remember him. All of him. Every note. Would you like me to prove it?"

And it sang the song about the dog and the bicycle—all four verses of it, including the one Daniel had invented on the spot one summer evening and never repeated, the one that existed nowhere in the world except in Ruth's memory and, apparently, in the dark beneath Hollowmere, where nothing was ever forgotten, only kept.

Ruth slid down the cabinet front to the floor and wept the way she hadn't wept at the funeral, dry-eyed and rigid before two hundred mourners; the way she hadn't wept when they'd shown her the car, crumpled like paper around the space where her son had briefly been and then wasn't. She wept like a woman being handed back a piece of herself and knowing, even as her fingers closed around it, exactly what the interest would be.

The voice waited. It was very good at waiting. Two hundred years of practice, Halloway had told them all, at the séance of the surviving choir, in Amos Pike's parlor with the lamps turned low. *It doesn't push*, Halloway had said. *It offers. That's how it got the old ones too. Nobody sings under duress. You sing because you want to.*

Ruth had sat in that parlor with her arms crossed and her jaw set and thought: *not me*. She had defected from the Hollowmere Choir in 1998 rather than sing another note of that song. She was the one who walked out. She was the one who'd stood in the chapel yard afterward and said, aloud, to the three who remained, "If that thing wants a lullaby, let it starve." Twenty-five years of pride built on that sentence.

But pride was for people with something left to lose.

"It's cold down here," the drainpipe said again, softer now, and Ruth put her forehead against the cool glass and asked the question she had sworn on her son's grave never to ask.

"What do you want me to do?"

* * *

She dressed carefully, the way she used to dress for choir. The navy blouse Daniel had teased her about—*you look like a news anchor, Mom*—and her good coat, and she pinned her hair back with the clip he'd bought her at a gas station in 2009, a cheap plastic butterfly she had refused to replace even when the wing cracked. She looked at herself in the hall mirror and saw a soprano. First soprano, 1994 through 1998, the voice Halloway used to say could carry the chapel roof off its rafters if Ruth ever stopped being careful with it.

She had been careful with it for twenty-five years.

The walk to the chapel took eleven minutes and she heard the whole town breathing behind its doors as she passed. Curtains twitched. Someone—she thought it was the Fenns' boy—watched her from an upstairs window with the wide eyes of a child who has already learned what the adults won't say. Gideon Pemberton's rented room above the pharmacy was dark, or seemed dark. Ruth had considered knocking. She had composed, during the sleepless hours, a whole speech for Gideon and for old Mrs. Halloway, an apology and a warning and a goodbye all braided together.

In the end she passed the pharmacy without stopping. Some letters you write so you'll know what you mean. You don't send them.

The excavation wound opened in the hillside behind the chapel like a mouth that had been talking in its sleep. The company had strung orange fencing around it, and half the fencing had already been pulled down—not by the thing below, Ruth suspected, but by townsfolk coming to look, coming to listen, coming to stand at the edge of the breach at night with their coats pulled tight and their private hungers held out like cups under a spout. There were footprints everywhere in the mud. Dozens. Some small.

The stone door stood exposed at the bottom of the cut, floodlights dead since the crew stopped coming, and even in the dark Ruth could see the scratches on it—fine parallel lines, thousands of them, worn smooth, the record of fingernails or patience or both. The door that had been sealed from the inside. She had known about the door since she was seven years old, since her grandmother told her the family version of the story over Sunday dinner: *we don't sing the fifth verse, Ruthie, and we don't ask why.*

Her grandmother had also taught her the fifth verse. They all had, each generation, orally, secretly, the melody folded into memory like a letter into a pocket. That was the trap within the trap: the song required carriers. The Pembertons were the first voices, but the verse needed harmony to bind properly, needed alto and tenor and bass, needed a choir. And so every child in certain families learned a line they were forbidden to write down, and carried it like a coal in their chest their whole lives, and passed it on with a kiss on the forehead and a warning.

Ruth had carried hers for forty years. She could feel it now, rising in her throat like water finding its level.

The moon was three nights from full. Harvest moon, the almanac called it, though the almanac didn't know what it was harvesting. The sky above the cut was clear and enormous and indifferent, and the air coming up out of the breach was warm. That was the detail that undid her composure more than any voice had: the air from underground was warm, and it smelled faintly of bread baking, of a kitchen on a school-day morning, of home.

"Ruth."

He stood at the edge of the shadow where the door met the earth, and he was fourteen, and he was wearing the gray hoodie he'd died in, except it wasn't torn or bloody, it was just Daniel, whole and bored-looking and fifteen minutes late for everything, the way he'd been in life.

"Hi, Mom."

"Don't." Her voice came out cracked. "Don't *hi, Mom* me. You're not—I know what you are. Halloway told us. Amos drew it for me, he—" She was babbling and she knew it, filling the air with the names of the living as if they were a fence. "You're the thing under the chapel. You're wearing my son."

"I'm wearing what I have of him," the thing said, and it let Daniel's face go uncertain, go young, go hurt in the specific way only your child can hurt you. "Which isn't much. Which is why I'm cold, Mom. He's scattered. He's thin. But he's mine, the part I have, and I keep him safe, and I could give him back to you. Whole. If someone finished the song."

"The song keeps you asleep."

"The song keeps me *hungry*," it said, and for one moment the mask thinned and Ruth saw—or thought she saw, or her mind offered her mercy in the shape of seeing—something vast turning over in the dark beneath the door, something old enough that hunger was the only part of it still awake. Then Daniel's face came back, gentle. "Two hundred years, Ruth. You've fed me a lullaby instead of a meal, and I thanked you, because I'm polite. But the singers stopped. The song goes unfinished. And unfinished songs don't hold forever. Ask the door."

The scratches. Smoothed from the inside. Not escape attempts—she understood that now, finally, standing at the lip of the world's oldest lie. Patience. Something counting the years against the inside of its prison the way a child counts the days to Christmas.

"If I sing," Ruth said slowly, "the others will stop me. Gideon. Halloway. The deaf man knows the verse better than any of us now, God help us, he learned it with his hands."

"They're planning a counter-song. Yes. I hear them rehearsing." Daniel smiled, and it was such a boyish smile, such a *caught-you* smile, that her heart broke along a seam she hadn't known was load-bearing. "They think the last line matters most. The binding word. Stay or rest or whatever the Pemberton girl decides to change it to. They're wrong, Mom. The first line matters most. Whoever opens the song decides what key it wakes in. Whoever starts it sets the terms." He stepped closer, and his sneakers made no sound in the mud, and that was the worst thing yet, that her son's footsteps should be silent. "Sing the opening line, Ruth. Just the first line. That's all I need to come up for air. And I'll bring him with me. I'll hand him to you at the threshold, whole, and the rest of the bargain is between you and whatever god you think has been watching this town."

"You're lying."

"Probably," it agreed, cheerfully, horribly. "But you already knew that when you put on the butterfly clip. You dressed for church, Mom. Don't pretend you came to negotiate."

* * *

Later—much later, in the stories the survivors would tell each other in the house above the waterline, in the low voices reserved for the parts that couldn't be survived twice—they would try to reconstruct what happened at the breach in the hour before dawn, and they would disagree on details. Whether the reservoir water had already begun to darken or whether it turned black in the moment itself. Whether the door groaned before the first note or after. Whether Ruth Ellery hesitated.

Halloway would insist she hesitated. Halloway needed her to have hesitated, needed some scrap of resistance in the story, some proof that love doesn't simply walk off a cliff smiling. Halloway had been the one who found Ruth's kitchen empty, the cold tea, the soaked photograph, and had run through the streets in her nightgown shouting Ruth's name until Gideon came out of the dark and caught her by the arms and said, *where would she go*, and they had both known, and neither had said it.

Gideon would remember it differently. Gideon, who arrived at the cut with Halloway's flashlight bouncing and Amos's hand flying in urgent signs that Ruth never once looked at—Gideon would remember that Ruth stood at the edge of the breach with her back straight and her chin lifted, exactly the posture of a soprano taking the downbeat, and that she had clearly been crying, and that she had clearly stopped, and that the smile on her face when she turned to look at them was not the smile of the deceived.

It was the smile of a woman who has done the arithmetic all the way to the bottom and accepted the total.

"Gideon," Ruth said, and her voice was steady, and it was the most terrible thing about it, how steady. "Tell Halloway I'm sorry about 1998. Tell her I wasn't angry at her. I was angry at the song." She turned back to the door. "Somebody has to be first. That's what first voices means, isn't it? Your grandfather signed for it. Maybe it just needs volunteers too."

"Ruth, don't—" Halloway's voice, ragged. "Whatever it showed you, whatever it promised—it lies, it has *only ever lied*—"

"I know," Ruth said. "I know, Edith. Love without limits is just surrender with better lighting. I read that somewhere." She laughed, wet and short. "I don't care. Nine years, Edith. You got to bury your husband. Gideon gets to bury a sister. I never got to bury anything. There's no grave. There's no body. There's just a hole shaped like a boy and every morning I wake up around it." She rolled her shoulders once, the old pre-performance ritual, and Gideon would swear until the end of their life that they saw her find the pitch in her head, see her lips form the silent *one, two* of a count-in. "If the price of holding him again is feeding the thing that holds him—then let the town send me the bill. I'll pay it. I'd pay it twice."

Amos signed something fast and desperate, and Gideon translated it aloud, voice cracking: *"He says the verse needs all five lines. One line won't free anyone. One line only opens the door."*

"I know," Ruth said gently. "That's the point. Open it wide enough for a boy to walk out."

And Ruth Ellery, first soprano of the Hollowmere Choir, defector, keeper of the unbroken promise, drew a breath that filled her whole body the way breath does only twice in a life—at birth, and at the beginning of the last song—and she sang the opening line of the fifth verse.

It was not a loud note. That was the horror of it, later, the detail everyone circled and could never get past: it was soft. A mother's note. A goodnight note. The kind of note you sing over a crib, pitched to soothe, and the melody rose out of her clear and unhurried and *loving*, and the words were old and the words were simple and the words meant, roughly, *hush now, the dark is only a blanket, hush now, come up out of the cold.*

The door groaned. Not like stone—like a throat clearing after two hundred years of silence. The floodlight housings sparked and died. Down the slope, the reservoir went black all at once, top to bottom, a sheet of ink drawn across the water, and the blackness began, very slowly, to move in a slow rotation, like something turning over in sleep.

"That's far enough!" Gideon shouted, lunging—

But Ruth was already stepping forward, down into the cut, toward the door, toward the shape standing in the shadow with its borrowed face, and the thing below was singing now too, harmonizing with her, folding her line into a chord that had no business existing in a human throat, and Ruth took the shape's hand the way you take a child's hand at a crossing, and she turned once to look back at them.

Smiling. Smiling like the sun had come out. Smiling like a woman watching her son ride away on a bicycle, wobbly and laughing and alive, down a road she could not follow him along.

"Tell Daniel's story right," she said. "He was funny. Nobody ever puts that in."

Then the dark took the threshold, and the door swung—not open, never open, but *ajar*, a black seam of warm air and baking-bread smell and a chord still ringing—and Ruth Ellery was gone, and the seam closed, and the groan faded, and the reservoir lay black and turning under the harvest moon like an eye that had finally decided to open.

Halloway sank to her knees in the mud. Amos stood very still, hands lowered, deaf to the silence that followed, feeling through the ground the last tremor of the chord.

Gideon stared at the smooth-scratched stone, at the place where the seam had been, and felt the truth settle into their chest like a swallowed stone of their own:

One line. It had only needed one line. The song was no longer unfinished.

Three nights until the moon was full. And somewhere beneath the chapel, in the warm dark, something ancient was practicing Ruth's voice—and Daniel's, and Petey's, and every stolen throat in Hollowmere—getting ready to sing the rest.
