## 14. Amos Who Cannot Hear It

Amos Pike woke, as he woke every morning, to silence.

Not the silence of the town — the town had never been silent to anyone but him. He had lived seventy-one years inside a quiet so complete it had a texture, a grain, like felt. Other people pitied him for it. Amos had long ago stopped pitying himself. Silence was a room he knew every corner of. Silence was the one place in Hollowmere where nothing could get at you.

Except that lately, he could feel it.

He felt it first in the floorboards, three days after Ruth Ellery walked out to the breach with her arms open and her mouth full of someone else's song. A tremor, low and patient, coming up through the joists of his little house on Chapel Lane like a heartbeat that belonged to the ground itself. He put his palm flat against the kitchen floor and waited. There it was again. *Dum. Dum. Dum.* Slow. Rhythmic. Almost gentle.

Almost like a lullaby.

Amos sat back on his heels and thought about Ruth. He had loved Ruth Ellery since they were sixteen, in the wordless way he loved most things — completely, and without any need to say so. He had watched her grieve her son for eleven years, and he had watched the thing under the chapel find that grief the way water finds a crack. He could not have heard what it offered her. But he had seen her face the last time they'd spoken, and faces were his music. Her face had been an instrument already half-played.

Now the ground was humming, and somewhere out there the reservoir crew was working night shifts by floodlights, and Petey Oakes sat on his porch across town humming with a mouth that wasn't entirely his, and Amos Pike — deaf, old, and the last clear-headed man in Hollowmere — got up, put the kettle on, and began to plan.

Because here was the thing the thing below had never understood about him, the thing even the choir had never fully grasped in fifty years of singing beside him: he had never needed the song in his ears.

He had always carried it in his hands.

* * *

Gideon Pemberton came to his door at dusk, looking like a person who had been awake for a week and was holding themselves together out of spite.

Amos saw them through the window before they knocked — saw the way they stood on his step with their shoulders up around their ears, the way they kept glancing at the ground as if the ground might answer them. He opened the door before they could knock. No sense letting them make a sound the night could steal.

"Gideon." He signed it, then said it aloud, his voice coming out in the flat, over-loud way hearing people always flinched at and Gideon never had. "Come in. Quickly. It's getting dark."

Gideon stepped inside and their hands started moving before their mouth did — fast, ragged signs, half of them half-formed. *The song. I found the hymnal. There's a fifth verse nobody wrote down. Halloway remembers pieces, I remember what Wren — * The signs tangled. Gideon stopped, pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes, and stood breathing in Amos's front hall like a swimmer who'd come up too fast.

Amos waited. He was very good at waiting. He guided them into the kitchen, sat them down, put tea in front of them, and only then touched their wrist — two light taps, *slow down* in the old Pike family signs, the ones his mother had made up because the school's formal signs were too slow for her temper.

Gideon looked at his hands. Then at his face. And something in them unclenched.

"You can't hear it," they said slowly. "The singing. It's been in the drains, in the well, in my floorboards. It's been wearing Wren's voice. And you can't hear any of it."

"No," Amos signed, and said it, because he wanted them to hear the word in his own flat voice, unhaunted. "It cannot reach me. It has tried."

Gideon went very still. "It's *tried* you?"

Amos nodded, and told it plainly, because Gideon deserved plainness and there was no one left in this town who gave it. Three nights ago. The humming in the floor. Then the humming had shaped itself, and he had felt it through his bare feet — felt the rhythm change, felt it begin to *spell*. It had learned, somehow, that his hands were his ears, and it had tried to speak to him the only way it could: by tapping. By pulsing up through the floorboards in patterns. Patterns that had slowly, horribly, arranged themselves into something like language.

*Come down,* it had tapped. *Come down, Amos. We will make you a world with no silence in it. We will give you the song whole. You have only ever had its shadow in your hands. Come down and hear it with your bones.*

"It offered you hearing," Gideon whispered.

"It offered me everything," Amos signed. "That is how I knew it was lying. Everything is always a lie. A true gift comes in pieces, and you spend your life fitting them together." He let his hands fall. "It does not understand me, Gideon. It has studied this town for two hundred years and it does not understand me at all. To it, deafness is a hole. A missing wall it can pour itself through. It cannot imagine that the wall was never missing. That I was never standing in a room with a hole in it. I was standing in a different room. A quiet one. And in a quiet room, you learn to hear things the loud world throws away."

Gideon's eyes were wet. "Then you're the only one it can't touch."

"I am the only one who can *teach*," Amos corrected. "There is a difference. Now. Show me what Halloway gave you, and what you remember, and we will see what is left of the fifth verse between the three of us." He paused, and then, because tenderness was a discipline he practiced like scales: "And then, Gideon, you will drink your tea. You cannot fight a god on an empty stomach. My mother said that. She was talking about the church committee, but it applies."

For the first time in days, Gideon laughed — a broken, startled sound, but a real one. Amos counted it a victory, and a beginning.

* * *

They worked for three nights.

The piano was the heart of it. Amos's mother's piano, an upright so old its soundboard had gone amber as pipe tobacco, stood in his parlor like an altar nobody worshipped anymore. But Amos worshipped it. He had learned music the only way available to him: he laid his forearms along the soundboard and let the wood speak into his bones. Every note was a small earthquake with a personality. Low C was a bear walking. High A was a wasp in a jar. His mother had taught him the hymns that way, her hand over his on the wood, tapping the rhythm against the back of his wrist while the felt-quakes rolled up his arms. *You don't need the air, Amos,* she'd signed. *The air is for other people. The wood is honest. The wood doesn't lie.*

The song below did not lie either. That was the worst thing about it.

On the second night, Gideon sat at the piano and played what Halloway had remembered of the fifth verse — four phrases, ragged, the oral thread frayed by twenty-five years of silence. Amos put his arms on the soundboard and felt the phrases move through the wood, and his whole body went cold.

Because he knew the melody.

Not from the choir. From *before* the choir. From his childhood, when his grandmother — who had sung alto in the Hollowmere Choir of 1946, who had stood beside old Pemberton the First Voice — had come to his parents' house and taught his mother a tune that was never to be written down, never to be sung aloud, only passed hand to hand, palm to palm, down the family line, in case. *In case of what?* his mother had asked. His grandmother's hands had answered: *In case the singers stop.*

Amos signed to Gideon to stop playing. Then he laid his hands flat on the soundboard, felt for the memory of the phrase, and began to tap it back — not the notes, but the *shape* of them, the rhythm and contour, the way his grandmother had taught it. Gideon watched his hands the way drowning people watch a rope.

"That's it," Gideon breathed. "That's — Halloway had the second phrase wrong. Or she had it right and the version she learned was wrong. Amos, where did you — "

"My grandmother. 1946. The same year your grandfather signed the agreement." Amos's hands moved carefully, choosing each sign like a man choosing stones to cross a river. "The choir kept two copies of the fifth verse, Gideon. One in the mouths of the singers. One in the hands of the deaf. They never told the singers about us. I think your grandfather understood something the others didn't. He understood that any voice can be stolen — he had seen it, whatever he saw down there in '46. So he gave the verse to the one people in town who could never be made to sing it. We were the vault. We were never meant to open it. Only to hold it."

"And now the vault opens," Gideon said. Their face had gone the gray of wet ash.

"Now the vault opens," Amos agreed. "Because the singers stopped, and the seal is failing, and a vault that is never opened is only a grave with better manners." He flexed his stiff fingers. "So we will do it properly. We will do it the way it was always meant to be done, at the end. Hand to hand. And you will feel every note in your bones before you ever give it your voice, so that when the thing below tries to wear Wren at you mid-verse — and it will, Gideon, it *will* — you will know the true melody in your body, in the wood, in the place it cannot counterfeit. It can copy a voice. It cannot copy a soundboard. It cannot copy your mother's hand on your wrist teaching you a rhythm when you were seven years old. Some things are only ever given once, and it was not there when they were given."

They worked. Phrase by phrase, Gideon's hands under Amos's hands, Amos tapping the rhythm against the backs of Gideon's wrists while the piano's felt-quakes rolled up through both their arms. The fourth phrase was the hardest — it turned where a melody should resolve, climbed where it should come home, and Gideon kept flinching from it, their signs going small.

"It wants to resolve," Gideon signed at midnight on the third night. "It's built to want to resolve. That's the trap, isn't it. Whoever sings it gets to choose the last word, but the melody — the melody decides how badly you'll want to choose wrong."

"Yes," Amos signed. "My grandmother's hands shook when she taught my mother this phrase. My mother told me that. She thought it was age." He looked at his own hands, spotted and steady on the amber wood. "It was not age."

* * *

Near dawn on the third night, Gideon fell asleep in the parlor chair, mid-sentence, their hands still half-raised in an unfinished sign. Amos draped a blanket over them and stood at the window, watching the light come up gray over the drowned fields that would soon be drowned properly, on purpose, by men with clipboards.

The ground was humming again. He felt it through the soles of his feet, patient as tide. *Dum. Dum. Dum.* And underneath it — fainter, farther, coming up through the water table itself — something new. A rhythm he recognized. A rhythm he had just spent three nights teaching into another person's bones.

It was practicing the fifth verse.

It had heard them. Of course it had heard them; the whole town was its ear now, every pipe and floorboard and flooded cellar. It was down there in the dark, wearing its chorus of the stolen, rehearsing the melody it had starved two hundred years for, getting it note-perfect.

But it was getting it *wrong*. Amos felt it clearly, the way he felt everything — in the grain, in the bones. The fourth phrase turned wrong under the ground. It sang the version Halloway had remembered, the frayed one, the one with the error in it. It had stolen its copy from the town's memory, and the town's memory was damaged goods.

It did not know about the piano. It did not know about his grandmother's hands. It was rehearsing a lie and did not know it was a lie, and when the harvest moon rose and it rose singing, it would sing the broken verse against Gideon's true one, and for the first time in two hundred years the thing below would be out of tune.

Amos stood in his silent kitchen and smiled, an old man's thin, fierce smile.

Then he felt the trucks.

Not heard — felt. The deep diesel tremor of heavy engines rolling up the valley road, one after another after another, too many, and stopping. He felt car doors. He felt boots, many boots, moving with the crisp synchronized purpose of men following an order rather than a conscience. He felt a generator start up by the reservoir office, a steady mechanical pulse nothing like a heartbeat.

Gideon stirred in the chair. "What is it?" they signed, bleary.

Amos kept his hands on the window glass a moment longer, reading the road through it like braille.

"Company trucks," he signed back. "A great many company trucks. They have stopped at the top of the valley." He turned to Gideon, and his hands slowed, making each word deliberate as a nail. "Whatever Corliss came here to do — he has stopped coming to do it quietly. Wake up, Gideon. Wash your face. We need to find out what a man does with that many trucks when what he really wants is for no one to ever leave Hollowmere again."
