## 22. Coda: A Visitor for the Choir

The water came in March, patient as anything that has already won.

Gideon stood on the ridge above old Hollowmere and watched it fill—the reservoir rising over the orchard first, then the roofs, then the chapel spire, which held out for two days like a man refusing to admit he was drowning before it went under without a sound. The company had wanted a ceremony. A ribbon, a plaque, a drone shot of the executive team smiling at their own reflection. Corliss had sent an invitation to every remaining resident of what the paperwork now called the Hollowmere Relocation District.

Nobody came. Gideon hadn't even opened theirs.

Now it was October again—harvest moon come and gone, another one waiting somewhere out past the winter—and from the ridge you could see the whole drowned village lying under forty feet of flat gray water, clear as glass on calm days. On those days, if you took one of Daley's boats out—and people did, though they never said why—you could look straight down through the cold bright column and see the chapel roof, green with silt, its door sealed shut from the inside. Fish moved through the streets where streets used to be. Sometimes the light hit the water just right and the whole village glowed up at you like something lit from within, like a lantern held underwater by a patient hand.

Gideon didn't take the boat out anymore. They'd gone down once, in June, with scuba gear borrowed from a state trooper who owed Halloway a favor, and had hung above the chapel door for four minutes looking at the smooth stone, the seam, the small brass plate their grandfather's generation had bolted there and no one had ever read aloud. Gideon had pressed a palm against it. The stone had been cold, and silent, and utterly ordinary, and Gideon had wept into their regulator anyway—not from fear but from the strange, unsharable grief of standing at a grave that was also a door that was also, somehow, a kept promise.

Then they had gone back up into the air they could no longer fill with singing, and gotten to work.

* * *

The new Hollowmere wasn't much. Twelve houses on the ridge above the waterline, a general store, a volunteer fire hall that doubled as everything else, including, on Wednesday evenings, a choir room.

It had been Halloway's idea, though she'd deny it. She denied most of her kindnesses the way other people denied crimes. It had started as an offhand remark in her kitchen the first week after the flood—"Someone ought to teach those children something besides television"—and Gideon, who could not answer, had simply stood up, found a pencil, and written on the back of a grocery receipt: *Wednesday. Fire hall. I'll do it.*

Halloway had looked at the receipt for a long time. Then she'd looked at Gideon—at the throat that worked silently when Gideon tried to speak, at the eyes that had learned to do all the talking the voice used to—and she'd said, "Well. Someone should," in the gruff voice she used instead of crying, and that had been that.

Twelve children came the first Wednesday. Then nineteen. Then twenty-six, because children talk, and what they said was that the quiet lady—or the quiet one; nobody in Hollowmere was quite sure anymore how Gideon preferred to be named, and Gideon had stopped minding—who lived up the hill with the deaf man and the old woman taught singing like nothing you'd ever seen. She couldn't sing a note. That was the miracle of it. She stood in front of you and moved her hands and her face and her whole body, and the sound came out of *you*, and it was better than anything you'd ever made alone.

Amos Pike sat at the upright piano they'd hauled up from the Ellery place, Ruth Ellery's piano, though nobody said that either. He tuned it by feel and played it by memory, and when the children sang he laid his forearm flat against the soundboard so the music came up through his bones, his eyes closed, his head tilted like a man listening to rain. He was the only adult in the room who heard the songs exactly as they were—pure vibration, no words, no memories stitched to them, no voices hiding inside them. Some evenings, after practice, he would sign to Gideon while the children ran wild in the fire hall: *Clean. Tonight it sounded clean.*

Gideon always signed back the same thing. *How can you tell?*

And Amos would shrug, and tap his ear, and grin his terrible gap-toothed grin. *I'm the only one here who can't be lied to.*

That was the arrangement now, the odd household above the waterline: Halloway, who cooked enough for six and complained about it nightly; Amos, who fixed everything that broke and heard nothing that mattered; and Gideon, who spoke in ink and gesture and had become, without meaning to, the thing their grandfather's signature had promised—a first voice, except inverted, a voice made entirely of everyone else's.

Some nights Gideon dreamed of singing. Not the lullaby—never the lullaby—but ordinary things, hymns and rounds and the nonsense song Wren used to hum while brushing her teeth, and in the dreams the voice that came out was rich and low and unmistakably their own, and Gideon would wake with tears drying in their ears and their hands already moving, shaping notes in the dark for no one, conducting the silence like a fool, like a widow, like a person keeping in practice.

For what, they didn't ask. You didn't ask questions like that in Hollowmere anymore. But you kept your hands warm just in case.

* * *

The hymnal was Gideon's project, the winter one, the long one.

The old books had gone down with the chapel—all of them, every copy, which Halloway swore was coincidence and Amos swore was not and Gideon privately suspected was neither, was simply the way of things below the waterline, where things ended when they ended and not before. So Gideon rebuilt it from scratch. They interviewed Halloway for eleven evenings, cross-checking her alto line against Amos's felt memory of the harmonies, testing each phrase against the piano until the soundboard told them the truth. They wrote out the descants. They transcribed the Easter canon and the harvest round and the two-part lament for the flooding of the mill, which had been written in 1911 and which, Gideon noted with grim satisfaction, was about the *last* time the valley tried to drown itself for progress.

Four verses. Every song in the book got its full text, its full music, its history in the margins in Gideon's careful hand. And every song stopped where it stopped, complete, finished, needing nothing.

There were rules, and Gideon wrote them inside the front cover where every child would see them before they saw a single note:

*Songs are finished things. If a song asks you for more than it has, put the book down and find me.*

*If you hear singing where no one is singing, do not learn the tune.*

*If someone offers to teach you a verse you've never seen written anywhere, run.*

The children thought the rules were a game at first, then a bore, then—slowly, as they grew old enough to notice how the adults went quiet around the waterline, how Petey Oakes still sat on his porch some evenings humming three notes over and over with wet shining eyes, unable to stop, unable to finish, unable to die of it—a serious thing. Hollowmere's children grew up fast that year. They learned the rules the way farm kids learn which fences carry current: early, thoroughly, and without needing to be told twice.

By spring the hymnal was done. Gideon had it printed at the shop in Carrow Falls, sixty copies, plain gray covers, and on the title page, beneath the name—*The Hollowmere Choir Book*—they printed a single dedication line, the only one they would permit themselves:

*For the singers, wherever they are resting.*

Wren's echo had gone out like a candle against Gideon's chest, and Gideon had spent a long dark winter learning the difference between a wound and a scar. This was the scar. It ached in cold weather. It let them work.

* * *

It was on a Sunday in late April, nearly a year to the week since the harvest moon, that Gideon found the fifth verse.

Practice had ended. The children had scattered down the hill toward supper, their voices thinning out along the road the way children's voices do, going home ahead of their bodies. Amos had carried the piano bench out to patch its leg. Halloway was in the kitchen of the house on the hill, and the smell of her bread had followed Gideon all the way back to the fire hall, where Gideon had stayed behind to pack up the hymnals—one of the small compulsions of the voiceless, this tidying, this putting-away, as if order could be conducted too.

They were stacking the last copies when they saw it.

The bottom copy. The demonstration book, the one Gideon used at the front of the room, its spine cracked soft from a year of handling. Gideon flipped to the back out of habit—to check the binding, to check the margins—and stopped.

On the final page, the empty page, the page Gideon had left blank deliberately, the way you leave a chair empty—in pencil, in a small, careful, childish hand, someone had written a verse.

Five lines. A melody sketched above them in wobbly notation, half-right, half-invented, the notation of a child who had been taught the rules of the staff but not yet its fears. And the words—

Gideon stood very still in the empty fire hall.

The words were not the old words. They were not *stay*, they were not the binding line, they were not the hunger dressed in hymn clothing. They were something else. Something small and plain and new. A verse about coming home at evening. About lamps in windows. About a choir that sings so that no one—*no one*, the child had underlined it twice—has to sing alone.

It was good. That was the terrible, wonderful thing. It scanned. It fit the harmonic ground the four verses had laid down, the way a seed fits soil it has never seen. Whoever wrote it had absorbed the whole book and answered it, the way verses have always answered each other across centuries, the way the living have always sung back at the dark—not to bind it, not to feed it, but to tell it what it could never be.

Gideon turned the page over. No name. Checked the cover, the spine, the flyleaf. Nothing. Asked Halloway at supper—wrote the question on a napkin—and Halloway shook her head and said half the children borrowed that book and half the adults too and weren't pencils free? Amos only tapped the page, once, gently, and signed: *Does it matter?*

And Gideon, sitting there between the deaf man and the old woman in the lamplit kitchen above the drowned town, understood that it did not, and that this was the whole lesson, the one their grandfather had died without learning, the one the thing below the chapel had starved two hundred years trying to steal: a song does not belong to whoever writes it down. It belongs to whoever needs it next. Some songs keep themselves. All you can do is leave the book open in the right room.

But—Gideon turned back to the penciled lines, and read them again, and felt the ache in the scar shift into something warmer—there was a question underneath the question. Because the melody was half-right. Because the child—whoever it was—had reached for a harmony they couldn't yet hear, and missed it, and the miss left a small open space at the end of the fourth line, a hole shaped exactly like a voice.

Like a first voice.

Gideon smiled. It surprised them, arriving; it had been a long time. Then they closed the book, softly, the way you close a door you intend to open again tomorrow, and set it on the piano, and went home through the spring dark to bread and lamplight and the two people who had become their family, while above the flooded valley the stars came out one by one, and far below the black water, behind a door sealed from the inside, something that had once demanded a fifth verse slept on without one—and would sleep forever, or until some child on a ridge above the waterline, humming a tune no one had taught them, decided otherwise.

The choir would meet Wednesday. There was a harmony to finish.

Gideon's hands were already warm.
