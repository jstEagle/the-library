## 2. The House That Counts Its Doors

The farmhouse announced itself before Theo could see it, the way grief announces itself before you understand what you're mourning. First came the smell—woodsmoke and cold earth and something sweeter underneath, like apples rotting in an orchard no one had tended. Then the sound: the tires on the gravel drive going quiet, as if the road itself had decided to whisper. Theo parked the car at the end of the drive because the drive was long enough to be a commitment, and walked the rest, duffel bag over one shoulder, the keys Edith Brightwater's lawyer had mailed in a padded envelope digging a familiar ache into their palm.

The house stood at the end of it like a held breath. Two stories, white paint gone gray at the corners, a porch that wrapped around the east side like an arm curled protectively over something sleeping. The windows were dark, but Theo had the strange, unshakable feeling that they were not empty—that the house was looking back with its lights off the way a person looks at you from a dark room, deciding whether to speak.

"You're it," Theo said aloud, to the house, to the lawyer's letter still folded in their pocket, to the whole ridiculous fact of inheritance. "I'm it. Whatever that means."

The house said nothing, which Theo would learn was its way of saying a great deal.

* * *

Inside, the air was warm.

That was the first wrong thing, though Theo didn't know to call it wrong yet. The house had stood empty for eleven months—since Edith's death in October, since the lawyer's slow machinery of probate had finally, in the first cold week of September, ground out Theo's name on a deed—and empty houses in autumn were supposed to be cold. This one was warm the way a body is warm. Not heated. Warm. As if the house had been holding its temperature all this time, waiting.

Theo set the duffel down in the front hall and stood in the dimness, listening. The silence had texture. Underneath it, so faint it might have been the blood in Theo's own ears, was a hum—not electrical, not plumbing. Something with a melody to it, or the memory of one, the way a word remembered is not the word itself.

The lights, when Theo found the switches, worked. All of them. Every bulb in every fixture, on the first try, in a house that had been vacant for the better part of a year. Theo stood in the kitchen with all four of its bulbs burning and felt something between gratitude and being watched.

That was when the notes started.

They were taped inside the cabinet doors. Theo found the first one reaching for a glass: a square of yellowed paper, the adhesive long dead, held in place by a strip of newer tape that someone—Edith, presumably, in her last months—had pressed over it with deliberate care. The handwriting was small and slanted and absolutely certain of itself.

*COUNT THE DOORS EVERY NIGHT BEFORE YOU SLEEP. THE NUMBER IS TWELVE. IF IT IS EVER THIRTEEN, DO NOT OPEN THE THIRTEENTH. DO NOT LOOK AT IT LONGER THAN YOU MUST. BLOW OUT NOTHING. SLEEP WITH A LIGHT ON.*

Theo read it twice, then laughed—one short, involuntary bark that the kitchen absorbed without echo. The laugh sounded wrong in the room, like a guest who'd come to the wrong party.

"Okay, Grandma," Theo said to the empty house. "Sure."

But Theo's hands, which had spent thirty-four years learning not to trust good things, were already opening the next cabinet.

* * *

There were nine notes in the kitchen alone.

They were taped inside cabinet doors, inside the pantry, under the sink, on the back of the door to the cellar stairs. Some were old—the paper gone soft and tea-colored, the ink faded to brown, the handwriting younger and less certain. Some were recent, written in the shaky but resolute hand of Edith's final year, on the backs of envelopes and the margins of a church bulletin and once, heartbreakingly, on the inside flap of a cereal box. The rules repeated. They accumulated. They were, Theo began to understand, less like instructions and more like a litany, the way a person prays the same prayer not because God forgets but because the person does.

*KEEP EVERY LIGHT BURNING FROM DUSK TO DAWN. A DARK WINDOW IS AN OPEN MOUTH.*

*NEVER SING PAST THE SECOND VERSE. IF YOU DON'T KNOW THE SONG, LEARN IT ONLY AS FAR AS THE SECOND VERSE. THE THIRD VERSE IS NOT YOURS.*

*THE FROST COMES IN OCTOBER. WHEN IT COMES, STAY INSIDE AFTER DARK. IT WALKS THE LINE. LET IT WALK.*

*THE MIRRORS ARE SLOW. THIS IS NOT A FLAW. DO NOT TRY TO FIX THEM.*

Theo stood in the kitchen with the notes spread across the table like a card hand, all the lights burning overhead, and read them until the words stopped being words. *The mirrors are slow. This is not a flaw.* What did that mean? Slow how? Theo looked up, reflexively, at the small mirror above the kitchen sink—a farmhouse mirror, spotted with age, framed in tin—and saw only their own face, tired, backlit by four insistent bulbs, looking back with an expression Theo couldn't quite read. Their own expression. Of course it was. What else would it be?

Outside, the light was going. September dusk came down over the fields like a lid.

Theo found the fuse box in the cellar and every breaker already on, and counted, without deciding to, the bulbs: kitchen, four. Hall, two. Living room, three. Downstairs bath, one. Upstairs, five more. Porch, two. Cellar, one that buzzed like a trapped insect. Seventeen lights, and Edith's notes said *every*, and so Theo stood in the middle of the kitchen with the last of the daylight dying in the windows and did not turn off a single one.

"No other home," Theo reminded the house quietly. "Remember that. You're what I've got."

The house hummed its almost-melody, and Theo chose to hear it as agreement.

* * *

The counting became the evening's ritual before Theo had agreed to it.

It happened like this: Theo unpacked—clothes into the wardrobe in the front bedroom, the one with the best light; the broadcast equipment into the small room off the kitchen that had clearly once been a pantry, its shelves still bearing the ghost-rings of canned goods; the microphone, the mixer, the laptop, the little red ON AIR sign that had hung in three apartments before this one and had never once meant as much as it was about to. The Night Line went live at midnight, every night, and had for six years, and tomorrow night—October first, the anniversary broadcast—Theo would do it from here, from the farmhouse, on the landline Edith had apparently kept for reasons the phone company could not explain and the notes did not mention.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight was for the doors.

Theo didn't decide to count them. That was the thing Theo would think about later, in the dark, in the weeks after—the way the counting happened *to* them, rose up out of the notes like water rising through chalk. The last note Theo read that first night was taped inside the hall closet, at eye level, where a person would see it every single time they reached for a coat:

*COUNT THE DOORS. ALL OF THEM. CLOSETS AND CUPBOARDS COUNT. THE CELLAR DOOR COUNTS TWICE. SAY THE NUMBERS OUT LOUD. IT LIKES TO HEAR YOU COUNT. IT IS LESS LIKELY TO COUNT FOR YOU.*

*It.* The pronoun sat in Theo's chest like a swallowed stone. Every other note named things plainly—frost, mirrors, lights, song. This one had an *it*, and the *it* was listening, and the *it* might count.

So Theo counted. Out loud, as instructed, feeling like an idiot and doing it anyway, because the handwriting had the authority of someone who had survived something. Front door, one. Back door, two. Cellar, three—and then again, at the bottom of the stairs, four, because the note said the cellar door counted twice and Theo was not going to be the Brightwater who found out why. Pantry, five. Hall closet, six. The three bedroom closets, seven, eight, nine. The cupboard over the stove, ten. The little door in the upstairs hallway that opened onto nothing—a knee-wall space, dust and rafters, eleven. The cellar's coal chute, which had a hatch and therefore, by the merciless logic of the note, counted, twelve.

Twelve doors. Theo stood at the top of the cellar stairs and said it out loud—"Twelve"—and the house seemed to settle, the way a dog settles when it hears its person's key in the lock.

Then Theo stood in the hallway, all the lights burning, and felt the other thing settle in behind the relief. Because here was the truth of the evening, the truth Theo kept setting down and picking back up: the rules worked. Not as superstition worked, not as a comfort blanket worked. They worked the way a fence works. Theo had walked into a house governed by a dead woman's handwriting and within four hours had been folded into its liturgy—counting doors, tending lights, keeping silence where the notes demanded silence—and the house had *responded*. The warmth. The hum. The way the silence after "twelve" had felt like being tucked in.

It felt like being loved. It felt, in exactly equal measure, like being kept.

Theo stood in the upstairs hallway, in the amber glow of five burning bulbs, and let both feelings be true at once, because there was no other home to go back to. The apartment lease had ended in August. The storage unit held everything else. The Night Line's listeners knew a voice, not an address. Edith Brightwater—stranger, grandmother, dead a year this October—had left Theo the only door that opened, and the door came with rules, and the rules came with a warmth that did not ask permission.

"Okay," Theo said to the hallway, to the eleven counted doors and the one that counted twice. "I'll play it your way. But I'm telling you now—I ask questions. That's the whole show. That's the whole me. You get questions."

The house hummed. Downstairs, in the pantry-turned-studio, the mixing board's standby light glowed a small patient red, like a held breath, like an open ear.

* * *

Sleep came hard and then came strange.

Theo dreamed of a song. That was all, at first—just a melody moving through the dream the way weather moves through a valley, and Theo knew in the dreaming way that it was the song the notes meant, the one with the verse that was *not yours*, and the dream was careful, was polite, offered only the first two verses and then folded itself closed like a hand. Theo woke at 3 a.m. with the melody intact behind the teeth, humming before fully conscious, and caught it—caught the hum rising toward something, a third phrase opening in the throat like a door—and stopped.

Stopped so hard it hurt. Sat up in the dark bedroom with every light in the house burning through the door seams, heart going, mouth closed around the unfinished phrase like a mouth closed around a secret.

The house was quiet. The house was warm. Somewhere below, the refrigerator cycled, the only honest machine in the building.

"Second verse," Theo whispered to the dark. "Got it. Noted. Never past the second verse."

The dark did not answer, but the dark, Theo noticed, was not entirely dark. The window over the fields held a thin gray light that was not dawn and not moonlight, and it lay across the frost-tinged grass in a line so straight, so deliberate, that Theo lay watching it for a long time, telling themselves it was a fence, a drainage ditch, a trick of the moon, anything.

It was September still. Frost didn't come until October. The notes were clear about that. *The frost comes in October. It walks the line.*

The line lay silver and patient at the edge of the yard, and Theo lay in the warm dark with the lights burning, and did not sleep again, and told no one, because there was no one yet to tell.

* * *

Morning came, and with it, practicality—the armor Theo wore best. Coffee. The kitchen. The notes, read again in daylight, where they were merely eccentric instead of terrible. Theo taped them back where they'd been, all of them, in their exact positions, and then—because a broadcaster's instinct is to document—photographed each one, and started a file on the laptop labeled *HOUSE*, and under it, a document labeled *RULES*, and typed them out in a neat numbered list that looked, Theo had to admit, completely insane. A list of rules from a dead grandmother, followed without question by a grown adult who hosted a midnight radio show. The listeners would love it. The listeners, Theo knew with a fond, tired certainty, had survived their own haunted houses; that was why they called, why they stayed on the line at 2 a.m. saying *I can't sleep either, tell me yours.*

The thought steadied Theo. Tomorrow night the show would broadcast from this kitchen, this warm and humming kitchen, and the insomniacs of three counties would drift in through the phone lines like moths, and the house would be full of voices if not of people, and voices were a kind of company Theo had always been able to live on.

Until then, there was the house to learn. Theo spent the day walking it, room by room, learning its grammar. The parlor with its sheet music on the piano, yellowed pages of a hymn titled only *Harvest Song*, the notation stopping—Theo checked twice, three times—after the second verse, the rest of the page blank, as if the composer had run out of ink or nerve. The front bedroom with its wardrobe that smelled of cedar and, faintly, of woodsmoke. Edith's room at the back, kept shut by Theo's own hand all morning, opened at last in the late afternoon: a narrow bed made with hospital corners, a Bible, a jar of pens, and on the nightstand, face down, one final note.

Theo turned it over. The handwriting was the last-year handwriting, the shaky resolute hand, and it was shorter than all the others, and Theo read it standing in Edith's room while the light went amber and then gray in the windows, and read it again, and felt the stone in the chest and the warmth around it both at once, care and captivity, inheritance and sentence, the whole doubled thing that was the Brightwater house:

*IF YOU ARE READING THIS, THE HOUSE IS YOURS AND YOU ARE ITS. I'M SORRY FOR BOTH. KEEP THE LIGHTS. COUNT THE DOORS. AND WHATEVER CALLS THE SHOW, CHILD—ANSWER KINDLY. THEY ONLY WANT TO BE HEARD.*

*—E.*

Theo stood a long time in the dimming room, the note in their hand, the hum in the walls rising by degrees as the dusk came down, and outside, at the edge of the yard, the silver line lay where it had lain all night—straight, patient, a little closer to the porch than it had been.

October was tomorrow. The show was tomorrow. Theo went down to the kitchen, turned on all four bulbs, and began to count the doors.
