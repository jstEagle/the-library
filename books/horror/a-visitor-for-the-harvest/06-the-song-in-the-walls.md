## 6. The Song in the Walls

Dev had driven past the Brightwater farmhouse twice before he found the nerve to turn in.

The gravel crunched under his tires like something chewing slowly, and the headlights swept across a porch that seemed to lean toward the road the way a listener leans toward a radio. He killed the engine and sat in the dark with his hands still on the wheel, listening to the tick of the cooling motor, gathering his excuses. *I was in the neighborhood* — a lie; nobody was in this neighborhood. *I wanted to meet you in person* — true, but insufficient, because what he actually wanted was to see whether Theo Brightwater looked like a man who heard what Dev heard.

For three weeks they had been comparing symptoms over the airwaves of *The Night Line*, that strange midnight broadcast where the callers were sometimes people who shouldn't have been able to call at all. Dev had listened to the show for two years before he ever worked up the courage to dial in himself. Insomnia made strange companions of strangers. You learned their voices the way you learned the voices of family.

And then Dev had told the microphone about his bathroom mirror — how it showed him lifting his razor a full second before his hand moved — and Theo's voice had gone very quiet on the other end of the frequency, quiet in the way of someone recognizing their own face in an unexpected window.

*"Mine does it too,"* Theo had said. *"Come to the house."*

So here he was, eleven days later, sitting in a borrowed car outside a farmhouse that looked, in the moonlight, like it was holding its breath.

He got out. The cold was the first thing — not autumn cold but something older, a cold with intention in it, pooling low along the ground like water finding its level. The frost had come early this year. It silvered the grass all the way up to the porch steps and then stopped, abruptly, in a line so clean it might have been drawn with a ruler. Beyond the line, the yard continued, dark and unfrosted, as if the season itself had been told where its jurisdiction ended.

Dev stood at the edge of the frost line for a long moment. Then he stepped over it, and felt nothing, and was almost disappointed by that.

The door opened before he knocked.

Theo stood in the frame, backlit by a hallway of lamps — every lamp, Dev would learn, always burning, always. They were shorter than Dev had imagined, or maybe just worn down by the hours they kept, with the kind of face that radio flattened into something ageless and slightly haunted. Up close, Theo looked like a person who had been awake for a very long time in the way that mountains have been standing for a very long time.

"You're Dev," Theo said. Not a question. "You look exactly like your voice."

"I don't know what that means," Dev said, "but thank you."

"It means come inside. It's almost midnight, and things get—" Theo paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—*chatty* after midnight."

* * *

The house was warm in a way that felt administered rather than natural, heat pushed through old vents by a furnace that groaned like a ship changing tack. Every surface held a lamp or a bulb: table lamps, floor lamps, a string of Christmas lights looped along a bookshelf in October, candles in jars on windowsills, unlit but waiting. The hallway walls were papered in a pattern of small yellow flowers that had faded to the color of weak tea.

"Grandmother's rules," Theo said, catching him looking. A sheet of lined paper was taped inside the open door of a hallway cabinet, the handwriting spidery and certain. *Count the doors nightly. Keep every light burning. Never sing past the second verse.* "She died before I was born. Never met her. Inherited the house anyway, rules included." Theo closed the cabinet gently, the way you'd close a casket. "I used to think she was just eccentric. Now I count doors."

"How many are there?"

"Twelve."

"And last night?"

Theo's jaw tightened. "Twelve. So far." They led him down the hall toward the kitchen. "You want coffee? I only drink it at night now. Daylight feels like it belongs to other people."

"That's exactly what insomnia is," Dev said, and Theo laughed — a real laugh, startled out of them, and Dev felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched since the mirror incident. This was why he'd come, he realized. Not for answers. For the sound of another person laughing at the same hour he was awake.

They sat at a kitchen table scarred by decades of rings from cups nobody remembered using. Theo poured coffee from a pot that had clearly been brewed more than once that evening, and for a while they talked the way Night Line listeners talked — sideways, obliquely, grief and wonder braided together. Dev told them about the mirror lag growing worse, four seconds now, five, until he'd covered the bathroom mirror with a bath towel and started shaving by feel. Theo told him about the frost line, and the figure that walked it, and Dev felt the hair rise on his arms even though the kitchen was warm.

"You saw it," Dev said. "Actually saw it."

"At each window it stops. Just stands there. Like it's taking attendance." Theo wrapped both hands around their mug. "The lights keep it out. Or slow it down. I don't know which anymore, and honestly I'm afraid to run the experiment."

Dev nodded, and then, because he'd driven four hours and there was no point pretending otherwise, he said: "There's something else. Something I didn't say on air."

Theo looked up.

"The humming." Dev set his mug down. His hands weren't quite steady. "It started the same week as the mirror. I thought it was my building at first — pipes, or the guy downstairs with his bass amp. But it's not mechanical. It's melodic. Three phrases, repeating. Always three. And it always—" he searched for the shape of it, "—it always stops like it's about to say something more. Like a sentence with the last word swallowed. I've caught myself waiting for the ending. Standing in my kitchen at 3 a.m., waiting, like an idiot, for a song in my wall to finish."

The silence that followed had texture. Dev watched Theo's face do several things at once — recognition, relief, and then a fear so fresh it hadn't yet learned to hide.

"When did it start?" Theo asked quietly.

"Three weeks ago Tuesday."

"The night of the anniversary broadcast," Theo said. "The night Marisol called."

* * *

They walked the house together after that, two insomniacs with flashlights they didn't need because every lamp was burning, hunting the sound the way you'd hunt a draft. The house was bigger than its exterior promised — farmhouses often were, rooms added by generations who needed somewhere to put their accumulating lives — and Dev felt the layout rearranging itself in his memory faster than he could map it. A parlor with a piano under a dust sheet. A stairwell with a banister polished pale by a century of hands. Doors, everywhere doors, and Theo touching each one lightly as they passed, counting under their breath, a rosary of carpentry.

"One," Theo murmured. "Two. Three..."

It was in the upstairs hallway that Dev heard it again.

At first he thought it was Theo — a hummed note, absent-minded — but Theo had gone rigid beside him, head tilted, eyes wide in the lamplight. And Dev understood that they were both hearing it, both frozen, both listening to the plaster.

It came from inside the wall. Not behind it — *inside* it, woven into the lath and horsehair the way a voice is woven into a telephone wire, present without origin. A woman's humming, or something shaped like a woman's humming, unhurried, patient, pitched in a register that Dev felt in his back teeth.

And he knew it. That was the terrible part. He knew the melody the way you know a song from childhood — not the words, never the words, but the *turns* of it, the places where the phrase leaned left instead of right. Three phrases. Rising, settling, rising higher. And then—

Then it stopped. Again. At the top of the third phrase, at the exact place where a fourth should begin, the humming simply ceased, mid-breath, and the silence afterward was so deliberate that Dev could have sworn the house was watching to see what he would do.

"Do you hear it?" he whispered, though he knew.

"Every night since I moved in," Theo whispered back. "I thought it was the wind in the chimney."

"It's not the wind." Dev pressed his palm flat against the wallpaper. The wall was cool and faintly, impossibly, alive beneath his hand — a vibration too regular to be settling timber, too structured to be anything accidental. "It's a hymn. I grew up singing in church choirs — ten years, soprano, then alto when it changed. I know hymnody. This has the architecture of a harvest song. Minor key, modal, old. Older than the radio. Older than the town, probably." He swallowed. "And it wants the fourth phrase. It's been stopping there for weeks, and every time it stops, it's asking."

"Asking what?"

"Asking someone to finish it."

The humming began again, and this time Dev counted the phrases against his heartbeat like a metronome. One. Two. Three. Stop. One. Two. Three. Stop. Patient as tide. And underneath his fear, treacherously, the musician in him was already reaching for the resolution — his mind supplying candidate notes the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. He caught himself humming a half-second of answer before he strangled it in his throat, and the wall went utterly, eagerly silent, as though it had heard him and was pleased.

"Don't," Theo said sharply, grabbing his wrist. Their grip was stronger than it looked. "Whatever you almost just did — don't. There's a rule. Edith's rule. *Never sing past the second verse.*"

"This isn't singing—"

"It doesn't know the difference!" Theo's voice cracked and dropped to a whisper. "I don't know what it knows the difference between. I've lived here two months and I don't know anything except that the rules work and breaking them costs something. My grandmother wrote those rules like someone who'd already paid for learning them."

Dev looked at the wall. The lamplight lay along the faded flowers of the wallpaper, and for one vertiginous moment he could have sworn the pattern had shifted — that the small yellow blossoms had arranged themselves into rows like notation, like a staff, like lines waiting to be filled.

"Why would a house want a song finished?" he asked.

"I don't think it's the house that wants it." Theo's voice had gone very flat, the professional calm Dev knew from the radio, deployed now like a shield. "I think the house is just... where it lives. Whatever it is. And I think the song is how it gets in. Or how it gets *us* out. I haven't decided which scares me more."

* * *

They ended up in the studio — the reason for everything, the room where the Night Line was born. It was smaller than Dev had imagined, a converted bedroom with acoustic foam going gray at the edges, a mixing board older than either of them, and a microphone on a boom arm that hung in the lamplight like something sleeping. The ON AIR sign above the door was dark, but the red glass of it seemed to hold a residual warmth, like a stove recently fed.

"This is where Marisol called from," Dev said. "The night she—"

"The night she called four days after she died. Yes." Theo sat down heavily in the chair before the board, and for a moment all the calm fell away and Dev saw the actual person underneath: young, alone, inheriting a haunted house and a haunted frequency in the same season. "I keep the channel open. Even off-air. I don't know if that's wise. I tell myself it's for the listeners, the living ones, the insomniacs. But some nights I think I just can't stand the quiet."

"The quiet's worse," Dev agreed. "The quiet is where the humming lives."

As if summoned, it started again — fainter here, filtered through two walls, but unmistakable. Three phrases. Stop. Three phrases. Stop. Theo closed their eyes. Dev found himself conducting it unconsciously, fingers twitching the phrase-shapes against his knee, and forced his hand still.

"There's something else," Theo said. "Something I found in the attic. A ledger. Names, dates of death, and a column marked 'paid.'" They looked up at Dev with an expression Dev had heard a hundred times through headphones but never seen: the face of a broadcaster deciding how much truth the audience can survive. "Every name in it has called my show, Dev. Every single one. The dead aren't haunting this house. They're *paying into it.*"

Dev stood very still. Above them, through the ceiling, through the attic floor, through whatever layers of wood and paper separated the living from the kept records of the dead, the house creaked once — a long, satisfied sound, like a throat clearing before speech.

And the humming changed.

It was subtle at first. Dev might have missed it if he hadn't spent three weeks mapping the melody's every contour. But the third phrase came around again and this time it didn't stop cleanly. It frayed. It lingered on its final note a half-beat longer, leaning forward, the way a singer leans toward the front row when they want to be sure everyone hears. And in the space where the silence usually fell, Dev heard — or imagined he heard, though he would spend the rest of his life unable to convince himself it was imagination — a single note, soft as breath, offered up like a first step onto a bridge.

Waiting to see if anyone would take the next one.

"It's closer than it was," Dev said. His mouth had gone dry. "In my apartment it stopped clean. Here it's— it's *asking* out loud now."

"Because you're here." Theo rose from the chair. "Because we're both here. Two people who've heard it, in the room where it broadcasts." They stared at the dark ON AIR sign, and Dev watched a thought move across their face like weather. "It doesn't want me to finish it. It doesn't want *you*. It wants an audience. It wants—"

The microphone on its boom arm turned, very slightly, with no hand near it, until the grille faced the doorway where they stood.

"—a choir," Theo finished.

From the walls, from the floor, from the piano under its dust sheet downstairs, from every direction at once and none, the humming swelled — three verses, complete, gorgeous, horrible — and stopped, one note short of the end, holding the silence open like a door.

Down the hall, in the kitchen, a light flickered. Just once. Just enough to be noticed.

Just enough to feel like applause.
