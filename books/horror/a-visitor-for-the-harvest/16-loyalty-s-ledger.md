## 16. Loyalty's Ledger

Marcus had driven four hundred thousand miles of American highway before he'd ever been trapped by a road, and he'd never once thought about what it meant to be the kind of man who couldn't leave. Truckers talked about home the way sailors talked about the sea—something you loved precisely because you were always leaving it. His rig had a bunk, a cooler, a photograph of his mother taped to the dash that had faded to the color of weak tea. Home was a direction, not a place. That was the whole trick of his life and he'd been proud of it.

Now the direction was a circle.

He'd tried it twice since finding his name in Edith's ledger—*Marcus Okafor, paid in full, October 14th*—and tomorrow's date sitting beside it like a headstone with the carving already done. Both times he'd walked out to the county road at dusk, keys in his fist, and both times the road had done its soft, patient thing: the pines sliding past, the mailbox at the end of the drive, and then the mailbox again, and the pines again, until he was standing at the porch with no memory of the turn. The second time, Ruthie had been waiting on the steps with a blanket over her shoulders, and she hadn't said *I told you so*. She'd just handed him a mug of the chicory coffee nobody liked and sat with him while his hands shook.

That was two days ago. Tonight the whole house was awake, and nobody was pretending otherwise.

* * *

They gathered in the kitchen because the kitchen had the most light. That was the rule Edith had left taped inside the cabinets—*keep every light burning*—and after the night the door count came back thirteen, nobody economized with the electricity anymore. Every lamp in the house blazed. The overhead fixture, a brass thing with frosted glass shades shaped like tulips, threw light into every corner, and still the corners seemed to hold their breath.

Dev sat at the table with Edith's ledger open in front of him and his laptop beside it, the screen glowing with the transcription he'd been building for a week—the harvest hymn, note by note, pulled out of the plaster walls like a splinter. He'd annotated it in three colors of ink. Marcus didn't understand half the markings, but he understood the shape of a man doing everything he could think of to keep from being afraid, because Marcus had done the same thing with logbooks and route maps his whole career.

Ruthie stood at the stove even though nothing was on it, her hand resting on the burner dial like it was a shoulder. Her husband's name was Gerald. She'd told them about him on the second night, over the dinner they'd cooked together out of spite and grief—how he'd died in March, how she'd called the Night Line every Friday at 2 a.m. just to hear a living voice say her name, how she'd made her bargain with the house before anyone could stop her. *Let him stay, and I'll sing the third verse.* The lights had flickered. The house had said yes. And now the third verse sat in all of them like a swallowed stone.

Theo stood by the window with their arms crossed, watching the frost line. The first frost had come and gone; the second was due any night now. Out past the porch, the property line was still a hard edge of white against the dark grass, the border where the frost stopped like it had hit glass. Nobody talked about the figure that walked it. Everybody thought about it constantly. Marcus could feel it in the room, the way you could feel a storm front in your bad knee.

"Okay," Dev said, and pushed the ledger toward the middle of the table. "I've been through it three more times. I want everyone to look at this with me, because I think we've been reading it wrong."

Marcus pulled the ledger close. The columns were as neat as a bank book—names, dates, and that third column in Edith's small, upright hand: *paid*. He'd looked at his own entry so many times he could draw it from memory. The ink of the date was fresher than the ink of the name. Edith had known he was coming before he'd known it himself, and she'd written it down anyway, the way you'd write down a train schedule.

"Read the dates," Dev said. "Not the names. The dates."

Marcus read. The names went back decades—callers, all of them, every name a voice he'd heard through the Night Line or a voice Theo had told him about. But the dates clustered. Four names in the winter of 1987. Two in the spring of '94. A gap of eleven years, then five names in a single autumn.

"She collected in bunches," Marcus said slowly.

"She collected when the song was sung," Dev said. "I cross-checked. Every cluster matches a documented performance of the hymn. The 1987 group—there was a church harvest festival that October, Edith directed the choir. The '94 pair—her niece's wedding, they sang it as a processional. Every time the third verse got finished, the ledger filled in." He tapped the page. "And look at what *doesn't* happen. When six people sing it, six names get paid. Not one name getting six times heavier. Not one person absorbing the whole debt. It divides. It *dilutes*."

The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled with a sound like a sigh.

"That's not what it feels like," Ruthie said quietly.

"I know what it feels like," Dev said. "It feels like a trap with our names on it. But Ruthie—look at the math. The house doesn't want a singer. It wants *a* singer, singular, because a solo contract is enforceable. One voice, one debt, one clean transaction. That's why the frost picks one of us. That's why Edith's ledger is a collection route and not a graveyard—because one person at a time has been carrying this for sixty years, and it's been enough. It's always been enough." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and ferocious. "But if it's never been enough—if we make it too much—"

"Then we all sing it," Marcus said.

The words came out of him before he'd decided to say them, the way the truth always came out of truckers at 3 a.m., in diners, to strangers they'd never see again. He stood up. His chair scraped.

"We all sing it," he said again, steadier. "All of us. The third verse, together, tonight, before the frost reaches in and picks somebody. You want to talk about the ledger? The ledger says the debt divides. Fine. Then let it divide six ways. Let it be so spread out the house can't collect on any single one of us. It's a contract, right? Contracts need terms. So we change the terms." He looked around the kitchen, at each of them. "I'm already paid in full. I've got tomorrow's date sitting next to my name. I've got nothing to lose and a road out there that loops back to this porch. So I'm not asking anybody to do anything I'm not standing up and doing first. We sing. All of us. Together."

The silence afterward had texture. Marcus could feel it against his skin, the way you felt the silence after a crash on the interstate—everyone alive, everyone recalculating.

It was Theo who moved first, and Marcus watched them come to the table like a man watching weather change. They put their hand flat on the ledger, over the columns, over the decades of names.

"It could work," Theo said. "The math works. Dev's right about the structure. A solo contract can't survive a chorus, because a chorus isn't one party, it's—" They stopped. Marcus saw them swallow something. "It's a family. That's what it is. Six voices is a family singing, and the house was built for one heir at a time. It's built on the idea that we're alone. That's the whole architecture of it. The rules, the mirrors, the frost—it all assumes one person in the dark, keeping the count, keeping the lights, keeping the song. It never once considered that we'd all be in the same room."

"Because Edith never let anyone be in the same room," Dev said softly. "Look at her handwriting. Every entry, same hand. Sixty years, one hand. She did this alone because she thought she had to."

"And we don't," Marcus said.

"No," Theo said. "We don't."

And then Ruthie turned around from the stove.

She didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice. She was a small woman with white hair and hands that had spent forty years doing things for other people, and when she turned, she turned the way a door turns—slowly, and all the way.

"No," she said.

"Ruthie—" Theo started.

"You heard me." Her hand came off the burner dial. "I'm not singing it with you. I'm not singing it *at all* with you. My bargain is made. Gerald stays because I pay. That was the trade. That was *my* trade, made with my grief, and it's mine to keep." Her chin trembled and her voice didn't. "You want to split the debt six ways? Then what happens to him? You think the house refunds him because we found a loophole? You think Gerald gets to stay in this house, near me, because five other people sang? No. The bargain was *my* voice for *his* staying. If you all sing it, the contract dissolves, and he dissolves with it. I've had two weeks with him again. Two weeks of his voice in the radio and his footsteps on the stairs. I am not going to be the reason it ends early, and I am not going to let *you* be the reason either."

"Ruthie," Marcus said, gentle, the way you talked to someone standing too close to the edge of a loading dock. "He's dead, honey."

The words were out and immediately he wished them back, but Ruthie didn't flinch. She looked at him with something worse than anger—with pity.

"So is Marisol," she said. "So is every caller Theo ever took on that show. So, in about a day, are you, Marcus, if that ledger's honest. Dead isn't the question. Dead is the *given*. The question is what we owe the ones who are still here, in any form, and I'm telling you what I owe. I owe Gerald a kept promise. You don't get to make my debts communal because it's convenient for yours." She looked at Theo, and her voice cracked at last, just one hairline fracture. "You of all people. You spent a year taking calls from the dead because you couldn't stand to hang up on them. Don't hang up on him."

Theo's face did something complicated and then went still.

* * *

They argued until 2 a.m., which was fitting, because the Night Line had always lived at 2 a.m., and this house had learned its hours from the show.

Dev argued structure: the ledger, the clusters, the dilution. He laid out the entries in a timeline on his laptop and made them look at the arithmetic of it, and the arithmetic was beautiful and cold, and Ruthie sat with her back to it.

Marcus argued loyalty, which was the only scripture he had. "We found each other," he said. "Insomniacs and widows and truckers and a night-radio host, and we found each other because of a show about the dead, and now we're going to let the dead divide us? That's exactly what the house wants. A solo singer. One at a time. Alone in the dark with the rules. That's been the design for sixty years and it's worked every single time, and the only thing that's never been tried—" his voice rose, cracked, rose again— "the only thing this house has never once had to face is *all of us at once, refusing to be separated*."

"And the only thing Gerald has never once had is more time," Ruthie answered, "and you want to take even that."

Dev argued truth, last of all, when the others had run out of angles. He closed the laptop. He put both hands flat on the table the way Theo had put a hand on the ledger, like you could hold a thing down by touching it.

"Ruthie," he said. "I was a choir kid. Did you know that? Church choir, twelve years old, and I loved it more than anything, and then my voice changed and it cracked in the middle of a solo and I never sang in public again. Thirty years. So believe me when I tell you I know what it is to want one thing back that's gone." He took a breath. "But the ledger doesn't lie. I've checked the ink, the dates, the clusters. Sharing doesn't dilute anything—*not sharing* is what concentrates it. Every death in this book happened because one person carried the whole song alone. Edith. Her mother, probably. Whoever came before. One voice, one debt, one generation, forever. That's not a contract, Ruthie. That's a conveyor belt. And we're standing on it arguing about who gets to fall off first."

"The third verse is a price," Ruthie said. "Prices are paid by someone."

"Or they're refused by everyone," Dev said. "That's the other option. It's always been the other option. Nobody's ever tried it because nobody's ever had anybody standing next to them."

Ruthie looked at him for a long time. The tulip lamps buzzed. Upstairs, faint and terrible, the plaster hummed the melody it always hummed—two verses, stopping at the edge of the third like a child at the lip of a diving board, waiting, wanting, patient as frost.

"I made a bargain," Ruthie said finally, and her voice was small now, and human, and it broke Marcus's heart more than any shouting would have. "I made it alone, in the dark, because I was alone in the dark. That's the truth of it. That's what you're all forgetting. I didn't come to this house to be saved. I came because I dreamed the song, and I thought—I thought if I could just get close to where the singing came from, I'd hear him better." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way women of her generation did grief, like wiping down a counter. "I'm sorry. I love every one of you. And I'm not singing."

* * *

Marcus went out to the porch after, because the house felt too small for what was in it, and because he wanted to look one more time at the road that wouldn't let him leave.

The frost line glowed faintly under the moon, a white seam in the dark grass, precise as a ruled line on a page. Beyond it, the county road ran straight and black toward the interstate and every place he'd ever slept that wasn't here. He stood at the rail and did the thing he'd done a thousand times in a thousand parking lots: he thought about the route. The route was simple. Down the drive, left on the county road, eleven miles to the highway. He could drive it in his sleep. He had, practically.

The road would loop. He knew it now the way you knew a knee was bad. But he stood there anyway, because a man who'd spent his life leaving owed it to himself to keep trying to leave, even knowing.

Behind him, the screen door opened. He expected Dev, or Theo. It was Ruthie, wrapped in her blanket, and she stood beside him at the rail without a word for a while, two people looking at a road that went nowhere.

"I meant what I said," she said.

"I know."

"But I want you to know why I'm scared of your idea, and it's not the math." She pulled the blanket tighter. "It's that if we all sing it together, and the debt divides, and it *works*—then the house is broken, and Gerald goes. And if it doesn't work, then we've spent our one chance at defiance on a gamble, and the frost comes in, and it picks somebody anyway, and we'll never know if we could have saved them by giving them up." She looked at him, moonlight on her face, her eyes wet and clear. "Either way, Marcus, somebody pays. That's the truth of what this house demands. I'm loyal to the truth of it. You're loyal to us. And I don't know which one of us the house is counting on."

Marcus looked back at the frost line, and his breath stopped.

The frost had moved.

Not much. An inch, maybe two—the white seam in the grass had crept toward the porch, and at its new edge, the frost wasn't lying flat. It was rising. Curling up the first porch post in thin, deliberate lines, branching, forking, and even from here, even in the dark, Marcus could see what the lines were.

Notes. Staves and notes, climbing the wood like ivy, writing themselves toward the door.

"Ruthie," he said. "Get Theo. Get everyone. It's starting."

The frost wrote on, patient as a ledger, and somewhere inside the walls, the house began—very softly, for the first time—to hum the third verse.
