## 1. The Songs They Won't Sing Twice

The bus to Hollowmere ran once a day, and it was late.

Casper Thornbury watched the fens slide past the window — flat country, black ditches, reeds standing in the water like a congregation that had been told to wait. October had gone grey at the edges. The driver, a heavy man with a radio murmuring football scores behind him, said nothing for forty minutes, and then, without turning around: "You'll be the Thornbury one."

"I will," Casper said.

"Thought so." He didn't explain how he'd thought so, and Casper didn't ask. Small places made their deductions from small evidence: a suitcase with a recorder case strapped to it, a face that belonged to nobody's memory of anybody local. He let them off where the tarmac gave up and became gravel, and the bus pulled away trailing diesel and commentary on the match, and then there was just the sound of the fens, which was mostly wind and water moving in places you couldn't see.

Casper had been coming to places like Hollowmere for eleven years. Folk songs lived in these pockets — drained land, dying villages, pubs with one fire and four regulars who remembered things nobody had written down. The work was patient. You bought the first round, you learned the names of the dogs, you asked about weather before you asked about songs, and eventually some old man would decide you were harmless enough to sing for. Casper was good at it. Good at being harmless, good at waiting, good at the little machinery of trust. It was a skill their grandmother Edith had apparently possessed too, though she'd never spoken of it; it was only after her death in March, and the solicitor's letter, and the key on a loop of red wool, that Casper learned she'd grown up here, on Marsh Lane, in a cottage with a lean-to scullery and an apple tree gone wild in the back.

The plan was simple. Six weeks in the house, recording whatever the village still carried. A chapter on fen harvest songs for the book. Maybe, if the material allowed, something personal — *the songs my grandmother knew* — which the publisher would like, sentiment being a reliable currency.

Tam Weller was in the churchyard when Casper walked through it, because Tam Weller was always in the churchyard; Casper would learn that later. He was eighty-something and built like a fence post that had decided against falling, and he was cutting grass off a grave with a pair of hand shears, snip by snip, with the concentration of a surgeon.

"You'll be wanting me to sing," he said, without looking up.

Casper stopped. "I hadn't—"

"Everyone comes for me. I'm the last one that knows them proper." He straightened up, pressed a knuckle into his lower back. "You can walk me home and buy me tea and I'll give you one. One song. And then we'll see."

*We'll see*, Casper wrote in the notebook that evening, underlined twice, turned out to mean *never*. But that came later.

They walked down lanes where the hedges grew over the path and the ditches ran quick and dark from recent rain. Tam named the dead as they passed the graves — not mournfully, the way you'd read a war memorial, but the way you'd check a list. *That's the Aldous girl. That's old Petch and his two boys, all three inside a year.* He said it the way other men said *that's the post office, that's the chapel.*

At his kitchen — low-beamed, brown with decades of pipe smoke, a paraffin heater ticking — Tam took his time. Tea first. Then he made Casper show him the recorder, turn it on, play back ten seconds of his own voice saying the date, and listen to it with his head cocked like a bird.

"That's me," he said, satisfied or disturbed, hard to tell. "Right then."

He sang *The Reaping of the Nine* sitting down, hands flat on his knees, eyes fixed on a spot on the opposite wall as if the words were written there. His voice was a dry, cracked thing, half-spoken, and it shouldn't have worked, and it did. The song was a harvest riddle: nine men cut the field, nine men bind the sheaves, and when the counting's done there are ten men walking home, and the tenth has no shadow, and no one speaks to him, and at the gate the ninth man turns and says, *come in, come in, there's a place laid for you,* and the song ends there. No verse explaining. No chorus resolving it. Just the invitation, and silence.

Casper sat very still while the tape hissed.

"That's the whole of it?" they asked.

"That's the whole of it." Tam poured himself more tea he didn't drink. "There's a second half."

"There usually is."

"There is. And I'm not singing it." He said it flatly, the way you'd decline a second helping. "Nobody sings it twice. First time's for keeping. Second time's for calling."

"For calling what?"

Tam looked at them properly for the first time. He had pale eyes, the colour of ditchwater, and they went through Casper and out the other side, checking something behind them.

"You're Edith's grandchild," he said. "She could've told you herself, if she'd ever come back. Forty years she stayed away. Forty years, and now the house wants somebody in it, and along you come with your little machine." He nodded at the recorder. "Turn that off."

Casper turned it off.

"The house on Marsh Lane," Tam said. "It's yours now, I expect. Solicitor's letter and all. Well. My advice is sell it. Sell it sight unseen, to a developer, to anyone from more than twenty miles away, and don't sleep a night under that roof. There's folk here that'll tell you the same, and folk that won't tell you anything at all, and both lots mean well. Or mean as well as they can."

"Why?" Casper kept their voice light, interested, the scholar's voice. "What happened there?"

"Nothing happened there." Tam gathered the teacups with great care. "That's the trouble with it. Nothing ever does."

He saw them to the door. The evening had come down cold and blue over the fens, and somewhere across the flat dark fields a wader called, three notes, descending.

"Your gran was a good woman," Tam said, from his doorway, into the dusk. "Remember that, whatever you find out. She did what she did for love, and it worked, and it cost. That's all I'll say, and I've already said too much." He paused. "Don't ask the others. Let it lie."

The cottage on Marsh Lane smelled of dust and lavender and, underneath, faintly, of apples — the wild tree pressing its branches against the kitchen window glass. Casper spent the first evening doing the ordinary archaeology of inheritance: opening drawers, finding string and receipts and a tin of buttons, running taps that coughed air before water. The electricity worked. The bed in the front room, where Edith must have slept on her rare visits home, was made up with a blanket still folded in its shop creases, as if she'd prepared it long ago for someone she expected.

Casper found this touching rather than strange, and recorded a note to that effect on the dictaphone, standing in the middle of the kitchen: *"Arrived Hollowmere, 14th October. House sound, dry, unoccupied but maintained — someone has clearly been airing it. Local informant T.W. provided one song, 'The Reaping of the Nine,' variant incomplete, informant declined further material. Attitude toward the family property notably fearful — classic protective superstition, cf. the Wiltshire material, 2019."*

Their own voice sounded cheerful and slightly too loud in the empty rooms. Casper smiled at themselves, played it back, filed it away.

Superstition was the wallpaper of every village they'd ever worked in. Fear of the thirteenth field, fear of whistling indoors, fear of naming the drowned. It wasn't nonsense, exactly — it was history wearing a mask, memory with the facts rubbed off. Somewhere under Tam's refusal there was a real event: a death, probably, a bad winter, a family shamed. Songs like *The Reaping of the Nine* grew on graves. Find the grave, find the date, and the fear would resolve into something sad and human and explicable, and go into the book as a footnote.

That was the trade. You took people's hauntings and gave them back to them as history, and everyone slept better.

Casper slept badly anyway, that first night, though they blamed the unfamiliar mattress and the wind getting into the chimney. Once, near three o'clock, they surfaced from a thin sleep with the impression of having heard a chair scrape on flagstones downstairs — one short drag, deliberate, like someone drawing up a seat. They lay listening for a long time. The house ticked and settled. Nothing else came, and by morning they'd filed it under *dreams, ordinary,* and got up to make tea in a kitchen where the light came green through the apple leaves, and everything was exactly as they'd left it.

Except, they noticed idly, rinsing their mug — the mug they'd used last night, left in the drainer — except that there were two mugs in the drainer now, and Casper had only brought one out of the box marked KITCHEN.

They stood looking at it for a moment. Old house. Old cupboards. Things migrate; things surface; previous lives leave sediment. Edith had owned dozens of mugs once, presumably. This one was brown, chipped at the lip, and dry.

Dry, Casper noted, and put it back in the cupboard, and told themselves the small cold feeling at the base of their throat was just the wind off the fens finding a new way in.

In the front room, stacked on the table the solicitor had left, waited the photograph albums — eleven of them, cloth-bound, Edith's handwriting on the spines going back to 1951. Casper had been saving them for a rainy day, a proper session, tea and gloves and the good lamp.

Outside, the rain arrived on schedule, soft and endless on the apple tree.

Casper made the tea — one mug — and opened the first album, and began, with the comfortable detachment of a scholar among documents, to look at their grandmother's life.
