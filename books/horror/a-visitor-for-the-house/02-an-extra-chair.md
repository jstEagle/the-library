## 2. An Extra Chair

The albums were in the front room, in the cabinet with the glass doors that Edith had kept polished until the wood went pale at the handles. Casper found them on their second morning in the house, after a night of listening to the fen make its small wet noises and deciding, around three a.m., that the noises were only the house settling into its own long memory.

There were eleven of them. Leatherette covers gone soft as cloth, spines cracked, each one labelled in Edith's upright hand: *Hollowmere 1961–68*, *Hollowmere 1969–75*, and so on through the decades like the rings of a tree. The last one was barely half full. It ended in March, four months before she died.

Casper made tea, set up the good lamp, and began the work they had actually come to do — or told themselves they had come to do, between the songs. Folklorists are archivists first; the singing is just what the archive does out loud. They photographed each page before touching it, logged dates, noted who appeared where. A discipline. A procedure. Something to do with the hands while the mind wandered.

The Thornburys were everywhere. Edith young, then less young, then old, always slightly apart from whatever group she stood in, as if she'd been pasted in from another photograph entirely. Casper's mother as a girl, gap-toothed and furious about something even then. And always, always, the kitchen table.

It was a big table, scrubbed pine, pushed against the wall under the window so the light fell across it. It was still there downstairs; Casper ate breakfast at it every morning. In the photographs it held birthday cakes, funeral ham, seed trays, a sewing machine, a Christmas goose. Ordinary table doing ordinary work.

And at its end, in nearly every picture, a chair.

Casper noticed it the way you notice a word repeated too often in a paragraph — first as rhythm, then as irritation, then as fact. A wooden chair, high-backed, plain, no cushion, standing at the end of the table nearest the door. Not pushed in. Not pulled out. Just *there*, squared to the table's edge with a precision that felt less like furniture and more like etiquette.

They flipped back to 1961. There it was. Forward to 1987. There. 2003, Edith's seventieth, everyone crammed along the sides laughing, and the chair at the end, empty, patient, catching the flash.

"Huh," Casper said, to the empty room, in the tone of a scholar who has found an interesting variant of a ballad.

Because here was the thing — and they checked twice, got up, walked downstairs, and looked at the actual table, which sat against the actual wall with two chairs tucked under it and a third leaning against the dresser holding a coat. No fourth chair. No high-backed wooden chair anywhere in the cottage. Casper had walked every room by now. They'd have remembered it.

So: a chair that had been removed from the house at some point but lived on in the photographs. That was fine. That was *interesting*. Families retired furniture all the time; grief attaches itself to objects, and someone — Edith, probably — might have wanted the chair gone without wanting it erased. Photographs keep what we discard. Casper wrote a note in the margin of their log: *chair removed c. ? — sentimental retention in family imagery? Ask Tam.*

They went back upstairs feeling pleasantly occupied. This was the part of the work they loved, honestly more than the songs: the small wrongness in the record, the detail that didn't fit, the thread you could pull until a whole hidden pattern came up. They'd built a career on such threads.

It took them until the last album — the half-full one, the recent one — to stop enjoying it.

Because there was Casper in it. Last week. The day they'd arrived, camera around their neck, taking pictures of the house for their own records the way they always did at the start of a residency: the gate, the garden, the kitchen. They remembered taking this one exactly. They had set the shot deliberately — the table under the window, the two chairs, the light coming off the mere. A clean establishing image. Two chairs. They would have sworn to it on their degree.

In the photograph there were three.

The wooden chair stood at the end of the table, squared to the edge, catching the window light. It had not been there when Casper pressed the shutter. They knew it the way you know your own signature. They had framed that image, composed it, chosen it — and the chair had not been in the frame, because the chair had not been in the room.

Casper sat very still for a moment, the album open on their knees, the tea going cold beside them.

Then they did what scholars do with the impossible: they looked for the boring explanation. Old film? No — digital. Lens flare shaped like a chair? They zoomed in until the pixels broke apart, and the chair stayed a chair, grain for grain, with a shadow under it falling the correct direction for the window light. Reflection? Of what, standing behind them?

They went downstairs and took the same photograph again. Table, two chairs, light. Checked the screen immediately: two chairs. Took another. Two chairs. Stood there in the kitchen with the camera raised, heart going oddly fast, and understood that they were now the kind of person who checks.

That night they dreamed of the table, though when they woke they couldn't say what had happened in the dream, only that someone had been sitting very straight at the end of it, waiting to be introduced.

* * *

Hollowmere answered questions the way the mere answered stones: with a surface that closed over everything.

Casper tried the shop first, because Mrs. Petchy at the till had been chatty on day one about the price of paraffin and the state of the bus service. Casper laid the printed photograph on the counter between the sweets and the batteries — the kitchen, the table, the chair — and asked, lightly, conversationally, whether anyone remembered when the family had gotten rid of that nice old chair.

Mrs. Petchy looked at the photograph for perhaps a second and a half. Then she looked at Casper, and her face did something Casper had seen once before, years ago, on a woman in a Norfolk village being asked about a drowned church bell: nothing at all, elaborately, with effort.

"Don't recall any chair," she said, and rang up Casper's milk and bread, and turned to serve the next customer, who had materialized with the speed of a man who'd been waiting for his cue.

At the pub it was worse, or rather the same, which amounted to worse. Casper bought a pint and showed the photograph around the snug with a smile and a folklorist's easy manner — *family curiosity, doing up the house, wondered about the old furniture* — and watched a whole room of people develop sudden interest in their drinks. One old fellow, mid-sentence about drainage, simply stopped talking, the sentence dying in his mouth like something he'd decided not to feed. The landlord collected Casper's glass with a touch more care than necessary, the way you handle something that might be hot.

Only Tam Weller, cornered by the fire later, gave them anything, and it wasn't much.

He looked at the print for a long time. He didn't pretend not to see the chair. He looked at it the way you look at a face in a crowd that you hoped you wouldn't recognize.

"Your gran kept that house," he said at last. "Kept it proper."

"Kept it how?"

Tam put the photograph face-down on the table between them, gently, as if tucking it in. "You'll want to leave the albums be," he said. "Cataloguing's all very well for songs. Some things aren't songs." He drained his pint and stood, joints cracking like green kindling. "She kept it forty year, your gran. Don't go undoing her work over a bit of furniture."

"Tam—"

But he was already moving for the door, and the whole pub watched him go, and then, in the manner of villages everywhere, pretended it hadn't been watching at all.

* * *

Back at the cottage, dusk coming down blue over the mere, Casper spread the prints across the kitchen table — the actual table, the one with two chairs — and arranged them chronologically, 1961 to last week, and made themselves look properly.

The chair never changed. That was the thing they couldn't stop seeing now. Chairs wear. Chairs get moved, repainted, re-caned, shoved out of true. This one was identical across sixty years of film stock and chemistry and light: same high back, same square stance, same exact angle to the table's edge, down to the millimetre. Photographs lie constantly — they flatter, they blur, they catch the world mid-blink — but they don't usually hold still like this. This was less like a chair in a photograph than like a chair *insisting* on being in one.

And it was closer. Casper wouldn't swear to it from the early albums — distance in old snapshots is a slippery thing — but in the recent ones, the ones from the last decade, the chair seemed to sit nearer the places people chose. Nearer Edith's seat in the pictures from her last winters. And in the shot from last week, nearer the empty space where Casper had stood to take the picture.

Nonsense. Parallax. Composition. They wrote both words in the log and underlined neither.

Outside, a moorhen called across the water, one flat cry, and the dark came up the lane from the direction of the church. Casper worked on. They photographed every remaining page, logged every date, and somewhere past midnight, cold now despite the heating, they arrived at the final print in the final album — the last picture Edith ever took, dated six days before she died.

It was the kitchen again. The table. The window. And the chair — but this time it was not at the table's end.

It had moved around. It stood beside Edith's own place, close enough that the armrests — it had no armrests, Casper realized, it had never had armrests, why had they thought that — close enough that the seats nearly touched. As if the two chairs had been drawn together for a conversation. As if something had finally been allowed to sit near the head of the table after sixty years of waiting at the end.

Edith must have known. She'd taken the picture herself; she'd have seen the screen. Six days left to her, and she'd spent one of them photographing that.

On the back, in her upright hand, fainter than her other labels, as if written quickly and pressed hard enough to hurt the pen:

*It knows I'm going.*

Casper sat in the kitchen of the dead woman's house, surrounded by sixty years of evidence, and heard the heating tick, and the mere breathe, and beneath both — very softly, from the front room, where the albums lived in their glass-fronted cabinet — the unmistakable sound of wood settling on flagstones.

One small, patient scrape.

Closer.
