## 3. What Mrs. Aldous Kept Locked

The floorboard in the back bedroom had been loose for as long as Casper could remember, which meant it had been loose for at least thirty years, because the memory was of being small and barefoot and told, in Edith's flat fen voice, *mind that board, love, it'll have your ankle.*

They'd been thinking about that board all morning.

Not obsessively — Casper didn't do obsessive; they did thorough, they did methodical, they did the comfortable rituals of a scholar who believed that anything strange could be filed under *folk belief, local* and left to moulder respectably. But after two days of photographs showing a chair that hadn't been there, after Mrs. Aldous next door going grey-faced and shutting her door mid-sentence, Casper's mind kept circling back to a loose board in a dead woman's house, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

Edith had died in February. The cottage had sat empty since, and Casper had arrived in June with recording equipment and a grant proposal and the pleasant assumption that grief would be mostly administrative. They were still holding to that assumption, more or less, the way you hold to a railing on a ship that has begun, very slightly, to list.

The board came up easier than it should have. No nails, just a snug fit, as if it had been lifted often and set back carefully. Beneath it: a cavity lined with an old tea towel, and inside the towel, wrapped again in oilcloth tied with string gone brittle, a book.

A ledger, once. A parish accounts book by its stamped cover, the kind churches sold off by the boxload in the seventies. Someone had pasted brown paper over the front and written nothing on it at all.

Casper carried it downstairs to the kitchen table — the table, the one from the photographs — and made tea they wouldn't drink, and opened it under the window where the light was best.

The first page was dated the fourth of January, 1974.

* * *

*4th January. Snow again. The child is three weeks gone and the house will not let it lie.*

Casper read that line four times. The handwriting was unmistakably Edith's — the same forward-leaning script that had addressed forty years of birthday cards — but younger, harder, pressed into the paper like she was writing through it.

*They want to give It a name. Aldous says a thing without a name wanders, but a named thing will sit. I said we are not naming It, we are naming a dead boy, and Aldous said there is no difference now and God help me I think she is right.*

*The Wrens gave him up. That is what nobody will say plainly. Their Hollis went through the ice on the drain behind the quarry and they pulled him out blue and too late, and Tom Wren sat in my kitchen with his hat in his hands and said, Edith, we can't bury him empty. And I knew what he meant and I let him mean it.*

*We are going to baptise the house.*

*9th January. Done tonight. Eleven of us at the Wrens' table, the same table that stands in this house now — Tom moved it here when they went away, said he couldn't look at it. We filled a bowl with drain water and snowmelt and Father Ansell wouldn't come so Aldous read the words herself out of her grandmother's book, the old words, the ones that aren't for church. She poured the water on the threshold and said the name into the gap under the door.*

*Hollis Wren.*

*And the house took it. I don't know how else to write what happened. The candle flames all leaned toward the door at once, like grass in wind, and then stood straight, and the cold that has sat in this room since the boy drowned — the cold that sits at the end of the table — was gone. Just gone. Aldous started to laugh and then started to cry and Tom Wren put his head down on his arms like a man at a graveside, which I suppose he was.*

*A named thing will sit still. Aldous was right. It sits. There is a chair at the end of the table now that none of us brought and none of us will move. We leave a plate on Sundays. It is not so bad. It is almost company.*

*I hate that I wrote that last sentence. I am leaving it because taking it out would be a lie.*

*14th February. It is not company. I don't know what it is. It waits at the table like a good child waits, and there is something in the waiting that is worse than any noise could be. Margaret Pell swears she heard it answer when she said goodnight to the room. Said goodnight, Edith, and something said it back, only backwards, the way a child learns.*

*The Wrens' girl asks after her brother. Nobody will tell her the truth. She is six.*

*Casper's mother, Casper thought. The girl was their mother. The arithmetic landed somewhere below the ribs and stayed there.*

*3rd March. It has begun to look at me. Not eyes — I want to be clear, there are no eyes, I am not a woman who sees bogeymen — but attention. When I stand at the sink I feel the whole weight of the room turned toward me the way a field turns toward the sun. Yesterday I set the Sunday plate down and my hand shook and I heard — felt — disappointment go through the house like a draught.*

*Aldous says it is content. Aldous says content is enough. But I watched it sit at that table for two months and I have decided no child should be made to sit still forever, not even for our sakes. Especially not for our sakes. We did not save that boy. We borrowed him.*

*11th March. I have found the words. They are the same words backwards, or near enough — Aldous's grandmother's book gives both, the giving and the un-giving, and warns against the second in a hand that shook worse than mine does now. To take a name back is to make the thing that held it homeless twice over. I know. I know what I would be doing.*

*But it looked at Margaret's granddaughter today. Followed her along the fence line all the way from the school path. A named thing will sit, but a hungry thing will reach, and I cannot spend the rest of my life setting plates for a thing that is learning to be hungry in a village full of children.*

*One of them is mine.*

*17th March. Tomorrow night. Pray for me, whoever finds this, though I have no right to ask it. If I succeed, this family buys its quiet with a theft, and the bill will come due someday, and I am sorry, whoever you are, that the bill lands in your lap. I have tried to think of another way for eleven weeks and there isn't one. Some debts you can only move, not pay.*

*If I fail — if you are reading this and the chair is empty and the house is cold — then leave. Don't pack. Don't say goodbye to anyone. Leave.*

*18th March. I took it back. God forgive me.*

*It screamed. Not aloud. In every closed door at once, in the pipes, in my own chest. And then the house went silent in a way silence has never managed before, a held-breath silence, a listening silence, and I sat at that table until dawn with my hands over my mouth and I understood that I had not banished anything. I had only made it patient.*

*Invisible things don't get chosen. That is what Aldous told me after, and she was right, and it has held for forty years, and I am sorry, I am sorry, I am sorry.*

*There is no more ink in this pen and I am glad. I don't trust myself to keep writing.*

*E.A.*

* * *

Casper sat for a long time without moving. The tea had gone cold in the mug, and at some point the light had shifted, and the kitchen had grown the particular dimness of a fen evening coming down early, the way it always did, the way Edith used to complain about and secretly loved.

Their first instinct — the trained instinct, the grant-proposal instinct — was context. Grief-madness, collective ritual, a village processing a child's drowning through folk ceremony. It happened. It was documented. There were papers to cite.

But the trained instinct had been failing them for a week now, ever since the photographs. And the photographs were the problem, weren't they. Because a village could work itself into a fever in 1974 and burn out by spring, but a chair did not appear in a digital image taken last Tuesday unless something was standing in this kitchen right now, patient, forty years learned, looking at the table where Casper ate breakfast.

*The bill will come due someday.* Edith had written to them. Across fifty years, across death, she had written directly to whoever found this — and Casper knew, with a certainty that had nothing scholarly in it, that Edith had known exactly who would find it. Had hidden it under the loose board she'd warned a small barefoot child about. *Mind that board, love.* Not because of the ankle.

Because of this.

Casper looked at the table. Six chairs. They counted them twice, the way you check a lock you already know you turned. Six.

In the photograph album upstairs, in pictures spanning five decades, there had always been seven places set at that table — seven, in photos from 1975, seven in photos from 1998, seven in the ones Casper themselves had shot last week. Seven chairs, and the seventh always at the end, half in shadow, empty.

Six chairs in the room.

Casper got up and turned on every light in the kitchen and told themselves, in a voice that only shook a little, that this was fine, this was explainable, this was a broken chair long since discarded and a trick of framing and albums that had been, what, edited? By whom? When?

Then they remembered the last entry. *I took it back.* And beneath it, in different ink, smaller, added later — years later, the handwriting older, looser, the E.A. shakier:

*It has started to come closer to my seat. I have not told Aldous. Forty years of patience, and now it is learning to walk again. If you are reading this, it is because I am gone, and I need you to understand one thing above all others:*

*It doesn't want the name back. Names are only how it keeps score.*

*It wants what the name was for. It wants to belong to someone.*

*Go to Tam Weller. He was there. He owes this family, and he knows he owes it, and he is the only one left who will tell you true.*

Tam Weller. The old man who had sung Casper one verse of the harvest song and then shut his mouth like a trap and told them to stay clear of this very house.

Casper folded the diary closed and held it against their chest and stood in the bright, overlit kitchen of a house that had been listening for forty years, and outside, down the lane, they saw a figure turn away from the gate — Mrs. Aldous's son, or someone built like him — walking off quickly, having stood there long enough to see the lights come on in every window at once.

Someone had been watching the house.

Someone had been watching to see whether Edith's grandchild found the floorboard.

Casper picked up their phone and photographed the final entry, hands steadier than they felt, and began, very deliberately, to plan a visit to Tam Weller — knowing even as they did it that the village would hear of it before they reached his door, and that whatever protection Edith had bought with her theft, Casper had just spent the first instalment of it by reading past the line that said *leave.*
