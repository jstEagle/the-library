## 9. The Threshold

The house knew the date before I did.

I woke on the twelfth of January with my breath fogging in a room where I'd left the radiator on, and the smell was already there — roast mutton and boiled potatoes and gravy gone to skin, a dinner cooked forty years ago and never cleared away. It came up through the floorboards like water through chalk. I lay in bed and listened to it settle over the cottage, patient as frost.

I had not slept. Not really. Since the photograph — since I'd seen myself seated in that chair, smiling, hands folded like a good guest — sleep had become a country I could only visit briefly, and always with someone standing behind me. So I was awake when the mirrors started.

I heard them before I saw them. A faint ticking, like ice expanding in a pond, coming from the hallway. Then the bathroom. Then the little gilt-framed mirror above Edith's dresser that I'd been avoiding for weeks.

Every reflective surface in the house showed the same thing: the kitchen table, set for two.

Not my kitchen as it was — cluttered with recording equipment, mugs, Tam Weller's file of papers. The table as it must have looked in 1974. A white cloth. Two places laid. Candles burned down to stubs. And at the far end, the chair.

It was closer now. Of course it was. It had been moving closer since the first photograph, that slow arithmetic of approach, and tonight there was no distance left to close. In the mirror the chair sat directly across from an empty seat — my seat — and on its surface, where a plate should be, there was nothing. Just wood, worn pale in the shape of someone who had once leaned there every day.

Waiting to be occupied from either direction.

I got up. My legs felt borrowed. In the bathroom mirror I watched myself pass behind the table — except I didn't pass behind it, because the table wasn't in the bathroom, but the mirror didn't care about physics anymore. It cared about the setting. And in the glass, my reflection paused at the door of that impossible room and looked back at me.

It smiled the smile from the photograph.

I stopped sleeping in the bedroom after that. I made tea I couldn't drink and sat in the kitchen itself, facing the actual table, daring the real room to match the reflected one. It almost did. Twice, out of the corner of my eye, I saw the cloth. When I looked straight on, there was only my own mess, my own solitary plate.

But the smell stayed. Mutton and gravy and something sweeter underneath — bread pudding, maybe, the kind Edith must have made, the kind a boy named Hollis Wren might have loved. A last meal, served nightly to a child who drowned in January and was given to the house so he would sit still.

*We gave It a name so It would sit still.*

*I took it back. God forgive me.*

Edith's handwriting had gone jagged on that final page, the pen pressed hard enough to tear through. Forty years I'd been alive because she tore a name out of a ledger and out of a ghost's mouth, and the bill had come due at her granddaughter's address.

Tam arrived at four o'clock, which meant he'd walked the whole way from his cottage in the grey, refusing the lift I'd offered days ago. He stood in the doorway without coming in, and I understood why when I saw him glance past me at the hallway mirror. Whatever he saw there made his face go the colour of the fen in February.

"It's tonight," he said. "The anniversary. The night they gave him to the house."

"I know."

"You don't." He came in then, finally, as if deciding that if the house wanted him it would have taken him decades ago. "You know it like it's written down. I know it because I carried the lantern while they did it. His mother held him and his father read the words, and Edith stood at the back with her daughter's hand in hers, praying it wouldn't be her girl this time. And when it was done — when Hollis Wren sat down at that table and *stayed* — your gran went home and thought about her own front door, and what stood outside it some nights, and she decided forty years of peace was worth one theft."

He lowered himself into a chair like a man much older than his years.

"She didn't steal a name, Casper. She stole a *self*. That's what a name is, out here. It's how the house knows who's asking to come in. Without it, Hollis has spent his whole death reaching for a word that got pulled out of his mouth. That's why the chair moves. That's why the photographs. He's not hunting you. He's trying to remember himself, and you're the nearest thing to the woman who took it."

"So I give it back."

"At the threshold." Tam's voice dropped, went formal, the cadence of something learned rather than said. "A threshold is the only place a house can hear honestly. Inside, the house speaks for you. Outside, you're just weather. But on the sill — half in, half out — that's where a bargain can be struck. That's where they gave him the name in '74, and that's where it has to be returned."

"And then what? He takes it and—" I gestured at the mirrors, the smell, the whole accumulating weight of the house. "What happens to me?"

Tam looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the light was failing, and the fen beyond the window had gone the colour of old pewter.

"That," he said, "depends on what you mean by giving."

* * *

I spent the last hour of daylight doing something I hadn't planned on: reading through Edith's diary one more time, all of it, cover to cover, looking not for instructions but for her. And near the end, wedged between the terrible final entries, I found a line I'd skimmed past twice before:

*A name given under fear is a chain. A name given freely is a gift. God help me, I hope someone someday gets to do it properly.*

She'd known. Standing in 1974 with her stolen prize and her safe daughter, she'd known the theft was only a postponement, and she'd written down — for whoever came after — the difference between a chain and a gift. Between banishment and welcome.

Between what the village did to Hollis Wren and what I could choose to do instead.

I put the diary down and looked at the kitchen table. My reflection in the darkened window sat across from an empty chair, and for the first time I didn't flinch. Because here was the thing I'd been circling for weeks without saying aloud, the thing underneath the fear like a stone under fen-water:

I knew what it was to be unseen. I'd built a whole life of it — the folklorist passing through, recording other people's belonging, never staying anywhere long enough to be missed. Invisible things don't get chosen, Tam had told me, and he'd meant it as warning, but I'd heard it as biography. The Guest and I had more in common than I liked to admit. Both of us reaching for a place at a table. Both of us unnamed by the people who should have claimed us.

The difference was that I still had my name. And names, Edith wrote, are meant to be given.

* * *

At eleven o'clock the candles lit themselves.

I don't know how else to write it. I was standing in the kitchen and the wicks simply caught, two small flames bending toward the doorway as if in deference, and the table beneath them was laid — really laid, no corner-of-the-eye trickery — white cloth, two plates, steam rising off food that had been cold for forty years and steamed anyway. The chair at the far end was drawn out slightly. An invitation, or a habit. Something that remembered being called to dinner.

The mirrors along the hall showed it all, every angle at once, a corridor of the same small ceremony repeating into infinity.

I picked up the diary. I took the ledger page — Tam had helped me reconstruct the torn name from the ink-ghost, and I'd copied it out in my own hand, because some things should be offered in writing as well as breath. And I walked to the front door.

The night outside was absolute. No moon, no stars, just the fen breathing its wet dark against the glass. Frost furred the edges of the doorstep. Halfway down Marsh Lane, I knew without looking, a figure would be standing where figures always stand in stories like this — but I didn't look, because this wasn't a story about looking. It was a story about speaking.

I opened the door.

Cold poured in. The candles behind me leaned. And on the threshold — that strip of stone, half house and half world — I felt the presence arrive the way you feel someone step up beside you at a graveside: not seen, but utterly, pressingly *there*. The air thickened. My breath stopped being mine alone; something beside me exhaled when I exhaled, a second rhythm just out of sync, the way two people breathing in a small room gradually fall into step.

It was waiting. Forty years of waiting, distilled into one held moment.

I thought of running. I want that recorded — the animal part of me screamed for the door, for the car, for anywhere that wasn't this strip of frozen stone. But courage, I understood then, isn't the absence of that scream. Courage is standing on a threshold with your whole body telling you to flee, and opening your mouth anyway. Courage is surrender that chooses its terms.

"Hollis Wren."

The name left me and the night *changed*. The frost on the step glittered as though a lamp had been uncovered somewhere behind the sky. The second breathing beside me caught — hitched — and then came a sound I will hear until I die: a small, cracked voice, testing its own edges, saying syllables it hadn't owned since 1974.

"*Hollis.*"

"Yes," I said. My eyes were streaming; whether from cold or something else, I've stopped needing to decide. "That's yours. It always was. Edith took it and I'm sorry, and I'm giving it back — not because I'm afraid. Because you should have it. Your name, your seat, your place at the table. All of it. Freely."

The presence pressed closer. Cold soaked through my coat, through my skin, into the place where fear lives — and found, I think, that fear wasn't all that was living there. There was recognition too. Two invisible things, meeting at a door.

"Come in," I said. "Not as a Guest. As Hollis. Come in and sit down."

And the house — Edith's house, my house — drew a breath around us both, and let him.

* * *

Inside, the table was set for two, and both chairs were filled.

I won't pretend I remember it clearly. Memory goes soft at the centre of miracles. But the mirrors kept their own record, and in the morning every one of them showed the same image: a young man at the head of the table, thin and damp-haired and wearing the expression of someone hearing his own name spoken after a long silence — and opposite him, me, smiling, unafraid, mid-sentence, as though we'd been talking for hours.

On the plate before him: bread pudding. Still warm.

When I woke, the smell of the long-cold dinner was gone from the house for the first time since I'd arrived. The mirrors showed only ordinary rooms. And on the kitchen table, where no plate had been the night before, there was a single object waiting for me:

One of Edith's photograph albums, open to a page I had never seen, showing a picture I had never taken.

In it, the kitchen table was set for three.
