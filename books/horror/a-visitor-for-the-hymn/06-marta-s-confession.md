## 6. Marta's Confession

Marta Voss was dying the way a candle dies—by inches, by guttering, by a light that kept insisting it was not yet finished. Isandro found her in the back room of the cottage on Chapel Lane, propped against pillows gone yellow with age, and thought: *she has been waiting for me.*

The room smelled of camphor and cold ash, though no fire had been lit in days. A crucifix hung on the wall, but it hung crooked, as if someone had taken it down once and put it back without conviction. On the bedside table, arranged with terrible neatness, lay a folded cloth, a glass of water untouched for so long that a skin had formed on its surface, and a bundle wrapped in oilcloth tied with waxed string.

"Sit," Marta said. Her voice was gravel in a paper bag. "You've been circling my house for three days like a crow over a lambing field. Sit down before I die out of spite."

Isandro sat. The chair groaned under her, an old thing with a seat worn to the shape of someone else's patience. Outside the window, the harvest moon was four nights away, and the light came through the glass already bruised and orange, falling across the bedclothes like something spilled.

"I read the ledger," Isandro said. "In the parish archive. The binding of 1847. The final verse left blank."

Something moved behind Marta's eyes—not surprise. Recognition, perhaps, or relief wearing surprise's clothes.

"And you came here instead of running." The old woman's mouth bent, not quite a smile. "That's either courage or stupidity, and in this village we've never learned to tell them apart."

"Marta—"

"Fifty years ago," Marta said, "I was twenty-two years old, and I had the voice of a lark, and they chose me."

* * *

The memory came to Marta the way floodwater comes to a cellar—not all at once but rising, finding every seam she'd spent half a century caulking shut.

She was twenty-two and standing in the flooded crypt beneath the chapel, and the water was at her knees and black as tea left steeping too long. The founders' bones were stacked in niches along the walls, and old Reverend Cobb stood waist-deep beside her holding a lantern that made the water glow amber, and the congregation above were singing the first verses down through the floor grates, their voices arriving muffled and strange, like singing heard from inside a coffin.

She had been chosen because she was Voss blood but not founder's blood—the ledger was specific about this, though no one had ever explained why to her, only that the completing voice must be kin enough to be trusted and strange enough to be *new*. She had rehearsed the final verse eleven times in this room. Eleven times she had opened her mouth in the dark and let the notes come, and each time the water around her ankles had grown warm, and each time something below the warmth had stirred like a sleeper turning toward a window.

On the twelfth time—the true time, the time that counted—the lantern had guttered. And in the dark, in the space between one note and the next, she had heard it answer her.

Not with words. With her own mother's voice, singing the lullaby her mother had sung only to her, the one about the boat on the grey water, and it sang it *better* than her mother had, with a sweetness her mother never managed, and that was how she knew it was a lie, because nothing true could sing so sweetly in a place like this.

She had closed her mouth. The verse died unfinished in her throat. And the silence that followed was not silence—it was attention, vast and patient and wounded, turning itself upon her like a searchlight.

She ran. Up the steps, through the vestry, out into the snow, barefoot, bleeding from where the stone had caught her, and she did not stop until she reached the coast road, and even then she did not stop properly for thirty years. She married a man in Leeds. She came back when he died, because Hallowmere is a hook in the flesh and coming back is what the hooked do. But she never sang again. Not a hymn, not a carol, not a hummed tune while washing dishes. Fifty years of silence, and the binding fraying all the while like a rope left out in weather, and every harvest the noises worse, and every harvest the forgetting creeping further up the village like damp up a wall.

"They told me it would hold anyway," Marta said aloud, in the yellow room, to the woman sitting in the shaped chair. "They said the founders' bones would hold it, that my part was a formality. They lied, or they believed it, and I've never decided which is worse."

* * *

Isandro listened until the story ended, and then she listened to the room—the tick of a clock somewhere, the wet sound of wind worrying the eaves, and beneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wall, the dragging she knew so well from her own ceiling. Here it came from below. From under the floorboards. From under the earth.

"You fled," Isandro said. It was not an accusation. She was a folklorist; she knew what fear did to people, had recorded a hundred testimonies of flight and its long aftershocks. But the word came out heavier than she intended.

"I fled." Marta said it plainly, the way you name a dead relative. "And every year since, I have paid for it in other people's memories. Do you know what the baker's grandfather forgot? His wedding day. He sat across from his wife at breakfast for forty years after, and he knew her the way you know a coat—you wear it, you're grateful for it, but you can't remember buying it." Her hand crept across the blanket toward the oilcloth bundle. "That's what a frayed binding costs. Not blood. Not death. *Past.*"

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because I'm dying, girl, and the thing below knows it. It's been feeding on me for weeks—every dream, every remembered face, it takes as I sleep. I wake emptier. By the harvest moon there'll be nothing left of me worth taking, and it will go looking for fuller pantries." Her fingers found the string on the bundle and worked at the knot with the patience of the very ill. "And because you've been recording. Don't look shocked. You think a village like this doesn't hear your machines humming at night? We hear everything. We've always heard everything. That's the trouble with Hallowmere."

The knot gave. The oilcloth fell open.

Inside was a notebook, bound in stained leather, its pages soft as cloth with handling. Isandro recognized the handwriting from the parish ledger—the same careful, cramped script, the same ink gone brown with age. But these pages held music. Lines and lines of it, notation dense as brambles, and threaded through the staves, in a different hand, in pencil, in a young woman's uncertain letters: corrections, breath marks, a single word repeated in the margins—*no*, *no*, *no*—and once, circled twice, *I am sorry*.

"The founders' notation," Marta said. "Complete. All seven verses, including the last. My grandmother copied it for me when they chose me, and I carried it to Leeds and back and never once let it out of my sight, because I thought—" She stopped. Her throat worked. "Because I thought if I kept it, someday I might find the courage to finish it. Fifty years, and the courage never came. Cowardice is inherited too, you know. Like eye color. Like the voice."

She pushed the notebook across the blanket toward Isandro.

"It has to be a voice from outside the bloodline. That's the design—that's the cruelty of it. The founders bound it with their own deaths, but a binding sung only by the dead goes stale; it needs the living, and it needs the living *strange*. An outsider. Someone the thing hasn't tasted yet, whose song it can't predict, whose memory is still—" she gestured vaguely, wearily, "—whole."

"I'm not a singer."

"Neither was I. It doesn't want skill. It wants *surrender*." Marta's eyes fixed on her, and in them Isandro saw the flooded crypt, the lantern, the answering voice—fifty years compressed into a single look. "It will offer you everything you've ever loved, sung back to you sweeter than you remember it. Your mother. Your childhood. Every hymn you've ever collected, every voice you've ever recorded, given back to you whole. And you will want to stop singing and listen. That is the moment. That is the exact moment everything depends on. And I am telling you now, from the far side of fifty years of knowing exactly what I should have done—" her voice cracked, and she let it, "—do not listen. Sing through it. Sing through it or become me, and believe me, girl, becoming me is not living."

Isandro took the notebook. It was lighter than she expected, and colder, as though the leather had been kept near a window all these years.

"What happens to the singer? After?"

Marta looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the wind rose, and somewhere down the lane a dog began to bark and then stopped mid-bark, as if it had forgotten why.

"No one knows," she said finally. "No one has ever finished it." And then, softer, almost tender, the cruelest kindness she had left: "But I think you already suspect. I think you've known since the night your machines answered you back. Why do you think you came here today? People don't walk toward things like this unless some part of them has already chosen."

Isandro stood. The chair released her reluctantly. At the door she paused, the notebook pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Marta. The verse you didn't finish. What are the words?"

The old woman smiled then—a real smile, small and sad and ancient, the smile of someone setting down a weight carried so long the shoulders have grown around it.

"You'll know them," she said. "When the water's at your knees and it starts singing to you in your mother's voice—you'll know them. They were always yours. That's the horror of it, child. That's the whole design."

Isandro walked home through the orange failing light, and behind her, in the cottage on Chapel Lane, Marta Voss began—for the first time in fifty years—to hum.
