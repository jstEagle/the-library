## 10. A Tape No One Remembers

The bus to Hallowmere ran once a week, and it ran empty.

Edith Callow had the back seat to herself, her satchel of archival tools wedged beside her like a traveling companion: cotton gloves, a magnifying loupe, a portable cassette deck she'd bought secondhand in Leeds because the county council wouldn't fund anything digital for fieldwork. Through the window the moors unrolled in their late-summer browns and golds, and the sky was doing that thing northern skies did—holding a brightness that felt borrowed, as though it might be reclaimed at any moment.

She'd been sent to survey parish records across six villages in the valley. Hallowmere was the last on her list, and the one she knew least. The file on it was thin in an odd way—not missing so much as *unwritten*, pages present but bearing only dates and initials, as if whoever had kept the records had grown tired of sentences. The last substantial entry was from 1847. After that, baptisms, marriages, burials, all in a hand that grew steadily more cramped, until even those thinned out and the ledger simply stopped being updated sometime in the nineties, mid-page, the ink trailing off like a footstep fading in snow.

The village itself appeared without announcement—one moment hedgerow, the next a cluster of grey stone roofs around a chapel whose spire leaned very slightly eastward, like a listener tilting its head.

Edith had been an archivist for nineteen years. She trusted paper the way other people trusted God, and she had learned that villages were never as quiet as their ledgers suggested. Every place kept two histories: the one it wrote down, and the one it hummed while pretending not to remember.

She intended to find both.

* * *

Mrs. Fenwick at the tea room was seventy-something and sharp as a new pin, and she poured Edith's tea without being asked twice.

"Surveying, is it? Well, you'll not find much. We keep ourselves to ourselves."

"That's rather what I'm hoping to document," Edith said pleasantly. "The keeping-to-oneselves. Local song traditions especially. Harvest customs. I understand there used to be a hymn sung here every autumn?"

Mrs. Fenwick's teapot paused above the cup—a hesitation no longer than a breath, but Edith had spent nineteen years learning to read pauses the way other people read print.

"There's a harvest service," Mrs. Fenwick said. "Always has been. Chapel, first Sunday past the full moon." She set the pot down. "We sing the same hymns everyone sings. Nothing special about ours."

"Someone told me there was a verse unique to the village. A local verse."

"Who told you that?"

The question came out flat, and beneath the flatness something else—wariness, or perhaps the particular blankness of a person reaching into a drawer and finding it empty and deciding, quickly, that the drawer had always been empty.

"I don't recall," Edith said, which was true; the note in her files had no attribution, just the words *local harvest verse?* in her own handwriting, and beneath them, puzzlingly, a second line she didn't remember writing either: *ask about the folklorist.*

"Nobody here would know anything about that," Mrs. Fenwick said. "More tea?"

Outside the window, an old woman crossed the lane with a walking stick, moving slowly, pausing at the chapel gate to look up at the leaning spire for a long moment before going in. Her face, when she turned, was the face of someone who had spent a lifetime carrying something heavy and had finally, recently, set it down—and could no longer remember what it had been, only that her arms still ached in its shape.

* * *

The chapel was cool and smelled of stone and candlewax and, faintly, of water—the mineral breath of somewhere below that stayed damp no matter the season. Edith sat through the Tuesday cleaning rota's worth of dust motes and copied inscriptions from the founders' memorial plaques along the north wall: names, dates, and beneath each name the same strange phrase, repeated eleven times like a refrain:

*SUNG SHUT AND KEPT SINGING.*

She wrote it down. She did not know why the phrase made the hair on her forearms rise, only that nineteen years of archives had taught her to respect that particular sensation. Paper could lie. Gooseflesh couldn't.

The vicar, a young man named Ellery who had come from outside the valley and spoke of Hallowmere with the fond bafflement of a man who'd married into a family with one locked room, let her into the vestry archive.

"We've boxes nobody's catalogued," he admitted. "There was talk of a researcher, years back—I have some idea someone came to do work on the hymns—but honestly I can't find any record of it. No correspondence, no permission letters. You'd think I'd dreamed it." He laughed. "My predecessor can't remember either. Odd, isn't it? The things that don't leave a trace."

"Very odd," Edith said.

In the third box, beneath a bundle of choir rosters and a child's crayon drawing of the chapel with too many windows, she found the tape.

It was a standard C90 cassette in a plain case, unlabeled except for a strip of masking tape across the spine, marked in handwriting she did not recognize: small, precise, slanting slightly leftward, the letters of someone who wrote quickly but had been trained to write well. Two words:

*HARVEST — COMPLETE.*

No date. No donor name. No accession number. In nineteen years, Edith had never found an item so entirely without provenance—and yet it had been catalogued, somehow, because it sat inside a folder with a number on it, a folder no one in the vestry could account for, in a box no one remembered packing.

"May I borrow the player?" she asked Vicar Ellery. "I brought my own, actually. Would tomorrow be too soon to return it?"

"Keep it as long as you like," he said. "Though I'll warn you—" he hesitated, and again Edith read the pause, that familiar Hallowmere pause, the sound of a drawer sliding open onto nothing, "—I don't think anyone here knows what's on it. It's not one of ours. Whatever it is."

*Not one of ours.* She turned the phrase over all evening, in her room above the tea room, while the moors went dark outside and the chapel spire lost itself against the sky.

* * *

She played the tape the next morning, alone, with the window open and the cassette deck's little counter glowing green.

Side A began with room tone—the acoustic signature of a stone interior, the chapel presumably, wind worrying something loose on the roof. Then footsteps, unhurried. Then a voice: a woman's, low and clear, speaking half to herself in the manner of someone narrating for a machine.

*"Testing. Isandro Vane, recording the harvest hymn, Hallowmere parish chapel. Congregation assembling. Marta Voss has agreed to let me sit in the gallery."*

Edith sat very still.

A folklorist. A real one, then—her instinct had been right, the vague memory in the vicar's mind had a body behind it somewhere. And yet no archive in the county held so much as a letter from her. No university listed her. When Edith had run the name through every index she could access, she had found exactly one result: a footnote in a defunct journal, citing *"field recordings, Hallowmere (Vane)"*—and the citation pointed nowhere. The journal's own records showed no article by any Vane. It was as if the woman existed only in references, a ghost quoted by documents that had forgotten her.

On the tape, the congregation began to sing.

It was a harvest hymn, all right, but not one Edith knew, and she knew hundreds. The melody moved in long, patient arcs, built for stone spaces, and the voices of the villagers carried it with the ease of lifelong habit. Edith transcribed as she listened, her pencil moving fast. First verse: thanksgiving, the fields, the ordinary grammar of harvest. Second verse: the storehouse, the winter promised against.

Then the third verse, and the singing changed.

Not in melody—in *weight*. The voices flattened, went careful, the way people walk differently when carrying something they must not spill. The words were harder to catch, and when Edith replayed that passage three times she got most of them, and wished she hadn't bothered, because they were not thanksgiving at all. They were a list. An accounting. *What was given, what was kept, what was owed*—phrased like a hymn, structured like a contract. And underneath the congregation's voices, so far down it might have been the building itself, another sound: a low sustained tone, patient as water finding a crack.

The fourth verse returned to lightness, almost giddily, as if the singers had surfaced from deep water and were laughing about it.

And then the fifth.

Edith's pencil stopped.

The congregation did not sing the fifth verse. There was a silence on the tape—ten seconds, fifteen—with only that low tone beneath it, rising slightly, expectant, like an instrument tuned and waiting. And then a single voice entered. The folklorist's voice. Alone.

Isandro Vane sang a verse Edith had never heard, in words that fit the hymn's meter perfectly and completed it perfectly, closing every arc the earlier verses had opened, answering every debt the third verse had named. Her voice was steady. It was also, unmistakably, afraid—and more than afraid: resolved, the way a voice sounds when the singer has already decided everything that matters and is now merely doing it.

The verse ended on a note that should not have resolved as beautifully as it did. The low tone beneath it stopped—not faded, *stopped*, like a door shutting—and the tape ran on in ordinary silence for a minute, then caught the sound of footsteps descending, water lapping stone, and then, faintly, a woman humming her way back up through the dark, getting quieter, getting quieter,

and then the tape ended, mid-hum, though the counter showed the side wasn't full.

Edith sat in the tea room's upstairs quiet for a long time.

Then she walked down to the chapel and asked Vicar Ellery, casually, whether the harvest hymn had a fifth verse.

"A fifth?" He looked genuinely puzzled, and his puzzlement had that quality she was beginning to know well—the smooth surface over absence. "It's four verses. It's always been four. Why, does your research say otherwise?"

"No," Edith said. "Of course not."

But that night, at the window of her room, she heard the village children playing in the lane below, singing snatches of the harvest hymn the way children steal melodies, and when they reached the end they simply—stopped. Skipped to the amen. Four verses and done, seamless, unquestioned, as though the song had been born complete.

As though it had never had a wound in it at all.

* * *

She spent her last morning in the churchyard, copying gravestones. Near the yew at the wall's corner she found a plot with no stone—just a rectangle of ground slightly greener than the grass around it, the way old graves stay green, fed by whatever is given back. The sexton, raking leaves nearby, couldn't tell her who lay there. "Before my time," he said. "There's no record. Funny—there's usually a record."

Edith stood over the green rectangle a while. The wind moved through the yew with a sound almost like breathing, almost like a held note finally released.

She thought about the handwriting on the tape label—small, precise, slanting left. She thought about a woman who had walked into a village with a satchel and a question and had walked, in some sense, out of the world; who had traded every memory of herself for the memory of everyone else; who lay under unnamed grass in a valley that had digested her completely, leaving nothing behind but a cassette in an unlabeled folder and a single line in a stranger's notes: *ask about the folklorist.*

Edith took out her notebook. Beneath the transcription of the fifth verse—which she had checked, twice, against no hymnal in existence, and found in none—she wrote the only epitaph available to her:

*Hallowmere harvest hymn, complete text recovered. Collector unknown. Sung once, by one voice, in the flooded dark. Remembered by no one. Recorded by me.*

She hesitated. Then, because nineteen years of archiving had taught her that the least important thing a record preserves is often the truest, she added one more line:

*Her name was Isandro Vane.*

She did not know where the name had come from. It had simply arrived, whole, the way a tune arrives—out of the tape's hiss, out of the yew-wind, out of some drawer in the world that opens only when no one is asking after it. She wrote it down before it could close again.

That evening the weekly bus came through, and Edith rode out of Hallowmere with the cassette in her satchel, over the moors, under the borrowed brightness of the sky. Behind her the village settled back into its four-verse peace, its green rectangle, its locked-room calm.

And in the tea room, Mrs. Fenwick paused while drying cups, for no reason she could name, and stood a moment with her head tilted—like the spire, like a listener—as if somewhere just beyond hearing, someone was humming a verse she almost, almost knew.

Then it passed, and she went on drying the cups, and Hallowmere kept its silence, sung shut and kept singing.
