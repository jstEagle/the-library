## 6. Loyalty Is a Kind of Lure

Quentin knew something was wrong with Dev before he knew what it was. That was the thing about fifteen years of friendship — you learned a person the way you learned a song, and when a note went missing, you heard it even if you couldn't name it.

It started small. Dev stopped complaining.

Dev complaining was a law of nature, like gravity or the way the studio's coffee machine produced something legally distinct from coffee. Dev complained about the transmitter hum, about the vending machine's vendetta against him personally, about the way Quentin insisted on playing the same Ella Fitzgerald track at 1:47 every morning "for pacing, Dev, it's *pacing*." But for the past week, Dev had been arriving at the station with a serenity that didn't fit his face. He'd smile at the dead air. He'd hum.

Quentin noticed the humming first, actually. It was the tune. Of course it was the tune. Three verses, incomplete, looping — the melody that had crawled out of the Aldercrest excavation and into his call-in lines like water finding cracks in a foundation. Dev hummed it while logging commercials. Dev hummed it while eating a sandwich, which should have been physically impossible and was at minimum rude.

"New song?" Quentin asked one night, casual, his hand resting on the fader like a man resting his hand on a door he wasn't sure he wanted to open.

Dev looked up, and for a half-second his eyes were somewhere else — somewhere warm, somewhere Quentin couldn't follow. "Old song," he said. "You'd know it if you heard the whole thing."

"Nobody knows the whole thing. That's the whole problem."

"Marisol does," Dev said, and went back to the logs, humming.

Quentin's blood did something cold and complicated. Marisol — the drowned woman, the caller who had described her own death in the sixth week of his show, back when he could still tell himself it was a prank, a hoax, an elaborate piece of performance art by someone with too much time and too little conscience. Marisol, whose voice had come through the board last week through a channel that was *not connected to anything*.

"Dev," he said carefully. "How do you know that name?"

Dev's pen stopped moving. When he looked up this time, he was fully present, and his expression was so gentle it frightened Quentin more than the humming had. "Because she talked to me, Q. Tuesday. In the stairwell, on the frequency you keep saying doesn't exist." He tapped his own chest. "She said to tell you she's sorry about the water. That it wasn't her choice, the way it happened. That she's been waiting to say that to somebody."

"Marisol Okafor drowned six days before she called my show," Quentin said, each word placed down like a glass he was terrified of dropping. "I checked. I checked the obituary, I checked the coroner's—"

"I know." Dev's smile was soft and terrible. "That's the *generosity* of it, Q. That's what you're not getting. Everybody down there gets one more conversation. One more. The door isn't hungry, it's *hospitable*."

* * *

That night Quentin followed Dev home.

He hated himself for it, mostly. He told himself it was concern, and it was, but concern and suspicion share a carpool. Dev lived four blocks from the station in a building that smelled permanently of someone else's curry, and his window — the one facing the alley, the one that never quite closed — was the one Quentin stood under at 2:40 in the morning, feeling like a burglar in his own best friend's life.

He heard the singing at 2:51.

Dev sang in his sleep the way other people talked in their sleep — loose, unguarded, the words slightly wrong, like a translation of a translation. But he sang the tune, all three verses, and then he sang the space where the fourth verse should be, humming through the silence the way you'd hum through a rest in sheet music, and the silence itself had a shape. It had a *wait*. Quentin stood in the alley with his arms wrapped around himself and understood, with the clarity that only comes at the worst possible hour, that his best friend was rehearsing.

And then Dev's voice changed.

It wasn't Dev anymore. It was a woman — older, a little rough, the kind of voice that had spent decades telling people to eat something, you look thin — and she was singing a lullaby in a language Quentin didn't know, and Dev was singing *with* her, harmonizing, his sleeping voice and the dead woman's voice braided together in the dark of that shabby apartment, and Quentin pressed his back against the cold brick and put his hand over his own mouth.

Because his grandmother had told him. Not in words — she'd been gone since 1974, vanished from a radio room with the transmitter still warm — but in the rituals she'd left behind, the salt lines, the counted doors, the rule she'd written on the inside of his mother's lunchbox: *never sing along*. He'd thought they were superstitions, the way you think a seatbelt is a superstition until the night it isn't.

The entity didn't take people. It *hired* them. And its currency was memory.

* * *

"Your mother's voice," Quentin said the next night, in the studio, after sign-off, when the ON AIR light was dark and the building was just the two of them and the hum of equipment that no longer entirely belonged to the power company.

Dev didn't even flinch. He was coiling cables, that serene half-smile in place. "What about her?"

"You've been hearing her."

The coiling stopped. Dev set the cable down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off. "You want to know what she said to me?" His voice cracked on *said*, just slightly, and Quentin saw the man under the serenity, the Dev who had cried at his mother's funeral and then never cried again in eleven years, the Dev who kept her voicemail saved on a phone so old it should have been in a museum. "She said, *Mijo, you left the porch light on again. You know how I worry about the bill.*" He laughed — wet, helpless. "Eleven years, Q. Eleven years I'd have given anything to hear her nag me about a lightbulb. And this — whatever it is — it just *gave* her to me. No price. No contract. It just said, here, you were sad, here's the sound of not being sad anymore."

"There's always a price."

"Then show me the bill." Dev turned, and his eyes were bright and furious and heartbreakingly sane. "Show me one person who's been hurt. Marisol got to apologize. Tomas got to tell his wife where he hid the deed to the house — she found it, Q, in the toolbox, exactly where he said. These aren't victims. They're *guests*."

"They're rehearsing," Quentin said. "You sing it in your sleep. All three verses. And then you sing the *gap*, Dev. You sing the silence where the last verse goes, and the silence is shaped like a wait, and something on the other side of it is waiting for you to fill it in."

Dev was quiet for a long moment. The transmitter hum filled the studio, and underneath it — so faint Quentin might have imagined it — something else hummed too, patient, in no particular hurry at all. Doors had that kind of patience. Mouths too.

"You checked on me," Dev said quietly. "In the alley. Tuesday night."

Quentin's stomach dropped. "Dev—"

"I don't sleep that deep. I haven't since she died." He wasn't angry. That was the worst part. He was *sad*, in the way you're sad about weather. "You stood under my window listening to me sing with my dead mother, and your first thought was to protect yourself from me." He shook his head. "It didn't give me her voice, Q. It gave me *her*. The conversation. The door's whole trick — its whole *generosity* — is that the dead don't have to be gone. Not all the way. Not if somebody on this side keeps the line open." He picked the cable back up. "You of all people should understand keeping a line open. It's literally your job."

"My grandmother kept a line open," Quentin said. "In 1974. You know what's left of her? A lunchbox rule and a missing-persons file nobody ever closed."

"Maybe she's not missing," Dev said, and his voice had that warm somewhere-else quality again, that borrowed serenity, and he was looking at the studio's dead equipment — the board that lit up when nothing was plugged into it, the channel that wasn't connected to anything. "Maybe she just found a better signal."

* * *

Quentin didn't sleep. He sat in the studio until dawn with the lights all on — every light, the way his grandmother's rules demanded — and he made lists, because lists were a kind of salt line too.

What he knew: the door under Aldercrest was warm. It was sealed from the inside. The song had three verses that everyone was learning and a fourth verse that no one had — and the fourth verse was the hinge, the invitation, the difference between listening and opening. What he suspected: the entity was building a choir one grief at a time, and it paid in exactly the coin each person was poorest in. For Dev, a dead mother's voice. For the crew below the theater — Nadia's crew, the ones still left, still humming — God only knew what coin. For the city, when the frequency finally went citywide: everyone's coin at once.

What he didn't know was how to save his best friend from the one thing that would actually work on him.

At 5:14 a.m., the board lit up. Not the dead channel — the whole board, every fader, glowing soft and green like a field under aurora. And through the monitors, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, came Dev's mother's voice.

Not singing. Talking.

*Quentin,* she said, warm and rough and patient, a voice he'd never heard in life and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize a photograph of a place you've never been. *Mijo. He's going to ask you to come down there with him. He's going to ask you tomorrow. And you'll want to say no, and you should know — I understand why you'll say yes anyway.*

The monitors went dark. The board went dark. Outside, the sky was doing its gray, indifferent morning thing, and the coffee machine was producing its legally distinct beverage, and the world was pretending nothing had happened, the way the world does.

Quentin sat very still with his hands flat on the desk, not singing, not humming, barely breathing.

Tomorrow, Dev was going to ask him to go down to the door.

And the dead woman was right. He was going to say yes.
