## 9. Mouthed, Not Sung

Quentin had always believed that the hardest part of any broadcast was the first thirty seconds — the moment before you spoke when the microphone was just a piece of metal waiting to be disappointed in you. His grandmother had told him that once, or he'd invented that she had; by now the two memories were married and had children together, and he couldn't tell which of them were legitimate.

But this morning, at 4:47 a.m., sitting in a studio that smelled like burnt dust and old coffee, Quentin understood that the hardest part was not the beginning.

It was deciding to never make a sound again.

The final verse sat in front of him on three surfaces, because he did not trust any single one of them not to lie. On his legal pad, in his own handwriting: eleven lines, transcribed from Marisol's fragments across four calls, each one delivered like a woman handing him pieces of glass she'd swallowed. On the photocopied page from the 1938 station log, in an engineer named Aldous Wren's careful hand: *cut here, cut here, cut here* — and beneath the third cut, squeezed into the margin like a confession too large for its container, the verse itself, written backwards, so that anyone who found it would have to work for it, would have to want it slowly. And on the screen of Dev's laptop, a reconstruction Quentin had built over two sleepless nights, each line cross-checked against both sources until they agreed.

They agreed. That was the worst part. The verse wanted to be known. It had been waiting under the city since before the city, patient as water, and now it lay on his desk in three tidy columns, humming faintly through the paper the way a refrigerator hums — so constant you stopped hearing it, until you remembered it was there.

"You don't have to do it this way," Dev said from the doorway.

Quentin didn't look up. Looking up meant seeing Dev's face, and Dev's face currently belonged about sixty percent to Dev and forty percent to something generous. "Yes, I do."

"There's another way." Dev came into the studio, and behind him, faintly, through the walls, through the streets, through the whole sleeping grid of Aldercrest, came the singing. Three verses, thousands of throats, rising off the pavement like steam. Mrs. Okonkwo from the laundromat. The night manager at the Gas-N-Go. Children who should have been asleep. All of them singing the first three verses of a song no one had taught them, in a key no one had chosen, and underneath it all, below the floor of the world, something harmonizing.

"The door is generous," Dev said gently. He'd said it every day for a week. "It gives people back. My mother talked to me last night, Q. Forty minutes. She remembered my birthday. She remembered the joke about the canoe." His voice caught on *canoe*, and for one second the forty percent receded and it was only Dev, his best friend, his producer, the man who had covered his shifts and eaten his fries and never once asked why Quentin counted doors. One second. Then the warmth smoothed back over. "All you have to do is sing along. Everyone gets to keep everyone. Isn't that worth one song?"

"It's worth everything," Quentin said. "That's the problem. Things that are worth everything usually cost exactly that."

He checked the board. The frequency was theirs — the entity had taken it days ago, had ridden the station's signal out into every radio, every phone speaker, every car stereo idling in driveways while their owners stood on lawns with their heads tipped back, mouths open, singing. Quentin still had the transmitter. That was the one thing the door could not do itself: it could borrow voices but it could not press buttons. Doors, his grandmother might have said, swing both ways, and a thing that lives behind one gets very good at pushing and very bad at knocking.

At 5:00 a.m., he opened the microphone.

"This is Quentin Oyelaran," he said, and heard his own voice come out steady, which felt like a small miracle performed by a stranger. "It's the last show. I know most of you can't hear me right now. Most of you are busy. But if there's anyone left in Aldercrest who's still listening instead of singing — this part is for you."

He looked down at the verse.

Eleven lines. Marisol had given him seven of them across their calls, fed to him in pieces between warnings, her drowned voice crackling through the board like she was calling from inside a seashell. *"Don't learn the last verse,"* she'd said, the first night. *"It's learning you."* And then, later, softer, when he'd promised — promised! — that he understood the danger better than she did: *"Then take them in order. The words remember the shape of the mouth that made them. If you change nothing, nothing changes you."*

The other four lines had come from Aldous Wren's margins, written backwards in 1938 by a man who had then walked into the theater basement, sealed the door behind himself from the inside, and sat down at a console to keep the song company forever. Nadia had brought her the photocopy herself, three days ago, her hands shaking, her crew reduced from nine to five, her own lips twitching at rest into shapes that almost hummed. *"He wrote it backwards,"* she'd said, *"because he knew someone would come looking. He made sure we'd have to read it slow. Like he was buying us time even from the grave."*

Quentin had spent two nights reading it slow. Reading it backwards, then forwards, then line by line against Marisol's fragments, checking each word the way you check a fuse before touching it. And somewhere around 3 a.m. tonight, holding the completed verse in front of him, he had finally understood what Marisol had been trying to tell him all along, what Wren had died to make findable:

The verse was an invitation. Sung aloud, it opened the way in. Sung along with — answered, harmonized, joined — it opened the way out.

But sung silently? Mouthed without sound, offered without voice?

It was still an invitation. But an invitation requires a gift, and the gift the door wanted was voice. If you gave it your voice without giving it your joining — if you performed the words with your lips and kept your throat shut, kept yourself outside the song even as you handed over the one thing it needed to finish itself —

Then the door had to choose. Take the voice and stay sealed, incomplete, hungry forever behind its hinges. Or refuse the gift and go on starving in the streets above.

Wren had known. That was why the skeleton sat at the console with its mouth open mid-note: not because he'd been caught singing. Because he'd been showing the door what it could not have. A man who spent his last breath teaching a monster the word *no*.

"Dev," Quentin said quietly. "I need you to leave the room."

"No."

"Dev. Please. Whatever happens in the next four minutes, I don't want you watching. I want the last thing you remember of me to be annoying, not heroic. Annoying lasts longer."

Dev laughed — a real laugh, sudden and startled, and for a moment Quentin loved him so much it hurt in the teeth. Then Dev shook his head, slow, and the warmth came down over his face like a stage curtain. "Sing with us, Q. It doesn't hurt. Nothing hurts after."

"I know," Quentin said. "That's how I know it's lying."

He turned to the microphone. Through the glass of the studio window, the ON AIR light burned red and steady, the most honest object in the building. Below his feet, far below, through concrete and clay and whatever older strata the city had been built on top of without asking permission, the door waited. Warm. Hinged from within. Patient as a held breath.

Quentin took a breath of his own. Held it.

And began.

His lips moved. Eleven lines, in order, unchanged, each word given its exact shape and none of them given air. He could feel the verse trying to climb his throat — feel it the way you feel a sneeze arriving, a pressure behind the face, a gathering — and he closed his throat the way his grandmother had taught him to close doors: completely, counting them, leaving nothing ajar. The words shaped themselves in silence. His mouth was a puppet of the song and his body refused to be its instrument.

Below the floor, something noticed.

The pressure turned. Quentin felt it turn, felt the attention of the thing beneath Aldercrest swing toward him like a searchlight, vast and delighted — *here, here is the last voice, here is the invitation completing itself* — and then the delight snagged, caught, confused. No sound. No joining. A gift with no giver attached.

The singing in the streets faltered. Just slightly. A thousand mouths lost half a beat, like a congregation whose organist has lifted his hands from the keys.

*Take it,* Quentin mouthed, offering the finished verse, offering the shape of surrender with none of the substance. *Take the voice. Not the town. Not them. Me.*

The silence stretched. Somewhere in it, impossibly, he heard Marisol — not words, just a tone, the tone of someone nodding.

Then the door chose.

It came up through the floor of the studio like a tide coming up through sand. Quentin felt it enter through his open mouth — the one door he had deliberately, carefully, counted-and-left-open — and it took his voice the way a fire takes a house: thoroughly, greedily, and with a kind of terrible gratitude. He felt his laughter go first, then his grandmother's lullabies, then every version of *goodnight* he'd ever said on air, then his name — *Quentin Oyelaran* — pulled out of him root and branch, a tooth extracted from the jaw of the world.

He did not scream. Screaming required a voice. That was, he thought distantly, almost funny. Almost. He'd have laughed, except —

Except he'd already given that away.

In the streets, the singing stopped mid-note. All of it, everywhere, at once — three verses hanging unfinished in the cold air like laundry forgotten on a line. People stood on their lawns blinking, mouths closing, wondering why their feet were cold and where they were and, in some cases, who. In the studio, Dev sank to his knees with his hands over his ears, and when he took them away his eyes were his own again, all hundred percent, and wet.

The frequency went dead. Not silent — dead. The particular silence of a carrier wave with nothing riding it, a channel with no one home.

Quentin sat very still at the console, lips pressed together, throat aching with the shape of everything it would never hold again. The ON AIR light clicked off. He tried to say *goodnight, Aldercrest* — the words he'd said ten thousand times, the words that were practically his signature — and produced nothing at all, not even breath-shape.

He raised one hand instead, and waved.

Dev stared at him. Dev frowned. Dev looked around the studio as if trying to remember why he was here, and his gaze passed over Quentin once, twice, and slid off both times, like rain off waxed canvas.

On the mixing board, untouched by any hand, one fader eased upward. The dead frequency breathed back to life for exactly three seconds — long enough to hold a silence, a specific silence, man-shaped, patient, listening.

Then it went dark again, and somewhere beneath the old Aldercrest theater, behind a warm door hinged from the inside, something that had waited a very long time to finish its song began, very softly, to hum — in a voice that used to introduce the late show.
