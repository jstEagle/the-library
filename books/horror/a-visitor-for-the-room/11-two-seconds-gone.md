## 11. Two Seconds Gone

The lag reached two seconds on a Tuesday.

I know it was Tuesday because I wrote it down — *Tuesday, 9:14 a.m., hallway mirror, delay now two full seconds* — in the notebook that had become less a journal and more a case file. My handwriting was steady. That was the last steady thing about that day.

I had developed a method for measuring it. Stand at the hall mirror, raise my right hand, lower it, and count. One second had been an itch under the skin, something I could explain away if I tried — a trick of light, a tired eye, a brain that had spent three weeks marinating in grief and old wallpaper glue. Two seconds was not a trick of anything. Two seconds meant that when I lifted my hand and put it down again, the woman in the glass lifted hers while mine was already back at my side, so that for one suspended heartbeat there were two of us in the hallway, out of sync, like film run through a projector with a slipping gear.

And in that heartbeat, she wasn't quite me.

Her face was my face. Her cardigan was my cardigan, the grey one Wren bought me in Portland because he said it made me look like a detective in a show he liked. But her expression arrived late to everything, and expressions are where we keep our selves. Mine said *I am afraid*. Hers said nothing yet. She was still deciding.

"Okay," I told her quietly. "Okay. I see you."

She didn't answer. She never answered. But as I turned away, I caught her turning too — a half-beat behind, always a half-beat behind — and I could have sworn her eyes went first to the attic stairs before they came to me.

The house had opinions about where I should look next. It was getting less subtle about sharing them.

* * *

Marisol noticed the minutes going missing before I did, which shames me to write. A witness who cannot trust her own clock is a compromised instrument, and I had become exactly that without feeling the blade.

It happened like this: Birdie came into the kitchen at what Marisol swore was four o'clock and found me standing at the counter with a paring knife in my hand and a potato, whole and unpeeled, sitting untouched beside me. The window over the sink showed a sky gone the color of a bruise healing. Marisol said my name twice before I heard her.

"You've been up here since two," she said. "I checked on you at three. You were peeling potatoes then, too."

"I just started," I said.

"Elodie." She took the knife from my hand with the gentle firmness of someone disarming a child, or a stranger. "There's a bowl of peels in the compost. A big one. And another one in the trash from yesterday that you don't remember either, do you."

I didn't. I searched for the memory the way you search a pocket for keys you're certain are there, and came up with lint. Between three and four there was simply — nothing. Not sleep, not blankness, not the soft gray fog of distraction. Nothing, shaped exactly like an hour, with my body walking around inside it doing small domestic tasks like a hand puppet worked by someone patient.

Birdie was watching me from her chair with her chin on her fists. Seven years old, and she'd already learned to read adults the way other children read picture books.

"You went somewhere," she said.

"I was right here, sweetheart."

"No." She shook her head, slow and certain. "You went *thin*. Like when you hold a flashlight behind your hand."

Marisol and I looked at each other across the kitchen, and neither of us laughed it off, because we were past laughing things off. Three weeks in this house will cure you of that.

That night I started sleeping with the notebook on the nightstand and a pen tied to it with kitchen twine, and I began writing the time at the top of every page, every time I picked it up, like a pilot logging hours. If the house was going to take pieces of me, I wanted receipts.

* * *

The missing time got longer. That's the part I need to set down plainly, because in the moment each loss felt survivable, explicable, almost boring — ten minutes here, twenty there — and only in aggregate does it form the shape of something eating you.

Wednesday: forty minutes, gone, between writing *2:03 p.m.* and finding myself in the parlor with no memory of crossing the hall, the room's two mirrors covered with bedsheets I had apparently hung myself. I remembered deciding the mirrors should be covered. I did not remember doing it. The sheets were tucked with hospital corners. I don't make hospital corners. Wren did. Wren is dead. I stood in front of those sheeted rectangles and felt the hair rise on my arms, because the shapes they made were wrong — too tall, both of them, taller than the frames beneath, as if whatever hung the sheets had measured for someone else's body.

Thursday: an hour and ten minutes. I woke — woke, though I hadn't been asleep — in the upstairs bathroom with cold water running over my wrists and ash in the basin.

Ash. Fine and gray, smelling faintly of creosote and something sweeter underneath, like flowers burned in a vase. I scrubbed until my skin was pink and the smell stayed anyway, and I wrote it all down with shaking hands: *ash, source unknown, 5:40 p.m.* Then I sat on the edge of the tub and made myself say it aloud to the empty, mirrored room:

"The house is taking my time. Something is using it. I am being edited."

Edited. That was the word that came, and it fit with a click I hated. Because that's what the missing hours felt like — not absence but revision. Somewhere between one sentence of my life and the next, an author I couldn't see was cutting scenes, tightening the manuscript, removing everything that didn't serve the story it was telling. And I was beginning to suspect what story it was telling. It was a story about a house. It was a story with a fixed number of people in it. And every minute it took from me was a minute spent deciding whether I belonged in the final draft.

* * *

Friday afternoon, I lost three hours.

I want to be precise about how it felt, because later — when the record mattered, when people would ask me how I knew — this detail was the hinge. It didn't feel like falling asleep or blacking out. It felt like being *set down*. Like a book closed gently by someone who intended to come back, and the coming-back was the waking: one moment I was standing in the upstairs hallway at 1:17 p.m. (logged, timed, witnessed by my own handwriting), and the next I was lying on the floor of my childhood bedroom with the afternoon gone amber outside the window and my watch reading 4:31.

Three hours and fourteen minutes. On the floor. On my back, hands folded on my sternum, arranged.

Arranged. There's no softer word for it. My arms were placed the way you'd place them on a figure in a casket. Someone — something — had taken my body out of the world for an afternoon and laid it out with care, the way you'd handle evidence, or a dress rehearsal.

And on my sleeves: ash. Both forearms, dusted fine, like I'd been reaching into a cold hearth. When I raised my arms the ash fell in little gray whispers onto the floorboards, and I lay there watching it settle and thought, with a clarity that felt borrowed from someone braver: *it's showing me the fire. It keeps trying to show me the fire.*

I got up. I documented. Photographs of the ash pattern, samples folded into an envelope, timestamps logged. My hands were steadier than they had any right to be. Grief and terror, it turns out, metabolize into the same fuel: a terrible calm, a clerk's composure. You do the record because the record is the only thing between you and being a person things merely happen to.

Then I went downstairs to tell Marisol we needed to talk about leaving, and stopped dead on the third step, because the hall mirror was showing me the hallway a full second and a half behind — and in that stolen second and a half, the reflection was not standing where I was standing.

It was kneeling. Both figures in the glass — the late one and the one arriving now, which was me — were arranged around a shape on the floor that my mind refused to render for a moment, and then rendered anyway: a body, laid out, hands folded, ash on its sleeves.

Me. It was rehearsing me. Or remembering me forward.

I did not scream. I want that in the record too. I walked down the remaining stairs on legs made of someone else's courage, and I found Marisol in the kitchen, and I said, "We have a problem bigger than ghosts," and I told her everything, and she listened the way she listens — completely, without blinking, the way you listen to weather reports for a storm already overhead.

* * *

"We leave tomorrow," Marisol said.

"We finish the record first."

"Elodie." She set down the dish towel with great care, the way she does when she's choosing not to throw it. "You lost three hours today. You came back with ash on you. Birdie counts in her sleep now — did you know that? I stand in her doorway at night and she's counting. One, two, three, four. In her sleep, Elodie." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, I could see her hating it, hating that fear was making her voice do things without permission. "This house is not documenting you. It's *drafting* you. There's a difference between a witness and material, and you are starting to look like material."

"If we run now, we run with half a ledger. Half a ledger doesn't free anybody. It just tells the thing we're scared enough to abandon the count."

"The count isn't ours to finish!"

"It's ours to *witness*." I heard my own voice go flat and certain and knew how it must sound — like Hester's voice on the tape, maybe, that careful inherited calm. I softened it. "Mari. Three days. Give me three days to finish the archive. Then we go together, all of us, and we take every page with us."

She looked at me for a long time. Outside, the wind moved through the dead garden, and somewhere in the walls the house made the sound it makes — not a creak, not settling, but a kind of swallowing.

"You're already thinner," she said finally. Not my body. She touched my wrist, where the veins stood up too clearly. "Every day you're a little more thin. Like Birdie said. Like light through a hand." She pulled her coat off the hook. "I'm taking Birdie to Ruthie Delacroix's for the night. You'll have the house to yourself. Try — *try*, Elodie — not to give it anything else to take."

The door closed behind them at 6:52 p.m. I logged it.

* * *

Alone in the house, I did the thing I had been avoiding: I went through the missing time looking for what was left in it. Because here's what I'd figured out, standing in that amber bedroom with ash on my sleeves — the house wasn't erasing me cleanly. Erasure leaves nothing. This left *residue*. Ash. Hospital corners. Sheets cut for the wrong body. Whatever was editing me was working fast and sloppy, and sloppy editors leave artifacts.

I started in my childhood bedroom, where I'd woken. Under the bed, in the dust: a clean rectangle, foot-shaped impressions, as if I'd knelt there a long time. In the closet, my mother's old sewing box, open, and inside it — I swear this to whoever reads this — a spool of gray thread wound tight, and the thread smelled of smoke.

In the hallway, the mirror had gone quiet. Two seconds of lag, holding steady, like a held breath.

At 9:41 p.m. I sat at the kitchen table with Hester's notes spread out and the tape recorder's dead cassette beside them, and I made a list titled *WHAT THE HOUSE HAS TAKEN FROM ME*, because naming losses is the first act of any accounting, and if this house understood anything, it understood accounting.

Time: four hours, eleven minutes, logged.
Sleep: unmeasurable.
My brother: taken long before the mirrors learned my name.
And now, apparently, my edges. My thickness. My certainty that I ended where my skin ended.

Under the list, without deciding to, my hand wrote: *what has it taken from everyone else?*

And under that, in handwriting that was mine but slower, more deliberate, like dictation: *ask the lady in the glass.*

I stared at those five words for a long time. I did not write them. I checked the pen; it was dry-capped beside my hand. I checked my memory of the last five minutes and found it intact, continuous, mine.

The kitchen clock ticked. The sheeted mirrors in the parlor stood like two closed doors. And upstairs, faintly, through the floor, came a sound I hadn't heard before and will not forget: a finger tapping wood. Once. Twice. Three times. Four.

A pause, long and courteous.

Then, softly, patiently, from directly above my head: a fifth tap.

I sat very still in the kitchen of the house I grew up in, and I counted along without meaning to, and when the fifth tap faded I opened the notebook and wrote, with a hand that only shook a little:

*The count is at five. We are four. Tomorrow I find out who the fifth one was — and why the house thinks I'm already on the list.*
