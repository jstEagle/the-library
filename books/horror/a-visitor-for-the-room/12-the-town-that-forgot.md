## 12. The Town That Forgot

The Harrow County Records Office opened at nine, and I was on its steps at eight-forty, breath fogging in the October cold, a thermos of coffee I wouldn't drink clutched in one hand like a prop for a person who still had ordinary mornings.

I hadn't slept. Nobody in the house had, really. Birdie had stopped counting to four out loud, which was worse than when she'd started. Marisol had taken to sleeping with the hallway light on and a hammer under her pillow, which I chose not to comment on because commenting would have meant admitting what the hammer was for. And I had spent the night with Hester's cassette transcribed in my notebook, Tobias Vetch's name written over and over until the letters stopped looking like letters.

*They barred the door. Tobias was behind it. Tobias was behind it. Tobias—*

Paper was the next frontier. If the house had swallowed a man, if the town had agreed to forget him, then somewhere there had to be a seam. A record. Institutions are lazy; they erase people, but they almost never erase them *completely*. There's always a margin note, a tax stub, a line in a ledger that someone didn't think mattered.

I believed that at eight-forty. I want that on the record, since records are the subject. I believed it.

* * *

The clerk was a woman in her sixties named Dolores Prine who wore reading glasses on a beaded chain and had the particular patience of someone who has spent forty years watching strangers handle documents badly.

"Lockridge," she said when I gave the name. Not a question. "You're the one from the house."

"I'm the one from the house," I agreed.

"Your grandmother used to come in for plat maps. Ruth. She'd bring peppermints." Dolores looked at me over the glasses. "You'll want the fire records. 1963."

I hadn't said what I was looking for. I hadn't said anything yet. "How did you—"

"Everybody who comes in asking about that house asks about the fire." She was already walking, keys jangling on a ring that held what looked like forty years of them. "It's the only interesting thing that ever happened here, so we keep it close. Follow me."

The basement archive was a long room of gray shelving under fluorescent lights that hummed at a pitch I felt in my back teeth. Dolores led me to a section labeled *INCIDENTS — COUNTY FIRE MARSHAL — 1960–1970* and pulled a box, then a second, then a slim folder that she handled with two fingers like it might be contagious.

"1963, the Lockridge place. Two dead, officially." She set the folder down. "A Mrs. Aldous Lockridge and—" she paused, checked the page, and something flickered across her face, there and gone, like a reflection you almost catch. "And a second party. Name's smudged on the original. Water damage."

"Can I see the coroner's report?"

"Coroner's reports are in the annex. This is what we have here." She tapped the folder. "Fire marshal's findings, insurance correspondence, property assessment. The usual."

The usual. I opened the folder with the care of someone defusing something.

The fire marshal's report was four pages, typed, the carbon paper's ghosts still legible on the backs. Cause of fire: electrical, wiring fault in the east wall, consistent with the era's habit of blaming electricity for everything. Time of alarm: 11:47 p.m., March 3rd, 1963. Structures lost: main house, total. Outbuildings: smoke damage only.

Casualties. I read the section three times.

*Deceased at scene: Aldous Lockridge (m, 61), found in the front parlor. Cause: smoke inhalation. Second deceased: [REDACTED].*

Not smudged. Not water-damaged. *Redacted* — a heavy black bar of archival ink, applied with a roller by someone who had done it before, the edges clean and confident. And beneath the bar, in different ink, in a hand that shook: *see annex, file sealed by order of the county commission, 4/11/63.*

"Who redacts a name in a fire report?" I asked.

Dolores had drifted to the far end of the shelf, pretending to straighten boxes. She came back slowly. "That's the thing people ask," she said. "And then they stop asking."

"Why would they stop?"

"Because the next question is *who ordered it*, and the answer is always somebody whose grandchildren still live here." She pulled out a chair for me, an unexpected courtesy, and sat down across the table with her hands folded. "You want the annex file. I can't give you the annex file. Sealed is sealed; I'd lose my job, and my job is the only thing keeping me in a town that already thinks I talk too much."

"But you're telling me it exists."

"I'm telling you what the stamp says." She stood. "I'm going to go make coffee. The coffee here is terrible. You'll want to be alone with the box anyway. People always do."

* * *

I worked through the box for two hours and the coffee, when it came, was indeed terrible, and Dolores was right that I wanted to be alone with it, though not for the reason she thought. I wanted to be alone because I kept finding the same thing and I didn't want my face doing what it was doing in front of anyone.

Tobias Vetch was not in the box. Tobias Vetch was a hole in the box, and the hole had a shape.

The 1963 county property assessment listed Lockridge Farm, 240 acres, structures, livestock — and under *labor*, a line: *hired hands (2): A. Lockridge Jr., T. V—* The rest of the line was cut out. Not inked over — *cut*, with a blade, the page patched from behind with a slip of newer paper so the ledger wouldn't fall apart. Someone had performed surgery on a county assessment book.

The insurance file — the one I'd half-hoped would be the attic file's twin — was present, but the schedule of covered persons had a torn corner, a neat triangle missing where four names should have been. The claim correspondence survived: Aldous Lockridge's widow, my great-grandmother, negotiating a settlement in letters that grew shorter and colder as the spring of 1963 went on. The last letter, dated June, was one line:

*I will accept the amount discussed on the condition that no further inquiry be made into the household's composition at the time of loss.*

*The household's composition.* As if Tobias had been furniture. As if he'd been a chair that burned.

I sat back. The fluorescents hummed. Somewhere above me, a door opened and closed, and the building settled around its secrets like an old dog around a bone.

There was one more thing in the box, at the bottom, that I almost missed: a cemetery plat map, Harrow Union Churchyard, 1965 revision. The kind of map gravediggers use. Plots in rows, names in a clerk's careful hand. I ran my finger down Row 9, where the Lockridge stones stood — Aldous, his wife, the generations fanning out from them like a fan of cards — and then I looked at the plots adjacent, the ones sold to no family in particular, the paupers' row along the fence.

Plot 9-14 had a name.

The name had been scraped off. Not redacted — the map was a hand-inked original, and someone had taken a knife or a razor to the vellum and *scraped*, leaving a pale ghost, a scar in the surface where letters had been. You could still read the pressure of the pen if you tilted it under the light. I did. I tilted it. I held my breath.

I could make out a *V*. And maybe an *e*. And nothing else. The vellum had been scraped so thoroughly, so *thoroughly*, that the man's name had been removed down to the skin of the page.

But the plot was there. The plot was still there. 9-14. Between the fence and the Aldous plot, close enough that, in death, Tobias Vetch had been laid down within arm's reach of the people who'd let him burn — and then erased from the map of the ground that held him.

I photographed everything. I photographed the redaction, the cut line, the torn corner, the scraped plot, my own hand holding the vellum to the light, because I had learned in the last two weeks that the only defense against a thing that edits is a witness with a camera. The flash caught the ghost of the letters and for one frame — one frame only, I checked it a dozen times — the pale scar of the scraped name looked less like a scar and more like a mouth.

* * *

Dolores found me at the table with the box repacked and my notebook full. She looked at my face and sighed, the sigh of a woman who has watched this exact scene play out before, in other years, with other visitors.

"The annex," I said. "Who do I ask for?"

"You can ask for County Commissioner Rell. His grandfather was on the commission in '63." She sat down across from me again, uninvited, and lowered her voice even though we were the only two people in a basement under a town of four hundred. "I shouldn't tell you the rest."

"Please tell me the rest."

She took off her glasses and polished them on her cardigan, which gave her somewhere to look that wasn't my face. "There was a woman came through in the eighties. A journalist, or said she was. Asking the same questions you're asking. She got into the annex — I don't know how — and she made copies, and then—" Dolores put the glasses back on. "Her car went off Route 9 into the reservoir. They never found her body. They found her briefcase, though, floating. Every page in it was blank. Every single page, and she'd been in the annex for six hours, and the logbook said she'd checked out materials."

The hum of the fluorescents seemed to move closer, to lean in.

"So you understand," Dolores said, "when I say people stop asking. It's not that they're satisfied. It's that they learn what questions cost." She stood, and then, at the last moment, she did something I will be grateful for as long as I have a self to be grateful. She reached into the box, pulled out the cemetery plat map, and slid it across the table to me, along with a single sheet of plain paper.

"That's a duplicate of the '65 plat. We were supposed to destroy the duplicates in the '90s reorganization. I was supposed to." She tapped the paper. "And that's the name of the sexton who was working the churchyard in 1963. Earl Mott. He'd be dead now, but his daughter still lives out past the grange. Ruth Mott. She does the flowers for the church." Dolores's eyes, behind the smudged lenses, were very steady. "Sextons keep their own books. County maps can be scraped. A sexton's plot book is his own hand, his own keeping, and nobody thinks about it because nobody thinks about sextons."

"Mrs. Prine," I said, "why are you helping me?"

"Because in 1963 I was nine years old," she said, "and I lived a mile from your house, and the night of the fire my father stood in our yard and watched it burn, and I remember him saying — I've remembered it my whole life, it's the earliest thing I remember him saying — he said, *'Somebody's screaming in the east field and nobody's going out there.'*" She straightened the box on the table, aligning its edges with the shelf, a small violence of tidiness. "Nobody went out there. I've had sixty years to wonder who was screaming. Go see Ruth Mott. And Lockridge —"

"Yes?"

"When you find what you find. Don't write it in pencil."

* * *

The churchyard was a ten-minute drive, and I took it slowly because the fog had come up off the low fields in the way fog does here, the way it had done in 1963, probably, lying down over the land like something being tucked in. I passed the turnoff for Route 9 and the reservoir glinted below, flat and gray, and I did not let myself think about a briefcase full of blank pages.

Harrow Union Church was white clapboard and a steeple with a wasp nest in the louvers, and the churchyard spread out behind it on a gentle slope, rows of stones going back two centuries. I found Row 9 by the fence. The Lockridge stones were granite, upright, well-kept — my family's names in a long confident column. And there, at the end of the row, against the fence line, plot 9-14.

There was no stone.

There was a *space* for a stone — a rectangle of ground slightly different in color from the grass around it, the way a removed rug leaves its outline on a floor — and the grass over it grew in a fine, close, unwilling turf, and the fence post at the head of the plot had, carved into it decades ago by some bored hand, a single letter that time had almost eaten:

**V**

I stood there in the fog with my notebook open and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that I was standing on a grave the county had scraped off its own map, in a town that had agreed, in some unspoken referendum held over sixty years, to forget that this ground held anything at all.

I took out my phone and photographed the plot. The photo showed the fence post, the letter V, the rectangle of ground. In the background, out of focus, the fog.

In the photo, the fog had a figure in it. Standing at the far end of Row 9, between the stones, at the exact distance where focus gives up. Tall. Patient. Head slightly inclined, the way a man inclines his head when he is listening to someone read his name.

I looked up from the phone. The row was empty. The fog lay still over the grass.

I looked back down. The figure had come closer. One row nearer. Still out of focus. Still listening.

My hands were shaking so badly the next photo blurred, and when I raised my eyes again the churchyard was empty, and the fog was only fog, and somewhere behind me a car door slammed — Ruth Mott, presumably, arriving with her flowers, or arriving because she'd seen a stranger standing at the paupers' row and the town had taught her what that meant.

I turned. A woman in a green coat stood at the churchyard gate with a bundle of chrysanthemums and an expression I recognized, because I'd seen it on Dolores's face and on the old woman's at the wake and on every face in this town that had ever brushed against the house: the expression of someone deciding, right in front of me, how much they were going to say.

"You'll be one of the Lockridge twins," she said. It wasn't a question either. "The one that's still—" She stopped herself. She looked at the empty plot at my feet, and then at me, and her expression changed into something older and sadder. "Or maybe I've got that backwards," Ruth Mott said. "Come inside. My father's plot book is in the house, and I've been waiting fifty years for somebody to come ask me about it."

She held the gate open. Behind her, at the end of Row 9, the fog stood up.
