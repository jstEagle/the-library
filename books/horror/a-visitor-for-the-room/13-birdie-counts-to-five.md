## 13. Birdie Counts to Five

Marisol had learned to sleep in the farmhouse the way soldiers learn to sleep in war — lightly, with one ear open, her body angled toward the door of Birdie's room. The house had a sound now that she couldn't unhear: a faint ticking from inside the walls, except it wasn't mechanical. It was patient. It was the sound of something waiting for its turn to speak.

She woke at 3:14 in the morning because the ticking had stopped.

Silence was worse. Silence meant attention.

She lay still in the narrow bed Elodie had made up for her, listening to the old house settle around her like a body shifting in its sleep. Down the hall, Birdie's nightlight threw a thin amber line under her daughter's door. Marisol counted her own breaths the way she'd taught Birdie to count them during thunderstorms — in for four, hold for four, out for four — and on the twelfth breath she heard her daughter's voice.

Not crying. Not calling for her.

Counting.

"...four," Birdie said, clear and small and awake. "Four."

Then nothing. Then, after a long moment, in a voice that wasn't quite her own cadence — slower, as if reading from a page she'd been given:

"Five."

Marisol was out of bed before she decided to be. She crossed the hall in bare feet, the floorboards cold as river stones, and pushed open Birdie's door.

Her daughter was sitting upright in bed, blanket pooled at her waist, facing the closet. Not the window. Not the door. The closet, where the old full-length mirror stood — they hadn't been able to move it out yet; Elodie had asked, gently, desperately, to keep every mirror where it was, *for the record*, and Marisol had hated herself for agreeing.

The mirror showed the room a half-beat late. Marisol had stopped looking into the mirrors three days ago, but she saw it anyway in her peripheral vision: her own reflection arriving at the doorway a fraction of a second after she did, like an echo you could see.

"Birdie." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Baby. What are you counting?"

Birdie turned. Her eyes were wide open and perfectly calm, which frightened Marisol more than tears would have. Seven-year-olds were supposed to cry at 3 a.m. Seven-year-olds were not supposed to look like clerks.

"He got to five," Birdie said. "He's never gotten to five before."

"Who, sweetheart? Who got to five?"

"The counting man." She said it the way other children said *the mailman*. "He was doing his fingers. One, two, three, four. He always stops at four. But tonight—" she held up her own small hand, splayed all five fingers against the nightlight's glow, "—he did this one too."

Marisol's mouth had gone dry. "And then what?"

"And then he pointed at the glass." Birdie nodded toward the mirror. "And the lady came."

*The lady.* Marisol felt the word land somewhere beneath her ribs. "What lady, baby?"

"The lady in the glass." Birdie lay back down then, suddenly, terribly ordinary, pulling her blanket up under her chin. "She's sad. She's been in there a long time. Longer than the fire." A pause, and then, with the merciless precision of children: "Longer than Grandma's grandma."

Marisol sat down on the edge of the bed and gathered her daughter against her and did not let her voice shake. "Go back to sleep, mi amor. I'm right here."

Birdie closed her eyes. Within two minutes her breathing went long and even, as though she hadn't just handed her mother a sentence that would keep Marisol upright until dawn:

*Longer than Grandma's grandma.*

Marisol stayed until the sky went gray, watching the mirror in the closet show her a room that kept arriving slightly late.

* * *

By seven she had made coffee strong enough to strip paint and was sitting across the kitchen table from Elodie, who looked like they hadn't slept either. There was ash on their sleeve again. They kept finding ash on their sleeves lately, and neither of them mentioned it, because mentioning it would make it real, and they had both become experts in the economy of the unreal.

"Birdie counted to five last night," Marisol said.

Elodie's hand stopped halfway to their mug. "Five."

"She says the counting man has always stopped at four. Four Lockridges. Us. But last night he added a fifth finger, and then—" Marisol made herself say it plainly, because plainness was armor, "—she says a lady came out of the glass. A lady who's been in there longer than the fire. Longer than our grandmother's grandmother."

Elodie set the mug down without drinking. Marisol watched them do the arithmetic she was already doing — the same terrible ledger-math the house itself seemed to practice.

"That's not possible," Elodie said quietly. "The fire was 1963. Hester's mother would have been a child then. Before that—"

"Before that is exactly my point." Marisol leaned forward. "Elodie. I've been telling myself this thing is about your family. Your debt. Your fire. Something Wren inherited and we got caught in the blast radius. But if there's a woman in those mirrors who predates the fire by generations—" her voice cracked and she pressed through it, "—then this house hasn't taken one person. It's taken *many*. One per generation, maybe. Maybe more. It's not a debt, it's a *collection*."

Elodie stared at the table. When they spoke, their voice had the flat, careful quality Marisol had come to recognize as their documentation voice — the voice they used when feeling would otherwise drown them.

"I need to talk to her. To Birdie. While it's fresh."

"She's seven."

"I know how old she is." Elodie's jaw tightened. "I also know that everything else in this house lies, and she doesn't. She's the only witness here whose testimony isn't compromised. I hate asking. I'm asking anyway."

Marisol thought about saying no. She thought about packing the car right then, waking Birdie, driving until the road stopped looping — though they both knew the road looped, they'd tried it twice, the highway folding them gently but inexorably back toward the mailbox with LOCKRIDGE stenciled on it. Flight was a door painted on a wall. Which left only one direction: through.

"Breakfast first," Marisol said. "Pancakes. Normal things. Then questions. And Elodie—" she waited until they met her eyes, "—you stop the moment she gets scared. The record isn't worth her."

"No," Elodie agreed. "It isn't. Nothing is."

* * *

Birdie ate three pancakes and drew pictures while the adults pretended it was an ordinary Saturday morning. Then Elodie knelt beside her chair with a fresh sheet of paper and a box of crayons, and Marisol stood at the counter pretending to wash dishes that didn't need washing, close enough to intervene, far enough not to crowd.

"Birdie, can you tell me about the counting man again? Just a little bit?"

"He stands in my doorway at night." Birdie selected the black crayon. "He doesn't walk. He's just *there*, like when you blink and someone moved. He does his fingers." She demonstrated, touching each fingertip in sequence. "One, two, three, four. He always stops at four. Until last night."

"What happened last night?"

"He did five." She said it with a small frown, as if the counting man had broken a rule she'd relied on. "And when he did five, the glass got warm. I could feel it from my bed. Like somebody breathing on a window. And then the lady was standing behind him."

Marisol's hands went still in the dishwater.

"What did the lady look like?" Elodie asked, gentle as a surgeon.

"Old-fashioned." Birdie began drawing: a tall figure, a taller figure beside it. "Dress down to here." She dragged the crayon past the figure's feet. "Hair pulled back tight. She was holding her hands like this—" Birdie clasped her fingers together, knuckles white, a gesture of someone holding something shut or being held shut, "—and she was crying, but quiet. The kind where you don't make noise so nobody hears you."

Marisol turned off the faucet. The kitchen was very quiet.

"Did she say anything?" Elodie asked.

Birdie shook her head. Then paused. "Not words. Numbers. She was mouthing numbers. Same ones over and over." Birdie's brow furrowed with the effort of exact recall. "Seven. Nine. Two."

*Seven nine two.* Elodie wrote it on their palm in pen, immediately, the way they wrote everything now — as if their own memory could no longer be trusted to hold anything overnight.

"Birdie, the lady in the glass. Has she been there every night? Since we came?"

Birdie considered this with the gravity of a judge. "No. She's been there *always*. She told me. Well—" she corrected herself, "—she didn't tell me. I just knew it, like you know the stove is hot. She's been in the glass since before the house was even this house. She said—" Birdie's voice dropped, going sing-song, reciting something received rather than remembered, and Marisol felt the hair rise on her arms, "—*they built the new house on top of my name.*"

The dish towel slipped from Marisol's hand.

"They built the new house on top of my name," Elodie repeated softly. "Birdie, sweetheart, did she say anything else? Anything about her name?"

Birdie shook her head. "Names are the only thing you can't get back once they're gone. That's why he counts them." She pointed at the tall figure in her drawing — the counting man. "That's his whole job. Counting names so they don't disappear all the way." She picked up the red crayon and, with terrible calm, drew a small flame at the bottom corner of the page. "But some of them disappeared anyway. Before he started. Those are the ones in the glass."

Marisol crossed the kitchen and lifted the paper from under Birdie's hands. Two figures. The tall one with raised fingers. The woman in the long dress, hands clasped, tear-tracks rendered as careful silver lines. And behind them both, filling the rest of the page edge to edge, rows and rows of small marks — tally strokes, dozens of them, grouped in fives, marching off the paper like a fence made of counting.

"Birdie," Marisol said, and her voice did shake now, and she let it, "did you draw these?"

Birdie looked up at her with mild surprise. "No. They were already on the paper. Underneath. You have to look sideways to see them."

* * *

They spent the afternoon in the attic.

Elodie went up first, alone, and came back down ten minutes later gray-faced, carrying a wooden crate that had not, by their account, been in the attic yesterday. Marisol didn't ask how they knew. In this house the question *how do you know* had stopped having useful answers.

Inside the crate: papers. Deeds, letters, a child's copybook, a lock of hair tied with ribbon gone brown. The sediment of a family older than the one Marisol had married into — or thought she had. Elodie spread documents across the kitchen table with the reverence of an archaeologist and the urgency of a drowning person, narrating in that low documentation voice.

"The Lockrides bought this land in 1841. But there was a homestead here before — a different surname entirely." Their finger stopped on a tax roll, yellowed and foxed with age. "Look. 1838 census. This property, household of six." They tapped a line where ink had been scratched away — not faded, *scratched*, deliberately, by a nib dug hard enough to score the paper. "Every other household on the page is legible. Only this one was effaced. Someone went down this list and removed one name."

"One name out of six," Marisol said slowly. "So five remained. Five people in the house when the count began."

"And Birdie's lady — hands clasped, dress a century out of fashion, mouthing numbers." Elodie looked up, and their eyes were wet and burning at once. "She's not a victim of the fire. She's the *first*. The original entry. The debt didn't start in 1963 — 1963 was just the most recent payment. This house has been running a tab for almost two hundred years, and every generation, it collects."

Marisol braced her hands on the table's edge. Beneath her palms, faint as a pulse through wood, she felt it: the ticking. Patient. Unhurried. A ledger being kept.

"Then why five?" she asked. "Why did the count go to five last night? If it takes one per generation, and it took Vetch in '63, and it tried to take Elodie—"

"It's not taking," Elodie said. "It's *reconciling*. Think about it. Four living Lockridges — me, you, Birdie, and—" they faltered on Wren's name, pushed through, "—and Wren, who it already collected. Four. Last night it added a fifth finger. A fifth entry." They turned the scratched census page toward her. "It's not counting us, Marisol. It's counting *everyone it ever took*. And last night it remembered one more. The lady. The one they erased so thoroughly that even the ledger lost her — until Birdie gave her back."

"The lady in the glass," Marisol whispered. "It forgot her. Even the thing that took her forgot her. Because the family scrubbed her name off the paper."

"And now it's remembering." Elodie's voice dropped. "Which means the count isn't finished. It's *correcting*. And I don't think it corrects by subtraction."

Outside, the light through the kitchen window stuttered — a full second, maybe two, where the afternoon went dim and the reflections in the darkened glass arrived before the things that cast them. Marisol watched her own face appear in the window pane a heartbeat ahead of her movement, reversed, wrong, and understood with total clarity that the lag was no longer behind them. It was reaching forward now. Reaching for whatever came next.

She looked at her daughter, who was calmly coloring at the end of the table, adding silver tear-tracks to a woman no adult had ever seen, and something in Marisol's chest closed like a fist.

For three days she had wanted only one thing: the car, the road, distance. She had fought Elodie about it. She had packed bags in secret. But flight was a painted door, and the road brought them home, and home was a creditor.

So be it.

If this thing ran on names — if its whole architecture was erasure, if its power lived in the scratched-out lines and the burned files and the funerals held for the wrong twin — then names were the weapon. Every name it had swallowed. Starting with a woman in a long dress who had been waiting in the glass for two hundred years for someone to ask her what she was called.

"Elodie," Marisol said, and her fear had transmuted into something harder and cleaner. "Get every document out of that crate. Every deed, every census, every letter. We're going to find out who she was — the lady, the scratched-out name, all of them. All of them, do you hear me? Because I am not letting my daughter grow up in a world where the answer to being forgotten is becoming a tally mark."

From the end of the table, without looking up from her drawing, Birdie spoke in her small, level voice:

"She wants to know if you'll say it right. Her name. She says people always say it wrong, and that's how they lost it the first time."

The ticking in the walls stopped.

In the window's darkening reflection, Marisol saw the kitchen behind her — and in it, faint as breath on glass, a woman in a long dress, standing between the chairs, hands clasped, waiting to be introduced.
