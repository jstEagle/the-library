## 11. Six Names

Edith Vane's house sat at the top of Harrow Street like a held breath — a narrow brick row house with iron railings furred white with frost that no other railing on the street seemed to have. Tamsin noticed it from the sidewalk and told herself it was shade, or drainage, or some accident of the roofline above. She had gotten very good at telling herself things. It was practically a profession.

The door opened before she knocked.

"You're the one who's been photographing the hole," Edith said. She was small and upright, dressed in a cardigan the color of weak tea, her white hair pinned with a precision that suggested it had been done at dawn and would not be touched again until tomorrow. Her eyes were pale blue and very steady. "I've watched you come up the street three times this week. You always stop at my gate before you decide not to come in."

"I wasn't sure you'd want visitors."

"I'm eighty-one years old, Miss Thornbury. I don't want visitors. I want answers, and visitors are the only thing anyone will trade them for." She stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before the cold does."

Inside, the house smelled of lavender and something older underneath it — stone dust, maybe, or the memory of stone dust. The parlor was immaculate: doilies on the chair arms, a mantel clock ticking with the patience of something that had outlasted several owners, photographs in silver frames arranged in careful rows. Tamsin's eyes went to them automatically, the way they went to any wall, any surface, cataloguing.

She found him in the third frame. A young man in a work jacket, squinting into sun, standing in front of a municipal truck with *HARROW STREET IMPROVEMENTS* stenciled on its door. He had Edith's pale eyes and a grin that took up most of his face.

"That's my brother," Edith said, following her gaze. She said it lightly, the way people say things they've said so many times the words have worn smooth as river stones. "Walter. He worked the 1974 project. They relocated after the collapse, all of them. Scattered to other cities. He writes sometimes."

"From where?"

Edith's teacup paused halfway to its saucer. Just for a moment. Then it completed its journey with a small ceramic click. "I couldn't tell you," she said. "He moves around."

Tamsin set her bag down on the carpet, deliberately, the way you set down something heavy when you intend to stay a while. She took out the printout she'd made at the archive, the microfilm page flattened now under glass at the library but reproduced here in gray laser toner: **MUNICIPAL PROJECT 74-H, PERSONNEL REASSIGNMENT FOLLOWING STRUCTURAL INCIDENT**, six names in a typewritten column.

She laid it on the low table between them, facing Edith.

* * *

For a long moment the only sound was the mantel clock. Edith looked at the page without touching it, the way you look at a snake you suspect is dead.

"Where did you get this?"

"The city archive. It isn't sealed. Nobody's asked for it in fifty years, apparently." Tamsin kept her voice level. She'd learned that from her father's notebook, oddly — the few legible entries were all in that same flat, disciplined hand, facts first, feeling nowhere. "Six names. All listed as reassigned. All listed with forwarding addresses that turn out to be vacant lots, demolished buildings, or in two cases, addresses that never existed at all." She tapped the page once, gently. "This is your brother. This is the foreman. These four are crew. And there's no record anywhere — pension records, tax rolls, death certificates, marriage licenses — of what happened to any of them after 1974. Six men walked into a tunnel and six paper trails end like cut threads."

"People fall out of records. It happens."

"Not six at once. Not this cleanly. Records don't end, Mrs. Vane. They fray. Someone forgets to file something, someone misspells a name, someone gets married and creates a whole new trail of paper. Fraying is what records do." Tamsin leaned forward. "These didn't fray. These were cut. By someone with access, someone official, someone who wanted six men erased so thoroughly that even their own families—" She stopped. Started again, softer. "Even their own sisters got a story instead of a truth."

Edith's hands were folded in her lap. Tamsin saw that the knuckles were white, though the rest of the old woman might have been listening to a report about roadwork.

"My mother kept a box," Tamsin went on. "My father was on this list too. Sixth name. He went down in '74 and never came up, and the city told her he'd been reassigned, and then they told her he'd moved away, and eventually they stopped telling her anything at all because she stopped asking. She kept his field kit in a storage unit for forty years because some part of her never believed them." She drew a breath. "I found the kit last week. There's a brass token with a spiral stamped on it. There's a notebook with pages torn out. And there's a note in his handwriting that says, *if it knocks, don't answer twice.*"

The clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked — twice, pause, twice — and both women flinched, and both pretended they hadn't.

"You think I know something," Edith said finally.

"I think you remember wrong," Tamsin said. "When I called you on Tuesday, you told me you remembered the 1974 project vividly. The noise, the trucks, the summer of it. But you placed yourself in Leeds that autumn, visiting an aunt. I checked. Your aunt died in 1971. There is no Leeds. There's no visit. You've built a room in your memory and furnished it and locked the door, and you go there whenever anything knocks." She nodded at the list. "You're on the deed to this land, Mrs. Vane. You have been since 1968. Nothing happened on this site — nothing was found, nothing was buried, no door was sealed — without your knowledge or your leave. So either you know what's under Harrow Street, or you know that you don't want to."

Edith rose. Tamsin thought she was going to show her out, and braced herself for the door, the cold street, another dead end. But Edith only walked to the mantel and stood looking at her brother's photograph, her back straight as a fence post.

"He was twenty-six," she said.

Tamsin didn't move. Didn't breathe loud.

"It was January when they found it. Not the summer — the summer was the digging, the ordinary part, storm drains and survey stakes and men complaining about their backs. My Walter was the youngest on the crew and the best with the boring rig. In January they broke through into a space that wasn't on any survey, and Mr. Calloway — the foreman, first name on your list — he sent everyone up and went to the city, and the city sent engineers, and the engineers said, fill it, pour it, forget it. That's what the engineers said." Her voice had gone somewhere distant and flat. "But it was winter by then, a bad one, and the men had already been down. Already seen it."

"Seen what?"

"A well." Edith turned around. Her face hadn't crumpled; that was the terrible thing. It had gone smooth, like ice forming over water. "Dry stone. Older than the street, older than the city, older than anything the engineers could name, and they tried — Roman, medieval, Victorian — and none of it fit. The spirals carved in the rim didn't belong to any century. And at the bottom, something pale, turning. Slow. Like something asleep that turns over in its sleep." She came back to her chair but did not sit. She stood behind it, hands gripping the back, knuckles bloodless. "And it spoke to them."

Tamsin's skin tightened. "Spoke how?"

"Through the frost." Edith laughed, a small dry sound with no humor in it whatsoever. "That's the part I've spent fifty years trying to forget and fifty years failing to. It didn't use a voice, not properly. It wrote. On the inside of the lantern glass, on the frozen mud, on the backs of their hands. Lines and ferns and feathers, and if you watched long enough the lines became letters and the letters became words. And the words said: *give me a name to keep, and I will hold the winter off.*"

The radiator under the window clanked. Outside, the light through the lace curtains had gone the color of dishwater.

"They thought it was a joke at first," Edith continued. "Calloway thought one of the men was scratching the words himself. But it kept writing while they all stood watching, and it wrote things it shouldn't have known. It wrote Calloway's daughter's birthday. It wrote the amount of money Walter owed the bank. It knew them, Miss Thornbury. It had been down there listening to the city for longer than there had been a city, and it knew every name that had ever been shouted over an open grate." Her grip on the chair tightened. "And the winter that year was killing people. Three men froze in their beds in this neighborhood in one week. The papers called it a cold snap. The well called it an opening bid."

"So they gave it a name."

"They gave it a name." Edith closed her eyes. "Not one of theirs. That was the argument — hours of it, down in that chamber, men shouting and crying and one of them praying. Because they understood, the way you understand a thing in your bones before your mind consents to it, that the name was the payment. Whatever it meant to *keep* a name, it would cost the man whose name it was. And no one would give their own. So they chose." Her voice thinned to a wire. "They chose the youngest. The one with no wife, no children, no one to fight for him. They chose my brother, and they told themselves it was arithmetic."

Tamsin felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does when a building settles, or when the world does.

"And Walter?" she managed. "Did he—"

"He heard them deciding." Edith's eyes opened. They were dry, and that was worse than tears. "He was standing right there. And do you know what he did? He thanked them. He said if it was going to be someone, better it be someone willing, because he'd read enough old stories to know that mattered. Willingness. Terms. It has terms, that thing. It can't take. It can only receive." She let go of the chair and looked at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "He shook every man's hand. He kissed my cheek — I was there, I'd come down to bring them thermoses of tea, God help me, I carried the tea down into that place — and he climbed over the rim of the well and he walked down. Willingly. And the pale thing rose to meet him like frost rising off a field at dawn, and it took him, and the winter lifted within the week. The papers called it a change in the weather."

"And the door."

"Calloway had it built. Sealed from the inside, so that nothing could follow it up — that was the reasoning, though I think mostly it was so that no one would ever have to see what was down there again. The mason was one of the six. When it was done, the city cut the reassignment orders, and the six men signed agreements, and they scattered, and they were forbidden to contact each other or their families about the site. Forbidden on pain of — I don't know what. Calloway collected the paperwork himself. He visited me once a year until he died, to make sure I was keeping the story straight." She smiled, and it was the loneliest expression Tamsin had ever seen. "Leeds. My aunt in Leeds. I built that room myself, Miss Thornbury. Every year I added furniture to it. It was easier than remembering my brother climbing down."

Tamsin's throat ached. "Your sister's son," she said quietly. "Or nephew. Someone should have—"

"There was no one else. Our parents were gone. I was the only one left to un-remember him." Edith finally sat, folding down onto the chair like something collapsing slowly. "And now you've brought the list into my parlor and torn the door off the room, and I can feel him everywhere. Fifty years of forgetting, undone in ten minutes. Do you know what that's like? It's like frost coming back across a window. Line by line by line."

* * *

Tamsin stayed another hour. She drank tea she didn't taste and listened to details she would later write down in her father's notebook, beneath the torn stubs of the missing pages, as if she could graft new growth onto the cut: the exact words written in frost (*a name to keep, and the winter stays outside*), the layout of the chamber as Edith remembered it, the sound the well made — not knocking, Edith insisted, though the men had called it knocking, more like a heartbeat heard through a wall, patient and vast.

At the door, Edith caught her wrist. Her grip was stronger than it looked.

"The token you found," she said. "The brass one with the spiral. Was it warm?"

"No," Tamsin said. "Cold. Cold as the ground."

Edith's face did something complicated. "It was warm when Walter carried it. He wore it on a cord around his neck. His lucky piece, he called it. He was wearing it when he climbed down." She released Tamsin's wrist. "If it's cold now, then it's been down there since. Or something has been holding it. And if something is holding it—" She stopped herself, pressed her lips together, and visibly, deliberately, began building the Leeds room back up around herself. Tamsin watched it happen: the shoulders settling, the eyes going gently vague, the smoothness returning like ice closing over water.

"Thank you for the tea," Edith said pleasantly. "Do come again sometime."

Outside, the cold hit Tamsin like a hand laid flat against her chest. Down the hill, the excavation site glowed under floodlights, and even from here she could hear it — faint, rhythmic, rising out of the ground beneath Harrow Street.

Three knocks. A pause.

Three knocks.

And then, unmistakably, a fourth — soft, almost shy, like a correction. Like something learning a new rhythm. Like something practicing a word it intended to say soon.
