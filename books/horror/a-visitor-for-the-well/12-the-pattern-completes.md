## 12. The Pattern Completes

Dev Okafor had worked construction long enough to know that a site at three in the morning was supposed to be silent. Not dead — never dead, the way a body is dead, but quiet in the way of things resting between shifts. The generators idled down. The mud held the day's bootprints like a ledger waiting to be read. The floodlights on their wooden poles buzzed and threw long shadows across the excavation, and if you stood at the edge and looked down into it, you could almost believe the hole was just a hole.

He'd been sleeping in the site trailer for eleven nights now. He told himself it was about security — equipment theft was real, insurance premiums were real, and the Harrow Street job had already eaten two months of schedule. He told himself the cot in the trailer was more comfortable than his apartment anyway, which was a lie he'd gotten good at telling. The truth was simpler and worse: he didn't want to be across town when something happened here. Whatever was going to happen here.

The cold woke him.

Not a sound — a temperature. He surfaced from a thin sleep the way a swimmer surfaces through ice water, all at once, gasping, and lay still in the dark of the trailer listening to his own breath fog above him. The little electric heater he'd bought at the hardware store was running. He could hear its fan turning. But the air coming out of the vents was cold, cold as the air going in, and frost had climbed the inside of the trailer's single window in ferns so intricate they looked printed, look pressed there by some enormous careful thumb.

He'd seen frost ferns before. Everyone had, on winter glass. But these were wrong in a way his mind kept trying to correct and kept failing to correct: they grew inward from the edges toward the center, converging, like veins feeding a heart rather than branching away from one. And at the center of the window, where all the fronds met, there was a shape. A spiral. Small, no bigger than a coin, drawn in clear glass where the frost refused to form.

Dev sat up. His hands were shaking, and he watched them shake with the detached interest of a man watching someone else's hands.

*It's just weather,* he thought. *Unseasonal front. Priya said the barometer dropped like a stone this afternoon.*

Priya. The name arrived in his head and brought everything else with it, the way a key brings a door. She hadn't gone home. She'd told him at end of shift — she'd stood by her truck in the orange work light, keys in hand, and said, "I'm not driving tonight, Dev. I don't trust myself behind the wheel yet." And he'd said okay, take the bunk in the second trailer, we'll talk in the morning, because what else do you say to a woman who's been hearing her own voice calling up out of the ground? You say okay. You say morning. You say the things that keep the night at arm's length.

He was out of the cot and into his boots without remembering lacing them. The trailer door stuck with cold and then gave, and the night took him whole.

The cold outside wasn't weather. That was the first thing he understood, standing on the frozen mud in his coat with his breath streaming. Weather moved. Weather came in off the lake or down from the north and you could feel its edges, feel it lean against you and pass. This cold was stationary. It sat over the site like a lid, and beyond the chain-link fence — he could see it clearly under the floodlights — the grass across the street glittered dry and unfrosted, moving in a wind that never reached him. The world on the other side of the fence was having an ordinary October night. The world inside the fence was somewhere else entirely.

"Priya." He said it aloud, and his voice fell straight down into the excavation and did not echo. That was the second thing he understood. Sound didn't come back up out of that hole anymore. He'd noticed it three days ago and told himself it was acoustics, the geometry of the cut, the damp soil absorbing. Now, standing alone at the edge at three in the morning, he let himself know what he'd known all along: the hole was swallowing sound the way it swallowed heat, the way it swallowed the light from the floodlamps so that the bottom of the excavation was always darker than physics allowed, always a few degrees past what the depth justified.

Her trailer was empty. He checked anyway — threw the door open, called her name into the narrow bunk room, saw the blankets thrown back, her boots gone, her phone on the pillow. Her phone on the pillow. Priya Raman had not been separated from that phone since Dev had known her; she took calls in the port-a-john, she checked load charts on it one-handed while eating lunch. It lay face-up on the blanket, screen dark, and beside it, arranged with terrible neatness, were her keys, her wallet, and her hard hat.

People didn't leave those things. People left in a hurry and grabbed those things without thinking. Leaving them behind, squared up like offerings, meant something else. Meant she hadn't left at all. Meant whatever walked out of that trailer had no use for pockets.

He ran for the excavation edge.

* * *

She was standing at the far side of the pit, down near the bottom, where the new concrete footings waited in their wooden forms like teeth not yet grown in.

Dev's first thought, absurdly, was that she was measuring something. She stood very straight, arms slightly out from her sides, palms forward, the way surveyors stand at instruments. Then he saw the frost.

It covered her left side completely. From the toes of her left boot up the left leg, across the left half of her jacket, up the left side of her neck, along her jaw, to her hair — which was white now, actually white, rimed and stiff — the frost lay on her in patterns, and the patterns were not random. Dev had spent enough evenings lately staring at window glass to recognize the vocabulary: ferns and fronds, spirals nested in spirals, lines that branched and rejoined. But on Priya the design was finished. Complete. Every line connected to every other line, every spiral closed, nothing trailing off unfinished the way frost always trails off, the way ice always finds itself interrupted by a warm edge. This was frost the way a signature is ink. It covered exactly half of her and stopped at her centerline with a precision that made his stomach turn over — a clean vertical boundary running from crown to sole, frost on one side, living skin on the other, as if she'd been dipped halfway into winter and withdrawn.

And she was breathing. He could see the vapor rising from her mouth, steady and slow, four seconds in, six seconds out, a rhythm too deep and too even for a waking person. Her right eye was open. Her left eye was frosted shut, a lace of ice sealing the lid like a gift being wrapped.

"Priya!" He was climbing down before he'd decided to, boots skidding on the frozen ladder rungs, the cold biting through his gloves so hard his fingers went wooden within seconds. "Priya, I'm coming, stay there—"

As if she could go anywhere. As if she were anywhere at all.

At the bottom of the pit the silence was total. His own footsteps made no sound. He crossed the frozen mud toward her and the frost on the ground rose to meet his steps — ferns blooming under each bootfall, mapping his approach, and he remembered Tamsin saying that word, *ledger*, weeks ago in the trailer with her maps spread out, and he'd thought it was a metaphor. Nothing down here was a metaphor.

Up close, the design on her was worse. Up close he could see that the pattern wasn't decoration. It was writing. Or it was a map. Or it was both — lines that resolved, when he let his eyes unfocus, into something like script, characters in no alphabet he knew, flowing down her arm and across her chest and around the curve of her shoulder, densest over her heart, where the spirals gathered into a knot the size of his fist. And beneath the frost, where the skin showed through thin places, her veins had gone dark and visible, tracing the same branching architecture underneath, as if the design were being executed on both surfaces at once, outside and in.

"Priya." He put his hand on her right shoulder — the warm one, the human one — and shook her gently. Her body swayed and returned to true, like a plumb bob. "Hey. Hey, it's Dev. Come back now."

Her right eye moved. Found him. Focused — and there was a horrible interval, two seconds, three, where he watched her look at him with absolutely nothing behind the looking, a flat animal attention, before something kindled behind it and she knew him again.

"Dev." Her voice was hoarse, scraped, as if she'd been using it for hours. As if she'd been talking to someone. "It's so cold."

"I know. I've got you. We're getting you out of here right now."

"I couldn't stop walking." Her eye tracked past him, up the wall of the excavation, to the sealed door in its niche of ancient stone — the door they'd found in August, the door with the iron ring fused solid, the door that had started all of this. "I kept telling myself to turn around and my legs wouldn't. It was like — you know when you're almost asleep and your arm twitches? Like that. Except the twitch was me. All of me. I was the twitch."

"What were you doing down here?"

"I don't—" She frowned, and the frown cracked the frost along her jaw with a sound like stepping on thin ice, and Dev flinched. "I got up to use the bathroom. I think. Did I leave my truck lights on?" She looked at him with sudden anxious clarity, a person surfacing. "Why am I — Dev, why am I covered in ice?"

"We're going to figure that out. Can you walk?"

"I can walk." She took a step and staggered and he caught her, and through her jacket his gloved hand felt the wrongness of her left side — rigid, dense, cold as a headstone. When he steered her toward the ladder she went, but her head turned as they moved, her frosted eye dragging across the bottom of the pit, across the footing forms, and she said, quietly, conversationally, in a voice that was hers and not hers:

"It's nearly done counting."

"Priya—"

"The knocking stopped," she said. "Did you notice? It stopped because it doesn't need to knock anymore. Knocking is how you ask. It's done asking." Her right eye rolled to him, wide and wet and terrified, and for a moment she was entirely herself, drowning in her own mouth. "Dev, I can't remember my sister's face. I've been trying since Tuesday. I know I have a sister. I know her name starts with M. There's just — a shape where she was. A Priya-shaped hole wearing her name."

"Stop talking. Save your strength. Ladder's right here."

"I used to know why I came to this city," she said, letting him lift her toward the rungs. "I used to know lots of things. They're going one at a time. Little ones first. My address. My PIN. My sister's face. It's polite about it, that's the worst part. It takes them so gently you don't feel the—"

Her voice stopped. Not trailed off — stopped, mid-word, like a needle lifted from a record, and for three full seconds she hung in his arms with both eyes closed and the frost on her jaw creaking softly as she breathed.

Then her right eye opened and she smiled at him, warm and puzzled, a stranger's smile.

"Sorry — do I know you?" Priya said. "You look really familiar. Are we on the same crew or something?"

* * *

They got her up the ladder together, which took twenty minutes because her left side would not bend properly at the knee and Dev ended up carrying most of her weight while she climbed with the unembarrassed cooperation of a child. At the top he wrapped her in the emergency blanket from the first-aid box and sat her in the trailer with the heater blasting and called the number Tamsin had left him — the only number, scrawled on the back of one of her survey sheets, under the words *for emergencies that are actually emergencies.*

Tamsin answered on the second ring, at 3:40 in the morning, voice instantly awake, as if she'd been lying on top of sleep fully dressed the way soldiers do.

"It's Priya," Dev said. "I found her at the bottom of the pit. Standing in the frost. Half of her was — Tamsin, the design was finished. It was complete. Like it had been signed."

Silence on the line. Then, carefully: "Is she hurt?"

"She's alive. She's warm now. The ice is melting off her." He looked through the trailer's inner doorway at Priya, who sat wrapped in silver foil, holding a cup of instant coffee she hadn't drunk, regarding the trailer's interior with mild tourist interest. "But Tamsin — she doesn't remember me. She asked if we worked together. She's been on my crew eight months. She doesn't remember the site, the door, any of it. She keeps asking why she's in this part of town. She says she was on her way home from her sister's place." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it. "She doesn't have a sister here. Her sister's in Chennai. She told me about her in July, when her nephew was born."

Another silence, longer. When Tamsin spoke again her voice had changed — flattened, stripped, the voice of someone reading instruments in a storm.

"The frost on her. Was it on both sides or one?"

"One. Left side. Stopped dead at her centerline."

"And the pattern — was every line closed? Every spiral complete? Nothing open-ended?"

"Yes. How did you—"

"Because it's a receipt," Tamsin said. "A finished pattern means a completed transaction. Something was taken and the well wrote it down. Dev, listen to me. In 1974 there were six names on that project roster. Six men went down and one stayed. Edith told me the crew gave the well a name to hold, and the frost stopped for forty years, and everyone got to live their lives thinking they'd gotten away with it." A breath. "But the well isn't holding a name anymore. Something's changed down there. It's collecting again. First it takes the small things — Priya told me she was forgetting pieces for days. Address, faces, names. It's pricing her. And when the pattern finishes—" 

She stopped.

"When the pattern finishes, what?" Dev said. "Tamsin. When it finishes, what happens?"

Across the trailer, Priya looked up from her coffee cup and caught his eye, and smiled that pleasant stranger's smile, and said, "You have a nice face. Do I know you from somewhere?"

"Tamsin," Dev said, turning away from her, gripping the phone, watching his breath fog the window where the frost ferns had begun, slowly, patiently, to grow again. "What does it mean when the pattern finishes?"

Down the line, very quietly, Tamsin Thornbury said: "It means it knows what it's taking. And Dev — it stopped knocking last night. It's not asking anymore. It's spelling."
