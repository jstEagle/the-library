## 18. The Man at the Bottom

The shaft did not end so much as open.

One moment Tamsin was climbing down rungs of iron gone soft with cold, her breath pluming in the lantern light, and the next the walls fell away on every side and she stood in a chamber that had no business existing beneath Harrow Street — a vaulted hollow of dark stone, dry as a held breath, wide enough that Dev's headlamp found nothing to land on across from them. The air smelled of snow on stone, the same smell as the door's exhale, but older here. Concentrated. Like standing inside the memory of winter rather than winter itself.

The frost ferns had followed them down. With each step they bloomed underfoot in feathering white branches, precise as etchings, and Tamsin understood now what they were: a ledger. The well was keeping accounts. Every step they took toward it was being written down somewhere, in a hand made of ice, and she tried not to think about what column their names were filed under.

"Tamsin." Dev's voice came out flattened by the space, stripped of echo. He had his crowbar gripped like a rosary. "You seeing this?"

She was seeing it.

At the center of the chamber, sunk into the floor like a pupil into an eye, stood the well.

It was smaller than she'd imagined — barely three feet across, its rim of dry-stacked stone carved with spirals that turned inward, always inward, so that looking at them too long made her feel as though she were being gently wound. No mortar. No water. The darkness inside it went down further than the light would follow, and something pale turned at the bottom of that darkness, slow as a minute hand, patient as geology.

But none of that was what stopped her breath.

It was the man sitting against the well's rim.

He sat with his back to the carved spirals, legs stretched out, hands resting open on his thighs, in the posture of someone who had been waiting a long time and had made his peace with the waiting. He wore a canvas jacket gone gray with dust, work boots, a knit cap pulled low. His skin was pale but not corpse-pale — more like paper left in a window. Not decayed. Not aged. Fifty years underground and he looked like a photograph of himself taken yesterday and left out in the sun too long, all the color leached but every line still sharp.

Tamsin knew the set of those shoulders. She had inherited them. She saw them every morning in her own reflection and had spent ten years telling herself it was coincidence.

"Dad," she said.

The word didn't echo. Nothing echoed here. It just went out of her and lay flat on the stone between them.

The man's head turned. Slowly, joint by joint, like something remembering how hinges worked. His face came around into the lantern light, and Tamsin felt her knees try to quit.

It was him. It was her father, Edmund Thornbury, who walked into the ground in 1974 and never walked out, who existed in her life now only as a boot full of artifacts and a mother who never said his name above a whisper. He was forty-one years old. He had been forty-one years old for half a century.

And he looked at her with eyes like frosted glass and said, pleasantly, the way you'd greet a stranger at a bus stop:

"Is it morning? I can't tell if it's morning."

* * *

Dev caught her elbow before her legs finished failing. "That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "Tamsin. That is not possible."

"He doesn't know me." Her voice sounded far away, borrowed. "Fifty years. He's been down here fifty years and he looks—"

She approached him the way you approach a dog that might be injured or might be rabid, crouching slowly into his field of view. Up close the wrongness resolved into detail. His breath, when he exhaled, fogged — and the fog did not dissipate. It hung in front of his mouth in small branching shapes, ferns of vapor, blooming and fading and blooming again, like he was breathing out tiny winters with every exhale.

His eyes tracked her. There was something behind the frost of them, some flicker, some animal attention, but it kept sliding off her face like water off waxed canvas.

"You've got his chin," he said suddenly. "My daughter has a chin like that. Did you know her?"

Tamsin's whole chest seized. "I *am* her."

He smiled at that, warm and vacant, the smile of a man agreeing with weather. "Everyone says something like that," he said. "Down here everyone's somebody. There was a fellow named Aldous, or maybe I was Aldous, it changes." He patted the stone beside him, an invitation. "Sit. It's easier if you sit. Standing up, the thoughts slide right off you."

She sat. The stone was cold through her clothes but not cruelly so — cold like a pew, like a place built for long attendance.

"What year is it?" he asked.

"2024."

He nodded gravely, absorbing this. "That's a good number. Even. Things should be even." A pause. His brow furrowed, and for one second something moved behind his eyes — something reaching, grasping — and then it was gone. "I had a wife. I'm nearly sure I had a wife. She made bread on Thursdays and she hummed when she kneaded, off-key, always the same song, and I can't—" His hands rose halfway to his temples and then dropped, defeated. "I can't find the song. I kept it right here. I kept everything right here."

He tapped his chest, and Tamsin heard it sound hollow. Actually hollow, a faint drum-note, like knocking on a door with nothing behind it.

* * *

Dev had circled wide, keeping the well's rim in view, keeping himself between the seated man and the darkness. Now he crouched near Tamsin, voice pitched low. "Look at his hands."

She looked. Her father's hands rested open on his knees, and across the backs of them, fine as lace, ran lines of frost — not sitting on the skin but *in* it, feathering patterns that shifted when she blinked, rearranging themselves like a document being redrafted. The same patterns Priya had worn. The same patterns Edith Vane's hands had traced without knowing.

"It's not keeping him alive," Dev murmured. "It's keeping him *together*. Look — the frost fills in where he's worn through. It's holding the shape of him."

"And wearing him out doing it," Tamsin whispered.

Because she could see it now, once she knew to look. The man at the bottom was complete the way a rented house is complete — furnished, tidy, everything in its place, and nothing in it belonging to anyone. When he spoke, the words came in fragments, whole sentences assembled from pieces of a life that no longer cohered:

"I have to be home by six, there's a—" (a pause, a search) "—there's a thing. On Thursdays. Bread."

"The crew went down on a Tuesday," Tamsin said gently. "You told Mom you'd be home for dinner Wednesday."

"Wednesday," he repeated, tasting it. "Wednesday. Yes. I remember Wednesdays. Wednesdays were for the survey notes." His face brightened with purpose. "I keep survey notes. Do you want to see my survey notes?"

He reached into his jacket and produced a notebook — her father's notebook, the twin of the one rotting in her bag, its pages dense with his cramped, slanting handwriting. But when he opened it, the pages were blank. All of them. Except that as he held it, faint lines surfaced on the paper like breath on glass — words rising, hanging, fading:

*the well holds what we give it*

*it gave back the cold instead of the name*

*I don't remember what I traded. I remember that I loved the trade.*

He stared at the surfacing words with mild interest, as though reading someone else's mail. "That's mine," he said. "I think that's mine. It's in my hand, anyway."

Tamsin's eyes burned. She looked away, toward the well, because looking at him was unbearable and looking away was worse.

The pale thing turned at the bottom of the shaft. In the lantern light she could see it better now — a shape like a figure seen through deep ice, elongated, drifting, turning end over end with the slow patience of something that had never once needed to hurry. And wrapped around it, trailing from it, threaded through it like ribbon through a spool, were *years*. She couldn't describe it any other way. Faint images hung in the pale mass the way frost hangs on glass: a woman humming over bread dough; a small girl with scraped knees holding up a drawing; a kitchen with yellow curtains; a birthday, candles, laughter caught mid-sound. His missing years. Fifty of them. Birthdays and Thursdays and Wednesdays and the off-key song, all of it spun into the thing at the bottom of the well, worn the way frost wears breath — visible for a moment on the cold surface, then gone, then forming again somewhere else.

It wasn't imprisoning him. It was *wearing* him. Wearing his life like a coat it had grown fond of.

"Dad." She took his hand. The frost in his skin was cold but the flesh beneath was warm — horribly, impossibly warm, alive, alive for fifty years down here in the dark. "Dad, look at me. My name is Tamsin. Tam. You used to call me Tam-o'-lantern in October. You taught me a knock — three and three, and then two, and I thought it was just a game, and I heard it under the city for ten years and I thought I was going crazy, and it was *you*, it was always you—"

Something happened in his face.

It was small. A crack in frosted glass. His eyes fixed on hers and for a moment — one suspended, aching moment — the vacancy drained and something looked out of him that was *him*, wholly him, Edmund Thornbury, surveyor, husband, father, the man who taught his daughter codes because he believed knowledge was the only inheritance that couldn't be repossessed.

"Tam-o'-lantern," he said. Wonderingly. "Three and three, and then two. So you'd always know it was me."

"Yes," she breathed. "Yes."

His grip tightened on her hand with sudden desperate strength. "Listen to me. Listen — I don't have long when I'm like this, it takes it back, it takes everything back — the name. They'll have told you about the name. We gave it a name to hold and it held the winter off, it *worked*, God forgive us it worked, and then it started collecting, because a name isn't a thing you give, girl, a name is a *load-bearing wall*—" His voice frayed. "I hid my true name even from your mother. Gave them the name I answered to instead. I thought I was clever. I thought I'd cheated it. But it keeps accounts, Tamsin. It has *fifty years* of accounts, and it's been sending the bill upstairs—"

The frost surged across his face like a tide coming in.

"—and the pale one knows yours," he finished, in a voice gone smooth and pleasant and empty, smiling at her with his daughter's chin. "Is it morning? I can't tell if it's morning."

* * *

Tamsin knelt there a long time, holding a hand that didn't hold her back.

Dev stood at the rim of the well, headlamp aimed down into the turning dark, and when he finally spoke his voice was rough. "The notes. Your dad's notes said return what was taken. That's why we came. Say the name back and it lets go of him."

"I know what the notes said."

"So say it."

"I don't *have* it." She laughed, and the laugh came out broken. "He hid his true name from everyone. From Mom. From the crew. From himself, maybe, by the end. It's not written anywhere. It's not in the notebook. How do you give something back that you can't even find?"

Dev was quiet. Then, slowly, he crouched beside the old man, studying him the way he studied blueprints — patiently, looking for the load paths. "Mister Thornbury. Sir. Can you hear me?"

"Is it morning?" the man asked kindly.

"Your name," Dev said. "Not the one on the paperwork. The real one. The one underneath. Where is it?"

The old man's head tilted. And then, with the serene confidence of the profoundly lost, he raised one frost-lined finger and pointed — past them, past the well, down into the turning pale —

"There," he said. "It's wearing it."

The thing at the bottom of the well turned, slow as a minute hand, and in the drift of its frozen years Tamsin saw it: a single thread burning brighter than the rest, wound tight at the pale thing's core, humming with a familiarity she couldn't place and couldn't escape. His true name. Fifty years of collateral, kept at the center of the collection, the way you keep the deed to the house.

And beneath it — fainter, newer, already spinning loose threads of its own — she saw a second pattern taking shape. Feathering, branching, precise as etchings.

A ledger entry, half-written.

In letters that were starting to look exactly like her own handwriting.
