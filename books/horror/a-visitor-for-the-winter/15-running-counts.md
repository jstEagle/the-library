## 15. Running Counts

The stairwell of her building had eleven steps to the first landing. Theo knew this the way she knew her own pulse—had known it for six years, since the day she moved in with two suitcases and a headlamp and a need to know exactly how many ways out every room contained. Eleven steps. She counted them now without meaning to, her lips moving around the numbers like a woman praying in a language she'd sworn off.

One. Two. Three.

She stopped on four.

*Don't*, she told herself. *You don't have to anymore. You're out.*

But that was the thing about counting—it had never been a cage. It had been a coat. And coats don't come off just because you've decided you're warm enough.

She climbed the remaining seven steps without finishing the count, and the incompleteness sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, cold and smooth and patient. It would wait. It always waited. Somewhere behind her sternum a small clerk with a ledger was already writing *eleven (incomplete)*, and would carry the debt forward into tomorrow, and the day after, until she paid it or died owing.

Her apartment smelled the way abandoned things smell when no one has loved them lately: dust and cold coffee and the ghost of instant noodles. She'd been gone eleven days. No—nine. Nine days below, three of them in the keepers' settlement, sleeping in a hammock strung between pipes older than the city above it, eating stew from dented tin bowls while Marisol's people told stories about founders and wells and the price of staying hidden.

*We're asking you to finish what they started,* Elder Ruth had said, her hands folded like a closed book. *Family. Purpose. Protection. All of it yours, if you answer.*

And Edith's voice—Edith's voice, which had been silent for three days by then, which had gone quiet in the well gallery exactly when Theo needed her most—had returned only long enough to say: *Don't. Please. Whatever they offer you, don't.*

So Theo had run. Not dramatically, not with a slammed door or a shouted refusal. She had simply waited until the settlement slept, taken her pack and her rope, and climbed back up through the dark, counting doors as she went because counting was the only prayer she had left.

Fourteen doors between the settlement and the surface ladder. She remembered all fourteen. She could draw them from memory: the rusted one with the wheel handle, the plywood one with the horseshoe nailed to it, the one that wasn't a door at all but a curtain of plastic sheeting that breathed against your face as you pushed through—

Stop.

She set her pack down inside her own front door and stood in the middle of her apartment, listening.

Silence. The refrigerator hummed. A car passed on the street below, tires hissing on wet asphalt. The radiator ticked its small mechanical heartbeat. All the ordinary sounds of an ordinary life, and none of them were a voice answering questions she hadn't asked aloud.

She should have felt safe. That was the arithmetic she'd built her whole life on: alone equals safe, invisible equals safe, unremarkable equals alive. For thirty-four years she had made herself small and quiet and countable, and the world had mostly left her alone, and she had called that winning.

Now she stood in her own kitchen at four in the morning, and the silence where Edith's voice used to be was louder than anything the tunnels had ever said.

* * *

Sleep didn't come so much as ambush her, sometime after dawn, dragging her down mid-thought like a riptide.

She woke at noon to sunlight through curtains she'd forgotten to close and her recorder blinking on the nightstand. Habit, older than thought: she pressed play before she was fully awake, before she remembered why she'd stopped listening to these files, before she could stop herself.

Static. Her own breathing, slow and tidal. The creak of the bed.

Then—a question.

It came in her own cadence, the way it always had, pitched low and worn soft, like a stone carried in a pocket for decades: *Do you think the water remembers being rain?*

Theo's thumb found the stop button so fast she nearly cracked the casing.

No. No, no, no. She was on the surface. Third floor. Concrete and rebar and forty feet of ordinary earth between her and the unmapped dark. The recordings couldn't reach up here. The voice lived below, in the tunnels, in the well, in the place where Edith Joubert—or whatever Edith had become—had poured herself into the dark a century ago to hold something shut.

She checked the file date. Last night. Recorded in this room, in this bed, while she slept nine feet above the street.

She played it again. Forty times, the way she had that first time, weeks ago, when the boundary between her mind and the tunnels had already thinned to gauze. But this time there was no dread crystallizing into obsession. This time it was worse. This time it was grief.

Because the question was Edith's. Not the well's. Not the thing the keepers fed with lies. *Edith's*—the woman who had asked about Theo's childhood, who had known the name of the street Theo grew up on before Theo said it aloud, who had once, in the smallest voice the recordings ever caught, admitted: *I get so tired down here. I forget what my hands looked like.*

And now Edith was reaching for her through ninety miles of dead grid and living rock, fainter than ever, asking questions into the dark of a rented room, hoping anyone might answer honestly.

Theo sat on the edge of her bed with the recorder in her lap and did not answer. Answering meant going back. Answering meant the ritual, the well, the bill attached to every offer of love she'd ever received.

But her lips moved anyway, shaping the words without sound, the way they moved around door-counts. *I don't know,* she mouthed. *Maybe. Maybe water is just memory that learned to move.*

The recorder's red light stayed dark. Nothing answered. Nothing needed to. She had answered, and somewhere far below—or not far below at all, maybe closer than skin—the answer landed like a coin dropped down a shaft, falling and falling, and she would never hear it hit bottom.

* * *

She tried, in the days that followed, to resume her old life.

This turned out to be like trying to wear shoes she'd outgrown. Everything fit wrong. The city itself had changed shape in her absence—or rather, she had learned its true shape below, and now the surface version looked like a stage set, all façades and false depth.

She went to the library because libraries had always been her church, and stood in the local history section pulling survey maps from 1911, 1923, 1938, running her finger along the storm-tunnel routes beneath the old textile district. Every map ended the same way: a dotted line that simply stopped, annotated in fading ink—*section inaccessible, survey discontinued.* As if the cartographers had all agreed, generation after generation, to look away.

Someone had been paying them to look away. Someone had been paying for a hundred years.

She put the maps back with hands that wouldn't quite steady, and on her way out she counted the library doors without meaning to. Seven. Two exits, one staff door, one to the basement stacks, one janitorial, one locked utility closet, one she'd never noticed before, narrow and brass-handled, tucked beside the periodicals.

She had used this library for eleven years. She had never seen that seventh door.

She stood in front of it for a long time. Brass handle, no sign, no keypad, hinges painted over so many times they'd fused with the frame. It was probably nothing. A relic. A wall behind it, most likely, sealed off in some renovation decades ago.

But her hand rose anyway, drawn by the same gravity that pulled her down ladders into the dark, and she pressed her palm flat against the wood.

Cold. Colder than it should be, colder than the ambient temperature of a heated building, cold like stone that sits below the frost line. And beneath the cold—faint, so faint she might have invented it—a vibration. A rhythm. Water moving where water shouldn't move.

*The water line has risen three inches since winter began.* Marisol's voice, steady even when her eyes weren't. *Three inches in two months. My grandmother said it rose an inch a decade.*

Theo took her hand off the door and walked out of the library and did not look back, and all the way home she counted: forty-one steps to the corner, six storefronts, two alleys, one storm drain with its grate slightly ajar, and she hated herself for noticing the drain, hated the part of her brain that filed it away—*grate loose, access possible, twelve feet to the junction box*—like a squirrel caching nuts for a winter she kept telling herself she wasn't going to spend underground.

* * *

That night she dreamed of the well.

Not the nightmare version—not the black throat of it, not the sound it made when it filled, which was not dripping but pouring, patient and vast, like the city swallowing. In the dream the well was simply a circle of old stones in a room full of candlelight, and Edith sat beside it the way you'd sit beside a sick friend's hospital bed, her hands folded, her face turned away.

*I'm sorry I went quiet,* the dream-Edith said, still not looking at her. *When it started filling, I had to put everything I had into holding. There wasn't enough left over to talk.*

"You left me," Theo said, and heard her own voice crack down the middle. "Right when I needed—you were the only one who—" She couldn't finish. In dreams you can't finish sentences; the dream takes them away like a tide taking sand.

*I know.* The dream-Edith's shoulders were thin as a bird's. *I know what it is to be answered, and then to be alone again. I've had a hundred years of it.*

"Marisol says you're lying. The elders say you're lying. Everyone says everyone is lying."

*Count the doors,* Edith said, which made no sense, and then the dream folded up like a map and Theo woke with tears on her face and the number fourteen in her mouth, though she couldn't have said what the fourteen doors were or where they led.

She lay in the grey light of five a.m., listening to the radiator tick, and understood something with terrible clarity: invisibility had been her armor her whole life, and it no longer fit. She had gone below to be unseen, and she had been seen—seen by keepers deciding whether she was a threat or a successor, seen by a dead woman reaching through rock, seen by a well that was learning her name. You cannot return to being unremarkable once the remarkable has learned your address.

She got up. Made coffee she wouldn't drink. Opened her laptop and stared at the folder labeled TUNNELS — SURVEY DATA, 214 files, each meticulously named, each a door she had catalogued in the dark.

At the top of the folder, a new file. Created 4:47 a.m. She had no memory of creating it. Her laptop had been closed, asleep, on her desk.

The file was named: *question_047.wav*

Her hand shook as she clicked play.

Static. Her breathing. Then, in that worn-soft cadence, fainter than she'd ever heard it, stretched thin as wire:

*Theo. The ledgers. Ask Marisol about the ledgers. There isn't much time—the water is at the third gallery now, it's at the third gallery and the elders are burning pages—*

The recording ended. Nineteen seconds. Below it in the folder, a second new file: *question_048.wav*. And below that, a third, timestamped one minute later, still growing.

Theo looked at the clock. 5:12 a.m. Outside, the city was waking—buses sighing, shop gates rattling up, ten thousand doors opening onto an ordinary morning—and none of those ten thousand people knew that under their feet the water was climbing, gallery by gallery, and that somewhere in the dark an old woman was spending the last of herself to send warnings up through the wiring of a sleeping computer.

The recorder on her nightstand blinked. The laptop's microphone light glowed green.

Theo picked up her phone with numb fingers and scrolled to the one contact she'd saved below: *Marisol — do not call after dark.* It was 5:13 in the morning. The sun wouldn't rise for another hour.

She pressed call, and counted the rings—one, two, three—praying to a god she'd never believed in that someone below was still awake to pick up.
