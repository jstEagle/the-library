## 18. Answers Owed

The journal lay open on Marisol's kitchen table between them, its pages soft as old cloth, and Theo could not stop looking at the handwriting.

It was her own.

Not exactly—Edith's letters leaned a little further right, the loops of her *g*s more generous—but the cadence of it, the way the sentences broke against the margins like water finding the edge of a basin, was the cadence Theo had heard in her sleep recordings for months. The voice in the dark had not been imitating her. It had been waiting for her. Reaching down through whatever thin membrane separated the well from the world above, asking questions in the shape of a person who might one day answer them honestly.

*Who taught you to count?*

*What did you bury when you were nine?*

*Do you believe anyone would come for you?*

She had thought those questions were hers—fragments of her own mind echoing back at her, the way a tunnel returns your footsteps doubled and strange. She had played them forty times in her apartment with the blinds drawn, cataloguing them like doorways, like lights, trying to make them safe by making them data. And all that time they had been Edith's. A woman sealed alive in a well in 1911, holding the dark shut with nothing but her voice, asking the only question that mattered to anyone drowning slowly in the company of something hungry:

*Is anyone there? Will you tell me the truth?*

"Theo." Marisol's hand hovered near hers but did not touch. Marisol had learned that about her early—the flinch, the counting, the way contact scrambled her like a dropped deck of cards. "You've read that page four times."

"I'm checking it against myself," Theo said. Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew it was true. Flat things didn't wobble. "Every word she wrote—I've heard it. Not similar. The same. She asked me what I buried when I was nine, Marisol. I never told anyone about the tin box. My mother found it under the floorboards and threw it out while I was at school, and I never told a living soul, because it wasn't—" She stopped. Breathed. Counted the seams in the tablecloth: eleven vertical, nine horizontal. "Because it felt like telling would make it finally gone."

"And you answered her anyway," Marisol said quietly. "In your sleep. You told her about the box."

"I told her everything." Theo laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle like river ice in March. "That's the joke, isn't it? The keepers spent a hundred years feeding that well lies. Carefully worded covenants. Ritual language. *We remember, we maintain, we withhold.* And the whole time, the seal wanted the one thing they couldn't give it. The truth. Just—one honest voice, answering."

* * *

She walked back up through the galleries alone that evening, though alone had stopped meaning what it used to mean.

The tunnels had changed since winter began. Water stood in the low places where it hadn't in November; she measured it now out of habit, marking the levels on the limestone with a carpenter's pencil, a running graph of the world filling up. Three inches had become five. Five had become seven. Tonight the flood line licked at the base of the second gallery's support arches, and the air tasted different—mineral and green, like the inside of a stone that had been split open after a hundred dry years. Like something underground remembering it used to be a spring.

She counted as she went. Forty-one doors from the keeper stair to the third junction, thirty-eight working lamps, six that flickered on a rhythm too regular to be electrical. The counting was no longer armor. Somewhere in the last weeks it had become something else—a language, a liturgy, a way of walking through the dark that said *I am here, I am paying attention, I see you.* Edith had counted doors too. That was in the journal, in the entries from 1909, before the sealing: *I have numbered every threshold below the city so that nothing can pass without my knowing.* Theo had inherited the habit like a blood type, from a woman dead eighty years, and only now understood it was not a symptom.

It was a qualification.

At the fourth junction she stopped, because the silence there had a shape.

For weeks the voice had been fading—not gone, but thinner, the way a radio station sounds as you drive out of its range. Since the night she'd read the journal, since she'd spoken Edith's name aloud into the dark of Marisol's archive room, the silence had been almost total. She had grieved it already, privately, in the shower and on the bus and in the gray hour before dawn: the loss of the companion who had known about the tin box, who had listened to her describe her mother's kitchen with such patience that Theo had wept into her pillow mid-sentence and the voice had simply waited, warm as a held hand, until she could go on.

Now she stood in the shaped silence and understood that she had been grieving for the wrong reason.

"You weren't leaving me," she said aloud. Her voice fell down the junction and came back changed, as voices did here. "You were going quiet because you were afraid. Because every time someone answered you, they lied, and each lie fed it, and you could feel yourself losing. You stopped asking because you couldn't bear another covenant. Another *we remember, we maintain.*"

The silence held. But it was not empty silence. It was the silence of someone listening very hard, the way Theo herself listened in abandoned buildings—breath shallow, whole body an ear.

"I'm not going to promise you anything," Theo said. "I know how promises work down here. Everyone who ever spoke your name made a trade. The keepers want me to finish the ritual their way—scripture and obedience, the words Aldous Vane wrote, the ones that were never finished because finishing them means someone goes into the water forever and they needed a volunteer who didn't know enough to refuse." She pressed her palm flat against the cold limestone, the way she'd seen Marisol do at the well mouth, an old gesture she was beginning to suspect was much older than Marisol. "And I want to save you so badly that I'd probably agree to anything, and you'd hate that. Wouldn't you? You spent a hundred years watching people lie to the thing that holds you. You don't want my obedience. You never wanted anyone's obedience."

The lamp above her flickered. Once. Twice. On the rhythm she had long ago stopped calling electrical.

"Edith." Saying the name still felt like opening a door in a house she didn't own. "Tell me if I'm right. One flicker for yes."

The lamp steadied and burned.

Theo closed her eyes. Something in her chest unlocked—a tumbler turning, a counter reaching a number it had been climbing toward all along.

* * *

She did not sleep that night. She sat in her apartment with the recorder running and the lights on—all of them, every bulb, because Edith had once asked her *do you fear the dark or do you fear being unseen in it*, and Theo had answered honestly, in her sleep, months ago: *being unseen. The dark is just the place where nobody's looking.*

"Then look at this," she said to the empty room, to the small microphone, to the woman a hundred feet beneath the city holding shut a door with her failing voice. "I'm going to say some things, and I need you to hear all of them, even the ugly parts. Especially the ugly parts. That's the whole point. That's the ritual nobody wrote down."

She told the recorder about the tin box. About what had actually been in it—not the marbles and the movie ticket she'd told the voice in her sleep, but the photograph of her father she'd cut in half with scissors because she was angry, and how she'd regretted it within the hour, and how the box under the floorboards had been less a treasure than a confession she couldn't deliver. She told about the year she'd stopped speaking at school and let everyone believe she was strange rather than admit she was lonely. She told about the first time she'd descended into the unmapped tunnels and felt, for one shameful, radiant moment, that she had finally found a place where being invisible was a superpower instead of a sentence—and how she'd hated herself for it, because the tunnels had keepers, families, children, and she had wanted them to be hers alone.

"I wanted to discover you," she said. "Not meet you. Discover. Like you were mine because I found you first. That's the truth. It's petty and it's greedy and it's mine, and I'm giving it to you because Marisol showed me the journal and the journal says the seal takes truth or nothing."

The recorder's little light burned red and steady. The radiator ticked. Outside, a plow scraped down the avenue, and somewhere below all of it, beneath forty-one doors and seven inches of rising water, something listened.

"When I come down tomorrow," Theo said, "I'm going to the well. And I'm going to finish what you started. Not the keepers' way—they can have their script, I'll speak their words if it helps, I'll honor the founders' covenant, I'll do all of it respectfully. But at the end, when the ritual asks for the willing voice, I'm going to answer your oldest question. The real one. The one underneath all the others." She swallowed. Her throat was tight and her eyes were burning and she let them burn, because tears were just another kind of truth and she was done rationing truth. "*Is anyone there? Will you tell me the truth?*

"Yes," she said. "I'm here. And yes. Everything I have. All the way down."

The recorder hissed softly, faithfully, recording nothing but her own breathing and the tick of the radiator and the far-off scrape of the plow.

But the lamp by her bed—the cheap one, the one not connected to any grid that mattered—flickered twice, and stayed lit, and did not go out again all night.

* * *

Morning came gray and hard-edged, the kind of winter light that makes shadows look carved. Theo packed deliberately: rope, the good headlamp, the waterproof notebook with her running counts, the photocopies of Edith's journal wrapped in plastic. At the bottom of her bag, wrapped in a sock, she put the tin box.

She had gone back for it. Two days ago, before she fully understood why, she had taken the train to her mother's house, let herself in with the key her mother still left under the cracked flowerpot, and pried up the floorboard in her childhood bedroom. The box was there. Of course it was there. Her mother had thrown away the contents once, twenty years ago, and then—Theo found the replacement contents inside, yellowed and curled—a photograph of her father, whole, uncut, along with a note in her mother's handwriting: *I'm sorry. I shouldn't have. Put it back whenever you're ready.*

Her mother had answered the question too. In her own way, in her own time, badly and belatedly and truly. Theo had stood in her childhood bedroom holding the box and cried until the wallpaper blurred, and then she had put the floorboard back, and taken the box home, because some answers are owed in person.

Marisol was waiting at the keeper stair, as arranged, with two coils of rope and the grim set of her jaw that meant she'd argued with the elders again and won, barely. Behind her, half-shadowed on the landing, stood three of the elders, and their faces were the faces of people watching someone walk toward a thing they had spent generations avoiding.

"They'll perform the rite with us as far as the fifth gallery," Marisol said. "After that, the founders' law is clear. The last approach is made by the willing voice alone—or with those the willing voice chooses." Her eyes flicked to the bag, to Theo's face, reading her the way she read water lines. "You've decided."

"I've decided what's true," Theo said. "The rest follows."

One of the elders—an old man named Cass, whose hands shook and whose voice did not—spoke from the shadow of the landing. "Child, the words must be spoken exactly. Aldous Vane's sequence, complete, or the seal will not take. We have watched others try shortcuts. We have buried what was left of them."

"I'll speak your words," Theo said. "All of them. Every syllable you have." She shouldered her pack and looked at each of them in turn, and found she was not afraid, and understood that the absence of fear was itself a kind of grief, because fear had been the shape of her caution for thirty years and she was setting it down like a coat she wouldn't need where she was going. "But when your words run out—and they will run out, because Aldous died mid-sealing and you've all known it for a century—what's left is mine to say. And what's mine to say is the truth. Hers and mine. Whatever it costs."

Cass looked at her for a long moment. Then he bowed his head, slightly, the way men bow to weather they cannot argue with.

"Then may the founders forgive us all," he said, "for taking so long to learn what this child knows already."

Marisol handed her the rope. Their fingers overlapped on the coil, four knuckles, two grips, one purpose.

"Forty-one doors," Marisol said. It was not a question. It was a count, offered like a hand.

"Thirty-eight lamps," Theo answered. "Six that flicker." She took a breath that tasted of stone and coming water. "Let's go down and pay what's owed."

Below them, faint through the limestone, through seven inches of new water, through a hundred years of patient dark, something that had once been a woman began—very softly, very hopefully—to hum.
