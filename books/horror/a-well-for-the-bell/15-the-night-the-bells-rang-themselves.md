## 15. The Night the Bells Rang Themselves

Ada had not slept in three days, and the sleeplessness had stopped feeling like a choice somewhere around the second dawn. It felt now like a vigil she'd been drafted into — the body refusing rest the way a sentry refuses it, some older part of her insisting that whatever was coming would come at night and that someone ought to be awake to meet it.

She was awake for it. That much she could say afterward, in the small hours when she still permitted herself the word *afterward*: she was awake.

It began with the dogs.

Harrow's Bend had perhaps forty dogs left in it, and by eleven o'clock they were all barking — not the ragged neighborhood chorus of a stray cat or a passing truck, but a single sustained note of alarm, every yard, every porch, every chained and unchained animal in town crying the same cry, as if a wire ran beneath the soil connecting them all to one terror. Then, at midnight, they stopped. All together. The silence that followed was worse than the barking; Ada stood on her back step in her coat and listened to a town holding its breath, and she thought: *they know something we don't. Or something we've forgotten.*

The frost had reached Main Street that afternoon. She had watched it come up through the asphalt seams like white veins surfacing under skin, spreading from crack to crack with a patience that made her stomach turn, because patience meant intention. By evening the traffic light at the corner of Delano and Main wore a rime of ice though the air stood at fifty degrees, and old Mr. Pruett's pharmacy window had fogged from the inside out, and when Ada pressed her palm to the glass the frost there arranged itself — briefly, before melting under her warmth — into a spiral. A bell curve. She had seen the shape enough times now to stop pretending she hadn't.

Inside the hospice, the counting had entered its final register.

*Five,* said Ruth Ellery, ninety-one years old and dying of everything at once, her voice thin as paper over the intercom speaker in the nurses' station. *Four. Four.* She kept snagging on four the way a record snags on a scratch, and each time she returned to it her voice dropped another degree, until the word came out of the speaker like breath on winter glass.

Down the hall, Hollis Brandt counted too, and in Room 6, and in Room 9, six voices total threading through the building's quiet ductwork, out of phase with one another, so that the numbers overlapped and braided — *five, four, five, four, three* — a chord made of arithmetic. Dev Osei sat across from Ada at the station, his big hands flat on the desk, and neither of them spoke about what they were hearing. They had passed beyond speaking about it sometime in the last week. Now they only worked inside it, the way miners work inside the mountain.

"They're slowing down," Dev said finally. "Not stopping. Slowing. Like they're rationing."

"Like they're saving them," Ada said.

"For what?"

Ada looked at the wall clock. 2:41 a.m. "For the last one," she said, and did not explain, because she couldn't have explained even to herself what she meant by that — only that she could feel it, the way you feel a storm front in an old tooth. Somewhere under the town, under twelve feet of clay and iron, something was waiting for the count to end, and the dying were being kept like matches, one strike apiece, held back for the moment they'd matter most.

At 2:52, the lights flickered. Not the hospice lights — all the lights. She knew because Dev stepped to the window and said, quietly, "Whole grid," and Ada came to stand beside him and saw Harrow's Bend laid out below the hill in its handful of streets, and saw the brownout pass through it like a wave through water, streetlight to streetlight, porch light to porch light, dimming and recovering, dimming and recovering, a pulse.

A pulse. The town had a pulse.

"Oh," Ada said, very softly. It was not a word she chose. It came up out of her like a burp comes up out of an infant, involuntary and pre-verbal, the body's commentary arriving ahead of the mind's.

"What?" Dev said.

"Nothing. I don't—" But she did know. She knew the way she'd known the frost spelled RETURN on her window, the way she'd known her grandfather's name before her mother said it aloud. The town wasn't near the well anymore. The town was *in* it. Had been in it, maybe, since 1974 — sitting on the mouth of it like a cork, like a lid, like a name held in a closed fist — and everything since then, the long slow prosperity of the six families, the decades without a flood or a fire or a bad harvest, the uncanny preservation of Marge Corliss and her five friends — all of it had been the well breathing gently, keeping its prize, waiting.

Tonight it had stopped breathing gently.

At 3:00 a.m., every bell in the county rang.

* * *

Later, people in Harrow's Bend would describe it in fragments, none of the fragments agreeing. Mrs. Terhune on Corliss Road swore it started with the Catholic church over in Marlow, nine miles east. The dispatcher at the sheriff's office insisted it was the Methodist bell first, right downtown, close enough to rattle her coffee cup. The truth — the truth Ada assembled later from a dozen shaking accounts — was that they had all begun at once. Every church bell in a fifteen-mile radius, every schoolhouse bell, the bell on the old fire truck that hadn't run since Carter was president, the little dinner bell on the porch of the Vane homestead where Ada's mother lived among forty years of hoarded newspapers — all of them, simultaneously, at 3:00 a.m., began to toll.

And there was no one in any tower. There was no rope pulled, no hammer swung, no hand anywhere within miles of any clapper. The bells rang themselves, and they rang *wrong* — that was the detail everyone agreed on, the detail that made grown men leave their houses and stand in their yards in their underwear looking east and west and north as if the sound might be visible. The bells rang underwater. Every one of them sounded as though it hung at the bottom of a deep cold lake, the tone thickened and slowed and bent, each stroke arriving already drowned, and between strokes there was a sound underneath the sound, a vast wet resonance, as if the ringing were happening somewhere else entirely and these hundred small bells were merely sympathetic — vibrating in agreement, in *answer*, to a single bell far below the ground that no map had ever marked.

Ada stood at the hospice window with her hands pressed flat against the frame and felt the sound enter her through her palms, through her sternum, through the soles of her feet, and somewhere beneath her fear there opened a door onto another room entirely, and in that room was grief — enormous, specific, ancient grief — and she understood with terrible clarity that the bells were not a warning.

They were a mourning. They had been silent for fifty years, and tonight they were ringing for Elias Vane, all of them at once, the whole county tolling for a man who had fallen into the dark ringing his own bell with both hands, and the sound was so full of sorrow that Ada's knees went and she caught herself on the windowsill and wept without deciding to, great helpless sobs that hurt her ribs, while behind her the patients' voices rose through the vents:

*Three. Three. Three.*

In the parking lot, the floodlights at the excavation site — half a mile down the hill, visible from the hospice's east windows — began to swing. She saw the hired men come out of the site trailer, six or seven of them, and she saw one of them fall to his knees in the mud, and then another, and then they were running. Not retreating in any orderly fashion. Running — dropping flashlights, vaulting the plastic mesh fencing, sprinting for their trucks with the pure animal urgency of men fleeing a burning building, and the trucks fishtailed out onto the county road with their lights off, two of them, three, gone north toward the interstate at speeds that would have gotten them arrested on any other night.

They left the site open. Floodlights swinging on their stands, throwing wheeling shadows across the plywood hoarding and the chain-link and the pit itself — and Ada could see the pit from here, could see the rectangle of the exposed iron door lying flat in its trench of frozen earth, and the door was standing open.

Open. Inward. Down.

Dev appeared beside her. He had his coat on. She didn't remember him putting it on.

"You're going down there," he said. It wasn't a question, and he didn't make it one, and she loved him a little for that — for the decency of not asking her to deny it.

"The guards are gone."

"I can see the guards are gone." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Ada. Listen to me. Whatever this is, it called those men out of a warm trailer at three in the morning and sent them running like children. You're a nurse. You weigh a hundred and thirty pounds. What exactly is the plan?"

She turned from the window. The bells were still tolling, that drowned slow tolling, and she realized she could feel each stroke now not in her ears but in her blood, and that the counting upstairs had changed — the patients weren't saying *three* anymore. She cocked her head, listening past the bells, and heard it: they had stalled. All six of them, all across the ward, hanging on the same number, saying it over and over like a held breath.

*Two. Two. Two. Two.*

"They're holding the door," Ada said. "Or holding it shut. Or — I don't know. Holding something. Dev, they've been counting down for months. Every one of them. Converging on zero on the equinox. And tonight they stopped at two and stayed there, and the bells rang, and the guards ran. That's not coincidence. That's cover. They're covering me." She picked up her keys from the desk, then set them back down — she wouldn't need keys; she'd walk; the site was twenty minutes downhill and she suspected the frost would hold the roads slick anyway. "I don't expect you to understand it. I barely understand it. But my grandfather is down there, and my name is down there, and I am done letting other people decide what gets paid and who pays it."

"And if it takes you?"

"Then it takes me," Ada said, "and at least it'll be honest about it. Nobody in this town has been honest in fifty years."

Dev looked at her for a long moment. The bells tolled through the silence between them, stroke after drowned stroke. Then he took the flashlight off the charging shelf and put it in her coat pocket himself, and closed her fingers around it.

"Twenty minutes," he said. "If you're not back at the fence line in twenty minutes, I'm calling everyone. Sheriff, state police, the news, my mother, everybody. I don't care what they think. I'll tell them all of it. The counting, the frost, the plaque, all of it."

"That might be the most useful thing anyone's done yet," Ada said. "Witnesses."

"Yeah, well." His voice cracked, just slightly, and he covered it by clearing his throat. "Go on, then. Before I talk myself into tackling you."

* * *

The walk down took eleven minutes. The frost held the road in a skin of white, and her boots crunched on it, and above her the sky was clear and starless — not clouded, but *starless*, which was wrong, which she noticed only peripherally, filing it away with all the other wrongs, because the stars had not gone out so much as been *occluded*, blotted, as if something between the ground and heaven had swollen to fill the view. The bells tolled overhead the whole way down, and with every stroke the frost on the roadside grass brightened, faintly, like a thing inhaling.

The site gate stood open. The plastic mesh had been torn loose along its whole eastern run, shredded outward, from the inside. The floodlights wheeled. And the cold —

The cold came up out of the pit like breath from a lung. She felt it before she reached the hoarding, a moving cold, a *living* cold, rolling up the slope in slow pulses timed to the bell strokes, and where it touched her face it did not bite but *brushed*, the way a hand brushes a cheek, and she thought: *it knows me. It has always known me. I was conceived in its shadow and it has been waiting for me to grow tall enough to walk here on my own legs.*

She climbed the scaffold steps they'd built for the surveyors and stood at the lip of the trench.

Twelve feet down, ringed by earth that glittered black with frost, the iron door lay open against the shaft's throat. Beyond it, darkness went down. Not the ordinary darkness of an unlit space — a darkness with texture, with depth, a darkness that breathed. The pulses of cold rose out of it in slow rhythm, and each pulse carried a scent she had no name for: stone and pond water and candle smoke and, underneath, faintly, impossibly, lilies. Funeral flowers. Someone had been mourned down there, long ago, and the mourning had never finished, and the smell of it had soaked into the rock like dye into cloth.

"Hello," Ada said. Her voice fell into the shaft and did not echo. It was taken. Swallowed whole, politely, the way a host takes a guest's coat.

The next bell stroke rolled over her, and in the instant of its decay she heard — beneath the county's hundred sympathetic bells, beneath the waterlogged tolling — a single note rise from directly below her feet. One note. Clearer than the others. Un-drowned. A bell rung clean, by a hand that remembered how.

Her eyes filled again, and this time she let them.

"I know," she whispered. "I know. I'm coming."

And then the shaft exhaled, and the breath of it moved up over her, parting her hair, and in her ear — in her *ear*, close as a mouth, closer than any living thing had stood to her in years — a voice said her name.

Not *Ada*. Not *Lockridge*. Not the name on her badge or her lease or her nursing license, the name she had answered to for forty-one years without ever once wondering where it came from or what it cost.

It said: ***Vane.***

The word went through her like a hook through cloth. She gripped the scaffold rail. Somewhere above, distantly, the bells were still tolling, and somewhere below, the patients were still holding their *two, two, two* like a dam holding a river, and Ada Lockridge stood at the mouth of the well that had eaten her grandfather and heard herself named by it — named truly, named *first*, the way you call a child home at dusk — and understood that the point of no return was not a line you crossed going down.

It was a line that had been crossed for you, fifty years before you were born, by people who wanted more time.

She put her foot on the first rung of the ladder bolted inside the door, and the cold rose to meet her like arms opening.
