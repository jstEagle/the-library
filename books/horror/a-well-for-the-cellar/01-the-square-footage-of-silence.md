## 1. The Square Footage of Silence

The gate to Hollowmere House had not been oiled in living memory, and it announced Emrys Crane's arrival with a sound like a long, arthritic complaint — the sort of noise he had spent ten years learning to file under *settling*, under *weather*, under *nothing worth noting*. He stood a moment on the lane with his clipboard against his chest and his car ticking behind him, and he let the sound finish, the way you let a clock finish striking, before he let himself through.

The house sat at the end of a half-mile of gravel that had gone soft and green at its edges, the way paths do when nobody walks them. Two storeys, flint and brick, a slate roof furred with lichen the colour of old copper. Chimneys — three of them, one leaning with the patient confidence of a thing that had been leaning for a century and intended to go on leaning long after everyone who might have corrected it was gone. The windows were dark even though it was barely half past nine in the morning, and the light was the thin, washed-out light of late September, the light that came before the harvest moon and made everything look like a photograph of itself.

Emrys Crane was forty-one years old and had been a property surveyor for fifteen of those years, and in all that time he had developed exactly one professional habit that his colleagues had noticed and teased him for, gently, at Christmas parties: he talked to houses. Not conversationally. He called out his measurements as he took them — *four metres twenty*, he would say, to an empty drawing room, *ceiling height, two forty* — because his father had done it, because his father's father had done it, because a voice in a room confirmed the room was empty before you put your hand on the wall. It was a safety ritual dressed as a surveying technique, and Emrys had never once examined it too closely, in the same way he had never once examined too closely the noises in the cellar of his own rented cottage in Marleigh Drove, which had been making a soft, rhythmic sound at night for the better part of a decade — a sound he had classified, in the private ledger of his mind, as *pipes*, then as *the boiler*, then as *the house settling*, and which he had finally, in an act of professional cowardice he was honest enough to recognise as such, stopped classifying at all.

Some things you measured. Other things you let be.

He let the gate be, and walked up the path.

The probate file was thin. Edith Verrow — no relation, he'd checked twice, though the name had snagged something in him the way a nail snags a sleeve — had died in June at ninety-one, the last of her line, and the solicitors in Croydon wanted the house valued for sale before the autumn. Standard instruction. Residential, detached, approximately four bedrooms, cellar, outbuildings, land to the rear of approximately one acre. Emrys had done four hundred of these. He knew the choreography: walk the perimeter first, note the boundaries and the damp course, then inside, room by room, tape and laser and pencil, calling out his figures to the empty air like a man taking attendance for a class that had all died.

He pressed the doorbell and heard, distantly, a bell that had gone toneless with rust, a felt thud rather than a ring. No answer, and he hadn't expected one. The key was in his breast pocket, in an envelope with the solicitor's stamp on it, and he put it in the lock and turned it, and the door opened onto darkness and the smell.

Every old house has a smell, and Emrys had a taxonomy for them the way other men have a taxonomy for beer or football teams. There was dry rot, sweetish, like pears going over. There was mouse, ammoniac and small. There was the coal-smoke ghost of a hundred winters, and the lavender ghost of a hundred wardrobes, and the particular cold-stone smell of cellars that never fully dried. Hollowmere House smelled of all of these, faintly, the way a chord contains its notes. But underneath them was something else, something he did not have a drawer for: a wet, green, mineral smell, like the air over a well, like the breath of a place that had been holding it a very long time.

*Ventilation,* he wrote on his clipboard. *Inadequate. Recommend assessment of sub-floor and cellar airflow.*

The pencil moved. The pencil always moved. That was the whole architecture of his life, really — that whatever happened, however strange, the pencil moved, and a thing written down in the correct form in the correct column stopped being a thing that had happened to you and became a thing that had happened to a property.

He started, as he always started, with the perimeter.

* * *

The back of the house was where the land went wrong. Not dramatically — there was no subsidence, no crack in the flint, no leaning of walls that a mortgage lender's valuer would flag. It went wrong the way a sentence goes wrong, by one small word too many. The lawn ran back perhaps forty metres to a line of elms gone grey with Dutch disease, and between the house and the trees the ground rose in a gentle, almost imperceptible swell, and Emrys stood on the terrace step with his laser rangefinder and got a reading to the tree line of 41.7 metres, and then, without moving the device, without breathing, got a second reading of 41.7 metres, and a third, and understood that the instrument was fine and that the wrongness was in him, in the feeling — unprofessional, unsurveyable — that the swell of the lawn was not a swell at all but a covering-over. That something had been filled in. That the ground remembered being a hole.

He wrote: *Rear garden, approx. 0.4 ha, gently undulating, mature boundary planting.* And then, because the pencil was honest even when he was not, he added in the margin, in the small tight script he used for things that would never go in a report: *filled?*

The wind moved through the elms and the house behind him creaked — a long, structural groan, timber taking up a load, the sound every surveyor knows and most surveyors love, the sound of a building being a building. Emrys catalogued it without turning around. *Main roof structure, movement under wind load, consistent with age. No cause for concern.*

He was very good at that sentence. He had been writing versions of it for ten years, about Hollowmere's roof and about a great many other things.

* * *

Inside, the house was cold in the specific way of houses that have been shut with their windows painted over or curtained, cold the way a held breath is cold. The hallway ran from front to back, flagstoned, wide enough for a coffin to be carried down with room to spare, and Emrys found himself thinking that thought and then finding it already there, waiting for him, as though the hallway had suggested it.

He began in the morning room, because you always began in the smallest room and worked up to the difficult ones, and he took out his tape measure, the good steel one with the brass case his father had left him, and hooked it on the skirting and walked it along the wall.

"Four metres sixty," he said, to the empty room, in the flat professional voice he used for empty rooms. He wrote it down. He walked the tape back along the other wall. "Four metres fifty-eight."

The tape went slack in his hand.

Then it did not go slack. It drew taut — a smooth, deliberate tension, the way a line goes taut when a fish takes the bait, or when a person at the other end of a rope leans back — and the steel ribbon, which he had been reeling in with his thumb, pulled *out*, another thirty centimetres of it, whispering against the case, and stopped.

Emrys Crane stood in the morning room of Hollowmere House with a tape measure that was measuring something, or being measured, and he did the thing he had trained himself to do for fifteen years, which was to breathe once, slowly, and reach for the drawer.

*Instrument fault,* he wrote. *Tape case spring tension inconsistent. Replace before next survey.* He reeled the tape in. It came willingly now, all the way, coiling home with a click, and he held it up to the light from the window and examined it, and the tape was fine, the tape was perfect, the tape was a tape.

But the room had heard him say *four metres fifty-eight*, and something in the room — in the plaster, in the joists, in the cold held breath of it — had seemed to Emrys, in the half-second before he filed the sensation away, to be considering the figure. Weighing it. Finding it a little generous.

He worked the rest of the morning room quickly and did not speak again in it.

* * *

By one o'clock he had done the ground floor and half of the first, and his clipboard was filling with the ordinary poetry of an ordinary house: *sash windows, secondary glazing, north-facing; plasterwork, original cornice, repair to ceiling rose; bedroom two, 3.9 by 3.4, fireplace blocked, hearth stone cracked.* He had catalogued forty-one separate noises. He was good at noises. It was, if he was honest — and he permitted himself honesty only in the margins, never in the body of the report — the actual profession he had practised all these years, beneath the profession he was paid for. Damp was easy. Damp was a stain and a smell and a reading off a moisture meter, and any fool with a £60 instrument could find it. But noises — a house's noises were its confession, and most surveyors walked straight through them. Emrys had spent a decade learning to hear a house the way a doctor hears a chest: this creak is a joist shrinking, that groan is a rafter bearing, this tick is copper cooling, that long exhalation under the floor is a draught finding the gap where a pipe once ran, and none of it, *none of it*, is anything else.

The catalogue for Hollowmere ran thus, in his margin script:

*Creak, stair, third tread — loose tread nail, trivial.*
*Creak, landing, intermittent — floorboard rub, seasonal.*
*Groan, roof, wind — structural, benign.*
*Tick, first-floor front — pipework, cooling.*
*Exhalation, under hallway floor — draught, sub-floor void.*
*Hum, cellar —*

He had stopped there. He had stood at the cellar door with his pencil poised for a full minute, and the hum had gone on underneath the door, low and even, a note rather than a noise, and he had written *— pending* and walked away from it, and his heart had been going like a man's who has looked over a edge and stepped back, and he had told himself, in the flat professional voice, that cellars were not on today's instruction, that the probate schedule said *cellar* and the cellar would be itemised in due course, tomorrow, or the day after, when he had his torch, his better torch, the one with the wide beam.

The cellar door was at the end of the kitchen corridor, under the stairs, painted the same eggshell as everything else, and it was closed, and he had not opened it, and he was aware — with the particular shame of a man who is good at his job — that he had arranged his day around not opening it, and that the arrangement had felt less like cowardice than like courtesy.

* * *

He ate his lunch on the front step, in the thin sun, because the kitchen had a table and chairs and he could not have sat at that table. The sandwich was cheese and pickle and it tasted of nothing at all, and while he ate he read the probate papers again, because reading them was easier than thinking.

Edith Verrow. Ninety-one. Sole occupant since 1961 — sixty-two years alone in a four-bedroom house; the solicitors had noted it as *unusual tenure pattern*. No next of kin. Effects to be valued and cleared; the clearance firm had already been in, they said, and found the house "largely as expected," which Emrys had underlined, because clearance firms did not say *largely as expected* unless something had been otherwise. He turned to their inventory. Furniture, ordinary. Linens, ordinary. Kitchen effects, ordinary. And then, at the bottom, in the clerk's careful hand:

*Cellar contents: not itemised. Client instructed that everything below stairs conveys with the property.*

*Below stairs.* Not *the cellar's contents*. Not *items in storage*. *Everything below stairs*, as though the cellar were not a part of the house but a country it contained, with everything in it — everything, whatever everything was — passing to the buyer like a deed, like an inheritance, like an obligation.

Emrys folded the paper along its existing creases and did not eat the second half of his sandwich.

He thought, briefly and without permission, of the noise in his own cellar in Marleigh Drove. The soft, rhythmic one. The one that had been *pipes* for three years and *the boiler* for four and was now, officially, in the ledger of his mind, nothing at all — unclassified, unclassifiable, a line item with no column. He had never gone down while it was happening. He had gone down in daylight, many times, with a torch, and found brick walls and a flagged floor and a boiler that was newer than the noise, and he had stood there in the honest light and felt the silence of the cellar like a held note, like something pausing, and he had gone back up and closed the door and not classified it.

Ten years of that. Ten years of the pencil moving, of the correct form in the correct column, of *no cause for concern* written in a hand so steady that even he believed it, mostly, in daylight.

He was still sitting on the step when the wind changed, and the smell came again — wet, green, mineral, the breath over a well — and it came, he noticed, from behind the house, from the gentle swell in the lawn, and it came, he also noticed, faintly but unmistakably, from the cellar door, which stood at the end of the corridor thirty feet away and through two closed doors, and which was, he realised as he looked at it down the length of the hallway, slightly ajar.

He had left it closed. He was certain he had left it closed. He was a man whose entire life was the certainty of small things — doors locked, tapes zeroed, figures carried forward — and he was certain.

The door stood open perhaps two inches, and the hallway light fell into the gap and did not come back out, and from the darkness below, low and even, patient as water finding its level, came the hum.

Emrys Crane stood up, brushed the crumbs from his trousers with two precise passes of his hand, and wrote in the margin of his clipboard, in the small tight script, the only sentence he had written all day that was entirely true:

*It knows I'm here.*

Then he crossed it out, because tomorrow was another surveying day, and there were figures to finish, and the house was four metres fifty-eight, and no cause for concern, and he had a report to complete by Friday — and as he walked back down the hallway, tape measure in hand, the hum beneath the cellar door changed. Softened. Settled into a rhythm.

Almost, he thought, like breathing.

Almost, he thought, like a song someone was saving to finish later.
