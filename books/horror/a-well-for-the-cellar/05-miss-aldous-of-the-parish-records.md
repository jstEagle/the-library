## 5. Miss Aldous of the Parish Records

The village was called Lower Sallow, and it had the particular quality of places that have decided to stop growing without admitting it. Emrys parked the car beside a war memorial whose bronze names had gone green with patience, and sat for a moment with his hands on the wheel, listening to the engine tick itself cool.

He had not slept well. That was becoming a pattern he did not care to examine too closely — the recorder on the bedside table at Hollowmere, its small red eye watching him in the dark, and his own voice rising out of the blackness in fragments he could not remember making. He had not yet played back last night's file. He told himself this was because he was busy. He told himself a great many things before breakfast these days.

What he needed, he reasoned as he climbed out into the grey morning air, was context. A surveyor's first instinct when a building misbehaved was always documentation: deeds, plans, previous surveys, anything that would let him fold the strange back into the known. Hollowmere House had come to him through a probate solicitor in Ashbourne with a file thin enough to slide under a door. No prior surveys. No floor plans older than 1974. The previous owner's name given only as *Callow, R.* and an address that turned out to be a caravan park two villages over. The house itself, in the eyes of the law, had simply appeared, like weather.

But villages kept their own records. Villages remembered.

* * *

The parish records office occupied what had once been the vestry of St. Brannoc's, a low stone building attached to the church by a covered passage, so that entering it felt like being swallowed politely. Inside smelled of cold paper and beeswax and something older underneath — the smell Emrys had begun to think of as the smell of things people had agreed not to discuss. He knocked on the inner door, which stood ajar, and a voice said, "It's open, I'm not hiding," in a tone suggesting she might have preferred to be.

Edith Aldous was perhaps seventy, small and upright as a folded letter, with white hair pinned in a coil at her nape and reading glasses pushed up into it like a second crown. She sat behind a desk built for someone twice her size, cataloguing index cards with a fountain pen, and she looked up at him with the frank appraisal of a woman who had spent fifty years deciding what strangers were allowed to know.

"Emrys Crane," he said, offering the word like a calling card. "Chartered surveyor. I'm appraising a property in the parish — probate valuation. I hoped you might have some historical material. Deeds, tithe maps, anything."

"Which property?"

"Hollowmere House."

The pen stopped.

It was a small thing, really. A nib lifting from paper, a hand going still above an index card. But Emrys had spent ten years training himself to notice the small things in buildings — the hairline crack that meant subsidence, the door that no longer met its frame — and he noticed this the way he'd learned to notice everything: completely, and against his will.

Miss Aldous did not look up immediately. When she did, her face had changed in a way he found difficult to catalogue. Not fear exactly. Fear was loud. This was quieter and worse — the look of a woman hearing a name she had spent a lifetime arranging not to hear, spoken now in a mild professional voice over her own desk.

"Hollowmere," she said. Her voice was level. Her hands, folding the index card closed, were not entirely. "You're valuing Hollowmere House."

"For probate, yes. The estate of—"

"I know whose estate." She set down the pen with great precision, aligning it parallel to the desk's edge, and Emrys understood that the gesture was not tidiness but ballast. "Sit down, Mr. Crane."

There was only one other chair, wooden, positioned rather far from the desk, as though visitors were expected to keep a respectful distance from the knowledge stored there. He sat.

"You'll want the Verrow papers," Miss Aldous said, mostly to herself. She rose, and he saw that one of her legs was stiff at the knee, and that she moved through the narrow aisles between the archive shelves with the unerring economy of someone who had walked them ten thousand times. "Though I don't know why I'm surprised. It always comes round again. Every thirty years or so, like a comet. Someone buys the house, or inherits it, or is fool enough to value it." She pulled a box from a shelf — a real archive box, acid-free card, labelled in faded iron-gall ink: *VERROW / HOLLOWMERE / 1887–1901* — and carried it back with both hands, as if it were heavier than its contents could account for.

"The Verrow family," Emrys said carefully. "I don't know the name."

"No," said Miss Aldous. "Nobody does. That was rather the point."

She opened the box on the desk between them. Inside lay folders, a ledger, a bundle of letters tied with ribbon gone the colour of weak tea, and a child's hymnal with a broken spine. Emrys reached toward the ledger and Miss Aldous's hand came down flat on top of it — not sharply, but with finality, the way one stops a child from touching a stove.

"In order," she said. "If you're going to know, you're going to know properly. Sloppy history is how these things get worse."

* * *

She told it standing up, one hand resting on the open box, in the flat competent voice of a woman who had taught village history to generations of bored schoolchildren and had long ago burned the drama out of her delivery. But the drama kept leaking through anyway, like damp through plaster.

"The Verrows came to Hollowmere in 1887. Josiah Verrow, his wife Constance, three children. He'd made money in Manchester — cotton, or something adjacent to cotton; the records are vague because the family wanted them vague — and he wanted land, and quiet, and a house that looked older than it was. Hollowmere suited. It had been a farmstead before that, and before that—" she waved a hand, dismissing several centuries, "—sheep, and before that nobody wrote anything down, which in my experience means somebody very much wanted it that way.

"They were respectable. Church every Sunday, subscriptions to everything, the girls taught piano by the vicar's wife. Two boys and a daughter. The daughter was the youngest. Margaret — called Meggie in the parish register margins, because whoever filled in the burial record knew her well enough to be fond of her, and couldn't help writing it down."

Emrys found that his hands had gone still in his lap. He was aware of the room around him — the dust turning slowly in the light from the vestry window, the tick of a clock somewhere among the shelves — and aware also that some part of him had already left it, had already gone ahead down the road of what she was going to say, the way water finds the low ground.

"She died in June of 1893," Miss Aldous said. "Drowned."

"Drowned," Emrys repeated. His voice sounded odd to him. Distant. "In the mere? The house is named for—"

"Not the mere. The mere is half a mile off and full of eels and picnickers. No." Miss Aldous looked at him directly then, and her pale eyes held something he recognised with a lurch: the expression of a person about to hand over a weight and watching to see if the recipient's knees would hold. "She drowned in the well behind the kitchen. The old one. There's a newer well nearer the dairy — that one's in the sale particulars, I expect, all picturesque and listed. The old one they capped. Stone slab over it, sometime after. You'd hardly know it was there unless you knew."

Emrys thought of the kitchen garden at Hollowmere, the tangle of bramble and elder behind the south wall where the ground dipped in a way he had noted, professionally, as *possible former structure, recommend investigation*. He thought of how the grass grew differently there. Greener. Thirstier.

"The account in the coroner's file," Miss Aldous continued, "is that the girl went out at dusk to fetch water — though why she'd use the old well when the new one was closer to the kitchen, the coroner never thought to ask — and fell in. Accidental death. The family left within the year. Sold the house to a cousin of the mother's, and the cousin sold it on inside twelve months, and after that the ownership reads like a game of hot potato conducted across four decades. Nobody stayed. Nobody said why. The village said nothing at all, which you'll understand was the village's principal accomplishment."

"And the well," Emrys said. His mouth was dry. "The cap. Was there any question about—"

"About whether she fell?" Miss Aldous regarded him for a long moment. Outside, a rook called from the churchyard yew, once, twice, like a knock at a door. "Mr. Crane, I am the parish archivist. I deal in what can be evidenced. And what can be evidenced is this: a girl of nine years old went out to a well she had no reason to use, at dusk, alone, and drowned in six feet of water that she had been taught to draw from since she was five. The capstone was laid by the mason Tredgett, who charged the estate for two days' work and took four, and who would not afterwards go near the house even to collect payment. His widow collected it. Those are the facts. What they add up to is not my department."

"But you have a view."

"I have been alive in this parish for seventy-one years," said Miss Aldous, "and archivist for forty-three, and I will tell you what I tell the schoolchildren: houses are just buildings until something happens in them, and then they are never just buildings again. Hollowmere had something happen in it. Whatever else you put in your report, Mr. Crane — and I can see you're a man who likes reports — you may put that."

* * *

He should have thanked her and left. He understood that later, looking back: there had been a door standing open and he had walked through it, because that was what he did, because a decade of rationalising cellar-noises had made him a connoisseur of thresholds. Instead he heard himself ask:

"Why does everyone leave?"

Miss Aldous had begun retying the ribbon around the letters. Her fingers paused.

"Because it doesn't let you finish," she said.

"Finish?"

"Whatever you're doing there. Valuing it, renovating it, grieving in it, hiding in it. The house has opinions about completion." She said it lightly, almost wryly, but her hands had resumed the tying and the bow came out crooked, which seemed to trouble her more than anything else she'd said. "The Callow woman — Ruth, your probate vendor — she lasted eleven months. Before her, a couple from Birmingham, eight months. There was a family in the seventies who lasted three years and produced a son who won't drive through this parish to this day. And before all of them, back and back, the same story in different clothes. People arrive with their plans and their measurements and their sensible modern certainties, and the house..." She stopped. Started again, more quietly. "The house listens to them. And then it answers. And most people find they cannot bear being answered."

Emrys's skin prickled from the nape downward, vertebra by vertebra, like fingers walking down a ladder.

"Answers it how?" he asked, and hated how much he wanted to know, and hated more that his voice, when it came out, was steady — the professional voice, the surveyor's voice, the voice he used to nail things down.

Miss Aldous looked at him for a long time. Something in his face must have told her something, because whatever she had been preparing to say — some deflection, some archivist's shrug — she abandoned it.

"How do the walls answer *you*, Mr. Crane?"

The clock ticked. Dust turned in the light. Somewhere out in the churchyard the rook knocked again at its invisible door.

"I document acoustic anomalies," he said. "Echoes. Old buildings produce—"

"Yes," said Miss Aldous. "I'm sure you do." She closed the box lid, gently, the way one closes an eye. "Come back when you've played your recordings. You will play them; men like you always do, you can't bear an unexamined file. Come back when you've heard them, and we'll continue the lesson. I'll teach you what I can. It isn't much. It was never meant to be much — that was the whole design of it." She pressed her lips together, and for a moment she looked suddenly, terribly tired, an old woman at the end of a long shift guarding something. "But I'll tell you one thing now, free of charge, because you've got an honest face and it'll save us both time.

"The Verrow girl was nine years old, and she has been in that well for a hundred and thirty years, and the house keeps her the way a throat keeps a swallowed stone. Everything else — the noises, the voices, the figures that come back wrong — everything is downstream of that. If you want to value Hollowmere House, Mr. Crane, you will first have to decide what you believe about a drowned child. There is no figure you can write that doesn't rest on it."

* * *

He walked back through the covered passage into the churchyard without remembering deciding to. The morning had thickened while he was inside; cloud had come down off the hills and sat on the village like a lid. His car waited by the memorial, patient and ordinary, and he stood beside it for a long moment with the keys in his hand, not unlocking it.

*A daughter lost to the well behind the kitchen.*

He thought about the phrase *lost*. How the parish register had probably used it, or words like it — how the whole apparatus of Victorian grief had reached for euphemism because the truth was unspeakable. Lost, like a key. Lost, like a ledger page. As though she might turn up.

Behind the kitchen. Where the ground dipped. Where the grass grew greener and thirstier, and where he had written, in his neat professional hand, *possible former structure, recommend investigation.*

He got in the car. He did not start the engine. On the passenger seat lay his clipboard, and on the clipboard lay yesterday's site notes, and his eye went to them without permission, down the columns of measurements he had called aloud to the empty rooms of Hollowmere House — *hall, fourteen foot two; stair, three foot nine; cellar door, two foot six* — and stopped at the last line, the one he had written in the margin late at night without quite recalling writing it, in handwriting that was his and somehow not quite his, smaller than his usual script, neater, younger:

*cellar, seven foot deep. she can nearly stand up.*

Emrys Crane sat in his car outside St. Brannoc's, in the rain that had finally arrived, and read that line four times. Then he started the engine, and drove back toward the house, and did not let himself think — not once, not all the way — about the recordings waiting on the bedside table, and what he would hear when he finally pressed play.
