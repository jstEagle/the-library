## 6. Four Verses, Three Surviving

The parish archive sat behind the church of St. Aldhelm's, a low stone building that had once been the tithe barn and now smelled, permanently, of cold paper and lamp oil and the particular dustiness of things nobody had wanted for a very long time. Emrys stood outside it for four minutes by his watch — he counted, because counting was what he did instead of breathing when his chest went tight — before he made himself push open the door.

Inside, Edith Aldous was already looking at him.

She sat at a long oak table beneath a green-shaded lamp, a pair of white cotton gloves folded beside her like sleeping birds, and she had the expression of a woman who had been expecting him since Tuesday. Perhaps she had. He hadn't telephoned ahead. He had told himself he would simply appear, ask his questions in the flat professional register he used with solicitors and estate agents, gather his facts, and leave. Facts were safe. Facts could be filed.

"Mr. Crane," she said. Not a question.

"You know my name."

"You signed the visitors' book yesterday." She nodded toward a ledger at the end of the table, its pages soft as cloth. "You wrote *property surveyor* in the purpose column, and then you stood there for rather a long time holding the pen." She turned back to her work — some kind of index, columns of dates in faded brown ink — but she didn't resume it. Her hands stayed flat on the table, as if pinning something down. "I wondered how long you'd take to come back. People who stand too long at the sign-in book always come back."

"I have questions about a house," Emrys said. "Hollowmere."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the building's bones, a pipe ticked as it cooled, and Emrys catalogued the sound automatically — *expansion joint, copper, cooling* — and hated himself a little for doing it, for reaching for the catalogue the way other men reached for drink.

Edith removed her spectacles. Without them her face looked younger and more frightened, which he had not expected; he had expected the opposite.

"Sit down, Mr. Crane."

He sat. The chair was hard and high-backed, and from this angle he could see down the length of the room: shelves to the ceiling, boxes upon boxes, the accumulated paperwork of two centuries of births and deaths and land transactions and tithes and quarrels about fences. A whole parish's worth of lives reduced to entries. He felt an odd kinship with the shelves.

"Hollowmere House is under probate," he said. "I've been engaged to produce a valuation and condition survey. Standard practice. There are certain — irregularities in the structure's acoustic behavior that I'd like to understand before I file. Old houses settle. But this one seems to—" He stopped, hearing himself, hearing how insane the sentence was about to become. "*Respond.* To measurement."

Edith was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Do you hear it answering you?"

"Yes."

"And does it answer with your own figures, or different ones?"

"Different. Lower. It corrects me." His mouth had gone dry. "It's never wrong."

She put her spectacles back on, and something in her posture changed — the archivist returning, the woman who dealt in verifiable things retreating behind the lenses like a clerk stepping behind a counter. "Then I'll show you something," she said, "and after I show it to you, you may wish you'd filed your report on acoustics alone and gone home. I want you to understand that going home is still available to you. It won't be, later."

"That's not a normal thing for an archivist to say."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She rose, smoothed her cardigan, and led him between the shelves to a case against the far wall — a glass-fronted cabinet, locked, containing the archive's small treasury: a medieval charter, a Victorian scrapbook, a regimental photograph of young men all dead now for a century. From a drawer beneath it she took a bundle wrapped in acid-free tissue, and her hands, usually so steady over documents, shook slightly as she untied the string.

"This came to us in 1974," she said, "with a bequest from the Verrow estate. Most of it was deeds and letters. This was at the bottom of the box, wrapped separately, as though whoever packed it hoped it would be mistaken for packing material." She laid it on the table under the lamp. "It's a hymnal. Printed 1881. Cheap edition, the kind given to children for Sunday school."

The book was small enough to fit in one hand, its boards covered in black cloth worn grey at the corners. Edith opened it with gloved fingers, past printed hymns in dense twin columns, past a pressed flower that had stained the page brown, to the endpapers — and there, filling the inside back cover and spilling onto a tipped-in sheet, was writing.

Not adult writing. The letters leaned drunkenly, the ascenders looped too large, words crowded into margins where the writer had run out of room. A child's hand. And among the childish lines, set out with the careful spacing of something copied many times, were verses.

Emrys read them and the temperature of the room seemed to drop, or perhaps only his blood did.

They were a lullaby. Four-line stanzas, simple rhymes, the kind of thing a mother might sing over a cradle — except the imagery was wrong for a cradle. Water ran through every verse. A well. A rope. A bucket descending. *Sleep below, sleep below, the water is slow.* The third verse ended with a line that made the hairs on his forearms lift: *and none shall call you up again.*

"There are three," Edith said quietly. "Count them."

He counted. Three stanzas, transcribed in wobbly pencil, each one numbered in the corner by the child's hand: 1, 2, 3. Where a fourth should be, the paper had been torn away — not cut, torn, raggedly, taking a strip of the endpaper with it, so that the numbering stopped mid-gesture. Someone had ripped out the fourth verse with such force they'd damaged the binding.

"The rest of the tipped-in sheet is missing entirely," Edith said. "We searched the bequest box twice. Whatever was written there is gone."

"Who wrote this?"

"A child. We believe — the handwriting matches letters in the Verrow family papers — that it was Alice Verrow herself. Aged nine or ten." Edith's voice had gone very level, the levelness of someone walking on ice and listening for cracks. "Alice Verrow drowned in the well behind the Hollowmere kitchen in October of 1893. Harvest moon, that year, fell on the eleventh. She was found on the twelfth."

Emrys stared at the pencil lines. A child copying out a lullaby, numbering her verses, pressing so hard in places the pencil had torn the paper's tooth. And then he thought: *why does a child copy out her own lullaby? Why does anyone write down a song they already know?*

Because they're afraid of forgetting it. Because the song matters more than anything else in the world, and paper is a way of holding it still.

"Why would a drowned girl write down the song?" he asked. "If the song was sung *to* her—"

"We don't know that it was." Edith turned a page with excruciating care. "Look at the first line again."

He looked. *Down, down, the water is kind.*

"It's not a lullaby sung over a cradle," Edith said. "Read it properly. It's instructions. It's a song sung *by* the sleeper, or *for* the sleeper — a song that keeps the sleeper sleeping. Whoever sings it, sings it *to the well*. Or to whatever sleeps in the well." She closed the hymnal gently, as if it might wake. "The Verrows had a nursemaid, a local girl, who kept the children. After Alice died, the family left within the year. The house passed through five owners in sixty years, Mr. Crane. Five. No one held it. Do you know what that pattern means in probate records?"

"Distress sales."

"Rapid distress sales, each cheaper than the last, each buyer gone within a decade." She met his eyes above her spectacles. "And in the margin of the 1911 sale particulars, in the auctioneer's own hand, there's a note. One line. I'll read it to you exactly." She recited it without looking at anything, the way one recites something learned by heart against one's will: "*Vendor instructs that the cellar be excluded from all measurements.*"

The pipe ticked somewhere in the walls. Emrys counted the ticks. Eleven.

"The cellar door in that house," he said slowly, "is warm."

Something flickered across Edith's face — not surprise. Recognition, and grief, and underneath both, guilt, worn smooth as a pebble from years of handling. He would come to understand that guilt later. That morning he only registered it as a shadow behind her eyes and moved on, because he was a man trained to move on.

"Tell me about the song," he said. "All of it."

Edith rewrapped nothing; instead she opened the hymnal again to the torn place and laid her gloved finger just above the ragged edge, not touching it. "There were four verses. Everyone old in this parish knows there were four — it's in the folklore, garbled, half-remembered. Children sang a skipping version of the first verse on the green until the war. My grandmother knew it whole, I think, though she claimed otherwise, and my grandmother was not a woman whose claims I trusted." Her jaw tightened. "The fourth verse was torn out. By whom, we don't know. When, we don't know. But here is what I can tell you, Mr. Crane, and I will tell you once, and then I would like you to consider very carefully whether you wish to continue with this appraisal."

He waited.

"The song has four verses," she said. "Three keep the sleeper asleep. The fourth—" her finger hovered over the tear, "—the fourth wakes her. Or ends her. Or ends the singer; the accounts differ, and the accounts are all we have, and every account agrees on one thing." She looked up. "You must never let the house sing the fourth verse for you."

"What happens if it does?"

"Then you belong to the arrangement." She said it flatly, without drama, which made it worse. "That is the term the old families used. *The arrangement.* The house keeps a sleeper, and the living keep the song, and as long as the song stays broken — three verses known, the fourth lost — the arrangement holds. The house cannot finish the song itself. It needs a voice. It will give you three verses, Mr. Crane, it will sing them to you beautifully, it will teach them to you until you know them better than your own name, and then it will wait for you to supply the fourth. Out loud. Willingly. In your own voice."

"And if I don't?"

"Then it waits. It is very good at waiting. It waited for Alice Verrow, depending on which account you believe, and it has waited ever since." Edith drew the hymnal toward her and began, with deliberate ceremony, to wrap it again. "But it prefers not to wait forever. It persuades. It finds what a person lacks and offers it. Loneliness is the usual lever. Loneliness, or grief, or—" she paused, weighing whether to say it, saying it anyway, "—or a self the person has buried. Something unfinished. The house is patient with unfinished people."

Emrys thought of the recorder on his bedside table. Of the second voice, soft and familiar, asking questions in his own cadence. He said nothing, but Edith watched his face and apparently read enough there.

"You've heard it already," she said. Not a question either. She seemed to deal mostly in statements today, statements dressed as warnings.

"There's a voice in my sleep recordings," he said. "It answers questions I don't remember asking. It knows things it shouldn't."

"Then listen to me now, because this is the part that matters." Edith tied the string with a double knot, her hands steady again now that the book was covered. "The rules are these. First: do not sing along. Not a note, not a hum, not a tuneless murmur while you wash. If the house hears your voice carrying even one verse, it will count that as participation, and participation is consent. Second: do not ask it for the fourth verse. It will offer. Refuse. Third—" she hesitated.

"Third?"

"Third, and this is the one people break, because it doesn't feel like breaking anything." She fixed him with a look that had probably quelled generations of Sunday school pupils. "Never let it finish for you. There will come a night — soon, I'd guess, given the season — when you are tired, or frightened, or simply worn down to the point where silence seems worse than anything. And the house will begin the song, all three verses, perfect, tender, and it will pause at the edge of the fourth. It will leave a gap shaped exactly like the missing words. And everything in you will want to fill that gap, because gaps are unbearable, because you are a man who measures things precisely so that nothing is left unaccounted for." Her voice dropped. "Let the gap stay empty. Bite your tongue. Hold a pillow over your face. Whatever it takes. The moment the fourth verse sounds in a living voice — any living voice, even whispered, even half-asleep — the arrangement completes, and the house takes its singer."

"Took the last owner's tenant?" Emrys asked. "There was a woman—"

"Ruth Callow." Something moved behind Edith's eyes. "Ruth understood the rules. Ruth never sang, never answered, never gave it so much as a hummed bar. And when the moon came full she walked out and left everything below stairs in the sale, and she has never once come back to this village, and I am not entirely sure the house let her go so much as decided she wasn't worth keeping." She picked up the wrapped bundle. "Invisibility is accepted as payment exactly once, Mr. Crane. The house has already spent that coin on Ruth Callow. It will want something else from you."

The lamp buzzed. Down the length of the archive, the shelves stood in their ordered rows, every life filed, every entry accounted for, and Emrys felt suddenly the full weight of his profession — a decade of measuring rooms so that nothing in him would ever have to be measured instead.

"One more question," he said. "The child who wrote this. Alice. Was she the sleeper? Or the singer?"

Edith stopped with the bundle half-lifted, and for a long moment she looked at the torn edge showing through the tissue, and her face did something complicated and old.

"She was nine years old," Edith said finally. "Whatever she was, she has been nine years old since 1893, and everyone who might have answered that question is dead, and the ones who aren't dead—" she set the hymnal back in the drawer, gently, and locked it, "—are silent. Go home, Mr. Crane. Take your measurements. File your report. And whatever you hear tonight, however kindly it sings, however much it sounds like something you lost a long time ago — do not sing back."

She walked him to the door. Evening had come down over the churchyard while they talked, and the harvest moon, three nights from full, hung low and swollen over the yew trees, orange as a lamp seen through well water.

At the gate, Edith spoke once more, quietly, not quite meeting his eyes.

"If you ignore every warning I've given you," she said, "ignore this one least: the house will not sound like a stranger. That's the trap. It never sounds like a stranger."

She closed the door, and Emrys stood alone in the churchyard with the moon rising, and in his coat pocket, unbidden, his hand found his phone, and he caught himself — actually caught himself, fingers curled around it, breath held — listening for a voice that wasn't there yet, waiting for it to begin.

* * *

He drove back with the windows shut and the radio off, and the silence in the car was the particular silence of a held breath. Hollowmere showed itself at the end of the lane the way it always did now: dark, expectant, its windows reflecting the fattening moon like coins at the bottom of water.

He got out. He stood in the drive with his kit bag and did not go in, not immediately, because from somewhere deep in the house — below stairs, below the floorboards, below the warm door he had never opened — came a sound he recognized from the archive, from pencil lines pressed into endpaper by a nine-year-old hand.

Humming. Three phrases, unhurried, looping. Verse one. Then verse two. Then verse three.

And then a pause, small and patient and perfectly shaped, like a gap left in a row of stitches, waiting for the thread.

Emrys Crane stood in the moonlight with his keys in his fist and understood, at last, the shape of the game: the house had shown him its hand, three cards face up, and was waiting to see whether he would ask to see the fourth — or sing it.

He went inside, and did not hum, and lay awake until dawn with a pillow ready and the recorder running, while downstairs, softly, the gap waited to be filled.
