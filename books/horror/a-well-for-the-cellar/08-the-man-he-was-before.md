## 8. The Man He Was Before

The house had gone quiet in the way that was worse than noise. Emrys lay on the narrow bed in the east room with his recorder running on the nightstand, its small red eye watching him, and he thought: *I have done this before. I have lain awake beside something I loved and pretended to be asleep.*

He did not mean to remember. But Hollowmere had a way of prying up floorboards in a man, of finding the joists beneath the plaster, and what it found under his was ten years old and had never once been aired.

It began, as these things do, with a smell. Rain on hot pavement outside the window, and underneath it — or perhaps only in memory — the smell of a flat above a bakery in Ashford: warm yeast, damp wool, Tomas's cheap sandalwood oil worked into the grain of the kitchen table where he carved letters nobody would ever read. The smell arrived whole, uninvited, the way grief always does, dressed as nothing at all.

* * *

Ten years ago there had been a summer that smelled like this one, and a man named Tomas Reyes, and a decision made in the space of a single telephone call that Emrys had spent a decade telling himself was not a decision at all but simply the sensible order of things.

He had been twenty-nine. His father's firm — Crane & Son, though there had never been a son on the letterhead until that year — had finally offered him partnership. Not asked; offered, in the way his father offered things, as a door already open through which you were expected to walk without looking back. *We'll need you full-time by September,* his father had said. *None of this freelance nonsense. A man builds something or he doesn't.*

And Emrys had said yes. That was all. Yes, into a telephone receiver, standing in the kitchen of the Ashford flat while Tomas sat three feet away at the table he'd sanded himself, carving a wooden bird for a windowsill they had chosen together, and Emrys had said yes to his father and no to everything else without ever saying the word no, because Emrys Crane had built his whole life out of words he never quite said.

Tomas had looked up when he heard the tone of the call change. He was good at that — hearing weather coming in people's voices. He had put down his knife.

"That's the firm," he said. Not a question.

"That's the firm."

"And?"

Emrys had turned to the window. There was a habit in it already, he could see that now, lying in the dark at Hollowmere with the house listening: the turning away, the measuring of exits. Even then, even at twenty-nine, he had been surveying the room for the shortest distance between himself and a thing he did not want to feel.

"And I said yes," Emrys said.

Tomas was quiet for long enough that the bakery below went through an entire cycle of its machinery, the great dough-mixer shuddering the floor like something turning over in sleep. "To what, exactly? Say it plainly. You're a surveyor. Give me the boundary lines."

"They want me in the Canterbury office. Full time. Partnership track." Each fact laid down like a peg in a line, neat, defensible, dead.

"And us?"

"There is no *us* on a partnership track, Tom. You know how he is. You know what he'd—" Emrys stopped. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "My mother still introduces me as her son who does property. Just *property*. Like I'm a filing cabinet. If I bring home—"

"If you bring home *me*," Tomas said, and his voice didn't rise, which was the terrible thing about it; it went down instead, down into some cellar of itself, "what happens?"

"You know what happens."

"I want to hear you say it."

"I'm not going to say it."

"Then we're already done," Tomas said, "and one of us should be honest about when."

* * *

Here was the thing Emrys had never told anyone, the thing the house had found under its floorboard and was now holding up to the light with its cold patient fingers: it had not been fear of his father. Or not only. It had been something smaller and more shameful than fear. It had been relief.

For six years he had loved Tomas Reyes with the whole unguarded width of himself, and it had been like living in a house with all the windows open — wonderful and drafty and impossible to keep anything private, including from himself. Tomas saw him. That had been the gift and the terror of it. Tomas laughed at his jokes before he finished them, noticed when he was frightened before he admitted it, once stood behind him at a party with a hand flat between his shoulder blades and said, *you don't have to perform for these people*, and Emrys had felt seen so thoroughly that he'd had to go stand in the garden alone for twenty minutes just to reassemble the version of himself other people expected.

His father's firm required no such assembly. At Crane & Son he was already the finished article: competent, quiet, correct. Nobody looked too closely at a man who measured things for a living. Measurement was a kind of invisibility — you were the instrument, not the subject. The tape ran out from you toward the world and never back toward yourself. He had understood this about his work years before he would admit it aloud, and he understood it now, at forty, in a haunted house that kept echoing his own figures back at him in voices lower and truer than his own, as if the walls were saying: *we can see the man inside the instrument, and he has been in here a very long time.*

So yes. Relief. When his father's call came, some sealed chamber in Emrys had exhaled, because the choice had been made *for* him, and a man can bear almost anything if he can tell himself he had no hand in it.

He had told Tomas none of this. He had given him the boundary lines instead — the office, the mother, the respectable future — as though love were a parcel of land whose value could be argued down.

Tomas had listened to all of it with his hands flat on the table, on the half-carved bird, and then he had said the sentence that Emrys would carry for ten years like a stone in his boot:

"You always measure everything so you never have to hold anything."

And then, because Tomas was fair even in heartbreak, he had added: "I'd have come to Canterbury, you know. I'd have taken anything. I just needed you to say my name to them. Once. Out loud. In daylight."

Emrys had not said it. He had packed his tapes and his levels and his father's leather-bound field books, and he had left the flat above the bakery on a morning of thin rain, and Tomas had not come to the door, and the bird — the little wren he'd been carving — had sat finished on the windowsill, facing out, as if it too had decided not to watch him go.

* * *

He heard from mutual friends, over the years, in fragments. Tomas had moved to Bristol. Tomas taught joinery to teenagers now. Tomas had someone — a nurse, maybe, or a teacher; the fragments disagreed — and seemed happy, seemed settled, seemed the way he had always deserved to be. Emrys received each fragment the way he received subsidence reports: filed, dated, never opened again. He told himself the filing was peace. It was not peace. It was a well cap over something alive.

Because here was the other thing, the thing that made shame different from regret: regret looks backward, but shame sits in the present tense, humming. Every year of the decade since, Emrys had caught himself doing it — the turning away, the tidy figure, the refusal to hold. A woman named Marion at the Canterbury office had asked him to dinner twice and he had measured his way out of it both times with polite arithmetic. His father had died four years ago, and at the funeral Emrys had realized, standing at the graveside with his level in his coat pocket out of pure habit, that he had won. He had gotten exactly what he chose. Respectability, the firm, the clean ledger, the name on the letterhead — Crane & Son, sole proprietor now, the *Son* promoted to the whole of it. And it had weighed precisely nothing. It had appraised at nothing. All those years of careful, invisible, dutiful building, and the structure stood empty, and he was the tenant who never sang, keeping the rooms dark, paying rent in silence.

His mother, before her stroke, had said something to him once that he had also filed and never opened. She had held his hand in the care home, thinner than he remembered her, and said: "You were such a loud little boy, Emrys. What happened to you?" And he had smiled and said, "I grew up," and she had looked at him with the terrible clear eyes of the dying and said, "No. You got measured."

* * *

At Hollowmere, in the dark, the recorder's red light burned steady. Emrys lay still and let the memory finish its work, because he was learning — slowly, unwillingly, the way a body learns a fever — that this house did not accept filings. It wanted originals. It wanted the documents torn out of the hymnal, the verse ripped away, the truth of what happened below stairs.

He thought of the girl. Edith Aldous had told him only the outline — the Verrow family, 1893, a daughter lost to the well behind the kitchen — but outlines were his trade, and he could already read the shape of it the way he read a crack in a load-bearing wall. A child, drowned, and a family singing to her afterward. Singing her to sleep. Keeping her quiet. And he had thought, when Edith first said it, *how monstrous, to sing a child into silence rather than grieve her aloud* — and the thought had turned in his chest like a key, because he knew that song. He had been humming it for ten years in a lower register. *Hush now, hush now, all is measured, all is sound.* He had sung himself to sleep with duty the way the Verrows had sung their daughter down the well, and every verse of it had been a lie told gently enough to pass for care.

The house creaked somewhere below — the third stair, the cellar door, the places it always spoke from — and Emrys did not flinch. He was beginning to understand that the noises were not threats. They were questions. *What did you bury? Who did you leave? Why do you measure things instead of touching them?* The second voice on his recordings had asked them in his own cadence, softer, truer, and he had answered before he could stop himself, and the answers had sounded, God help him, like a young man in a flat above a bakery, like a man with all the windows open.

He sat up in the dark. The moon through the east window was a night from full, fat and low and orange-rimmed, and it lit the room the color of the rain-wet pavement outside the Ashford flat, the color of the morning he left.

"Tomas," he said aloud, to no one, to the house, to the boy he had been. Just the name. Out loud. In daylight, or near enough — the first pale grey of it coming up over the orchard.

The house went very still, the way a person goes still when they've been waiting years for you to say a thing.

Then, from below — from the cellar, from the capped well behind the kitchen, from whatever depth the house kept its truths — came a sound Emrys had not heard before. Not a whisper, not a hum. A single wet footstep. Then another. Small. Child-sized. Coming up.

Emrys reached for the recorder and pressed stop, and in the sudden silence understood that the house had heard the name, had weighed it, had entered it into its own ledger against the coming of the moon — and that whatever climbed the stairs now was coming to collect.
