## 14. The Voice Has His Face

He dreamed the way water dreams: without edges.

Emrys stood in the kitchen of Hollowmere House, but it was not the kitchen he had measured and photographed and entered into his report under *Room 4 — Kitchen, 4.2m × 3.8m, damp at north wall*. This kitchen was warm. Copper pots hung from hooks that gleamed. Bread sat rising under a cloth on the sill, and through the window the garden went on forever, hedges soft with late summer, a well nowhere in sight. The light was the light of four o'clock in October, gold and forgiving, the kind of light that made even duty look like a choice you'd have made freely.

Someone was humming in the next room.

He knew the tune before he knew he knew it. The first verse of the lullaby, the one Edith had shown him in the child's careful hand — *hush now, under, hush now, deep* — except here it was sung the way it must have been sung in 1893, unhurried, a mother's tempo, a rhythm meant to slow a small heart down to the speed of sleep. The house had hummed it at him for days, always slightly wrong, always hungry. This version was right. This version was worse.

"Hello?" he said, and his voice came out young.

That was the first thing he noticed about himself — that his voice had changed, dropped back thirty years into the register of a boy still growing into it, and when he looked down at his hands they were smooth, unlined, the knuckles unscarred by a decade of tape measures snapping shut on them. He turned them over. No wedding ring, though there had never been one. No callus on the middle finger where the pen pressed. A boy's hands. Hands that hadn't yet learned to hold things at arm's length.

"In here," said the voice from the next room. "You're late."

*You're late.* As if this were an appointment. As if some part of him had been expected here all along, had been waiting patiently through ten years of square footage and probate valuations and rented cellars with noises he refused to name.

He walked through the doorway because there was nothing else to do, and because — and this was the shame of it, the thing he would carry out of the dream like a stone in his shoe — because he wanted to.

* * *

The sitting room was his grandmother's sitting room, or close enough. Faded roses on the armchairs. A clock that ticked too loudly, the way clocks only tick in childhood houses. And in the chair by the window, turning its face toward him with the unhurried grace of someone who has all the time the dead are said to have, sat Emrys Crane.

Not the Emrys who had arrived at Hollowmere with a laser measure and a thermos and a decade of practiced deafness. A younger one. Twenty-six, perhaps twenty-seven. The age he had been the summer everything split down the middle. Dark hair worn too long, the way his father had hated. A shirt with the top two buttons open. That terrible, unguarded openness around the eyes — the look of a man who still believed the world would make room for him if he simply stood in it honestly.

The face smiled at him, and it was like watching a photograph smile.

"You've been listening to my recordings," Emrys said. His young voice cracked on *recordings*, and he hated it, and the other him's smile widened as if the crack were a gift.

"I've been *making* them," the younger Emrys said. "You sleep so loudly, you know. All those questions. You've been asking them for years. I just got tired of answering into a pillow."

"The questions." Emrys's mouth was dry, though in dreams dryness is a courtesy, a detail offered for realism. "What did you bury. Who did you leave."

"Why do you measure things instead of touching them." The younger man finished it for him, gently, the way you finish a line of a song someone has faltered on. He rose from the chair. Up close he smelled of cut grass and cheap soap and something else underneath, something mineral and cold, the smell of the well behind the kitchen, and Emrys stepped back before he could stop himself.

"Don't," the younger one said, and there was no anger in it, only a patience so vast it felt like drowning. "Don't do that. Don't flinch from me. I'm the only part of you that never flinched."

"You're not me."

"No?" The younger Emrys tilted his head, and the gesture was so familiar it hurt — Emrys did it himself when he was being patient with a client who didn't understand load-bearing walls. "Then tell me what happened to me. Go on. You're a surveyor. You catalog things. Catalog me. Date of last sighting. Condition. Reason for removal from the inventory."

"I don't—"

"August," the younger one said. "Ten years ago. A flat above a laundrette in Manchester that smelled of other people's washing. Tomas asleep with his hand open on the pillow, the way he sleeps, the way he probably still sleeps, if he still sleeps the way he—" the voice caught, or performed catching, Emrys couldn't tell which, and the performance was somehow more devastating than the real thing, "—did. And you standing in the doorway with your father's phone call still in your ear. *Crane & Son needs someone steady.* Steady. You loved that word more than you ever loved him, and you didn't even love the word. You loved what it let you hide behind."

"Stop."

"You took the job. You packed while he was at work because you couldn't do it to his face. You left a note. A *note*, Emrys. Four sentences. You measured the distance between yourself and the door every day after that, and you called it a career."

"*Stop.*"

The room obeyed him. The clock stopped ticking. The light held itself perfectly still, like a photograph of light rather than light. In the silence Emrys could hear, very far below the floorboards, water moving. Not rushing. Waiting.

The younger Emrys sat back down and folded his hands, and for a moment he looked less like a ghost and more like a man at an interview, presenting his best self, and Emrys understood with a lurch that this was exactly what he was doing. This was a pitch. Everything here was a pitch.

"I'm not here to hurt you," the younger one said. "I know how that sounds. Everyone who says it is lying, and I can't prove otherwise, and you're a professional, you don't take things on faith. So let me show you the terms instead. You like terms. You like clauses and schedules and figures you can defend."

He gestured, and the window behind him filled not with the endless garden but with the cellar of Hollowmere House as Emrys had never seen it — because he had never opened the door, because the door was warm, because some animal ledger in him had always known that certain entries, once opened, cannot be closed. The cellar in the window was clean. Dry. Lit by a lamp he recognized, absurdly, from his grandmother's hall. On the floor, chalked in a child's uneven hand, were words he could not read from here but somehow already knew.

"The harvest moon is in three nights," the younger Emrys said. "You know what happens then. The house sings all four verses, and the fourth verse will be in your voice, because it's been practicing with mine, and when it finishes, it will want an answer. It always wants an answer. Ruth Callow stayed invisible and it took her once and let her go, but it's had a taste of you now. It knows you answer questions. It's been feeding on your answers for a week like a cat with cream."

"So I leave. I drive out tonight. I file the report from a Travelodge and I never think about this place again except badly, at three in the morning, for the rest of my life."

"And the girl?"

The question landed in the still air between them and did not fall.

"She drowned," Emrys said quietly. "In 1893. She was — Edith wouldn't say how old. Small. Her family sang to keep her sleeping and something went wrong, or someone did, and she's been down there ever since, and the house keeps her, and the house is *hungry*, and none of that is my responsibility. I'm a surveyor. I appraise property. I don't perform exorcisms."

"No," the younger Emrys agreed. "You bury things. It's the family trade. Your father buried your mother's illness under renovations. His father buried whatever it was he buried. You come from a long line of men who keep excellent records of everything except themselves." He stood again, and this time he crossed the room, and this time Emrys did not step back, because the alternative was to admit how much he wanted to. "But here's the clause no one read to you. The house doesn't actually want *you*. It wants someone who'll stay. Someone who'll sing. Someone who'll be its occupant, unaccounted for. And I—"

He touched his own chest, that open, unguarded chest, and smiled the smile of a man offering a bargain he has rehearsed for ten years in the dark.

"—have been staying all along. I never left that flat in Manchester. I'm still there, in the doorway, with the note half-written. Every night you sleep, you visit me. So the offer is simple, and it's fair, and you can enter it into your ledger: *let me sing the fourth verse for you.*"

Emrys's breath stopped.

"I know it," the younger one said softly. "I've known it since the second night, when the house sang the first three and left the space where the fourth should be, the way a sentence leaves a space. I've been learning it in the dark while you pretend to sleep. I'll go down to the cellar on the harvest moon. I'll sing it whole, in my voice, which is your voice, which will satisfy the house as well as yours would. And you'll walk out. Free. Clear title. Unencumbered. You can put that in the report — *no further action required*. Doesn't that sound like peace? Doesn't that sound like the rest of your life, tidy and quiet and entirely unchanged?"

"It sounds," Emrys said, and his young voice shook, "like you want something."

"Of course I want something." The younger Emrys laughed, and the laugh was the worst thing yet, because it was *his* laugh, the one he'd had before he trained it down to a chuckle suitable for site meetings. "I want what I've always wanted. I want you to stay. Stay here, in this kitchen, in this light. Stay with me. We're very good company, you and I — we always were, before you decided we weren't. Stay, and sleep, and let me carry the singing. You've carried everything alone for ten years. Let someone carry it for once. Even if the someone is only the part of you that you threw away."

The light thickened. The bread on the sill in the other room seemed to rise higher, impossibly, the dough swelling like a lung. Far below the floorboards the water turned over, attentive, listening, and Emrys understood suddenly that this room was not a memory dressed up by his sleeping mind. It was a room the house had built. Furnished from his childhood, staffed with his own discarded face, warmed to the exact temperature of the life he had refused. The house had studied him the way he studied buildings, room by room, creak by creak, and it had found the one chamber he kept locked and built a copy of it and put a fire in the grate.

And God help him, it was warm. After a week of cold rooms and colder figures, after ten years of measuring distances, it was warm, and the chair by the window had his name on it in everything but paint, and the younger Emrys was holding out his hand — that smooth, unscarred, untouched hand — and saying, in the voice of a boy who had once been loved completely by a man named Tomas:

"Stay. Just say you'll stay. You don't even have to mean it. You never mean anything; that's the arrangement. Say it, and I'll do the rest, and you can go back to your ledgers and your rented cellar and your noises you don't hear, and you'll never have to hold anything again as long as you live."

Emrys looked at the hand.

He thought of Tomas's hand open on the pillow. He thought of the fingernail grooves scored into the underside of a well cover, from inside. He thought of a drowned girl waiting three nights for a moon, and of Edith Aldous going pale at the name of a house, and of the torn page in a child's hymnal, and of the final line the house had written in his own report: *One occupant, unaccounted for.*

And he understood, with the terrible clarity that dreams grant and mornings revoke, what the occupant was. What it had always been. Not the girl in the well. The girl was a prisoner. The occupant — the one the house needed, the one it fed, the one it sang to and sang through — was whoever agreed to stop. Whoever sat down in the warm chair. Whoever let someone else sing for them, and called it mercy.

His buried self wasn't offering to save him.

His buried self was the bait.

"I have to go," Emrys said.

The light did not dim. The younger Emrys did not lower his hand. But the smile changed — softened, or curdled, it was impossible to say — and behind the familiar face, for just a moment, Emrys saw something else looking out. Something patient and flooded and very old, wearing a young man's grief the way a hermit crab wears a borrowed shell.

"You'll come back," it said, in his voice, in the voice of everyone who had ever asked him to stay anywhere. "They always come back. The ones who measure instead of touch — they always come back to the rooms where nobody ever asked them to be anything. Three nights, Emrys. The moon is getting fat. And I'll be here, and the chair will be warm, and the fourth verse is *lovely*. You have no idea how lovely. Come and hear it. Come and stay. Come home."

The floorboards beneath him went soft as water, and Emrys fell — not downward, but backward, out of the warm room, out of the offered hand, down through the dark toward the body lying cold in the narrow bed of Hollowmere House, where somewhere a recorder was running, and somewhere below the floor a cellar door stood warm and patient, and somewhere in the walls a voice that wore his face was already rehearsing.

He woke with the taste of well water in his mouth and dawn grey at the curtains, and on the pillow beside his head, printed in damp that had no source, was the shape of a small hand — reaching, or beckoning, he could not tell which — pointing toward the cellar door.

Two nights now. And the moon, when he pulled back the curtain, was nearly round.
