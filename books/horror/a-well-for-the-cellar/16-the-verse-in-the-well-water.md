## 16. The Verse in the Well Water

The string was nylon, thirty metres of it, bought that morning from the hardware shop on Fenwick Street where the proprietor had asked no questions beyond whether Emrys wanted a hook as well. He had said yes. He had not explained what he intended to hang.

He stood at the kitchen window of Hollowmere House and looked out at the well behind it, the way he had looked at it every morning for a week now — the way one looks at a wound through a bandage, knowing the shape beneath but refusing the arithmetic of what lies inside. The stone cap sat flush with the earth, its iron ring furred with moss. The fingernail grooves scored into its underside were invisible from here. He did not need to see them. He had put his fingers into them once, already, and his fingertips still carried a memory of cold that no amount of washing seemed to lift.

The recorder sat on the kitchen table between the teacup he hadn't drunk from and the appraisal file he could no longer open without his hands shaking. It was a good machine. He'd used it for eleven years, first for site notes, then — after the noises started in his own cellar, after the first winter of thumps and dragging sounds that he had catalogued as *thermal expansion, pipework, settlement* — for sleep. He knew its tolerances the way he knew the tolerances of joists and lintels. He trusted it more than he trusted himself, which was, he understood now, precisely the problem. A man who trusts machines over his own ears has already decided what he does not want to hear.

*You always measured everything so you'd never have to hold anything.*

Tomas's voice, ten years late, arriving down a telephone line like something lowered on a string.

Emrys tied the recorder to the nylon with a double hitch, checked it twice, then a third time because checking things was the only prayer he had left. He wrapped the machine in a freezer bag against the damp, taped the seal, and threaded the string through the gap where the cap had been lifted — he had levered it up two inches the night before and wedged it with a slate, telling himself it was temporary, telling himself a great many things were temporary.

The well breathed at him.

There was no other word for it. The air that came up through the two-inch gap was cool and wet and moved with a slow tidal patience, in and out, in and out, as though the shaft below were the throat of something asleep. It smelled of iron and old leaves and, underneath, faintly, of pond water in a church font — that mineral sweetness that is water which has been prayed over or wept into, he couldn't have said which.

He knelt. His knees found the flagstones and the flagstones were warm. That was the thing about Hollowmere now; he had stopped being surprised by warmth in places where there should be none. The house had been warming to him all week, room by room, the way a body warms a bed, and he no longer pretended this comforted him.

"Right," he said aloud, because saying things aloud had become necessary, a way of proving his voice was still only his own. "I'm going to lower you down. I don't know if you can hear me. I think you can hear everything."

The draft stirred the hair on his forearms. In, and out.

"I'm not going to sing," he said. "I want you to know that. Whatever you give me back, I'm not going to sing it. I've learned that much."

He fed the string over the lip of the well, inch by inch, letting it run across the stone where a girl's fingers had worn their grooves a hundred and thirty years ago. Three feet. Six. The string went taut, slackened, taut again as the recorder swung against the shaft wall. Ten feet. Fifteen. He counted automatically — he would always count, that was the ruin and the engine of him — and at twenty feet he felt the resistance change, the string drifting instead of dropping, and he knew the machine hung above water now, above whatever surface waited down there in the dark like a held breath.

He let out five more feet and tied the string off around the iron ring. Then he sat back on his heels in the wet grass and waited.

The house waited with him. He could feel it the way you feel someone standing behind you in an empty room — not the presence itself but the attention. Every beam, every lath, every pane of glass in Hollowmere had turned its face toward the well behind the kitchen. Even the wind seemed to go around the house rather than through the garden, leaving a pocket of stillness around the cap, around him, around the string that trembled faintly between worlds.

He gave it ten minutes. Then twenty. The light thickened toward evening, the harvest moon three nights off now and already fat, already rising earlier each evening like something impatient to be seen. He thought about Ruth Callow in her caravan, who had never sung, never answered, and who had paid for her silence with a lifetime of being unseen. He thought about Edith Aldous's face when she had said *the last woman who sang it whole*, and how she had not finished the sentence, and how the unfinished part had followed him home.

He thought about Tomas, and did not allow himself to think about Tomas, and thought about him anyway.

At forty minutes, the string moved.

Not the wind — there was no wind. Not settling — the recorder weighed less than a pound. The string simply drew inward, gently, deliberately, the way a hand draws a note from a piano string, and Emrys watched the nylon slide off the iron ring and begin to rise, foot by foot, reeling itself in, until the recorder broke the surface of the well into the last of the daylight and swung to rest against the stones, dripping, patient, returned.

He untied it with hands that were steadier than he expected. Steadier than they had any right to be. He peeled away the freezer bag, dried the machine on his shirt hem, and carried it inside, and the whole way across the garden he felt the house lean after him like a listener leaning toward a door.

In the kitchen he set the recorder on the table. He pressed playback.

First: nothing. Forty minutes of nothing, the hiss of the machine's own blood, the small tick of its workings. He almost laughed — of course, of course, a fool with a string and a box, a surveyor's superstition, he would write it up as—

Then the hiss changed.

It deepened. It took on texture, the texture of a large enclosed space, of stone walls and standing water, and beneath it, so faint he had to close his eyes and turn the gain up until the speakers hissed like rain, there was breathing. Small breaths. Child-sized. In, and out, in the same tidal rhythm as the air from the shaft.

And then she sang.

The voice was thin and clear and terribly young, and it came up out of the recording the way light comes up out of deep water — bent, dimmed, but unmistakably light. She sang the first verse, the one the house hummed, the one he had caught himself humming at the cap with his hand flat on the stone: *Hush now, under, hush and lie, the moon's a lamp in a darkened sky...* And then the second verse, and the third, each one delivered with the careful precision of a child who has been made to rehearse, and after the third verse she paused, and the pause lasted long enough that Emrys found himself leaning toward the machine, his hands flat on the table, his breath held, waiting the way the house was waiting.

When she spoke, it was not to him. Or it was entirely to him. He could not decide, and the not-deciding was worse than either.

"*Whoever finds this,*" the drowned girl said, and her voice had the echo of the shaft in it, the sound of words spoken twenty feet down with stone on every side, "*whoever is brave enough to come looking — the song isn't finished. They tore it off so I couldn't say it. But I remember it. I've had such a long time to remember.*"

A breath. Water moving somewhere below her, far below, though she was speaking from the water, or the water was speaking shaped like her, and Emrys did not examine this too closely because examining things too closely was how he had spent a decade not hearing his own cellar.

"*I wasn't a monster,*" she said, and her voice cracked on the word, cracked the way a child's cracks, ordinary and human and unbearable. "*They wrote me down as a monster because it was easier than writing down what she did. I only wanted someone to sing me to sleep. I only wanted the last verse, so I could finish it myself and stop. Do you know what it is, to know half a song forever? To be left hanging on the third line of your own lullaby, a hundred years, a hundred years, while everyone upstairs lives their lives and locks their cellars?*"

The recording hissed. Emrys realized his face was wet. He did not remember starting to cry; it seemed to have happened the way weather happens, without his permission, somewhere in the middle of the second verse.

"*I don't want the man who sings it,*" she said softly. "*The man who sings it stays. I've had them. I've kept them. I'm sorry for that. I'm sorry for all of them, the ones in the walls, the ones who answered the house because answering was easier than being alone.*" Another breath, and the water-sound rose around it, gentle, tidal. "*I want the man who learns it and gives it back. Who says it without singing. Who understands that some songs are cages and the cage isn't mine.*"

She began the fourth verse then, and Emrys did the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than the phone call, harder than the well, harder than ten years of silence: he listened without joining. His lips stayed still. His throat locked. The verse poured into him like water into a dry channel — four lines, simple, a child's cadence, words about sleep and moonlight and being let go — and he took them in whole and silent, memorizing not just the words but the shape of the pity underneath them, because the verse was not a spell, he understood suddenly, it was a permission. It was the sentence everyone else had refused to finish for her. *Sleep now, truly, sink and rest, the well's a bed and the dark's a guest...*

The recording ended on the last word, and the hiss closed over it like water.

Emrys sat in the kitchen of Hollowmere House for a long time. Outside, the moon was coming up over the hedge, orange and enormous, two nights early in his heart. On the table, the appraisal file lay shut, and he found he could not look at it, because he knew now what the house wanted him to write in it, and it was not a figure.

He got up. He washed his face. He took the recorder, and the verse folded small and silent inside him like a letter he had promised to deliver, and he went to find his coat, because there was one more person he had to see before the moon rose full — and when he opened the front door, Edith Aldous was already standing on the step, grey-faced, holding a hymnal with a torn page, and she said, "You've heard it, haven't you. I can see it in your face. God help us both, boy — she gave you the verse."
