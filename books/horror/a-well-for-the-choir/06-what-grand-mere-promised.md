## 6. What Grand-mère Promised

The letters were in the cedar chest at the foot of Grand-mère's bed, and Elias found them on his fourth night in the house because Elodie told him where to look.

She had gotten better at it. The first nights she could only manage shapes—her hand flat against the inside of the glass, fingers spread, pressing as if the mirror were a window in a burning house. But he had started bringing a notebook to the hallway floor, sitting cross-legged with his back against the wall opposite her, and they had fallen back into the old grammar of their childhood. One tap for yes. Two for no. Three for *look again*. She had tapped out *chest* by spelling it against her knee, letter by letter, her mouth shaping each one with exaggerated care while her eyes stayed fixed on him with an intensity that made his chest ache.

*C-E-D-A-R.*

He had almost forgotten that game. They'd invented it when they were nine, during the summer of the tonsillectomies, lying in twin beds across the room from each other, too hoarse to talk, spelling words against the mattress springs so their mother wouldn't hear them planning to run away to New Orleans. He had not thought about it in twenty years. His body remembered anyway.

The chest was under quilts that smelled of camphor and dust. Inside: christening gowns, a lock of hair tied with ribbon gone brittle as straw, and letters. Dozens of them. Bundled with string, ordered by year, the oldest paper gone the color of weak tea.

He carried them to the kitchen, set them under the brightest light, and began to read.

* * *

The earliest was dated 1911, written in a hand so fine and slanted it looked like rain falling sideways.

*My dear Odile,*

*You asked me in your last letter whether I regret it, and I will answer you honestly, because you are the only one left who remembers her voice. I do not regret it. I miss her every day, but I do not regret it, and you must understand the difference if you are ever called to make the same accounting.*

*Before Célestine went into the glass, we were afraid all the time. You were too young to remember what the winters were like before the singing. What came up from the water. What it did to the dogs, to the milk, to Papa's mind. The choir took her, yes—but it gave us back our sleep. There is no arithmetic that makes that painless. There is only arithmetic that makes it necessary.*

*One voice per generation, Odile. That is the whole of the bargain, and it has held since before the parish had a name. Keep the song alive and the thing beneath the well keeps sleeping. Let the song lapse and—*

*Well. Ask the Boudreaus why there is no house at the end of their lane anymore.*

*Do not let them tell you it is a comfort. It is not a comfort. It is a payment, and we are a family that pays.*

*Your mother in all but blood,*
*Alphonsine Delacroix*

Elias read it three times. The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere above him, in the walls, the house made a sound like a throat clearing.

*One voice per generation.*

He thought about the hymnal in the attic, the margins crowded with names, most of them marked with dates of death that belonged to no one. He had assumed it was some kind of family Bible joke, some morose Delacroix bookkeeping. Now the names rearranged themselves in his head. Not deaths. *Payments.* A ledger.

He got up and poured himself two fingers of the bourbon he'd found in the pantry, then stood looking at the dark window over the sink—at his own reflection, faint and doubled in the black glass—and drank it down.

* * *

By the sixth letter he understood the shape of it, and by the twelfth he understood the horror, and the two understandings arrived separately, which somehow made both worse.

The bargain, laid out across a century of correspondence between Delacroix women who wrote to each other the way soldiers write home:

There was something beneath the well. Something old enough that the parish's founding records—which he would later learn Father Aubert kept locked in a safe—referred to it only obliquely, as *the water's tenant*, as though it were renting. The first Delacroix, a woman named Alphonsine who arrived with nothing but a hymnal and a voice the neighbors described in letters as *uncanny*, had found a way to bind it. Not to kill it. Not to drive it off. To sing it to sleep, the way you might sing to a child, or a wolf, or a fire.

But the song required voices. Living voices fed it while they lived; dead voices kept it after. And the binding needed renewal—one voice, given fully, each generation. Given how? The letters circled the question without landing on it, the way people circle the word *cancer* or *war*. Alphonsine's daughter wrote of "going to the glass." Her granddaughter wrote of "joining the choir." A woman named Sylvie, in 1954, wrote the plainest sentence in the whole bundle: *Maman walked into the mirror on the last night of October and did not walk out, and the well was quiet by morning, and we buried an empty casket because there was nothing left to bury.*

An empty casket.

Elias sat very still in the kitchen, listening to the house breathe around him, thinking about the funeral four days ago. Thinking about the casket, closed, heavy, handled by six pallbearers who had grunted with its weight. Thinking about the brass rail and his reflection arriving late.

Thinking about the fact that everyone in that church had believed the body was his.

* * *

Elodie was waiting for him that night. He knew it before he reached the hallway—the air near the mirror had a quality he was learning to recognize, a stillness like the moment before an orchestra begins.

She stood in the glass with her hands clasped, and when she saw him sit down with the notebook and the letters stacked beside him, her expression changed. He had been cataloguing her expressions the way a man dying of thirst catalogs rain. This one was dread.

"You've read them," he said, and felt foolish saying it aloud to someone who couldn't hear, but talking helped him think, and she seemed to read his lips well enough. "One voice per generation."

One tap. Yes.

"And it's always—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "It's always twins."

Two taps. No.

He blinked. "No?"

Her hands moved, quick and frustrated, and then she stopped herself, closed her eyes, and spelled it out slowly against her thigh. He copied the letters into the notebook, his handwriting getting worse with each one.

*T-W-I-N-S O-N-L-Y S-I-N-C-E M-A-M-A-N*

"Since Maman," he said. Their mother. Who had died when they were eleven, too fast and too completely for two children to understand, in a hospital two parishes away, while the twins stayed with Grand-mère for one strange golden autumn they had never once discussed as adults—as if by unspoken agreement the whole season had been sealed.

Elodie nodded in the glass. Then she pointed, insistently, at the letters beside him. At the bottom of the stack. The most recent ones.

He sorted through them. Most were decades old. But at the very bottom, in an envelope with no stamp, addressed in Grand-mère's tight hand to *My successor, whoever you turn out to be*, he found a letter dated fourteen years ago—the autumn their mother died.

*If you are reading this, then I am gone, and the arrangement is in motion, and I have done what I could with the time I was given.*

*Understand this above all things: the choir does not choose. We choose for it. That is the only mercy left in the bargain—that the family picks which child goes, and the picking can be steered. Since your mother, the debt falls on twins; the choir has developed a taste for paired voices, God forgive us, and will accept nothing else. But within the pair, the choice is ours.*

*I have chosen Elodie.*

*I chose her because she is the stronger. Because she sings true without trying, the way Alphonsine sang, and the choir takes the truest voice first when it is offered rightly. And because Elias is weaker, and softer, and would not survive the choosing, and I love him too much to waste him.*

*Do not judge me. Or judge me, but understand: I have watched this family pay its debts for eighty years, and I have learned that the only sin worse than the payment is paying double because you flinched.*

*When the twins are grown—when the house calls for them—I will make the offering. Elodie will go into the glass, and Elias will live out his life never knowing, and the well will sleep another fifty years, and that is the best ending available to any of us.*

*Burn this after reading.*

*Marguerite Delacroix*

Elias read it twice. Then he looked up at the mirror.

Elodie was crying. Silently, the way she had cried at nine years old with a sore throat, refusing to make a sound about it. In the glass her tears moved wrong—too slow, catching light that wasn't in the hallway—but they were unmistakably tears.

"She chose you," he said. His voice came out cracked. "Grand-mère chose you. Fourteen years ago."

One tap. Yes.

"And you knew."

A pause. Then, slowly, one tap.

The bourbon turned to ice water in his stomach. "You knew. All this time. Since you were—" He did the arithmetic and hated the answer. "Since we were eleven."

Elodie wiped her face with the heel of her hand—a gesture so familiar, so *hers*, that it hurt more than anything else that night—and then she began to spell again, slower now, weighing each letter.

*S-H-E T-O-L-D U-S B-O-T-H*

"No she didn't."

*T-H-I-N-K*

And he thought. He sat on the cold hallway floor and made himself go back through that autumn, the sealed season, the golden weeks at Grand-mère's house. The afternoons spent in the attic while Grand-mère played piano below. The hymns they were taught—not school songs, not church songs, but other ones, in French so old it barely qualified as French, melodies that snagged in the memory like fishhooks. How Grand-mère had drilled them, patiently, for weeks. How Elodie had learned them easily and he had struggled, and how Grand-mère had finally said, *that's all right, chérie, you'll carry the tune for her*—and he had thought nothing of it. He had been eleven. He had thought nothing of anything.

"She was teaching us the verses," he said. "Both of us. So one of us could sing along when the time came."

One tap.

"And the one who sings along—" He stopped. He looked at his sister in the glass, at the tear tracks drying wrong, at the hallway behind her in the reflection, which was the same hallway and yet subtly deeper than it should be, its doorways receding into a dimness his actual hallway did not possess. "The one who sings along joins the choir. That's the mechanism. That's how it takes you. Not walking into mirrors. Singing."

Elodie's face did something complicated—grief and confirmation braided together—and then she pressed her palm flat to the glass, and he pressed his to the other side, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

Then she pulled back and spelled one more line, and her hands were shaking.

*I-T W-A-S M-E-A-N-T T-O B-E M-E*

"I know," he said.

*N-O-T Y-O-U*

"I know, Elodie."

*B-U-T T-H-E-Y H-A-V-E Y-O-U N-O-W*

The hallway lights flickered—once, twice—and steadied. In the mirror, Elodie glanced past his shoulder, down the hall toward the front door, and her whole body went rigid.

He turned. Nothing there. Just the dark hall, the counted doors, all seven shut and latched, exactly as he'd left them at dusk.

When he turned back, Elodie was pointing. Not at the hall. Downward. Past the floor, past the foundation, down toward whatever lay beneath the house and the yard and the roots of the dead pecan tree—down toward the wellhouse at the edge of the property that he had not yet gone near, that he had been carefully, deliberately not going near, the way you don't look directly at a dog you suspect is rabid.

She spelled it out one final time, each letter struck hard enough that he could almost hear it through the glass.

*T-H-E-Y D-O-N-T K-E-E-P I-T A-S-L-E-E-P F-O-R F-R-E-E*

"What do you mean? What aren't they keeping it asleep for free?"

But she was fading—he could see it happening, the edges of her going soft, the glass taking on the ordinary dullness of ordinary silvered glass—and the last thing she managed, before she was gone entirely, was not spelled at all. She mouthed it, slow and enormous, shaping each word with the full force of her lungs:

*Go listen to it.*

Outside, far across the dark lawn, the wellhouse door—which he had checked and rechecked and padlocked himself—swung open with a sound like a sigh.

The padlock dangled, unlocked, from the hasp.

He had locked it that morning. He was certain. He was almost certain.

Elias sat alone in the hallway with a century of his family's confessions spread around him on the floor, and listened to the house hum.
