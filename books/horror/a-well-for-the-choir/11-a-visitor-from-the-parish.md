## 11. A Visitor from the Parish

The knock came at nine in the morning, which was the first strange thing about it. Nothing in this house happened in daylight anymore.

Elias had been asleep on the couch again—bedrooms were a coin flip now, and the couch at least stayed where he left it—and the sound pulled him up through layers of dream he didn't want to name. In the last one, Elodie had been teaching him a word with three syllables, her mouth moving slow behind the glass, and just as his lips found the shape of it the whole mirror had filled with water from the bottom up, like a glass being poured, and she'd kept mouthing the word underwater without bubbles.

He crossed the hall. Every mirror he passed, he looked at out of habit now, the way you check a lock. His reflection checked him back, more or less on time. The lag was down to a flicker most mornings—half a heartbeat, a stutter of glass—and some part of him had started to find that worse than the full second. A full second was a fact. A flicker was a question.

The knock came again. Three raps, evenly spaced, patient. The knock of a man who had knocked on doors for fifty years and expected them to open.

Through the sidelight—a narrow pane of wavy old glass that showed nothing but a smear of color—he saw black. Clerical black.

His stomach dropped before his mind caught up. He opened the door.

Father Aubert stood on the porch with a hat in one hand and a leather satchel in the other, and he was older than Elias remembered, which was unfair, because Elias hadn't seen him since he was nine. The priest had buried Grand-mère; Elias had watched from the back row in a clip-on tie. Now the man's face had gone soft and yellowed around the eyes, like paper kept too long near a window, and his hands were spotted and steady.

"Monsieur Delacroix," Aubert said. "Or—" He stopped himself. The pause was small and terrible. "May I come in?"

"You buried my brother," Elias said. It came out flat, an accusation he hadn't planned.

"I buried what was in the casket," Aubert said. "Whether it was your brother is a question I have been asking God for two weeks." He lifted the satchel slightly. "I've brought something that might help us both ask it better."

* * *

Elias made coffee because his hands needed a task, and because watching a priest sit in Grand-mère's kitchen felt like a thing that required props. The percolator took forever, the way it always did, ticking and shuddering like something trying to get out. Aubert sat with his hat on his knee and did not look around the room, which was its own kind of looking. People who refused to look around a room were always the ones who'd already memorized it.

"Your grandmother's house," Aubert said. "I haven't been inside since her funeral. She served café noir so strong it could stand a spoon in it."

"She's dead."

"I'm aware." He said it gently. "I was there."

"The parish was there." Elias set the cup down harder than he meant to. "The whole parish was there, Father, singing hymns over a body wearing my face, and not one person thought to say, excuse me, isn't this the wrong twin?"

Aubert wrapped both hands around his cup without drinking. "What would you have wanted said?"

"That it was her. That the woman in the box was Elodie."

"And who would have believed you?" The priest's voice stayed level, almost kind. "You were grieving. You were alone. You arrived the day before the funeral after eleven years away, and the body—the body was identified by me, by the coroner, by the dental records your sister kept current and you let lapse. Monsieur Delacroix, I have spent forty years telling grieving people true things they cannot hear. I know what it looks like when a living man insists the dead one is someone else." He finally drank. "It looks exactly like this."

"So you think I'm crazy."

"I think you're correct," Aubert said, "which is worse."

The percolator ticked. Somewhere upstairs, or in the walls, or in the space between those two things, the house made a sound like a throat clearing.

Elias sat down across from him. "Say that again."

Aubert reached into the satchel and brought out a ledger—old, cloth-bound, the spine sun-bleached to the color of weak tea. He laid it on the table between them with the care of a man handling a relic, which, in a way, he was.

"Parish burial records," he said. "Delacroix line. Going back to 1891." He opened it to a ribbon marker. "Read the names."

Elias read.

The handwriting changed decade by decade, ink going from iron gall brown to fountain-pen blue to ballpoint black, but the pattern never changed. *Marie-Celeste Delacroix, d. 1903.* *Joseph Delacroix, d. 1927.* *Antoinette Delacroix, d. 1949.* And beside each name, in a second column, in every era the same second hand, smaller, tighter: a date that did not match. Marie-Celeste's burial recorded in April; the column noting her death the previous November. Joseph buried in spring; died, according to the margin, the winter before. Over and over. Every Delacroix. A gap between the day they stopped and the day anyone admitted they'd stopped.

"What is this?" Elias asked, though his mouth had gone dry, because he knew. He'd seen the same arithmetic in Grand-mère's hymnal, in the margins crowded with names and wrong dates. Two books keeping the same lie.

"That is the question I brought it here for," Aubert said. "Because I'll tell you what I told myself for forty years: clerical error. Sloppy record-keeping. Rural parish, no telephone, bodies sometimes found late. It happens." He turned pages with one spotted finger. "It does not happen eleven times in a row, in the same family, always in the same direction. The recorded death is always *later* than the real one. Never earlier. As if the family needed time to—" He searched for the word and didn't like any of them. "—to prepare a substitute."

"Substitute," Elias repeated.

"A body. A story. A name." Aubert closed the ledger softly. "Two weeks ago I buried a Delacroix twin under the name Elias. The dental records said Elias. The coroner's report said Elias. But I have been a priest in this parish for thirty-one years, and I baptized both of those children in the same font on the same morning, and I will tell you something I have never said aloud to another living soul." He looked at Elias directly, and his eyes were very tired and very clear. "When I looked into that casket, I knew her. Not as Elodie—I hadn't seen either of you since you were small. But I knew the face. Because I had seen it before, monsieur. In this book." He tapped the ledger. "Every generation, the same face comes through this parish twice. Once alive, once dead. And once—just once per century—it comes through *both ways at once*, and nobody says a word, and the margin gets its date."

The kitchen light buzzed overhead. Elias became aware that he could hear the house breathing—that low, tidal sound in the walls he'd stopped noticing sometime in the second week, the way you stop noticing surf.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked. "Why now? Why come here?"

"Because of the window," Aubert said.

* * *

The priest would not take coffee refills and would not move from the kitchen table, and when Elias suggested they talk in the parlor, Aubert glanced toward the hallway—the hallway with the tall mirror at its end—and said, "Here is fine," with a firmness that made Elias's skin prickle. The priest had been in this house before. The priest knew about the hallway.

"It was three nights after the burial," Aubert said. "I couldn't sleep. This happens, at my age, after a funeral—especially a funeral that sits wrong in the stomach. So I got up and went to the rectory kitchen for warm milk like an old woman, and the rectory faces east, toward your property, across the cane fields. And there was light in an upper window of this house."

"Which window?"

"The nursery," Aubert said, and watched Elias carefully when he said it. "The northeast corner room. Your grandmother kept it shut for sixty years—I know because she told me so herself, many times, the way people tell you things they want you to repeat. *Never open the nursery, Father. The latch sticks and the paint is lead.* She must have mentioned it a hundred times. Nobody mentions a room a hundred times unless they're afraid you'll go in."

"There's no light in the nursery," Elias said. "There's no electricity run to the nursery. I checked the breaker box my first week. That circuit doesn't exist."

"I know," Aubert said. "That's why I'm here."

He turned his hat slowly on the table, one degree at a time, like a man steering something.

"There was a figure in the window. Standing. Backlit, so I couldn't see a face—but I could see the outline, and the outline was a young woman in a nightgown, and she was doing something with her hands. Moving them. I thought perhaps she was signaling. Distress. So I did what any reasonable man does at two in the morning—I called the sheriff, and we drove out together, and by the time we came up your drive the light was gone and the window was dark and Sheriff Boudreaux told me I'd seen a reflection of Venus off the glass, and wrote it down in his log as a wellness check, all clear." Aubert smiled without any humor in it. "Venus. At the north-northeast horizon. Through cane stubble. In fog."

"But?"

"But I went home, and I didn't sleep, and the next night I watched the window again. And it was there again. Same hour. Same figure. And the third night I brought binoculars, monsieur, because I am an old man and I wanted to be wrong, and I wanted to be right, and both of those things can live in the same chest." He stopped turning the hat. "She wasn't signaling. She was conducting."

"Conducting."

"Her hands moved the way hands move in front of a choir. Up, hold, release. Left, right. And her mouth—" For the first time, the priest's steadiness cracked, just at the edge, a hairline fracture running through the voice. "Her mouth was open, and I could see that she was singing, and I could not hear a note of it across half a mile of open field. And then, monsieur, while I watched—she stopped. She lowered her hands. She turned her head, slow, like a lighthouse beam, until she was facing directly at the rectory. Directly at me. And she raised one finger to her lips."

The kitchen was very quiet. The percolator had finished long ago; the ticking Elias heard now was coming from inside the wall, and it was not mechanical.

"She was shushing me," Aubert said. "An old priest, half a mile away, behind glass. She shushed me like I was a child interrupting mass." He exhaled. "And then the light went out, and I haven't slept properly since, and yesterday I went into the archive and pulled this ledger, because if there is a Delacroix woman standing in a sealed nursery singing to the dark, I need to know how many times this parish has pretended not to see it."

Elias's hands were shaking around his cup. He set it down. "The woman in the window. Was she—" He had to make himself say it. "Did she look like the body you buried?"

"No," Aubert said. "She looked like you."

* * *

Afterward they walked the grounds, because Aubert asked to, and because Elias wanted to watch the priest's face when they passed the wellhouse.

The morning was gray and windless, the cane gone to seed along the property line, and their footsteps sounded wrong on the gravel—too few echoes, as if the ground were absorbing one of every two. Elias had noticed that weeks ago and filed it under *old house sounds*. Watching Aubert notice it too, seeing the priest's stride hitch and resume, made it new again. Made it undeniable. That was the thing about witnesses, Elias was learning. You could live inside a haunting the way you lived inside weather—explaining each drop, each gust, each pressure change, alone. But put another person under the sky with you, and suddenly the rain was rain.

Aubert stopped at the wellhouse door and stood before it longer than he needed to.

"My predecessor, Father Gaspard, served this parish from 1931 to 1968," he said. "He never once set foot past this fence line. I asked him, once, why. He was dying, in the rectory, and I was young, and I thought it was superstition and I teased him about it. And he looked at me—this man who had buried half the parish, who feared nothing, who once waded into floodwater to give last rites to a drowning man—and he said, *'The Delacroix ground keeps its own offices, boy. We are not needed there.'*" Aubert touched the wellhouse doorframe, lightly, two fingers, the way you'd check a wound. "I thought he meant they were Protestant."

"They're not Protestant," Elias said.

"No," Aubert agreed. "I don't believe they're anything anymore. I believe they're *staff*." He turned. "Monsieur Delacroix. Elias. May I call you Elias? — I'm going to say something now, and I need you to hear all of it, because I may not get another chance. Whatever is happening in this house, it has been happening longer than the church has been keeping its records, and the church has been keeping them wrong on purpose, and I helped. Forty years of margins, and I signed every one. I buried a woman under her brother's name two weeks ago because the paperwork said to, because the family said to, because it was easier than the truth, and I am done being easy." His voice dropped. "So here is the truth as I have it. There is a song in this family. There has been for a century. It is sung by the dead to something that is not dead, and it requires a voice from the living, one per generation, and your grandmother cheated the schedule, and the debt has come due with interest, and the interest is *you*."

The words landed one by one, each with its own weight, and Elias realized his mouth had gone dry again and that he was nodding—not in agreement, but in recognition, the way you nod at a diagnosis you'd already suspected.

"How do you know about the song?" he managed.

"Because I've heard it," Aubert said simply. "Twice in thirty-one years. Once at your grandmother's deathbed—she was humming something under the anointing, something with a melody that folded back on itself, and the hospice nurse fainted, and I told everyone it was the heat. And once—" he looked back at the wellhouse, at the bricked-over door, at the seams in the mortar that Elias had never quite been able to convince himself were only settling cracks, "—once from underneath. During the flood of '98. The whole parish was sandbagging, and I came out here to check on the property because your grandmother had evacuated to Baton Rouge, and the water was waist-high in the field, and I heard singing coming up through it. Dozens of voices. Underwater voices. And they were singing *harmony*, Elias. To something that was humming back."

The wind moved through the cane, and for a moment—Elias would swear to this later, would swear it on whatever was left to swear on—the cane moved against the wind, all of it, a quarter mile of stalks bending toward the house.

Aubert saw it too. He didn't comment. He picked up his satchel.

"I'm leaving the ledger with you," he said. "Photocopies exist. Read the margins. And Elias—" He paused at the gate, hat on, an old black silhouette against a gray field, and for a second he looked less like a priest than like a man standing at the edge of a country he'd been mapping his whole life without ever entering. "Last night was the fourth night. The woman in the window. And last night, for the first time, there were two figures."

Elias's heart stopped and restarted wrong. "Two."

"One standing at the glass, conducting. One sitting on the nursery floor, cross-legged, facing the room. Facing *away* from the window." Aubert met his eyes. "Like an audience member. Like someone who had come to listen." He settled the hat lower. "I don't know which one of you is which, son. But somebody in that nursery is learning the song. And whoever taught them is running out of nights to teach it."

He walked to his car, a pale sedan parked crooked in the drive, and drove away without looking back, which Elias understood was the hardest thing the old man had done all morning.

Elias stood in the yard a long time. Then he went inside, past the hallway mirror—which showed him walking past, a half-beat late, and showed him, at the far edge of the glass, in the depth of the reflected corridor, the nursery door standing open, though he had locked it that morning—and climbed the stairs to see for himself.

The nursery was cold. The window was shut. On the floorboards, in the dust that sixty years of sealed rooms lay down like snow, there were two sets of marks.

One was the long drag-trail of a nightgown hem, circling, circling, the path of a conductor turning.

The other was small, and recent, and shaped like knees.
