## 12. The Choir Rehearses

The first voice came out of the wall above the kitchen sink.

Elias was standing at the counter with a glass of water he had no intention of drinking—just something to do with his hands at midnight, because midnight had become the hour the house changed its posture—and the sound started as a hum so low he thought it was the refrigerator. He'd been in the house nine days now. Nine days of lights burning in every room, of counting doors at dusk like his sister used to, of mirrors that moved a half-second behind him like shy animals deciding whether to approach. He had learned the house's small noises the way you learn a roommate's breathing. The refrigerator hummed. The pipes ticked when the temperature dropped. The floorboards over the old cistern groaned once around two in the morning, every night, like something turning over in its sleep.

This was none of those.

It was a woman's voice, and it was singing a note that did not belong to any song Elias knew, and it was coming from inside the plaster.

He set the glass down without drinking and stood very still, the way you stand still when you hear something in the woods and understand that your stillness is the only thing keeping you part of the landscape. The note held. It didn't waver, didn't swell, didn't ask anything of him. It just existed, patient as water damage, somewhere between the kitchen and whatever room had been a kitchen before this one.

Then a second voice joined it.

* * *

He should have gone to bed. He wants to be honest about that, later, when honesty is the only currency he has left—he should have turned off the kitchen light, climbed the stairs, counted the doors on his way up because counting them calmed him even though he no longer believed they were all there, and pulled the covers over his head like a child in a story where children know better than adults what to be afraid of. Instead he stood in the dark kitchen with his hand flat against the wall, feeling for vibration, and listened to his family rehearse.

Because that's what it was. That was the word his mind supplied before any other, before *haunting*, before *menace*, before *run*. Rehearsal. The voices came one at a time, each finding its place in the arrangement like instruments tuning to an invisible oboe—a tenor from the direction of the pantry, a soprano high up near the ceiling joists, a bass so deep it seemed to come up through the foundation rather than the walls. They weren't performing. There was no audience except him, and they weren't singing *at* him. They were working through it. Pausing. Starting again from a phrase that hadn't sat right. A voice would drop out, and another would carry the line alone for a measure, and then the first would slip back in, corrected, settled, sure.

Elias pressed his palm harder against the plaster. It was cool and slightly damp, the way the walls of this house were always slightly damp, and beneath his hand he could feel—not vibration exactly, not the physical tremor of a speaker cone—but a kind of pressure, rhythmic, like a pulse heard through a door.

"Hello?" he said.

The music didn't stop. But it changed. The way a conversation changes when someone new walks into the room—the same words, but angled differently now, aware of him. The soprano lifted a phrase and let it hang, unfinished, the musical equivalent of a glance.

He knew that glance. He'd grown up under it. Every Delacroix gathering of his childhood had contained some aunt or great-uncle who would look at Elias across a crowded living room and hold the look a beat too long, not with hostility but with an attention that felt like being measured for something. A suit. A coffin. A future.

The voices resumed, and now they were definitely singing toward him.

* * *

He followed the sound through the house because he couldn't not. That was the trap of it, and he understood even then that it was a trap, understood it the way you understand a dream is a dream while it's happening: the knowledge changes nothing about your feet. The music moved—or he moved and the music stayed equidistant, which amounted to the same thing—filling whichever room he entered next, always just ahead of him, always in the walls he was walking toward. The hallway. The parlor with the sheet-draped furniture. The room that had been Grand-mère's sitting room, where the wallpaper was a faded pattern of morning glories climbing toward a ceiling they never reached.

In the sitting room, he recognized her.

It happened between one phrase and the next. The alto line rose out of the north wall—clear, unhurried, carrying the melody the way she used to carry casseroles, balanced on one hip like it weighed nothing—and Elias's whole body reacted before his mind caught up. His throat closed. His eyes went hot. Because he knew that voice. He had heard it every Sunday of his childhood, humming over the stove, humming over her sewing, humming the same three bars of something old while her hands worked, and he had assumed, the way children assume their grandparents are permanent fixtures of the world, that the humming had stopped forever the day they put her in the ground.

Grand-mère Odile was singing in the wall, and she sounded exactly the way she had sounded alive, and Elias sat down hard on the arm of a draped chair and wept like a child.

That was the terrible thing. Not the deadness of it. The *hominess*. If a stranger had played him a recording of these voices and asked him to describe them, he would have said: a family. A big one, spread across generations, gathered in a large room, singing something they all learned young. There was warmth in the blend. There was the slight drag of age in the lower voices and the brightness of youth in the upper ones, and underneath everything ran the particular unison that only blood can produce—that way siblings and cousins harmonize without trying, because they learned pitch from the same lungs, the same lullabies, the same Sunday pews. It sounded like Christmas Eve at Tante Rose's. It sounded like funerals, which in his family were also gatherings, also warm, also full of food and argument and old songs sung slightly off-key on purpose because that was how the dead liked them.

They were singing for him to come along. He could feel that in the music the way you feel an empty chair at a table. The arrangement had a gap in it—a space in the middle register, a place where a voice his own would fit—and every time the melody passed through that space, the whole choir leaned into it, gently, expectantly, the way a family leans toward a doorway when they hear car tires in the drive.

"Stop," he said. His voice cracked on it. "Please."

The music softened but did not stop. Grand-mère's alto carried on, patient, and he realized with a fresh wave of grief that she wasn't ignoring him. She was comforting him. This was what she had done his whole life: when he cried, she didn't scold or fuss, she simply kept doing whatever she was doing—stirring, sewing, humming—so that he could cry beside her without being watched, until the crying burned itself out against her calm. She was doing it now. Singing steadily in the plaster so that her grandson could sit in a haunted room and fall apart without having to perform his fear for anyone.

"I miss you," he whispered.

The alto dipped, acknowledged, held.

And that was the moment he understood how dangerous this all was—not intellectually, not as a theory about ghosts and bargains, but in his body, in the soft animal part of him that doesn't care about danger if the danger is warm. He wanted to go toward the wall. He wanted to put his ear against the morning glories and listen until the song carried him somewhere. He was twenty-nine years old, grieving, alone in a decaying house in a parish where everyone who remembered his family was either dead or lying to him, and here was the sound of home pouring out of the walls like heat from an oven, and every instinct he had said *go to it, go to it, you're cold and it's warm.*

He made himself stand up. He made himself walk backward out of the room, facing the wall the whole way, because turning his back on it felt like abandoning her, and leaving felt like abandoning her too, and both things were true and neither mattered.

At the doorway, the choir reached the end of the piece. All the voices arrived together at a final chord—major, resolved, welcoming—and held it, and held it, and in the held chord Elias heard with perfect clarity the shape of the invitation: *this is where your part comes in. This is the rest we saved for you. Whenever you're ready.*

The chord ended. The house was silent. The silence had the texture of a held breath.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat on his bed with every light in the room burning—all of them, the overhead fixture, both lamps, the flashlight from the kitchen drawer propped against the headboard—and he wrote in the notebook he'd started keeping since the mirrors began to lag. Facts first. He'd learned that much: when the world goes strange, write down the facts first, because facts are a rope you can follow back.

*Midnight. Voices in the walls. At least six, maybe more. Men and women, different ages. One is Grand-mère. I'm sure. They're rehearsing something—they stopped and restarted twice. There's a part missing in the middle. My range.*

He looked at that last sentence for a long time. Then he wrote:

*I don't know how I know my range fits. I just do.*

Under the lamp light the notebook page looked ordinary, and that ordinariness steadied him. He flipped back through earlier entries—the mirror delays, timed with his phone stopwatch: four-tenths of a second in the hall mirror, growing; Elodie's mouth moving in the glass, three nights running now, forming words he couldn't parse; the hymnal in the attic with its torn last page and its margins full of names, every Delacroix for a century, most of them annotated with dates of death that belonged to somebody else. He'd thought the names were a record. Standing in the sitting room with his grandmother's voice still ringing in his molars, he understood they might be a roster instead.

A choir needs singers. Someone had been filling the seats for a hundred years.

The clock on his phone read 3:17 when the humming started again—quieter now, farther away, back near the sitting room, the way musicians drift back to the practice room after the break. And threaded through the humming, so faint he had to hold his breath to catch it, came something new. Not singing. Speaking. Grand-mère's voice, or the shape of it, murmuring in the wall the way she used to murmur over her cards on the porch, half to herself, half to whoever was meant to overhear.

He couldn't make out the words. But he caught the rhythm of them, the rise and fall, and twice he caught his own name folded into the cadence like a coin in bread dough.

*Elias.* Softly. Between phrases. *Elias.*

Not a summons. Worse. A check-in. The way she used to call his name up the stairs at bedtime—not *come here*, just his name, just to confirm he was where he was supposed to be, safe, accounted for, hers.

He turned off the lamps one by one and lay down in the dark and stared at the ceiling, and the house hummed around him like a hive settling, and the worst part—the part he would not write in the notebook, the part he would carry privately like a stone in his shoe—was that for one long minute, drifting at the edge of sleep, wrapped in the sound of his family singing softly in the walls of the only home he had left, he felt completely, catastrophically safe.

He woke at dawn to sunlight and silence and the smell of coffee he had not made.

He lay still and listened. Nothing. Just the ordinary sounds of an old house cooling into morning. He got up, counted the doors on his floor out of habit—seven, correct, correct—and went downstairs to make real coffee, and it was only when he passed the hallway mirror that he stopped.

His reflection was already looking at him.

Not delayed. Not lagging. Waiting—facing him squarely, eyes level, before he had fully turned toward the glass. And as he watched, frozen with his hand on the banister, his reflection raised one finger to its lips.

Behind it, deep in the mirror's gray distance, Elodie's shape flickered once—like a signal fire glimpsed between hills—and was gone.
