## 15. The Torn Page

The hymnal sat on the kitchen table where I'd left it, open to the last intact page, and I stared at the ragged stub of paper in the gutter of the binding like a man staring at a stump where his hand used to be.

Three verses. That was what Elodie had told me—three verses bind it—and the hymnal held two. The third had been torn out so long ago that the paper's edge had gone soft and brown as an old scar. Whoever took it hadn't ripped it in haste. They'd cut it free with a blade, close to the spine, careful as surgery.

I turned back through the book. The margins were still crowded with names, generations of Delacroixes written in a dozen different hands, most of them paired with dates that belonged to someone else's death. My own name was there now. I hadn't written it. The ink was fresh enough to smudge if I licked my thumb, and I very much did not want to lick my thumb.

*Find the page,* Elodie had signed against the glass last night, her hands moving fast, urgent, before the mirror fogged from the wrong side and she had to go. *Grand-mère kept everything. She never threw anything away. She was too afraid of what would happen if she lost count.*

So: find the page.

The house, naturally, had other plans.

* * *

I started in the attic, because that was where the hymnal had lived, wedged in a cedar chest beneath a christening gown gone yellow as old teeth. The attic at noon should have been an oven. It was cold. Not drafty-cold—the vents were sealed, the windows painted shut—but cold the way a cellar is cold, cold with intention, as though the house were holding its breath up there and the breath had temperature.

The cedar chest was empty except for the hymnal's outline pressed into dust.

I stood there a long moment, listening to the rafters tick. Then I went downstairs to check the parlor, because Grand-mère had kept her important papers in the secretary desk there—deeds, insurance papers, the funeral arrangements for my mother that she'd made twenty years in advance because Delacroix women didn't leave anything to chance or to their children.

The parlor door opened onto the nursery.

Not the real nursery—that had been on the second floor, east side, and I'd counted it every dusk since I came home. This one was smaller, wronger, its window looking out not on the live oaks but on a gray expanse that might have been fog or might have been nothing at all. A crib stood in the corner. Mobiles hung above it, little wooden birds, turning slowly without any breeze.

On the floor, arranged with terrible neatness, lay a scatter of sheet music. Dozens of pages. Hymnals, folk songs, a Chopin nocturne, all of them spread face-down like a dealt hand.

My heart did something complicated. I knelt and began turning them over, one by one, checking the page numbers, the keys, the watermarks. None of them matched the hymnal. None of them were even from the same printer. But under each one, in pencil, faint as breath, someone had written a single word:

*No.*

Every page. *No. No. No.* Forty sheets of music, forty refusals, in handwriting I recognized because I'd spent the last week learning it out of letters and ledgers and the flyleaf of my mother's Bible.

Grand-mère had collected music the way other people collect evidence. And she had condemned all of it.

Except, apparently, one page.

* * *

The house shifted while I slept—or while I lay in bed not sleeping, which amounted to the same thing in this place. I knew because I'd taken to leaving a thread tied to the doorknob and anchored to the bedpost, and by morning the thread ran taut across the room to a door that hadn't existed when I closed my eyes. Not my bedroom door. A new one. Narrow, unpainted, breathing a smell of wet stone.

I didn't open it. Elodie had taught me that much: *never take a door the house gives you for free.*

Instead I made a map. On the back of a piece of wrapping paper I drew the house as I'd first known it, room by room, and then I drew it again as it seemed to be now, and I laid one over the other against the window light. The differences were small but everywhere, like a face seen through warped glass. The pantry had migrated six feet west. The hall closet had become a staircase going down into blackness. The bathroom mirror—I checked—was still lagging, three seconds late now, showing me standing when I was already sitting down, a ghost of myself haunting me politely.

And in the overlap, in the one part of the house that appeared identical on both maps, sat Grand-mère's room.

She had died in it eleven years ago, and no one had touched it since. It was the only room in the house that never moved. I understood why now. You don't rearrange the altar.

* * *

Her room smelled of lavender gone to dust and something underneath it, older, mineral, like the air off a deep cave. The bed was made. The vanity sat against the wall with its triple mirrors—one flat, one convex, one tilted—and I kept my eyes off them as I worked, because I wasn't ready yet to see who else might be standing behind me.

A rosary on the nightstand. A Bible with the Song of Solomon marked in ribbon, which struck me, absurdly, as the funniest thing I'd encountered all week. A photograph of the family, 1962, everyone squinting into the sun, Grand-mère a girl of nineteen already wearing the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

And above the vanity, screwed directly into the plaster, hung a frame.

It was ornate, gilded, the kind of frame meant for a wedding portrait or an oil painting of some ancestor's land holdings. But it held no picture. It held a single sheet of paper, yellowed, trimmed close along one edge where it had been cut from a binding, and covered from margin to margin in musical notation.

I stopped breathing.

There it was. The third verse. Framed like a saint's relic above the mirror where she must have sat every morning of her last thirty years, putting on her face beneath the song she'd stolen, guarding it the way you guard a thing you can't destroy and can't use.

I got the frame down. The screws fought me; the plaster crumbled around them like stale bread. When the frame came free, the wall behind it bore a perfect rectangle of unfaded paint, and inside that rectangle, scratched into the plaster itself in a hand shaking with age or fury, were words:

*I AM SORRY. IT WAS THE ONLY WAY TO KEEP HER.*

I read it four times. *Keep her.* Keep who? Her daughter? Her granddaughter? Some twin whose name never made it onto any stone?

The paper trembled in my hands. I carried it to the window, to the honest gray daylight, and I looked at the notation properly for the first time.

And my stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the foundation, through whatever the house was floating on, all the way down to the thing that listened.

* * *

Here is what I know about reading music: not much. Elodie was the musician in our family. She had the piano and the choir loft and the ear that could catch a wrong note in another room. I had the rhythm section of my own heartbeat and a childhood of sitting under the piano while she practiced, absorbing the shapes of songs without ever learning their names.

But I knew enough. I knew a treble clef from a bass. I knew that a key signature sits at the start of a line and tells you which notes are bent. I knew that music, whatever else it is, is consistent—a C is a C is a C, or none of it means anything.

This page was not consistent.

The key signature changed twice in eight measures, sliding from something in flats to something in sharps and back again, which no hymn does, which almost nothing does. There were accidentals stacked three deep in front of notes that shouldn't have been able to hold them. One measure contained seventeen beats. Another contained none at all—just a rest stretching across the bar lines like a held breath, and above it, in faded pencil, a single instruction in French:

*Ne chante pas ceci tel qu'il est écrit.*
Do not sing this as it is written.

At first I thought: she couldn't write it down correctly. Maybe the true verse couldn't survive transcription, the way a dream dies in the telling. Maybe the corruption was the price of paper.

Then I looked closer, and I saw the truth, and the truth was worse.

The errors weren't random. Random errors look like noise—smudges, hesitations, a note wobbling where the hand slipped. These errors were architectural. Every mistake pointed the same direction. Each altered interval dragged the melody downward, half-step by half-step, measure by measure, so that anyone sight-reading the page honestly would arrive at the end having sung something subtly, catastrophically wrong—not the verse that binds, but its inversion. Its opposite. A verse that wouldn't close a door but open one, wide, and hold it open, and invite.

She hadn't failed to write it correctly. She had chosen not to. She had spent hours—days—with the true verse in front of her, and she had bent every line of it, deliberately, patiently, the way you bend a nail so it can't be driven, and then she had framed her forgery above her own mirror where she'd have to look at it every morning for the rest of her life.

A confession she displayed instead of hiding. A trap she left loaded in plain sight.

Because she knew. She knew that someday a Delacroix would come hunting for this page, desperate, running out of nights, and they would find it exactly where she wanted them to find it, and they would sing it exactly as written, and—

And what? Wake it? Feed it? Open the well? Or worse—sing it *with the choir*, join the harmony, give consent in the one language the dead could accept?

I thought of the names in the hymnal's margins. All those twins with borrowed death-dates. I thought of Grand-mère's letter, the one I'd found folded inside the parish records: *One voice per generation, freely given, keeps the rest of us.* Freely given. The whole bargain balanced on that word, *freely*, and here was proof that she had known it—known it well enough to rig the only written copy of the verse so that no one could ever give it freely, because no one could ever give it *accurately*. If the verse could only be sung right by someone who truly knew it, learned it note by note from a living teacher—

From a twin.

From Elodie.

The page shook in my hands. She hadn't protected the family from the verse. She'd protected the verse from the family, and left the only true copy living in the one place she trusted: her granddaughter's memory. And then she'd died, and the choir had come for Elodie anyway, ahead of schedule, out of turn, hungry—and Elodie had died carrying the verse down with her, unsung, untaught, the last safe vessel of it buried under the wrong name.

Until she came back through the glass and started teaching me.

* * *

I sat on the floor of Grand-mère's room until the light went long and amber, the forged page spread across my knees, and I felt the betrayal move through me in stages, like weather.

First: anger at her. Cold, complete, the anger of a man reading his own name in a ledger of livestock. She had known what the family was for. She had known and stayed and sung and framed her cowardice in gilt.

Then, slowly, the anger turned over, the way a card turns, and showed its other face.

Because if she'd wanted the cycle to continue, she'd have left the page clean. A correct verse, framed and waiting, would have made everything easy—every generation could have learned it from paper, sung it, paid the debt, kept the dark asleep, kept the house standing. Instead she'd sabotaged the only shortcut. She'd made sure the verse could pass only mouth to mouth, twin to twin, love to love. She'd built a lock that required exactly one key: someone willing to be taught by someone willing to teach, both of them knowing the cost.

She hadn't preserved the bargain. She'd planted the loophole. She just hadn't been able to spring it herself—because springing it meant the song ended, and if the song ended, the dark woke, and someone had to stand in the doorway and sing it alone, once, finally, as an ending and not a renewal. And maybe she hadn't found the courage. Maybe she'd searched her whole life for the person who had it and died looking.

Or maybe—this thought arrived last, quiet as frost—maybe she'd left the corrupted page as a test, and the scratch marks on the plaster as a warning, and the whole arrangement as a message across sixty years addressed to whoever came next:

*Don't trust what's written. Don't trust what's offered. Learn it from someone who loves you, and give it away, and don't let them keep you.*

The house groaned around me, floorboards flexing like ribs.

In the vanity's flat mirror, my reflection raised its head a full second after I did. Behind it, far down the corridor of glass, something pale flickered and was gone.

I folded the forged page into my jacket, over my heart, where I intended to keep it—not as scripture but as evidence. Somewhere in this house or in this parish was the truth of what Grand-mère had done to get that page, and why the scratch on the wall said *keep her*, and why Father Aubert, who had buried a body under my name, went white as candle wax whenever I mentioned the year 1954.

The hallway lights flickered on, one after another, leading toward the stairs.

The house wanted me to follow. For the first time since I'd come home, I wondered whether that was a threat—or an invitation to the one conversation it had been waiting a century to have.

Downstairs, in the parlor, the telephone began to ring.

I hadn't known it was connected. I hadn't known it worked. It rang on and on, patient as a psalm, and through the receiver, when I finally picked it up, came a voice I knew from Sunday mornings of my childhood, dry and old and terrified:

"Elias," said Father Aubert. "Before you touch that page again—you need to know what your grandmother asked me to do."
