## 20. The Test of the Blink

Elias did not sleep that night. He sat on the floor of the kitchen with his back against the cabinets and a pot of coffee going cold beside him, and he made a list.

He wrote it in pencil on the back of an electric bill, because pencil could be erased and he was afraid of what he might need to unwrite. The list had one heading: *Things only Elodie knows.* Under it, the entries came slowly at first, then in a flood, the way water finds a crack.

The name of their imaginary dog. The rules of the game they invented in the back seat of their mother's car. The word they used for the space between the fence slats where the neighbor's cat watched them. The wrong lyrics to "You Are My Sunshine," which Elodie had learned wrong from their mother, who had learned them wrong from her own mother, so that the Delacroix family had been singing a corrupted version for three generations without knowing or caring.

He looked at the list and felt sick, because every item on it was also something the mirrors had heard. The house had ears. The house had been listening to him talk to Elodie's reflection for two weeks, and everything he knew, it knew too.

Almost everything.

There were three things on the list the house had never heard. Three things that existed nowhere but inside the two of them, sealed up like letters never mailed. He read them again and again until he had them by heart, and then he burned the list over the sink with a match, watching the pencil curl and blacken, and went upstairs to the mirror.

* * *

She was already there when he entered the hallway. That was the first thing that frightened him — she was always there now, waiting, as if she had nothing else to do, as if she had all the time in the world, which of course she didn't, because time was exactly the thing she'd run out of.

"Elias." Her mouth shaped it through the glass. He'd gotten good at reading her; two weeks of nightly lessons had built a vocabulary of nearly forty words, a small private language of shapes and repetitions. *Elias. Listen. Careful. Behind you. Not me.*

"Not yet," he said aloud, and her face changed — confusion first, then hurt, then something smooth sliding over the hurt like ice forming on a pond. Too fast. Real Elodie's feelings arrived in tangles, overlapping, contradictory, the way living feelings do. This grief came pre-organized.

"I have to ask you some things," he said. "And I need you to answer them right. And if you can't—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "If you can't, I'm going to cover this mirror with black cloth and I'm not coming back."

The thing wearing his sister tilted its head. It was a gesture Elodie had never made in her life; she'd always turned her whole body toward things that puzzled her, shoulders and all, like a bird. This creature just tilted its head, economical, curious, a scientist observing.

Then, slowly, it smiled. And the smile was perfect. That was the worst part. Every other detail might waver — a blink held a half-second too long, a breath that didn't fog the glass even when the hallway was cold enough to see your own — but the smile was flawless, because the smile was the bait.

It mouthed: *Ask.*

* * *

"First one," Elias said. His voice sounded strange in the empty hall, too loud, like shouting in church. "When we were eight. The dog."

The reflection's eyes brightened with recognition. It began to answer immediately, hands moving, mouth shaping words with fluent speed — *Biscuit, the brown one, we buried him under the oak after the truck—*

"No." Elias's heart was hammering but he kept his voice flat, cold, a customs officer's voice. "Wrong question. Not Biscuit. Biscuit was the neighbor's dog. I asked about ours."

The smile flickered. Just for a moment — a skipped frame, a needle jumping the groove. Then it recovered and tried again, slower now, more careful: *We didn't have a dog.*

"We did," Elias said. "For one summer. You named her. Tell me the name and tell me why we gave her away."

The thing in the glass stared at him. And in the silence he watched it work — he could almost see the machinery turning behind those borrowed eyes, searching, riffling through everything the house had ever heard him say, everything Grand-mère's hymnal might have recorded, every scrap and shadow of the Delacroix archive. It found nothing. There was nothing to find. He had never spoken that summer aloud in this house, not once, not to anyone, because it wasn't a story you told, it was a wound you kept.

The reflection's mouth opened. Closed. Opened again, and what came out was a deflection, smooth as oil: *Why are you doing this? I'm scared, Elias. Something is behind you. Turn around. Turn around—*

"You've said that eleven times," he said quietly. "In two weeks. Eleven times. And there has never once been anything behind me. You know how I know? Because the real Elodie would have gotten bored of that trick by the fourth try. She'd have found a new way to say it. She couldn't repeat herself even when it mattered. Drove Mom crazy."

He stepped closer to the glass. Close enough to see that the pupils were slightly wrong — not the color, the depth. Like painted eyes on a saint's statue, flat, catching light without holding any.

"Second test," he said. "Sing 'You Are My Sunshine.'"

The thing hesitated. Then it sang — and God help him, the voice was right, it was Elodie's voice, thin and sweet and slightly nasal on the high notes, and if he hadn't known better he'd have wept. But it sang the right words. *Please don't take my sunshine away,* clean and correct, the way the radio sang it.

"Wrong," Elias whispered. "That's the wrong words. We don't know the right words. Nobody in our family knows the right words. Mom sang it wrong, and Grand-mère sang it wrong before her, and when I was nine you told me the real lyrics were about a man who died in prison, and I believed you for six years."

The smile was gone now. What looked back at him out of the mirror was patient, and vast, and very old, and for a long moment it simply studied him the way the dark beneath the well studied the family above it — with the calm of something that had waited out generations and expected to wait out him.

Then it spoke, and it didn't bother mouthing anymore. The voice came from everywhere, from the walls, from the floorboards, from the drain in the bathroom two rooms away, a chorus speaking in unison through one borrowed face:

*You are clever, little verse. Cleverer than the last ones. But cleverness only delays. The song needs finishing. The well needs quieting. Ask your questions. Waste your nights. We have sung longer than your language has existed, and we will sing after it rots.*

The mirror went dark — not shattered, not empty, just dark, like a window into a room with no lamps. Elias stood alone in the hallway with his heart slamming and his hands shaking, and he realized he was grinning.

Because it had talked. Because it had lost patience. And things that lose patience are things that can be hurried into mistakes.

And because, underneath the dark, faint as a pulse, he could hear humming. A third voice, small and ragged, fighting up through the choir's harmony like a swimmer coming up through deep water.

Elodie. Still in there. Still hers.

* * *

He spent the next day preparing the third test, and it was the one that mattered, because it wasn't a fact. Facts could be stolen, guessed, excavated from the house's memory. The third test was a feeling.

Their mother had died on a Tuesday in October, four years ago, and at the funeral Elias had done something shameful. He had laughed. Midway through Father Aubert's eulogy, at the exact wrong sentence, a laugh had climbed out of him like a trapped bird, and he'd had to press his fist against his mouth while half the parish stared, and Elodie — Elodie had reached over and taken his hand and squeezed it twice, and later, driving home, she'd said the sentence that had lived in him ever since:

*"Grief is just love with nowhere to put itself. Sometimes it comes out sideways. Don't apologize for where it goes."*

He had never repeated that sentence to anyone. He had barely let himself think it directly, because thinking it hurt, and he'd spent four years walking around the memory the way you walk around a bruise. It existed nowhere but in the sealed vault where the two of them kept the things that mattered too much to spend.

That night he stood before the darkened mirror and knocked on the glass, three times, slow — the knock they'd used as children to mean *it's me, open up.*

Nothing.

He waited. He knocked again. He said, to the darkness, "I laughed at Mom's funeral. At Aubert's eulogy. Tell me what she said to me after. Not the words — anyone could guess words. Tell me what it *felt* like. Tell me what you did with your hand."

The dark stayed dark for so long he began to count his own heartbeats, and somewhere past a hundred he turned to go.

Then the surface shivered. Light returned to the mirror like blood returning to a numb limb, painful, patchy — and there she was, and she was *wrong*, diminished, flickering like a candle in wind, her edges fraying into static. Whatever the choir had done to lock her away, it had cost her. Or she had fought her way back, and fighting cost more.

Her mouth moved. Weakly. He leaned close, closer than he'd ever stood to the glass, close enough that his breath fogged it and hers didn't.

*Squeeze,* she signed. *Twice. Your hand. And I said—*

She stopped. Her face crumpled, and for the first time since the funeral, since the casket, since any of it, Elias saw his sister actually cry — not the smooth performed grief of the impostor but the ugly, gasping, real thing, tears that didn't fall because there was no gravity where she was, just welling and trembling in eyes that finally, finally had depth.

*Grief is love with nowhere to put itself,* she signed, letter by letter, each shape costing her visibly, her fingers shaking. *Don't apologize for where it goes. And Elias — I wasn't comforting you. I was holding on to you so I wouldn't laugh too. We were laughing at the same sentence. At the same time. You never knew that. You never knew I was fighting it too.*

Elias pressed his forehead against the glass. It was cold as well-water.

"It's you," he said. "Oh God, Elodie, it's really you."

*Took you long enough,* she signed, and even now, even frayed to a thread, even drowning, there it was — the smirk, the old smirk, one corner of her mouth crooked up, and Elias laughed and sobbed at once, a sound with nowhere to put itself.

* * *

They talked until the sky behind him grayed toward dawn, in their patched-together language of signs and mouthed words, and what she told him braided relief and grief so tightly together that he stopped being able to tell them apart.

*It wears me,* she signed. *When it needs to. It reaches down into wherever I am and pulls me up like a glove and puts itself inside. Everything you talked to for two weeks — most of it was me, really me, but steered. Held. It let me want to save you, because my wanting is the hook. You'd never follow a stranger into the glass. You'd follow me anywhere. So it makes sure you're always talking to me.*

"How do I know this is different?" Elias asked. "How do I know you're not wearing yourself right now?"

Her answer was slow, and terrible, and true.

*You don't. That's the design. That's why it let you build your little tests — it wants you fluent in doubt. Every hour you spend testing me is an hour you don't spend learning the verse. It doesn't need to fool you forever. It only needs to keep you suspicious until the night of the song, and then you'll stand at the mirror with the last verse in your mouth and no way to know if the voice telling you the final notes is your sister or the thing that ate her.*

Elias felt the floor tilt under him — not literally, though lately he could no longer be sure. "Then teach me now. Tonight. While it's you. Give me the verse and I'll learn it and it won't matter who's in the glass afterward."

Her hand came up against the inside of the mirror, palm out, and he matched it, palm to palm through the cold, and her fingers were translucent at the edges, worn thin as communion paper.

*I can't give it to you all at once,* she signed. *Not because I'm cruel. Because it listens through me. Every note I show you, it hears. If I hand you the whole verse, it will corrupt your memory of it before you can use it — it'll rewrite what you learned the way it rewrote Grand-mère's page. You have to learn it the only way it can't touch. In pieces. Out of order. Carried in parts of you it doesn't know how to read.*

"What parts?"

She smiled, that crooked smirk, and signed the answer, and the answer was the tenderest and most frightening thing she'd ever said to him:

*The parts that are yours. The places where you're not a Delacroix at all. The summer dog. The funeral laugh. The wrong words to the sunshine song. It owns our family, Elias — a century of names in a hymnal, a century of voices in the plaster. But it doesn't own the things we made together that were never part of the bargain. So we'll hide the verse inside them. Note by note. Sewn into the songs and stories it never heard.*

She pressed her palm harder against the glass, and where her hand touched, for just a moment, the surface warmed under his.

*But we have to hurry. It knows I'm loose. It knows you passed the tests. And Elias — it doesn't get angry. Anger is a living thing's luxury. It does something worse.*

"What's worse than angry?"

Her eyes went past him, to the dark end of the hallway, and whatever she saw there drained the smirk off her face entirely.

*It gets efficient,* she signed. *Turn around slowly.*

He turned.

At the end of the hall, where the wall had always been, there was a door. Old wood, iron hinges, standing closed and patient in the pre-dawn gray. He had counted the doors of this hallway every dusk for two weeks. He knew their number the way he knew his own heartbeat.

This door was new.

And from beneath it, faint and unhurried, rising through the floorboards like water through a foundation, he could hear singing — the first two verses, full strength, a hundred Delacroix voices in perfect harmony.

Rehearsing. As if the third verse were already a foregone conclusion.

Behind him, Elodie's palm struck the glass, once, hard — their old signal, the one that meant *run*.
