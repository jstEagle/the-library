## 24. What the Choir Wants

I have been dead for eleven days and I am still learning the shape of it.

People imagine death as a door closing. It isn't. It's a room you were always in, finally lit. I can see the whole house now—not with eyes, exactly, but the way you know a song is playing in another room, by the way the walls hold it. The house holds me the way the walls hold music. I am everywhere in it and nowhere at all, and every mirror is a window I can press my face against, and Elias cannot hear me, and the choir will not stop singing.

That last part is what I need to tell him. That last part is why I fight through the glass every night until my—until whatever I have instead of hands—aches.

Because I was wrong about them. I was wrong all my life, and Grand-mère was wrong before me, and her mother before her, back and back like a rope of women all holding the same end of the same lie. We thought we were the flock. We thought the choir was the shepherd.

We are not the flock.

We are the feed.

* * *

Let me start where I started, which was the hymnal, three weeks before I died.

I had gone up to the attic to look for the christening gowns—Elias and mine, twin white things our mother kept in a cedar chest—and instead I found Grand-mère's hymnal open on the worktable, as if she'd only just stepped away, though she'd been thirty years in the ground. The attic was cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather. Cold like a held breath.

I'd seen the book before, of course. Every Delacroix child has seen it, brown leather gone soft as ear, the gold cross worn to a smudge. What I had never done was read the margins.

The margins were full of names.

Not just Delacroixes—the whole tree, branches and roots, going back past Grand-mère to a woman named Séraphine who signed herself with an X because she couldn't write. And beside nearly every name, a date. I ran my finger down the column doing the arithmetic my whole education had trained me to do, and that was when the floor of my understanding gave way, quiet as ice.

The dates of death were wrong.

Not wrong the way records go wrong—off by a year, a transposed digit. Wrong the way a lie is wrong. Tante Odile, who died at ninety-one in Baton Rouge, entered here at forty-four. Grand-père Armand, who dropped dead of his heart at sixty-two on the steps of St. Brigid's, written down as twenty-nine. My own mother's name, and beside it a date eleven years before she actually died—eleven years before I was born.

Beside each name, too, a small mark. A check, most of them. But some names had two checks, and a few had none, and those few were always twins. Always one of a pair, never both. Séraphine's entry said *l'une* in a cramped old hand. One of them.

I stood in that cold attic holding a hundred and forty years of my family's deaths in my hands, and upstairs the radio was playing, and somewhere below me Elias was laughing at something on his phone, ordinary as rain, and I understood—I want to be honest, I understood immediately, the way you understand a snake is a snake—that we had been counted. Not loved. Counted. Tallied. Kept the way a farmer keeps a ledger of heads.

I should have run then. I want that on the record, wherever records go when the person keeping them is dead. I should have taken Elias by the wrist and driven until the gas light came on and kept driving. But I did what Delacroixes do. I got curious. Curiosity is our inheritance too, and it has killed us one per generation since before anyone alive could name the reason.

* * *

Here is what I pieced together in those three weeks, from the hymnal and the parish records Father Aubert let me copy and the letters sewn into the lining of Grand-mère's hope chest like contraband:

There is something under the well. There always has been; the well came after, the way a fence comes after the thing you're fencing. Before there was a parish, before there was a Delacroix, there was a dark under this land that slept, mostly, the way deep water sleeps, and the first of us settled on top of it because land is cheap when everyone else's animals won't drink the water.

Séraphine found out what lived below. I don't know how—a letter fragment says *it answered when no one else would*, which tells me more than I wish it did. She made a bargain, or it made one and she agreed to the terms, which may be the same thing. The dark would sleep. In exchange, it would be sung to. Not worshipped—fed. Song was its food, or its sedative, or its leash; the letters disagree, and I think the truth is all three. One voice, given regularly, would keep it dreaming. And the voice had to come from the blood. From us.

So the choir began. The dead of our family, singing in the walls, generation after generation—one new voice added each time, one voice paid out like coin. And the living Delacroixes, told they were protected, told the singing kept the dark away, kept their lights on and counted their doors and raised their children in a house that was not a fortress.

It was a pen.

* * *

I need Elias to understand this and he won't, not from me, not all at once, because I didn't understand it either until I was on the inside looking at the machinery. So I'm writing it here, in the only place I can still write—in the mirror-language we invented when we were nine, pressed face to face with the bathroom glass, mouthing words across the border of ourselves. He'll learn it again. He learned everything fast, my brother, except the things that mattered most.

Here is the arithmetic of the farm, laid out plain:

A century and a half of Delacroixes. Say four generations a century, say one voice per generation, sometimes two when the dark was restless—there are years in the ledger with two dates close together, and now I know what those clusters mean. They mean it woke hungry. They mean someone's grandmother sang twice.

And what did the family get? Nothing. That's the revelation that sits in my chest like a stone I swallowed. Not protection. The dark was never held back *from* us—it was held *by* us, and the thing it was being held away from, the thing the choir actually guards against, is its own waking. We were never the beneficiaries of the bargain. We were the payment schedule. Every lullaby our mother sang us was a fattening. Every ritual—the lights, the doors, the counting—was a husbandry practice, keeping the stock calm and home and easy to find when the collection came due.

Grand-mère knew. I found her last letter, the one she wrote the year before she died, and her hand shook so badly the ink had feathered. She wrote: *I believed I was the gatekeeper. I was the gate. Forgive me. I fed your mother's name to it and called it duty.* She tried, near the end, to substitute a name in the parish rolls—to cheat the count, buy one generation free. Aubert wouldn't help her. She did it anyway. And the choir took the wrong twin anyway, because the dark doesn't read ledgers. It reads blood. It hears the note it wants and takes the throat that carries it.

She died thinking she'd failed. She hadn't failed. She'd succeeded at exactly what the system was built for: producing one more voice, on time, and dying before she had to watch it happen.

* * *

Last night I got through to him.

It cost me more than I can describe—reaching through the glass is like pushing your arms through wet clay that pushes back, and every second I spend visible, the choir spends trying to pull me back into the chorus. They don't like it when one of us talks to the living. I used to think that was because it broke some sacred rule. Now I know better. It breaks the feeding schedule. A spooked animal runs.

He stood in the hallway at three in the morning in his boxers with his hair smashed flat on one side, and he watched my mouth move in the mirror, and I watched him not-understand, and I wanted to scream loud enough to crack the glass, and I did try, and the sound went nowhere, the way sound goes when you're a memory the house is having.

But he stayed. That's the thing about Elias. He stayed. He stood there while I mouthed the same three signs over and over—*not safe, listen, count*—and by the fourth night he was answering. Slowly. Badly. With his whole stupid stubborn heart.

So now I teach him. Between the choir's rehearsals, when the walls are quiet, I press myself flat against the silvered side of the world and I give him the truth one sign at a time, because the big truths can't be rushed through a mirror any more than grief can be rushed through a body.

Tonight I gave him the word for *livestock*. We'd never needed that sign as children. I had to build it from pieces: the sign we made for the neighbor's cows, plus the sign for *us*, plus the sign for *always*, which was the first sign our mother ever taught us, the one that meant *forever and ever amen.*

He went gray. He sat down on the hallway floor with his back against the wall beneath the mirror, and I watched him put it together, watched him arrive where I arrived in that cold attic, and I would have given anything—anything left of me—to take it back out of him. To let him keep believing the family story, the warm one, the one where we were guarded and beloved and the singing above us was our ancestors keeping watch.

But he has to know, because knowing is the only weapon we have left. You can't refuse a bargain you don't know you're making. And the choir is moving toward its collection. I can feel it in the walls the way you feel weather in an old scar. The rehearsals are getting longer. The harmonies are tightening around a hole in the music shaped exactly like a third verse, and the hole is shaped like him.

They think they've already won. They think the boy in the house is the promised voice, and in a way they're right—he is promised, he's been promised since before either of us drew breath, written in a margin in shaking ink. What they haven't accounted for is that promises require consent to become debts, and consent requires understanding, and I am going to spend what's left of my existence making sure he understands every clause.

Let them sing. Let them fill the plaster with a century and a half of harmony. My brother has one thing none of them ever had, standing where they can't reach it:

Someone on the other side of the glass, telling him the truth.

* * *

Dawn is coming, or the house's version of it, gray seeping through boards that remember windows. Below me I hear Elias moving through his rituals—lights on, doors counted, his lips shaping the numbers the way I taught him, the way our mother taught me. He thinks he's keeping the wards. I let him think it. A man walking a tightrope shouldn't be told the rope is fraying; he should just be taught, quickly, quietly, how to fly.

But there's something else in the sound of him this morning. Something underneath the counting. His footsteps pause outside the nursery—the room the house grew overnight, the room that wasn't there when we were children—and I feel him listening to it the way I once listened to the attic. Curious. Our whole fatal inheritance, gathering in him like weather.

And far below the floors, so far down it isn't sound anymore but pressure, the dark turns over in its sleep and hums along with the choir, patient as interest accruing, and I understand suddenly, completely, with the terrible clarity of the dead:

It isn't waiting for the verse.

It's waiting for him to get tired enough to dream.
