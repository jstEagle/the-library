## 25. Elias Stops Sleeping

The third night without sleep, Elias learned that his mother's voice had a specific weight to it.

Not the way she'd sounded at the end, when the cancer had thinned her out and every word cost her something. Not even the way she'd sounded in the good years, calling them in from the yard at dusk, *Elias, Elodie, lights on, doors counted.* It was the voice from before all of that—the voice that lived underneath memory, the one you heard when you were small enough to be carried. A voice with no words in it yet. Just warmth, shaped like a name.

*Elias.*

It came at 3:14 in the morning, from inside the wall beside his bed, and it was so precisely her that his body answered before his mind could stop it. His eyes were closing. His head was tipping toward the pillow he had propped upright against the headboard like a barricade. And somewhere below thought, in the animal part of him that had spent nine months listening to that voice through a wall of flesh, he began to lean toward the sound—

—and caught himself with his own hand, fingers digging into the mattress, nails through the sheet.

"No," he said aloud. His voice came out sanded down, wrong. "You don't get to use her."

The wall said nothing. The house said nothing. But he felt it the way you feel someone standing behind a door—not hearing them, just knowing the wood was warmer where they breathed.

He got up. He turned on every light on the second floor. He counted the doors: eleven. He wrote it down in the notebook: *Day 6. Doors: 11. Lights: 23. Mother's voice: 3:14 AM, east wall, bedroom.*

Then he went downstairs and made coffee he wouldn't drink, because the ritual of making it was the only thing keeping his hands busy enough not to shake.

* * *

Here is what six days of sleeplessness teaches you about your own house:

It has a rhythm. Everything does—tides, hearts, rot—but a house's rhythm is slow, geological, and you never hear it because you're asleep for most of its day. Elias was awake for all of it now, and the Delacroix house breathed. The foundation settled in long sighs every few hours. The pipes ticked in patterns too regular to be pressure. And the lights—

The lights were going out.

Not burning out. Not flickering and dying the way old bulbs do, with a stutter and a filament-flash. They simply dimmed, one by one, in an order that had no logic except patience. The hall sconce first, on the second night. Then the kitchen overhead. Then the lamp in what had been Grand-mère's reading chair, which no one had touched since 1998 and which had been dark since then anyway until, on the fourth night, it glowed softly to life for one hour and then went out forever, as if the house had wanted him to know it could do that.

By the sixth evening, twenty-three bulbs had become seventeen.

He replaced them. He'd bought a case of bulbs at the hardware store in Ville Platte, along with the coffee and the batteries and the cheap digital watch he wore now instead of his phone, because his phone kept showing him things in the black mirror of its sleeping screen—a face there, once, that looked like his but smiled a half-second before he did. He climbed stepladders and swapped dead bulbs for live ones, and the new bulbs lasted exactly as long as the house wanted them to, which was never as long as the old ones.

It wasn't malice. That was the thing he kept coming back to, standing under a fixture in the upstairs hall with a fresh bulb sweating in his grip. Malice would have been easier. Malice you could fight. This was something else—this was a schedule. A weaning. The house was dimming itself around him the way you dim the lights in a nursery, gradually, gently, so the child doesn't notice the dark arriving, so the child drifts off without ever having decided to.

They weren't trying to scare him awake.

They were trying to sing him to sleep.

* * *

On the seventh day, Father Aubert called.

"You sound terrible," the old priest said. No hello. Aubert had never wasted a syllable on greeting in his life; he arrived at conversations the way he arrived at funerals, already mid-procession.

"I'm fine."

"You're lying. I've buried four generations of Delacroix men and every one of you lies the same way—too fast, too flat." A pause, paper rustling. "I found something else in the parish rolls. I'm coming by."

"Don't." Elias closed his eyes and immediately regretted it; behind the lids, in the red-dark, something shifted. "Father, I mean it. Don't come here after dark. Don't come here at all if you can help it."

"Is that concern for me or for me?"

"It's concern for anyone who hears what this house sounds like at midnight and lives long enough to describe it."

Aubert was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into the register he used for the confessional, low and level and stripped of performance. "My grandmother grew up two miles from this house. She told me about the singing when I was a boy. She said the Delacroix women sang to keep something fed, and that the worst thing you could ever do was hum along, because humming along was how they got you." Another pause. "Elias. Have you been humming?"

"No."

"Have you wanted to?"

Elias looked at the notebook on the counter. At the tally marks. Six days. Forty-one hours of true silence from the thing under the well since he'd learned the halfway verse—forty-one hours of quiet that felt less like peace than like held breath.

"Yes," he said. "Every time they sing. That's the problem."

"That's always the problem," Aubert said. "Lock your doors. Count them twice. And Elias—for God's sake, whatever you do, don't lie down."

The line went dead. Outside, the sun was going down over the tree line, and Elias stood at the window and watched the house's shadows lengthen and counted, under his breath, the doors he could see from where he stood. Four. There should have been five.

There should have been five.

* * *

The fifth door was gone by full dark.

He walked the downstairs circuit with the notebook and a flashlight, checking each room against the map he'd drawn on the third day, when the rearrangements had started again in earnest. The pantry was still the pantry. The parlor was still the parlor. But the short hallway between them—the one with the coat closet and the door to the back steps—had shortened. The closet was there. The back-step door was there. The space between them had simply been eaten, quietly, the way a dream eats distance, and when he paced it off the hallway was four feet shorter than it had been that morning.

Four feet. The width of a door.

The house was taking pieces of itself and doing something with them. Building. He didn't let himself think about what.

Upstairs, he ran Elodie's ritual. Every light burning—that was harder now, seventeen bulbs fighting a losing war, and he'd taken to carrying a lit oil lantern from room to room like a man walking a ship's deck in weather. Doors counted at dusk: he stood in the center of the upstairs hall and turned slowly, counting aloud, his voice cracked and strange in his own ears.

"Eleven."

The word hung there. And then, from the far end of the hall, from the direction of the mirror that held her—or held something wearing her—came a sound so soft he almost missed it.

A correction.

"*Twelve,*" said his mother's voice.

He spun. The hall was empty. The mirrors showed him only himself, gaunt and gray, lantern shaking. But the number had landed in the air between the walls like a stone dropped into still water, and the ripples were still moving when he backed into his room and wedged the chair under the knob.

Twelve. There were eleven doors. Unless the house had built another one today. Unless the house was building them faster now. Unless somewhere in the dark of the shifting rooms there was a twelfth door standing open, waiting, patient as everything else in this place, for him to finally get tired enough to walk through it.

He sat on the floor with his back against the bed frame and opened the notebook to a clean page and wrote, in handwriting that had begun to slant:

*Day 7. They are building doors. They are using my mother's voice. They are dimming the lights one bulb at a time and they are not in any hurry at all.*

*That's the worst part. They can wait. I can't.*

* * *

Sleep deprivation does its work in stages, and Elias catalogued them the way he catalogued everything now, because naming things was the last weapon he had.

Stage one was the buzzing. Day three. A fine electric hum behind the eyes, the world going slightly too sharp, edges glittering.

Stage two was the slips. Day five. Words going missing mid-sentence. Standing in rooms without remembering entering them. Finding the lantern set down in places he hadn't left it.

Stage three—he was in it now, day seven, hour ninety-something—was the company.

Because that was the truth nobody warned you about: stay awake long enough and you were never alone. The mind, refused its dreams, began generating them in daylight, thin ghosts of attention drifting at the edge of vision. And the house knew. The house had been waiting for stage three. It filled his peripheral vision with movement that was almost movement—a hem of a dress turning the corner of the stairwell, a shape seated in Grand-mère's chair that resolved, when he looked directly, into shadow and upholstery. It was testing him. Probing the thin places in his attention the way water probes a dam, finding the cracks, widening them by fractions.

And at night—when he fought hardest, when he slapped his own face and drank cold coffee grounds and pinched the skin of his forearms black and blue—at night the choir sang lullabies.

That was the word for it. He'd resisted the word for two days, but there was no other. The voices in the plaster had abandoned the hymns. What rose through the walls now, low and slow and unbearably tender, were the songs their mother had sung them to sleep with—*Fais do do*, *Alouette*, the Cajun lullaby about the little boat—and they sang them wrong in only one particular: they left spaces where the echo of a child's voice should go. Little rests, little silences, shaped exactly like a small boy singing along.

His mother's alto led them. He knew it was not her. He knew it the way he knew the mirrors lagged, the way he knew the wellhouse breathed—it was knowledge that lived below belief, in the marrow. But knowing did nothing against the pull. That was the horror of it, the thing he would remember if he survived: the song didn't argue with him. It didn't threaten. It simply offered, over and over, in the voice that had meant safety before he had words for anything, the same warm invitation: *come rest, come rest, everyone you love is already here.*

On the eighth night, he caught himself with his lips moving.

Just shaping the vowels. Just following the melody a half-beat behind, mouth open, no sound—yet. He snapped his jaw shut so hard his teeth clicked and pressed both hands over his mouth and sat in the lantern light shaking, and in the walls the lullaby paused—paused, the way a listener pauses—and then resumed, softer, sweeter, infinitely patient.

*Soon,* the pause had said. *We can wait. We have waited a hundred years. We can wait one more week.*

* * *

Day nine. Hour two hundred and six.

He found Elodie at 4 a.m., because 4 a.m. was when the house's patience thinned and the real her could push through, worn and flickering, into the surviving glass.

She looked terrible. Worse than he did, and he could see himself. Her image guttered like a candle in draft, her edges fraying into the silvered dark behind her, and when she signed to him her hands moved slowly, deliberately, spending effort like coins.

*You haven't slept.*

"Not in nine days." His voice was a ruin. He kept it below a whisper anyway; the walls listened. "I can't. If I sleep, they—"

*I know what they do.* Her hands faltered, steadied. *Elias. Listen. You cannot win this by staying awake. Nobody outlasts them. Grand-mère tried. She stayed awake for nineteen days and on the twentieth she walked to the wellhouse herself, smiling, and sang the whole verse with them, and she was glad. Do you understand? Glad. That's what they take from you at the end—not your will. Your wanting.*

He stared at her. The lantern hissed. Somewhere below the floors, faint as a pulse under ice, the dark turned over in its sleep.

"So what do I do?" he whispered. "Fall asleep and let them have me?"

*No.* Her hand flattened against the inside of the glass, and he put his palm against the outside, matching it, the way they'd done as children through the window between their rooms during the lightning storms, when the rule was whoever fell asleep first owed the other a secret. *You sleep. But not here. Not in this house. You drive to Aubert's rectory and you sleep in a building they don't own, and you eat, and you come back tomorrow with a plan instead of a corpse that hasn't fallen down yet.*

"And leave the house alone? Leave you alone with them?"

Her expression did something complicated—grief and love and exasperation braided together, the exact face she'd made at twelve when he'd refused to leave her side during her appendicitis and the nurses had to make him.

*Elias. I have been alone in this glass for a long time. I can be alone in it for one more day. What I cannot survive is watching them wear you down note by note until you walk into the choir smiling and call it your idea.*

"They'll close the roads," he said. "Or move the doors. Or—"

*Then go now. Before they finish dimming the lights.* She glanced past him, at something in the room behind his shoulder, and her image shuddered. *Elias. How many bulbs are burning right now?*

He counted without turning around. He knew the answer before he finished.

"Nine."

*Nine,* she repeated, and her hand slid down the glass. *Yesterday there were fourteen. They're not weaning you anymore, little brother. They're finishing.*

* * *

He packed in four minutes. Notebook, lantern, the halfway verse written out in his own hand, the photograph of the two of them at eight years old on the pier at Grand Isle. He did not look at the mirrors while he worked, because looking had become a conversation and he couldn't afford one more sentence of it.

At the top of the stairs he stopped.

Every light in the house had gone out behind him. Not dimmed—extinguished, in sequence, room by room, following him down the hall like footsteps. Only the lantern burned, and its circle of light had shrunk to the size of a grave blanket. And in the dark ahead of him, at the bottom of the stairs, where the front door should have been—

There were two front doors now. Side by side. Both standing open onto the porch, onto the night, onto the road that led away.

One of them opened onto the driveway. He could see the truck, the cypress trees, the ordinary stars.

The other opened onto darkness that was not night. Darkness with depth to it, darkness that hummed very faintly, in a key he had learned by heart, and from somewhere down inside that darkness his mother's voice rose once more, gentle as breath:

*Elias. Come say goodbye properly, baby. You can't leave without saying goodbye.*

He stood at the top of the stairs with the lantern shaking in his fist, and he understood, with the terrible clarity of the ninth day without sleep, that the house had stopped being subtle because subtlety had failed, and that the two doors were not a trick he could reason his way past—they were a choice the house believed he would eventually make correctly, and the house had a hundred years of evidence that Delacroixes always, eventually, chose correctly.

He picked up the lantern. He fixed his eyes on the left-hand door—the one with stars above it, real stars, the truck's rusted fender catching their light—and he began to descend, one step at a time, counting them aloud in a voice like gravel.

"One. Two. Three."

From the right-hand doorway, softly, in unison, a hundred voices counted with him.

"Four. Five."

They matched his pace. They matched his breathing. By the ninth step they were breathing when he breathed, and he understood that they had been practicing this his whole life.

"Ten. Eleven. Twelve."

His foot found the ground floor. The left-hand door stood four feet away. Cold night air moved through it, smelling of pine and distance and the living world, and Elias walked toward it without running, because some part of him knew that running was a kind of singing, that panic had a rhythm and they could harmonize with anything that had a rhythm.

He was three feet from the threshold when every remaining light in the house came on at once—all of them, every fixture, blazing—and in that sudden noon-bright instant he saw, reflected in the brass rail of the coat stand beside the door, his own reflection.

Standing still.

Smiling.

While he was still moving.

He did not look at it again. He stepped through the left-hand door into the night, into the cold, into the ordinary world, and he did not stop walking until he reached the truck, and he did not stop shaking until he was five miles down the parish road with the windows down and the radio playing static, and only then, at a red-clay crossroads where the live oaks made a tunnel of dark, did he allow himself to whisper the thing he'd been holding in his mouth since the stairs:

"They showed me my reflection. Already theirs."

In the passenger seat, the notebook lay open to the last page he'd written, and beneath his final line, in handwriting that was almost his—slightly too neat, slightly too calm, written by no hand that had been in the truck—were four words:

*Come home and sleep.*

Elias drove toward Ville Platte with the windows down and the radio loud, and behind him in the rearview mirror the Delacroix house receded into the dark, every window burning, every light lit, glowing like a lantern held up at the edge of a well—patient, radiant, and waiting for him to get tired enough to need it.

He had one day. One day of sleep, one day of food, one day of Aubert's records and Aubert's roof. And then he was going back, because Elodie was in that glass and the verse was half-learned and the dark under the well had noticed him, and none of those facts cared whether he was rested.

But as the trees closed behind the house, one thought followed him all the way to the parish line, quiet as a lullaby:

They had shown him the reflection smiling because they wanted him to know it was already done.

All that remained was for him to catch up to it.
