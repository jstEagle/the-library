## 30. Learning the Last Verse

The mirror is the only thing left in the house that tells the truth, and it is telling it to me.

I stand on my side of the glass and Elias stands on his, and between us there is nothing but a quarter inch of silvered dark and everything we have ever failed to say. The house groans around us like a ship in ballast. Somewhere below the floors, below the foundations, below the roots of the live oaks that have been drinking from our family's shame for a hundred years, something vast has turned over in its sleep and begun to listen.

But here, in this room, there is only us.

"Again," I say—or rather, I shape it. My voice went wherever voices go when the choir takes you. What I have instead is my mouth, my hands, my face pressed close enough to the glass that my breath fogs it from the wrong side, which is a thing I did not know dead girls could do until last week.

Elias watches my lips the way he used to watch Maman's hands over the piano keys. That same terrible concentration. That same stubbornness underneath it, the refusal to look away first.

*Again,* I mouth.

He opens his mouth and gives me nothing. Not silence exactly—silence would be a relief. He gives me the *shape* of the note, jaw dropped to the right height, throat rounded, lips parted just so, and behind his eyes I can see the melody running like current under ice. He has it. He's had it since Tuesday. He will not let it out.

Good. God, good. Because if he lets it out—if one note of that verse crosses his teeth with the choir awake and listening—the house will take it as signature. As consent. As the Delacroix name written in the family ink.

So we do it the way Maman taught us lullabies, back when we were small enough to share a bed during storms. She would sing a line, and we would mouth along without sound, learning the melody into our bodies before our voices were ever allowed near it. "First you carry it," she said. "Then you can give it away."

I am carrying it for him now. It is the only weight left to me.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about being dead in your own house: you remember everything, and none of it belongs to you anymore.

I remember Maman's hands. I remember the specific way she cupped them, palms up, like she was holding water she didn't want to spill, and how she'd move them up for higher notes and down for lower ones, and how Elias and I used to fall asleep watching those hands rise and fall like something breathing.

So that is how I teach him.

I raise my hand slowly, fingers spread, and hold it at the height where the verse begins—that suspended fourth, the note that hangs like a held breath—and I watch him match it. His hand comes up. His mouth shapes the sound. His chest rises and holds.

He cannot hear himself do it. He cannot hear me at all. We are two people conducting an orchestra that exists only in the space between us, and somehow, impossibly, the music is getting through.

*Down a third,* I sign, hand falling. *Now down a third, and hold.*

His hand falls. His mouth rounds. And I watch his heartbeat happen in his throat—I can see it, the pulse jumping under his jaw, quick and arrhythmic with fear, pounding against the melody the way a bird beats against a window. He is terrified. He should be. But he does not break the line. He holds the shape of the note the way you hold a door against wind, and inside the shape, his heart hammers and hammers and hammers.

That is the whole lesson, really. That is everything Grand-mère never understood and the choir never learned: the song was never meant to be sung *clean*. It was meant to be sung scared. Sung by someone whose body is fighting the melody every second, whose grief and love are tangled all through it like roots through a coffin. A willing voice is just a voice. A voice that carries its fear as part of the offering—

That is the loophole. That is what Grand-mère never found, because she never loved anyone more than the bargain.

* * *

We work until the light changes.

The house dims around us—it has been dimming things on purpose lately, punishing his wakefulness, trying to wear him down to the soft place where dreams live and the choir can reach him—but the room we're in stays lit. I think Elodie's mirror keeps its own weather. I think some part of whatever I am now lives in the glass, and the glass protects its own.

Or maybe the house wants him rested for the wrong reasons. I try not to think about that.

We break the verse into pieces, the way you'd feed something small. First phrase alone, over and over, until his mouth moves through it without hesitation. Then the second phrase stitched to the first. Then the turn—the place where the melody drops below where a comfortable voice wants to go, down into the cellar of the register, the place where Grand-mère's corrupted notation had deliberately led singers astray. I correct him there with both hands, pressing flat toward the floor: *lower, lower, they lied about this part, everyone always sang it too high because the lie was easier.*

He frowns. Mouths: *Why lie?*

Because a verse sung wrong doesn't end anything. A verse sung wrong just renews the debt. Grand-mère knew it. She framed the false page above her own mirror like a saint's icon and let a century of Delacroixes sing her corruption into the walls, and the dark below kept sleeping, kept being fed just enough almost-right song to stay drowsy, and the family kept paying, generation after generation, twin after twin.

*Because the truth ends it,* I tell him. *And they didn't want it ended. They wanted it maintained. A farm, Elias. We were livestock with a hymnal.*

He goes very still. I watch him decide not to say anything, and then I watch him decide to say it anyway.

*I'm sorry,* he mouths. *I'm sorry I wasn't there.*

Oh, my brother. Oh, my other half, my mirror-self, my first language. He thinks the failure was absence. He thinks if he'd come home a week earlier, sat with me in the kitchen, noticed the shadows moving wrong—he could have stood between me and the choir.

He couldn't have. I know because I tried everything, and I was the one who understood the bargain best, and it still took me.

But I don't tell him that. Some mercies are lies, and this is one of them.

Instead I press my palm flat to the glass, and I wait, and after a moment he presses his against mine, and the cold that comes through is not glass-cold. It is older than glass. It is the temperature of the space I live in now.

*You're here now,* I tell him. *That's the only attendance the song ever required.*

* * *

Somewhere past midnight, the choir tries to interrupt.

It starts as a hum in the wall behind him—a low alto line, warm and familiar, threading itself into the pauses between phrases. I know that voice. Everyone in this family knows that voice. Grand-mère, singing the way she sang when we were children and thought her hymns were comfort, inserting herself into the spaces where a tired mind might drift, where a half-learned melody might reach for help and find it offered.

That is how they get you. Not with force. With harmony. They wait until you're struggling with a phrase and they slide underneath you and carry it, and it feels like mercy, it feels like family, it feels like coming home—and then you realize you've sung three bars you never chose to sing, and the choosing was the whole point.

"Elias." I don't have the word for how loud I can be, but I spend everything I have. "*Don't.*"

He hears me. Or he sees me—my mouth, my eyes, whatever of me reaches him. His lips snap shut on the shape of the next phrase before it completes. His hand comes up flat against the humming wall, and he says, out loud, in his real living voice, cracked and furious:

"She's teaching me. You don't get to help."

The hum stops. The silence afterward has texture, has patience, has the quality of something large adjusting its grip.

And then, from everywhere—from the plaster, the pipes, the floorboards beneath his feet—the choir begins to sing the first verse. Full strength. All the Delacroixes at once, two centuries of them, and woven through the harmonies I can hear the newer voices now, the ones taken recently, and one of them is a priest who died mid-liturgy with holy words still in his mouth.

They are singing it beautifully. That is the horror. They are singing it better than any of us ever could, and it means nothing to the dark below except another meal served on schedule.

Elias stands in the middle of it with his eyes closed and his mouth shut and his fists clenched, and he listens to his entire dead family sing, and he does not make a sound.

When it's over, he looks at me through the glass, and his eyes are wet, and he mouths: *How do you bear it? Hearing them?*

I consider lying again. I've gotten good at it; death is mostly administration.

*They aren't them anymore,* I tell him. *The singing wears them smooth. What's left is just the tune. Hold onto the off-key things, Elias. Hold onto Maman missing the high note in the lullaby and laughing. That's the part they can't copy.*

He nods. He wipes his face with the heel of his hand, impatient with his own grief, the way he's been since he was six years old.

*Show me the turn again,* he mouths.

* * *

So I show him the turn.

This is the heart of the verse, the hinge, the place where the melody stops describing the dark and starts addressing it—not a leash anymore but a gift, laid down, released. Grand-mère could never have taught this part even if she'd wanted to, because you cannot teach surrender honestly unless you mean it, and she never meant anything freely in her life.

I move my hand through the phrase slowly. Up past where the first verse climbed, higher than the corrupted page allowed, into the register where a voice has to commit or crack. And then the fall—not a drop, a *release*, the way you open a fist, the way you let go of a rope you've been holding for a hundred years.

His mouth follows mine. His hand follows my hand. His heart pounds against the melody, against the shape of it, against the meaning of it, and I watch him learn the hardest thing in the song, which is not a note at all. It is the intention underneath the notes. The difference between giving and paying.

*Say it with me,* I sign. *Not the words. The meaning. Every time your hand falls, think: freely. Freely. Freely.*

His lips move around the silent word. I watch it land in him somewhere below music, below language, in the place where Maman's lullabies still live, where the storm-nights and the shared bed and the cupped hands are stored, all of it, everything we were before the house told us what we were for.

Freely. Freely. Freely.

By the time the sky outside goes gray, he has the whole verse. Mouth only. Silent as snowfall. Perfect.

He stands there in the graying light, swaying a little with exhaustion, and looks at me through the glass, and for a long moment neither of us signs anything. There's nothing left to teach. There's only what comes next, and we both know what comes next, and the knowing sits between us like a third person in the room.

*You'll rest,* he says. Not a question. A promise he's checking the terms of.

*When it's done,* I say. *When it's given, not paid. Then yes. I think so.*

*And you?* I ask him, though I already know, though I've known since the funeral, since the casket, since the brass rail and the late reflection. *What happens to you?*

He smiles, and it breaks my heart in the old way, the ordinary way, the sister-way.

*I'll be fine,* he mouths. *I've got one second of lag to give away. That's all it ever wanted from me anyway.*

No. That's not all. It wanted his voice, his consent, his forever. But he's decided to believe it costs less than it does, because believing otherwise would make him hesitate, and he cannot hesitate tomorrow night. So I let him have the smaller price. Love is letting someone carry the true weight alone if carrying it together would crush them.

One more thing before the daylight takes him to sleep. I press close to the glass.

*Tomorrow night, when they sing the first two verses—they'll be loud enough to crack the windows. They'll offer you everything. Rest, safety, me. They'll make it sound like arithmetic. Like sense.*

*I know.*

*And when the dark comes up through the floors, it will feel like the end of the world. Let it feel like that. Fear is part of the offering. Sing it scared, Elias. Sing it the way Maman taught us. Carry it first.*

*Then give it away,* he finishes.

Yes. Yes. That.

He puts his palm on the glass one last time, and I put mine against it, and for a moment—for one full second, the length of the lag, the length of everything—we are twins again, matched and mirrored, two halves of one breath.

Then he turns off the lamp and goes to lie down in a house full of listening doors, and I stay in the glass with the verse finished and the night coming, and far below the foundations, patient as geology, the dark begins to rise toward the melody it has waited a century to hear completed.

Tomorrow at midnight, every door in this house will open at once.

And my brother will stand alone at the last mirror in Louisiana with a whole song in his silenced mouth, and I will watch him choose, and whatever he chooses, I will love him through the glass until the glass is only glass.
