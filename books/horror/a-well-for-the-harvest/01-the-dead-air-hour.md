## 1. The Dead Air Hour

The station smelled like burnt coffee and old carpet, the way it had for eleven years, and Casper Vasquez had long ago decided that was part of the brand. *KRLM 1340 AM*, broadcasting from a cinderblock box behind a defunct car wash on the east side of town, where the tower's red light blinked through the window like a patient heart that refused to stop beating no matter how many times the accountants pronounced it dead.

"Welcome back to Open Line," he said into the microphone, leaning close enough that his breath fogged the pop filter. "It is one forty-seven in the morning, which means we have officially entered the hours when reasonable people are asleep and unreasonable people are awake and dialing. Which, of course, is my favorite demographic."

He let the board operator's laugh die in the empty room. There was no board operator anymore. There hadn't been since March. The console lights glowed green and amber at him like the eyes of some loyal animal too dumb to know it had been abandoned.

"You're listening to the only show in this market brave enough — or stupid enough, my program director says both — to take your calls live and unfiltered. Ghosts. Grief. Government mind-control beams. That thing your neighbor does with his leaf blower at six a.m. on Sundays. I want to hear all of it." He tapped the mic with one knuckle, a habit he'd stolen from a host he'd idolized in college, a man who now sold insurance in Phoenix. "Lines are open. The number's on your screen, if you still have a screen. If you're driving a rig through the dark somewhere out there on Route 9, keep those headlights up and those opinions coming."

He killed the mic and sat back in the chair that squeaked in exactly two places, both of them under his shoulder blades, so that every lean backward sounded like a small confession.

The studio was three rooms' worth of equipment crammed into one: the console, a rack of aging processors that hummed in a key he'd come to think of as B-flat, a corkboard pinned with sponsor cards from the funeral home and the pest control place and Marge's Diner, and a window that looked out onto nothing but the parking lot and, beyond it, the dark slope of the hills where the town ran out of nerve. On the wall above the monitors hung the framed certificate from his twenty years in radio, and beneath it, taped crookedly, a postcard his mother had sent him before she died — a lighthouse, no message, just her handwriting on the back: *So you remember somebody has to keep the light on.*

He'd built a career on being the light that didn't flicker. Twenty-two years on the air, the last nine in this graveyard slot, and his whole method fit inside a matchbook: never be the one who sounds scared. The callers who phoned in at two a.m. wanting to talk about the shadow person in their closet, the EVP recording of their dead husband, the dream that came true down to the license plate number — they weren't really calling about ghosts. They were calling because they were lonely, or grieving, or frightened of something ordinary wearing a costume, and what they needed wasn't belief. It was someone steady. Someone who could take the wildest story in the world and hold it up to the fluorescent light of common sense until it either turned out to be a moth or flew away.

Most nights it was a moth.

"Let's go to the phones," he said, punching up line one. "Caller, you're live on Open Line. Who's keeping the dark company tonight?"

A man named Denny wanted to talk about Bigfoot again. Casper let him get thirty seconds in before he pounced, gently, the way you'd take a toy from a toddler — not cruelly, but firmly, with a smile in the voice.

"Denny, Denny. Last month it was a chupacabra outside Fairview. Before that, the UFO over the reservoir that turned out to be — say it with me —"

"A weather balloon," Denny said, deflated.

"A weather balloon. Denny, I love you like a brother, but if Bigfoot wanted to be found in this county, he'd have called this show himself by now. We're an easy number to find."

Denny laughed. They always laughed. That was the trick nobody understood about cruelty done well: it worked best when the target helped you swing the bat.

Line two was a woman convinced her late mother spoke to her through the kitchen radio. Casper did his soft-voiced routine for that one, the gentle skeptic, the man who'd lost people too and knew the hunger in the wires. "Your mama's talking to you, sweetheart, but she's not using the radio. She's using the recipe card in her handwriting. She's using the way you still set out two cups. Radios are just radios. But grief — grief gets a signal everywhere."

She cried a little. She thanked him. He felt the small warm coal of having done the job right settle in his chest, and he banked it, the way he always did, against the cold that came with the hour.

Because here was the thing Casper never said on air: the cold got worse every year. Three a.m. used to feel like his. His kingdom of static. Lately it felt like standing in a large empty house with all the doors open, listening.

* * *

At 2:09, the phone lines went strange.

Not dead — stranger than dead. All five lines lit at once, which happened maybe twice a year, usually after a tornado warning. And beneath the hum of the console, under the processor's B-flat drone, there was a new sound, faint as a hairline crack in glass. A hiss that rose and fell. Almost rhythmic. Almost like breathing.

Casper frowned at the board and touched the fader for the off-air monitor. The hiss stayed. He checked the transmitter remote readout — power nominal, modulation clean — and told himself it was atmospheric. Solar activity. The ionosphere doing its late-night laundry.

"Still with me out there?" he said on air, covering. "Sounds like we've got some weather on the band tonight. If you can hear me, you know the drill — hang tight, the signal always comes home."

He punched up line one. Nothing. Line two. Nothing. Lines three, four, five — each one picked up and put down, silent, like doors opening onto empty rooms.

Then line four lit again, and this time when he answered it, someone breathed.

"Open Line, you're live."

Static. Long, oceanic static. And then a woman's voice, middle-aged, unhurried, with the particular calm of someone who has rehearsed a difficult sentence until it stopped hurting.

"Is this Casper Vasquez?"

"It is. And who do I have?"

"My name is Marlene Duffy." A pause, polite, almost formal. "You probably don't remember me. I called your show last spring about the humming in my walls. You told me it was my water heater."

"I tell everybody it's their water heater," Casper said, warming. "Half the time I'm right. Marlene Duffy — sure, yeah, I remember the name. What's the water heater doing now? Singing hymns?"

"No." The word arrived without drama, without weight, the way a key arrives in a lock. "I'm calling to tell you about my funeral. It was Tuesday. At Hollis Brothers, on Delmore Street. Visitation started at six. There were fewer people than I'd hoped, honestly, but the Hendersons came, and my sister flew in from Tucson even though we hadn't spoken since Dad's stroke. Pastor Klein did the service. He mispronounced 'resurrection.' He always does. He did it at my mother's too."

Casper smiled the smile he kept holstered for prank calls, the professional amusement, the practiced cruelty worn lightly like a good coat. "Okay. Okay, I'll bite. Whose bit is this? Because it's a good one. The detail work is excellent — Pastor Klein's a nice touch, he's buried half this county. Who put you up to it? Was it the guys across town? Tell them the production value is wasted on me."

There was a silence on the line. Not static silence. Human silence. The kind that has a temperature.

"Casper," the woman said, and her voice didn't change at all, which was somehow the worst part, "the flowers were white lilies and blue delphinium because my sister remembered I liked them, except she was wrong, I liked peonies, and I lay there thinking, well, that's Marlene for you, never once corrected anybody in her life, why start now. The casket was the mid-range one. My son picked it. He cried with his whole body, Casper. I wanted to touch his shoulder and I couldn't, and that's the part I keep coming back to. Not the light. Not the tunnel. My boy crying, and my hand wouldn't work."

The smile was still on Casper's face. He could feel it sitting there, fixed, like paint. Somewhere below it, something in his chest had begun, very quietly, to descend — the sensation of an elevator starting to move in a building you thought you knew floor by floor.

"That's —" He heard his own voice and course-corrected, made it light, made it radio. "That's genuinely affecting stuff, Marlene. Somebody wrote that. Somebody with talent. So here's what's going to happen: you're going to tell me who's producing this, and I'm going to laugh on air, and then I'm going to buy them a drink, because for a second there — and I mean a real second — you had me."

"I had the obituary in the paper Wednesday," she said. "Page B4. Marlene Ellen Duffy, sixty-one, of Cedar Hollow Road. You can look. I'd wait, but I don't know how much longer the line holds. It doesn't hold long. It didn't hold for Reverend Pell either, and he's been trying to reach you for years."

The studio around Casper seemed to tilt by a degree, the way a room does when you stand up too fast, except he was sitting perfectly still. The console lights burned green and amber. The tower light blinked its patient blink beyond the glass. Everything was exactly where it always was, and everything was wrong.

"Reverend Pell," he repeated. The name came out of his mouth feeling borrowed.

"He says you'll know soon." A soft sound, almost a laugh, tired and fond. "You sound like your grandmother, you know. Same voice. Same way of talking when you're frightened — you get very neat, very controlled. She used to answer the phone at the farm that way. *Vasquez residence.* Neat as a pin. Right up until the end."

The hiss beneath the console swelled, just slightly, like a tide finding the edge of its mark.

"Marlene." He made himself say it slowly, testing the weight of it, the way you test ice. "Marlene Duffy died six days ago. If that's true, then whoever I'm talking to knows it, and knows I might check, and this is the cruelest damn prank anyone has ever tried to run on this program. And I have taken calls from a man who believed his toaster was possessed by Elvis, so the bar is high."

"Check the paper," she said gently. "And Casper — when you check, and you will, because you're your grandmother's people through and through — I need you to listen to what I actually called to say. Not the funeral. The funeral's just proof. What I need you to hear is this: they're going to call you from the farm. One after another. And every single one of us is going to sound calm, because we are calm, we've had time to get calm, and that's going to frighten you worse than screaming would. Don't let the calm fool you. We are not calling to haunt you, honey."

The line crackled. Through the crackle, for half a second, Casper heard — or thought he heard, and would spend the rest of his life unsure which — a great many voices underneath hers, layered like sediment, murmuring, patient, in no hurry at all.

"We're calling to warn you," Marlene Duffy said. "The ground out at Hollow Creek is open now. And your name is on the invitation."

The line went to dead air — true dead air, the flat black nothing of a disconnected circuit — and Casper Vasquez sat alone in the cinderblock box behind the defunct car wash, holding a telephone that had already forgotten her, while out past the edge of town the tower light blinked, and blinked, and blinked.

After a long moment, very quietly, so the open mic wouldn't catch it, he said the thing he hadn't said aloud in thirty years:

"*Grandma?*"
