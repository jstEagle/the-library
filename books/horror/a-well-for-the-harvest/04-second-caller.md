## 4. Second Caller

The station smelled like burnt coffee and old carpet by the third hour of the night, and Casper Vasquez had learned to love both smells the way a sailor loves salt — not because they were pleasant, but because they meant he was still at sea.

He sat in the booth with his tie loosened and his shoes off under the desk, feet flat on the cold linoleum, watching the VU meter breathe. Outside the glass, downtown Kessler Falls lay under a skin of fog so thick the traffic lights bled into it, red and green smears like bruises on wet paper. The AM signal went out over the valley on 5,000 watts of aging transmitter, reaching farmers who couldn't sleep and truckers who couldn't stop and insomniacs who had nothing else between them and the inside of their own skulls.

That was his congregation. He'd built it on doubt.

"Open Line," he said into the mic, his voice dropping into the register his producer called *the velvet knife*. "It's two-forty-one in the morning, you're listening to 1240 AM, and I want to know what's keeping you up. Ghosts? Grief? The mortgage? Call in. I promise to believe exactly as much of it as I can prove."

The board lit up almost immediately. It always did after Marlene Duffy.

He hadn't stopped thinking about her. That was the trouble. Six days dead, buried Tuesday, and she'd called his show at 2:13 a.m. describing her own funeral with the weary precision of a woman reviewing a disappointing restaurant. He'd checked the obituary twice — once for air, once alone in the parking lot with his phone screen too bright against the dark. *Marlene Ellen Duffy, 71, beloved mother and grandmother.* There was even a photo. He knew that face. She'd called the show for years, always around harvest time, always to tell him the corn talked if you stood out in it long enough, and he'd always laughed, gently, the way you laugh at someone you've decided is harmless.

Last night she hadn't sounded harmless. She'd sounded tired. And she'd known things.

But morning would come, and morning was where hoaxes went to die, so Casper did what he always did: he put it in a box in his head labeled *pending* and picked up the next caller.

"Open Line, you're live. Who's this?"

Static first. Not the clean hiss of a bad connection but something denser, grainier, like wind through dry stalks. Then a man's voice, old-fashioned in its cadence, each word placed down carefully as a stone in a wall.

"Good evening. Is this the young man who takes calls from the departed?"

Casper smiled at the board. "That depends. Are you departed?"

"I am Amos Pell. I was the minister of the Hollow Creek congregation from 1898 until my death in November of 1924. I am calling regarding a document."

"Okay." Casper leaned back, letting the smile carry into his voice. This was good radio, actually. Whoever was doing this — and he was now fairly sure it was the crew at KRKN across town, retaliating for the ratings — they'd committed to the bit. Period-appropriate diction, a name you could look up or not, a hook. "Reverend Pell. Welcome to Open Line. You've been dead a hundred years, give or take. How's the afterlife treating you?"

There was a pause. In it, Casper heard something that made the smile stiffen on his face: breathing. Slow, wet, patient breathing, the kind you hear through a shared wall in a cheap motel.

"You are not taking me seriously," the voice said, without offense. As though noting the weather.

"Humor me, Reverend. What document?"

"The deed of covenant for the Hollow Creek Farm parcel. Dated the fourteenth of October, 1891. Signed by Ezekiel Vasquez, grantor, and witnessed by myself and one Harlan Grieves, county clerk." A dry sound that might have been a chuckle. "You will not find it in the county archive, Mr. Vasquez. Your family kept that one private."

Casper's hand, reaching for his coffee, stopped halfway.

He told himself it was a guess. *Vasquez* was right there in the station's call letters, in the show's branding, in every promo the man's accomplice would have heard. Any prankster with a search engine could connect the host to the name. Fine. Fine.

"What's in this private deed, Reverend?"

"Shall I recite it?"

"If you like."

"I shall." And then the voice changed — flattened, formalized, the cadence of a man reading aloud in a room where everyone else is standing. "Know all men by these presents, that Ezekiel Vasquez of Hollow Creek, being lawfully seized of the hereinafter described parcel, does grant and convey unto the party of the second part — whose name is not written but understood — all rights to the water beneath, the roots beneath, and the rest beneath, in perpetuity, in consideration of which the said Vasquez and his heirs, assigns, and descendants do covenant and agree to furnish a well for the harvest—"

"Stop." Casper heard himself say it before he decided to. His mouth had gone dry in a way coffee wouldn't fix. "Say that last part again."

"—a well for the harvest," the voice repeated, mild as Sunday. "One soul per generation, freely given, freely grown. In witness whereof, the parties have set their hands this day and year above written. Amen."

Silence on the line. Under it, faintly, that breathing again, and beneath the breathing something else — a low, rhythmic creak, like a floorboard bearing weight over and over, or a rope swinging from a beam.

Casper realized he was standing. He didn't remember getting up.

"That's very good," he said, and hated how thin his voice came out. He keyed the talkback to his producer's booth. "Denny, tell me you're recording this."

Denny's voice came back tinny and bored: "Always recording. Why?"

"Nothing. Go back to sleep." He released the button. To the caller: "Reverend Pell. Assuming you're real, assuming any of this is real — why call a late-night radio show? Why me?"

"Because you answer," the voice said simply. "The others stopped answering years ago. But a Vasquez answers. It's in the blood, alongside everything else that's in the blood." A pause, and when the voice resumed it had softened, gone almost gentle, which was infinitely worse. "Your great-grandfather signed that page with a steady hand, Mr. Vasquez. I watched him do it. He wept afterward, but his hand never shook. They train themselves, your people. Steady hands. Closed mouths. Your father had the steadiest hands I ever saw, right up until the end."

The studio seemed to tilt. Casper gripped the edge of the desk. The fog outside the glass pressed closer, or seemed to.

"My father died eleven years ago," Casper said. "Heart attack."

"He left a note," the voice said. "In the drawer of his workbench, under the screwdrivers. You found it three days later, when you went looking for a socket wrench to fix the porch step. You have never told anyone what it said. You have never even said it aloud to yourself."

The clock on the board read 2:52. Somewhere in the building a door closed. Casper became aware of his own heartbeat, loud and stupid in his ears.

"You looked it up already, didn't you?" he managed. "Marlene Duffy's obituary. My father's death. It's all public record. Somebody did their homework."

"Mr. Vasquez." The voice was patient the way deep water is patient. "I have been dead for one hundred years and some months. Do you imagine I needed to look anything up?"

"Then prove it." The words came out of him fast, hot, the old reflex — skepticism as armor, cruelty as a drawbridge raised. "Anyone can recite a spooky poem. Anyone can Google a death certificate. Give me something that isn't in any record. Give me something only a Pell would know."

"A Pell," the voice repeated, tasting the word. "Very well. Ask me what you actually want to ask. You've been circling it since I said my name. I can hear it in your breathing, boy. Ask."

And Casper, who had spent twenty years teaching an audience that there was always a rational floor beneath the fall, who had dismantled psychics on air and laughed ghost stories into confetti, heard himself ask the question he'd sworn on his father's grave never to ask anyone:

"What do you know about my grandmother?"

The silence that followed was total. Not static-silence, not dead-air silence — a held-breath silence, the whole frequency leaning in. Even the board lights seemed to dim. Casper stood in the booth with his hand hovering over the fader and felt the night lean against the window glass like a face.

Then, softly, almost tenderly, the voice began again. Not the covenant this time. Something else. A hymn, low and droning, words half-swallowed:

*"Sow in the morn thy seed, at eve hold back thy hand—"*

"No," Casper said. "No, don't—"

*"—to feed the field below, to feed the hungry land—"*

His grandmother used to sing that. Standing at the kitchen sink, hands in dishwater, staring out the window at the tree line, singing it so quietly he'd thought for thirty years he'd invented the memory. She'd stopped the summer the well went dry at the old place. She'd stopped singing, stopped talking much at all, and in September she'd walked out into the back field and they'd found her shoes at the edge of the corn, side by side, neat as church.

Nobody ever said the word *suicide*. Nobody ever said anything. The family was good at that. Steady hands, closed mouths.

"Who told you that song?" Casper whispered.

The line clicked.

Not hung up — *clicked*, like a latch, like a lock turning. Then the dial tone, flat and ordinary, filling the booth. Casper stared at the phone as if it might explain itself. On the board, the light for line two blinked patiently, waiting for him to pick up the next caller, because the show must go on, because it was 2:56 a.m. and there were four minutes of break to fill and an audience of strangers awake in the dark.

He sat down. He discovered his legs wanted to disagree with the sitting and he overruled them.

"Open Line," he said, and his voice came out level, professional, a masterwork of fraud. "We're going to take a quick break. When we come back — weather, road conditions, and whatever else the night wants to give us. Stay with me, Kessler Falls."

He killed the mic. The ON AIR sign went dark.

For a long moment he just breathed. Then he pulled the keyboard close and typed with fingers that felt borrowed: *Amos Pell Hollow Creek minister.* The results crawled up the screen. A historical society newsletter, scanned and yellowed: *Rev. Amos Pell presided at the dedication of the Hollow Creek meeting house, 1901.* A census fragment. A grave registry entry — *Pell, Rev. Amos, 1861–1924, plot 3, row 7.*

And one more result, timestamped forty minutes ago, from the regional paper's archive: *Hollow Creek Farm Excavation Uncovers Historic Stone Structure; Developer Promises 'Respectful Progress.'*

He clicked it. The photo loaded slowly over the bad station internet: a gray morning, a chain-link fence, a backhoe with its arm raised like a praying mantis, and there in the torn earth — flush with the ground, older than anything had a right to be — a doorway.

Stone posts. A lintel. An iron ring fused shut with rust, or with something that had chosen to look like rust.

Casper zoomed in until the image broke apart into pixels. He read the caption twice. *Crew foreman Delia Marsh declined to comment on the structure's origin.*

He looked at the clock. 3:04 a.m. Four hours until sunrise. Ninety miles to Hollow Creek.

The phone rang. Line one. He let it ring, watching it, and the ringing filled the booth like a hymn nobody was singing, and somewhere ninety miles away, in a hole in the earth that had just been opened for the first time in a hundred years, something that answered to the names of the dead was learning the sound of his voice.

He reached out and turned the mic back on.

"Ladies and gentlemen," Casper Vasquez said to the sleeping valley, to the truckers and the grieving and the sleepless, to whoever — whatever — was listening, "we're coming back early tonight. Because I think we need to talk about Hollow Creek."

* * *

Ninety miles east, in a farmhouse with its windows boarded and its phones working only inward, a landline rang in the dark, and Delia Marsh counted heads for the fourth time that hour, and came up one short of five.
