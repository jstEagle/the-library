## 6. Ruth's Notes

The county archive smelled of cold paper and mouse, a smell Ruth Okafor had loved for twenty years and loved still, even now, even with the taste of that soil still in her mouth — warm, wrong, soft as something breathing under a blanket. She had driven into Hollow Creek proper at first light, leaving Delia's crew to sit on their hands at the site while the developer's lawyers argued about jurisdiction and liability. The halt order was temporary. Ruth knew how temporary orders worked. Which was why she was here, three hours before the archive opened, standing on the steps like a woman waiting for a pharmacy to fill a prescription she was ashamed of.

Mrs. Aldous, the archivist, let her in early because Ruth had once helped her authenticate a land grant and because small towns remembered debts longer than cities did.

"Vasquez," Mrs. Aldous said, when Ruth told her what she wanted. "There's not many left by that name around here."

"I know one," Ruth said. "A radio man. I don't think he knows he's from here."

"They usually don't." Mrs. Aldous led her back past the microfilm readers, past the glass case of Civil War buttons, to a shelf of ledger boxes with hand-lettered spines. "Farm registries, well permits, coroner's inquests. If it happened in this county between 1860 and 1950, it's in one of these boxes or it never happened at all." She set a box down with both hands, the way you'd set down something sleeping. "You want coffee?"

"No thank you."

"You'll want it by noon. Everyone does."

Ruth did not want coffee. What she wanted was to be wrong.

* * *

She started with the well because wells were her habit. Water infrastructure was the skeleton of any settlement; you could read a town's whole biography in its aquifers. The permit ledger for Hollow Creek Township ran in a careful clerk's hand, each entry dated, each depth recorded in feet. Vasquez entries appeared like fence posts: 1871, dug forty-two feet, sweet water. 1889, deepened to sixty-one feet after a dry summer. 1897—

1897 stopped her pen.

There was no 1897 entry for deepening. There was an entry dated October 3rd, 1897, in a different hand entirely, a hand that pressed so hard into the page it had torn through to the next leaf: *Well declared dry by order of E. Vasquez, owner. No further work authorized. No further work to be attempted.*

No further work *to be attempted*. Wells went dry all the time. You deepened them, you redug them, you abandoned them and sank a new one two hundred yards east. Nobody wrote *no further work to be attempted*, because nobody needed to. Unless somebody had tried.

Ruth turned pages backward, looking for the summer before. August 1897: a complaint filed by a neighboring farm, the Pell farm, alleging that the Vasquez well had "gone strange" — the clerk had transcribed the complaint verbatim, and the verbatim was worth the transcription:

*The water comes up warm as bathwater and it will not clear. We have drawed it every day a week and it stays the color of tea and there is a sound in the pipe when the wind is off the cornfield, like a man swallowing.*

Like a man swallowing. Ruth sat back. The archive's radiator ticked. Somewhere a fluorescent light buzzed at a frequency just below headache.

She pulled the coroner's inquest index next, because eleven was a number she'd been carrying since yesterday without knowing why. No — that wasn't true. She knew why. Sal had said it, joking, the morning they broke ground: *Eleven bodies, that's my bet. Old farms are body farms.* He'd laughed. They'd all laughed. The soil had been warm under their boots and they had laughed about it.

The index gave her names the way an index gives you anything: flatly, without mercy.

1881 — HANNAH VASQUEZ (b. 1862), cause: exposure, found in cornfield, verdict: accidental.
1884 — PETER LUND, hired hand, cause: undetermined, verdict: open.
1889 — SARAH PELL, cause: drowning (no water source identified), verdict: open.
1893 — JOSIAH VASQUEZ (b. 1858), cause: undetermined, verdict: open.
1897 — MARGARET COLE, midwife, cause: undetermined, verdict: open.
1902 — DANIEL VASQUEZ (b. 1870), cause: undetermined, verdict: open.
1908 — THOMAS PELL JR., cause: exposure, found at property line facing the house, verdict: accidental.
1915 — AGNES VASQUEZ (b. 1881), cause: undetermined, verdict: open.
1921 — CLARA MOSS, schoolteacher, cause: undetermined, verdict: open.
1930 — ELIAS VASQUEZ (b. 1899), cause: undetermined, verdict: open.
1937 — RUTH ANN VASQUEZ (b. 1912), cause: undetermined, verdict: open.

Eleven. Over fifty-six years, but clustered — and the clusters matched the dry years. She checked the rainfall records out of pure professional reflex, the part of her brain that wanted data instead of dread, and the pattern held like a stitch: the well ran low in '81, '89, '15, '30, '37. Every drought year, a name. And between the drought years, nothing. Forty years of quiet, then the door sealed in '97, and the deaths didn't stop — they just changed shape. Before 1897: drownings, exposures, accidents. After: *undetermined*, over and over, *undetermined*, until the word stopped meaning *we don't know* and started meaning *we agreed not to look*.

And every fourth name, like a refrain: Vasquez.

Ruth wrote it all down in her field notebook, her handwriting getting smaller as she went, until the last page looked like something written by a person trying to take up less room in the world.

* * *

The inquest files themselves were in a second box, and Mrs. Aldous brought her coffee at noon exactly as promised, and stood reading over Ruth's shoulder until Ruth felt the woman's breath on her neck and realized she hadn't moved in some time.

"That Cole woman," Mrs. Aldous said. "Margaret Cole. My grandmother delivered babies with her."

Ruth looked up. "What do you remember about her?"

"Only what Gran said, which was that she went out to the Vasquez place one night in October and never came home, and that when they asked Elias Vasquez — that would be the grandfather, I suppose — he wouldn't let searchers past the yard. Said the fields were his to walk. Paid for the funeral himself, though. Bought the nicest plot in the cemetery and put up a stone with no dates on it." Mrs. Aldous frowned at the memory. "Gran always said that was the strangest part. A stone with no dates. Like nobody could agree when she'd died. Or like the stone was for something else."

"May I see the file?"

"It'll be thin," Mrs. Aldous said. "They mostly were, for that farm."

She was right. Margaret Cole's inquest was four pages. Three were testimony from Elias Vasquez, and the testimony was a masterpiece of nothing: yes, the midwife had been summoned, no, he couldn't recall for whom, yes, she'd arrived, no, he hadn't seen her leave, the fields were large, the night was dark, women did sometimes lose their way. The fourth page was a note appended by the coroner himself, in ink so faded Ruth had to angle it against the window light:

*Searched the property twice. Second search conducted against owner's objection. Found nothing. Note for the record: the well on this property is capped with a stone slab weighing approximately 400 lbs., which requires four men and a block-and-tackle to move. Owner states it has been in place since before his purchase. Neighbor Pell states otherwise. Neighbor Pell declined to elaborate. Case closed.*

Neighbor Pell declined to elaborate. Ruth thought about a man who had seen something under a 400-pound stone and decided that silence was the better portion, and she thought about herself, sitting here, already deciding the same thing.

Because that was the thought, wasn't it? It had arrived sometime during the eleventh name and it sat in her chest now like a swallowed stone: *Delia is going to ask me what I found tonight, and I am going to lie to her.*

* * *

She stayed until the light went amber in the high windows. She copied everything — the permit entries, the inquest index, the coroner's note, the rainfall correlations — and then, because she was a thorough woman and thoroughness is its own kind of cowardice when you point it at the wrong question, she went looking for the deed.

It took two hours. Land records lived in their own room, in their own boxes, organized by township and section, and the Vasquez parcel was easy to find because the parcel had never changed hands outside the family. Never. Not once, not through bankruptcies or widowhoods or the great migration that emptied half the county into cities. One hundred and thirty years, and the same surname on every transfer, father to son to daughter to cousin, the way a disease passes through bloodlines.

The original grant was a document Ruth had handled a hundred documents like, and yet her hands sweated on it. It was written on vellum, in a copperplate hand, and it contained language she had seen only in medieval charters and one very strange case study from her doctoral years — language about covenants running with the land, obligations binding heirs and assigns *in perpetuity*, consideration rendered not in currency but in kind.

She read the clause three times. Then she photographed it. Then she read it aloud, very quietly, in an empty room, because some sentences don't feel real until your own voice betrays you by speaking them.

*"...the grantee and his heirs shall keep the well, and the well shall keep them..."*

She closed the folder. She sat very still. The radiator ticked. Outside, ordinary traffic made its ordinary sounds, and somewhere a dog barked twice and lost interest, and the world went on being the world, which was suddenly the most frightening fact available.

*A well for the harvest.* She'd heard the phrase before — where? Not in the archives. Somewhere recent. Somewhere with wind in it.

Then she had it: the cornerstone. Yesterday, when they'd cleared the foundation rubble of the old farmhouse, Tomas had pried loose a corner marker and Delia had brushed the dirt off it and there had been letters cut into the limestone, worn shallow, and Ruth had knelt and traced them with her fingers and read them without thinking much of it, because archaeologists read inscriptions the way other people read street signs.

*A WELL FOR THE HARVEST. EST. 1861.*

She had assumed it was agricultural. A motto. Farmers put mottos on everything — *God Speed the Plow*, *Bless This Field*. She had photographed it and moved on, and the photograph was on her phone right now, and she opened it and looked at the carved letters for a long time, and the letters looked back the way letters do, which is to say patiently, which is to say forever.

* * *

She drove back out to the site as the sun went down, and the drive gave her forty minutes to decide, and she used every one of them.

Here is what Ruth Okafor knew about herself, and had known since graduate school, since a dig in Kenya where she had kept quiet about a find for six weeks because the timing was wrong, because the ministry official was hostile, because she wanted the publication for herself: she was a woman who withheld. She called it discretion. She called it professional judgment. She had built a whole vocabulary of respectable words for the act of knowing something and not saying it, and the vocabulary had never once saved anyone, least of all her.

And here is what she knew about the crew. Delia, who carried responsibility like a load-bearing wall and would carry this one too, straight-backed, until it broke her. Sal, who joked because jokes were how he blessed things. Imani, who video-called her son every night at seven and held the phone up to the sky so he could see the stars over the farm, and whose face when she talked about him was the most defenseless thing Ruth had ever seen. And Tomas, who was a lit match looking for a room full of curtains, and who had already, Ruth was almost certain, been asking questions about the sealed door that no one should be asking.

If she told them about the eleven names, the droughts, the well, the covenant clause — what would change? Delia would pull the crew off, and the developer would replace them with men who didn't have names yet, and the door would get opened anyway, because doors like that got opened; the whole history of her discipline was a history of doors being opened by people who knew better. Or worse: Delia would believe her, truly believe her, and spend the rest of her life knowing she'd walked away from something and never learned what it was.

Or — and this was the argument her mind kept making, smooth as a lawyer, and she hated how good it sounded — the records were ambiguous. Eleven deaths over fifty-six years was sad, not supernatural. Farms killed people; isolation killed people; undetermined meant undetermined. The clause was boilerplate from a superstitious era. A warm soil anomaly had a dozen geological explanations. She was an archaeologist, not a ghost hunter, and she would look like a fool, and worse than a fool, she would look like a woman who saw patterns in noise, and no one would ever hire her again.

All true. All reasonable. And underneath the reasonableness, humming like the pipe in old Pell's complaint, the truth she actually trusted: *the soil was warm, and the well was sealed with four hundred pounds of stone, and a man who saw what was under it chose silence, and eleven people have no dates on their stones.*

She parked at the site as the last light failed. The floodlights were on, harsh white circles in the dark, and the crew was gathered around the site trailer's folding table eating from foil trays, and the conversation stopped when she came in — not suspiciously, just the way talk stops when someone arrives from elsewhere, from the world of information.

"Find anything?" Delia asked.

Everything, Ruth thought. I found a hundred and thirty years of a family paying for its land in the only coin it had. I found a well that drank people in dry years. I found a contract with a clause that hasn't finished executing.

"Paperwork," she said. "Ownership history, permits. Nothing urgent. The county's slow."

Delia nodded and went back to her tray. Sal made a joke about bureaucrats. Imani asked if she could get a copy of the parcel map because her son liked maps, and Ruth said sure, sure, and smiled, and the smile cost her more than the whole day's work.

Later, alone in her tent with the flap zipped, she transcribed her notes into her laptop and encrypted the file, which was a thing she had never once done in twenty years of fieldwork, and she understood, doing it, exactly what she was becoming. The archivist's phrase came back to her: *if it happened in this county, it's in one of these boxes or it never happened at all.* She had spent today moving eleven people from one category toward the other. She had taken their names out of the dark and then, by silence, put them back.

She lay down. Through the canvas she could hear the site at night: wind in the corn, the generator's drone, Delia's footsteps on her rounds. And beneath it — faint, intermittent, deniable, the kind of sound you could listen for and lose and tell yourself you'd imagined — a rhythm from the direction of the excavation, from the door lying open in its pit of floodlit earth.

Not wind. Wind doesn't swallow.

Ruth reached for her phone to check the recording app she'd installed that afternoon, the one pointed at the pit, and saw that it had captured four hours of audio, and that the waveform, when she scrolled through it, showed something at regular intervals she could not explain and could not stop listening to.

Every eleven minutes, exactly, the recording breathed in.

She did not play it for anyone. That was the decision she made, lying in the dark with the phone glowing against her palm like a held coal — the last decision she would make freely for a long time, though she didn't know it yet. She locked the file, set the phone face-down, and stared at the ceiling of the tent until the breathing on the recording and the breathing in her own ears became impossible to separate.

Outside, far off in the black field, something patient began to hum a tune she almost recognized, and Ruth Okafor, keeper of secrets new and old, pulled her sleeping bag to her chin and told herself the oldest lie in the archaeologist's vocabulary:

*In the morning, I'll tell them. In the morning, I'll say everything.*

The morning, when it came, would bring a drunk man with a crowbar, and it would be too late by an entire century.
