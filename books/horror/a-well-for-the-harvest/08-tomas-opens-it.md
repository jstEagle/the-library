## 8. Tomas Opens It

The whiskey was Delia's fault too, the way Tomas told it, the way he'd keep telling it for the rest of his short acquaintance with the truth. She kept the bottle in the site trailer's bottom drawer, under the invoices and the hard hats, a bottle of rye she'd never once offered him, because Delia Marsh did not offer things to Tomas Marsh. Delia Marsh issued instructions. Delia Marsh signed the checks. Delia Marsh got the farm in the will while he got a handshake and a phone number he was supposed to call if he ever needed anything, as if needing something were a disease he might catch and she'd already washed her hands of him.

He drank it straight from the bottle, sitting on the tailgate of his truck in the blue hour after the crew had gone, with the excavated door lying in its pit below him like a mouth someone had sewn shut and someone else had unpicked, stitch by stitch, all day long.

That was the thing about the door. He couldn't stop looking at it.

The crew had uncovered it at noon — a stone frame set flush into the earth, no wall attached to it, no structure, just the door itself, standing upright in the ground as if the ground had swallowed everything around it and left the one part that mattered. Granite, maybe older. An iron ring set into its face, fused with corrosion so complete it looked less like metal than like a knot of some black wood. Ruth Okafor had knelt at the edge of the pit for an hour with her brushes and her little hand trowel, and when Delia asked her what she thought, Ruth had said a strange thing in her careful, accented voice. She'd said: *The hinge side shows tool marks. The ring side doesn't. Someone sealed this from the inside, and then someone else — later, maybe much later — poured the seal.*

Delia had ordered photos, a halt, a call to the developer. The developer had called back inside ten minutes and used words like *schedule* and *liquidated damages* and *I don't pay you to have feelings about rocks.* Delia had stood in the pit with her phone against her ear, and Tomas had watched her jaw work, watched her swallow whatever she actually felt the way she always had, since they were kids, since their father died, since the will. She'd hung up and said, "We finish the trench around it. Nobody touches the door itself. Nobody."

*Nobody touches the door itself.*

Which was, Tomas understood somewhere below the whiskey and below the anger, in the cold cellar of himself where he kept the things he didn't want to look at, exactly the kind of sentence that gets a man killed in every story his grandmother ever told. But his grandmother had left the farm to Delia too, in spirit if not on paper, and Tomas was done with stories that had no room in them for him.

The evening came down over Hollow Creek like a lid. The sky went the color of a bruise healing. Out past the pit, the remains of the old cornfield — volunteer stalks, decades wild, that the crew was contracted to clear — stood in rows that weren't quite rows, and the wind moved through them with a sound like a crowd settling into seats.

Tomas drank. The rye was good, which made it worse. She'd been holding out on him even her liquor.

He climbed down into the pit because it was shorter, drunker logic, to stand at the door than to keep standing above it. Up close the stone was warm. Not sun-warm — the sun had been gone an hour, and the stone was in shadow, and it was warm anyway, blood-warm, the warmth of something that had been holding its breath for a long time and had just, in the last day or two, dared to start again.

The iron ring was furred with corrosion, but underneath, when he gripped it, he felt the true metal, sound and patient. It didn't rattle. It didn't give. It was as if the ring and the door had grown into each other, one thing now, the way a scar is one thing with the skin.

"Delia," he said out loud, to the pit, to the dark coming down around him, and heard how small his voice sounded, how much it sounded like a boy's voice, twelve years old, standing in a kitchen while their father's lawyer explained the word *bequeath.* "Delia gets to say who touches what. Delia gets the farm. Delia gets the name on everything. Well, hell with it. Hell with her."

He got the pry bar from the truck. A four-foot wrecking bar, steel, the kind of tool that argues with the world and usually wins.

The first pull did nothing. The second did nothing. The third made a sound he would spend the rest of his life trying not to hear — not the groan of rust parting, but something wetter, softer, the sound of a scab lifting, and beneath it a long exhalation that was not wind, because the corn had stopped moving.

The ring came loose in his hands like a tooth.

He stood there holding it, breathing hard, whiskey and sweat and that warm stone smell — loam and iron and something faintly sweet underneath, like corn going over in the field, like honey left too long — and the door just stood there, sealed no longer, and did not open.

Of course it didn't open. Doors open from the inside. Ruth had said so. He'd been standing right there when she said it.

Tomas Marsh, forty-one years old, drunk on his sister's rye with his sister's pry bar in one hand and his sister's farm under his boots, put his shoulder against a door that had been shut for a hundred years and pushed.

It swung inward without a sound. That was the first wrong thing — a door that heavy, that old, should have screamed on its hinges, should have ground against stone, should have announced itself to the whole county. It moved the way a held breath moves when it's finally let go. It moved like it had been waiting, and had gotten good at waiting, and was pleased.

The second wrong thing was the dark. He'd expected a chamber, a root cellar, a tomb — dirt walls, maybe, or older stone, the ordinary dark of any hole in the ground, the dark that ends. What he looked into did not end. His flashlight beam went out and out and simply stopped mattering, the way a shout stops mattering over open water. He threw a clod of dirt in after it, an instinct older than thought.

No echo. The clod went into the dark and the dark kept it. No tap of stone, no soft thud of soil. Nothing came back, and the nothing came back to him like a voice, and he understood — with the clarity that whiskey sometimes grants, the terrible kind — that he had not opened a room.

He had opened a throat.

Tomas climbed out of the pit. He did not remember climbing out of the pit. He remembered standing at the rim afterward, shaking, the pry bar still in his fist, and looking down at the open door below him, and finding that he could not look away, the way you can't look away from a wound, from your own blood, from the thing you've done. The door stood open in the earth and the dark stood in the door and neither of them moved, and the cornfield around the site had gone absolutely still, every stalk at attention, every leaf turned toward him like an ear.

"Delia," he said again, and this time it wasn't an accusation. It was a prayer with the wrong name in it.

He shut the door. He pushed until his palms burned and it swung back to closed as silently as it had opened, and the ring lay in the dirt where it had fallen, and he set it on the stone lip of the frame like a man returning a borrowed cup, and he got in his truck, and he drove to the motel, and he drank the rest of the rye out of a coffee cup and did not sleep so much as lie down inside his own heartbeat and wait for morning.

He would tell her. That was the plan. He'd tell Delia first thing, before the crew, before Ruth, and she'd be furious, and she'd be right, and somehow that was the part he dreaded most — not the thing in the pit, but his sister's face when she understood that he had touched it, that he had opened it, that the first Marsh in a hundred years to lay hands on that door had been the one who got nothing.

He never got the chance.

* * *

Sal Herrera was the first one on site the next morning, because Sal was always the first one on site. He made coffee for the whole crew on a camp burner in the bed of his truck and he hummed while it percolated, off-key, some cumbia his wife loved, and Tomas heard it from across the lot and thought: *I'll tell Delia, and she'll be furious, but Sal will make coffee, and Ruth will take notes, and the world will keep its shape around the mistake, the way it does.*

Except the world didn't.

The morning was wrong from the start. The light came up thin and yellowish, like broth, like light seen through a jar of something. There was dew on everything except the corn. The corn was dry. The corn had been dry for a hundred years, Ruth said later, when they tested it, though it was still standing, still green at the crown, still growing — if you could call it growth, something that drank nothing and never fell.

Delia arrived at seven and Tomas met her at the trailer steps with the whole confession loaded in his mouth, and she looked at him and said, "You look like hell," and he said, "Delia, the door—" and that was when Sal walked past them both.

Not toward the pit. Not toward the porta-johns or the trucks or the trench. Sal walked out toward the cornfield, coffee thermos still in his hand, humming, unhurried, the way a man walks when he's going to check on something he already knows is fine. The dew didn't wet his boots. Tomas noticed that and hated that he noticed it, the way you hate the small clear details that make a thing undeniable.

"Sal," Delia called. "Sal, we're not in that section yet."

Sal turned his head — just his head, the rest of him still walking, still easy — and smiled at her, and it was Sal's smile, the one from the Christmas party, the one from his daughter's quinceañera photos on his phone, and he said, "It's already open, boss. You should see it. You should see how far down the light goes."

And he kept walking.

They shouted. Delia shouted his name the way you shout at a child near a road, and Imani started after him, and Delia caught her arm, and Ruth said, quietly, in her careful voice, "Don't go to him," and nobody asked her why, because nobody wanted the answer, because the answer was already walking away from them between the stalks.

Sal walked into the corn the way a man walks into a lake he trusts, without hesitation, without looking back, and the corn closed behind him, and it did not rustle. That was the thing Tomas would carry, the thing that sat in his chest like the ring had sat in his hand: the stalks didn't move. Sal passed through them and they didn't bend. It was as if the corn parted for him in advance, the way water can't, the way a crowd does, the way — the thought arrived whole and terrible — the way a mouth opens.

Then he was gone. The humming went on a few seconds longer than the man did. That was the worst of it, the humming continuing, off-key and content, after there was no Sal left to hum it, hanging in the still air over the field like heat off a road.

They called his name for an hour. Delia organized it like a search, because Delia organized grief the way she organized everything, into grids and assignments and times, and Tomas watched her do it and understood that the grids were how she kept from screaming, and loved her for it, and hated her for it, in the same beat of his hungover heart.

The cornfield did not give him back. It gave them his thermos at noon, standing upright on the packed earth at the field's edge, in a spot where no one had walked, still warm. Full.

Ruth stood at the edge of the pit all afternoon with her camera and did not take a single picture. When Tomas finally worked up the courage to go to her — because if anyone would tell him what he'd done, it would be her, the one who knew things — she was looking down at the open door, at the dark that didn't echo, and she said, without turning around:

"When did you open it?"

"Last night. I— Ruth, I was drunk, I didn't—"

"The seal was poured from the outside," she said. "Someone came along, years after, and made sure it stayed shut. They used the ring. They poured lead and ash and something else into the housing, and they did it in a hurry, and they did it scared." She finally turned, and her face was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want the answer to. "You broke a hundred-year-old lock on a door that was locked from the inside, Mr. Marsh. Which means whatever was inside when they locked it—" she stopped. She looked out at the corn, at the dry green acres of it, still and attentive. "It's been inside this whole time. We didn't let it in. We let it *out*."

Tomas looked at the door. It stood open in the earth, patient, unhurried, and the dark in it did not move, and the dark in it did not end, and he thought of Sal's smile turning on its neck like a weathervane finding wind, and he thought: *I did that. I opened it. I, Tomas Marsh, who got nothing, who was left nothing, who was so hungry to be owed something that I pried open the one thing on this earth that was owed to no one.*

The sun went down early. He'd swear to that later — that the sun went down a full hour before it should have, and came down the wrong color, the yellow of old paper, of broth, of light through a jar — and as the light failed, every phone on the site rang at once.

Not cell phones. The landline in the trailer, the dead line, the line the utility company had confirmed disconnected in March. It rang with the bright, cheerful, insistent sound of a phone that has all the time in the world.

Delia looked at it. Ruth looked at it. Imani had both hands over her mouth. And Tomas, standing in the doorway of the trailer with the last of the light going out of the corn behind him, heard the ringing resolve — the way ringing will, when you're tired, when you've done what he'd done — into something with a rhythm to it. A cadence. Almost words.

Almost a voice he knew, humming, off-key, patient, from somewhere past the field where the light goes down and doesn't come back.

Delia picked up the receiver, and said, "Sal?" — and from the earpiece, faint and clear and wrong, came the sound of wind moving through corn that had not moved all day.
