## 10. Lockdown

The farmhouse smelled of dust and mouse droppings and something older underneath, something like the inside of a sealed jar. Delia stood in the kitchen doorway with her hand still on the cold iron latch and made herself look at all of it at once: the sagging ceiling, the wallpaper peeling in long tongue-like strips, the window over the sink that looked out on the field where Sal had walked into the corn and not come back.

"Inside," she said. "All of you. Now."

Tomas came first, because Tomas always came first when he was ashamed, shouldering past her with his head down, smelling of bourbon and dirt from the wellhouse. Then Ruth Okafor, moving carefully, her canvas satchel clutched to her chest like a child. Then Imani, who was shaking so hard her hard hat chattered against the doorframe as she passed. Pete Reyes last, big and slow, glancing back at the yard as if the equipment might follow them in.

Delia shut the door and threw the latch and then stood there with her palm flat against the wood, feeling for anything on the other side. Nothing. Just wind, just the ordinary push of October air against an old house. But ordinary had stopped meaning anything about two hours ago, when the light went strange and the distance between the tree line and the fence seemed to shrink every time she wasn't looking directly at it.

"Talk to me," she said, turning around. "Somebody tell me what we actually saw."

Nobody answered. In the corner, the radio they'd brought in from Pete's truck hissed static, and beneath the static — she was almost sure, she was *almost* sure — there was a rhythm, slow and patient, like breathing through cloth.

* * *

They worked the way crews work, which was the only mercy left to them. Delia directed and they obeyed, because muscle memory is a kind of shelter and she intended to use every kind available.

The boards were in the barn, salvaged from a previous job — rough pine planks, a bucket of deck screws, two cordless drills with half a charge between them. They boarded the ground-floor windows first. Delia measured by eye and let the planks run long, crossing each window in an X, because an X felt like something you'd do against vampires and right now she would take vampire rules over whatever rules were operating out there in the corn.

Imani hammered. She hammered harder than the screws required, driving each one home with a violence that had nothing to do with carpentry, and nobody told her to ease up. Her son's name was Marcus. He was seven. He had leukemia, and she'd taken this job because the developer paid a completion bonus that would cover the good trial in Denver. Delia knew this because Imani had told her three weeks ago on the tailgate of the pickup, eating a sandwich, laughing about how her boy wanted to be a paleontologist and a firefighter and a ghost hunter, all three, simultaneously, somehow. Delia thought about that laugh now and drove her own screws until her wrist ached.

Ruth worked differently. Ruth moved through the house touching things — the banister, the doorframes, the cold mouth of the fireplace — with the flat of her fingers, reading it. She paused longest at a small door under the stairs, no bigger than a closet, padlocked from the outside with a lock gone green with age.

"Don't," Delia said, before Ruth could ask.

"I wasn't going to open it."

"You were thinking about it."

Ruth looked at her. In the gray light leaking through the gaps around the boarded windows, her face was careful, scholarly, and underneath the care something frightened and honest. "I think we should know everything about this house," she said quietly. "That's all. Knowing is the only weapon anybody's handed us."

"The only weapon anybody's handed us is a crowbar and a bucket of screws." Delia heard her own voice come out flat and hard, foreman's voice, the voice she used when the trench walls were looking soft. "We board up. We wait for morning. Morning comes, we call the state police, the developer, whoever answers, and we get off this property. That's the plan. The whole plan. Nobody opens anything else."

From the front room, where he'd installed himself in a threadbare armchair with the bottle, Tomas laughed. It wasn't a real laugh. It was the sound a laugh makes when it dies in transit.

* * *

At some point — Delia couldn't have said when; time had begun to move like syrup — she did the count.

It was a thing her father used to do, back when she ran crews for him, back before the will and the silence and the ten years of not speaking. *Count your people,* he'd say. *Every head, twice a day. A foreman who loses track of her people isn't a foreman, she's just the loudest one standing.* She stood in the kitchen and counted, out loud, pointing at each of them like a schoolteacher.

"Tomas."

"Present." Bitter. Drunk. Alive.

"Ruth."

"Here." Cross-legged on the floor with her notebook open, writing by flashlight even though it was afternoon.

"Imani."

A pause too long. Then: "Here." From behind a boarded window, where she'd been looking through the gap between two planks at the field.

"Pete."

"Yo." From the hallway, phone pressed to his ear. "Yo, Delia, my phone's dead. Not dead-dead. It's got battery. There's just — there's no signal. No bars, no nothing. And I got full bars an hour ago."

Five. Five remained, counting herself. Six this morning. Six at lunch, when Sal had complained about the soil being warm and everyone had laughed at him except Ruth, who hadn't been laughing either, Ruth who had known things and kept them folded away in her satchel like letters never sent.

Delia counted again anyway. Twice a day, her father said. She counted four times in ten minutes and got five every time, and the fifth time she caught herself standing outside Imani's door at midnight — if it was midnight, if the gray dimness outside meant midnight — listening to the woman breathe, just to confirm the number.

* * *

The phones were Pete's discovery, and it was the detail that finally broke something loose in Delia's chest, some load-bearing piece of her skepticism she hadn't known she was carrying.

Outgoing calls failed. All of them. Pete tried his wife, Imani tried her mother, Ruth tried the university, Delia tried 911 and got a dial tone that rang on and on into nowhere, a tone with no pulse in it, like a heart beating in a body that had already been embalmed. But incoming calls connected. When Pete's wife called him — they confirmed this later, comparing timestamps — the phone rang, and his voice reached her, and hers reached him. The line worked inward only. The farm would let sound in but not out, like a cupped ear, like a mouth that listened and never spoke.

"That's not how cell networks work," Pete kept saying, pacing the kitchen, holding his phone up toward the ceiling as if signal might pool up there like heat. "That is not — there's no failure mode that does this. Towers don't pick directions. This whole county has towers. I'm telling you, this isn't technical."

"Then what is it?" Imani asked, and her voice cracked down the middle, and nobody answered her, because there was no answer that didn't end in the word none of them would say.

Delia took out her own phone and looked at the screen. One bar, flickering. Enough, maybe, for a text. She typed to her ex-husband, who lived forty miles away and owed her nothing and would still come, she thought, if she asked. She typed: *Something wrong at Hollow Creek site. If you don't hear from me by tomorrow night call the sheriff.* Her thumb hovered over send.

The message sat there. Sending. Sending. The little spinner turned and turned, patient as the thing in the yard.

She put the phone face-down on the table and did not look at it again for a long time.

* * *

It started circling at dusk — if it was dusk; the sky had been the color of dishwater since noon and showed no interest in changing.

Pete saw it first. He was watching through the gap in the boarded kitchen window, and he didn't say anything, just made a sound low in his throat, and Delia crossed the room and put her eye to the gap.

The yard was maybe sixty feet across, brown grass gone to seed, the rusted skeleton of a swing set near the fence line. And along the far edge of it, where the corn began, something was walking. Not emerging. Walking. It had been there the whole time, she realized — it was simply passing, the way a neighbor passes, unhurried, its pace steady and metronomic, following the boundary between yard and field as though patrolling it. As though it owned the patrol.

She couldn't describe it afterward, and hated herself for that. Tall. Wrong in the shoulders. The corn bent away from it as it moved, stalks leaning outward like grass under wind, except there was no wind. It wore the shape of a man the way a scarecrow wears clothes — filled out, but without any insistence on the details. Its head turned once, slowly, and though she could see no eyes, no face, nothing at that distance but a pale suggestion, Delia felt herself *noticed*. Thoroughly. The way you feel a hand on the back of your neck.

She stepped back from the gap. Her heart was going like a fist on a door.

"It's circling," she said. "Everyone away from the windows. Don't — it noticed me looking. Don't give it faces to match."

"What does that mean?" Tomas demanded from the front room. "What does that mean, *match*?"

"It means it's learning us," Ruth said softly, from the floor. She hadn't looked up from her notebook. "Sal walked out. It has Sal now. It knows what we sound like. What we grieve like." She turned a page. Her hand was steady; her voice was not. "In the records I found — the disappearances, 1890s through the thirties — there's a pattern. Families reported knocking. Voices at doors. Things asking to be let in. The ones who opened—" She stopped.

"The ones who opened what?" Imani whispered.

Ruth closed the notebook. "Boarded windows are correct," she said. "Whatever else happens tonight, boarded windows are correct."

* * *

Night fell the way water fills a basement — gradually, then all at once, then deeper than it should. The house creaked through its old vocabulary of settling sounds, and every creak dropped all five of them into the same frozen posture, tools in hand, breath held, counting each other with their eyes.

Delia organized them because organizing was the only prayer she knew. Watches, in pairs. Pete and Imani till whenever, then Ruth and her, then Tomas — though she watched Tomas drain the bottle and doubted he'd be any watch at all. She wedged the crowbar under the kitchen sink where anyone could reach it. She found a shotgun shell box in the barn, empty, and the barn's absence of a gun, and decided not to mention it.

Around what might have been ten o'clock, the circling thing stopped.

They all heard it stop — the faint crunch of its footsteps on dry stalks, which had become a background rhythm none of them admitted to leaning on, simply ceased. The silence after was worse. It was the silence of something that has arrived somewhere and is considering the door.

Then, from the porch, gentle as a knock between friends:

*Rap. Rap. Rap.*

Nobody moved. In the flashlight glow, five faces, five held breaths.

"Miss Marsh?" said the voice on the other side of the front door. It was Sal's voice. Perfectly Sal's voice, warm and tired and reasonable, the voice that had joked about the soil being warm, the voice that had a wife named Carol and a daughter in Sacramento. "It's cold out here, Miss Marsh. Door was already open when I got there. That's what I keep trying to tell everybody. I just want to come in from the cold."

Delia picked up the crowbar.

She crossed the front room floor by feel, past Tomas staring white-eyed from the armchair, past Ruth rising slowly with her flashlight, and stood before the door with her forehead nearly touching the wood, close enough to hear — or imagine she heard — breathing on the other side. Patient. Unhurried. Willing to stand there until the sun burned out.

"We're going to be okay," she said aloud, to her crew, to the dark, to herself, in her father's cadence, count-your-people-twice-a-day. "Morning comes. We hold till morning comes."

Outside, the voice smiled — she heard it smile, heard the shape of it change — and knocked again.

And from the corner of the room, the truck radio they'd forgotten to turn off woke up all by itself, cleared its throat of static, and began, very faintly, to play a hymn.
