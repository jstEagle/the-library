## 13. What the Corn Remembers

The dead did not call back that night. They called in the afternoon.

Casper learned this the way he'd learned everything lately — reluctantly, and against every instinct he'd spent twenty years sharpening into a weapon. He had slept four hours on the studio couch with his shoes on, the playback recorder clutched to his chest like a teddy bear he'd deny owning, and when he woke, the sun was a pale coin behind gauze clouds and the station smelled of burnt coffee and old carpet. He played the tape again before he did anything else. Before coffee. Before checking whether he still had a job.

*Little Sparrow,* said the second voice, breathing between Marlene Duffy's words like wind through a screen door.

He had not been called Little Sparrow since he was nine years old. His father had stopped using it the summer before the bridge, and his mother had never known it existed. It belonged to a kitchen in a house that no longer stood, to a woman who used to hum while she shelled peas, to hands that smelled of loam and dish soap. It belonged to Hollow Creek Farm, which he had never visited, which he had never wanted to visit, and which was apparently the only place left in the world where anyone remembered what to call him.

He sat with that for a long time, watching dust turn in the light from the window, until the phone rang.

The board lit up line one. The caller ID read the same string of digits it always read now — the farm's exchange, the one Ruth Okafor's records said hadn't carried service since 1971. Casper looked at it for eleven rings. Then he answered, because that was the job, because answering was the only thing he'd ever been good at, and because some traitor part of him had already started to hope.

"Open Line," he said. "You're on the air."

"Good afternoon, Casper." Marlene Duffy's voice was clearer in daylight, or maybe he'd just gotten used to it. She sounded like a woman calling from her kitchen about a coupon dispute, patient, faintly amused, unbothered by death in the way people are unbothered by weather. "You slept with the tape recorder. That's sweet. It doesn't help, but it's sweet."

"Doesn't help how?"

"They don't live in your machines, honey. They live in the ground under them. You could burn every wire in this county and they'd still find a way to talk." A pause, and he heard her settle, the creak of a chair that had probably rotted decades ago. "But you already know that. You've known since Tuesday night. So let's skip past the part where you pretend you're still deciding whether to believe me."

Casper pulled his legal pad closer. His hand was steady. He noted that with detached professional interest, the way he'd note a caller's odd diction or an unlikely coincidence — *subject remarkably calm given circumstances; possible dissociation.* "Then tell me what you want, Marlene."

"What I *wanted* was to see my granddaughter graduate. What I *want* now is different." Her voice didn't waver, but something underneath it shifted, like current under ice. "I want to tell you the rules."

"The rules."

"Every covenant has terms. This one has rules. And you're going to need all of them, because you're the heir, and the heir doesn't get to learn as he goes. The heir gets one chance."

Casper wrote *RULES* at the top of the page and underlined it twice. "Start talking."

"Rule one," Marlene said. "It cannot take what is freely kept. Everything it has ever gotten, it got because someone gave it — gave a name, gave a consent, gave a person who wasn't watched closely enough. It's patient. It will wait years for you to hand something over. But it can't reach out and grab. That's not pride. That's law. Older than your deed, older than your country. Understand?"

"It took Sal," Casper said. "Sal walked into the field."

"Sal walked toward the field," she corrected gently. "After Tomas opened the door. Opening the door was a gift. It made everything after that easier, but easier isn't the same as lawful. It still needed Sal to come the last hundred feet on his own feet. And Sal was tired, and scared, and it sounded like his mother calling him home for supper." A silence. When she spoke again her voice had gone quieter, almost tender. "It sounds like whatever you most wish would call you. Remember that. Rule two: it lies, but it lies with true things. Every word it says will be accurate. The lie is in what it leaves out."

Casper thought of the tape. *Little Sparrow.* True. Accurate. His grandmother's voice, or something wearing it perfectly. "And rule three?"

"Rule three is mine, not the covenant's." Marlene laughed then, a real laugh, rusty and warm, and Casper felt it land somewhere below his ribs. "Rule three is: don't trust the dead, Casper. Not even me. We're useful and we mean well and we are also, every one of us, a little bit its now. It's been drinking from us for years. Some nights I can't tell which thoughts are mine and which ones it planted like seed corn." The laugh drained away. "So check everything I tell you. Check it twice. If I ever tell you to open a door, or answer a knock, or walk out into a field because someone you love is standing there — you run the other direction, and you remember that I told you to run."

"Why are you helping me?"

Another pause, longer. He heard her breathe, though he understood now that breath was a courtesy, a habit of the shape she'd worn.

"Because I was invited too," she said. "Forty-one years ago. My husband's family farmed two sections over, and when we married, his mother took my hand at the reception and walked me to the fence line and introduced me. That's all it takes. An introduction. A welcome onto the land. I thought it was a country superstition, a hazing for new brides. I laughed." Her voice thinned. "I've been laughing for forty-one years, Casper, and last Tuesday they put me in the ground six miles from that fence, and I woke up standing in the corn, and I knew exactly whose field I was standing in, and I knew exactly why nobody had ever warned me properly. Because everyone who knew was either dead or too scared to say it out loud."

"So say it out loud now."

"I'm saying it on the radio, honey. To however many people are listening at two in the afternoon on a Wednesday." A dry chuckle. "My granddaughter listens to you sometimes. She thinks you're funny. Tell her—" The voice caught, snagged on something. "Tell her the tomatoes on the south side need less water than she thinks. She'll know what that means. Nobody else will."

Casper wrote it down anyway. *Tomatoes. South side. Less water.*

Reverend Amos Pell called at 4:47 p.m., during the news break, and Casper took him off the air without asking permission from anyone, including Amos.

"You received Marlene's instruction," Pell said. His voice was different from Marlene's — older cadence, formal, each word placed like a stone in a wall. He sounded the way Casper imagined sermons sounded in 1920, when fire-and-brimstone was a craft and not an insult. "Good. She was always sensible. Now attend, because I have less patience and less time, and both of those facts should frighten you more than they appear to."

"Less time? You're dead. What's your deadline?"

"Sunrise," Pell said simply. "Yours, specifically. There is a dawn coming that belongs to you, boy, and if you are not standing at that door when it arrives, the choice stops being yours. The covenant does not require your willingness forever. It requires it once, at the appointed hour, and if you fail to appear, willingness is presumed. It has been presumed for four generations of your family." A pause. "Your great-grandfather Elias did not appear. Your grandfather Josiah fled to California and died there believing distance was protection. Your father—"

"Don't." The word came out of Casper like a splinter coming out sideways. "Don't you talk about my father."

Silence on the line. Long enough that Casper checked the connection light. Still green. Still the impossible number.

"He was a kind man," Pell said finally, "and a frightened one, and he chose the only exit the covenant leaves open to a coward, which is to say he removed himself from the ledger the crude way, and the debt passed over him to you. I am not speaking ill of him. I am telling you that you have inherited a door, and doors do not care how you feel about them."

Casper stared at the ON AIR sign, dark now, and at the city beyond the window going gold and gray with evening traffic, ten thousand people driving home to houses where nothing knocked. "What breaks the rules?" he asked. "Marlene gave me the rules. Give me the failures. What do heirs do wrong?"

"Everything," said Amos Pell, with something horribly like a smile in it. "They hesitate. They negotiate. They try to substitute — send another in their place, offer money, offer blood, offer some poor stranger off the street. The covenant wants the heir. Substitution voids nothing; it merely adds a name to the wall and leaves the debt standing. They try to destroy the door." He actually chuckled. "Elias's brother took a sledgehammer to it in 1931. The hammer broke. The man who swung it was harvesting by morning. They try to wait it out, thinking the thing will starve. It has waited longer than your language has existed, child. It invented waiting."

"And the ones who succeeded? Did anyone ever end it?"

The silence this time was different. Not dramatic. Grieved.

"No," Pell said. "No one has succeeded. Four generations, eleven souls from this farm alone, and others besides — the covenant traveled with the family when they moved, you understand, it is attached to the blood as much as the soil, though the soil is where it feeds best. No one has ended it. But no one has ever done the one thing that might." He lowered his voice, and the radio speaker crackled as if the signal were straining to carry the words across ninety years. "Refusal. Open refusal, spoken aloud, at the threshold, by the heir, naming what he loves more than the land. The original terms permit it. I have read the deed. I read it in 1922, when I was still a living fool who thought a parish boundary meant something to a thing like that. The clause exists. It has never once been used, because every heir who reached the door found something waiting there wearing the face of everyone they'd buried, offering them exactly what they'd always wanted, and love curdles into appetite at a threshold, boy. Love becomes *keep*. Keep becomes the tithe."

"So what do I do differently?"

"That," said Reverend Amos Pell, "is the question that has cost eleven lives to ask, and I do not have the answer. I know only this: the thing below is hungry, but hunger is not malice. It was bound to this work before your family ever crossed the ocean. It serves the covenant as much as you do. It is as trapped as the dead in its field." His voice dropped to nearly nothing. "Pity it, if you can manage it. Pity is the one coin it has never been paid, and I think — I have thought, these long decades in the corn — that pity might be the one thing it cannot digest."

The line went to static. Then, faintly, beneath the static, singing — dozens of voices, wordless, rising and falling in a slow minor cadence that Casper recognized with a full-body flinch as the hymn from the tape, the harvest hymn, the one his grandmother used to hum while she shelled peas.

He killed the line. Sat in the darkening studio. Wrote at the bottom of the legal pad, in letters pressed hard enough to score the page:

*PITY. IT HAS NEVER BEEN PAID PITY.*

Ruth Okafor arrived at the station at nine that night, smelling of rain and diesel and old paper, carrying a document box hugged to her chest like a rescued infant. Delia Marsh was with her, and behind them, hovering in the doorway with the sheepish posture of a man attending his own sentencing, came Tomas.

"We saw the broadcast," Ruth said, without preamble. "Last night. The open mic. My sister-in-law in Lagos called me at four in the morning because she heard our grandmother's name in the static." She set the box on his desk. "I'm done keeping things from people. Here's everything."

"Sit down," Casper said. "All of you. Because I've got something too, and you're going to want to be sitting."

They sat. Delia Marsh had a crowbar laid across her knees like a scepter, and she held it through everything that followed, and Casper decided not to comment on it. Tomas wouldn't look at his sister. Ruth opened the box and began laying out documents — deeds, ledgers, a church register, photographs gone sepia and soft.

Casper told them about Marlene's rules. About Pell's warnings. About pity. He watched their faces as he spoke, and it was Delia's face that undid him — this hard, competent woman who'd boarded her crew into a farmhouse and counted heads like a sergeant, and when he said *it sounds like whatever you most wish would call you*, her jaw tightened and her eyes went wet and furious and she stared at the floor the whole rest of the paragraph.

When he finished, Ruth slid a photocopied page across the desk. The deed. The covenant clause, circled in her own red ink.

"*Answering the door in person,*" she quoted. "*Naming what he loveth more than the land.* It's real, Casper. It's been real the whole time. Your family signed this in 1887."

"My family signed a lot of things in 1887," Casper said. "That's what families do. They sign things, and then eighty years later their descendants find out what the signatures cost." He rubbed his eyes. Behind him, the phone board lit up — line one, line two, line five, the impossible number blooming across every channel at once, and beneath the fluorescent hum of the studio he could hear, very faintly, coming up through the floor, through the foundations, through the whole skeleton of the building, the sound of singing.

Delia stood. The crowbar came up off her knees.

"How long until sunrise?" she asked.

Casper checked his watch, and then checked it again, because the hour was wrong — later than it should be, much later, the evening having folded itself away while none of them were looking. Outside the window, the sky over the city was the color of deep water, and the streetlights had come on though he couldn't remember dusk arriving.

"Six hours," he said. "Maybe less. Maybe less than that."

Line one was ringing. He picked it up, because answering was the only thing he'd ever been good at, and because six hours was not enough time to become good at anything else.

"Open Line," he said. "You're on the air."

The voice that came through was Sal's. Calm. Patient. Standing, it seemed, very close to a microphone buried somewhere under the world.

"Casper," it said warmly. "Come on out. We saved you a seat at the table."
