## 16. The Well

Tomas Marsh had been drinking since noon, which was a lie he told himself the way other men told themselves they'd only had two beers. It had been noon when he cracked the first can in the cab of his truck, parked at the far end of the equipment lot where Delia wouldn't see it, and it was now — he checked his phone, and the screen said 4:47 p.m., though the light through the clouds said something closer to dusk, or to nothing at all — and there were eleven empty cans in the footwell, arranged with a drunk's terrible precision, like offerings.

He wasn't hiding from her because of the beer. He was hiding from her because of everything else.

The farmhouse windows were boarded now, all of them, Delia's orders, hammered shut with plywood they'd torn from the site fencing. Five of them left in the house: Delia, Ruth Okafor, Imani, old Pete Kowalski who hadn't spoken a full sentence since Sal walked into the field, and Tomas himself, sleeping on a cot in the parlor like a guest at a funeral that refused to end. Phones worked inward. Calls out went nowhere — not dead air, but worse, a soft wet silence, like holding the receiver against someone's closed mouth.

And last night, Imani had almost opened the door for Sal's voice.

Tomas sat in the truck and drank and watched the wellhouse across the yard.

It was a small stone building, maybe eight feet square, older than the farmhouse by decades if you read its masonry right, which Ruth had tried to do once before Delia told her to stop cataloguing and start helping board windows. Its roof had half fallen in years ago; saplings grew from the gutter line, thin and pale, their leaves turned even though it was July. The door hung open on one hinge. Inside, everyone knew, was the well itself — dry as bone, the crew had found it the first week, before the door in the field, before any of this. A round stone throat dropping down into dark. They'd dropped a flashlight down it on a lark during lunch break, back when they still had larks, and heard it hit water far below.

Except the farm's water table was forty feet up. Everyone knew that too. The county survey said so. The flashlight had fallen a hundred feet, easy, before the splash.

Tomas had thought about that splash every day since.

* * *

He got out of the truck because the beer had reached the stage where sitting still felt like drowning slowly, and walking felt like the only honest thing left. He didn't decide to go to the wellhouse. His legs decided, and he followed them the way you follow a dog that smells something you can't yet.

The grass between the lot and the wellhouse was wrong. He noticed it without wanting to notice it, the way you notice a room has gotten colder. It grew in a spiral. Not bent by wind — wind would have laid it flat, all one direction — but curved, each blade leaning slightly toward the center of the yard, toward the wellhouse, the way iron filings lean toward a magnet under a sheet of paper. His boots made no sound crossing it. He looked down twice to check that he was actually stepping on anything.

The doorway swallowed him.

Inside, the air was warm. Not stuffy-warm, not closed-building warm. Warm like skin. Warm like the inside of a mouth, and Tomas stood in the little stone room breathing it and felt his stomach turn over with a nausea that had nothing to do with the beer. There was a smell too, faint, underneath the dust and the green rot of the saplings: cut corn, and under that, sweeter and fouler, something like the inside of a silo in August, fermentation gone long past alcohol into something else.

The well mouth was exactly where he remembered it. A ring of fitted stones, three feet across, flush with the floor, no lip, no curb, no railing — just an opening in the earth like a wound that had healed over wrong. Someone had laid a sheet of plywood over it weeks ago, standard safety, and the plywood was still there, still weighted with two concrete blocks.

But the blocks had been moved. Neatly. Set aside side by side, like shoes removed at a door.

Tomas crouched at the edge, careful, drunk-careful, one hand flat on the cold stone floor, and looked down.

The dark went down and down and did not stop.

He fumbled his phone out, thumbed the flashlight on, and aimed it into the shaft. The beam was a good one — six hundred lumens, Delia had bought them all the same model — and it should have lit the walls to thirty, forty feet, should have shown him the curve of the stones, the mineral streaks, whatever lived in a hole like this. Instead the light just *ended*, a few feet down, cut off clean as if the beam had struck black velvet. The wall of the shaft caught the glow for about eight feet and then simply declined to exist below that.

"Okay," Tomas said aloud, because saying things aloud had become a habit in this place, a way of proving your voice still worked, that the air would still carry it. "Okay, sure."

His voice fell into the hole and did not echo.

That was the thing he'd never been able to explain to anyone, the thing that kept him awake on the cot while Pete snored and Imani cried quietly into her jacket. Sound should bounce. Any hole bounces sound; he'd spent twenty years on job sites, shouted down culverts and foundation pits and elevator shafts for the pure boyish pleasure of hearing his own voice come back doubled and strange. This hole took sound the way the ground takes rain. It went down and was used.

He leaned closer. The warmth rose up out of it against his face, gentle, rhythmic, and after a moment he understood with a lurch that it was rising and falling in a rhythm, slow and vast, and that rhythm was breathing.

*Don't*, said some sober remnant of him, small and clear, like a voice down a hallway. *Go get Delia. Go get Ruth. Don't do what you're already doing.*

But he was already doing it. He was already reaching for the plywood, dragging it aside, the concrete blocks scraping, and the sound of the scrape fell straight down into the dark and was eaten, and somewhere below — far below, or very close, distance meant nothing in that throat — something adjusted itself the way a sleeper adjusts, making room.

There was a ladder. Of course there was; there always is, in these old places, iron rungs set into the mortar, rusted to lace but holding, because everything in this valley held longer than it should. He saw the first rung at knee height, then the next below it, descending into the cut-off dark where his flashlight gave up.

Tomas Marsh was thirty-eight years old. He had a DUI in 2019 and another pending. He had a sister who got the farm in the will and a mother who'd died calling her name instead of his, and he had a phone with four missed calls from a woman named Carla who used to love him, and he had eleven empty cans in a truck at the edge of a yard where the grass leaned toward a hole in the earth. He was not a brave man. He was not even, most days, a particularly functional one.

He put his boot on the first rung anyway.

Later — much later, in the cellar, in the dark, in the part of this story that came after — he would try to explain it to Delia, and the only true thing he could say was: *it felt like going home.* Not wanted. Not summoned. Just that the descent had the texture of memory, of a staircase his body had climbed ten thousand times, so that his hands knew the rungs before they touched them, and the dark rising around him felt less like entering a place than returning to one.

* * *

He counted rungs because counting was all he had.

At twenty, the warmth was total, a bath of it, and sweat ran down his spine and his shirt clung and still he descended. At forty, past the depth where the county survey swore the water table lived, past the depth where the flashlight should have splashed, the shaft changed character. The stones became smoother. Then they became carved.

He stopped at rung fifty-something — he'd lost exact count around the point where terror and beer met and cancelled each other out into a kind of lucid calm — and held the phone light against the wall, and the wall held still for it this time, allowed itself to be seen, and Tomas felt the calm drain out of him like water through a cracked tank.

Names.

The walls were covered in names.

Carved, chiseled, cut deep and shallow, crowded and spaced, column after column after column, ascending and descending beyond the reach of his light in both directions. Some were crude, scratched with a nail or a knife by shaking hands. Some were fine, professional work, serifed and confident, done by masons who charged by the letter. Some were so old the letters had worn to suggestions, ghosts of alphabet. And some were fresh.

He found the fresh ones at eye level, because of course he did, because the well wanted him to find them, and the newest of them glistened.

Wet-cut stone. He knew it from gravestone yards, from monument shops, from a summer in his twenties setting markers in a cemetery outside Scranton. Fresh-cut granite weeps; the stone holds moisture from the cutting and gives it up slowly, and for a few days the incised letters look dark, damp, newly alive. These letters gleamed like that. Like they'd been cut within the hour. Like the stone itself was bleeding lymph, or like something below was licking them.

He read them downward, the columns running in rough chronological order, and the names were ordinary and that was the horror of them, that was the whole horror of the entire valley distilled into a list: HANNAH VASQUEZ, 1891. ELIAS VASQUEZ, 1903. A cluster of names from the teens and twenties with dates that shared months, that shared *weeks*. Names with no dates at all, later, as if the carver had grown tired of pretending the dates mattered. Names in a child's unsteady hand. Names that repeated — the same name cut twice, sixty years apart, and Tomas understood with a slow cold turning in his gut that it wasn't a coincidence, that the farm took what it was owed and it was owed the same families, generation after generation, the way a bank is owed interest.

VASQUEZ. PELL. DUFFY. MARSH.

He stopped on MARSH.

His thumb found it before his eyes did, tracing the groove in the dark, and the groove was old, decades old, and the name above it was his mother's maiden name and the name beside it was an aunt nobody spoke of, the aunt who'd "moved away" in 1978, except she hadn't moved anywhere, she was right here, cut into a wall four hundred feet beneath a cornfield, and Tomas pressed his forehead against the cool stone between the letters and breathed the wet-stone breath of the place and felt — this was the worst part, this was the part he would never tell anyone — *welcomed.*

The stone was warm where the names were fresh. Warm like skin. He pulled his hand back.

Keep going, said the sober voice again, fainter now, further away, as if it were the one down the hole and he were the voice in the dark. Keep counting. Counting keeps you you.

So he counted. Sixty rungs. Eighty. The shaft widened gradually, the spiral of it opening like a throat relaxing, and the names kept coming, and the air kept breathing, that vast slow tidal rhythm, in and out, in and out, and somewhere below there was a sound now that might have been water and might have been whispering and was probably both, was probably the same thing here, was probably what passed for speech in a place like this.

At a hundred and ten rungs — he made himself believe the count, needed the count — his light caught the bottom of the current column of names, the freshest section, the wet ones, and he read them top to bottom the way you read anything, top to bottom, and the last complete name was:

SALVATORE BRUNI.

Cut clean. Finished. Dry at the edges already, aging fast, becoming history even as Tomas looked at it.

Below Sal's name, half-finished, the chisel-work trailing off mid-letter, the stone still weeping around the incomplete strokes:

TOM

The fourth letter was a suggestion. An S begun and abandoned, a single diagonal groove, as if the carver had been interrupted. As if the carver were waiting for something. As if the carver worked only when it could see its subject clearly, and right now, a hundred and ten rungs down in the breathing dark, it could see him perfectly, and had paused — patient, unhurried, the way the thing circled the farmhouse yard, the way it did everything — with the chisel lifted.

Tomas's phone light flickered.

He looked at the half-name for a long time. Long enough that the wet stone seemed to move, seemed to crawl, seemed almost to be writing itself further while he watched, though he knew — he told himself he knew — that was the beer, that was the dark, that was a man's mind folding under weight it was never built for.

Then he did the bravest thing he had ever done in his life, and it was small, and it was stupid, and it was this: he reached out and touched the unfinished S, one fingertip in the wet groove.

The stone read him back.

There was no other way to say it, then or ever. Something on the far side of that wall paid attention to him — fully, totally, the attention of a thing that had waited a century behind a sealed door and had all the time in the world and intended to use it — and Tomas felt himself being taken inventory of, weighed, measured, priced. Every debt. Every failure. Every name he'd ever failed to earn. And underneath all of it, gentle as a hand on the back of the neck, unmistakable as a signature:

*Almost done.*

Tomas climbed.

He climbed the way animals climb, hands and boots, no thought, the phone clenched in his teeth so the light bounced wild off the carved names streaming upward past his face — hundreds of them, thousands, generations of the owed — and the breathing air rushed down past him, exhaled now, amused, unhurried, and the whisper-water sound below him rose into something that was almost words, almost a hymn, almost his own name sung by a congregation of the cut.

Daylight, when it came, was gray and thin and came from the wrong direction, and he hauled himself out of the well mouth onto the plywood-less floor of the wellhouse and lay there gasping like a landed fish, and the warm air of the little stone room curled around him, satisfied.

Across the yard, the farmhouse windows were boarded and blind. Beyond them, out in the field, the corn stood taller than it had that morning. Taller than it had ever stood in July. And between the rows, at the far edge of seeing, something walked toward the wellhouse — patient, unhurried — carrying, in the last gray light, what looked very much like a chisel.

Tomas scrambled backward until the well mouth was at his back and understood, with perfect drunken clarity, that he had made a mistake no drink could excuse:

He'd shown it his face. Now it could finish the letter.
