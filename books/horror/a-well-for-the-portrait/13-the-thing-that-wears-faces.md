## 13. The Thing That Wears Faces

The singing stopped at midnight.

Wendell had been counting the hours by it — the way you count by a dripping tap or a neighbor's dog — and when it cut off between one verse and the next, the silence was worse. It was the silence of something finishing. Of an audience settling.

She stood at her window at the inn with the lights off and watched the village below. The last of the house lights had gone out an hour ago, all except one: the curate's window, where a candle burned steady as a pilot light. Somewhere down the lane a dog began to bark, thought better of it, and stopped.

*Come down,* the dark below the valley seemed to say, in no voice at all. *You were invited too.*

She did not come down. Not then. She sat on the edge of the bed with her recorder in her lap like a talisman that had never once worked, and she told herself every rational thing she knew, and none of them held weight anymore. Rationality was a boat, and the boat had a hole in it, and the water coming through was warm and patient and knew her mother's name.

At three in the morning she dressed, took the ledger from under the mattress where she'd hidden it like a child hiding contraband, and went out into the cold.

Not to flee. She'd tried fleeing two nights ago; the roads had laughed at her, looping her gently back to the wellhead as if she were a marble rolling around the rim of a bowl. There was no leaving Harrow's Bend until whatever was owed was paid. So if she could not leave, she would go down and look at the creditor.

That was the story she told herself, anyway. The other story — the one she kept in the locked room behind her eyes — was that some part of her had been walking toward the shaft since the bus dropped her at the bend, and tonight it simply finished the walk.

* * *

The excavation site looked different abandoned. Floodlights still stood in their tripods, dead-eyed. A hard hat sat upside down in the mud, filling slowly with rain. The generator had been left running — someone had meant to come back — and its idle throbbed through the ground like a second heartbeat, arrhythmic, wrong, as though the earth itself had learned to pulse from watching men.

The well shaft gaped at the center of the site, ringed in orange barrier fencing that someone had cut open and folded back with terrible courtesy, like a bedsheet turned down for a guest.

Wendell stepped over it.

The cold came up out of the shaft the way cold comes out of a walk-in freezer, except freezers don't breathe, and this did. Long slow exhalations, each one carrying a smell she couldn't place at first and then could: old water, yes, but also candle smoke, and under that something sweeter and more awful — the smell of a room where many people have lived and none have left. Attic air. Preserved air.

She knelt at the lip and shone her torch down.

Stone steps spiraled into blackness, slick with condensation. Twenty feet down, the light gave up. But not before it caught something: a handprint on the wall, pressed into pale mineral bloom, small, five-fingered, human. And beside it another. And another. Dozens of them, descending, a frieze of farewells left by everyone who had ever walked down these stairs and understood they were not walking back up.

"Elowen," Wendell said aloud, just to hear a voice that was hers.

"You say that name like it's yours."

The voice came from inside the shaft. It came from below, and it came from everywhere the cold touched, and it was — this was the part that made her sit down hard in the mud — it was her own voice. Not an imitation. Hers, with her flat vowels and her habit of clipping the ends of sentences, the voice she heard on playback of her own field recordings and always flinched at.

"Who's there?" she managed. Stupid question. She knew who was there. Every interview she'd ever conducted had prepared her for exactly this moment and helped her not at all.

"Everyone," said the shaft, in her voice. "Eventually."

And then it began to rise.

* * *

It did not climb. Climbing implies effort, limbs, negotiation with gravity. It rose the way a bubble rises through oil — smoothly, without hurry, as though the shaft were doing the moving and the thing merely occupied the space being offered.

Her torch found it in pieces.

A shoulder first, broad, wearing the foreman's high-visibility vest, the fabric real, mud-stained, torn at the seam where — she remembered now, with a lurch — the foreman had snagged it on the barrier fence the day they'd struck the door. Then a hand resting on the stair rail, and the hand was Maren's, spotted and knuckled, and above the wrist the arm changed mid-forearm into something younger, smoother, sexless, the color of drowned things.

Then the face.

It wore Elowen's face the way a museum wears a painting — displayed over something else, mounted on a surface that was not a skull. The portrait's woman: the same long jaw, the same deep-set eyes, the same faint scar through the left eyebrow that Wendell had touched every morning of her life in the mirror without knowing why. But the paint had been accurate about everything except aliveness. Behind those painted eyes there was nothing looking out. The face was a lampshade. The light inside it was absent, and the absence was somehow brighter than any presence could have been.

Where the body should have continued past the shoulders, it didn't continue so much as *suggest*. Ranks of faces pressed at the skin like coins under cloth — the innkeeper's heavy brow surfacing at the collarbone, a child's profile dimpling the forearm, the curate's thin lips ghosting across the back of one hand. It was assembled from the taken, and it was still assembling, and Wendell understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the only reason it wasn't finished was that it was saving a place.

There was a gap at its throat. A smooth, expectant hollow, shaped — she made herself see it — shaped like the lower half of her own face.

"Hello, Wendell," it said, in her voice, with Elowen's mouth.

"I'm not talking to you." Her own voice came out sanded down to gravel. "I'm talking to whoever's left in there. If anyone is."

Something moved behind the borrowed face. Not hope. Hope has warmth. This was more like a draft through a ruined house — the memory of habitation stirring in rooms that had been empty for two hundred years.

"They're here," the thing said kindly. "All of them. I keep what I wear. Ask them anything." It tilted Elowen's head, a gesture of such perfect human curiosity that her stomach turned. "Ask your mother how she liked the tune I hummed for you. She wrote it for your fourth birthday. She never told you that, did she? She thought you'd forgotten. You hadn't. You recorded it off her humming in 2011, file forty-one on your old drive, and you've never known why it made you cry."

"Stop."

"It's a lovely tune," it said, in her mother's voice now, gentle and off-key the way her mother had been. "I sing it better than she did. I've had practice."

Wendell got to her feet because sitting felt like agreeing. The mud sucked at her knees. The generator throbbed. The thing hung half-out of the shaft, patient as architecture.

"What do you want?"

"What I've always wanted. What your family promised me." Elowen's smile was a fixed thing, painted on, but the voice modulated — softened — became reasonable, became almost tender. "Completion. The covenant isn't cruelty, Wendell. It's arithmetic. I was invited. Invitation is a door, and doors want to be used. Every lowering, I reach up and touch the world for one night — voices, faces, a little singing — and then I go back down and wait. Two hundred years of waiting. Do you know what that is, for something like me? It's starvation with good manners."

"And the reservoir broke the seal early."

"The reservoir cracked the shell. Your curate's ancestors built the seal; your county council drilled through it for a pump housing." The painted face managed something like amusement. "Your species is very good at digging. Very bad at asking what's underneath."

"So the tithe falls due. One chosen."

"One chosen." It let the words hang. Rain began again, fine and needling, and the drops that fell toward the thing bent away from it slightly, as if embarrassed. "But here is what the village won't tell you, because they don't know it, because Elowen wrote it plainly and they've spent two centuries mistaking a contract for scripture." It extended Maren's spotted hand, palm up, an offer, a greeting, a beggar's cup. "The ritual doesn't require *you*, Wendell Thornbury. It requires *a willing vessel*. Any willing vessel. That's the whole of the law. Willingness is the hinge. It has always been the hinge."

The cold breathed out of the shaft and wrapped her like a coat.

"You're lying," she said.

"I have never once lied," it said, and the terrible thing was that it sounded wounded. "Lying is a human technology. I offer terms. Here are mine: walk away. Turn around, go back to your inn, sleep until dawn. At dawn I rise, and I take this village — all of it, every face, every voice, every name chalked on every door — and I go out into your wide world wearing them, and I will be *magnificent*, Wendell, I will be cities — and you will live. Whole. Untouched. Free. You'll write your book. You'll win things. You'll grow old with your scar and your mother's tune and no one will ever know what you traded for it, because I keep my bargains and I keep my silence."

"No."

"You haven't heard the rest." The hollow at its throat seemed to widen, very slightly. "Or: complete the ritual. Any vessel. The curate has already volunteered — did he tell you? He knelt at this shaft yesterday afternoon and offered himself with tears in his eyes, and do you know why? Because he thinks sacrifice will make him important. He wants to be the man who saved Harrow's Bend. He wants his name in the vestry glass. Even his devotion is currency, Wendell. Everyone's is. Yours most of all."

"I wouldn't consent. Whatever the rite needs, I'd never—"

"Not you." The voice dropped, and it used her own voice to do it, which was the cruelest instrument it owned. "Anyone willing. Think, scholar. Think like the folklorist you pretend to be instead of the daughter of the founders you actually are. Who in this valley is old enough to be tired? Who has been carrying guilt for fifty years like a stone in her chest? Who promised me, half a century ago, that she would deliver the founder's return — and has spent every lowering since waiting for you to arrive?"

The rain fell straighter now. The generator throbbed. Somewhere in the dark of the shaft, far below, dozens of voices murmured her name in unison, softly, the way a congregation murmurs *amen*.

"Maren," Wendell whispered.

"Maren," it agreed, in Maren's voice, warm as bread. "Fifty years ago she bought her life with a promise. The promise comes due tonight. She knows it. She's awake right now, sitting up in her kitchen, waiting to hear whether you'll let her pay. She'll say yes, Wendell. She'll say it gratefully. Forty years of guilt makes willingness very easy to find." The painted face leaned closer, and close up Wendell could see that the oil-paint texture of it moved, subtly, like skin remembering paint. "One word from you and it's done. The old rite, the true rite, completed as written. I go back down. The seal holds for another age. The village keeps its children, its dogs, its dull sweet lives. And you walk out of this valley tomorrow morning with your book and your name and your face, and you will have saved everyone, and cost no one —"

"—except Maren."

"—except a woman who has been dying of guilt since before you were born, and who will thank you with her last borrowed breath."

Wendell's hands were shaking. She hated that. She hated more that the shaking wasn't only fear. Some cold practical clerk deep in her skull was already weighing it: one against four hundred. A guilty volunteer against a stolen town. The arithmetic was clean. The arithmetic was *seductive*, and it knew it was seductive, and it had been composed for her specifically, tuned to her frequency, pitched by a thing that had listened to her voice for two hundred years the way she listened to field recordings.

"That's the bait," she said quietly. "Isn't it. The trade isn't really Maren or the village. The trade is that whichever choice I make, I make it *willingly*. You need my willingness for something. That's why I'm the chosen. That's why the roads loop. That's why you learned my mother's tune." She took a step back from the lip, mud sliding under her boots. "You don't want a vessel. You want my *yes*."

For the first time, the thing went still.

Stillness on it was worse than motion. The faces beneath the skin stopped pressing. Elowen's painted eyes fixed on her, and in them, for one flickering instant, Wendell saw not absence but appetite — vast, ancient, and suddenly, unmistakably impatient.

"Careful, Wendell," it said, and now the voice was layered, hers under her mother's under Maren's under the foreman's under a hundred others, a chord of the taken. "Cleverness kept Elowen alive for nine days after she went down those stairs. Nine days of clever questions. I remember them fondly." The hollow at its throat pulsed once, like a throat clearing, like a mouth remembering itself. "But cleverness doesn't change the terms. Dawn is in ninety minutes. The village is coming to this shaft whether you speak or not — they're already walking, all of them, in the rain, in their nightclothes, gentle as lambs, certain they're delivering mercy. When they arrive, someone goes down. The only question left in this valley tonight is *who consents*."

It began to sink, unhurried, folding back into the dark like a hand closing.

"But you should know," it added, her own voice rising up the shaft like heat, "that I've read your ledger. All of it. Including the page Elowen tore out and hid in the binding — the page with the words of the rite. And I should tell you, scholar to scholar, as one keeper of archives to another—" the painted face smiled its fixed smile from the gathering darkness, "—the rite requires willingness. It does not require *honesty*."

Then it was gone, and the shaft breathed out one last long exhalation that smelled of candle smoke and attics, and Wendell stood alone at the wellhead in the rain with her heart slamming and her mind racing, because she had caught it — she was almost sure she had caught it — a flaw in the contract, a door left unlocked, and the thing had just shown her the lock and dared her, with its own borrowed patience, to try the handle.

Behind her, down the lane, the first of the villagers appeared in the rain. Silent. Gentle. Carrying lanterns.

Walking toward the well.
