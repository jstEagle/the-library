## 15. Ritual of the Second Sealing

The ledger was where she'd left it, but it had changed.

Wendell stood in the vestry doorway with the oil lamp raised and understood, in the way you understand a room has been searched — not by evidence but by atmosphere — that someone else had been here. The Elowen Thornbury ledger sat on the reading desk exactly as she'd placed it that morning, spine aligned with the desk's edge, but the dust around it had been disturbed in a wide arc, like something large had leaned over it. Or breathed on it.

She didn't touch it at first. She set the lamp down, pulled the vestry door shut behind her, and slid the bolt — an old iron thing, stiff, screaming faintly on its track. The curate's chalked names were still visible in her mind's eye: forty-one slips of paper pinned inside his door, and hers among them, written not in the curate's hand but in handwriting she recognized from her own field notebooks. Her own hand. Written before she'd ever arrived.

*One chosen by the thing itself.*

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty vestry, because saying things aloud had always been how she kept herself together. "Right."

She opened the ledger.

The early pages she'd already read — the famine year, the summons, the terrible arithmetic of the first tithe. She turned past them now, past the entries in Elowen's careful copperplate, past the decades of names recorded in two columns: *given* and *forgotten*. Past 1850, past 1900, past the war years when the ink changed hands and the columns grew sparse and then dense again, as if the thing below had learned patience during the shortages and hungered after.

The binding rite began on the page dated the third of October, 1798.

It wasn't what she expected. She'd braced for Latin, for sigils, for the baroque apparatus of folk magic she'd spent her career cataloguing — iron nails, black thread, the names of God folded into acrostics. Instead there was only this, in Elowen's hand, the letters growing larger toward the bottom of the page as though written by someone losing control of their grip:

*There is no rite. There is only consent.*

*It cannot take what is given freely, and it cannot refuse what is given freely. This is the shape of the covenant and I believe — I have come to believe — it is the shape of the thing itself. It does not want blood. Blood is nothing to it. It wants the door opened from this side, by a hand that chooses to open it.*

*So the binding is this: a Thornbury must stand at the threshold and consent. Not to die. It will be more precise than that, because precision is all we have left. You must consent to stay. To remain behind the door, in its company, for as long as the door stands sealed. Your willingness is the lock. Your body is the counterweight. While one of us remains within, willing, it cannot pass the threshold wearing any face but those it has already taken.*

*And it has taken so many faces already.*

Wendell read the paragraph three times. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above her, in the church proper, a beam settled with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

She kept reading.

*I write this so that no daughter of mine is lied to, as I was lied to. My father told me the binding would free the vessel after seven years. He told me the door could be opened again once the thing slept. He said these words to me at the threshold with tears in his eyes, and I believed him, and I went through willingly, and the door closed, and I am writing this on the other side of it.*

*Understand: there is no afterward. There is no term of service. The consent does not expire because the thing does not sleep — it only waits, and listens, and wears the faces of the given while it waits, and every year the lowering feeds it enough to keep it patient rather than hungry. The vessel stays. The vessel stays until the door is broken or until there is no vessel left to stay, and both of those are the same catastrophe.*

*But hear the rest, because the rest matters. Willingness is the whole of the mechanism. Not love. Not duty. Not sacrifice — sacrifice is a word men use to make a woman's consent sound noble. Willingness. If you stand at that threshold and your yes is false — if some part of you holds back, if your mouth says the words while your heart keeps a door of its own open — the binding fails. It will know. It knows the texture of a mind the way we know the texture of cloth. A false yes is worse than no yes. A false yes is an invitation with the latch broken.*

*So whoever comes after me must mean it. Entirely. That is the cruelty my father built into our family and never had to pay for himself. Someone must walk to that door wanting to stay, and they must keep wanting it in the last second, in the dark, with the thing's breath on their face and the voices of everyone they have loved calling them deeper.*

*I have been here four months. I am still willing. I do not know how long I can remain so. Pray for the successor, whoever she is. Pray that she comes to the door honest.*

*— E.T., from within*

The page ended there. Wendell realized she was standing. She didn't remember standing. Her legs had made the decision without consulting her, some old mammalian logic insisting that if the document was going to say things like *from within*, she should at least be upright for it.

She sat back down. Made herself sit.

*From within.* Two hundred years, and the ink hadn't faded — she noticed that now, noticed it the way you notice a wound. Every other document in the vestry archive had browned, crumbled, gone the color of tea. These pages were as dark and sharp as the day they were written, as if the ledger existed in two places at once and the vestry copy was only the shadow of the true one, wherever Elowen was keeping it.

Wherever. As if it were a location. As if *within* were a place with a desk and a lamp and time enough to write.

"Cold clarity," Wendell said to herself. "Be cold. Be clear."

Her mentor at Cambridge used to say that about fieldwork: when the material turns on you, when the archive starts arguing back, you get cold and you get clear and you write down exactly what is in front of you. So she did what she always did. She took out her notebook. She uncapped her pen. And she reduced the horror to bullet points, because bullets couldn't reach up out of the page and take her face.

*1. The binding requires a Thornbury's willing consent, spoken at the threshold.*

*2. Consent = agreement to remain, indefinitely. No release clause. No term.*

*3. Willingness must be genuine at the moment of speaking. False consent voids the seal — and worse.*

*4. Therefore: every exit costs a self. There is no version of this where the village is saved and I walk away whole. The menu has one item on it.*

She stared at point four for a long time. Then she added:

*5. The curate believes sacrifice restores order. He hasn't read this. Or he has, and he thinks a curate counts as a Thornbury, which it doesn't, which means his offer last night — God help him, his brave stupid offer — buys nothing. The thing took his face anyway. It takes what it can and calls the taking a transaction.*

*6. Maren knew. Fifty years ago Maren stood where I'm standing and read this same page, and instead of walking to the well she traded them my name. "The founder's return." She bought six decades with a promise she knew would come due.*

*7. Which means the question is not whether I go down. The question is whether I go down meaning it.*

That was the one she couldn't make cold. She wrote it and then her pen hovered over the page and her hand wouldn't add anything, because everything she might add next was either a lie or a scream.

Because here was the arithmetic underneath the arithmetic, the sum Elowen had tried to warn her about: willingness could not be manufactured. She'd spent three days trying to imagine consenting — really consenting — to walk into that shaft and stay there, in the dark, with the taken, forever, and every imagining collapsed at the same point. Not at the fear. Fear she could carry; she'd been carrying it since the well sang to her. It collapsed at the *forever*. Her mind simply refused the term. Some part of her, the part that had booked another week at the inn out of stubbornness, the part that had descended one step into the open shaft and retreated — that part would hold something back at the threshold. Would keep its own little door cracked. And Elowen had told her exactly what a false yes purchased: a broken latch. An invitation with the lock smashed. The thing pouring through wearing her face and forty-one others, into a valley, and then out of it.

Unless.

She turned back through the ledger, fast now, hunting. There had to be more. Elowen had written for months — the later pages were full of marginalia, observations from within, notes on the thing's behavior that read like field notes themselves, and the recognition of that nearly undid her: *she was a folklorist too, down there in the dark, taking notes on it.* Entries like:

*It repeats what it hears. It has my mother's voice now, and it uses it to ask me questions. I have stopped answering.*

*It offered me a trade today. It will always offer a trade. The trade is always the same trade shaped differently: give me more than you owe and keep less than you are.*

*The villagers above think the lowering is worship. It is maintenance. There is a difference and the difference is everything. Worship invites. Maintenance contains. They must never confuse the two again, and they will, they always will, because worship feels better.*

And then, near the end of the usable pages, cramped sideways in the margin as if written in haste, in a hurry, in the last light of whatever lamp she had:

*A thought. The consent must be willing, but nothing says the willing mind must be deceived about nothing. It checks the heart, not the head. If a successor could reach the threshold having truly accepted — having grieved herself in advance, having let go of the world so completely that staying costs her nothing she is still holding — then the yes would be clean. The trick is not to fool it. The trick is to become someone for whom the door is not a loss.*

*I do not know how to become that person. I was twenty-three. Perhaps in another life, someone arrives here already ruined enough to mean it.*

Wendell closed the ledger.

The vestry was very quiet. Through the wall she could hear the church clock, and beneath the clock, fainter, rhythmic, the sound she'd been hearing for two days now whenever the wind dropped: singing, from below, dozens of voices, patient as water.

Already ruined enough to mean it.

She thought about what she'd lost in the last week, itemized it the way she'd itemized the rite. Her certainty — gone, dead on the day she saw her own face in 1798 oil paint. Her name — compromised, chalked on a door in her own handwriting. Her future — the village had decided it for her, gently, apologetically, the way you decide things for the dying. Her mother's voice was down in that well. Her childhood address was in the foreman's mouth. Everything she was had already begun migrating downward, and what would remain above if she ran — if running were even possible, which the looping roads suggested it wasn't — would be a woman whose own song was being sung better by something else.

Maybe that was the preparation. Maybe that was what Elowen meant. Not courage. Not heroism. Just loss, completed in advance, so that at the threshold there was nothing left in her hands to hold back.

"I can't," she said quietly. And then, because honesty was the only tool left that still worked: "I don't know if I can. That's not the same thing."

The lamp guttered. She trimmed the wick.

Outside, dusk was coming down over Harrow's Bend, and with it the sounds of the final preparations — doors being chalked, livestock penned, the low murmur of a village moving as one body toward one purpose. Tomorrow was the lowering. Tomorrow the village would come for her, gently, apologetically, certain it was mercy, and Maren — Maren had promised her an hour, bought with the only song sung twice, and an hour was what she had.

An hour to become someone who could mean it.

Wendell picked up the ledger, tucked it under her arm, and lifted the lamp. In its light her shadow swung huge across the vestry wall, across the shelf of parish records, across the small framed photograph of the present curate's ordination — except the photograph wasn't the curate anymore. It hadn't been since yesterday. The hollow-smiling man in the frame wore his stolen face like a coat hung on a nail, and as Wendell watched, the smile in the photograph widened by a degree no photograph should manage.

"You'll get my yes," she told it. "One way or another, you'll get a yes."

The smile held. The singing below rose half a note, sweet and terrible, in a voice she had loved since before she could remember.

She blew out the lamp and went to find Maren Cole.

* * *

Maren's cottage smelled of peat smoke and old wool, and the old woman was waiting up, dressed as if for church, two cups of tea already poured and going cold.

"You've read it," Maren said. Not a question. "You've got the look. Elowen had the look, in the paintings. The look of a woman doing sums she hates."

"Maren." Wendell set the ledger on the table between them. "I need you to tell me everything you remember about the night you promised it my name. All of it. Even the parts that shame you."

Maren looked at her for a long moment, and then at the window, where the last light was failing over the drowned valley, and when she spoke her voice was steady in the way of a woman who had rehearsed a confession for fifty years and never expected an audience.

"Then pour yourself something stronger than tea," she said, "and bolt that door. Because the parts that shame me are the parts it listens hardest for."

Above the cottage, on the hillside, the dry well began — very softly, very sweetly — to sing the first verse of a lullaby in a voice that was almost Wendell's own.
