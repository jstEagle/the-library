## 17. The Village Decides

The curate woke before dawn and put on his good coat.

He had not worn it since Easter. It smelled of camphor and of the vestry's old cedar drawers, and when he buttoned it at the throat his fingers were steady — that surprised him. He had expected to shake. He had spent the night shaking, in fact, lying fully dressed on top of the bedclothes while the thing below sang through the mouths of half the village, but now, with the decision made and the hour upon them, the trembling had gone out of him entirely, the way pain leaves a broken bone once it is properly set.

*This is mercy,* he told himself, and found that he believed it.

That was the strange gift of it. He had imagined he would have to lie to himself, would have to wrap the thing in scripture until it looked like something else. But no. Standing at his window watching the mist come off the drowned valley in long grey ribbons, he felt only clarity. The covenant was old. The covenant was just. A debt two hundred years standing did not stop being a debt because the debtor had grown comfortable. And Wendell Thornbury had been sent to them — delivered, really, by bus and by bloodline and by Maren Cole's fifty-year-old bargain — the way rain is delivered to a dry field.

She would go into the well. She would go willingly or she would not, but she would go, and the valley would be spared, and in a year no one would remember her name except as a line in a ledger, which was more than most of the dead got.

He chalked the last name on his own door himself. *Wendell.* His hand did not waver.

* * *

In the kitchen of the Harrow's Bend Inn, Dora Pennifold-Quist set out forty cups and began filling them with tea.

It had been her idea, the tea. Not because anyone needed refreshment — God knew nobody would drink it — but because an occasion required a shape, and a shape required small domestic acts. You could not simply walk a woman to a well. You had to feed the men first, boil the water, lay out the cups. Otherwise it wasn't an occasion. Otherwise it was murder, and no one in Harrow's Bend was committing murder this morning.

"We're not doing anything," she said aloud to the kettle, in the tone she used for correcting new staff. "We're seeing her home."

Her husband, Ted, stood by the window with his arms folded, watching the lane. He had not slept either. None of them had slept; the singing had seen to that, rising from the dry well shaft at midnight in voices they all knew — the foreman's dead father, Dora's sister who'd gone off to Leeds in 1987 and never written, a child drowned in the reservoir before the war whose name even the church records had lost. The singing had not been threatening. That was the worst of it. It had been *welcoming*. It had sounded like a family expecting someone home for supper.

"Briggs won't come," Ted said. "Young Briggs from the excavation crew. He said he'd have nothing to do with it."

"He'll come." Dora arranged the cups in their saucers, each one precisely aligned. "Everyone comes. Everyone always has. That's what a lowering is, Ted. It isn't a mob. It's a parish."

Ted grunted, but she saw him glance at the door, checking the time, and she knew she was right. Briggs would come. They all came. Her grandmother had come, in 1931, carrying a basket of bread rolls for afterward, because whatever else a lowering was, it was also a funeral, and you fed people at funerals. Her grandmother had spoken of it the way she spoke of haymaking: hard work, necessary, best gotten through early.

Dora set out the milk jug and thought, briefly, of the folklorist's face — that open, clever, skeptical face, the one that had smiled at Dora across this very table three weeks ago and asked about songs. She had liked the girl. She had liked her laugh, her terrible handwriting, the way she'd said *fascinating* as though the word were a sweet she couldn't stop sucking.

Then she thought of the water rising over the valley in 1954, and the eleven houses under the reservoir, and the year the singing stopped because the tithe had been paid, and she straightened the milk jug a quarter inch and went on laying out cups.

* * *

At the worksite, foreman Alun Briggs stood at the lip of the shaft and did not look down.

His crew had walked off on Tuesday — all of them, even the ones from outside the valley, even the ones who didn't believe in anything, because there are things a body believes without consulting the mind, and the sound that came up out of that stone door at night was one of them. But Alun had stayed. Someone had to stay. Someone had to keep the site from being sealed by county men with concrete trucks before the matter was settled properly, because if the thing below rose angry and unpaid, it would not knock politely at the church door. It would come up through the ground itself, through foundations and floorboards, wearing whoever it found first.

So Alun stayed, and drank thermos coffee, and did not look down, and waited for today.

He heard them coming up the track a little after eight: the whole village, it seemed, moving quietly, in twos and threes, the way people arrive at a christening. Old Tam Weller with his stick. The Prowse sisters in matching coats. Young Briggs — his nephew, no relation, just the same surname — trailing at the back with his hands jammed in his pockets and his jaw set like a boy refusing to cry at his own father's funeral.

"You don't have to be here," Alun told him.

"Neither do you."

"Aye, well." Alun looked at the shaft. Cold air breathed out of it, slow and regular, like something sleeping. "Somebody's got to mind the door."

They came, in the end. All of them. Even the boy. Even Mrs. Ferris, ninety-one, wheeled up the track in her chair by her grandson, wrapped in a blanket, her face serene as Sunday. Dora brought the tea urn on a trolley and set it beside the site office as though this were a fete. The curate arrived last, in his good coat, and stood at the head of the gathering, and everyone went quiet the way a field goes quiet when the weather turns.

"It's not a punishment," the curate said. He had rehearsed nothing; the words came out of him smooth and warm, and he marveled a little at his own voice. "I want everyone to understand that. It's not a punishment and it's not a cruelty. It's a returning. She belongs to it. She has always belonged to it. We are only — facilitating what was promised before any of us were born."

Someone murmured assent. It might have been Mrs. Ferris.

"And we'll be kind," the curate went on. "We will not frighten her. We will not touch her unless she makes us. We will walk her to the wellhead and we will explain, patiently, as many times as she needs, and if she weeps we will let her weep, and we will not think less of her, because what she does today is harder than anything any of us will ever do, and she does it so that our children sleep soundly tonight."

Alun watched the faces around him and saw no cruelty anywhere. That was the thing he would carry to his grave, he suspected: how gentle they all looked. How reasonable. Forty-odd people in their Sunday coats, nodding along, some of them near tears — grief, real grief, for a woman they'd known three weeks and were about to feed to a hole in the ground. There was no malice in it. There was only arithmetic, old and unquestioned, the way a farmer doesn't hate the lamb.

*We're not doing anything,* Dora had said in her kitchen. *We're seeing her home.*

And the cold air breathed up out of the shaft, and somewhere far below, patient as geology, something waited to be paid.

* * *

They came for her at nine o'clock.

Wendell heard them before she saw them — the crunch of many feet on the gravel path of the inn's front garden, unhurried, deliberate — and she was already at the window, dressed, the ledger of Elowen Thornbury zipped inside her jacket against her ribs. She had not slept. She had spent the night reading by lamplight with the curtains drawn, learning the binding rite syllable by syllable, and when the singing started at midnight she had pressed her hands over her ears and then, hating herself, taken them away again, because one of the voices below was her mother's and she could not bear to miss it.

Now she watched them assemble in the garden. Forty people, give or take. The Prowse sisters. Old Tam. Mrs. Ferris in her wheelchair. Dora Pennifold-Quist, whom she'd liked, whom she'd genuinely liked, standing with her hands folded like a woman waiting for a coach. And at their head, the curate, in his good coat, smiling up at her window with an expression of such tender apology that her stomach turned over.

She understood, in that moment, that they were not ashamed.

That was the revelation that undid her more than the portrait, more than the ledger, more than the singing. They had gathered in daylight, in their best clothes, openly, and there was not one hidden face among them. Whatever this was to them, it was not crime. It was duty. They had probably done it a dozen times in two hundred years, and buried the memory the way the covenant demanded, and dug it up again when the bill came due, and every single time they had told themselves exactly what the curate was telling himself: *this is mercy.*

She thought about running. She had thought about nothing else since dark. But she had tried the roads last night — walked a mile toward the pass with the ledger against her ribs and the moon going in and out — and every road had bent, gently, apologetically, back toward the well, until she stood again at the shaft's edge with her own breath smoking in the cold air that poured out of it. The valley was a bowl and the bowl had a drain, and everything in it flowed one direction.

So she did not run. She picked up her recorder — the little silver dictaphone, absurd, indispensable — and she went downstairs and opened the front door before they could knock.

"Good morning," she said.

"Good morning, Miss Thornbury." The curate stepped forward, hands clasped, the picture of pastoral warmth. "I expect you know why we've come."

"I expect I do."

"We'd hoped you'd come willingly. It makes such a difference — to you, I mean. To what happens after." He said *after* the way other men said *the hereafter*, and several of the villagers bowed their heads slightly at the word. "But no one will lay a hand on you. I give you my word. We only ask that you walk with us."

Behind him, Dora's eyes were wet. Old Tam took off his cap. The whole garden stood in the attitude of a funeral procession waiting for the coffin, and Wendell understood with terrible clarity that to these people she was already dead — had been dead since 1798, really, since Elowen signed the first page of the ledger — and this morning was merely the burial.

"All right," Wendell said, because the rite required a Thornbury at the threshold, willing, and willingness could be performed as easily as grief, and she had learned last night exactly which words to carry in her mouth like a stone under the tongue. "Let's walk."

They formed around her without being asked — not gripping, not herding, just flowing, the way water flows, until she was in the center of them and the inn was behind her and the lane stretched ahead toward the worksite, and the whole village moved as one body, slowly, gently, terribly kind.

* * *

Maren Cole had not meant to be there.

She had told herself she wouldn't be. She had spent fifty years keeping her bargain the way you keep a rotten tooth — carefully, painfully, never biting down on that side of your mouth — and she had told herself, when the chalk appeared on doors and the names were burned, that her part was done, that the founder's return was the founder's business, that an old woman could not unmake what an old woman had made.

But she had heard the singing at midnight. And one of the voices in it had been her sister's — Bronwen, given in 1974, the lowering Maren had bought her own life out of by promising the thing below its next payment in advance. Fifty years she had lived with that promise sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone. And now the bill had come due, and the girl it was due from stood in the middle of the village like a lamb in a pen, and the voice of Bronwen was singing under the ground, and Maren found she could not keep the tooth.

She followed the procession at a distance, leaning on her stick, and she listened to the curate's soft voice, and she watched them turn onto the track toward the wellhead, and she hated every single one of them — and herself most of all, herself longest — and she understood that if she let them reach the shaft mouth, some part of her would die down there with the girl and stay dead forever.

"Maren." The curate saw her and smiled, kindly, including her. "You shouldn't be walking so far. Let someone fetch the—"

"The song," Maren said.

Her voice cracked out over the track like a whip, and the procession stopped. Forty faces turned to her. Wendell turned too, and Maren met her eyes — those eyes, Elowen's eyes, the eyes she'd watched grow up in a portrait above a well for fifty years — and something in the old woman's chest broke loose and came flooding up her throat.

"There's a song," Maren said, louder, and her voice shook and she let it shake. "There's a song we sing once and never twice. Every soul here knows it. Every soul here has sworn on their mothers' graves never to sing it twice, because the second singing is an opening, and the first singing is only a warning." She raised her stick and pointed it, trembling, at the assembled village. "And I am going to sing it twice."

"Maren—" the curate began, gently, reaching for her arm.

"*Don't you touch me.*"

The words came out with such force that he actually stepped back, and in the silence that followed, Maren Cole drew a breath that hurt all the way down, and began to sing.

It was the lullaby. The one she'd given Wendell three weeks ago and cut off mid-verse, white-faced, terrified. The melody rose thin and cracked over the valley — the dirge transposed, the drowning song, the song the water taught them — and the villagers flinched from it as though from cold. Several covered their ears. Mrs. Ferris began quietly to weep. The curate's face went grey.

And Maren sang past the verse where she had always stopped. She sang the fourth line, and the fifth, and the sixth, words she had sworn never to speak, and as she sang them the cold air breathing from the distant shaft changed — Wendell felt it against her cheek, a shift in pressure, a deepening, like a held breath finally released — and far below the ground, something stirred and *listened*.

Maren finished the verse. Stood panting. Then, staring straight at the curate, at the whole gentle murderous parish of them, she sang it again.

The second singing was different. The second singing had weight. It rolled down the track and into the earth, and every villager present felt it pass through them like a current, and behind them, a mile off, the stone door at the bottom of the shaft boomed once — a sound none of them heard with their ears, but all of them felt in their teeth — and the singing that had risen nightly from the well stopped. Utterly. As though something below had turned its attention elsewhere, or been *held* elsewhere, for a little while.

"What have you done?" the curate whispered.

"Bought her an hour." Maren's voice was wrecked, ragged, triumphant. "One hour, curate. While the door holds and the thing below listens to me instead of you. One hour in which no power in this valley can make her walk another step toward that well — not yours, not its, not theirs." She swept the stick across the frozen villagers. "You want her? Come and take her. In an hour. And between now and then, every one of you stand here and look at what mercy looks like, and tell me again it wears your face."

No one moved. No one spoke. Forty people stood on a gravel track in the morning light, and the banality of them was suddenly, horribly visible — the good coats, the tea urn, the wet eyes — a whole parish of ordinary kindness bent to an extraordinary harm, undone by one crooked old woman with a stolen song.

Wendell crossed to Maren and took her hand. The old woman's fingers were ice.

"An hour," Wendell said quietly.

"Less, maybe. Don't waste it." Maren pressed something into her palm — a key, small and iron, green with age. "Vestry. Under the east floor. What Elowen left. Go, girl. *Go.*"

Wendell ran.

Behind her the village stood motionless, and the curate watched her go with his hollow smile fixed in place, and in his pocket his hand closed around a piece of chalk, and he began, silently, to count the minutes.
