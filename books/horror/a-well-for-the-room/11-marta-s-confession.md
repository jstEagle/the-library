## 11. Marta's Confession

Marta had baked bread the way her mother taught her — knead it until your arms ache, then knead it longer, because grief lives in the shoulders and has to be worked out somewhere. She had been baking every day since the folklorist arrived. Ivo thought she'd taken a fancy to the woman. Her grandson was nineteen and still believed kindness had simple reasons.

The truth was that Marta could not stop her hands. If they were busy with dough, with flour, with the small honest violence of punching down a rising loaf, they were not wringing themselves in her lap at three in the morning while she listened to the wind come off the fell and tried to hear whether it carried anything back to her.

It never did. That was the arrangement. The thing below answered whoever listened longest, but it did not speak first. It had never once spoken first in fifty years, and Marta had spent fifty years being grateful for that silence in a way she could not explain to anyone who hadn't been raised in Hollowmere, where you learned before you learned your letters that some doors are load-bearing.

Now the door was rattling.

She'd known since the frost began. She'd seen it from the lane on her way to feed the hens — Xanthe Vane's windows furred white with writing, looping and patient, and even at forty paces Marta had recognized the hand. Not Xanthe's hand. *Her mother's* hand. Edith Crane's slanted, hurried script, the script that filled three ledgers in the vestry and stopped mid-sentence in the fourth, because Edith had been the last one to listen longest, and listening longest is a debt that comes due.

Marta set the loaves to prove and sat down at the kitchen table and put her face in her floury hands.

She had chosen the rental application herself. That was the part no one knew. That was the part she would carry into whatever ground they eventually put her in.

* * *

In 1974, Marta was eleven years old, and the documenter's name was Ruth Ellery, and she stayed in the cottage by the well for one hundred and eleven days, which Marta knew because her mother counted them aloud each night like a rosary, kneeling at the foot of her bed with the door to the pantry open so she could watch the bricked alcove.

Edith Crane had been the keeper. That word wasn't written anywhere; it lived only in mouths, passed from host to host the way a fever passes. A keeper didn't fight the thing below. A keeper fed it — not names, not exactly, not the way the founding families had paid it in the hunger winter, when they'd whispered their dead children's names down through the well stones until the water went quiet. A keeper's work was smaller and crueler. A keeper *listened*. Every night, a little longer than the night before. You sat in the cottage with the recorder running or the Bible open or whatever tool the times gave you, and you let the thing below learn the shape of your attention, because attention was the wall, and a wall that nothing leans against falls over.

Ruth Ellery had come with a tape machine and a grant letter and a laugh like a hinge that needed oiling, and Edith had welcomed her in and made up the bed by the well-room and begun, that very first night, to listen.

Marta remembered the sound of it. Not the voice — she'd been too young, and mothers are careful — but the sound of Edith sitting up past midnight in the chair by the pantry, not moving, not sleeping, just *attending*, the way you attend a sickroom. For one hundred and eleven nights. And on the hundred and twelfth morning there was frost on the windows in Edith's own handwriting, and Edith read it standing very straight with her hands folded, and then she walked out across the frozen garden in her nightgown and lay down on the well-stones in the yard, which had been uncovered then, before the alcove was bricked, and she said, clearly, so Marta heard it through the glass:

"It's finished counting."

And that was all. The thaw came early that year. Ruth Ellery's tapes were found blank. There was no body, because there had never been an illness; there was only a keeper who had leaned against the wall until the wall learned her shape, and then stepped through.

The parish ledger called it *departed in the night, cause unrecorded*, and the village called it nothing at all, because calling it anything would have been saying it twice.

* * *

The binding needed a keeper in the cottage each generation. That was the second thing no one wrote down. The founding families' rite had built the prison, but prisons need wardens, and the warden's post was the chair by the pantry, and the wage was everything.

After Edith, the keeping should have passed to Marta. She was Edith's daughter; the cottage was hers by blood if not by deed. But Marta had married young and moved to the inn at the top of the lane, and she had told herself — she had told herself for fifty years, in the voice you use for lies you've worn smooth as river stones — that distance mattered. That the thing below answered whoever listened *longest*, and she would simply never listen at all. She would sing every song once and never twice, she would keep her back to the pantry in any house she entered, she would raise Ivo to hum only outdoors, and the wall would hold on its own, the way old walls do.

But walls don't hold on their own. That was the lesson of Edith's straight back and folded hands. Something had to lean.

By the time Marta was sixty, the cottage had stood empty four winters. She felt it the way you feel a tooth going bad — a low wrongness under the jaw of the whole village. The frost came earlier each year. The hens stopped laying in November instead of January. Old Tam's wife dreamed of water in a dry room, three nights running, and Tam came to the inn and didn't order anything, just stood by the fire and said, "Marta. Your mother's chair is cold," and left.

So Marta had done what keepers do. She had gone looking for someone to lean.

Not a local. Never a local — a local would know too much too soon, would bolt before the binding took, and a broken binding mid-taking was how you got a hunger winter again. It had to be an outsider. It had to be someone who *listened for a living*. Someone whose whole self was attention, already shaped like the wall wanted, needing only to be placed against it.

She had put the cottage listing in three journals and waited, ashamed, telling herself each week that she'd take it down tomorrow.

Xanthe Vane's application came in February. Marta read it at this same table, with these same flourless hands, and her heart had done something complicated — lifted and dropped at once, like a bird hitting a window from the inside. Because the application was perfect. Grief in the recent past — a sister, a year gone. A profession of listening. No family to come looking. A stated love of "isolated communities with strong oral tradition." She might have written it herself, if she had been building a key rather than a person.

Marta had held the letter a long time. Then she'd written back: *You may have the cottage by the well. One warning — never hum indoors.*

And she had lit a candle for Edith, and another for Ruth Ellery, and she had not let herself think about what she was doing, because thinking about it was a kind of listening, and listening was the debt.

* * *

Now the frost was writing, and the tallies were moving, and the girl — the woman, thirty-four years old, with her sister's name said carefully like a bruise — was walking around Hollowmere with a recorder in her pocket, documenting everything, documenting *herself*, feeding the wall with both hands without knowing she was leaning.

Ivo had come in an hour ago, snow in his hair, and said, "Gran, she played me a tape. There's two voices on it. Both hers." He'd been excited. Nineteen years old and excited, the way Ruth Ellery had probably been excited once, the way every documenter was excited right up until the excitement curdled. "She thinks it's some kind of echo phenomenon. She wants to write a paper."

"A paper," Marta said.

"For a journal. She showed me the form. Gran, why are you doing that with your hands."

Marta looked down. She was wringing the tea towel as though it owed her money. She made herself stop. "Ivo. Go to the hall. Stay there till dark."

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to tell her something tonight that you're not ready to carry, and I need to know you're somewhere warm when I do it."

He'd gone, after a look she pretended not to see — the look that said *you always do this, you always decide what we're allowed to know* — and now the bread was proving and the light was going blue over the fell and it was time.

Marta untied her apron. Folded it. Put it on the chair Edith used to sit in, here at the inn, in the years after, when Edith's hands shook too badly to knead and Marta had taken over the baking and neither of them ever said the word *well* aloud, not once, not in fifty years, because you don't say it twice and you barely say it once.

Then she took the bottle of damson gin down from the high shelf — the bottle for funerals and confessions, which in Hollowmere were often the same event — and poured two measures, and walked down the lane through the falling snow to the cottage by the well, carrying her guilt in front of her like a lantern.

* * *

Xanthe opened the door before Marta knocked. Of course she did. The house had told her someone was coming. Marta tried not to think about that either.

"You look," Xanthe said, "like my mother did the year after."

"The year after what?"

"Exactly." Xanthe stepped back to let her in. The cottage smelled of woodsmoke and tape hiss and, underneath, faintly, of wet stone. Marta's eyes went, traitorously, to the pantry door. Nailed shut now — she could see the fresh nailheads, four of them, driven in within the last day or two. Good girl, she thought, and hated herself for the tenderness of it, because tenderness was how this worked, tenderness was the mechanism, the founding families hadn't starved their way into a pact with a monster, they'd starved their way into a pact with something that *answered*, and being answered, after a killing winter of silence, had felt like salvation.

"I brought gin," Marta said. "And I've come to confess."

Xanthe's face did several things quickly. Marta watched her decide to be a professional about it — chin up, recorder instinct twitching toward her pocket — and then decide, visibly, not to reach for the pocket. That small mercy undid Marta more than anything else could have.

They sat at the table. The fire ticked. Outside, the snow came down with the patience of something that had all night and all winter and, behind it, all the winters ever.

"Before I start," Marta said, "you'll want to record this. Don't."

"That's not really how my work—"

"I know exactly how your work is. I chose you *because* of how your work is." She heard it land. Watched Xanthe go still the way animals go still. "Pour yourself a drink, love. This is going to take the length of a candle."

And Marta told her.

She told her about 1974, about Ruth Ellery and the hundred and twelve mornings, about her mother's nightgown on the snow and the sentence spoken through glass. She told her about keepers and walls and leaning. She told her about the empty winters, the hens, Tam at the fireside — *your mother's chair is cold* — and about the three journals and the listing she kept promising to take down.

"And then," Marta said, and here her voice went thin and she let it, because a confession you deliver in a steady voice isn't a confession, it's a report, "your letter came. And I read it, and I knew inside ten lines what you were. What you'd lost. How you listened. And I wrote back and invited you here anyway, and I have baked bread every single day since you arrived because if my hands are working then they can't shake, and I am so sorry, Xanthe. I am sorry in the place where sorry doesn't help."

The silence afterward had texture. Marta sat in it and did not flinch, though every nerve wanted the door.

Xanthe turned her glass slowly on the table, once, a full rotation, like a dial being tuned. "Your mother," she said finally. "She knew. All those nights, sitting up listening — she knew what she was doing it for."

"She knew it was mercy. Whether it was mercy for the village or for the thing below, she never settled. I think it was both. I think that's the horror of it, if you want horror. It's lonely down there. It's been lonely down there since before there was a village, and the founders didn't trap it, they *hired* it, and nobody ever checks on hired things." Marta heard herself and stopped. "Listen to me. Fifty years of not talking and now I'm defending it. That's what it does. That's the first thing it does. It makes you fair-minded about it."

"Has it talked to you?" Xanthe asked. Quiet. Real question, not a researcher's question. "Ever?"

"No. Keepers aren't answered. We're the wall, remember. It answers the ones who lean." Marta looked at her own hands, flour still in the creases despite the walk through snow. "But I've heard it answer my mother. Through the floor. On the last night. Two voices, and both of them hers, and one of them kinder than my mother ever managed to be, and that's how I knew it was lying, because my mother was kind, but she was never kind like that. Nothing is kind like that for free."

Xanthe set the glass down. Her hand was steady, which frightened Marta more than shaking would have. "Why tell me now? Tonight? The frost started days ago. The tallies—"

"Because today I saw its handwriting on your windows, and it wasn't yours." Marta met her eyes. "It was my mother's. Which means it's not learning you anymore, Xanthe. It's *remembering through* you. It's wearing the last keeper's hand to finish the last keeper's verse, and when the pattern completes—" She stopped. She had promised herself she wouldn't say the rest, because saying it was half a step toward singing it, and rules are rules, and rules are all that's left of the founders' wisdom.

"When the pattern completes *what*?" Xanthe asked. And then, softer, in the voice of someone who has spent weeks being answered in her own timbre and knows exactly what a loaded pause sounds like: "Marta. Please. Whatever it is — I'd rather hear it from you than from the window."

Marta reached across the table and took her hands. They were cold, the folklorist's hands, recorder-cold, pen-cold, and Marta warmed them the way you warm anything you may not get to keep.

"There's a verse," she said. "The one no one sings. The founders wrote it down once and then burned the page and each kept half of it in memory, and the halves died out till only the frost remembers it whole. It's finishing it on your glass, line by line, night by night. And when the last line comes — at dawn, on the shortest days, dawn is when the wall is thinnest — the binding breaks. Unless." Her throat closed. She worked past it. "Unless someone sings it twice. On purpose. With their whole self. Because sung twice by choice, it doesn't wake the thing below. It folds it into the singer. Takes it up into a life. Ends the counting forever — one way or the other."

Xanthe didn't pull her hands away. That was the worst part. Marta had braced for anger, for the door slamming, for the bus and the pass and the village left to its arithmetic. Instead the woman just sat there, cold-handed, steady-eyed, doing what she did best.

Listening longest.

"Oh," Xanthe said quietly. "Oh, you clever, cruel, loving woman. You didn't rent me a cottage."

"No," Marta said, and the tears came at last, fifty years late, hot and useless and honest. "I'm afraid I rented you a chair."

Outside, the snow kept falling, and behind the nailed pantry door, faint as a pulse, something began — very softly, in a dead woman's handwriting of a voice — to hum.
