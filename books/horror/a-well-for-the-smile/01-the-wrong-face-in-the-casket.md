## 1. The Wrong Face in the Casket

The plane landed in Enugu at 4:17 in the afternoon, and by 5:30 Ulyana Okonkwo had already been told three times that she looked exactly like her brother.

"You and Uzo," said the taxi driver, adjusting his mirror to look at her instead of the road. "Same face. God is wonderful."

"God is something," Ulyana said, and gave him a tip large enough that he stopped talking about faces.

She had not been home in eleven years. Lagos had swallowed her whole — the advertising job, the small apartment with the good light, the slow accumulation of a life that fit her like a tailored dress. Enugu was a dress she'd outgrown, or perhaps one she'd never actually worn; it had hung in the family closet all this time, waiting. Now she was back to bury the person who had worn it best.

Uzo had died on a Tuesday, of something the doctors called "sudden cardiac event" and her mother's old neighbors called, in lowered voices, "the well." Nobody would explain the second version. They simply said *the well* the way you might say *the war*, and then changed the subject to whether she'd eaten.

The house on Ogui Road still stood where it had always stood, slightly too large for its plot, its gate painted the green of old money pretending to be new. The compound smelled the way it always had — frangipani, dust, the ghost of her mother's cooking — and for one dangerous moment Ulyana felt twelve years old again, running through the side passage with her brother's hand in hers, both of them laughing at nothing, at everything, at the private joke that twins carry like a coin sewn into their lining.

Then she saw the casket.

It sat in the front room under the ceiling fan, surrounded by plastic chairs and wilted garlands, because the wake had already begun without her. Her auntie Ngozi spotted her first and made a sound like a kettle coming to boil, and then Ulyana was being embraced, scolded, fed — a plate of jollof rice appeared in her hands before she had agreed to hold it — and gently steered toward the coffin to pay her last respects.

She looked down.

And the corpse looked back up at her with her own face.

Not *similar*. Not the family resemblance that strangers cooed over. Her face. The small scar above the left eyebrow from the bicycle accident when they were nine. The gap between the front teeth that she'd paid a Lagos dentist a shameful amount of money to close, and which had stubbornly reopened within a year, as if her face had opinions about improvement. Even the way the hairline sat — slightly higher on the right, a trait she'd inherited from nobody she could name — was reproduced there in the dead man's skin with the fidelity of a portrait artist who had worked from life.

Except the dead man was supposed to be Uzo. And Uzo, whatever else he had been, had been unmistakably himself: broader in the jaw, darker, with their father's heavy brow. She had seen photographs of him in the casket planning — he'd sent them to her two years ago with the message *just in case, sis, don't let Aunty Ngozi choose anything with gold trim, she will embarrass us before God.* The man in the photographs had been her twin. The man in the casket was her.

"Ah-ah," said Aunty Ngozi, appearing at her elbow with the timing of a woman who could smell a scene developing. "Don't cry like that. You'll spoil your face."

"My—" Ulyana's voice came out strange. "Aunty. Whose body is that?"

Ngozi followed her gaze into the casket and frowned, the way one frowns at a soup that tastes almost right but not quite. "That is your brother now, my dear. Rest him."

"That is *my face*."

"It is the embalming." Ngozi said this with tremendous authority, as though embalming were a craft known to turn brothers into sisters the way a grinder turns pepper into powder. "These mortuary boys, they rush. The chemicals, the swelling — the face becomes soft, it becomes anyone. When I buried Obiora's father in 2009, he came out looking like a woman from Onitsha. We managed."

"We *managed*," Ulyana repeated.

"What else do people do?" said Ngozi reasonably, and pressed another spoonful of jollof rice toward her as if grief were primarily a nutritional problem.

* * *

The funeral director arrived an hour later, summoned by phone, a damp-palmed young man named Emeka who shook her hand twice because the first shake seemed to have failed him somehow.

"Madam, I want to assure you," he began, "that we are professionals."

"He has my face."

"Yes." Emeka wiped his palms on his trousers. "We have identified the... situation."

"The situation."

"The chemical process, madam, sometimes interacts with the — with the individual composition of the deceased's —"

"Emeka." Ulyana kept her voice level, which cost her something. "My brother and I are twins. Identical twins. I understand families see resemblance where there is none. But this man has a scar only I have. He has my teeth. My hairline. If you put a wig on him, my own mother would have kissed his forehead and gone home satisfied."

Emeka looked at the casket, then at her, then back at the casket, doing the arithmetic of resemblance with visible discomfort. His lips moved slightly, comparing features the way a customs officer compares a passport photo to a nervous traveler.

"There may have been an exchange," he admitted at last, very quietly. "At the mortuary. Bodies are — sometimes labels come off. Sometimes bodies themselves come off." He caught himself. "Labels. Labels come off."

"So somewhere in Enugu," Ulyana said slowly, "there is a drawer containing my brother's actual face."

"I wouldn't say a *drawer* —"

"Where is the paperwork? The intake forms. Photographs. Something."

And here Emeka did something odd. He didn't check his files, though his files were sitting right there in a battered leather portfolio. Instead he glanced — quickly, involuntarily — at the corner of the room behind Ulyana, as if someone had cleared their throat there, and then he smiled a smile with no floor underneath it.

"The paperwork is in order, madam," he said. "Everything is in order. The family confirmed the body. Your brother confirmed the body."

"My brother is *in the body*."

"Your other brother," Emeka said, and then went pale, as if the sentence had left his mouth without permission and he'd only just heard what it said.

* * *

Because that was the thing she hadn't yet written down even in her own head, the thing she'd been circling since the taxi: there was a man at the gate calling himself Uzo. Alive. Healthy. Dressed in a white kaftan and receiving condolences with the gracious weariness of a man who found his own funeral slightly excessive.

She had seen him from the taxi window, actually — standing at the gate in the gold evening light, greeting mourners, laughing at some uncle's joke. She had thought, absurdly, *what a handsome stranger,* and then the stranger had turned and looked directly at the taxi, directly at her, and raised one hand in a small wave. Not surprised. Not confused. Welcoming. Like a host who had been watching the road for her arrival.

She had told herself it was a cousin. A lookalike relative — such things happened. But no cousin had been mentioned in any of the forty-one messages Uzo had sent her in the week before he died, and no cousin stood at the center of the mourning like the sun at the center of the day, and no cousin, she was nearly certain, waved at her out of a window he should not have known she'd pass.

Now, as dusk came down over Ogui Road and the generator coughed awake, Ulyana watched the mourners watch her. It was subtle. Nigerians are too polite for staring; they do it in the grammar of glances. A woman in blue ankara would look at the casket, then at Ulyana, then become fascinated by her phone. An old man near the door performed the same circuit three times, casket-Ulyana, living-Ulyana, phone, with the regularity of a metronome. By the tenth circuit she wanted to take his phone away.

They weren't looking at her like a grieving sister. They were looking at her the way people look at a photograph that has appeared in a house where no photograph was taken. They were looking at her like she was the one who didn't belong in the room.

"They think I'm the ghost," she murmured to Aunty Ngozi.

"Eh?" said Ngozi, who had heard perfectly well and chosen the strategic deafness of aunties everywhere.

"The way they're looking at me. Like I'm the one who died."

"Nonsense." Ngozi patted her knee. "They are looking at you because you look like your brother. Same face. God is wonderful."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because it is true!" And then, lowering her voice, with the air of a woman imparting genuinely useful intelligence: "But between us — your brother has been strange since he came back from Lagos last month. Quiet. Sitting alone. And he has started eating with his left hand."

"People eat with their left hands all the time."

"Not Uzo." Ngozi's eyes held hers, and for a moment beneath the perfume and the platitudes there was something old and frightened looking out. "Your brother was right-handed like your father. So right-handed that when he broke the right wrist at seven, he cried not from the pain but because he had to write with the wrong hand. I held him while he cried. I know which hand my grandnephew eats with."

The fan turned overhead. Somewhere outside, a mourner laughed, and the laugh sounded wrong in the dark, like a recording played in an empty hall.

"And the body," Ulyana said. "In the casket. You've looked at it properly, Aunty. You've sat with it."

"I have sat with it," Ngozi agreed.

"And?"

Ngozi smoothed her wrapper over her knees, twice, three times, buying time with fabric. "And I have decided," she said finally, "that tomorrow I will sit with it again. Some things you must look at more than once before you are allowed to say them." She stood, kissed Ulyana's forehead, and paused. "Lock your door tonight. Not against thieves. Thieves we understand."

She shuffled off toward the kitchen, leaving Ulyana alone in the front room with the fan, the flowers, and the casket.

The house had gone quiet around her, that particular Enugu quiet — crickets, distant generator hum, the settling of an old building into the night. Ulyana stood over her own dead face and made herself study it the way she'd once studied proofs at work, hunting the flaw, the tell, the thing that wasn't right.

There. At the corner of the mouth. The corpse was smiling.

Not the slack arrangement of a resting face. Not embalmer's artistry. A smile — small, closed-lipped, patient — the smile of someone keeping a secret, or keeping a place. She knew that smile. She had seen it across a pillow for twenty-three years, across a breakfast table, across the rim of a dry stone well behind the house on afternoons when the game got serious and neither twin would blink.

*Whoever smiles longest wins,* Uzo used to say. *And the winner gets to stay.*

The corpse wore her face, and it was winning.

From the darkness beyond the window, from the direction of the gate, a voice called out cheerfully — her brother's voice, warm and familiar and entirely wrong:

"Sis! Come and eat! We have so much to talk about before morning."

Ulyana did not move. In the casket, very faintly, in a face that had no breath left to move it, the smile widened by a single millimeter.

Morning, she understood, was when the signing was scheduled. Morning was when whoever remained would belong to this house forever.

She just had to work out which of them that was.
