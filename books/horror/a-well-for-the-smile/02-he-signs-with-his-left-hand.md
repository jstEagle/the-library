## 2. He Signs with His Left Hand

Sleep did not come so much as it was politely declined, and by six in the morning Ulyana had given up pretending and gone to stand at the gate of the family house with a cup of tea she had no intention of drinking.

Enugu in the early hours was kind to her. The red dust hung soft in the light, a boy was hawking bread from a tray balanced on his head with the serenity of a man carrying state secrets, and somewhere down the road a radio argued cheerfully about football. For eleven minutes — she counted, because counting kept her sane — the world behaved exactly as the world should.

Then the gate opened from the inside.

"Ulyana." The man standing there said her name the way her brother used to say it: two syllables, the second one dropped slightly, as if he'd always been half-bored even by her existence. "You look terrible."

"You're dead," Ulyana said.

It seemed important to establish this early, before any other business could be conducted.

"I'm *standing*," said Uzo Okonkwo, who had been buried yesterday afternoon in a casket wearing her face, "and you're blocking my sunlight. Some things don't change."

He looked good. That was the obscene part. He looked better than good — he looked like Uzo at twenty-five, which was impossible, because Uzo had been thirty-four when the call came from Lagos saying he'd collapsed, and grief had already begun drafting its edits on her memory of his face. This version was unedited. This version had the gap in his teeth that the dentist in Enugu had fixed when they were nine. This version's skin held the particular shine of someone who has never once worried about anything.

"You died," Ulyana said again, more firmly, the way one corrects a child or a stubborn goat. "I identified the body. I signed papers. There were *flowers*, Uzo. I chose flowers for your coffin. Do you know what that cost me emotionally?"

"A lot, apparently." He leaned against the gatepost, arms folded. "And yet here I am. Maybe you should ask yourself why you're so certain about which of us is which."

"Because I'm me. It's the one thing I've never had to think about."

"Mm." He smiled, and the smile was warm and familiar and wrong in a way she couldn't yet name, like a song played a half-tone too high. "Tea?"

"I have tea." She lifted the cup as evidence. It had gone cold. Everything, she suspected, was about to go cold.

* * *

They sat on the veranda because sitting on the veranda was what Okonkwos did when something needed discussing, and the house itself seemed to lean in to listen. Ulyana noticed that Uzo took the chair on the left — Uzo had always taken the right, always, since childhood, some twin superstition about keeping their hearts on opposite sides of the table.

"You want to know how," he said.

"I want to know *why you're using my face*."

His hand went briefly to his cheek, an oddly shy gesture. "The embalmer made an error. A terrible, tragic error. You saw the result. I'm told the family is considering legal action."

"The family is *me*, and I'm considering several things, none of them legal."

"Ulyana." He said her name gently now, the way you speak to someone standing too close to a ledge. "Grief does strange things. You flew in exhausted, you saw a body, and your mind — quite understandably — confused it with the person you love most in the world. Which is yourself. No judgment. Twins are famous for it."

"My mind is fine."

"Is it?" He tilted his head. "How are the mirrors treating you this morning?"

Ulyana's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

Because that was the thing she hadn't told anyone, the thing she'd filed under *jet lag* and *hysteria* and *that second glass of wine on the flight*: last night, brushing her teeth in the bathroom she'd shared with Uzo for eighteen years, she had noticed her reflection blink a moment after she did. Not before. After. Like an echo. Like something following her movements at a respectful distance.

"They're fine," she said.

"Liar." He said it fondly. "You always were a terrible liar. Remember when you ate my birthday cake and blamed the cat? We didn't *have* a cat."

"We got a cat the next year specifically because of that lie. Mother said if we were going to lie, we should lie with infrastructure."

For one dangerous second, laughing felt possible. Then she caught his hands on the table — he was gesturing, animated, alive, infuriatingly alive — and the laugh died where it stood.

Left-handed.

Her brother had been right-handed. Fiercely, obnoxiously right-handed; he'd once written a school essay titled "The Right Hand Is the Thinking Hand" and gotten a B-minus and a note home. Every memory she owned of Uzo held a pen, a fork, a cricket bat in his right fist.

The man across from her poured tea with his left, stirred with his left, set the spoon down on the left side of the saucer with the precision of a man laying out evidence.

"You're left-handed," she said quietly.

"I'm ambidextrous." Smooth. Too smooth. "Death broadens one's horizons."

"Uzo couldn't use his left hand for anything except losing at arm wrestling. You know how I know? Because I *won*. Every time. Eleven years old to thirty-four, I never lost once, and you sulked for entire afternoons—"

"People change."

"*Dead people don't.*"

The word landed between them and neither picked it up. On the street, the bread seller passed again, going the other way, and did not look at the house. Ulyana noticed that. She was beginning to notice a great many things not looking at the house.

* * *

At noon, the buyer arrived.

She heard the car before she saw it — a long black Mercedes that moved up the red-dust road without raising any dust at all, which her brain flagged and then, exhausted, declined to pursue. The driver opened the back door and out stepped a tall man in a grey suit the colour of harmattan sky, with a briefcase and a face arranged around two features: a mouth built entirely for politeness, and eyes that did not, in any meaningful sense, do anything.

"Miss Okonkwo." His voice was pleasant, mid-register, the voice of airport announcements. "Mr. Adeyemi. We spoke on the phone. Condolences on your loss."

"Thank you," said Ulyana, whose loss was currently leaning against the doorframe eating groundnuts with his left hand.

Adeyemi extended his hand. She shook it. His palm was cool and dry, like paper that had been kept in a drawer.

"Shall we conclude the business?" he said. "I dislike leaving matters open overnight. Overnight is when matters get ideas."

"That's not a normal sentence," Ulyana said.

"No," Adeyemi agreed pleasantly, and led the way inside.

* * *

The documents were laid out on the dining table — the same table where the twins had done homework, fought over jollof, and once conducted a full trial over a missing Walkman (Uzo was found guilty; the Walkman was found under his mattress). The lawyer, a damp little man named Barrister Eze who arrived smelling of rain though the sky was clear, spread the pages with the reverence of a priest arranging communion.

"Sale of the property at Number 7 Nsukka Street," Eze recited, "from Mr. Uzochukwu Okonkwo, sole surviving—" he paused, swallowed, "—sole owner, to Mr. Adeyemi, consideration having been paid in full. Miss Okonkwo, you're present as a witness only. Your signature isn't required."

"Good," said Ulyana. "Because I'm not signing anything in this house until somebody explains the mirrors."

Nobody asked her what she meant. That was the worst part. Uzo studied his fingernails. Adeyemi watched her with those doing-nothing eyes. Even the house seemed to hold its breath, and from the hallway, faintly, she could hear the hall mirror — she would have sworn she could hear it — settling into place like a guest taking a seat.

"Sign here," Eze said to Uzo, "and initial each page."

Uzo picked up the pen with his left hand.

Ulyana watched the pen move. Watched the signature appear — *Uzochukwu Okonkwo*, in handwriting that was her brother's handwriting mirrored, every letter flipped, legible only if you held it up to glass. She thought, distantly, that if you held it up to the hallway mirror, it might read perfectly. She thought she might test that later, and then thought she very much did not want to know.

"And the buyer countersigns," Eze said.

Adeyemi took the pen.

Here is what Ulyana saw, and she would spend the rest of that day deciding whether she had truly seen it: Mr. Adeyemi bent toward the page, and his reflection in the dark window behind him — the glass doors onto the garden, throwing back the whole room like a second, quieter signing — bent a full second later. The pen touched paper. Then the reflection's pen touched the reflection's paper. When he straightened, his reflection followed, late, obedient, like a shadow that had learned to imitate a man but hadn't mastered the timing.

She looked at the window. She looked at Adeyemi. Adeyemi looked at her, and smiled with his polite mouth, and his eyes continued their policy of strict neutrality.

"Everything satisfactory?" he asked.

"Your reflection is slow," Ulyana said.

"Is it?" Adeyemi glanced at the window. In the glass, his reflected face turned to look a heartbeat afterward, and Ulyana's stomach performed a slow, cold roll. "These old houses," Adeyemi said. "The light comes in strangely. One grows used to it."

"One," said Ulyana, "does not grow used to it. One moves away. Preferably to another country. Possibly another plane of reality."

"Which is why the sale is timely." He closed the briefcase with a click that sounded, absurdly, final — a sound like a well cover sliding shut. "The house will be mine by morning. All its... contents included."

"Its contents are me."

"For now," said Adeyemi, "the contents are being decided."

* * *

That evening she found Uzo alone on the veranda, watching the sun go down over the neighbourhood with an expression she refused to find nostalgic.

"Who is he?" she asked. "Really."

"A buyer."

"Uzo."

"Don't say my name like that. It sounds like Mother."

"Someone has to say things like Mother. She's not here to say them herself." Ulyana sat down beside him, because her legs were tired and because some part of her — the stupid, hopeful, twin-shaped part — still wanted to sit beside her brother at sunset. "Whatever this is, whatever game you're playing, whatever came out of whatever hole — I'm telling you now, I'm not signing. Not the sale, not the condolence book, nothing. I've seen enough films. Nothing good starts with a signature."

Uzo was quiet for a while. The first star came out, uncertain, above the roofline.

"You always were the careful one," he said at last, and there was something in his voice she couldn't place — pity, maybe, or its uglier cousin. "Careful with your words. Careful with your steps. Never leaned too far over anything."

"It's called sense."

"Mm." He turned to her, and in the dying light his face did something small and terrible — slid, just slightly, the way heat shimmers above tarmac — so that for half a breath she was looking at her own face wearing her brother's expression. "Keep being careful, Ulyana. But you should know: tomorrow at dawn, everyone signs *something*. Even refusing is a signature, if you write it down. And the thing below the well has waited twenty years to read."

Below the well.

She hadn't mentioned the well. She hadn't thought about the well in twenty years, and she had certainly not said the word aloud, not once, not to the funeral director, not to the neighbours, not to God.

"How do you know about the well?" she whispered.

Uzo smiled with her mouth, and reached over with his left hand, and patted her knee twice — the exact rhythm, the exact two taps, their mother used when she wanted them to stop asking questions.

"Get some sleep," he said. "Big day tomorrow."
