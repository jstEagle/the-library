## 6. Adeyemi Comes at Dusk

Mr. Adeyemi arrived at twilight, because twilight was when houses told the truth.

He had learned this over a long career. In the morning a house was all ambition — fresh paint, swept compound, the smell of yesterday's cooking deployed like perfume for visitors. At noon it performed prosperity. But at dusk, when the light went thin and amber and the lizards retired from the walls to wherever lizards went to think about their lives, a house stopped performing and simply *was*. Dusk was inventory time. Dusk was when you counted what you actually owned.

Adeyemi owned many things. He did not own this house yet.

He parked his car on the road rather than in the compound — a small courtesy, or a small superstition; he had never bothered to distinguish between the two — and stood at the gate for a moment, taking his inventory. The house sat behind its wall the way an old woman sits behind her wrapper: composed, watchful, concealing more than she shows. Bougainvillea had gone feral along the fence, purple and aggressive. The windows were dark except one, upstairs, where a lamp burned behind curtains that moved without any wind he could feel.

The gate opened before he touched it.

"Good evening," said the man inside. Uzo Okonkwo, according to every document Adeyemi had prepared. A handsome man, neatly dressed, smiling with exactly the right amount of warmth — which is to say, none at all, but arranged to look like some.

"You're left-handed," Adeyemi observed, because the man had unlatched the gate with his left hand, and because Adeyemi made a habit of noticing things out loud. It unsettled people. Unsettled people revealed things.

"I've always been," said the man.

"No," Adeyemi agreed pleasantly. "You haven't."

* * *

He walked the house the way he walked every house: slowly, hands clasped behind his back, saying little. The seller trailed him room by room, narrating features — original tiles, good roof, borehole water — in the voice of a man reciting something he had memorized from the outside of a book. Adeyemi nodded at intervals. He was not listening. He was counting mirrors.

There were seven. A tall one in the entrance hall with a frame of carved ibeji figures, which someone had once thought funny or protective and which was now neither. A dressing mirror in the master bedroom, its silvering spotted with age like the skin of a very old fish. A small round one in the kitchen, clouded near the edges. Two in the corridor. One in what had been the children's room, bolted low on the wall at the height of a child — a detail Adeyemi noted and filed away, because details about children were always load-bearing in houses like this. And one in the study, behind the desk, draped with an old cloth as if the family had grown tired of being looked at.

"Seven mirrors," he said.

"Is that significant?"

"Everything is significant. That's the trouble with property." He took out his matches. "Do you mind?"

"Should I?"

"That depends entirely on what happens next."

He struck the first match in the entrance hall and held the flame up before the tall mirror, arm extended, flame steady. This was an old test, older than his profession, older probably than his profession's profession. A true mirror reflected fire as fire: instant, obedient, dumb. A lagging mirror reflected fire as *memory* of fire. And a hungry mirror —

Well. There were three kinds of mirror, and he had only ever needed matches for two of them.

The flame appeared in the glass. He counted. One. Two. On *three*, the reflection caught up, and the little fire bloomed in the carved frame between the ibeji twins, who stared back at him with their sewn-shut serenity.

"Lagging," he said.

"The electrician says it's the wiring," the seller offered.

"The wiring." Adeyemi struck another match. "Does the electrician also explain why your reflection blinks after you do?"

The seller did not answer. In the mirror, both their reflections watched the exchange with expressions slightly wrong — the seller's smile arriving a half-second late, like thunder after lightning, so that the whole hallway felt briefly like a place where sound and sight had been divorced and were fighting over custody.

* * *

He tested all seven. The kitchen mirror lagged by two seconds and showed, for a moment, steam rising from a pot that was not there. The children's-room mirror did not lag at all — it simply refused to show the match, showing instead the empty room, the small bed, the window going violet with evening, as if the fire were beneath its dignity or outside its jurisdiction. That one interested Adeyemi most. Refusal was a choice, and choices meant preferences, and preferences could be negotiated with.

The study mirror, still under its cloth, he saved for last. When he pulled the cloth away, the glass was warm.

"Noted," he murmured, and did not strike a match at all. Some mirrors you tested. Some you greeted.

By the time he came back down, the sky through the windows had gone the color of a bruise healing, and the sister was standing at the foot of the stairs.

Ulyana Okonkwo. He had read her name on documents for weeks before he ever saw her face, and now here it was, walking around attached to a living woman, wearing an expression he recognized from long experience: the look of someone who has spent the day being gaslit by architecture. Her eyes were red-rimmed. Her posture was excellent. Grief and defiance had reached some kind of armed truce in her shoulders.

"Mr. Adeyemi," she said. "You're early."

"I'm punctual. The signing is tomorrow. Inspection is tonight."

"People usually inspect in daylight."

"People usually buy land." He smiled at her, a real smile, brief and almost kind, because he liked her already and knew it would not help her. "May I ask you something, Miss Okonkwo? When you look in a mirror, how long does it take you to recognize yourself?"

She didn't answer. Smart woman. Answering questions like that was how people ended up in ledgers.

* * *

They ended up in the sitting room, because houses funnel you toward the rooms they want conversations held in, and this house wanted the sitting room — the room with the framed photographs of the twins as children, gap-toothed and identical, standing side by side in front of a dry well with their arms slung over each other's shoulders, grinning like the well owed them money.

Adeyemi looked at the photograph for a long time.

"We used to play a game," Ulyana said, following his gaze. Her voice had gone flat, the flatness of someone deciding to say a dangerous thing just to see what it flushed out. "At the well. A dare."

"I know."

"How could you possibly—"

"Miss Okonkwo." He turned from the photograph. Outside, the last light was draining out of the sky, and the lamps in the room made everything warmer than it deserved to be. "I am going to tell you something plainly, because you strike me as a woman who has had enough of plainness withheld from her, and because lying to you would be a waste of both our evenings. Do you know what I actually do? My profession?"

"You buy property."

"I acquire *residences*." He let the word sit there. "For a client. My client is not interested in Enugu, or in this street, or in this square footage, or in the borehole water, which is excellent, by the way, I checked. My client is interested in the fact that this house has been vacant for twenty years in a way that no surveyor has ever managed to record. Vacant in the way a shell is vacant. Something lived here once. Something was invited. And my client wishes to purchase the tenancy."

The seller — the thing wearing her brother's face and calling itself Uzo — shifted by the window. Its reflection in the darkened glass arrived late even for a reflection.

"You're mad," Ulyana said.

"Frequently. It keeps the work interesting." He set his hat on the table between them. "But consider the paperwork, since you clearly have been. Who drafts a deed of sale for a house with a contested corpse in the parlor? Who schedules a signing for six in the morning, before any court opens, before any bank does? Who needs witnesses at dawn, Miss Okonkwo? Not land registries. Land registries keep office hours."

"Then what—"

"Dawn is when the well accepts new residents. It always has been. Your mother knew; ask her ledger, if you've found it yet — you have that look, so I'll assume yes. Twenty years of entries. *One smile, owed.* She paid faithfully, God rest her, and her payments kept two children alive through a game that should have taken one of them the first week." He picked up his hat again, turned it in his hands. "But debts don't die with debtors. They transfer. To the estate. To whichever twin is still standing when the account comes due."

"And your client buys—"

"I'm buying the resident." He said it gently, the way one delivers any diagnosis that is technically accurate and wholly useless. "The house is a formality. The papers are a formality. Even the corpse is a formality, though I admit it's an unusually well-dressed one. What changes hands at sunrise is residency. Occupancy. Belonging." He rose. "You're just deciding which one."

Silence. Somewhere upstairs, the lamp behind the curtain went out on its own.

"Which one what?" she asked, very quietly.

"Which twin stays. Which twin goes below. Which one of you the house remembers, and which one it releases." He put on his hat. "I'd advise you to sleep on it, but I suspect sleep left this premises some time ago, along with the wiring's innocence."

At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at her with something that in a better man might have been pity.

"One piece of professional advice, free of charge, which should tell you how seriously I mean it. Tomorrow, when the papers are laid out, everyone will tell you that refusing to sign is safe. That silence protects you. That no signature means no sale." His eyes flicked, briefly, to the darkened hallway mirror, where three figures stood where two people stood. "In matters like this one, Miss Okonkwo, refusal is also a signature. The well has never cared *how* you say yes. Only that you say it before the sun does."

He let himself out. The gate closed behind him without being touched.

Behind him, in the house, seven mirrors began — all together, all at last in perfect synchrony — to show the sitting room empty.
