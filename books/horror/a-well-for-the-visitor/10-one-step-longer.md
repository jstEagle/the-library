## 10. One Step Longer

Theo Holloway had always trusted numbers the way other people trusted God.

Numbers didn't lie to you. Numbers didn't die in a grain accident and leave you the man of the house at fourteen. Numbers didn't look at you across a dinner table with that careful, exhausted tenderness your mother used — the tenderness that said *I can't carry this alone but I'll never say so*. Numbers just were. Two plus two. Four walls. A house of fixed and knowable dimensions.

Which was why, on the ninth night after the surveyor arrived, Theo sat cross-legged on his bedroom floor at 2:14 a.m. with a tape measure unspooled between his knees and his heart going like a trapped bird, because two plus two had stopped equaling four.

His room was 11 feet 6 inches along the north wall. He'd measured it the first night out of boredom, the second night out of habit, the third because he couldn't sleep. On the fourth night it was 11 feet 7 inches. He'd told himself he'd misread the tape. He'd measured five times, kneeling on the cold floorboards with his phone flashlight clenched in his teeth, and five times the steel tongue kissed the baseboard at eleven-seven.

Tonight it was 12 feet even.

Six inches in nine days. Half an inch a night, give or take. The house was growing, and it was growing on a schedule.

Theo sat very still in the dark and listened. Under the floor, faint as a pulse through a wrist, came the ticking he'd been pretending not to hear for a week — the sound Birdie had named first, because Birdie named everything, the counting boy and the humming walls and the warm iron down in the cellar. *The house is ticking, Theo. Don't you hear it? It's like it's happy.*

He hadn't heard it then. He heard it now. And beneath the ticking, almost too low to register, something else: a creak of timber taking on weight, the slow groan of a structure settling into itself the way a body settles into a chair it intends to keep.

He got his notebook.

* * *

The notebook had started as a joke. That was the thing Theo would try to remember later, in the bad stretch, when he tried to reconstruct how it had happened to him — it had started as a *joke*, a way to laugh at the weirdness instead of drowning in it. *Holloway House: A Survey,* he'd written on the first page, in his neatest engineering-lettering. *Prepared by T. Holloway, age 17, licensed by absolutely no one.*

His dad had been the one who loved this stuff. Not ghosts — Dad hadn't believed in ghosts — but buildings. The way an old barn's frame carried its history in its joints. The way you could read a house like a ledger if you knew where to look. He'd walked Theo through the farmhouse when Theo was ten, tapping beams. *Your great-grandfather added the east room in '52. See how the sill doesn't match? Every generation leaves their handwriting in the wood.* Theo had eaten it up. He'd been the kid who drew floor plans in the margins of his math homework.

So the survey began as a tribute, or a coping mechanism, or both. He measured every room. He drew them to scale on graph paper, quarter inch to the foot. He noted the anomalies the way a scientist notes anomalies — dryly, without flinching, trusting that the act of recording was the same as the act of controlling.

*Hall closet: 3'2" deep. (Was 2'10" on initial measurement? Recheck.)*

*Staircase: 13 risers. Counted twice. Mom says there have always been 13. Rafael says 14. Birdie won't say.*

Rafael. The surveyor who'd shown up to appraise the place and somehow never left, whose truck sat dead in the dooryard like a headstone, who looked at the walls the way you'd look at a dog that had bitten someone once — carefully, from an angle, remembering. Theo liked him, mostly. But Rafael flinched at the house, and Theo had decided early that flinching was a luxury they couldn't afford. Somebody had to look straight at the thing. Somebody had to keep records.

By the second week the notebook stopped being a joke.

*Oct. 14. Total interior area per plan: 2,847 sq ft.*
*Oct. 15. Total interior area per plan: 2,849 sq ft.*
*Oct. 16. 2,851.*

Two square feet a day. Compounding. He checked his arithmetic until his eyes burned. He remeasured rooms he'd already done, found the extra inches hiding in corners, in closets, in the depth of window reveals — nowhere dramatic, nothing you'd notice walking through, just the patient accrual of space, like interest. Like the house had opened an account the day they'd moved in and had been making deposits ever since, and now, with the surveyor under its roof, it had decided to get aggressive about it.

At 2 a.m. on the seventeenth, sitting in the kitchen with his graph paper spread out beside the cold stove, Theo did the projection. Two square feet a day, compounding at the observed rate. He ran it out a year. Then five years. Then twenty.

The number he got made him put the pencil down and hold his own hands steady.

In twenty years the house would be bigger than the lot it stood on.

* * *

It was around then that he found the addition.

He wasn't looking for it. That was what he'd swear, later, to anyone who asked — though nobody asked, because by then nobody was asking Theo much of anything except whether he was sleeping, and he'd learned to lie about that with a smile. He was mapping the second floor at midnight, taping his way along the north corridor toward the linen closet, and the corridor was longer than it should have been.

Not much longer. Nine feet, maybe. But he knew this hallway the way he knew his own hands, and his feet told him before the tape did: he was walking past the point where the linen closet door should be, past the window that should be throwing moonlight across the boards, and the moonlight kept coming, and the boards kept coming, and ahead of him the corridor simply continued into a darkness that had no business being there.

Theo stood at the threshold of it for a long time. Long enough for the cold to find the gap between his collar and his neck.

Then — because he was his father's son, because somebody had to keep records — he stepped inside.

The addition was unfinished. That was his first coherent thought, and it was almost comforting: bare studs, subfloor, insulation batting gone gray and soft as old fur. No drywall, no fixtures. A room mid-construction, exactly the kind of thing a growing family might have started and abandoned. Except the Holloways hadn't started anything. Except the lumber smelled new, sap-sweet, like a lumberyard, like the trees had only just agreed to be boards. Except when he held his phone light up to the nearest stud he found a pencil mark on it, faint and old-fashioned in its cursive:

*For the boy who counts.*

Theo's skin went tight from scalp to sole. He backed up two steps. The ticking under the floor was louder here, quicker, and he understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the ticking wasn't a clock at all. Clocks measure time. This was measuring *him* — his heartbeat, maybe, or his attention, or the exact weight of the fact that he had just read those words and could not unread them.

"For the boy who counts."

He should have run. He knew that even then; some animal ledger in him recorded the entry *danger* in a hand far older than pencil. Instead Theo Holloway, seventeen years old, eldest son of a dead man, did the most dangerous thing available to him.

He took out his tape measure, and he measured the room.

Forty feet by thirty. Twelve hundred square feet of house that did not exist on any deed, any plat, any map — house that had grown overnight out of want and timber and whatever listened under the well. His hands shook so badly the first reading came out nonsense, and he steadied himself against a stud — against the stud with the writing, he noticed only afterward, and could not stop noticing — and measured again.

Twelve hundred square feet. In one night. When the rest of the house managed two.

The house wasn't growing evenly. It was growing *toward* something. Toward him.

He went back to his room. He did not sleep. At dawn he drew the addition into his plans in a different color of ink, red, and wrote beneath it: *unauthorized construction. origin unknown. occupant: none.* And then, because the pencil seemed to move without his full permission, he added: *yet.*

* * *

His mother noticed before anyone. Mothers did.

"You're up all night," she said on the porch, hanging laundry in air that smelled like rain deciding. "I hear your floor creak till three, four o'clock. You've got shadows under your eyes like bruises."

"Studying," Theo said. "College apps."

She gave him the look — the one that saw through him to the wall behind him and the wall behind that — but she let it go, because she was carrying enough, because Birdie had started sleeping with the lights on and Rafael had started standing at windows like a man watching for weather that came from underground, and because a mother rations her wars. Theo hated using that. Hated being a line item in her triage. He smiled the smile she needed and carried his notebook upstairs, and that night he slept in the addition for the first time.

He told himself it was tactical. The addition was the front line; you defended the frontier. He brought a sleeping bag, a lantern, his notebook, a thermos of coffee strong enough to strip paint. He set up in the corner farthest from the written-on stud and he lay awake listening to the house tick beneath him like a thing digesting, and somewhere in the small hours — he never could pinpoint when — listening turned into something else. Something warmer.

Because here was the truth he circled for three nights before landing on it: the addition was quiet. Not silent — the ticking lived in the floors everywhere now — but free of the other sounds. Free of Birdie's nightmares murmuring through the wall. Free of his mother's 5 a.m. sighs, the ones she thought nobody heard, the sound of a woman doing arithmetic of her own. Free of Rafael's pacing, and the mutter of his voice through the vents saying names Theo didn't recognize, old names, deed-names. Out here there was just the clean smell of new lumber and the tick, tick, tick, and Theo discovered that if he matched his breathing to it, he slept like the dead.

Like the dead. He woke the first morning in the addition thinking that phrase and laughing at himself, alone, in a room that hadn't existed a week ago.

The second morning he woke and the room was larger again. Three more feet along the south wall. He measured it, logged it, drank his coffee, and felt — he made himself write it honestly, because the notebook deserved honesty even if he didn't — *felt pleased.* Pleased, the way you feel when a savings account ticks upward. The house was building for him. For the boy who counts. Nobody else got a wing. Rafael got suspicion and locked doors. Birdie got games and warnings. Theo got square footage, accruing daily, compounding, *his.*

On the fifth morning he moved his pillow and blanket in permanently. He told his mother the guest room draft was bothering his sinuses. She looked at him for a long moment, and over her shoulder, through the kitchen doorway, he saw Rafael watching with an expression Theo had learned to hate — pity wearing a mask of politeness.

"Theo," Rafael said quietly, later, catching him on the stairs with the tape measure coiled around his neck like a stethoscope. "Can I ask what you're doing?"

"Mapping the house."

"Why?"

"Because somebody should." Theo heard how it sounded, the zealotry in it, and pushed on anyway. "You've seen the numbers, Rafael. You're a surveyor. You know what I'm finding is real."

"I know what I'm finding is real," Rafael said. "That's why I sleep with my boots on." He glanced down the hall, toward the corridor, toward the seam in the wallpaper that hadn't been there in September. "The house gives you what you love, Theo. Did you figure that out yet? It found your mother's grief and offered her a bargain. It found Birdie's loneliness and sent her a friend. It's finding yours right now." His eyes went to the tape measure, to the notebook under Theo's arm, gentle and terrible. "What does a seventeen-year-old kid love enough to build a whole wing for?"

"Control," Theo said, before he could stop himself. And then, quieter, with the honesty the house had been feeding him like sugar water: "My dad died and everything got uncertain. Everything. Money, Mom, all of it. But measurements don't lie. If I can map it, I can hold it. If I can hold it—" He stopped.

"If you can hold it, it can't be taken away," Rafael finished. "Yeah. I had a thing like that too, when I was your age." He put a hand briefly on Theo's shoulder, and his grip was colder than the hallway. "Mine was forgetting. Be careful which one you feed."

* * *

The dreams started the sixth night.

In them, Theo walked the addition and it went on forever, stud after stud receding into sweet-smelling dark, and each bay of the frame held a room not yet built, and each room had a name penciled on its stud in his father's handwriting. *Library. Workshop. Room for the telescope.* Rooms his dad had talked about building, someday, when money allowed, someday that had never come. The house had read Theo's grief the way it read everything, and it was building the someday now, board by board, night by night, an entire future raised from the dead in cheap new pine.

Theo woke from the dreams not screaming but *happy*, which frightened him more than screaming would have. He woke with the plans half-drawn in his mind — additions to the addition, wings off the wing, a whole estate of might-have-beens — and his pencil moving before his eyes fully opened, lantern-light swimming on the graph paper, the ticking under him quick and glad.

*Oct. 29. Addition: 1,880 sq ft. Growth accelerating. Note: I am no longer afraid of the addition. Investigate why. Note: I no longer particularly want to investigate why.*

He read that last line back in the gray of morning and crossed it out. Then he uncrossed it. The notebook deserved honesty. That was becoming the only law he trusted.

His shadow had started doing something odd around then, though he filed that under *sleep deprivation* and refused the entry for several nights. He'd notice it at the edge of the lantern's throw, thrown huge against the bare studs — and it would move a half-second before he did. Lift its arm while his arm still hung. Turn its head while his stayed still, regarding him with the patience of something that had already read to the end of the book. When he stared at it directly it behaved, snapping back into obedience like a dog caught on the furniture. But the lag was real. He timed it once, actually timed it, phone stopwatch in one hand, arm rising in the other: zero point six seconds. His shadow reached the top of its arc before he did.

Every measurement has an error bar, he told himself. Every instrument drifts.

But he knew, in the place where he kept the true ledger, that instruments didn't drift. Only people did. People drifted, degree by degree, half an inch a night, compounding — and the house was very, very good at interest.

* * *

On the last night of October, Theo's mother knocked on the addition's nonexistent door and found him sitting in the center of the finished-est part of the floor with his plans spread around him in a fan, forty sheets of graph paper, the whole future of the house drawn out in red ink to the year 2044.

"Theo." Her voice did the thing it did when she was holding fear in both hands and calling it calm. "Rafael wants to try something tomorrow. Driving out. All of us, one car, see how far the road lets us get." She knelt in the sawdust, careless of her jeans, and took his chin in her hand the way she had when he was small. "I need you to come. I need you to leave the notebook."

Theo looked at her. Behind her, through the plastic-sheeted gap where a window would someday be, the dooryard lay silver under a fat cold moon, and beyond it the county road ran straight and dark toward a world of fixed dimensions.

"Mom," he said, and heard how far away his own voice sounded, how much of it had already been absorbed into the timbers, "the house grows two square feet a day. But the addition grows twelve. Do you understand what that means?" He picked up the newest sheet, the one he'd finished at 4 a.m., the master plan, the someday made ink. "It means it likes me best."

Her face did something he'd never seen it do, something that made the ticking under the floor stutter like a skipped beat.

And down the hall, faint through the fresh-lumber dark, a boy's voice began to knock — the old pattern, the one Rafael flinched at, the one Birdie answered happily — *shave and a haircut* — and waited, patient as principal, for Theo to pay the rest.
