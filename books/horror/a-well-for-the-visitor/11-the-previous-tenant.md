## 11. The Previous Tenant

The attic hatch was where the house didn't want it to be.

Rafael understood that now, three weeks into his confinement, the way a man understands the habits of an animal that has decided not to eat him yet. The pull-cord for the ladder hung in the upstairs hallway on Mondays and Thursdays. On other days it migrated — to Birdie's closet, once, and once to the ceiling above the toilet, which had made Theo laugh for the first time since the roads closed, a short barking sound like something breaking politely. But the attic itself was never mentioned by anyone in the family. Marisol dusted every room with a ferocity that looked like prayer, and her rag never went up. Theo's nightly maps stopped at the ceiling of the second floor as if the plaster were the edge of the known world.

So of course Rafael went up.

He told himself it was professional habit. A surveyor appraises what exists, and the house kept adding square footage like a miser adding coins, and somewhere up there was the ledger. He told himself this while Marisol watched him drag the ladder down, her arms crossed, flour still on her knuckles from the bread she'd abandoned when he pulled the cord.

"You don't have to," she said.

"I know."

"That's not what I meant." She looked at the square of darkness above them the way people look at open water. "Rafael. Nothing up there wants to be found."

"Then it won't be."

She almost smiled at that — he'd learned her smiles came in halves, the left side always arriving late — and went back downstairs to feed children who ate like the food might be counted too.

* * *

The attic was cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather. October pressed against the roof shingles outside, but the cold in here came from underneath, rising through the joists like the memory of the well conducted upward through bone and timber. Rafael's flashlight beam moved across rafters furred with gray dust, boxes labeled in a dead woman's handwriting — CHRISTMAS, TAXES, THEO'S BABY THINGS — and then across something that made him stop breathing for a moment.

A room.

Not a room — the *shape* of one, framed out in the corner where the roofline dipped low. Two-by-fours stood naked and unfinished, no drywall, no floor but the rough planking, and yet the space inside the studs was swept clean. Swept, and furnished: a cot with a army blanket folded at its foot, precise as a hospital corner. A candle stub. A tin cup turned upside down on a crate, drying.

Someone had lived here. Recently enough that the blanket held a crease.

And against the far stud, wedged where a wall would someday be if the house ever got around to growing it, sat a metal document box, green, rusted at the latches, with a name stenciled on the lid in flaking paint:

**E. HOLLOWAY**

Holloway. Not the same Holloways — Marisol's husband had been Daniel Holloway, and Daniel's people had come to the farm in the nineties, bought it cheap from an estate sale nobody would talk about. Rafael knew because he'd read the deed chain in week one, back when he still believed paperwork meant anything. Before Daniel: a family named Pruitt. Before the Pruitts—

He knelt and worked the latches. They gave with a sound like a small surrender.

Inside: a birth certificate. A marriage license. A photograph of a young man with a long face and careful eyes standing in front of this same porch, squinting into the same sun. And beneath the papers, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, a journal. Cloth-bound, hand-sewn, the cover soft with handling. On the first page, in a slanted, patient hand:

*Edwin Holloway. 1974. If you are reading this, you are either my family or you are the next one, and God forgive me, I cannot tell which is worse.*

* * *

Rafael read sitting on the swept planking with his back against the cold studs, the flashlight propped against the tin cup so it threw Edwin's words upward in long shadows. He read the way he'd learned to read survey reports — fast first, for shape, then slow, for error. There was no error here. That was the horror of it. Edwin Holloway had been a careful man, and careful men make the best witnesses.

*March 1974.* The count had started, as it always started, with small things. A door gaining a hinge. The staircase running one step long. His wife, Ruth, finding her garden rows measured in the morning by someone who left no footprints. Their boy, Amos, six years old, talking about a friend named Wren.

*I did not believe in it. I am a farmer; belief is for winter. But Ruth stopped sleeping, and Amos drew the same picture forty times — a stick boy under a stick house with a circle below it, and numbers coming down the circle like rain. I asked him what the numbers were. He said, "Daddy, they're us."*

*April. I went down to the cap with a crowbar. I am ashamed of how long it took me to work up the courage, and more ashamed of what happened. The iron was warm. Under the warmth there was a ticking, and under the ticking there was a voice, and the voice was patient the way water is patient. It did not threaten me. It thanked me for coming down. It said the family was nearly complete and asked if I wanted to hear the number.*

*I ran. I will write that plainly because my father taught me plain writing saves lives: I ran up the stairs like a child and I did not go down again for nine days, and when I finally did, the ticking had doubled.*

Then, in May, the entries changed. The handwriting got larger, looser, the letters of a man writing fast before courage cooled.

*Ruth has an idea and I hate it and I love her for it. The thing below does not want blood. We checked — the Pruitts' bones are in the churchyard, all of them, died ordinary deaths over sixty years, and the well never touched their bodies. It wants the COUNTING. It wants to finish its arithmetic. So let it finish on me.*

*I will give it myself. One instead of four. I have thought about it every way a man can think about a thing and I cannot find the flaw. It counts people. I am a person. I will go down on the last night of May and I will tell it: take me, close the book, we are done, the rest of them are yours to release.*

*If this works, burn this journal and take Amos to Ruth's sister in Ohio and never say the word "well" again in your life.*

*If this doesn't work, then whoever finds this needs to know why, and I am sorry, because I don't know why yet. I only know that when I stood at the cap tonight and offered myself, the ticking slowed — interested — and then it went on. Like a shopkeeper waving off a customer with the wrong coin.*

*I offered it my body and it declined my body.*

*What else is there?*

Rafael lowered the journal onto his knees. In the dark above him, between the rafters, something shifted — not footsteps, just the house settling, just wood remembering its weight. He waited until it finished.

There were only a few pages left. June 1974. The handwriting had shrunk again, cramped and small, a man writing in the margins of his own mind.

*I understand now. God help me, I understand.*

*It does not count bodies. It counts MEMORIES. A person is only a person to this house insofar as the house remembers them — and the house remembers what we remember. Ruth remembered her mother's face until the day she died and the house holds that face still; I have seen it in the hallway mirror at 3 a.m., an old woman smiling where my reflection should be. What we carry, it keeps. What we carry, it can spend.*

*That is why my body was the wrong coin. My body walks out the door every day to the far field. My body could leave. But my MEMORY — the shape of me in this house, the way I know which stair creaks, the smell of Ruth's bread, Amos's weight on my shoulders — that cannot walk anywhere. That is nailed in. That is the timber.*

*It requires a willing memory. Not a sacrifice of flesh. A sacrifice of SELF. Someone must stand at the cap and give it everything they remember of being themselves, freely, completely, holding nothing back — and hold nothing back is the whole impossible part, because the moment you try to keep one memory safe, one small warm thing, you have paid with a counterfeit coin and it knows, it always knows—*

*I tried. I stood down there on Midsummer night and I opened myself like a door and I gave it twenty years of being Edwin Holloway and do you know what it took?*

*Nothing.*

*Because at the bottom of everything, in the last locked room of me, I was keeping one thing. Amos laughing. Just that. One second of my son laughing on this porch, and I could not give it up, I physically could not, my hands shook trying, and the well went silent with disgust and the count went on.*

*A man cannot unlove himself by decision. That is the trap. That is the whole design. It builds a house you love and then asks you to stop loving yourself in it.*

The last entry was dated July 3rd, 1974, and it was different. Shorter. The handwriting wasn't Edwin's anymore.

Rafael recognized it anyway.

Round, careful letters. A child's best cursive, taught by nuns or by fear, the *y*'s looping too far, the crossbars ruled straight as if drawn along an invisible edge. He knew that handwriting the way you know your own face in a bad photograph. He had written grocery lists in it fifteen years ago. He had written *I will forget Wren* fifty times in a composition book until the words stopped meaning anything, and the house had believed him, and let him go.

The entry read:

*i readed the book. edwin is gone now. ruth says he walked into the fields and the fields kept him. amos dont remember him at all wich is sad but also safer.*

*mister wren says i shouldnt be up here but mister wren says alot of things. he says the house likes me. he says thats bad. he says if i stay till im counted i can maybe trade places like edwin tried but i have to WANT it all the way and i dont want it all the way i want to GO HOME even tho i dont know where home is.*

*the new family comes in august. daniel holloway. wren says they will be good to me if i stay but wren lies sometimes to be kind.*

*i am going to forget. wren showed me how. you write it down and then you mean it and then the house carries it for you and you get to be light. i am going to forget the well and the counting and mostly him. sorry wren. you were my best friend and that is exactly why i cant keep you.*

*if i ever come back, i wont know why, and that is the point.*

*r. eriksen*

*age 9*

The flashlight trembled in Rafael's grip, and the beam swung across the unfinished room, across the cot and the cup and the swept floor, and he understood — with the slow, tectonic certainty of a foundation failing — that the room had been built *for him*. That some part of this house, some patient rafter-deep intention, had been waiting fifteen years for the boy who wrote those words to climb back up and finish reading his own sentence.

Below him, faint through two floors of old pine, the cellar ticks began.

Not steady. Not patient.

Quickened — the way a clock quickens when it hears its own hour walking toward it.

And under the ticking, muffled and many-voiced, the knocking in the walls picked up the rhythm, and Rafael realized with a surveyor's cold clarity that the knocks weren't random at all. They'd never been random. They were a number, and the number was getting smaller, and the last page of Edwin Holloway's journal was still resting in his hands, and the ink of that final childish line had not faded the way the rest had.

It was fresh.

As if the house had been keeping it wet, all these years, for him to read.
