## 12. What Rafael Forgot

The cellar door had been locked when Rafael first surveyed the house — he'd noted it in his report as *utility access, secured* — but on the ninth night it stood open, and the dark below breathed out the smell of wet iron and old rain.

He should have gone back upstairs. He knew that the way you know a stove is hot: abstractly, uselessly. Instead he took the flashlight from his jacket pocket and put one foot on the top stair, which was cold through his sock, and then another, and the house let him descend the way a throat lets food go down.

The stairs were stone. He remembered that before he saw it — remembered the particular hollow knock of boot heel on cellar stone — and the remembering arrived like a fish surfacing, whole and gasping, from water he hadn't known was there.

*You've been down these stairs before.*

"No," he said aloud, to no one, to the walls that listened. "I haven't."

The beam of the flashlight found the well cap in the center of the floor: a disc of black iron wide enough to dance on, ringed by bolts, each bolt head worn smooth as if generations of hands had worried them. It was warm. He could feel it from six feet away, a heat like an animal sleeping under a blanket, and beneath it, faint and patient, came the ticking.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

He'd heard it every night since he'd stayed. Through the floor of the guest room, through the soles of his shoes at dinner, soft as a second heartbeat under the house's first. Marisol didn't seem to hear it. Theo heard it and measured it — Rafael had seen the boy's notebook, columns of intervals shrinking week over week, graphed like a fever chart. Birdie heard it and counted along, lips moving, and the house hummed its low approval through the joists.

Rafael crouched beside the cap. The ticking was louder here, or perhaps not louder — *closer*, as if each tick were happening inside his own ribs rather than under the iron.

And the smell rose up around him, wet iron and old rain and something sweeter underneath, apples going soft in a cellar bin, and suddenly he was eight years old again, and he was not visiting this memory. He was standing in it.

* * *

A woman's hands, flour to the wrists. A kitchen with yellow curtains and a radio playing something with horns in it. A man's voice calling from the yard, *Rafe! Rafe, come see what we found!* — and the astonishing, unearned warmth of being called for. Of belonging to a name someone used kindly.

He was eight. He was small for eight. He had a paper grocery bag of belongings that contained three shirts, a toothbrush, a library book about planets, and a photograph of a mother whose face time had already begun to blur. He was a foster child. His caseworker's car idled in the gravel drive, and the caseworker said, *This is the Aldous place, Rafe. Last stop on the list, so let's make it work,* and Mrs. Aldous knelt down and looked at him the way nobody looked at children in the group home — like he was a person who had arrived, not a case that had been assigned.

*We've got room,* she said. *We've always got room.*

There were other children. Two Aldous boys of their own, red-haired and loud, and two more fosters who rotated through like weather. And there was Wren, sitting on the porch rail, swinging his legs, watching Rafael with eyes that were too calm for a child's face.

*Who's that?* Rafael asked.

Mrs. Aldous followed his gaze and frowned slightly, the frown of someone looking at empty air. *Who's what, sweetheart?*

And Rafael understood, with the terrible clarity children have and lose, that he was the only one who could see the boy on the rail. That this made him special. That special, in this house, might mean something other than good.

* * *

Rafael came back to himself on his knees on the cellar floor, the flashlight rolling away from his hand, its beam spinning across the stone. His face was wet. He didn't remember crying. The ticking went on beneath him, unhurried, satisfied, like a clock that had been waiting fifteen years for its owner to wind it.

"I lived here," he whispered.

The house did not answer. The house never answered in words. But the warmth of the well cap deepened, and the air in the cellar thickened, and he felt the memory-gates straining against whatever held them shut — a dam of forgetting with hairline cracks running through it now, and behind the dam, everything.

He crawled to the stairs because his legs wouldn't do anything prouder, and he climbed, and the kitchen above was bright and ordinary and smelled of coffee, and none of it felt real anymore. None of it had ever been real in the way he'd thought. He had walked into this house nine days ago with a clipboard and a professional detachment he'd built like armor over twenty years, and he had called it a stranger's farmhouse, and the whole time the boards under his feet had been boards he'd run across as a child, barefoot, chased by the Aldous boys, laughing.

Laughing. In this house. Before the count.

* * *

That night he didn't sleep so much as sink, and sinking, he remembered more.

The summer he turned nine. The way the house had begun to change around the edges — a hallway a little longer than yesterday, a window where no window had been, rooms that didn't sum. Mr. Aldous pacing the property line with a surveyor's chain, muttering numbers that wouldn't hold still. Mrs. Aldous nailing a horseshoe over the cellar door and taking it down the next day because the ticking got louder when iron hung near iron.

And the children. One by one.

Not taken all at once — that would have been a horror anyone could name. No, they thinned out gradually, politely. The Aldous boys' cousin came to visit in June and left in July and no one could quite say afterward whether he'd ever existed; his bedroom became a sewing room, and everyone agreed it had always been a sewing room. A foster girl named Denny, who'd been there when Rafael arrived, simply wasn't at breakfast one morning, and her spot at the table was gone by lunch, the bench shortened, the plate in the cupboard with no chip or wear, brand new, as if it had never been used. As if she had never been used.

And the adults explaining it without explaining. *She moved on to another placement.* *Families change, Rafe. That's what families do.* And the house humming its low approval whenever the explanations were spoken, and the ticking keeping its time.

Rafael, nine years old, had done the arithmetic children aren't supposed to be able to do. He'd counted the chairs. He'd counted the beds. He'd counted the names in Mrs. Aldous's Bible, where births were recorded in a fine old hand, generation after generation, and noticed that every family line ended. Every single one. Not faded out — *ended*, the last entry followed by nothing, the ink of the final dates all the same brown-black, as if written by the same pen, in the same year, over and over across two centuries.

1911. 1953. 1968. Each family's final page torn out at the same place.

He'd shown the Bible to Wren. They'd sat on the porch rail together, Rafael holding the book open on his knees, and Wren had looked at it for a long time.

*Now you understand,* Wren said. *It counts. It's always counting. It counts a family down until there's nothing left to count, and then it rests, and then new people come, and it starts again.*

*Counts down to what?*

*Zero,* Wren said, and smiled, and his smile was the saddest thing Rafael had ever seen, though he couldn't have said why. *Zero is quiet. Zero is rest. Everyone here is just waiting to find out their number.*

*I don't want a number.*

*No,* Wren agreed. *You especially don't.*

*Why me especially?*

Wren had swung his legs and looked out at the orchard, at the light going gold and long, and said nothing, and Rafael had known even then that the silence was an answer shaped like mercy.

* * *

The cracks widened on the third night of remembering. Rafael sat up in the guest room bed at 2 a.m., drenched, and the memory came through whole:

The winter he was ten. The count nearly finished.

He could see the Aldous table now with adult eyes laid over child ones: four places where there had once been seven. Mr. Aldous gray-faced, measuring the cellar stairs every morning with a carpenter's rule and finding them longer. Mrs. Aldous smiling at Rafael across the table with an expression he recognized only now, fifteen years later, from Marisol's face — the fierce arithmetic of a mother counting her remaining children and hating the sum.

*Rafe,* Mrs. Aldous had said that winter night, kneeling in front of him by the fire, holding his hands. *If anything happens — if you wake up someday and can't remember living here — that will be all right. Do you hear me? Whatever you can't remember, it isn't your fault, and it isn't a loss. Some things are kinder forgotten.*

And he had cried and said he didn't want to forget, and she had held him, and over her shoulder he had seen Wren standing in the doorway, and Wren had shaken his head slowly, once, the way you signal someone not to cross a road.

Because it was already decided. Because Wren had already gone down to the well.

Rafael remembered — the last crack, the flood — waking in the night to the sound of the cellar door, and following, and seeing Wren standing on the iron cap in his bare feet, arms out, head bowed, speaking words that weren't words, and the ticking racing like a heart in panic, and the bolts turning themselves, and Wren looking back at him with those too-calm eyes and saying:

*Go out the front door, Rafe. Walk to the end of the drive and keep walking and don't look back. By morning you won't remember my name. You won't remember this house. You'll be free of your number.*

*I don't want to forget you.*

*I know.* And Wren had smiled, and it broke Rafael's heart then and broke it again now, fifteen years downstream. *That's why it has to be me. The house takes willing memories, willing ones are worth more than bodies — it can't take yours if I give it mine instead. I'm already counted, Rafe. I was counted first, a long time ago. I'm spending my number on you.*

*Wren—*

*Forget me,* the boy said gently. *It's the only gift I have. Forget me, and live long enough to be happy.*

And Rafael had walked out the front door into the snow, and the snow had swallowed the house behind him, and by the time he reached the county road he was a boy with a paper bag and no address, and when the caseworker found him shivering at the bus depot she said, *Good Lord, where did you come from?* and he honestly, truly did not know.

They gave him a new placement. Then another. The photograph of his mother blurred past recovery. And sometimes — he remembered this too, now, the shape of the absence — sometimes he would catch himself knocking a pattern on things without knowing why. Four knocks, pause, two knocks. A rhythm he'd invented on this porch with a boy no one else could see. A rhythm the walls of the Holloway house had answered on his first day of the appraisal, and he'd told himself it was settling.

Settling. God. Fifteen years of settling.

* * *

Morning came gray through the guest room window. Rafael lay in it a long time, staring at a water stain on the ceiling that he now recognized — had traced with his finger as a child and named *the whale*. The debt sat on his chest with a weight he could finally measure.

He had not escaped. That was the thing he'd gotten wrong for fifteen years, the load-bearing lie of his whole adult life. You didn't escape this house. Wren had bought him out — had spent himself like currency — and the purchase had broken the count mid-number, left the well hungry and unfinished, left the Aldous line to dwindle out alone in the years after, because the herald was gone and the mechanism jammed and the house had slept, sulking, for a decade and a half.

Until Rafael Eriksen walked up the drive with a clipboard and knocked on the door he'd once run out of, and the house woke, and the ticking started, and a little girl began to see a boy on the porch rail.

Birdie. Birdie could see Wren because Rafael could see Wren — because the forgetting had never fully taken, because some root of him had stayed planted here, and the house had grown up around that root the moment he returned. His debt hadn't expired. It had accrued. Fifteen years of interest, compounding quietly, and the interest was being paid in Holloway names.

Marisol. Theo. Birdie. The count was at four — Birdie had said so herself, furious and unafraid — and every one of those four was standing on a bill Rafael had signed with his own forgetting.

He got up. He dressed. In the bathroom mirror a man looked back at him with his father's unknown face and his mother's blurred one, and beneath both, faintly, if you knew how to look, the face of a boy who had once laughed in this house.

Downstairs, the kitchen smelled of coffee and pancakes, and Marisol stood at the stove with her back to him, and Birdie was setting the table, counting the plates under her breath — *one, two, three, four* — and stopping at four every time, and starting over, hoping, he realized, to make five appear. To make the count wrong.

"Sleep okay?" Marisol asked, not turning.

"No," Rafael said.

She turned then. She'd been fraying for days — he'd watched it happen, watched her find her husband's papers, watched trust thin between them like ice on a pond — and now she looked at him straight, spatula in hand, a woman deciding something.

"Good," she said. "Because I need you honest this morning. I need to ask you what you're not telling us, Rafael. And I need you to tell me."

Behind her, at the window, brief as breath on glass, a boy stood on the outside sill of the second story, looking in, nodding once — *tell her* — and was gone.

Rafael set down his coffee cup. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Everything else in him was shaking.

"It starts," he said, "with a family called Aldous."
