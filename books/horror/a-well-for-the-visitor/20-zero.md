## 20. Zero

Birdie counted the way other children breathed — without deciding to. She counted stairs (fourteen going down, fifteen coming up, and Mama said that was impossible but Birdie knew it was just the house being hungry). She counted the ticks under the cellar floor, which had been slow as rain in October and were now fast as a heart running from something. She counted the people left in the room with her, because Wren had taught her that counting was just another word for loving: you counted the things you could not stand to lose.

One. Mama's hand on her hair.

Two. Rafael, kneeling by the boarded window with his surveyor's pencil behind his ear like always, even now, even at the end of everything, as if measuring the world could keep it honest.

Three had been Theo, and three was gone into the walls, and Birdie did not let herself think of him as a number anymore because numbers were how the house ate you. She thought of him as Theo-with-the-pencil-chewing and Theo-who-let-her-win-at-rummy and Theo-whose-shadow-walked-ahead-of-him-now, and she held all of those in her chest like stones.

And one more. The one who wasn't a person exactly, or was more than a person, who had been a boy once centuries before anyone built this house over his well. Wren.

The floor ticked.

Mama flinched. Rafael didn't; he'd gone past flinching days ago, into some country where his face was smooth and still like water before rain. He looked at Birdie across the lamplight of the front parlor — the only room left that still behaved, the only door that opened onto anywhere at all.

"Birdie," he said. "How many?"

She listened. She put her ear against the boards, against the warm wood that hummed like a struck bell, and she counted what came up through the soles of her feet, through her knees, through her small fierce heart.

"Zero," she said.

* * *

The house heard her say it.

Birdie felt it hear her, the way you feel someone step up behind you on a dark stair. Every board in every wall went taut and quiet, the whole farmhouse holding its breath around them, and then — from below, from the deep stone throat beneath the cellar, from the place where the iron cap sat warm as a sleeping animal — came a sound none of them had ever heard it make.

It exhaled.

Not the ticking. Not the knocking, not the long slow count that had eaten generations of Holloways and Hales and everyone whose names ended in the same year on the same deed. This was vast and low and relieved, a sound like the earth itself setting down something heavy it had carried for four hundred years, and the lamp flame bent sideways though no wind moved, and dust lifted off the ceiling beams in a slow silver spiral, and Mama grabbed Birdie and Rafael grabbed both of them, and the three of them stood in the middle of the parlor holding on to each other while the house let go.

"It's done," Rafael whispered. "Wren — it's done."

But Wren wasn't there. Wren had walked down the cellar steps an hour ago beside Rafael and had not come back up, and Birdie understood, in the way she understood the counting games, that he was never coming back up, and that this was not the same as dying, and also that it was.

The walls began to shrink.

* * *

Birdie felt it first in her teeth, a pressure change like a storm arriving in reverse. Then she saw it. The parlor's far wall — the one that had grown six feet closer every night since the windows turned to plaster — shuddered, sighed, and slid backward. Not fast. Slowly, the way ice goes out of a pond in spring: reluctantly, then all at once, then reluctantly again. The wallpaper uncreased. A seam that hadn't been there yesterday appeared and widened, and behind it was the old wall, the true wall, with its water stain shaped like a rabbit if you squinted.

"The staircase," Birdie said, and ran for the hall.

Mama called her name but Birdie was already moving, seven years old and quick as a wren herself, bare feet slapping boards that were cool now instead of warm. The staircase stood where it had always stood, except it was shorter. She could see it plainly: fourteen steps where there had been twenty-two, the extra ones folding away like paper fans closing, each false step dissolving upward into nothing with a sound like a page turning. At the top, the door to the addition — Theo's wing, the wing that grew — hung open onto darkness that was getting less dark.

"Theo!" she screamed into it. "THEO! You can come out now! It's zero!"

The darkness breathed.

Then it gave him back.

He came out wrong, the way things come out of deep water — gray-lipped, eyes wide, frost on his eyelashes in a warm house, his shadow lagging behind him now instead of ahead, stumbling to catch up like a dog called home. He made it three steps past the threshold of the growing wing and folded, and Birdie caught him as much as a seven-year-old can catch a seventeen-year-old, which is to say she got under his arm and screamed for Mama until the whole house rang with her voice, and the house, shrinking, carried the sound all the way to the kitchen without her having to stop for breath.

Mama came flying. Rafael came up from the cellar with the iron crowbar still in his hand and the well dust on his knees, and together they pulled Theo across the hall, across the threshold, into the ordinary world of the kitchen with its cold stove and its dead phone, and laid him flat on the linoleum.

For a moment nobody breathed.

Then Theo coughed — a huge, ugly, wonderful cough — and said, "There were so many doors," and started to cry, seventeen years old and crying on the kitchen floor while his little sister patted his cheek and his mother wrapped herself around him, and outside, faint and far away, a car passed on the county road going somewhere in a straight line.

Birdie heard it and started laughing and couldn't stop.

* * *

They sat up all night, the four of them, in the kitchen, because nobody wanted to be in any other room and nobody wanted to sleep. The house kept shrinking around them, gently, apologetically, like a big dog curling smaller after being scolded. Rooms they had lost came back. The bathroom door reappeared where a wall had swallowed it. The hallway grew its coat closet again. Once, near dawn, the whole frame settled with a groan and a picture fell off the wall — a landscape of some lake, crooked for years — and when Mama hung it straight it stayed straight, and Birdie watched her do it twice to be sure.

By morning the square footage matched the deed. Rafael checked. He got out his tape measure and his clipboard and his stubborn surveyor's soul and he measured every room twice, and when he came back into the kitchen his hands were shaking and his eyes were wet and he said, "It sums," like those two words were the most beautiful thing he'd ever said about a building, and maybe they were.

"Is it over?" Mama asked. "Really over? Or is it just — resting?"

Rafael looked at Birdie.

Everyone looked at Birdie, because Birdie was the one who spoke counting, the one Wren had chosen to teach, and they all knew the answer lived in whatever she could hear through the floors.

She slid off her chair. She pressed her palms flat to the linoleum, then her ear, the way she used to press her ear to the warm cap in the cellar, listening for the mechanism underneath the world.

She listened a long time.

Nothing.

No ticks. No knock-pattern, the one Wren used to tap on her headboard, shave-and-a-haircut minus the haircut, the rhythm that meant *I'm here, I'm counting.* No hum of approval when numbers were spoken aloud. No hunger in the boards. Just the house being a house: pipes ticking as they cooled, which was only water, which was only plumbing, which was only the dumb blessed ordinary noise of a thing that was not alive and had never really wanted to be.

"There's nothing under us," Birdie announced, sitting up. "Just dirt."

"Just dirt," Rafael repeated, wondering.

"And Wren," she added, because that was different. That was not nothing. "He's in the dirt too. But he's not counting." She considered. "He's finished."

Nobody said anything for a while. Mama reached over and smoothed Birdie's hair, and her hand was trembling, and Birdie let her, because mothers needed to do that kind of thing the way the house had needed to count, and it would take Mama a long time to unlearn it, and Birdie had decided she could wait.

"What does finished mean?" Theo asked hoarsely. He had a blanket around his shoulders and a mug of cocoa he hadn't drunk. "For him. Is he —"

"He's zero," Birdie said simply.

And she found, testing the words in her mouth like river stones, that she liked the sound of it. Zero. All her life — all seven years of it, which was her whole life — numbers had been teeth. Numbers were how the house held them, how it kept its books, how it ate. Four people, three people, two, one. She had lain awake doing the arithmetic of her own family and hating every digit. But zero wasn't a tooth. Zero was an open hand. Zero was the count with nothing left in it, the ledger closed, the well paid off. When she said *zero* the word didn't hum. It just lay there, round and empty and clean, like a ring box after Christmas.

"I like zero," she told them. "It's my favorite number now."

"It was mine first," Rafael said quietly, and smiled, and it was the first real smile Birdie had seen on him, the smile of a man putting down a weight he'd carried since he was eight years old.

* * *

The roads ran straight.

They tested them that afternoon, all four of them crammed into Rafael's truck, because none of them quite believed it and none of them would say so out loud. Rafael drove to the county line and back, then past it, then ten miles east toward Millbrook, and the road did what roads do: it went where it went, mile after mile, fence post after fence post, and the farmhouse did not appear in the rearview mirror wearing a different direction, and the cell phone showed today's date, and when Mama called her sister in Ohio the call connected on the first ring and her sister answered sounding annoyed and alive and gloriously ordinary, and Mama had to pull over because she was crying too hard to drive.

Birdie sat in the truck bed — Rafael said it was fine, just this once, rules were different today — and watched the world run backward past her. Fields. A church steeple. A dead gas station coming back to life with two cars parked at its pumps. Everything unshrinking, everything returning to its right size, as if the whole county had been held in a fist for months and was slowly opening its fingers.

When they got home the porch light was off.

That was the thing that undid Birdie a little. For as long as she could remember, the porch light had burned day and night, yellow and patient, waiting for them, welcoming them home the way a spider's web welcomes a fly, and she had hated it without knowing why. Now it was dark. Just a bulb in a fixture, minding its own business. She stood on the steps looking at it for a long time.

"You okay, Birdie-bird?" Rafael asked from the doorway.

"He used to sit there," she said. "On the rail. Before you came. He told me he used to sit on the rail of houses all over this county, hundreds of years, waiting for whoever the house picked next." She touched the rail. It was just wood. Cold, splintery, ordinary wood. "It's weird that it's just wood."

"Yeah," Rafael said. His voice did something complicated. "It is."

Inside, the house smelled like dust and lemon cleaner and supper starting — Mama was cooking, actually cooking, actual food, the first real meal in weeks — and the rooms were all their right sizes, and upstairs Theo was taking a shower with the door unlocked, which he hadn't done since his shadow started walking ahead of him, and Birdie stood in the middle of the hallway and counted, one last time, just to be sure, just because counting was hers and she wasn't going to let the house ruin it forever.

One mama. One Theo. One Rafael. One Birdie.

Four.

Four was a good number. Four was everybody. Four was a full table and a full truck bed and enough hands to carry Theo inside when he couldn't walk. Let the house have had its zeros. Birdie was keeping the fours.

* * *

That night she dreamed of the cellar, the way you dream of places that took something from you. But in the dream the iron cap was gone, and in its place was a circle of clean dark earth, soft-turned, like a garden bed prepared in spring for something that hadn't been planted yet. And sitting on the edge of the circle, feet dangling down into the dirt that wasn't a well anymore, was Wren.

He looked older. Not much. A year, maybe. Enough.

"You're not scared," he said, in the dream, in his voice like a knock on wood.

"No," Birdie said. "Are you?"

He thought about it seriously, the way he thought about everything, tilting his head. "I don't know yet," he said. "I've never been finished before. It's very quiet." And then, because he was still Wren, still the boy who taught her games: "Do you want to know what zero sounds like, from down here?"

"Yes," said Birdie, who always wanted to know.

"Like before the first number," Wren said, smiling his old smile, younger and older than any boy had a right to be. "Like the part of the song before anybody starts singing. Like a porch with nobody on it, waiting for nobody, and that's all right." He lay back into the clean earth, arms spread, comfortable as a kid making a snow angel in July. "You'll forget me, probably. That's allowed now. Forgetting doesn't cost anything anymore."

"I won't," Birdie said fiercely, because she was seven and she meant it.

"Okay," said Wren, pleased, and closed his eyes, and the dream filled up with the sound of nothing at all, and Birdie slept the whole night through without waking once, in a house that was only a house, above ground that was only ground, while somewhere far below a boy who had finally stopped counting slept too.

In the morning, Rafael's truck was packed.

He stood by it in the gray early light with his clipboard under his arm, and Mama stood on the porch — the empty, ordinary, nobody-sitting-on-it porch — with her arms crossed against the cold, and neither of them was saying anything, and Birdie watched them through the kitchen window with a piece of toast in each hand, working out faster than either of them what the silence meant.

Because the appraisal was done. The house summed. There was no reason for Rafael Eriksen to stay, and every reason in the world, and Birdie could see him standing there trying to measure the distance between those two facts and finding, for the first time in his life, that the numbers wouldn't hold still.

Mama solved it the way she solved everything.

"Rafael," she said, loud enough to reach the kitchen, loud enough to reach the road, loud enough maybe to reach the clean dark earth below. "What are you doing Sunday?"

Birdie grinned around her toast.

Sunday, she thought. One mama, one Theo, one Rafael, one Birdie, one dinner table, one house that was only a house.

One was her second-favorite number after all.
