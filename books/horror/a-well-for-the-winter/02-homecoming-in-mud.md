## 2. Homecoming in Mud

The county road gave up on gravel a mile from the property and became something older — two ruts of packed clay with a spine of grass between them, the kind of road that existed because people had driven it, not because anyone had decided it should be there. Astrid's rental sedan bottomed out twice before he stopped trying to avoid the potholes and simply surrendered to them, letting the car take the abuse while he gripped the wheel and watched the Sterling land unfold through the windshield like a sentence he'd been dreading since childhood.

October had already done its work here. The birches along the fence line had gone the color of old bone, and their leaves lay in wet drifts against the wire, plastered there by last night's rain. The fields were fallow, half-turned, black earth gleaming where the plow had opened them. Beyond them, gray under a sky the color of dishwater, stood the house.

It was smaller than he remembered. That was the first lie memory told him, and he corrected it automatically, the way he corrected errors in a spreadsheet: the house hadn't shrunk; he had grown, and his sense of the place had calcified at eleven years old, the summer of the last visit, the summer nobody talked about afterward. Two stories, white clapboard gone the gray of unbleached linen, a roof with one sagging plane over the east wing. A barn to the north with its foundation stones showing through the mud like broken teeth. And past the barn, half-hidden by a stand of spruce that hadn't been there in his childhood —

The well-house.

He saw it before he meant to see it. It sat low and square in its clearing, stone-walled, roofed in cedar shakes gone silver-black with weather, looking less like a building than like a headstone someone had built a roof over. Even at this distance he could see the chain wrapped around the door. New chain. Galvanized steel, bright as a scar against the dark wood of the door, with a padlock hanging beneath the handle like a dropped jaw.

Someone had locked it recently. Someone had looked at that little building and decided it needed locking *now*.

Astrid filed the observation away in the part of his mind that never stopped working, the part that had made him good at his job — the part that noticed when a number appeared where no number should be. He parked behind Maren's truck, a Ford so old its model year was a matter of family legend, and sat for a moment with his hands on the wheel.

He had rehearsed this. On the six-hour drive he had rehearsed it the way he rehearsed difficult client meetings: grief first, questions second, demands third. He would say he was sorry about Anders. He would not mention the casket. Not yet. Not until he'd seen their faces, heard their voices, established a baseline the way you establish a baseline before an audit — because everything after that depended on knowing what normal looked like for these people.

He got out into air that smelled of wet earth and woodsmoke and something underneath both, faintly mineral, like water standing too long in iron.

The front door opened before he reached the porch steps.

Maren stood in it. His sister was forty-one now and the years had spent her differently than they'd spent him — she'd gone lean and hard, weathered, her hair pulled back in a knot so tight it drew the skin at her temples. She wore a man's flannel shirt over jeans and rubber boots, and she looked at him across the muddy yard with an expression Astrid could not immediately classify, because it was not grief and it was not surprise.

It was recognition. The grim, unsurprised recognition of a woman watching a prophecy walk up her driveway.

"Astrid." Just his name. No warmth in it, but no cold either. Something more careful than either.

"Maren." He climbed the steps. Up close he could see new lines around her mouth, and the particular pallor of someone who hadn't slept properly in weeks. "I'm sorry. About Anders. I know I should have come sooner, I—"

"You came when you could." She stepped aside to let him in. "You always did."

The phrase snagged on something. He carried it into the house with him.

* * *

Inside, the homestead smelled exactly as it had in his memories, which unsettled him more than any change could have. Woodsmoke and dried herbs hanging from the ceiling beams, onions in a basket, the deep animal warmth of a house heated by a single cast-iron stove. The walls held the same photographs — sepia Sterlings staring out of oval frames, generations of narrow faces and light eyes, and among them, on the mantel, a framed picture of Anders and himself at eight years old, identical boys in matching sweaters, squinting into a sun that had set thirty years ago.

Maren took his coat without asking and hung it on a peg that already had a space cleared for it. He noticed that. There was a space. As if the peg had been waiting.

"Torvald's in the barn," she said. "Ilsa's doing lessons. She'll want to see you."

"How is she? How are—" He stopped, recalibrated. "How are all of you? This must be—"

"It's been a hard week." Maren said it flatly, the way you'd report rainfall totals. She moved to the stove and lifted a kettle that was already steaming, poured water over grounds in a pot that had been sitting warm for him. For him. "Sit down, Astrid. You look like you drove the whole way without stopping."

"I did."

"I know. You always did that too." She set the coffee in front of him, and her hand, he noticed, was steady in the way of hands that have practiced being steady. "You look like him."

The words landed in the quiet room like a dropped glass.

"Don't," Astrid said. Then, because the silence demanded more: "Don't do that. Don't start with the twin thing. I'm here to bury my brother, Maren. Or to find out why there's a stranger in his casket."

Her face didn't change. That was the thing that frightened him, later, when he understood what he'd been looking at: her face didn't change at all. No confusion, no shock, no *what are you talking about*. She only looked at him for a long moment with those pale Sterling eyes, and then she said, quietly:

"So the funeral home called you too."

*Too.* The word went through him like a splinter entering skin — painless on entry, wrong only once it was in deep. "Called me about what?"

But footsteps on the stairs cut the conversation off, and a girl came down into the kitchen — fifteen, maybe sixteen, tall for her age, with Anders's same light eyes and a braid coming loose over one shoulder. She stopped dead when she saw him.

For three full seconds she stared. And Astrid watched something move across her young face that he would spend the rest of this story trying to name: not the shock of seeing an uncle she barely knew, but the shock of seeing something *expected*. Checked off. Like a tally.

"Uncle Astrid," Ilsa said. Her voice was careful, pitched flat, the way children speak when they've been coached. "You came home."

"I came for the funeral," he said. "I'm sorry about your—"

"It always starts when someone comes home," she said, to no one in particular, or perhaps to Maren, and turned and walked back up the stairs as calmly as if she'd forgotten something.

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Rain began again against the kitchen window, soft and businesslike.

"What does she mean by that?" Astrid asked. "What starts?"

"Eat something," Maren said. "Then we'll talk. Torvald will want to be there, and Torvald talks slow, so we may as well start while he's walking up from the barn."

She put bread and cheese and a jar of pickled things in front of him, food from the land, food off this ground, and he ate because his body insisted even while his mind ran its ledgers. And all the while he catalogued, because cataloguing was how he kept fear at arm's length: the extra place setting in the cupboard, dusted clean. The calendar on the wall with every date in October marked with small pencil crosses except the twenty-second — tomorrow — which bore a single word in Maren's handwriting: *Anders.*

Not *funeral.* Not *burial.* Just the name, alone, like a debt coming due.

* * *

Torvald came in at dusk, filling the doorway the way he always had, a big man gone stooped, beard gone entirely white so that his face seemed to float above his collar. He saw Astrid and stopped, and for a moment the old man's eyes went bright and wet, and Astrid thought: *here, finally, here is the grief,* and braced for the embrace.

Instead Torvald crossed the room, took Astrid's face in his huge cracked hands, and studied him — actually studied him, turning his head left, then right, like a man inspecting livestock or checking a coin for counterfeit.

"You're thinner," Torvald pronounced. "City food. Sit. Eat."

"He ate," Maren said.

"Then eat again. Grief burns a man hollow." Torvald lowered himself into the chair opposite, and the floorboards complained under him, and for a while they simply looked at each other across the table while the rain worked at the windows.

"Uncle Torvald," Astrid said carefully, "I need to ask you some things. About Anders. About the week before he—"

"The well-house is locked," Torvald said.

The non sequitur hung in the air. Astrid blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"The well-house." Torvald's voice was mild, reasonable, the voice of a man discussing fence posts. "You'll have seen the chain. It stays locked. Whatever else we talk about tonight, that doesn't change. That's not for you, and it's not for me, and it's not for anyone living."

"Why would I care about the well-house?"

"Because you're going to ask," Torvald said placidly. "They always ask. Your grandmother asked. Her father asked. It's the first question everyone asks, and the answer is the same one it's been since 1891, and the answer is: it stays locked."

The year landed on the table between them like a stone. 1891. Astrid felt his professional mind seize on it the way a terrier seizes a rat — a specific number, offered without prompting, from a man who otherwise spoke in generalities. Numbers were chosen. Numbers meant something.

"That's a very precise date," Astrid said.

"That's a very old family," Torvald replied, and drank his coffee.

Maren rose abruptly and began stacking dishes that didn't need stacking. Through the window behind her, Astrid could see the yard going blue with dusk, and beyond the barn, the well-house a darker shape in the gathering dark, its new chain invisible now, its padlock a suggestion, a glint. Watching it, he found himself thinking about the undertaker's ledger again — the body arriving already embalmed, tagged by no one, the neat column of figures that added up to nothing. And underneath that thought, surfacing slowly, another: *they knew.* Maren's unsurprised face. Ilsa's checked-off stare. Torvald answering a question Astrid hadn't asked yet.

They had known the casket was wrong. They had known before he did. Perhaps they had known before the casket was ever closed.

"I'm going to see him buried tomorrow," Astrid said. "Whoever is in that box. And then I'm going to find out where my brother is. And Uncle — I'm going to ask about the well-house, and I'm going to keep asking, because I don't believe in curses, I don't believe in family debts, and I especially don't believe in answers that come with a date attached and a locked door. I believe in records. And somewhere in this house there's a record of whatever you're all not telling me."

The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked — Ilsa, moving, or something moving like Ilsa.

Torvald looked at him for a long time, and the old man's expression did something complicated then, something that contained, Astrid realized much later, both pity and a terrible kind of respect.

"Your grandmother kept books," Torvald said finally. "Attic. West side, under the eaves, there's a trunk with a false bottom. She said if a Sterling ever came home who counted things, we were to let him count." He pushed back his chair and stood, joints cracking like green firewood. "Fair warning, boy. She also said the counting goes both ways."

He paused at the foot of the stairs, one hand on the rail, and did not turn around.

"And Astrid. When you go out to the well-house — and you will, they always do — count the links in that chain before you touch it. If there's more links than there were yesterday, come back to the house and don't ask me why."

He climbed the stairs, slow and heavy, and was gone.

Astrid sat at the table in the lamplight with his untouched coffee going bitter in front of him, listening to the rain, and outside the window the well-house stood in the dark with its patient lock, and he told himself the shiver along his arms was just the draft from a badly sealed window in an old house.

He told himself that. But he was already counting.
