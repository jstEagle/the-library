## 6. Anders Is Alive

Torvald Sterling had spent sixty-one years learning which sounds on the homestead meant nothing and which ones meant everything, and the telephone in the front hall had always belonged to the first category. It was a wall-mounted relic with a bakelite handset gone yellow as old teeth, installed by his father for the grain broker's calls, and in a good year it rang maybe twice. In a bad year it didn't ring at all, because the people who needed to reach them already knew better than to try.

So when it rang at nine minutes past three in the afternoon — not night, not morning, but the flat dead center of an October day that couldn't decide whether to be autumn or something else — Torvald's hands went cold before his mind caught up.

He was in the kitchen, shelling beans into a colander while Maren scalded jars at the stove. Ilsa was at school. Astrid had been at the house four days now, sleeping in Anders's old room, going through their grandmother's things up in the attic like a man auditing a company he suspected of fraud. Which, Torvald supposed, was exactly what he was doing.

The phone rang a third time.

"I'll get it," Maren said, wiping her hands.

"No." The word came out of him harder than he intended. He set down the colander. "I'll take it."

Maren looked at him — that long, level look she'd inherited from her mother, the one that made lying feel like standing in an open field — but she said nothing. She'd stopped asking questions years ago. That was the arrangement. That was how the Sterlings survived: you did what needed doing, you kept what needed keeping, and you didn't ask after the rest.

Torvald lifted the handset. "Sterling residence."

For a moment there was only the sound of the line itself — that faint oceanic hiss of a connection crossing distance — and beneath it, wind. Real wind, moving through real open space, and the far-off clank of something metal swinging, a sign or a flag bracket outside whatever building housed the payphone.

Then breathing. Wet breathing. The kind of breath a man takes when his lungs are half-full of something they shouldn't be full of.

"Uncle Torvald." Anders's voice. Thin, stretched, coming from somewhere deep the way a voice carries up from the bottom of a cistern. "It's me."

Torvald's knees found the chair back behind him without his permission. He sat down hard on the bench by the coat hooks, the handset pressed so tight to his ear the plastic creaked.

"Anders." He kept his voice low. Maren was ten feet away with her back turned, but water running covers words, and he needed it to cover his. "Son. Where are you?"

"Town. The gas station." A pause, and in the pause Torvald heard something that would live behind his eyes for the rest of his life however long that turned out to be: a slow, liquid shifting, like a body turning over in shallow water. "I don't have long. The quarters — I only have so many quarters, Uncle Torvald, and I can't leave the booth to get more."

"Why not?"

Silence. Long enough that Torvald thought the line had died. Then, quieter, almost gentle, the way you speak to a child or a dying animal:

"You know why not."

Torvald closed his eyes. Through the kitchen window he could see the well-house at the edge of the yard, gray clapboard gone silver, its door shut fast under the new chain he'd locked there himself eleven days ago with hands that shook so badly it had taken him three tries to work the padlock. Beyond it, the excavation crew's orange stakes still dotted the east field where the county wanted its road easement, little flags snapping in the wind like a wound that wouldn't close.

"They're going to open it," Anders said. "Aren't they. The crew."

"The county sent papers. Easement survey. I paid them off—"

"They came back, though. Didn't they." Not a question. "I can hear them sometimes. At the top of the stairs. Boots on stone." Another wet breath. "Uncle Torvald, listen to me. Listen very carefully, because I'm only going to say this once and then I have to hang up, because every minute I'm on this line it gets easier for it to find my voice again later, and I need my voice to stay mine for a while yet."

Torvald gripped the bench with his free hand until the wood bit into his palm.

"Don't open it," Anders said. Each word laid down separately, like stones across a creek. "Don't let them open it. Whatever they tell you — easement, survey, court order, doesn't matter. Whatever Astrid says. He means well, but he counts things, and this is one thing that doesn't want counting. You keep that door shut. You hear me? Pins held for a hundred years. They hold one more winter. One more winter and we figure out the rest."

"Anders—" Torvald's throat closed around the name. "You come home. You hear me? You walk out of that booth and you come up the drive and we'll—"

"I can't." And there it was, the thing underneath everything, surfacing for just a moment before he pushed it back down: fear, plain and enormous, in a voice Torvald had known since the boy was born seven minutes after his brother. "You know I can't. I sealed it from the inside. That was the whole point." A sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could drown. "Tell Astrid — no. Don't tell Astrid anything. He'll do the math. Let him do the math. He's better at it than any of us."

The wind on the line rose. Somewhere behind Anders's voice, faintly, impossibly, Torvald heard another voice join it — a woman's voice, singing something old, something with a lullaby's shape and none of its comfort — and Anders said, sharply, "I have to go," and the line clicked, and the dial tone came through flat and endless as a frozen lake.

Torvald sat on the bench for a long time. The handset sweated in his grip. In the kitchen, Maren turned off the tap and began, without comment, to ladle beans into jars.

He hung the phone up gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child.

* * *

He found Astrid at dusk, up in the attic, sitting cross-legged on the floorboards amid a drift of their grandmother's papers. His brother's son — no, his nephew; Torvald had to keep correcting himself, grief kept flipping the relationships over — had his shirtsleeves rolled to the elbow and his reading glasses pushed up into his hair, and he looked, in the lamplight, so much like Anders at twenty-five that Torvald's chest hurt to look at him.

Astrid had papers spread out in rows. Ledger pages. Torvald recognized the handwriting even upside down: Edith Sterling's small, ruthless script, numbers marching in columns straighter than fence posts.

"You knew," Astrid said, without looking up.

Torvald stood in the attic doorway. Below them the house ticked and settled. Out past the window glass the first true dark of evening was pooling in the hollows of the land, gathering toward the well-house.

"Astrid—"

"The ledger goes back to 1891." Astrid tapped the page in front of him, once, precisely. "Every winter, one name struck out. Every single winter, Torvald. One hundred and thirty-four winters. Do you know what the probability of that being coincidence is? I ran it. I ran it last night instead of sleeping, because I thought if I could show myself it was noise — grief noise, pattern-hunger, whatever you want to call it — I could stop seeing it." He finally looked up, and his eyes behind the glasses were red-rimmed and terribly steady. "It isn't noise. So either my grandmother was insane in a way that produced perfect arithmetic for six generations, or she was keeping books on something real. And I've been sitting here all day trying to decide which answer is worse, when the whole time you've been walking around downstairs knowing. Haven't you."

Torvald came into the attic. His knees complained on the ladder and his knees complained on the floorboards and he lowered himself down opposite Astrid with the care of a man whose body had started presenting him with the bill for everything he'd ever carried.

"Your grandmother called it the account," he said. "Not the debt. The account. She said debts were things you fell into. An account was something you kept."

Astrid said nothing. He waited, the way auditors wait, and Torvald understood suddenly why Anders had said let him do the math. Some men you could lie to. This one you could only delay.

"Anders called today," Torvald said. "From the payphone at the Kessler station. Quarter to four this afternoon."

Astrid went very still. Stillness, on him, was louder than shouting.

"He's alive," Astrid said.

"He's alive." Saying it aloud cracked something in Torvald that he'd been keeping soldered shut since the funeral home, since the casket, since the stranger wearing his nephew's face. "Wet-sounding. Distant. Said he couldn't leave the phone booth. Said—" his voice failed and restarted, lower, "—said he could hear boots on stone at the top of the stairs."

Astrid took off his glasses. He cleaned them on his shirttail with slow, mechanical motions, put them back on, and Torvald watched him do the thing he did — watch him refuse, one more time, to break.

"The dental records," Astrid said quietly. "The body in the casket wasn't Anders. I confirmed it Tuesday. Same face. Different man — different fillings, different root work, thirty years' difference in wear. The undertaker's ledger says the body arrived embalmed, tagged, no next of kin listed, no transport record. Somebody delivered a corpse with my brother's face to that funeral home, and everybody in this family acted like that was normal." He folded his hands. "So tell me the rest of it, Uncle Torvald. All of it. Because I've read the account, and I know the count comes due every winter, and I know my brother's name is the one that got struck this year — except it didn't, did it? Because he's alive. Which means somebody else filled the slot. Which means somebody arranged it." His voice didn't rise. It dropped instead, down to something cold and level. "Which means a member of this family put a dead stranger in my brother's grave."

The lamp flickered. Neither of them looked at it. Up here you learned not to look at the flickering things; looking was a kind of answering.

"It was me," Torvald said.

He'd rehearsed it, in the bean rows, in the truck, in the dark behind his eyelids for four nights running. None of the rehearsals survived contact. The truth came out of him the way water comes through a failing dam — not all at once, but unstoppably, in the places least able to bear it.

"Anders came home early. A week early. He was due the following Friday — you understand what I'm telling you, Astrid, we knew, we always know, the week it falls due sits on this house like weather — and he walked in the door on a Thursday evening with his bag over his shoulder and he said, *I'm ready this time.*" Torvald's hands opened and closed on his own knees. "We tried to talk him out of it. Maren cried. Ilsa — God, Ilsa, she's twelve, she adores him. And he sat right there at the kitchen table and he laid it out like you'd lay out a plan to refinance the farm. Calm as Sunday. He said the count was coming for the youngest blood in the house regardless, that it always bends toward the youngest when the elders stall, that his grandmother's own notes proved it — and he'd read her notes, Astrid, he'd been reading them for two years, same as you. He said the only way to keep it off Ilsa was to give it what it wanted before it decided to help itself."

"So he went to the well-house."

"So he went to the well-house." Torvald stared at the rafters. "And I helped him. That's the part you need to carry too, nephew, because I won't have you carrying it alone thinking we were fools. We weren't fools. We were bookkeepers. He told me what he meant to do and I — I drove him to town the next morning, and we bought the iron pins from Halvard's nephew at the hardware store, and Anders thanked the boy and smiled at him, and I sat in the truck and thought, *this is the last errand.*" His jaw worked. "That night he walked out to the well-house and went down the stairs and set those pins in the brackets from the inside, and I chained the outer door behind him, and that was eleven days ago. Eleven days. And then the county papers came, and the crew came with their machines, and I paid them triple to leave because I knew — I *knew* — what was under that floor, and I thought money was enough. Money's always been enough for men who don't look too hard at what they're buying."

"But the crew came back," Astrid said. "Or they will. Unpaid curiosity travels farther than cash."

"Yes." Torvald bowed his head. "Yes, I expect they will."

Silence filled the attic, thick as wool. Downstairs a clock ticked. Outside, far off across the fields, something moved along the tree line — not an animal, animals moved differently — and neither man turned to check, because checking was a courtesy you extended to ordinary darkness and this darkness had stopped being ordinary the day the first Sterling signed the first line.

"Anders said one more thing," Torvald said at last. "At the end of the call. Before he hung up." He met his nephew's eyes. "He said, *let him do the math.*"

Astrid exhaled slowly through his nose. When he spoke, his voice had changed — steadier, stripped of the shaking disbelief that had ridden him since the graveside, replaced by something Torvald recognized from boardrooms and bankruptcy hearings and the cold arithmetic of men who fix what's broken by understanding exactly how it broke.

"Then he'd better mean it," Astrid said, "because I've found the flaw in the account, Uncle Torvald. Two winters in a hundred and thirty-four where nobody was taken. Two. And both times, the entry reads the same way." He turned the ledger page around and slid it across the floorboards into the lamplight, and Edith Sterling's handwriting sat there small and merciless:

*Given freely. Account holds.*

Outside, the wind rose, and against the wind — faint, patient, unmistakable — came the sound of knocking from the direction of the well-house. Three knocks. A pause. Three more.

Neither of them answered.
