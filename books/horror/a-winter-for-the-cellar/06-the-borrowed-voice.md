## 6. The Borrowed Voice

The inn gave Ulyana a room at the back, above the kitchen, where the pipes knocked like a polite ghost and the walls were thick enough to hold warmth and secrets in equal measure. She had come to think of it as the archive room, because that was what it had become. Her tape recorder sat on the desk between the water pitcher and the stub of a candle, its reels wound tight with the village's winter, and each night she played the day's recordings back through the single earphone, cataloguing, annotating, listening the way other people prayed.

She had forty-one songs now. Forty-one songs that would never be sung twice.

The habit of playback had begun as scholarship and was becoming something else, though she could not yet have named what. There was a particular pleasure in hearing Vinterhollow at night, when the village outside her window had gone blue and silent under the snow. The voices on the tape were warmer than the voices in the street. They had no frost in them. Old Marta's laugh, the creak of the innkeeper's floorboards, the wind worrying at the eaves of the church — all of it preserved, all of it hers, a small country of sound she could enter whenever the cold outside grew too much like a held breath.

On the fourth night after the frost had climbed her window in the shape of the ledger's sigils, she sat down to review the recordings from her first week. The lullaby was on the third reel.

She had thought about that lullaby every day since. Old Sanna had sung it for her in the widow's cottage on her second morning in Vinterhollow, a song about a child who would not sleep because the dark beneath the house was singing back, and Ulyana had done what she always did: she had thanked her, praised her, and asked, gently, with the smile that had opened a hundred doors across a hundred parishes, whether she might sing it once more so the machine could catch it properly.

Sanna had looked at her the way one looks at a child who has asked why the river cannot flow uphill. Not angry. Not frightened, exactly. *Grieved*, perhaps, in the way one grieves a thing that has not happened yet.

"Once a year," she had said. "That's the whole of the bargain. You can have it once. Pay me if you like. Pay me in silver. But I'll not sing it twice, girl, and you'd do well not to ask it of anyone here again."

Ulyana had paid her anyway, for the once, and had left with the reel turning and the old woman's voice thin and clear as a wire drawn through the cold.

Now she threaded the reel and pressed play.

The room was warm. The candle burned. The lullaby came out of the earphone small and perfect, Sanna's cracked soprano carrying the melody of the sleeping child and the singing dark, and Ulyana closed her eyes and followed it through to the end, the way she had a dozen times, the way she could have in her sleep.

The song ended. The tape hissed.

And then, in the hiss, the lullaby began again.

Ulyana's eyes opened.

She had not moved. The reel turned with the same patient whisper it had turned all evening. But there in the earphone, unmistakable, the melody rose again — the same seven phrases, the same falling cadence at the end where the child finally slept — and it was not Sanna singing.

It took her a long moment to understand why she was so certain, and when she understood it, her hands went cold on the desk. It was not the timbre, though the timbre was wrong too, too smooth, too unbroken, a voice without any of Sanna's age in it, without any age at all. It was the *breathing*. Every singer breathes. Every singer's phrasing is built out of the places where breath must be taken, the little collapses and recoveries that make a human voice a human voice. This voice did not breathe. It moved through the melody the way water moves through a channel that has been worn for it, without effort, without pause, without the small mortal interruptions that prove someone is behind the sound.

The second singing ran to the end. The tape hissed again. Then, faintly, so faintly she had to turn the volume up until the hiss became surf, she heard a third voice — her own — saying, in the recording of that first morning: *Thank you, fru Sanna. Might you sing it once more, so the machine can—*

The tape stopped there. It had always stopped there. The reel had only so much length, and she had wound past this point a dozen times without hearing anything but the hiss of blank tape.

She rewound. She played it again. The lullaby, once, in Sanna's breathing voice. The hiss. The lullaby again, in the voice that did not breathe.

She played it a third time, and this time she took the reel off the machine and held it up to the candlelight, turning it, looking for splices, looking for the telltale thinning where tape had been cut and joined. The acetate was continuous. She checked the second copy she had made — the safety duplicate she kept wrapped in wool at the bottom of her trunk — and it was the same. Both of them. Both reels, both recordings, carrying a second performance that had not been sung in her presence, that had not been sung by any throat she could name, that had arrived on her tape the way frost arrives on glass: silently, completely, from the outside in.

She sat for a long while with the earphone still in, listening to the hiss, which now did not sound like surf or snow or static at all but like something waiting just past the edge of audibility for her to press play again.

Outside her window, the frost had begun to climb.

* * *

She did not sleep. She told herself it was the tea, and then she told herself it was the cold, and then, somewhere past the hour when the inn's last lamp went out below her, she stopped telling herself things and simply listened. The house settled. The wind moved through the village with the particular patience of wind that has all winter to work. And beneath it — beneath it, under the floorboards, under the foundations, under the frozen earth — she heard the humming.

It had been there for three nights. She had told herself the first night it was the pipes. The second night she had put her ear to the wall and followed the sound down to the floor, and the floor had been warm near the kitchen chimney and cold everywhere else, and the humming had been in neither. It came from below, from a depth the house did not have, steady and low, a melody without edges, and it was only now, in the small hours with the lullaby still in her ear, that she recognized it.

The lullaby. The dark beneath the house, singing back.

Sanna's song was not a song *about* that. It was a song *from* it. It was the answer, worn smooth by generations of once-a-year singings, to the thing that hummed under every floor in Vinterhollow — and Ulyana had carried a recording of it into the house above the cellar like carrying a lit lamp into a room full of open jars.

She got up and turned the tape recorder to face the wall. Her hands were steady in the way that hands are steady when the body has decided the mind cannot be trusted with the situation. She pressed record and let the reels turn for one full minute, capturing the humming, capturing the silence beneath it, capturing whatever else might be in the room with her that her ears alone could not find.

Then she stopped the tape, rewound it, and played it back.

The humming was there. And under the humming, at the very floor of the recording, where the machine heard what the ear could not: breathing. Slow. Even. Unhurried. The breathing of something that had all winter to work.

She did not play it a second time.

* * *

In the morning she took her notebook to the inn's common room and began, in her neat scholar's hand, to write down everything: the reel, the second singing, the voice without breath, the humming, the breathing under the floor. Documentation, she told herself. If she wrote it down, it belonged to her discipline and not to her fear. If she wrote it down, it was data, and data could be analyzed, and analyzed things could not, by definition, be *happening to* her.

Halfway down the page she became aware that the room had gone quiet.

Not empty — the innkeeper's wife was still at the stove, and two of the road crew sat by the window with their coffee, and old Henrik dozed in the corner chair as he did every morning. But the talk had stopped. The particular low weave of village morning-talk, the weather and the roads and who was ailing, had been set down all at once, the way a burden is set down when the arms grow tired, and no one had picked it back up.

They were not looking at her. That was the strangest part. In any other village, in any other story, they would have stared, and staring she could have answered. But the innkeeper's wife studied her stove. The road crew studied their coffee. Henrik slept with a diligence that looked rehearsed. The whole room had arranged itself around her silence the way water arranges itself around a stone, and Ulyana understood, with a clarity that arrived like cold water in the ear, that they knew.

Word had traveled in the night. Not gossip — Vinterhollow did not seem to trade in gossip; it traded in *weather*, and something had come into the weather. The old woman's refusal, the priest's quiet warning, the ledger with her name in it — all of it had been background hum until now, and now the hum had a shape, and the shape was her, sitting at this table with her notebook open, and every soul in the room was politely, carefully, *kindly* pretending she was not there.

She closed the notebook. "The bread smells wonderful," she said to the innkeeper's wife, because the silence needed breaking and she could not bear to break it by asking what they knew.

"Does it," said the innkeeper's wife, to the stove.

Ulyana paid for coffee she did not drink and went out into the street.

* * *

Vinterhollow in daylight was a village out of a woodcut: steep roofs bowed under snow, smoke standing up straight from the chimneys in the windless cold, the church at the center with its bell tower like a dark finger raised in caution. She had loved it. She could admit that now, walking its one long street with her breath trailing her like a scarf — she had loved it the way one loves a place that has not yet refused one anything.

But the street had learned a new trick overnight.

The baker, who had waved her into his shop her first morning and pressed a warm roll on her unasked, was sweeping his step when she passed, sweeping with great attention, and did not look up. The woman who mended nets — nets, in a village a hundred miles from open water, which Ulyana had privately filed among the village's small charming mysteries — turned her back to the street as Ulyana approached and turned it back again only after the sound of her boots had passed. Two children building a snow wall by the churchyard wall stopped their play when they saw her coming, and stood with their mittens in the snow, watching her with the solemn, unblinking attention of children who have been told something by their parents and have not been told what it means.

At the corner where the street bent down toward the frozen millrace, she stopped and looked back the way she had come. Her own footprints ran behind her, a single line of dark prints in the white, and on either side of them the village stood with its face turned away — shutters half-closed where they had been open, doors drawn in, smoke rising from chimneys whose households were, she would have sworn, watching her through the gaps.

They were not hostile. That was the thing she kept returning to as she walked. Hostility she would have known how to answer; she had been a stranger in a hundred places and strangers learn the grammar of being unwelcome. This was something else. This was *pity*, perhaps, or *grief*, or the particular courtesy that the living extend to the condemned. They were not avoiding her the way one avoids an enemy.

They were avoiding her the way one avoids the window at night. The way one avoids the grave one has just filled.

*Old Sanna's words came back to her as she stood in the cold:* I'll not sing it twice, girl, and you'd do well not to ask it of anyone here again. *And beneath the words, beneath even the humming she had carried out of her room on tape, another sentence surfaced, one that no one in Vinterhollow had said to her aloud but that seemed suddenly to have been said to her in every silence since she arrived:*

*It isn't that the songs must not be sung twice. It is that they must not be sung twice* near her.

* * *

She spent the afternoon in the church vestry, because it was the one place in the village where she had been neither welcomed nor refused, and because Father Aldous had not forbidden her to return — he had only asked her, in that voice like snow beginning to fall, to stop looking. She was not looking, she told herself. She was listening. There was a difference, and the difference was that listening required the ledger to be closed on the desk in front of her, which it was, and required her to sit with her hands folded, which they were, and required her to do absolutely nothing except feel the building breathe around her, which she did.

The church breathed. She had not noticed it before. Old timber structures breathe — the beams tick as the cold deepens, the joints sigh — but this was not that. This was slower. Slower than any building's settling, slower even than the humming beneath her room. It was the rhythm of something very large and very patient drawing a breath it did not need, and it came, she realized with her hands still folded and her heart going hard against them, from below. From under the floor. From under the crypt, under the founders' stones, under whatever the founders had buried and sealed and sung against for two hundred years.

The ledger lay closed beside her hand. She did not open it. She had promised. But the frost on the vestry's small window had grown since her last visit, and it had grown *in pattern* — the same interlocking sigils she had seen in the margins, the same geometry she had photographed on her own bedroom glass, and it had grown downward, she noticed now, the way frost always grew upward on every other window in the world. Down the pane. Down toward the floor. Down toward the breathing.

"Miss Sterling."

Father Aldous stood in the vestry doorway. She had not heard him cross the church, and his boots had made noise on the flagstones all week; she had catalogued the sound on her first reel. He stood with his hands in his sleeves and his face doing what all the faces in Vinterhollow did: nothing, kindly.

"I heard you've been asking after the lullaby," he said. "The widow's song."

"I recorded it once. That's all. Once."

"Yes." He came into the room slowly, and she saw that his eyes went, before they went anywhere else, to the floor — down, the way the frost had gone down, the way her attention kept going down. "Once is the bargain. Once is what we can bear. You'll understand that I have to ask what you heard, when you played it back."

The question was so precisely wrong that for a moment she could not answer it. Not *did* you play it back. *What did you hear.*

"It sang twice," she said, because lying to him suddenly seemed as dangerous as telling the truth. "On the tape. The second time it wasn't her voice. It didn't breathe."

Aldous closed his eyes. He stood there in the frost-light with his eyes closed for the length of three breaths — his own breaths, she noted, human breaths, uneven and mortal — and when he opened them he looked older, and he looked, for the first time since she had met him, afraid.

"Then it has your recording," he said. "And if it has your recording, it has your voice on it. Your voice, asking for the song. Asking for the second singing." He crossed the room and put his hand, gently, on the closed ledger, as if it were a lid. "Miss Sterling. Tonight, when you play your tapes — and you will play them, I know your kind, you will not be able to help it — count the voices. Every voice on every reel. And if you ever count one more than you recorded—"

He stopped. Outside, the church bell rang the hour, and the sound of it went through the floor and down, and for just a moment, in the silence after the last stroke, Ulyana heard the thing below answer — not in humming now, but in something with the shape of a word, worn smooth by two hundred years of patience, rising through the stone like a bubble through dark water.

"—count them," Aldous finished quietly, "and come to me. And God forgive us both, do not sing along."
