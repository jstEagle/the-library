## 13. The Second Singing

The dare happened at the well, in the blue hour between the lamplighter's rounds and full dark, and I would have missed it entirely if the cold hadn't driven me to my window.

I had taken to watching Vinterhollow at dusk the way one watches a sleeping animal — cataloguing its small movements, learning which silences were natural and which were held. The village had grown quieter around me since the frost began writing on my glass. Not hostile, exactly. Nothing so honest as hostility. The baker still sold me bread; the innkeeper still poured my tea. But conversations ended a half-beat early when I approached, the way a song ends when someone realizes they've sung past the verse they meant to stop at. Doors closed softly rather than firmly. Softness, I was learning, could be its own kind of wall.

Below me, at the well where the square's three lanterns pooled their light, children were playing the game they played every evening: *frost-keeper*, a chasing game with rules I'd been trying to transcribe for a week. One child was the keeper. The others froze when she turned. It was charming. It was exactly the kind of living folklore I had come north to find, before the ledger and the lullaby and the humming through my wall taught me that folklore here was not decoration but architecture — load-bearing, ancient, and not to be touched.

The girl who broke it was small, maybe seven, with a red wool cap her mother had knit too large, so that it slid down over one eye when she ran. I knew her distantly as Maren, daughter of the cooper. She had been caught by the keeper four times running, and the other children had begun their jeering — the universal grammar of childhood, identical in every language I'd ever recorded — and then Maren planted her boots in the snow, pushed the red cap off her face, and said something I couldn't hear.

The other children went still.

Not frozen-in-the-game still. A different quality of stillness, the kind adults make without knowing they're making it, the kind I had seen on every face in this village whenever I asked my questions twice.

Maren threw back her head and hummed.

I knew the tune instantly. Of course I did. It was the first winter song anyone had sung for me, six weeks ago now, on my second night in Vinterhollow — old Sanna Voss, in her kitchen thick with the smell of juniper smoke, singing what she called the *door-song*, the one mothers sang at first snowfall. She had sung it once, beautifully, her voice cracked as river ice but true underneath, and when I lifted my recorder and asked her sweetly, professionally, *could we do it again from the top*, she had looked at me with an expression I mistook then for stubbornness and had not mistaken for anything since.

*Once is a gift,* she'd said. *Twice is a knock.*

Now Maren hummed it once through, all eight bars, standing in the trampled snow while the other children watched her with their breath hanging white and motionless above them like small ghosts refusing to move on.

And then — because a dare is a dare, because she was seven and the cap was too big and losing four times in a row is a wound no child should have to carry unavenged — Maren hummed it again.

I heard the second singing clearly. That was the terrible part, how clearly I heard it. The first time through, her humming had been a child's humming, careless, slightly flat on the fourth bar the way Sanna's had been flat, the way the song apparently demanded to be flat. The second time, something corrected her. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that night: the pitch did not change. The tempo did not change. But the *weight* of it changed, the way a room changes weight when someone enters it behind you. Her humming acquired a resonance it had no business having in a small chest, a low harmonic underneath the melody like a hand placed flat against a drumhead. The snow around her boots did not stir. The lantern flames did not flicker. Everything stayed exactly where it was, and everything became wrong.

The other children scattered. Not laughing, not shrieking — scattering the way birds scatter before a storm arrives, silently, all at once, each one already knowing. Maren stood alone by the well with her cap over one eye, and after a moment she shrugged, the shrug of a child whose dare has landed and found no audience, and walked home through the snow, leaving small red-mittened prints behind her.

I stood at my window with my heart going like a trapped bird and told myself a hundred reasonable things. Children repeat songs constantly; repetition is the engine of oral tradition; I had recorded a hundred doubled verses across a dozen countries and nothing had ever climbed out of anywhere. Reasonable things. True things, everywhere else in the world.

But I had heard the harmonic. And I had spent enough nights in this village now to know that the silence which followed Maren home was not the ordinary silence of a square emptying. It was a listening silence. It had texture. It leaned toward the church the way grass leans toward light.

I did not sleep. I told myself that was the tea.

* * *

At some hour that had no name — past midnight, before the milk carts, the hour the village itself seemed to hold its breath — I heard the door-song again.

It came from outside, thin and clear, riding the wind down my street. A woman's voice. Sanna Voss's voice, unmistakably, with its crack and its truth beneath. Except Sanna Voss had died eleven days ago, and I had attended her burial, and Tomas had dug the grave himself in ground so hard his father's spade rang like a bell with every stroke, and I had stood at the graveside with the other villagers while Father Aldous spoke the words for the dead, and none of us had sung, because of course none of us had sung. You do not sing at graves in Vinterhollow. You especially do not sing *that* song at graves.

I got to the window in time to see nothing. Snow, lanterns, the black bulk of the church with its cellar door facing the square like a closed mouth. The voice continued for perhaps a minute more — one full pass through the melody, unhurried, almost tender — and stopped precisely where the song stops, on the note that falls instead of rises, the note Sanna had called *the latch*.

Then the wind resumed being only wind, and I sat on the edge of my bed in my coat with my recorder clutched to my chest like a talisman that had already failed, and I waited for morning the way you wait for a verdict.

* * *

Morning came grey and windless, the kind of cold that doesn't bite so much as press, evenly, everywhere, like a palm laid over the whole village. I was in the square before I fully decided to be there, and I was not alone. Word travels fast in places where words are rationed; whatever else Vinterhollow withheld, it did not withhold alarm.

They were standing in front of the church cellar door.

Three of them. The cooper — Maren's father, a broad man I'd seen laugh exactly once, at his own daughter's christening story. Old Britta Halvard, who sold candles and had refused to sell me any during my first week, saying only, *you'll not need them where you're going.* And Lars Eklund, the schoolmaster, whom I knew best of the three, a gentle, precise man who had helped me translate the oldest parish records and who had gone pale and silent the day I'd shown him the founders' ledger and the name Sterling written in a hand four centuries dead.

They stood in a line in the snow, shoulder to shoulder, facing the cellar door. Their eyes were open. Their eyes were open and they were asleep — I knew it within seconds of reaching them, knew it the way you know a painted face isn't looking at you. Their breathing was slow and deep and even, sleeper's breathing, and frost had grown on their eyelashes and in their hair and along the collars of their coats, frost that had come from inside somehow, blooming outward from the skin. Their lips were moving. All three sets of lips, moving together, shaping words with no sound in them, or sound below sound, a murmur pitched under the threshold of my hearing — though my hearing had been changing lately, and sometimes I thought I caught the edge of it, a syllable like *under*, a syllable like *counting*.

The village stood around them in a wide, ragged ring, and no one moved.

That was the thing I will never write about correctly, the thing language keeps failing on: no one moved. Forty people, maybe fifty, gathered in the freezing square at dawn, and nobody stepped forward, nobody shouted, nobody wept or prayed or did any of the thousand things people do. They stood. They breathed. They watched the three sleepers the way you watch water rising around your ankles — with the awful calm of people who have read this page before. An old man near me whispered, not to me, not to anyone: *only three. Only three this time.*

Only three *this time*.

I pushed through the ring before my fear could vote. Later I would understand that everyone let me through too easily, that hands parted for me with a gentleness that was itself a kind of horror, but in that moment I only saw Lars Eklund's frost-white face and I dropped to my knees in the snow in front of him and took his mittened hands.

"Lars." My voice cracked. "Lars, can you hear me? It's Ulyana. Lars."

His lips kept moving. Up close I could see that the frost on him was patterned — of course it was patterned, I would have known those curves anywhere, I had watched them climb my window night after night, the marginal sigils from the founders' ledger rendered in ice across a sleeping man's cheeks like a mask fitted to him by a careful, patient hand. His eyes stared through me at the cellar door, and in their depths there was a reflection I could not account for: the door was shut, bolted, blank — but in Lars's eyes it stood open, and something in the dark beyond it was looking back with an attention I felt physically, the way you feel a draft.

"His lips," said a voice beside me, low and rough and close, and I started because I hadn't heard Tomas arrive, though he must have come the moment word reached the graveyard, snow still on his shoulders, his father's iron-tipped spade in his fist as though he'd forgotten to put it down or couldn't. "Watch what his lips are doing."

I watched. And then I heard it — or didn't hear it, felt it, in the bones of my ears, in the hollow behind my sternum where songs live before they become sound. The shape of the mouth movements wasn't speech. It was melody. Three mouths, three sleepers, all tracing the same phrase over and over, the opening phrase of the door-song, the phrase that goes up and holds —

— and waits. Waits for the answering phrase. Waits for the second voice.

"It's calling the countermelody," I whispered. "It's — Tomas, they're singing the first half. Something's supposed to answer."

"Nothing is going to answer." His hand closed on my shoulder, heavy and warm through my coat, and there was a tremor in it he would have denied under torture. "No one here will ever answer it. That's the whole — " He stopped himself, jaw working. Around us the ring of villagers had drawn fractionally closer, and every face wore the same expression: grief pre-worn, like a coat handed down through generations, fitted long ago to shoulders not yet born.

Father Aldous came through the crowd then, and I had never seen him look old before. He carried no book, no cross, nothing but his own bare hands, and he knelt in the snow beside Lars and looked into the schoolmaster's dreaming eyes for a long, long moment, and whatever he saw there made him close his own.

"We put them to bed," Aldous said quietly, to no one, to everyone. "We sang them down last night, the way we do, the way it's done. First snowfall, the door-song, once, all together, to keep the — " His voice snagged. "To keep the counting slow. And it worked. It always works. Unless somewhere, in the same night, the song is sung a second time. Then the singing-down is undone. Then the door hears both halves of its own name, and it calls, and anyone who has ever given it their voice answers in their sleep."

My blood went to ice water. "Sung a second time," I said. "By whom?"

Aldous looked at me then, and behind the exhaustion in his face was something worse than accusation. Pity. "You know the rule, Miss Sterling. Everyone knows the rule. Once is a gift. Twice is a knock." He rose, knees cracking, an old man in an old story with no good pages left. "Someone knocked last night."

Behind him, at the edge of the ring, a small figure in a red wool cap stood holding her mother's hand, her face blotchy with crying, her eyes enormous. Maren. She had confessed — I learned this later, in fragments, from the cooper's wife — confessed at dawn, sobbing, that she'd hummed the song twice on a dare, that the second time it had felt like the song was humming *her*, that she'd run home and hidden under her blankets and heard the well-water moving though the well was frozen solid.

She was not blamed. That was the most frightening thing of all. No one raised a hand to her, no one even raised a voice. Her mother simply drew her close, and the villagers made a small space around the two of them, a space of mercy and quarantine, and old Britta Halvard's husband said, in the tone of a man reading a weather report for a storm that has already arrived: "Three sleepers. The door takes what it's owed. We know the rite for waking them."

"You do?" I said. "There's a rite to wake them?"

"There is," Aldous said. "It costs what it costs." And he would say nothing more, not to me, not with Tomas's hand still on my shoulder and forty pairs of eyes carefully not looking at either of us.

* * *

They woke the sleepers at noon, behind closed church doors, and I was not permitted to see it. What I was permitted to learn, afterward, from the schoolmaster himself, sitting pale and diminished in the vestry with a blanket around his shoulders and a cup of tea he never drank, was this:

He remembered nothing. He remembered going to bed. He remembered a dream of a long stone corridor and a sound like a fork struck against glass, ringing and ringing, and a voice asking him — politely, patiently, with the courtesy of a host who has waited a very long time — whether he would sing the rest.

"And I wanted to," Lars said. His hands shook around the cup. "God forgive me, Miss Sterling, I *wanted* to. It wasn't horror in that dream. It was belonging. It was every winter of my life arranged into order, every snowfall counted and kept, and a place in the arrangement with my name on it. I opened my mouth to sing the answering phrase, and then — " He looked at me, and his eyes were wet. "Then I heard my mother's voice saying my name, the way she said it when I was small and sleepwalking, and I stopped. Woke on the stones with frost in my beard. Whatever pulled me back, it used her. It can reach into a man and find the one voice that would make him stop."

He set down the cup because he could no longer hold it steady.

"The thing I cannot get past, Miss Sterling — the thing I keep turning over — is that it only needed three of us. It had the whole square to choose from, every soul in Vinterhollow asleep and reachable, and it took three. A first voice, a second voice, and—" He faltered. "And a witness. It always takes a witness. That's what the old records mean, the ones I translated for you, the ones I should have warned you about properly. *Tres ad ostium* — three to the door. One to call, one to answer, one to remember. It's building itself a congregation, Miss Sterling. Three at a time. And it has been counting winters for four hundred years, and it is very, very patient, and last night it learned that the newest voice in this village — "

He stopped. He looked at my recorder bag, slumped by the chair with its reels of tape, its weeks of collected song, its careful archive of every melody Vinterhollow owned.

"— sings beautifully," he finished, barely audible. "And that it has already sung everything once."

I walked home through the falling afternoon snow, and the village watched me go with its soft closing doors, and above the church, in the lead-colored sky, the crows had arranged themselves along the roofline in a pattern I refused to look at directly, because I already knew it would match the sigils, and because the frost on every window I passed was pointing — gently, delicately, like a finger raised in polite greeting — down.
