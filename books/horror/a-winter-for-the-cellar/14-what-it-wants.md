## 14. What It Wants

The knock came at dusk, three soft raps like knuckles on a coffin lid, and Ulyana knew before she opened the door that something was wrong with the rhythm of it. Too patient. No one in Vinterhollow knocked patiently; they knocked the way people did when the cold was at their backs and their hands were full of bread or firewood or grief.

Marta Voss stood on her step with snow settling into the grey braids coiled at her ears. The old woman who had refused to repeat the lullaby. The old woman whose refusal had been the first stone of everything Ulyana had come to understand about this place.

"Frau Voss," Ulyana said, and stepped back to let her in, because you let an elder in out of the cold; that was manners anywhere in the world.

The woman did not move. She stood in the blue hour with her hands folded at her waist, and the snow did not seem to land on her shoulders so much as hesitate near them, uncertain.

"You have been writing down our songs," Marta said.

"I have. With permission. Always with permission."

"Yes." A pause that had weight to it, like a stone set carefully on a scale. "You write them down so they can be sung again."

"So they can be *kept*," Ulyana said. "Kept isn't sung. Kept is kept."

The old woman's mouth moved into something that wanted to be a smile but arrived late, like a guest who had forgotten the address. "That is what your grandmother said."

The words went through Ulyana like a needle finding the seam of a coat. "My grandmother never came here."

"No," Marta agreed pleasantly. "She didn't need to. Her name was already here." And then she walked past Ulyana into the room as though she owned the threshold, as though she had been walking through thresholds since before doors were invented, and sat down in the chair by the cold hearth without asking whether the fire should be lit.

Ulyana stood with her hand still on the latch. The wind outside had gone strange — not stopped, but *attentive*, the way a room goes attentive when someone begins to say the thing everyone has been avoiding for years.

"Frau Voss, are you well? Should I fetch Father Aldous—"

"He knows I'm here." The old woman folded her hands more tightly. "He's listening. They're all listening. That's rather the point of me tonight."

*Of me.* Not *of my visit*. Ulyana felt the distinction settle over her skin like frost forming on glass, pattern by pattern, and she thought of the sigils climbing her window, of the ledger's margins, of the humming through the wall that no one would name.

"Who is speaking?" she asked quietly. It cost her something to ask it plainly, the way it costs something to look directly at the sun. "Because it isn't you."

"It is and it isn't." Marta Voss turned her head, and in the last grey light from the window Ulyana saw that her eyes were the exact color of ice over a well — dark water beneath a bright lid. "She lent me her throat the way you'd lend a neighbor a cup of flour. She's resting. She'll want it back by morning. But the matter couldn't wait for morning, and neither could we."

"We."

"The ones below." The old woman said it the way other people said *the neighbors across the lane*, with a small nod toward the floorboards, toward the church, toward the whole buried architecture of the village. "And before you run — and you will want to run, your family always wants to run, it's practically a family trait — understand that running has been tried. By Sterlings. Several times. The snow is very good at finding them."

Ulyana's hand found the table's edge behind her. The wood was cold enough to burn. "What do you want?"

"*Want* is such a warm word." The borrowed mouth smiled again, and this time the smile fit better, which was worse. "We want completion. We want the work finished. Your founders began a thing and left it half-done, the way men do when the work turns difficult and there is wine upstairs. Half a binding is worse than none, Frau Sterling. Half a binding is a door held shut by two tired arms. Eventually the arms tire. Eventually someone must take up the song where they left off."

"My name," Ulyana said, and her voice surprised her by staying level, "is not in any ledger I've seen. There was a Sterling. In 1811. A signatory. I assumed coincidence."

"Assumed." The old woman laughed softly, and the laugh had layers to it, like ice cracking on a pond — several voices under one sound, all of them amused, all of them old. "You folklorists. You collect songs the way magpies collect buttons, and you never once ask why every button in the world seems to be waiting for *you*. Why the archive called to you. Why you dreamed of this valley before you ever saw it on a map — don't bother denying it, we know what you dreamed, we were under the dream the way a cellar is under a house."

Ulyana had dreamed of it. She had told no one. She had told herself it was nothing, a trick of the grant proposal, the mind building scenery out of paperwork. She had written in her journal, the first night, *I have been here before,* and had crossed it out twice.

"What work?" she asked. "Finish it how?"

"That is the negotiation." Marta Voss leaned forward, and the chair creaked beneath her like a ship's timber. "One of yours began it. One of yours must end it. Above the door or below it — the terms allow either. Above, and you sing the closing verse standing in the cold, and you keep your face and your name and your little machine full of songs, and you walk out of Vinterhollow in spring with everyone else. Below—" the pause here was delicate, almost courteous, "—below, and you sing it where the singing is needed most, and you keep everything else. Every memory of every Sterling. Four generations of them, stored like preserves. Their faces. Their voices. Their lullabies. You would know your grandmother's hands. You would know why she hummed the way she hummed. You would belong to us entirely, and we — I promise you this — we keep what belongs to us very well. Nothing rots down there. Nothing is ever lonely. Nothing is ever lost."

"And if I refuse?"

The old woman spread her hands, and the gesture was so perfectly human, so perfectly grandmotherly, that Ulyana's stomach turned. "Then the winter continues its arithmetic. It has been adding. You've seen the frost. You've heard the humming. Three nights ago a child sang a song twice on a dare, and three of your neighbors stood in the square like candles, and the village pretended not to see because pretending is the only skill we've practiced longer than singing. Refusal is allowed. Refusal simply has a price, and the price is paid in installments, and the installments are paid in people."

"Those villagers in the square — are they alive?"

"For now. Asleep, mostly. Dreaming our dreams, which are very old and very quiet. They'll wake when the work is done. Or they won't." The dark-water eyes blinked once, slowly. "We are not cruel, Frau Sterling. Cruelty requires impatience. We have counted ten thousand winters from the inside, and we counted them calmly. But we are *tired* of counting, and the door is thin now, and you — you are the thinnest part of the door."

Outside, the wind resumed, all at once, as though released from holding its breath.

Marta Voss rose. Whatever had been wearing her was folding itself away, and the woman's own face came back into it like light returning to a window — smaller, wearier, hers. For a moment her eyes were only an old woman's eyes, frightened and apologetic.

"I'm sorry, child," she said, in her own cracked voice, and Ulyana believed this part completely. "I'm sorry. It asked and I — you can't refuse it in your own kitchen, you can't, it knows where your mother sleeps—" She stopped herself, hand at her mouth, horrified at what she'd revealed, and then she was gone, out into the snow, walking fast, an old woman again, fleeing the thing she had carried like a cup of water she hadn't dared spill.

Ulyana stood alone in the cold room for a long time. Then she went to her recorder, her faithful machine, and played back the evening, and there on the tape was the whole conversation — except that in the spaces between her own questions, where Marta Voss had paused, the pauses were not silent. In each one, faint as breath on glass, something was humming the binding song. The complete version. The version Ulyana had only ever heard in dreams.

It was teaching her the melody. Patiently. Note by note. Whether she agreed or not.

* * *

Father Aldous was waiting for her in the vestry at dawn, which meant he had not slept, which meant he had known she would come.

The candle he'd lit had burned down to a stub swimming in its own wax. He sat at the long table with the founders' ledger open before him, both hands flat on the page as if holding it shut, or holding himself down. When she entered he did not stand. Priests, she thought, got to skip the courtesies; their office was confession, and confession required no greeting.

"She came to you," he said.

"Something came wearing her." Ulyana sat across from him. Between them the ledger lay open to the page she had studied a week ago, in another life: the cellar rite, the eleven signatures, the name *Sterling* in faded iron-gall ink. "It made an offer. Both sides of the door, it said. Finish the work above and live; finish it below and keep my dead. It knew about my grandmother. It knew about my dreams. Father — how long has it known I was coming?"

Aldous looked at her then, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terribly kind, the kindness of a man who has spent decades being gentle with a truth too heavy to lift.

"Since 1811," he said. "Perhaps longer. The ledger records a Sterling among the founders, but the parish register records something the ledger doesn't. Read the marginalia. The part I asked you to stop reading, because I am a coward and a caretaker and those are often the same profession."

She turned the pages. The vellum was soft as skin at the corners. There, in a cramped later hand, squeezed between entries about roof repairs and candle allotments:

*The rite requires a Sterling above and a Sterling below. We had one. We could not ask for two. God forgive us, we sealed the door knowing the count was short.*

Below that, in fresher ink, dated the year of Ulyana's birth:

*A daughter born to the Sterling line. The frost wrote her name on the north windows of the church. We have begun praying for her before she can walk.*

Ulyana closed the book. Her hands were steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have. Somewhere beyond the walls the wind moved through the village, and beneath the wind, beneath the floors, beneath the frozen ground itself, she could feel it — not hearing exactly, not presence exactly, but *attention*, vast and unhurried, turned upon her like the eye of a lens.

"They summoned me," she said. "My whole life. The grants, the archive, the map that fell open to this page. None of it was chance."

"None of it," Aldous agreed gently. "Or all of it was, and the chance was arranged. I've given up distinguishing. It is very good at making doors look like roads."

"And Tomas." The name left her mouth before she could stop it, and she watched the priest flinch. "His father. The gravediggers. They've always known a Sterling would come."

"Every generation of his family has kept one letter, passed father to son, unopened until the door opens. His father opened his last week. I don't know what it says. I know what all of them have said, because I've buried four generations of gravediggers, and each one confessed the same duty on the same deathbed: *keep her ignorant until the frost comes, then get her out before it writes her name.*" Aldous pressed his palms against his eyes. "They were never guarding the door, Ulyana. They were guarding you. All this time, the spades and the graves and the silence — it was a cradle built around a stranger's granddaughter, and none of them ever knew why except that their fathers told them, and their fathers' fathers, all the way back to a night in 1811 when eleven people did a terrible arithmetic and one of them was named Sterling."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to trim it.

"It offered me my ancestors," Ulyana said. "Below the door. It said nothing is ever lost down there."

"I know what it offers. It has offered every Sterling the same, in one form or another. It keeps its promises, that's the horror of it. It has never once lied." Aldous lowered his hands. "But it has also never once told the whole truth, and the missing piece is always the same piece: what finishing the work actually *requires*. The founders' instructions are incomplete. Deliberately, I think — the second singer tore out the final page before she fled, and what remains—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "What remains is notation, of a kind. Written in frost. On windows. Every winter. I have spent thirty years watching the ice spell out instructions I cannot read, waiting for the one person who can."

Ulyana looked at the frosted pane of the vestry window. The morning light came through it grey and dim, and across the glass the frost had grown overnight into patterns she now recognized — sigils from the ledger's margins, but extended, elaborated, continued past where the ink had stopped, like a sentence completed by a different hand.

Pointing down.

"Then show me," she said. "All of it. The instructions, the torn page, whatever the founders left. Because it will come for me again — it told me so without telling me — and next time I intend to be the one who asks the questions."

Aldous rose at last, an old man unfolding, and took the candle, and led her toward the stairs that went down beneath the church — down, she noticed, toward the cellar, toward the door, toward the attention waiting below with the patience of ten thousand winters.

At the threshold he paused.

"There is one more thing," he said, not turning around. "The founders' rite needs two singers. One above the door. One below. And Ulyana — the frost has already chosen who sings below."

Beyond him, at the bottom of the stairs, the darkness waited, and in it, faint and clear as a tuning fork struck once and held forever, she heard a man's voice beginning to hum the countermelody.
