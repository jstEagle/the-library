## 10. Photographs of the Ritual

The albums lived in the cedar chest at the foot of the stairs, and Odile had been avoiding them for three days.

She told herself it was because the chest was heavy, because the hinges needed oiling, because there were boxes from the attic that had to come down first. All true. None of it the reason. The reason was that photographs had stopped being safe, and she wasn't ready to find out what else in this house had stopped being what she remembered.

But on the fourth morning she carried the chest into the kitchen — past the table, past the empty space where no chair stood, where no chair had ever stood, she checked twice — and set it under the window where the light came in clean and gray, the kind of flat winter light that made everything look documented instead of remembered.

"Okay," she said, to herself, to the house, to whoever was keeping score. "Let's see what you've done."

She put on cotton gloves. Not because the albums were fragile — though they were — but because she wanted her hands to be doing something deliberate. Something archival. Something a professional would do. If she treated this as evidence processing rather than grief with pictures attached, maybe her pulse would cooperate.

It didn't.

The first album was green cloth over board, corners worn to the cardboard, her grandmother's maiden name embossed in flaking gold: *A. OYELARAN*. Odile knew this album. She'd looked through it a dozen times as a child, sprawled on this same kitchen floor while her grandmother cooked, asking *who's that* and *where was this* and getting answers in that warm, unhurried voice that made even the sad photos feel like gifts.

She opened it to the first page.

Lagos, 1954. A wedding party outside a whitewashed church. Her grandmother's parents at the center, young and stiff-collared, surrounded by family whose names Odile had once known and could now only half-recall. She'd studied this photo enough times as a girl to have its geography memorized — who stood where, which uncle's hand rested on which aunt's shoulder, the flower girl mid-fidget at the edge of the frame.

There was a man standing behind the bride's father.

He hadn't been there before.

Odile was sure of it the way you're sure of your own address. She'd have noticed him as a child — children notice the wrongness in old photos, the faces that don't match the smiles around them. And this face didn't match. He was tall, narrow, dressed in a dark suit that belonged to no decade the photograph could name — the cut too modern for 1954, or too timeless, the kind of garment that existed outside fashion the way a knife exists outside cooking. His hands were folded in front of him. He was not smiling. He was not frowning. He was standing the way furniture stands.

And he was positioned directly behind the groom's chair.

Because there was a chair. Of course there was a chair. The wedding party had arranged themselves around a single wooden chair — set slightly apart, angled toward the camera, empty — and Odile realized with a slow cold drop in her stomach that she had never questioned it. In her memory of this photo, the chair had always been there. An heirloom, maybe. A seat of honor left vacant for an elder who couldn't travel. Some explanation a child accepts and files away.

Except she'd gone through these albums with a forensic eye two weeks ago, cataloguing every image for the estate inventory, and there had been no chair. She had the inventory sheets to prove it. Item forty-one through sixty-three, photographic materials, described in her own neat hand. No chair noted. No man noted.

She photographed the page. Her phone's shutter click sounded obscenely loud in the quiet kitchen.

On the phone screen, the image refreshed — and the man was closer. Not dramatically. An inch, maybe, relative to the people around him. As if between the moment the camera captured the album and the moment the screen rendered it, he had taken one small step forward out of 1954.

Odile set the phone face-down on the table and breathed until her hands stopped shaking.

Then she turned the page.

* * *

By noon she had every album open across the kitchen floor in chronological order, eleven of them spanning seventy years, and she had stopped pretending she was doing anything other than what she was doing: documenting a haunting's paperwork.

The pattern held without exception.

Every photograph that contained more than two people contained the chair. A christening, 1961 — the chair at the edge of the frame, and behind it a woman in a high-collared dress, her face soft and wrong, like a face remembered by someone who hadn't loved her. A backyard barbecue, 1978 — the chair pulled up to the picnic table among the folding chairs, and behind it a broad-shouldered figure whose features kept sliding when Odile tried to fix them, so that looking at him directly felt like reading a word you suddenly weren't sure how to spell. Her grandmother's graduation portrait, 1969 — solo shot, studio backdrop, no chair anywhere in the frame — except when Odile photographed it, the print showed a plain wooden chair beside her grandmother's stool, and a shape standing behind it that the lens had caught only as a darkness with a suggestion of shoulders.

They were different figures. That was the worst part, or a worst part; the parts kept competing. Different heights, different clothes, different eras of wrongness. But they shared a stillness, a quality of having been placed rather than arrived, and they all stood the same way: directly behind the chair, hands folded, close enough to touch its backrest. Attendants. Ushers. Whatever you called the people who stand beside a thing to indicate that it matters.

And in every decade, they were closer to the camera than the last.

She measured it. She couldn't help herself; measurement was the prayer her skepticism knew. She laid a ruler against the prints and calculated ratios — the man in 1954 stood roughly four feet behind his chair. The woman in 1961, three and a half. By the nineties, the figures were near enough that their sleeves brushed the chairbacks. In the most recent album — her cousin Adaeze's wedding, six years ago, everyone laughing under a tent — the figure behind the chair stood so close that his chest nearly touched it, and his face, for the first time, was almost legible.

Almost. It resolved and then refused, the way a name sits on the tip of the tongue. Odile stared until her eyes watered and got nothing but the sense of being politely waited upon.

Six years ago, near enough to touch. And the chair in her own recent photographs — the insurance shots, the ones that had started all of this — had begun at the far wall of whatever room they depicted and had been advancing ever since.

It wasn't one haunting. It was a procession. Seventy years of small steps, generation by generation, album by album, all of it converging on a table she ate breakfast at.

Her phone buzzed. Marcus.

*You free today? I keep thinking about what you said on the phone. About the chair moving. I haven't slept. Can I come by?*

She stared at the message for a long time. Part of her wanted him here — wanted a witness, a second set of eyes, someone to say *I see it too* so she could stop feeling like the house's only translator. But another part, the part that had watched him dismiss her twice already, that remembered his voice going gentle in the way people go gentle when they think you've broken — that part hesitated.

*I'm going through Grandma's photo albums,* she typed. *If you come, bring your skepticism. I want you to try to debunk this. Seriously.*

His reply came fast: *That I can do.*

* * *

Marcus arrived at three with a bag of oranges and the determined cheerfulness of a man walking into an intervention he hoped to redirect. He was her mother's sister's son, thirty-four, built like a friendly door, and he had spent their whole childhood being the one who believed things *after* checking twice. It was why his refusal to believe her now hurt in such a specific place.

"Okay." He set the oranges down, surveyed the albums covering the floor, and blew out a breath. "Walk me through it."

"Start anywhere. Pick an album."

He crouched over the Lagos wedding, and she watched his face do exactly what hers had done: the flicker of recognition, the hitch of doubt, the long pause where memory argued with the page. "This is Nana's parents' wedding," he said slowly. "I know this picture. There's—" He stopped. Started again. "There was never a guy standing there."

"No."

"There was never a *chair*, Odile, I'd remember a chair, kids remember weird stuff in photos—"

"You'd think." She handed him her phone with the estate inventory open. "Two weeks ago I catalogued every print in this house. Read my notes. Then look at the same photos now."

He read. He looked. She watched the professional curiosity war with something older and more frightened, and she hated herself a little for finding his fear reassuring.

"This is a printing thing," he said finally. "Old photos get reprinted, restored, AI-upscaled, whatever. Somebody scanned these and the software hallucinated—"

"The software hallucinated the same composition in eleven albums across seven decades? Same chair, same posture, same placement behind the subject? And edited the physical prints?" She tapped the wedding photo. "Touch it. These aren't scans. This is the original paper. Feel the emulsion."

He touched it. She saw him feel the figure — actually run his thumb across the dark suit, the way you'd check whether a smudge was sitting on the surface or in it. It was in it. Developed there. As much a part of the silver gelatin as her great-grandfather's face.

"That's not possible," Marcus said quietly.

"I know."

"Things don't get *into* photographs after they're developed. That's not how chemistry works, that's not how—"

"Marcus." She kept her voice level. "Three weeks ago I would have written the report you're trying to write right now. I would have said pareidolia, confirmation bias, misremembered prints. I took the inventory photos for insurance because I'm careful, and the chair showed up in them anyway, and it has moved closer in every single frame since, and I have timestamped metadata proving it. I am not asking you to believe in ghosts. I'm asking you to believe in documentation. You're good at documentation."

He sat back on his heels. For a long moment he just looked at the spread of albums, at seventy years of family joy each attended by its silent figure, and she saw him arrive somewhere he didn't want to be.

"It's in all of them," he said. "Every generation."

"Every one."

"Like it's been... attending. This whole time." He picked up the Adaeze wedding print and held it closer to the window light. "This one's closest. Look how close this one is. Why is it closer in the newer ones?"

"Because it's approaching," Odile said. "It's been approaching for seventy years. Maybe longer — I haven't gotten to the oldest box yet." She nodded toward the cedar chest, where a bundle of loose photographs lay beneath the albums, wrapped in brown paper and tied with string. "Those are pre-album. Forties, thirties, maybe earlier. I haven't opened them."

"Why not?"

"Because I think the sequence goes backward," she said, and heard how it sounded, and said it anyway. "I think if I go back far enough, I'll find the first photo. The one where the chair is farthest away. Or the one where it arrives." She untied the string before he could answer, because if she waited she wouldn't do it.

The brown paper crackled open. Inside were perhaps forty photographs, sepia and silver and one daguerreotype in a velvet case, and Odile lifted them out one at a time, laying them in a line across the kitchen floor like a timeline, like a sentence being spelled out.

The earliest was the daguerreotype: a stern family posed outside a timber building, 1860s by the clothing, a town she almost recognized — the main street of this town when this town was young. And there, at the edge of the group, stood the chair. Far away. At the very limit of the frame, small as a thumbnail, angled toward the camera with the patience of a thing that had never once been in a hurry.

Behind it, barely distinguishable, a figure. Farther from it than any figure she'd yet seen. Almost respectful in its distance. A sentry at the start of a long watch.

"Odile." Marcus's voice had changed. "Look at the dates on the backs."

She flipped the nearest prints over. Her grandmother had labeled many of them in her fountain-pen slant — names, places, years. But interspersed among them were others, labeled in a different hand entirely, older, formal, the letters upright as headstones. And those labels weren't captions.

They were entries.

*Harvest year, third cycle. Attendance confirmed.*

*Harvest year, fourth cycle. The line holds. Payment deferred.*

*Harvest year, fifth cycle. She came willingly. The chair was received.*

"Oh no," Marcus said. "Oh, no, no — these are — this is a record. Someone was keeping a record *in the photographs*. On the backs of the family photos, like—" He stopped, and Odile watched him get there, watched the color leave his face by degrees. "Like a ledger."

The word landed in the kitchen and stayed there.

Her grandmother's handwriting on the most recent print — a Christmas morning, Odile herself aged nine, gap-toothed and delighted in front of a tree — read simply: *Fifth cycle closing. Sixty years. Prepare the child who documents.* Dated three weeks before her death.

"She knew," Odile said. Her voice came out strange, flattened, as if it were coming from the bottom of a well. "She knew it was coming due. She wrote it down and she died before she did anything about it, and now it's—" She gestured at the floor, at seventy years of attendants frozen mid-approach, at the timeline converging on a kitchen table where a chair did not currently sit but would, she understood now, absolutely, inevitably sit. "Now it's mine. Whatever it is. It's mine to finish."

"That's grief talking," Marcus said, but gently, without conviction, the way you repeat a formula you've stopped believing in because the alternative is worse. "Nana was sharp but she was also ninety-one, and old ladies write cryptic things, and—"

He stopped.

Odile followed his gaze. He was staring at the Christmas photo. At nine-year-old Odile in front of the tree. At the space just behind and to the left of the child she had been — where, in the photograph, unmistakably, stood the corner of a wooden chair. And behind the chair, at a distance greater than any figure in any album but less than the daguerreotype's distant sentry, stood a shape in dark clothes with its hands folded, its face a smooth absence, watching the little girl with what could only be called, in the grammar of images, attention.

"It's been next to me the whole time," Marcus whispered. "It's been *assigned* to you. Since you were a kid."

Odile reached for the photo. Her fingers trembled, and she hated that, and she picked it up anyway and held it in the window light, and as she held it — she would swear this later, swear it on anything, the way you swear on things that sound insane — the figure shifted its weight. One degree. The smallest possible step.

Toward her.

Outside, the wind moved through the bare maples, and somewhere down the block a dog began to bark at nothing, steadily, without stopping, and Odile set the photograph down very carefully, the way you set down something that is awake.

"Marcus," she said. "There's a woman on Delmore Street. Ruth Okonkwo. Nana used to send her cards every December — fifty years of cards, I found them in the desk drawer. And yesterday, when I mentioned the tunnels to her on the phone, she hung up on me." She looked at him. "Nobody hangs up on me. I'm the reasonable one. So either she's cruel, or she's terrified, and I need to know which. Tomorrow morning we're going to knock on her door, and this time I'm bringing the photographs."

Marcus looked at the line of images stretching across the kitchen floor — a century of his family, smiling, celebrating, harvested — and at the far end, the daguerreotype, the chair small and patient at the edge of the beginning.

"What are you going to ask her?" he said.

Odile gathered the photos into a stack, squaring their edges with the care she gave to things that mattered, and found that her hands had gone steady again, the way they did when a story finally showed her its spine.

"I'm going to ask her what my grandmother was preparing me for," she said. "And then I'm going to ask her what happens in sixty years if nobody pays."
