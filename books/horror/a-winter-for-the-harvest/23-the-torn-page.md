## 23. The Torn Page

The diary said *stone*, and it said *north*, and it said something else that had kept Odile awake until three in the morning:

*She will look for my words where words are kept. She should look where the house keeps its weight.*

Elias Wren — the fled founder, the coward, the man who'd loved his daughter more than the town — had written his diary like a man leaving breadcrumbs for a bird he hoped would never come. But birds always come. Hunger sees to that.

Odile stood in her grandmother's backyard at dawn with a thermal imaging gun borrowed from Marcus's construction crew and a cold cup of coffee balanced on the porch rail. The house sat on its lot like an old animal at rest, and she was about to poke it.

"North wall," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them feel like procedures instead of hauntings. "Foundation. Where the house keeps its weight."

The thermal camera showed nothing unusual. Of course it didn't. The house had spent four months teaching her that instruments were a courtesy it extended and could revoke.

She walked the north foundation anyway, slow, dragging her palm along the fieldstone the way her grandmother used to drag hers along church pews — not reading, just touching, keeping company. The stones were lichen-crusted, mortared sometime before the war, uneven as teeth. Halfway along, her fingers found a seam that wasn't a seam.

It was too straight. Too deliberate. A rectangle the size of a hymnal, outlined by a hairline gap in the mortar, set low — knee height — behind the dead hydrangea her grandmother had never gotten around to cutting back.

Odile crouched. The gap was clean on all four sides. Someone had cut this stone to be removed, then mortared it back in place and let sixty years of weather do the disguising.

*Where the house keeps its weight.*

Her phone camera came up out of habit. She took the picture before she touched anything else now; that rule had been beaten into her by the chair, by the pantry hatch, by every photograph that knew things before she did.

The photo showed the stone already pulled free. In the image, the recess behind it gaped black, and something pale rested inside — paper, folded, catching light that wasn't there yet.

The stone in front of her was still seated. Still mortared. Still whole.

"Okay," Odile whispered. "Okay. You're ahead of me. Fine."

She got the flat bar from the porch. The mortar around the false seam crumbled like stale bread — it had been scored, she realized, deliberately weakened by whoever set the stone, so that the right person with the right reason could get it out with hand tools. Elias Wren hadn't hidden the page. He'd *banked* it. Left it in trust.

The stone came free with a grinding sigh, and cold air breathed out of the gap against her knuckles — the same cellar-cold that poured from the pantry hatch, the tunnel cold, the below cold. She told herself it was just a crawlspace breathing. She was getting very good at telling herself things.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, was a fold of paper.

* * *

She didn't open it in the yard. Some instinct older than skepticism made her carry it inside, wash her hands first — actually wash them, like a surgeon or a communicant — and sit at the kitchen table under the good light. The chair's absence pressed on her from all sides. Since the night it had appeared physically, it came and went on its own schedule now, and this morning the kitchen was only furniture. She'd stopped trusting those mornings. They felt like the pause between lightning and thunder.

The oilcloth peeled away from paper that should have rotted decades ago but hadn't — another courtesy, or another threat. The handwriting matched the diary: Elias Wren's small, cramped, careful script, the letters of a man who wrote slowly because he thought fast and didn't trust himself.

The top edge was torn. Ragged, wet-looking even dry, as if the paper remembered being ripped.

She read.

*To whoever finishes what I could not —*

*I have tried three times tonight to write the closing lines of the rite and three times I have stopped, not because I fear the words but because I finally understand what they purchase, and I cannot pay it, and I will not pretend otherwise, and if that makes me a coward then let the record show the coward did the arithmetic the brave men refused to do.*

*You have read the rite. You know it asks for payment. Ruth Okonkwo's mother called it "a tithe of remembering." The founders spoke of it in the soft way men speak of taxes. I believed — we all believed — that the Below wanted memories the way a fire wants wood, indiscriminate, and that a completer could feed it scraps. A birthday. A face from school. The smell of a summer you wouldn't miss.*

*I burned such a memory myself, in the chamber, as a test. It worked. It took the taste of my mother's kitchen and left everything else standing, and I walked home lighter and thought: this is nothing. This is a fair price for a town's prosperity.*

*Then I read the lines I am about to give you, the ones Aldous Hale wrote last, alone, after the others had gone to bed drunk on their own importance. And I understood that the scraps are only the opening bid.*

*The rite does not bind the Below with words. Words are just the handle. It binds with weight. Something must go down into the dark in exchange for the thing staying down, and the only currency it accepts is love. Not the feeling — the RECORD. Every memory you hold of the person your heart is bound to. All of it. Total. As though they had never been.*

*Not some memories. Not most. Every one. The rite reaches into you and takes the whole shelf, and what remains is a shape where a person used to be, and you will stand in your own kitchen looking at photographs of a woman you know you must have loved, because everyone will tell you so, and feel nothing but the faint embarrassment of a man who has forgotten a name he was just introduced to.*

*I could not do it. My daughter is six years old. She has a gap between her front teeth and she laughs through her nose when she is truly happy and she calls cinnamon "sin-a-mon" and I am the only person alive who knows all of these things at once, and the rite would take her out of me entire, and I would live the rest of my life in a town saved by my forgetting her.*

*I chose her. God forgive me, I chose her, and I ran, and the rite has hung unfinished for sixty years, and the chair has been walking toward the table ever since.*

*Here are the lines. I copied them true. What you do with them is between you and whatever you love.*

Below that, in the same cramped hand, were eleven lines of the rite's closing passage. Odile read them twice. They were beautiful in the way liturgy is beautiful — rhythmic, binding, built of repetitions that closed like a fist. *What was given, return. What was promised, seal. Let the debt rest in flesh and not in paper, in one willing throat and not in a thousand unwilling graves.* And then the payment clause, plain as a contract:

*And for the sealing, the completer renders up the beloved entire: every hour, every word, every warmth, till the beloved is a stranger in the completer's own blood, known only by testimony.*

Odile set the page down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. She'd expected shaking. Instead there was a terrible clarity, the clarity of a diagnosis.

Every memory of the person you're saving.

She thought: *I could choose someone else.* A stranger. Pay in a currency she barely held. But the rite required a resident of the Oyelaran line, and the line's dead crowded this house, and the Below had already shown her which bond it intended to collect. It had been practicing her grandmother's voice in the tunnels. It had put the chair at *her* seat. The thing below didn't want a random beloved. It wanted the one whose loss would hurt enough to matter. Payment in counterfeit was no payment at all.

And there was no one else. That was the joke at the bottom of everything. Her grandmother had signed the ledger three weeks before dying — had signed knowing, probably, that the debt would fall to the granddaughter sorting through cardigans and recipe boxes. Had signed anyway, because the alternative was the thing below, loose, hungry, patron of disappearances.

*She knew,* Odile thought. *She signed it and she died and she never told me, because telling me would have been worse than the debt.*

Or maybe — and this thought arrived like a splinter — maybe her grandmother had believed there was another way. Maybe that was why the renewal notice sat unsigned in a drawer. Maybe the old woman had been hunting for a loophole in her last weeks and run out of time.

Odile looked down at the torn top edge of the page. Something bothered her about it. She turned the fragment over, held it to the light.

There was writing on the torn strip. Faint, reversed — mirror-script, pressed through from the other side of the sheet long ago, visible only at certain angles. She fetched her grandmother's reading lamp, angled it low.

Three words bled through the paper, backwards, in Elias Wren's hand:

*it remembers you*

She sat very still.

Not *it takes everything*. Not a warning about the mechanism. A warning about the residue. Because she had already tested the transaction — chapter and verse, the birthday she'd burned, the chair receding a few inches, the appetite opening like a hand. And afterward, in the hollow where the memory had been, something had remained. A warmth with no object. Grief-shaped space that ached without naming what it missed.

The rite would take her grandmother entire. But the taking wasn't erasure. It was *transfer*. The memories wouldn't die. They'd go down, into the dark, into the thing being bound — and the thing would hold them, keep them, wear them. It already spoke in her grandmother's cadence. After the rite, it wouldn't be imitating.

It would *remember* her grandmother better than Odile did. It would hold every hour, every word, every warmth — the laugh through the nose, the sin-a-mon, the cardigans, the recipes, the way she hummed when she canned tomatoes — all of it, sealed in the dark below the house, in the keeping of the thing the memories were paying to imprison.

Odile would forget. The Below would remember. Forever.

"That's the trade," she said to the empty kitchen. "That's the actual trade."

The house said nothing, but the light through the window seemed to thicken, the way it does when clouds consider moving.

She thought about the fled founder again, and for the first time she didn't judge him. He'd understood the full terms and refused — not out of weakness but because the terms were monstrous in a way the founders' confident liturgy had disguised. Sixty years of prosperity built on people being hollowed out and their loves imprisoned underground. Ruth had called her own refusal cowardice. Maybe it had been the only honest act in the town's history.

But refusing hadn't worked. Refusing had left the door ajar for sixty years, and the chair walking, and Vane circling with his pen. The arithmetic didn't care about honesty. Either someone paid, or the thing came up.

Odile carried the page to the table's center and weighted its corners with the salt cellars, her grandmother's, blue glass. Then she did what she always did now, the only ritual she trusted: she photographed it. Front, back, angled, macro on the mirror-script.

In the third photograph, the kitchen behind the page was different. The chair was there — at her seat, close, closer than she'd seen it since the night it crossed over — and on the chair's seat lay a folded piece of paper, white and waiting, as if something down below had drafted its own addendum and was offering it for review.

She lowered the phone. The kitchen was empty. No chair. No paper.

But under the smell of old plaster and yesterday's coffee, faint as a memory of a memory, the house smelled — briefly, unmistakably — of cinnamon.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing the thing she'd been avoiding since the diary: inventory.

Not the insurance kind. The other kind. She sat at the table with a legal pad and began listing her grandmother the way you'd catalog a collection before selling the house — except she was the house, and the collection was going to be sold whether she listed it or not.

*The laugh.* Two laughs, actually. The public one, warm and performative, deployed at church and funerals. And the real one, private, through the nose, usually at her own jokes, usually before she finished telling them.

*The hands.* Always moving. Shelling peas, kneading bread, smoothing Odile's hair when Odile was small and pretending to be asleep on the couch during Sunday movies.

*The sayings.* "Cold hands, warm heart, and yours are freezing, come here." "God gives every bird its worm, but He doesn't throw it in the nest." "Leave the door open a crack; the house needs to breathe."

*The recipes.* Jollof she'd learned from Odile's grandfather and defended against all improvement. Pepper soup for illness, real illness, the kind doctors argued about. A pound cake she measured entirely by feel and could never be talked into writing down, which meant that when Odile forgot it — and she would, she understood that now with a certainty like gravity — the cake would die with her, and no one would ever taste it again, anywhere, ever.

She filled four pages. Then she stopped, pen hovering, because she'd realized what she was doing: building the fortress. Chapter thirty-one of a plan she hadn't admitted she was making. Memorizing on purpose, so that the losing would at least happen to a well-stocked mind.

No. Not so the losing would happen. So that the *keeping* would happen somewhere. If the Below was going to hold her grandmother's memory forever, then Odile wanted the deposit complete. Every detail accounted for. Nothing left behind in some drawer of her mind, unclaimed.

"You're going to be the richest prisoner in the world," she murmured. "I hope it's cold down there. I hope you haunt it."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere in the walls, pipes settled the way they always did at dusk, and Odile chose — deliberately, professionally, the way she documented everything — to hear it as the house agreeing.

* * *

Marcus called at nine. She almost didn't answer. Since he'd come back hollowed — since he'd paid his own memories to make the chair leave him alone — their conversations had the quality of talking to a photograph of her cousin. His voice was right. The warmth behind it was gone, repossessed.

"Ruth called me," he said, without greeting. "She wants us both there tomorrow. Says it's time she told us the rest."

"The rest of what?"

"I don't know." A pause, and in the pause, for half a second, something moved behind his voice — not warmth, exactly. An echo of warmth. The ghost of a groove worn deep by feeling that had since been scooped out. "She said — she used this phrase. She said, 'Tell Odile the founder's page isn't the whole truth.' She said you'd know what that means."

Odile looked at the legal pad. Four pages of a woman, itemized, ready for deposit.

"She also said," Marcus went on, slower now, "and Odile, I don't know why this stuck in me, I don't even know who she means, but she said: 'Ask her if she's counted the cost of what the Below does with what it's given. Ask her what it builds with it.'"

The line hissed. Outside, the wind came off the fields and pressed against the north wall, against the gap where the foundation stone sat on the porch beside the dead hydrangeas, and the house breathed its cellar breath through the gap it had opened, patient, receptive, a slot built for deposits.

"I'll be there," Odile said. "Ten o'clock."

She hung up and sat in the kitchen a long time, turning Ruth's question over. *What it builds with it.* The visions in the tunnel — the town prospering, golden, fed. The boom years and the disappearances. The thing below didn't just consume what it was given. It used it. It wore her grandmother's voice because her grandmother's voice worked on her. So what would it build, after the rite, holding a complete woman — her laughter, her recipes, her forty years of Sundays — sealed in the dark beneath this house?

Prosperity, the founders promised. The town fed on love, literally.

And Odile understood, sitting in the kitchen with her four pages of inventory, that she had found the missing lines and learned the price, and the price was worse than total. Total would have been clean. Total was a bonfire. This was worse: the rite didn't burn the beloved. It *delivered* her.

Down.

To him.

She picked up the pen. At the bottom of the fourth page, under the entry about the pound cake, her hand wrote — steadily, in her own crisp professional script, the script of a woman who documents things because documentation is the only prayer she knows —

*Note: verify with Ruth whether the rite can be amended. Whether payment can be directed. Whether the Below can be made to hold her without using her. There has to be a clause. There is always a clause.*

She underlined it twice.

From the pantry, behind the tiles she'd nailed back down months ago, came a single soft knock. Like a knuckle on a door. Like something on the other side, politely, letting her know it had heard.

And that it agreed to discuss terms.
