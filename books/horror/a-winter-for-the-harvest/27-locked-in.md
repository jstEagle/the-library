## 27. Locked In

The front door opened onto brick.

Not a hallway. Not the porch with its peeling paint and the wind chime her grandmother had hung the summer Odile turned nine. Brick — old brick, mortared with something gray and gritty, laid in courses that ran past the doorframe and into the walls as if the tunnel had always been there, as if the house had been built around it like a pearl around a grain of sand.

Odile stood with her hand on the knob, her go-bag over her shoulder, and watched the brick for a long time.

She had been planning to leave. That was the thing she kept returning to, afterward, in the hours when thinking was still something she could choose to do. She had a plan. She had a duffel packed with two changes of clothes, her camera, the ledger, the transcription with its torn-away ending, and a printout of the fled founder's diary pages she'd photographed at the historical society. She had a car with a full tank. She had a cousin who wasn't answering his phone and a neighbor who was dying and a signing ceremony scheduled for Thursday morning, forty-eight hours out, at which a man with a warm handshake would dissolve the only thing standing between this town and the thing beneath it.

She had a plan. And the house had other plans, and the house had gone first.

She pressed her palm flat against the brick. It was cold in the way the tunnels were cold, a cold that felt less like temperature and more like attention. She rapped her knuckles against it twice. The sound came back dull, dense, final. Not a door disguised as a wall. A wall that had decided, at some point in the last hour, to be where the door was.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept her anchored, kept her in the category of person who narrates her own life instead of drifting through it like footage. "Okay. Back door, then."

* * *

The back door opened onto brick too.

The kitchen window showed brick. The dining room window showed brick. The bay window in the parlor, the one her grandmother had kept full of African violets, the one that looked out on the fence and the crabapple tree and, beyond them, Ruth Okonkwo's kitchen light — that window showed brick as well, but different brick, older, and set into it at eye level was a niche, and in the niche was a shelf, and on the shelf were the tools. Trowels. A mason's level. A hammer with a handle worn smooth and pale by hands that had been dead for sixty years, abandoned mid-stroke in the half-built chamber below, and here they were, arranged neatly, displayed like an exhibit, like a promise.

Odile took a photograph of the window. Her hands were steadier than she expected them to be. That frightened her more than the brick did — the calm, the procedural calm, the sense that some part of her had already accepted the terms of the situation and was simply documenting.

The photo developed on her screen. In it, the window showed not brick but the kitchen itself, seen from outside, and in the kitchen of the photograph stood a woman with her back turned, holding up a phone, taking a picture of a window.

She lowered the phone. The parlor was empty. The violets were gone — she noticed that now, the way you notice a missing tooth. The bay window's sill held nothing but a ring of dried soil where the pots had stood, as if the house had packed them away for a long journey, or eaten them.

"Marcus," she said, and dialed. It rang through to voicemail in two seconds flat, which meant it wasn't ringing anywhere at all. "Marcus, it's me. The house is — the exits are gone. If you get this, I need you to call Ruth, or come by, or — just call me back. Please."

She hung up. She stood in the parlor listening to the house.

Houses make sounds. She'd learned that over the past weeks, learned it the way you learn a language by living in a country: the tick of the water heater, the settling groans of old framing, the particular sigh the furnace made before it kicked on. This was not that. This was the sound a house makes when it is thinking, if a house could think — a pressure in the walls, a subsonic hum she felt in her molars more than heard, and beneath it, faint and rhythmic, something like breathing. Or digging. It was hard to tell the difference, and she suspected the house knew that, and enjoyed it.

* * *

She did what she always did, what had kept her alive and solvent through a decade of urban exploration in places that did not want her: she inventoried, she mapped, she made a plan.

The plan was simple. Houses are boxes. Boxes have seams. Somewhere in this box there was a seam the house hadn't gotten to yet — the chimney, the crawlspace, the coal chute her grandmother had sworn existed even though no one had ever found it. She would walk the perimeter, interior now, room by room, and she would find the seam, and she would be through it before the house finished whatever it was doing.

She started at the front and worked clockwise.

The coat closet: coats, and behind them, brick. The stairs to the second floor: fine, normal, carpeted, leading up to a landing that was also fine and normal, which was somehow worse, because it meant the house wasn't sealing everything, it was sealing the ways *out*, and it wanted her to have the interior. It wanted her comfortable. It wanted her to feel the whole shape of what she was losing before it took the last of it.

The bathroom window: brick, and in the brick, faintly, a handprint pressed into the mortar before it set. Small. A child's hand, maybe, or a small adult's. She did not put her hand against it to compare.

The bedroom window over the kitchen: brick. The closet: her grandmother's clothes, hanging exactly as they'd hung since the funeral, mothballs and lavender and wool, and at the back of the closet, where the access panel to the attic ought to be, brick again, but this brick was warm. She held her palm against it for three seconds before she understood what the warmth meant and snatched her hand back. The tunnels ran under the house. The tunnels ran *through* the house now. The house was not sealing her in so much as it was becoming continuous with what was below, the way a river becomes continuous with the sea, and she was standing in the last dry room of it.

Downstairs again. The pantry. The hatch.

She'd pried it up weeks ago, that first discovery, iron fittings older than the house itself, and the photograph she'd taken of it had shown it already open. It was open now. It had been open, she realized, since the night she'd found it — she'd closed it, she was sure she'd closed it, she'd set the tile back down and weighted it with the cast-iron Dutch oven, and the Dutch oven was sitting on the counter where she'd left it that morning, and the tile was leaning against the wall, and the hatch stood open on darkness, and the cold came up out of it like a tide.

From the darkness, faintly, the smell of her grandmother's kitchen. Pepper soup. Fried plantain. That specific sweetness of her grandmother's cardamom tea, the one she'd made when Odile was sick, when Odile was sad, when Odile had come home at twenty-two with her life in boxes after the thing with Daniel, and had sat at that table and been fed without a single question asked.

The house was not trying to frighten her.

The house was trying to make her comfortable.

That was the thought that finally cracked the procedural calm, and Odile sat down on the pantry floor with her back against the cabinets and let the fear come, properly, all at once, the way it does when it has been waiting politely at the door. Her breath went short. Her hands went cold around the phone she wasn't going to call anyone on, because there was no one to call, because the one person who might have come was a hollowed man in a rented room somewhere who no longer remembered the woman whose house this was, and the other person who might have come was ninety-one years old and dying and had already told Odile, with tears in her eyes, that she had failed this test once and would not survive failing it again.

No one was coming.

She breathed. She counted, the way her grandmother had taught her to count when the asthma came — in for four, hold for four, out for six — and the counting did what it always did, which was to put her back inside her body, inside the moment, inside the problem. And the problem was this: the house had sealed itself, the chair had moved to the head of the table, the signing was in forty-eight hours, and the only way out of the house, she was increasingly sure, was the way she'd been avoiding for weeks.

Down.

* * *

She made herself walk into the dining room.

She had been avoiding the dining room since the morning. She told herself it was because she needed to check the second floor first, then because she needed to eat, then because there was no reason at all, and finally she stood in the doorway and made herself look.

The table was set for two.

Her place was where it had always been — third chair on the left, the one with the wobble her grandfather had never fixed, facing the window. The place across from it was laid too, but that wasn't what stopped her breath. What stopped her breath was the head of the table.

The chair was there.

It had been in the photographs first — the extra wooden chair, absent from every room, present in every frame, closer in each image, a patient arithmetic of approach. Then it had been in the kitchen, physical, facing her seat. Then Marcus had fled and it had waited. Now it stood at the head of the table, where her grandmother had sat for forty years, and it was not the grandmother's chair. The grandmother's chair was still in the garage, under a tarp, where Odile had put it in the first week because she couldn't look at it. This was the other one. The one from the photographs. Dark wood, high-backed, its spindles worn smooth in the shapes of hands, and it sat at the head of the table with the unhurried authority of something that had been invited.

On its seat lay a folded piece of paper.

Odile did not want to pick it up. She picked it up, because the alternative was to stand in the doorway forever, and the house would wait forever, and she had forty-eight hours.

The paper was old, yellowed, soft at the folds. It was a page from a ledger — she recognized the hand, the same careful nineteenth-century script as the founders' ledger in the bricked alcove below. But this was not a signature page. It was a list. Names, in two columns, some struck through, some not.

At the top, in larger letters, as a heading, as a title:

**RESIDENTS REMAINING.**

And beneath it, the names. She scanned them with a numb, documentary attention. Founders' names, family names she knew from the historical society, from street signs, from the plaque outside the library. Ruth Okonkwo's name was there, unstruck, with a small notation beside it in a different, newer hand: *declined.* Marcus's name was there, struck through, and beside it: *paid in part. exempt.* And at the bottom, in ink so fresh it still shone, in handwriting that made her stomach drop through the floor because it was *her* handwriting, her own cramped left-slanting script she'd had since grade school:

**Oyelaran, O. — pending.**

She read it four times. Her own handwriting. She had never written that page. She had never seen that page. But the house had written it in her hand, the way it had photographed itself open before she'd opened it, the way it did everything a step ahead of her, everything a step ahead, everything patient, everything sure.

The last line of the page, beneath her name, in the same fresh ink:

*The chair does not come for the house. The chair comes for the resident. There is one.*

* * *

She took the page with her. It seemed important to take it — evidence, or leverage, or just so that it wouldn't be sitting there on the chair, addressed to her, waiting. She put it in the go-bag with the ledger and the diary pages and the torn transcription, her little archive of the unmeasurable, and then she did the last thing there was to do above ground.

She walked the house one more time. Not looking for exits this time. She knew how that search would end.

She walked it looking at what she was leaving.

The kitchen, where the light came in golden in the late afternoon and turned the counter into something out of a memory she hadn't made yet. Her grandmother's recipe box on the shelf, the index cards soft with decades of thumbs, the pepper soup recipe written in her grandmother's looping hand with a note in the margin: *more scotch bonnet than you think, then a little more.* The living room where they'd watched Sunday broadcasts with the volume too loud because her grandmother's hearing had gone but her opinions hadn't. The front hall mirror where her grandmother had straightened Odile's collar before every school picture, every funeral, every date, saying the same thing every time: *Stand up straight. The world takes you at the height you carry.*

Odile stood in front of the mirror now. The mirror showed the hall, and her, and behind her, at the far end of the hall, the dining room doorway, and in the doorway, nothing. Just the empty frame and the golden light and the sense — not a sighting, never a sighting, the thing was too careful for that — of being looked at from a room away.

"Thursday," she said to the mirror, to the house, to whatever was listening from below or beside or inside. "He signs Thursday. Whatever you think you're doing in here, it doesn't matter if he signs. So either you let me out, or you help me. Those are the options."

The house breathed. The brick hummed. Somewhere below, in the dark under the pantry, in the half-built chamber where the founders' tools still lay where they'd been dropped, something shifted its weight, the way an old man shifts in a chair when the conversation finally gets to the point.

And then, from everywhere and nowhere — from the walls, from the floor, from the open hatch and the sealed windows and the warm brick behind the closet — came a voice.

It was her grandmother's voice. Not an imitation. Not a recording. It was the real cadence, the real warmth, the real faint rasp at the end of sentences where the years had settled, and it said:

"Come down, my love. We should talk terms."

Odile stood in the golden light of the kitchen with her hand on the recipe box and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she understood, with the terrible clarity of the fully committed, that the house had not locked her in at all.

It had opened the only door that mattered.

The hatch at the end of the hall exhaled cold air, and the chair at the head of the table turned, very slightly, to face it — an invitation, a courtesy, a thing making room.

She picked up the go-bag.

She went down.
