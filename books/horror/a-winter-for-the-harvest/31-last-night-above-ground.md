## 31. Last Night Above Ground

Odile woke before the alarm on purpose.

She had set it for six out of habit, then lain awake from four-thirty watching the ceiling of her childhood bedroom do its slow morning trick — the water stain above the window resolving, as the light came up, into the shape of a hand. She'd catalogued that stain years ago, back when she still believed houses were just houses. Now she lay still and let it happen again, because she had decided, somewhere around two in the morning, that today would not be spent preparing the rite.

Tomorrow could have the rite. Today was for something else.

She got up quietly, though there was no one left in the house to wake. Marcus's room stood open and stripped, his duffel gone, the mattress bare except for one pillow he hadn't wanted. The hallway smelled like old plaster and cold air rising through the pantry floor, which she had covered again with tiles she no longer pretended were holding anything down. The chair sat at the head of the kitchen table when she passed it. She did not look at it directly. Looking at it directly felt like feeding it, and today she needed to feed herself.

She put the kettle on and took out her notebook — not the field notebook with the tunnel maps, not the transcription of the rite with its torn-away ending, but a cheap spiral-bound thing she'd bought at the pharmacy on Delancey, the kind schoolkids used. On the cover she wrote, in block letters:

**THINGS TO REMEMBER ABOUT IYA ADUNNI**

Underneath, smaller: *You will not remember why this matters. That is the point. Write it so it matters anyway.*

The kettle clicked off. She poured the water over the last of her grandmother's loose tea — the tin nearly empty, the leaves inside dark and oily-smelling, imported by some cousin-of-a-cousin who never labeled anything — and carried the cup to the kitchen table, where she sat in her own seat, across from the chair, and began.

*Her laugh,* she wrote first, because it was the thing she was most afraid of losing and therefore the thing to go after while her hands were steady. *Not a laugh exactly. A sound she made when something was funny — a short exhale through the nose, like a horse deciding against spooking, followed by "Ah-ah," said twice, getting softer. She almost never laughed with her mouth open. When she did — twice in my whole life that I saw — it was because my grandfather, dead twenty years, appeared in a dream and told her the price of tomatoes.*

She stopped. Read it back. It wasn't enough. It was a description of a laugh, not the laugh.

She closed her eyes and went looking.

This was the discipline of it, she realized — the part nobody warned you about. Memory didn't come when summoned like a witness; it came like a cat, and you had to sit very still and pretend you weren't interested until it wandered close on its own terms. So she sat with her tea going lukewarm and let the kitchen be quiet, and after a while — the radiator ticking, the refrigerator humming its one flat note — it came.

Not the sound first. The context. Sunday afternoons, the small television muting Nollywood films between scenes, and Odile at eleven or twelve telling some story about school, embellishing shamelessly, and her grandmother listening with her chin propped on her knuckles, eyes half-shut, giving nothing away — and then, at the exact moment Odile's lie reached its most ridiculous point, the exhale. *Ah-ah. Ah-ah.* And Odile, caught, collapsing into giggles, and her grandmother saying, "You are your father's daughter. He also lied beautifully."

Odile opened her eyes and wrote all of it down. Fast, messy, the pen tearing slightly on the third line. Then she read it aloud, once, in the empty kitchen, because she had read somewhere — or decided, it amounted to the same thing now — that spoken things sat deeper than written ones.

"You are your father's daughter," she told the room. "He also lied beautifully."

The chair did not move. But the light through the window seemed to lean in a little, the way an audience leans.

* * *

By nine she had filled six pages and made herself stop laughing at her own recollections, because every time she laughed she could feel the memory loosen afterward, like a knot worked at. That was the trick of the payment, she understood now — it didn't rip things out. It waited until you held them loosely and then asked you to open your hand.

So she switched subjects. Recipes. Recipes were safer, or seemed safer, because they lived partly outside her — written in her grandmother's hand on index cards soft as cloth, stained with decades of palm oil and pepper, kept in the second drawer beside the stove. She took the whole stack out and spread them across the counter like a hand of cards.

Efo riro. Asaro. Ofada rice with the sauce that made Marcus cry actual tears and claim it was the peppers. Moi moi wrapped in leaves instead of foil because, her grandmother insisted, foil moimoi was for people who had given up on God. Jollof — the jollof, the one Odile had tried to reproduce in three apartments in two cities and failed every time, and her grandmother had watched each failure over video call with that nose-exhale and said, "You are rushing the base. Everything is the base. Life is also the base."

Odile picked up the jollof card. Her grandmother's handwriting was small and slanted and absolutely certain, ingredients listed without quantities for half the items — *peppers (the right ones)*, *oil (enough)*, *onion: do not be shy* — because she had written these cards for people who already knew, for daughters and granddaughters who had stood beside her at this same stove, and the gaps were not omissions. They were invitations. Come stand here. Watch. Smell when it changes. You cannot learn this from paper.

And Odile had always meant to. There had been time. There had been *so much* time, entire visits spent sprawled in the living room with her laptop while the smells happened without her, promising next visit, next visit, next —

She gripped the edge of the counter until her knuckles ached.

"No," she said out loud. "No. That's not today either."

Grief was a door she couldn't afford to walk through today, because grief was a kind of forgetting too — it blurred, it generalized, it turned a specific woman into a warm fog — and today's work was specificity or nothing. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, hard, once, the way her grandmother wiped counters, and went back to the cards.

She cooked.

It was possibly insane to spend the day before a ritual descent standing over a hot stove, but the logic held when she turned it over: the body remembered what the mind lost. Muscle memory was a vault. If she made the jollof today — really made it, slowly, attending to every stage — then some residue of it would live below language, below memory, in her hands. They could take her grandmother from her head. Let them try to take her from Odile's wrists.

So she did it properly. She blended the peppers the night-before way even though there was no night before — red bell, scotch bonnet, the dried ones soaked soft — and she did not rush the base. She sliced onions until her eyes streamed and did not be shy. She let the oil take the tomato paste and turn it brick-dark, let it go past the point where a nervous cook would panic, because she remembered — suddenly, wholly, the way a photograph develops — standing on a step stool at seven years old while her grandmother said, "Now it is burning. Good. Now we save it." And the saving was the stock, and the stirring, and the long patience, and the smell that rose up and filled the whole ground floor and drifted out the kitchen window into the yard where Ruth Okonkwo's laundry line stood, and Ruth calling over the fence, "Adunni! What are you doing to us over there!" and her grandmother's answer, the exhale-laugh, "Suffering you. Come eat."

Odile stood in the steam and cried freely, and kept stirring, and the crying did not blur anything. It sharpened. That was the strange mercy of it: tears were a fixative. Every sob burned the scene in deeper — the stool, the spoon, the voice, the fence, the laundry, the light coming gold through the window at four in the afternoon the way it only did in October.

She ate the jollof standing up, straight from the pot, which her grandmother would have called barbaric and then done herself.

Then she washed the pot by hand, the way the woman being forgotten would have insisted, and dried it, and hung it on its hook, and wrote on page nine of the notebook: *The pot is heavy. Cast iron, black, older than me. It hangs on the hook by the window. If you find this notebook and don't know why the pot matters — it matters.*

* * *

In the afternoon she walked the house.

Room by room, deliberately, the way she'd photographed it for insurance in the first week — except now she was photographing with her mind, and the insurance was different. The living room, where the good sofa lived under plastic covers that came off only for Christmas and funerals, and how her grandmother had finally stopped re-covering it the last year, saying, "Let the sofa breathe. I will also breathe." The hall mirror with the crack running through the corner that she refused to replace because the crack arrived the week Odile was born and she considered it load-bearing. The sewing room that was also the argument room, where every serious conversation in the family's history had been conducted among bolts of fabric, because, her grandmother said, it was harder to say something unforgivable while touching something beautiful.

Odile touched the fabrics. Wrote down their colors. Wrote down the phrase about the unforgivable.

Upstairs, the bedroom. The rosary — no, not a rosary, the prayer beads, cowrie and wood, that hung on the bedpost and clicked at night when the wind came through the window gap, a sound Odile had slept beneath on every visit of her childhood and had not thought about once in fifteen years until this moment, and the remembering of it hit her like a wave breaking over a seawall, the whole ocean arriving at once: summer nights, the fan turning, the beads clicking their dry little rhythm, her grandmother's breathing in the dark, slow and certain, the breathing of a woman who had made peace with whatever came through windows.

"She was not afraid," Odile wrote. Her hand shook. "Whatever is under this house, she spoke to it every sixty years and climbed back up and made breakfast. She was not afraid, and she never told me, and I am allowed to be angry about that and I am allowed to love her for it and both of those are true and I am writing them down so that whoever-I-am-tomorrow knows they were true."

She read it back. She made herself believe it.

Downstairs again as the light went long and orange. In the pantry she paused over the tiles, feeling the cold seep up through the seams, and crouched, and pressed her palm flat against them.

"Tomorrow," she said. Not a promise. An appointment.

* * *

Ruth Okonkwo came at dusk, unannounced, carrying a covered dish in the ancient transactional grammar of the neighborhood. Odile let her in and watched her take in the kitchen — the notebook, the index cards fanned out, the pot on its hook — and something in the old woman's face folded and refolded.

"You're cooking her food," Ruth said.

"I'm learning it. Late."

"It's not late." Ruth set the dish down — pepper soup, by the smell, the kind that cleared sinuses and sins alike. "It's never late until it's finished." She looked at the chair at the head of the table, and Odile watched her look at it, and neither of them commented, which was itself a kind of comment. "You sleep tonight?"

"That's the plan."

"Liar." Ruth smiled without humor. "I didn't sleep before mine either. Sixty-one. I lay awake doing exactly what you're doing. Making lists." Her eyes went distant, toward a year Odile had only seen in ledgers and diaries. "You know what I remember most? Not the ritual. Not the tunnels. I remember that I made egusi that day, and I can't tell you why that's what stayed. The body keeps what it keeps." She patted Odile's arm, briefly, her hand light as paper. "Eat the soup. Sleep if sleep comes. And girl—" she paused at the door, and for a moment she looked every one of her eighty-some years, and then some, "—whatever you write down tonight, write it like a letter. Not a record. A letter. Records are for strangers. Letters are for someone you love."

The door closed behind her. Odile stood in the quiet and understood that Ruth had just handed her the last piece of the method, free of charge, the way neighbors do.

A letter. Fine. She turned to a fresh page and started over, not from facts but from address.

*Dear whoever I am tomorrow,*

*I'm writing to tell you about a woman named Adunni Oyelaran, and I need you to understand from the first line that I love her, even though by the time you read this the word won't mean what it means right now, sitting here, writing it. Love her anyway. Trust the word the way you'd trust a bridge built by someone who crossed it every day for forty years.*

*Here is what she smelled like: cocoa butter and camphor and, underneath, something green I have never found anywhere else and looked for everywhere. Here is what her hands felt like: soft on top, iron underneath, the paradox of women who knead dough and carry firewood and still end their lives with palms like bread. Here is what she said when I left for university, at the gate, holding my face in those hands: "Go and be brilliant. But come home and be ordinary sometimes. Brilliance is loud. Ordinary is where I live."*

*I lived there too. I just didn't know it was a place.*

The pen slowed. Outside, full dark had come down over the yard, and the house made its nighttime sounds — settling wood, ticking radiator, the faint dry click from upstairs that might have been wind through a window gap and might have been beads, and Odile chose, deliberately, deliberately, to believe it was beads.

*One more thing,* she wrote. *When you finish reading this and feel nothing — and you will feel nothing, that's the whole arrangement — I want you to notice that you're crying anyway. Because I'm crying now, writing it, and some of this has to survive in the ink itself. Some of this is in the paper. Some of this is in the pot on the hook and the crack in the mirror and the way you'll flinch, tomorrow, at the smell of onions browning, without knowing why. She's in all of it. We don't get to keep her. But we don't get to lose all of her either. That's the deal I've made with whatever is listening under the floor.*

*Go down tomorrow. Say the words. Sit in the chair if you have to.*

*And then come back up, and make the jollof, and don't rush the base.*

*— Odile, who loved her*

She signed it. She read it aloud once, to the kitchen, to the chair, to the beads clicking softly overhead, to the cold air rising patient through the pantry floor.

Then she laid the notebook on the table beside the ledger and the restored final lines, squared them together like tools for a job, and went upstairs to lie down and not sleep.

She was almost asleep — drifting in that border country where the house's sounds become large and slow — when she heard it. From the kitchen. Small. Distinct.

The scrape of a wooden chair being drawn back from a table.

Not closer to her seat. Closer to the pantry tiles.

Waiting for morning, patient as a debt, and Odile lay in the dark with her heart going hard and whispered into the pillow, "Tomorrow. I said tomorrow," and the house — the whole house, walls and beams and the deep brick throat beneath them — seemed to exhale, and agree, and let her keep the last night she would ever have of knowing why it mattered.
