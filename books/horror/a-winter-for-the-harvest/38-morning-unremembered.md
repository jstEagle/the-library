## 38. Morning, Unremembered

She woke on the kitchen floor with her cheek against cold linoleum and the smell of bread in the air, which was impossible, because no one had baked bread in this house in months.

Odile lay still for a long moment, cataloguing. That was her training. You woke up disoriented in a strange place, you didn't move until you'd inventoried: body first, then surroundings. Her body reported nothing wrong — a deep ache in her legs, like she'd walked a very long way, and a rawness in her throat, as if she'd been speaking for hours. Or singing. Her hands were filthy, chalk and clay ground into the creases, under the nails. There was a smear of something dark along her left forearm that she decided not to look at too closely.

The kitchen was just a kitchen.

Morning light came through the window over the sink the way it was supposed to, flat and gray and ordinary, catching the dust on the counter, the dish rack with two plates drying in it. The refrigerator hummed. A fly tested the glass of the window with small patient clicks. She knew — some part of her knew how to be afraid of this room, the way you know how to flinch from a stair that isn't there in the dark — but there was nothing here to be afraid of. The table stood where tables stand. Four chairs around it, tucked in neatly.

Four chairs. She counted them twice without knowing why.

* * *

She got up in stages, testing the floor like ice. Everything held. The hallway was a hallway. The front door opened onto the porch, the porch onto the yard, the yard onto Ruth Okonkwo's fence, and beyond it the street, wet with last night's rain, a delivery truck idling three houses down. The world outside had kept going. That seemed important. She stood on the porch in her ruined clothes and breathed the cold air and felt — nothing. A clean, scoured nothing, like a whiteboard after it's been erased. Not grief. Not relief. Just the particular emptiness of a room after the furniture's been carried out.

Her phone was on the counter where she'd apparently left it, dead. She found the charger in a drawer — third drawer, left of the stove, and something flickered at the edge of her mind at knowing that, some warmth, gone before she could turn toward it — and plugged it in. While it woke she put water on to boil, because her hands wanted a task, and while the water heated she noticed the photographs.

They were spread across the kitchen table. Dozens of them. Every photograph from the house, it looked like, pulled from albums and frames and shoeboxes and laid out in careful rows, face-up, like evidence at a hearing or cards dealt by someone very methodical. She had no memory of doing it. Her handwriting was on the back of the nearest one — *kitchen, window side, 11:40 p.m.* — dated four days ago.

She turned it over.

It was a picture of this kitchen, taken from the doorway. Her grandmother's kitchen — she knew that the way you know a fact from a file, without any feeling attached to it, the way you know the capital of a country you've never visited. In the photo the table sat against the wall and there were chairs around it, and she counted them the way she'd counted the real ones, twice, because —

Because there were only three in the photo. And she had been counting four all morning.

She picked up the next one. Three chairs. The next, an old faded print of a woman standing at this same counter, laughing at whoever held the camera — three chairs. A Christmas photo, tinsel, a turkey, faces she didn't recognize and couldn't make herself care about: three chairs. She went through the whole table, every image, fast now, her breath going shallow. Not one extra chair anywhere. Whatever had been in these pictures — whatever had been getting closer, frame by frame, patient as erosion — was gone.

The kettle began to shriek and she startled so badly she knocked two photos to the floor.

* * *

She drank tea she didn't remember liking and tried to reconstruct the week, and the week would not reconstruct. It wasn't fog exactly. Fog implied something to see through. It was more like a hole with smooth edges — she knew there had been events, the way you know a road continues past where the map ends, but when she reached for specifics her attention slid off. Tunnels. The word came to her unbidden and meant nothing. A ledger. Vane. Names with weight and no content, like labels on jars that had been emptied and relabeled with something else.

There were gaps in her hands, too. Chalk under the nails. A blister on her palm shaped exactly like the handle of something she couldn't name. When she flexed her fingers, they moved with a familiarity that suggested recent practice at — what? Digging? Writing? Praying?

On the counter, beneath the tea tin, she found pages.

Her own handwriting. She recognized it the way you recognize your own face in a shop window — with a jolt of ownership and estrangement at once. There were many pages, written in different sittings, the ink changing, the pressure of the pen changing, some entries calm and some scrawled so hard the paper had torn. She sat down at the table, moved the photographs aside, and began to read.

*Her name is Abike Oyelaran. Everyone called her Granny Bee except me; I called her Mama. She came to this country in 1974 with two suitcases and a recipe book written in Yoruba in the margins of an English hymnal.*

Odile stopped.

She read it again. The words described a woman completely, specifically, lovingly — the way she hummed when she cooked, off-key, always the same three notes repeated like a broken music box; the way she said *come and eat* as if it were a spell; the scar on her thumb from a can lid, 1989, that she told as a story with a beginning and middle and end. Odile read about a laugh that started silent, in the shoulders, before it ever made sound. About hands that smelled of nutmeg and dish soap. About a house that always smelled like bread on Sundays.

She felt nothing.

That was the horror of it, she understood dimly — not the words themselves but the absence they sat inside of. It was like reading someone else's love letters. Like reading a eulogy for a stranger. The handwriting was hers. The knowledge was hers — she knew these details were true the way she knew the capital of that unvisited country. But the person they belonged to had been carried out of her, furniture and all, and the room where that person had lived was swept and clean and humming with the refrigerator's ordinary sound.

*She is why I am doing this,* the page said. *Whatever it costs. She is worth the cost.*

*I am writing this so that somewhere, in ink, outside my head, she will have existed. If I can't keep her, the paper can.*

*If you are reading this and you are me: you paid. Don't go looking for change.*

Odile set the pages down very carefully, aligned their edges, squared them against the table's corner. Her vision had gone blurry. She wiped her cheek with the back of her hand and found it wet, and was surprised, genuinely surprised, the way you'd be surprised by rain through a closed window.

She was crying. Great, silent, stupid tears, dropping onto the pages, blurring the ink of a woman named Abike Oyelaran who called her granddaughter *ọmọ mi* and whose laugh started in the shoulders.

"Why," she said out loud, to the kitchen. "Why am I —"

The kitchen declined to answer. The refrigerator hummed. The fly clicked against the glass. Somewhere below the floor — far below, deeper than any cellar, deeper than the pipes and the foundations and the old packed earth — something settled, satisfied, and was quiet.

She cried for eleven minutes. She knew it was eleven because she watched the clock, because documenting things was what she did, was apparently the only thing left of herself that hadn't been spent. Then it passed, the way weather passes, leaving no explanation, only damp.

* * *

Marcus came by in the afternoon.

She heard his truck before she saw him, and when she opened the door he stood on the step holding a casserole dish like a shield, looking at her with an expression she couldn't parse — grief and guilt and recognition all fighting for the same space on his face.

"Hey," he said. His voice cracked on it. He cleared his throat. "You're okay."

"I'm okay." She stepped back to let him in, and he hesitated on the threshold, actually hesitated, one foot raised, like a man asked to cross a line painted on the floor. Then he came in and set the dish on the counter and stood there with his hands in his pockets, turning slowly, taking in the kitchen as if checking it for something.

"Chair's gone," he said quietly.

"What chair?"

He looked at her then, really looked, and whatever he saw made him look away fast, jaw working. "Nothing," he said. "Nothing. Bad joke." He opened his mouth again, closed it. She watched him decide, visibly, not to say whatever was queued behind his teeth. *I'm sorry. I don't remember her either. We did something. I think we did something terrible and necessary and I can't tell you what because you'll ask me why I'm crying too.*

"Food's for you," he said instead. "Jollof. Family recipe." And then he flinched, slightly, at his own words, at *family recipe*, a phrase that clearly meant something to him that he could no longer locate.

They stood in the kitchen together, two people weeping inwardly at a stranger across the counter, drinking tea neither of them remembered sharing before, talking about nothing — the weather, the road work on Delancey, whether the preservation trust would take the house. At the door he paused.

"You'd tell me," he said. "If something was wrong. You'd document it."

"I document everything," Odile said. "It's what I do."

He nodded like that hurt him, and left.

* * *

That evening she went through the house room by room with her camera, because that was the job, that had been the job from the start — insurance documentation, estate inventory, a professional's clean record of a dead woman's property. *A dead woman's property.* She said it aloud in the empty hallway and it landed like a fact about Antarctica.

In every frame: rooms. Just rooms. Furniture where furniture goes, light where light falls, nothing extra, nothing waiting, nothing patient. She photographed the pantry and the tiles sat flat and sealed. She photographed the hallway and it was exactly as long as it was wide. She photographed the mirror in the bathroom and it showed only her — a thin, exhausted woman with chalk still ghosting her hairline, eyes red-rimmed from crying she couldn't account for.

At the bottom of the stairs she found the ledger.

It was sitting on the third step, square and centered, as if placed. Old leather, cracked spine, pages soft as cloth. She knew its weight in her hands before she'd fully lifted it — knew it, the way her hands had known the drawer where the charger lived. Inside, signatures marched down the decades in fading inks. Halfway down the final page, in her own handwriting, fresh, the ink barely dry:

*Paid in full.*

Below it, a second line, smaller:

*Ọmọ mi. Come and eat.*

Odile closed the ledger. She held it against her chest and stood on the stairs in the failing light and wept again, briefly, terribly, without one single reason she could name — and then it passed, and she wiped her face, and she carried the ledger to the kitchen and set it on the table beside the photographs, beside the pages of her own handwriting describing a woman who had smelled of nutmeg and dish soap, a woman whose laugh had started in the shoulders, a woman Odile Oyelaran had loved enough to pay everything for and could not remember ever meeting.

Outside, the first snow of the season had begun to fall, fine and dry, settling on the roof, the yard, the whole patient town.

She put the kettle on.
