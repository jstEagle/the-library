## 19. Burning the Book

**Marisol**

The house began to scream at 11:47 p.m., and it screamed in the voice of a settling foundation—groans from the joists, a long tearing sound from somewhere above the second floor, as if the roof were being peeled back like the lid of a can.

Marisol stood in the hallway with a box of kosher salt under one arm and her heart going like a trapped bird under her ribs. The mirror at the end of the hall—the tall one with the tarnished frame that Edith had covered with a lace shawl for as long as anyone could remember—had thrown off its covering sometime during dinner. The shawl lay on the floor in a heap, and the glass was not showing the hallway anymore.

It was showing him.

The winter tenant sat in the chair at the head of the dining table, except the chair was not in the dining room now; it was *in* the mirror, and he was leaning forward with his long hands folded, patient as a man waiting for a train that had been delayed a century. His face was the face of someone who had been handsome once and had let it go out of use, like a room closed off for the season. When he saw her looking, he inclined his head. Courteous. Always courteous.

"Marisol," he said. His voice came not from the glass but from the walls, from the radiator pipes, from under the floorboards, everywhere and nowhere. "You don't have to stand there shivering. There's a place set for you. There has always been a place set for you."

She upended the salt into her cupped palm and threw it at the mirror.

The grains hit the glass and stuck—not scattered across the floor but adhering to the surface in a creeping white frost, and where they landed, the figure blurred, his edges going soft as breath on a window. He did not flinch. That was the worst part. He simply looked at her through the salt-frosted glass the way you'd look at a child who had drawn on the wallpaper.

"That won't hold me long," he said gently. "Salt is a promise, and promises fade. But I admire the effort. Edith used to do exactly that."

"Don't say her name."

"Edith." He said it slowly, tasting it. "Edith, who signed for all of them. Edith, who thought love was a debt she could keep paying forever. She's listening right now, you know. They're all listening. Can't you hear them?"

And God help her, she could. Under the groaning of the house, under the wind that had no business being inside, there was a sound rising through the floorboards like water coming up through a basement drain—a murmur of voices, dozens of them, layered, some of them singing, some of them just saying names over and over. Names of the dead callers. Names crossed out in the ledger.

From the study came Felix's voice, tight and controlled the way he got when control was the only thing keeping him upright: "Marisol! Is it holding?"

"For now!" she shouted back. "How's the book?"

A pause. Then, quieter: "The book doesn't want to burn."

* * *

**Wren**

Wren had forty-one photographs spread across the kitchen table, and she was building a wall.

Not a wall of paper—a wall of *seeing*. She'd figured it out an hour ago, crouched over her camera in the dark of her room while the house shook around her: the thing in the mirror hated being witnessed. Every photograph she'd taken of it—the smear of almost-human shape pulling the chair, the gaunt silhouette in the hallway glass, the long fingers on the table's edge—every image made it flicker, made it hesitate, made the chair stop moving for a few seconds. Her aunt's house had spent a hundred years making sure nobody looked directly at it. Wren had been looking all winter. Looking was the one thing she knew how to do.

So she taped the photographs to the kitchen doorway, floor to lintel, overlapping like scales, until there was no way through the kitchen without passing through a corridor of evidence. Forty-one images of the tenant, all of them true at once, all of them refusing to be rationalized away.

The house noticed immediately. The lights in the kitchen dimmed to brown. The temperature dropped so fast her breath bloomed. And the humming—the humming that lived in the extra chair, the lullaby tune that had been seeping through the walls since December—stopped, and in its absence Wren heard how loud the silence was.

"You're just a kid," said a voice from the pantry.

Wren didn't scream. She'd stopped having screams left around eleven o'clock. She picked up the flashlight, clicked it on, and shone it into the pantry, where the shelves were receding backward into a darkness deeper than the pantry had any right to contain.

"I'm fourteen," she said. "I've read more about hauntings than you've read about anything, probably, because you've been sitting in a chair for a hundred years."

The darkness laughed. It wasn't unkind. That was the trap of it, she understood now—it was never cruel, not really. Cruelty would have been easier to fight. It was *reasonable*. It offered. It waited. It made you feel like leaving was your own rude idea.

"The papers are on the table," the darkness said. "One signature. One name, and everyone else walks free into the morning. Isn't that what families do? Take turns carrying the heavy thing?"

"We don't take turns," Wren said. "We carry it together. That's the whole point. You wouldn't get it—you've never had anybody."

The silence after that had a different texture. Hurt, maybe. Or calculation. With the tenant it was impossible to tell the difference, and that, too, was the design.

"Wren!" Marisol's voice, from the hall. "I need more photos! It's pushing through the salt!"

Wren grabbed her camera and ran.

* * *

**Marisol**

The mirror was losing.

The salt frost was melting from the center outward, and through the clearing glass the tenant had risen from his chair. He stood close against the inside of the mirror now, so near that Marisol could see the stitching of his coat, the gray whorls of his irises, the way his breath—if it was breath—fogged the glass from the other side. Behind him, impossibly deep, the mirror held a version of the house that went on and on, hallway after hallway, each one colder and emptier than the last. A house built entirely of waiting rooms.

"There is a version of this," he said, "where nobody gets hurt. Where you stay, and the boy stays, and the girl stays, and the table is full every night for the rest of time. Edith wanted that. Why do you all act like wanting it is a sin?"

"Because we didn't choose it," Marisol said. Her hands were shaking. She poured another line of salt across the hallway floor anyway, a barricade between the mirror and the stairs. "That's the whole difference. You collect people. We *chose* each other. You wouldn't know the difference because to you a resident and a prisoner spell the same word."

Something moved behind him in the mirrored depths—shapes, faces, pressed against the far end of that endless hallway like children at a window. The dead ones. The ones whose names were crossed out. One of them raised a hand, and Marisol realized with a jolt that she recognized her: Mrs. Okafor, from the radio, six days dead and still wearing her church hat.

They weren't pressing toward the tenant. They were pressing toward *her*. Toward the exit. Toward the sound of living voices.

"They're not yours," Marisol breathed. "They never signed. They just died near you, and you kept them."

"Everything that enters a house belongs to the house," the tenant said. "That is the oldest law there is."

"Then it's a good thing," said Wren, skidding into the hallway with her camera already raised, "that we're burning the law."

* * *

**Wren**

Felix came out of the study carrying the ledger the way you'd carry something venomous—one hand under it, one hand hovering, ready to drop it.

He looked terrible. Three days without sleep had carved hollows under his eyes, and his hands had a fine tremor he was trying to hide by gripping the book harder. But when he saw the two of them standing in the hallway—Marisol behind her salt line, Wren with her camera—he stopped, and something in his face changed. Softened. Like he'd forgotten, for a second, what he was afraid of, because he was looking at them.

"It's fighting me," he said. "The pages won't catch. I've tried three times. The fire just... goes around them."

"Because it's still counting," Wren said. "As long as the ledger exists, the count exists. It knows if it holds on long enough, one of us signs."

"So we hold longer," Marisol said.

The house convulsed. Down the hall, every door slammed at once—not shut, *slammed*, hard enough to crack the plaster—and the temperature plunged again, and from the dining room came the sound none of them would ever be able to describe properly afterward: the chair, dragging itself across the floorboards, one slow scrape at a time, coming for the table, coming for the papers, coming for whoever was fool enough to be standing nearest when the longest night ended.

"Not yet," Felix whispered. "It's not solstice yet. We have till midnight. Nobody signs before midnight. Nobody signs *at* midnight. Nobody signs at all."

"Then burn the damn book," Marisol said.

They moved together—three people who eight weeks ago had been strangers occupying the same square footage, moving now like they'd drilled it. Marisol swept the salt line wider, cutting off the hallway so the mirror-thing couldn't flank them. Wren ran ahead down the stairs, taping photographs to the banister as she went, building the corridor of seeing behind them so nothing could come up at their backs. And Felix carried the ledger down into the front parlor, where the fireplace was, where Edith's portrait hung above the mantel watching with her crooked half-smile, where the fire they'd lit at dusk was burning low and blue and stubbornly refusing to touch the book.

The ledger squirmed in his arms. That was the truth of it; Wren saw it plainly as she came down the stairs. The leather cover flexed like a lung. The pages riffled themselves, spitting out names—generations of names, boarders and widows and lost men who'd signed without surnames—all of them fluttering like moths trying to escape a jar.

"Open it to the last page," Wren said. "The blank line. Burn that first. The count lives in the blank line."

Felix looked at her—really looked, the way adults sometimes did when a kid said something that rearranged the furniture of their thinking—and nodded once.

He knelt at the hearth. He opened the ledger against his knee, thumbing past a century of entries, past Edith's careful handwriting renewing the lease year after year, *signed, Edith Thackeray, last resident*, past the crossed-out names that glowed faintly like coals, down to the final page, where a single ruled line waited under the words *the last resident*, empty and hungry.

The house understood, all at once, what he meant to do.

Every window in the parlor blew inward at once—but there was no glass, only cold and snow swirling into the room, and in the snow the voices rose. All of them. The dead callers, the crossed-out names, Mrs. Okafor and the man who'd called himself the night watchman of a factory that burned in 1953 and the woman who'd phoned in every Tuesday for nine years asking Felix to play her husband's song. Their voices came up through the radio in the corner, which switched itself on with no one touching it, volume climbing, climbing, until it wasn't static anymore, wasn't voices anymore, was—

—a choir.

* * *

**Marisol**

It was the most beautiful terrible sound Marisol had ever heard.

Dozens of voices, harmonizing without rehearsal, without breath, singing a song with no words—or words worn down past meaning, syllables that might have been a hymn or a counting rhyme or both. The radio cabinet vibrated. Dust fell from the ceiling in golden threads through the firelight. And in the mirror upstairs, she knew without looking, the tenant had gone rigid, because the choir was singing *against* him, the dead pulling in the opposite direction from the house, and the whole structure of Corliss Street groaned between them like a rope in a tug-of-war.

"Burn it," she said. "Now, Felix. While they're holding him."

Felix fed the open ledger into the fire, last page first.

The flames took it the way fire takes dry grass—all at once, roaring up the chimney, and the light in the room turned white, and the heat should have driven them back but instead it felt like standing in summer, like June through a window, like every warm thing the house had ever pretended to offer finally given honestly. The blank line blackened. The words *the last resident* curled and lifted as ash. Page after page of names caught, and with each name that burned, the choir swelled—one voice, five voices, twenty—and somewhere above them the mirror shattered with a sound like a bell dying, and the humming stopped, and the scraping of the chair stopped, and the house exhaled.

Snow swirled through the broken windows and settled on everything, softening the wreckage, silvering Edith's portrait, catching in Wren's hair as she stood in the middle of the parlor with her camera hanging forgotten at her side, crying without any noise at all.

Marisol put her arms around the girl. Felix knelt in the ashes with his sleeves singed and his hands open in his lap, staring at the fire as though he couldn't believe it had obeyed him.

On the radio, the choir was thinning. Not fading—*leaving*. Voice by voice, the dead were going, the way guests leave a party: reluctantly, gratefully, calling out thanks as they go. Mrs. Okafor's voice came last, clear through the little speaker, warm as Sunday:

"Thank you for listening, dear. All those nights. We heard you. We always heard you."

Then static. Clean, ordinary, empty static.

The clock on the mantel ticked past midnight. Solstice. The longest night, ending.

Felix started to laugh—a cracked, disbelieving sound—and Marisol reached down and pulled him up off his knees and into the hug, all three of them, standing in the ruins of the parlor with snow falling on their shoulders, holding on like the count was still running and they were the numbers keeping each other alive.

Outside, far down Corliss Street, a porch light came on in a house that had been dark for fifty years.

Nobody mentioned it.

Nobody had to.
