## 4. One Second Late

The house made its first honest attempt on my nerves at eleven forty-seven.

I was in the guest room that had been mine until I was seventeen—the one with the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a hand, or a map of somewhere drowned—and I heard the pipes knock twice from inside the wall. Not the arrhythmic clank of old plumbing doing its dying animal impression. Two knocks. Evenly spaced. Patient. The kind of sound something makes when it wants you to know it's there but isn't ready to be seen.

Fifteen years ago I'd have pulled the blanket over my head and invented three rational explanations before my heartbeat finished its second verse. Fifteen years ago I'd been trained for it. Almeidas don't hear things; Almeidas have *old pipes*. Our house didn't groan, it *settled*. The whispering in the hall wasn't whispering, it was *drafts through the sash windows*, and if you believed otherwise you got the look from Grandmother—mild, disappointed, faintly hungry, like a woman watching a child reach for a stove.

But I'm not fifteen years ago anymore. I've lived in cities where the pipes are other people's problems and the only things knocking are landlords. So when the wall knocked twice, I lay there in Mateus's childhood sheets—Iria had put me in this room without asking, and I hadn't had the spine to argue with a grieving woman about bedding—and I said, out loud, to nobody:

"No."

The wall did not answer. That was somehow worse. A pipe would have knocked again, indifferent, mechanical. This felt like a thing that had made its point and was now waiting to see what I'd do about it.

I did what any reasonable adult does at midnight in a haunted inheritance. I got up to brush my teeth.

* * *

The bathroom at the end of the east hall is the one I always used as a kid, because it's the farthest from Grandmother's room and therefore the safest place to cry where the crying couldn't be counted against you. It still has the same mirror—a tall oval in a frame of tarnished silver roses, the glass gone slightly amber at the edges like tea left standing. There's a chip in the lower left corner where nine-year-old Mateus threw a hairbrush at ten-year-old me and missed. He apologized by drawing a mustache on my reflection in soap, which, in hindsight, should have been our first clue about how seriously this family took the boundary between a person and their picture.

Iria's rules, delivered over dinner with the gravity of a woman reading terms and conditions aloud: cover the mirrors at dusk. Never sing past the third verse. Don't repeat what the house says back to it.

I had nodded along the way you nod at customs you intend to observe the way tourists observe local dress—with respect, photographically, and never actually. But I'd also spent the evening cataloguing exits the way I catalogue everything now: front door, swollen, opens inward; kitchen door, rusted latch; the servants' stair behind the pantry that Mateus and I used for smuggling biscuits and, later, cigarettes. Old houses are labyrinths, and I grew up in this one. If it came to running, I knew the maze better than the minotaur did.

Probably.

There were no sheets over the bathroom mirror. Iria had covered the big ones—the hall glass, the vanity in her room, the great mirror in the sealed room at the end of the west wing that everyone pretended was a linen closet—but she'd missed this one, or decided a small oval in a back bathroom wasn't worth the linen. Either way, there it was, holding the room in amber: me, the sink, the window with its curtain of frost, the towel ring, the door.

Me, looking tired. Me, looking like a woman who had buried her twin four days ago and had spent those four days noticing things. Me, whose brother in his casket had worn a chipped front tooth that matched mine exactly, down to the shape of the chip, which happened when we were six and he fell off my shoulders playing centaur and I carried the guilt and he carried the gap until the dentist filled it and I kept mine, because keeping it felt like paying him back.

I squeezed toothpaste onto the brush. Mint. The cheap kind from the village shop, because I'd arrived with nothing but a suitcase of black clothes and a grief I hadn't unpacked yet either.

I brushed my teeth. I looked at myself doing it, because that's what mirrors are for—you watch yourself perform the small maintenance of being alive, spit, rinse, and the reflection performs it with you, perfectly synchronized, the oldest duet in the world, so familiar you stop hearing the music.

I blinked.

The woman in the mirror blinked a moment later.

Now. Let me be precise here, because precision is all I have left, and I have learned that when your mind starts offering you exits—*you were tired, you misjudged the timing, the light flickered*—precision is the only door that doesn't lead back into the house.

I am not talking about a half-second. I'm not talking about the natural smear of a slow blink, or the way fluorescent light stutters. I am telling you that I closed my eyes and opened them, and the reflection's eyes stayed closed after mine were open. Closed long enough for me to see, clearly, in the amber glass, my own face with its eyes shut while I stood there looking at it with mine open. And then they opened. One beat late. Like an echo that had gotten confused about which of us was the voice.

The tap was still running. I turned it off. The silence afterward had texture—it pressed against my eardrums like water pressure, like the whole house leaning in.

"Okay," I said to the mirror, in the bright, brittle voice people use for dogs and dentists. "Okay. You're exhausted. You buried your brother. You're seeing things."

This is called the rationalization reflex, and I want to be fair to it, because it worked beautifully right up until I tested it.

Because here is the thing about being a novelist: I lie professionally. I know every trick a mind uses to make a story feel true, and I know, even better, every trick it uses to make a true story feel deniable. I could write you the tired-grief-hallucination scene in my sleep. I've written it. Readers love it. It lets them close the book feeling clever instead of afraid.

So I did what my protagonist would do. I tested it.

* * *

First test: the mundane one. I raised my right hand.

The reflection raised its right hand—one beat late. A full second, maybe more. Long enough that I had time to think *there*, long enough for the word to form completely in my head before the glass caught up. My hand went up. The room held its breath. Then the woman in the mirror lifted hers, unhurried, like someone following a conductor who kept checking his watch.

I lowered it. She lowered it. Late again.

Second test: asymmetry. I raised my left. She raised her left. Fine. Consistent. Whatever this was, it wasn't sloppy. It was *lagging*, the way a video call lags, the way a recording lags, the way—

The way a copy lags the original.

Third test, and this is the one I want on the record, because it's the one that ended my career as a rational woman: I moved and then stopped mid-motion. I raised my arm and froze it at shoulder height, abruptly, the way you'd halt a horse.

My arm hung there. In the glass, the woman's arm rose past shoulder height—continued rising, smooth and committed, all the way up until her fingers touched the top of the mirror frame—and then stopped, and came back down to match me, and stood there mirroring my frozen pose as if that had been the plan all along.

As if it had finished the movement I'd abandoned.

I stood very still. My heart was doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing. Frost crawled up the window in feathered patterns, and the radiator under the sink ticked, and somewhere below—in the walls, in the floor, in the bones of the house—came that sound I'd been filing under *pipes* since the night I arrived. A low, sliding hum. Almost tonal. Almost melodic.

Almost like someone humming a song they only half remembered, through several feet of plaster.

Every Almeida knows the hymn. Four lines, repeated, a counting song—Iria sang it to Sol at bedtime, Sol hummed it on the stairs, skipping verses she said "the house doesn't like," which is a sentence I have been trying to return to sender ever since. The tune lives in us the way our eye color does. You don't learn it. You discover you already know it.

And the house, tonight, at midnight, was humming it back through its plumbing. Off-key. Slower than the way we sing it. The way you'd sing something if you were learning it by ear from the next room.

From the next *side* of the wall.

I gripped the edge of the sink. The porcelain was cold enough to hurt, which I noted with gratitude—pain is excellent evidence, pain insists on the real world—and I watched the mirror, and the mirror watched me, one second behind, my own face wearing an expression I recognized but wasn't making yet. Fear arrives on my face in stages: first the eyes go wide, then the mouth goes small, then the whole arrangement settles into something terribly calm. The woman in the glass was one stage ahead of me. She'd already reached calm. She was waiting for me to catch up.

"Pipes," I said.

She mouthed it with me. Late. Her lips moving around the word like she was tasting it.

I thought about Mateus. Not the body in the casket with my tooth—that thought was a locked room I wasn't ready to open—but Mateus alive, age eleven, standing in front of this same mirror making faces, and me behind him making worse ones, and Grandmother appearing in the doorway with that look, that mild, hungry look, saying: *Don't play with your reflections, children. They remember.*

We'd laughed. We'd laughed because she was Grandmother and Grandmother said things like that the way other grandmothers said *don't run with scissors*, and because we were children and children laugh at death when it wears a costume.

They remember.

I turned off the light.

Here is what I know about fear, having harvested it professionally for twenty years: the worst of it isn't the monster. The worst of it is the moment the explanation dies. For fifteen years I had carried a working theory of my childhood—that the house was old, that the family was strange, that the noises were pipes and the rules were superstition and the screaming I heard the night I left was wind—and that theory had been load-bearing. Take it out and everything I'd built on top of it, every choice, every year of distance, every polite non-answer I'd given when friends asked why I never went home—all of it sagged.

The pipes theory died in that bathroom at twelve-oh-something a.m., killed by a raised hand and a second's delay, and I stood in the dark and let it die, because I am done being a woman who hears screaming and calls it weather.

In the dark, the mirror held a faint gray ghost of the room. I could still see her. She could still see me. We regarded each other across the second between us, that narrow strait of time, and I understood—with the calm that had finally caught up to my face—that the lag was not a malfunction. A malfunction gets worse randomly, degrades, dissolves into static. This was clean. Disciplined. Improving.

It was practice. Something over there was rehearsing being me, and getting better at it, and the second between us was not a defect in the mirror.

It was a margin. A safety distance. The gap a thing keeps while it studies you.

The hum in the walls rose, almost imperceptibly, and resolved—for one clear, awful moment—into words. Or the shape of words. A counting rhythm underneath them, patient as dripping water:

*One for the staying, two for the gone—*

I did not sing back. I did not finish it. I walked out of the bathroom with my spine held together by spite and closed the door behind me, and in the dark hallway the covered mirror at the far end bulged once against its sheet, gently, the way a chest rises before speech.

Back in my room, I did not sleep. I sat on the bed Mateus had slept in as a boy and I made a list, because lists are how I pray. One: the noises are not pipes. Two: the reflection is late, and lateness can be measured, and anything that can be measured can be tracked. Three: tomorrow I would start measuring.

And four—the item my pen hovered over, the one I wrote small, as if smallness could contain it:

*Iria covers the mirrors at dusk. Which means Iria has seen what happens when she doesn't.*

Down the hall, faint through two closed doors, Sol's voice drifted from her room, half-asleep, practicing her numbers in a singsong. And beneath her, beneath the floor, beneath the foundations, so low I might have invented it, something counted along.

* * *

Morning came gray and thin. At breakfast Iria watched me over her coffee with eyes that had done arithmetic on my face overnight, and before I could decide whether to tell her, she set down her cup and said, in the tone of a woman confirming a diagnosis she'd dreaded:

"You covered the bathroom mirror last night, didn't you."

I hadn't. I'd forgotten entirely.

"No," I said.

Iria closed her eyes briefly, the way people do at funerals. Then she stood, went to the hall closet, and took down the stack of folded bedsheets she'd apparently begun keeping by the door.

"The photographs came from the studio this morning," she said, not turning around. "They're on the table in the parlor. From the funeral." A pause, long enough to snow in. "You should look at them, Marguerite. Before Sol finds them."

Her hands, I noticed, were shaking too badly to fold the sheet she was holding.

"And bring the good scissors," she added quietly. "Some of these need cutting."
