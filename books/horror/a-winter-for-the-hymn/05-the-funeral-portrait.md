## 5. The Funeral Portrait

The photographs arrived three days after the funeral, in a padded envelope that smelled faintly of the photographer's cigarettes and, beneath that, of something sweeter I chose not to name. I recognized the return address from the card tucked into the funeral flowers: *Estúdio Vilar, Retratos Finais — Memórias que Duram.* Memories that last. There's a slogan that assumes a great deal about what a person wants to keep.

I took the envelope to the kitchen table because the kitchen had the best light and the fewest mirrors. The house had begun to feel like a museum where every exhibit was watching me back, and I'd developed the habit of positioning myself in rooms the way you'd position yourself in a chess endgame — always aware of which surfaces could see you. The kitchen had one window over the sink and no glass but that. I could live with one window. The window, at least, had the decency to show me the yard instead of myself.

Sol was at school. Iria was at the parish office, arguing with Father Bastos about the cost of the plot, an argument she would win because Iria had never once lost an argument in her life, including, I suspected, the ones she'd had with the dead. That left me alone with an envelope of photographs of my brother's funeral, which was either the worst possible way to spend a Tuesday morning or the only honest one.

I made coffee first. Ritual before ruin — that's a family motto too, though we never stitched it on anything.

* * *

The first photos were what you'd expect. The church, gray stone wearing its snow like a borrowed coat. The hearse. The procession of black coats, our family's traditional costume, worn so often down the generations that I sometimes think the coats outlive us and simply get passed along. Iria at the front, spine like a fence post, daring God to say something. Sol in a coat too big for her, holding Iria's hand and looking at the casket with the frank, unblinking interest of a child who has not yet been taught which questions are forbidden. Me, in the background of half the shots, looking like a man who had wandered into someone else's funeral and was too polite to leave.

Fifteen years away, and the camera still knew exactly where to put me: at the edge of the frame, half-turned, already leaving.

I flipped through them with the detached efficiency of someone sorting mail. Condolence card. Flowers. The priest's hands. The priest's hands again, from a different angle, because apparently the photographer had strong feelings about the priest's hands.

Then the open-casket shot.

I want to be careful here. I want to describe it the way I saw it, which took several passes, because the eye is a coward and does its lying in real time. The first time I looked, I saw what I expected: Mateus in the box, waxen and still, dressed in the good suit Iria had chosen, hands folded over the stem of a lily. The second time, I noticed the light was wrong. The third time, I stopped noticing anything except the thing itself, and my coffee went cold in its cup while I sat there like a man nailed to his chair.

The casket was empty.

Not subtly empty. Not *the light is bad and the face is shadowed* empty. It was empty the way a drawer is empty when someone has taken the silver out — the suit was there, yes, arranged and filled out with whatever morticians use to suggest a person, the lily laid across the folded hands. But where the face should have been there was only the satin pillow, dented, and the suit's collar stood up around nothing, and the whole arrangement had the unmistakable quality of a stage set viewed from the wings. A costume waiting for its actor. The photographer had shot it straight on, at the angle mourners would have seen, and I kept trying to reconstruct what I had actually seen at the funeral, three days ago, standing in that exact spot. Had I looked? I had looked. I remembered looking. I remembered the face — Mateus's face, older than I remembered him, heavier, with the gray coming in at the temples, but his. I remembered the chipped tooth.

I remembered the chipped tooth.

I set the photograph down on the table with the exaggerated care of a person handling something venomous, and I made myself breathe, and then I picked it up again because that's the deal you make with horror: you don't get to look away and also look. You have to choose. And some stubborn, self-destructive part of me — the part that had come back to this house at all — chose look.

Behind the casket, in the photograph, stood two shadows.

I don't mean two people standing in front of a wall, casting shadows. I mean two shadows standing behind the casket, on the floor, where the funeral home's carpet was pale blue and would have shown any shadow cast upon it as a soft gray smudge. These were not soft. They were black the way ink is black, black the way the inside of a closed mouth is black, and they had edges — crisp, deliberate, the edges of things that had been *placed*. They were roughly human-shaped, if a human were made entirely of the decision to be tall and thin and wrong. One stood directly behind the head of the casket. The other stood slightly to the left, angled toward the first, and if you let your eye do the thing eyes do — the pattern-finding, the face-making — the angle looked like leaning. Like two figures conferring over the contents of the box.

Over the *empty* contents of the box.

I got up and walked to the window and looked at the yard for a while. The snow was doing its patient, burying work. Somewhere a crow said something rude. I ran through the ordinary explanations the way you run through a list of emergency numbers. Double exposure — but the rest of the frame was clean, sharp, no ghosting anywhere. A trick of the flash — but the flash would have softened shadows, not manufactured two of them with better edges than anything else in the room. Photoshop — from Estúdio Vilar, whose website I had glimpsed and which appeared to have been designed in 2004 and not touched since. And even if someone had faked it, the question that matters isn't *how was the image made.* The question is *what did the camera see that I didn't.*

Cameras are honest in a way people aren't. They don't flinch. They don't protect themselves with disbelief. A camera at a funeral sees whatever is there, and if what was there was an empty box and two standing shadows, then what I had seen — the face, the gray at the temples, the chipped tooth — was the thing that needed explaining.

The chipped tooth.

I'd told myself it was coincidence, back in chapter one of this disaster, when I still believed in coincidence the way you believe in a childhood friend who's moved away: fondly, and without evidence. Mateus and I were twins. Twins share teeth the way they share birthdays. Baby Marguerite fell off the porch step; baby Mateus fell off the porch step; two chipped front teeth, one family, no mystery. That was the story. That was the story I'd wrapped around the wrongness like a blanket over a birdcage, and I had slept, more or less.

But the casket in the photograph was empty, and an empty box does not have teeth.

* * *

I did what any reasonable person does with an impossible photograph. I compared it to the others.

There were four shots of the open casket in the envelope. The photographer had worked the angles — left, right, straight on, and one from above and behind, looking down past the shoulders of the mourners into the box, the kind of shot that always feels faintly obscene, like reading someone's mail over their shoulder. In the left-angle shot, the box was full. Mateus lay there, complete, his face composed into the particular dignity that embalming lends the dead, which is the dignity of a man who has been argued with until he agreed. In the right-angle shot, full again. In the overhead shot, full.

Only the straight-on shot was empty. Only the straight-on shot had the shadows.

I laid all four out on the table in sequence and stared at them until the coffee pot clicked itself off in the next room like a small animal giving up. Here is what I noticed, and I noticed it slowly, the way you notice you've been walking in the wrong direction — not all at once, but with a sickening accumulation of small wrongnesses:

In the empty shot, the shadows were sharp. In the other three, there were no shadows at all behind the casket. Not faint ones. Not ambiguous ones. The carpet behind the box was blank blue in every other frame, as if the shadows had been removed from those photographs — or as if they had only stood there for the one exposure, only long enough for one click of the shutter, and then stepped back out of the light.

And the mourners. In the full-casket shots, the mourners at the front — Iria, Sol, the aunts and cousins whose names I mostly still knew — stood in their positions, heads bowed or faces set. In the empty shot, the same mourners stood in the same positions. But Sol's head was turned. In every other photograph she looked down at the casket or straight ahead with that unnerving child's directness. In the empty shot, she was looking back over her shoulder, up and to the left, toward the space behind the casket where the shadows stood. Her mouth was slightly open. Nine years old, at her uncle's funeral, and something behind the box had made her turn around and look.

I had been standing near her. I tried to remember — had she turned? Had she tugged someone's sleeve? I remembered the cold, which everyone remarked on afterward, the church so cold that Father Bastos's breath showed during the eulogy and he joked weakly about the heating bill and nobody laughed. I remembered Iria's hand on my arm, sudden and hard, at some point during the service, though I couldn't have said when. I remembered thinking the organ had sounded wrong, one note flat, one note held a half-second too long, like a singer losing the count.

Like a singer losing the count.

I put that thought down carefully and backed away from it, the way you back away from a dog you don't know. There would be time for the hymn later. There was, I was beginning to understand, always time for the hymn later; it kept, the way grief keeps.

* * *

Iria came home at one o'clock, victorious and cold, stamping snow off her boots in the hall with the rhythm of a woman who has won an argument about plot prices and considers the day well spent. I had the photographs face-down on the table by then, all except the one, which I had put in the drawer with the good scissors and the dead batteries, because some part of me apparently believed drawers could keep secrets in this house, which tells you how far gone I already was.

"You're sitting in the dark," she said, coming into the kitchen and turning on the light I hadn't needed and hadn't wanted. "And the coffee's cold. Have you eaten?"

"Iria." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "At the funeral. Did anything seem — off? To you?"

She hung her coat on the hook. She took down bread and a knife. She did all the small domestic things that are also, in this house, a language, and the sentence they spelled was *not yet, not here, not that question.*

"Off," she repeated.

"The cold. The organ. Anything."

"The organ is older than the church," she said. "It's always flat. And the cold—" the knife went through the bread, even strokes, "—it's January, Marguerite. We buried your brother in January. What did you expect, geraniums?"

"Did you look at him?" I asked. "In the casket. Did you look at his face?"

The knife stopped. Just for a moment — a half-second, a held note — and then it resumed, and if I hadn't been watching for it I'd have missed it. But I had been watching for it. I had spent thirty-nine years learning to read this woman's silences the way sailors read water, and that pause had a shape, and the shape was *guilt.*

"I looked at him," she said. "He looked like himself. He looked like he was sleeping." She set a plate of bread in front of me, unasked, unrefused. "You've been in this house three days and you're already hearing pipes and seeing ghosts. It's grief. It does this. It makes the ordinary strange. Eat."

"He looked like himself," I said. "Describe him to me."

She looked at me then, and I looked back, and for a moment we were two Almeidas doing the thing Almeidas do across kitchen tables, which is conducting an entire war in the space between one sentence and the next. Her eyes went to the drawer. Just briefly. Just a flick. But I saw it, and she knew I saw it, and neither of us said anything, because that is also the family language: the fluent, native tongue of *I know that you know.*

"Dark hair going gray," she said finally. "Your mother's jaw. The suit fit him well, in the shoulders. He'd have wanted the gray tie, not the black one, but the undertaker only had the black." A small, real grief moved through her face as she said it, and I understood that whatever Iria knew or suspected, she had also lost him — lost him twice, maybe, once to the house and once to the box — and that her cruelty, where it existed, was load-bearing.

"The tooth," I said. "Did you see the tooth?"

Something crossed her face then that I had no translation for. Not guilt this time. Something older and more tired. She picked up her coffee, and her hand was steady, because Iria's hands are always steady, that's practically her religion.

"Eat your bread, Marguerite," she said. "And when you're done, go rest. You look like you've seen a ghost."

It was, I'm nearly certain, a joke. It was the first joke I had ever heard her make about the family's condition, and it landed in the kitchen like a dropped glass, and neither of us bent to pick it up.

* * *

I waited until she went up for her nap — Iria naps at three with the regularity of a train schedule, and trains, whatever else you say about them, can be counted on — and then I took the photograph out of the drawer and sat with it in the best light the kitchen offered.

Two shadows. One empty box. One child looking back over her shoulder at something no adult had turned to see.

And then, because I am my mother's child and my mother's child was thorough in the ways that get people hurt, I did the thing I should have done first. I took out my phone and I photographed the photograph.

The screen filled with the image: the church interior, the casket, the mourners, the two standing shadows with their crisp, deliberate edges. I zoomed in on the shadows. I zoomed until the image broke into gray blocks and the shadows dissolved into noise, and in the noise, in the last fraction of detail before the picture gave up being a picture, the left shadow — the one that leaned — had something at its center. A lighter patch. Not a face. Not quite. More like the memory of a face, the place where a face would go, the way a dent in a pillow is the memory of a head.

And I knew the shape of that dent. I had spent three days telling myself I'd imagined it, that grief was playing tricks, that the chipped tooth was a twin thing, a coincidence, a family of shared flaws. But I knew the shape the way you know a voice on the phone after twenty years. It was Mateus's face. Or it was the space where Mateus's face had been, standing behind the box that was supposed to hold him, leaning toward the other shadow, and the other shadow was angled just enough, just so, that if you let your eye do the thing eyes do —

— it looked like they were talking. Like one was telling the other something. Like one was *counting* something, and the other was listening, patient, with all the time in the world, because it had been standing in churches behind caskets for longer than the church had been standing.

My hands were shaking. I noticed it the way you notice weather, from a distance. I set the phone down on the table, and the screen dimmed, and in the dark glass of the dead screen I caught my own reflection — pale, hollowed, lit by the gray window — and for one irrational second I checked it. Checked that it moved when I moved. That it blinked when I blinked. That it was, in the small ways that matter at midnight and at three o'clock and in kitchens at four in the afternoon, keeping up.

It was. For now, it was.

But the shadows in the photograph had been standing behind an empty box, and the box was supposed to hold my brother, and the tooth I had seen in the corpse's mouth — my tooth, our tooth, the chip from the porch step — was a detail no embalmer puts there, a detail that comes from somewhere, from someone, from a face that needed to look right to the one person in the church who would recognize it.

The house was quiet around me. The snow ticked against the window. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled with a sound like a syllable.

I put the photograph back in the drawer, and I stood in the kitchen of the house I'd been born in, and I admitted it to myself at last, in the plain words I'd been refusing for three days:

Mateus was not in that box.

And whatever was standing behind it — whatever had worn his face for the mourners, chipped tooth and all, long enough for one photograph and one grieving sister to see — had been standing *in* the church, among us, close enough to touch.

The question was no longer how my brother died.

The question was what the house had done with him — and whether, somewhere in its cold arithmetic, my brother was still countable.

I went to the hall closet where Iria kept the family papers, because if there was an answer in this house, it would be written down somewhere, in a hand that trembled, in a book that nobody was supposed to find.

I was right. God help me, I was right.
