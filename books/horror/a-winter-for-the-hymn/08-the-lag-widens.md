## 8. The Lag Widens

The second night, the mirror was two seconds late.

I know because I counted. Counting is what you do when you can't sleep and you're too much of a coward to turn on the light. I stood in the bathroom at one in the morning with my hand raised like a schoolchild asking a question no teacher wanted to answer, and I watched my reflection hold the pose for two full seconds after I'd dropped mine. Two seconds doesn't sound like much. Two seconds is the space between lightning and thunder that tells you the storm is still far away. This storm was not far away. The storm was in the glass, wearing my face, and it was learning to move on its own schedule.

I told myself: pipes. Old houses settle. Old houses creak and groan and occasionally their plumbing develops opinions. Fifteen years away from the Almeida house had not cured me of the family talent for explaining away the inexplicable, which is apparently heritable, like the chipped tooth and the bad knees and the tendency to be buried in the wrong casket.

I went back to bed. I did not sleep. Around three, I heard the humming again—downstairs, or inside the walls, or inside my own skull, hard to say—and this time it got as far as the third verse before stopping so abruptly that the silence afterward felt like a held breath.

*The house doesn't like past the third verse,* Sol had said, on the stairs, in her small matter-of-fact voice, the way other children report that the dog doesn't like thunder.

I lay in the dark of my childhood bedroom and thought about how, when I was nine, I had said almost exactly the same thing to my father, and he had looked at me with an expression I couldn't read then and can read all too well now, an expression that said *good girl, you're learning the rules* — and underneath that, faintly, like a watermark: *God help us, she's learning the rules.*

* * *

Morning arrived gray and apologetic, the way winter mornings do here, seeping through the curtains rather than breaking over them. I came downstairs to find Aunt Iria on a stepladder in the front hall, rehanging sheets over the great mirror.

Nobody had asked her to. Nobody had uncovered them—I'd checked yesterday, twice, once out of caution and once out of the specific morbid curiosity that runs in our blood alongside everything else. The sheets had been up since dusk, corners tucked, exactly as the rules required. And yet there was Iria at seven in the morning, sixty-eight years old, balancing on a ladder with a fistful of thumbtacks, covering mirrors that were already covered.

"Aunt Iria," I said.

She didn't startle. That frightened me more than anything else about the tableau. A woman her age, on a ladder, should startle when spoken to. She just kept pressing tacks into the frame with her thumb, one after another, precise as a seamstress.

"The sheets slipped," she said.

"They didn't slip. I checked them last night."

"Then they were going to." She stepped down off the ladder, brushed plaster dust from her cardigan, and surveyed her work with the satisfaction of a woman who has done a chore, not a ritual. "You want coffee? There's coffee."

There was always coffee. In this house, coffee is the answer to every question except the ones that matter, and those get answered with rules.

"Iria." I kept my voice level. Fifteen years of city living had taught me to negotiate; fifteen years of forgetting had let me forget that negotiation doesn't work on Almeidas, who treat truth the way other families treat the good china—brought out only on occasions, handled carefully, never used for anything practical. "Why are you covering mirrors that were already covered?"

She poured two cups. Set one in front of me. Sat down across from me at the kitchen table, which is oak, older than both of us, scarred with initials from generations of children who grew up to leave or grow up to stay, depending on the coin-flip nobody talks about.

"When your grandmother died," she said, "the sheets were up. Every one. Up at dusk, down at dawn, forty years of habit, hers longer than mine. The morning after the funeral I came down and every sheet in this house was folded on the floor. Neatly. Corners squared." She sipped her coffee. "Nobody folded them. Nobody was in the house but me."

"What did you do?"

"What I'm doing now." She looked at me over the rim of her cup, and her eyes—the same gray as mine, the same gray as Mateus's, the whole family sharing one pair of eyes passed around like a casserole—were tired in a way that sleep doesn't touch. "I put them back up. And then I put them up again the next day, even when they hadn't moved. Because the house needed to see me doing it. You understand? It's not the sheets that matter. It's the showing of the work. Like a child putting away toys so the parent sees it's done properly."

"And if you don't show the work?"

She set the cup down. For a moment I thought she would actually answer, actually open the ledger of family knowledge and read me a line or two. Her mouth opened. Closed. She stood, collected her cup, and began washing dishes that were already clean.

"Eat your breakfast," she said. "Funeral's Saturday. There's arrangements."

* * *

I spent the middle of the day doing what I do instead of coping, which is making lists. I have published four novels; people assume writers are brave because we put things on paper. We are not brave. We are archivists of our own terror, and I filled three pages of a notebook with observations, timestamps, and increasingly unhinged marginalia.

Because the lag wasn't holding steady. That was the thing. That was the thing I couldn't stop circling.

At ten a.m., brushing my teeth, the reflection tracked me perfectly. Not a flicker of delay. Just me, foaming at the mouth like any other grieving sibling, ordinary as tap water.

At noon, passing the hall mirror under its sheet, I lifted a corner—a violation, yes, I know, add it to the list of rules broken since I came home, right beneath *never sing past the third verse* and above *don't ask Iria direct questions*—and caught my own face looking back maybe half a beat behind. I dropped the sheet. Stood there with my heart doing something arrhythmic and unscheduled.

At two p.m., in the bathroom again, deliberately, notebook in hand: one full second of lag. I raised my right hand; the glass raised its right hand one second later. I lowered it; the lowering arrived late, like mail from a distant country.

And at six, at dusk, with the light dying blue in the windows and the house beginning its evening sounds—the tick of cooling radiators, the groan of joists, the faint, patient hum somewhere below the range of honest hearing—I tested it one last time and got three seconds.

Three seconds. In twenty-four hours it had gone from nothing to nothing to half a beat to one to three. Whatever was in the glass wasn't just waking up. It was accelerating. Learning. Practicing, the way you practice anything—badly at first, then less badly, then well enough that you stop noticing the effort.

Except I noticed. God help me, I noticed, because of what happened at six-oh-four.

I was testing it. Hand up, hand down, the little scientist of my own haunting. On the fourth repetition I got bored—or scared, boredom being fear's respectable cousin—and I started to raise my hand a fifth time and then aborted halfway, brought it back down, turned away from the mirror to write down the time.

In the corner of my eye, in the silver, my reflection finished the gesture.

It raised the hand the rest of the way. Held it there. Then—it took me a moment to understand what I was seeing, because the mind files impossible things under *later*—it turned its head. Slowly. Following me as I walked away from the mirror, though I had already stopped looking at it, though by every law of optics and decency it should have been doing whatever I was doing, which was walking away.

It watched me leave the bathroom. My own face, wearing an expression I hadn't made. Something between patience and appetite.

I stood in the hallway with my back against the wall and my notebook shaking in my hands and I wrote, in handwriting that looked like it belonged to a much younger person, someone nine years old hiding under blankets: *it doesn't need me anymore. it's practicing being alone.*

* * *

Iria rehung every sheet in the house that evening. All of them, top to bottom, including the little round mirror in the upstairs hallway that hasn't reflected anything but ceiling since 1994, including the vanity tray mirror in the guest bath that's barely bigger than a saucer. She worked through dusk without a lamp, moving room to room in the failing light, tacking and tucking, and I followed her like a shadow with questions.

"You felt it," I said. "That's why. You felt something change today."

She smoothed a sheet over the guest bath mirror. "I felt the house working," she said. "Like feeling a car engine running rough. You don't need to be a mechanic. You just know."

"Working at what?"

"Marguerite." She said my name the way you'd set down something heavy—carefully, with bent knees. "Your brother is not yet in the ground. Let him get there. Then we'll talk about whatever you think you saw."

"Whatever I *think*—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin. "Aunt Iria, I am the last person in this house who thinks things. Thinking is the one luxury this family has never afforded itself. I *saw*—"

"*Don't.*" It came out sharp, and then she mastered herself, the way she always does, folding the sharpness away into some drawer deep in her chest where I suspect she keeps everything—the folded sheets, the folded griefs, the folded years. She put her hand flat against the covered mirror, gently, the way you'd quiet a dog. "Not in front of the glass," she said, quieter. "Never argue in front of the glass. They remember arguments. They use them later."

"*They.*"

She left the bathroom. I stood there a while, listening to the radiator tick, watching the sheet-covered mirror bulge very slightly, very briefly, as if something behind it had exhaled.

*They*, she'd said. Plural. As if the reflections weren't copies at all. As if they were residents.

* * *

Sol found me at nine, sitting on my bed with the notebook, pretending to read it. She came in without knocking, which is a child's privilege and also, in this house, possibly a survival strategy—doors here stick, and I've begun to wonder whether that's age or policy.

"Aunt Marguerite." She climbed onto the bed and sat cross-legged, studying me with Mateus's exact evaluating squint. "Are you okay? You look like the time Tio Mateus saw the fox in the chicken coop."

"I'm fine, pequena."

"You're writing in your sad book."

"It's not a sad book. It's a... notes book."

"For your stories?"

"Something like that."

She nodded gravely, accepting this, and then leaned close, and lowered her voice, and said the thing that took the floor out from under my evening:

"The man in the wall says you've been counting."

The cold went through me like a draft with intent. "What man in the wall, Sol?"

"The singing one." She said it the way she'd say *the tall one* or *the nice one*—a descriptor, not an alarm. "He sings harmony when I practice. He gets the verses wrong sometimes. He does the count really fast, like—" and here she demonstrated, a rapid breathless mutter of numbers that made my skin crawl precisely because it sounded like something I'd heard through the walls at three a.m., sped up, played back. "Tio Mateus used to sing slow. This one sings fast. Like he's excited."

"Sol." I took her hands. They were warm. Thank God, they were warm. "Has this—man—ever talked to you? Said words?"

"He mostly sings." She considered. "Yesterday he said my name. But everybody knows my name."

"Did he say anything else? Anything about you, or about me, or about the house?"

She frowned, working at it, and for one merciful moment I thought she'd lost it, the way children lose things, the whole event sliding off them like water. Then she said:

"He said, 'Tell her the lag is a door opening slowly.'" She shrugged. "I don't know what lag means."

I don't remember what I told her. Something soothing, something with hot chocolate in it, some parental incantation to move her along and out and into bed. I remember walking her down the hall, past the sheeted mirrors, keeping her on my blind side away from every one of them. I remember tucking her in, and her falling asleep mid-sentence, mid-thought, the way children surrender to sleep like tiny drunks, without ceremony.

And I remember standing in her doorway afterward, listening.

Because from inside the wall of her bedroom—faint, patient, muffled, like a voice through snow—someone was humming the hymn. Slowly now. Savoring it. Getting every note right.

And then, clear as a bell through the plaster, a voice that was almost Mateus's and almost mine and almost no one's said, softly, conversationally, the way you'd remark on the weather to a neighbor across a fence:

*"Two days."*

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the kitchen until dawn with every light burning and the notebook open in front of me, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding, because numbers make things real and reality has been the enemy lately.

Two seconds yesterday. Three today. If it keeps widening—if it's a door opening slowly, and doors open at their own pace, and this house has never once hurried for anyone—then the question isn't *what is it*. I'd stopped asking that around the time my reflection waved goodbye on its own schedule.

The question is *what happens when the lag reaches zero.*

When the reflection stops being late. When it catches up. When my face in the glass moves exactly when I move—which will look, to anyone watching, like perfect harmony, like health, like nothing wrong at all.

Or when it stops waiting for me entirely, and simply goes first.

Outside, the sky was turning the color of old dishwater, and the first snow of the season was starting to come down, fat and silent, filling the yard, erasing the driveway, smoothing the world over the way snow does—kindly, thoroughly, like a sheet laid over a mirror.

Somewhere upstairs, a clock I've never been able to find chimed once.

And in the dark window over the sink—uncovered, uncurtained, because Iria only ever covers mirrors, and I am only now beginning to understand that the distinction matters—in the black glass of the kitchen window, my reflection raised its coffee cup to me.

I hadn't picked mine up yet.

I decided, with the calm of the truly exhausted, not to find out what happens if I drink first.
