## 1. The Wrong Sibling

The ferry to Ashgrove Hollow ran once a week in winter and never after dark, except tonight, when the pilot took my money and my silence and pushed off from the mainland pier at quarter past eleven with the ice already groaning along his hull.

I stood at the stern while the town lights shrank behind us. In my left hand I carried a canvas satchel holding two changes of clothes, a razor, and a tin urn that weighed almost nothing and more than I could bear. In my right hand, a leather portfolio stuffed with forty-one unfinished letters, all addressed to me, all written by my sister, none of them finished.

Ysolde had been dead eleven days. The coroner on the mainland used the word *drowned*, said it twice as if once hadn't taken, and then looked at me the way people look at twins—like I was a mirror she'd accidentally walked into and couldn't get out of.

"You're the brother," he'd said.

"I'm the other one," I'd said, because that was true, and it was the only sentence about us I'd ever found completely honest.

The lake was black glass with white scars of ice where the current worked underneath. The ferry's single lamp threw our shadows forward onto the water, so that we seemed to be traveling toward ourselves. I counted the seconds between the engine's pulses because counting was what I did instead of crying. I had been counting things since I was four years old. Doors, stairs, crows on a wire, the number of times Ysolde could hold her breath under the surface of the swimming hole before I panicked and pulled her up. She always got to nine. She always laughed at ten.

The Hollow came out of the dark the way a thought comes out of sleep—not gradually, but all at once, as if it had been there the whole time and had simply decided to let me see it.

A stone jetty. A boathouse with its doors hanging open like an unbuttoned coat. And above both, filling the whole shoulder of the hill, Ashgrove Hollow itself: three stories of grey gables and black windows, too large for any family that had ever lived in it, shaped like something that had grown rather than been built. It had no lights on. Of course it had no lights on. The power company had cut the estate off in 1987, according to the solicitor's letter, and no one had paid a bill since.

Except that even from the water, even at this distance, I could see that one window on the third floor was glowing a soft, steady amber.

I told myself it was the moon. The moon was not out.

* * *

The pilot didn't offer to help with the urn. He kept the engine idling and watched me step onto the jetty with the expression of a man who has delivered things to this shore before and has learned not to ask what they are.

"Roads'll close by Thursday," he said. "Snow coming down from the pass. After that you're here till thaw."

"I know."

"Nobody stays out here in winter." He said it like a fact he was reading off a manifest. "Not since—" He stopped himself, which was its own kind of information. "You want me to wait?"

"No."

He looked relieved in a way that made my skin prickle, and then the ferry backed away from the stones and the sound of it faded and there was nothing left in the world but the wind, the ice talking to itself out on the lake, and me, standing in front of a house I had sworn at seventeen I would never enter again.

I went in anyway. Grief is not a decision; it's a tide, and tides don't consult you.

* * *

The front door was unlocked. Not merely unlocked—unlatched, pulled open six inches by some earlier hand, exhaling warmth into the cold.

Warmth. That was the first wrong thing, though I didn't have the vocabulary for it yet. The house should have been a tomb at forty below. Instead the hall breathed out at me like something sleeping, smelling of woodsmoke and beeswax and, beneath both, faintly, of lake water.

I stepped inside and did what I always do. What I had done since I was small enough that Ysolde had to carry me up these stairs herself.

I counted the doors.

It started as a game between us, before I understood it was anything else. There were twenty-two doors in Ashgrove Hollow when we were children—or rather, there were twenty-one doors and one door-shaped space behind the cellar stairs that opened onto brick, and Ysolde had insisted we count it anyway, because, she said, a house should add up. Twenty-two. We had walked every corridor together one August afternoon, ages nine and nine, chalk in hand, marking each lintel until our father caught us and washed the marks off and looked at us for a long time afterward in a way I only now, standing in the hall with my sister's ashes against my hip, understood as fear.

Twenty-two doors. That was the number. That was the sum the house was supposed to come to. A house should add up.

I hung my coat on the peg by the door—the same peg, still there, still scarred where I'd thrown a hatchet at it when I was twelve and missed—and I began to walk.

Front hall: four doors. Study, drawing room, dining room, and the servants' stair. Four. Correct.

Kitchen passage: three. Pantry, scullery, cellar. Three. Correct.

Main staircase, seventeen steps—I counted them without meaning to, my hand trailing the banister where the varnish had worn pale in two parallel lines exactly the width of a child's hands, ours, hers and mine, worn there by years of sliding down—and at the top, the long gallery corridor: seven doors. Her room. My room. Our parents' room. The linen closet. The blue guest room. The sewing room. The attic stair.

Seven. Correct.

East wing: five doors, all shut, all stiff with disuse. Five. Correct.

That made nineteen. The remaining three were downstairs again—the conservatory, the garden door, the coal chute cover that Ysolde had always counted out of spite because it wasn't really a door and she liked arguing with the total. Twenty-two. The house added up. The house had always added up, and the adding-up had been the one fixed point of a childhood that otherwise tilted, in ways I couldn't name, toward the water.

I stood at the bottom of the main stairs with the urn in my arms and did the arithmetic again, slower, the way you reread a letter that has said something unbearable in passing.

Nineteen upstairs. Six downstairs, if you counted the coal chute, which Ysolde would have, so I did.

Twenty-five.

I counted a third time. Room by room, handle by handle, pressing my palm flat against each panel of painted pine the way you check whether a sleeper is still breathing. Nineteen. Six. Twenty-five.

There were three doors in Ashgrove Hollow that had not existed when I was a child.

No—that wasn't right either, and the not-rightness of it sat in my stomach like swallowed ice. Because the doors weren't new. I put my hand on the first of them, in the east wing, at the end of the corridor where the wallpaper went dark with damp: oak, iron-banded, the hinges bloomed with rust, the paint on the frame cracked in the pattern of decades. This door had been hanging here for fifty years at least. Maybe a hundred. It had simply never been part of my count, and my count had never been wrong, and those two facts could not live in the same house.

Unless the house had been keeping some of its doors from me.

I heard myself laugh—one short syllable, absurd in the empty corridor—and the laugh came back to me off the walls a half-second late, as if the house had to think about whether to return it.

* * *

I found Mrs. Vell in the kitchen, and I use the word *found* deliberately, because I had not heard her arrive, and the kitchen door had been one of the correct ones, and yet there she was at the scrubbed table with a lamp lit beside her, mending a shirt cuff that I recognized, with a lurch, as belonging to my grandfather, who had been dead for thirty years.

She was older than memory. That is the only way I can describe her. When we were children she had been old; she was old now in exactly the same way, as if *old* were her name and age had nothing to do with it. Grey hair pinned in a coil. Hands like tree roots. Eyes the pale, patient blue of winter sky over a frozen field.

"Master Yara," she said, without surprise, without looking up from the cuff. "Your room's aired. I put fresh sheets on your sister's bed too, in case you'd rather."

"In case I'd rather," I repeated.

"Grief does strange things to the living." She bit her thread. "Some can't sleep in a dead twin's bed. Some can't sleep anywhere else."

"The power's been cut since the eighties," I said. "Who's paying for the lamps?"

For the first time she looked up. Her face did something I want to call kindness and have never been able to call anything else since, though God knows I tried, later, when kindness would have been a mercy to believe in.

"This house pays its own bills," she said. "It always has. You knew that when you were small. Children always know. It's the growing up that makes them forget."

"There's a light on in the third-floor window."

"Yes." She folded the shirt with terrible gentleness, as if it were a body. "There generally is."

I wanted to ask her whose room that was. I wanted to ask her about the doors. I wanted to set the urn down on her clean table and say, *Mrs. Vell, my sister drowned in a lake ninety miles from here with no water in her lungs, and the coroner crossed out his own word and wrote another one, and I need someone in this house to tell me the truth before I open that tin.*

What I said was: "Is there anything to eat?"

Because grief also does that. It makes you small. It makes you ask for soup when you mean to ask for salvation.

"There's stew on the stove," she said. "There's been stew on the stove since Tuesday. It doesn't go off." She rose, joints cracking like ice on a pond, and paused at the kitchen door with her hand on the frame. "Don't count the doors tonight, Master Yara."

My blood went very quiet.

"I beg your pardon?"

"It upsets the house," she said, "when the numbers change," and then she was through the door and gone, and I sat alone in the lamplight with the stew cooling in front of me and my sister's ashes on my knees, and I did not eat, and I did not move for a long time.

* * *

Later—I couldn't tell you how much later; the clocks in that house have never agreed with each other, or with anything else—I carried the urn and the portfolio up to my old room and set them on the desk beneath the window, and I opened the portfolio, because I could not not open it.

Forty-one letters. Ysolde's handwriting, which was my handwriting mirrored, slanting the other way, so that reading her was like reading myself in glass. She had written to me every few months for fifteen years, from the Hollow, and I had written back, and neither of us had ever sent anything. We had agreed on that at seventeen, the day we left—she to stay, I to go—a pact sealed on the ferry dock with our pinkies hooked together like children, because Ysolde believed the house listened to promises made aloud, and writing was safer. *We'll keep the letters,* she'd said. *When it's safe, we'll trade them all at once. Everything we never said, all at once.*

She died with her half of the trade done. Forty-one letters, unsent, stacked in a solicitor's office with my name on the band.

I read the first one. Dated the autumn after we left. *Dear Yara (which is to say, dear mirror, dear other half of the sum)—*

I read the last one. Dated nine days before she died. It was unfinished. They were all unfinished; that had been the rule, somehow, though I don't remember agreeing to it—every letter stopped mid-sentence, mid-thought, as if she could approach everything and conclude nothing. But the last one stopped worse than the others. It stopped in the middle of a word.

*Yara. The song is going wrong. I can feel the verse slipping and the house knows it, the house has started counting ME now, do you understand, it counts me at night the way we used to count the doors, and last night the number was*

And there it ended. No signature. The pen had pressed hard enough to tear the paper on the final stroke, as if her hand had been pulled away.

I sat in my childhood bedroom with the torn letter in my lap and the urn beside me, and outside the window the snow began to come down from the pass, early, thick, silent, sealing the roads the pilot had promised, and somewhere below me—I could not say which floor, which wall, which direction, only that it was below and inside—the house began, very softly, to hum.

It hummed a melody I knew. Half of a melody. A lullaby Ysolde used to sing when we were small, lying spine to spine in her bed, her voice in my ear and mine in hers, trading verses back and forth until we slept.

It hummed her half of the song.

It stopped, precisely, at the place where my half began.

I did not sleep. I sat with my back against the headboard of my own narrow bed, in a house that was warm without fuel and lit without power and humming without a throat, and I watched the door of my room—which was, I checked, the correct door, the nineteenth, the one that had always been there—until the window went grey with morning.

At some hour past midnight, the humming changed. It did not stop. It moved. Down the corridor, slow and unhurried, pausing outside each door as it passed, the way a nurse checks on patients, or a landlord checks on locks.

It paused outside my door longest of all.

And then it went on, and descended the stairs, and faded into the foundations, and I heard—or I dreamed, and I will spend the rest of this story trying to tell those two apart—a voice, small and clear and unmistakably my sister's, saying from somewhere under the floor:

*Nineteen. Six. Twenty-five. You always were the one who counted, Yara. Now count what's missing.*
