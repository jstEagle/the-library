## 8. Mrs. Vell's Confession

Mrs. Vell had dusted the east hall portrait every morning for sixty years, and in all that time she had never once looked at the woman's face.

This was not carelessness. It was discipline, the way a man who works near a cliff learns not to look down. You kept your cloth moving. You kept your eyes on the gilt frame, on the small brass plate that read *LISSANDRE ASHGROVE, 1874–*, on the little carved rose at the top left corner where the paint always chipped first. You did not look at the face, because the face was the one thing in this house that changed when you weren't watching, and Mrs. Vell had made her peace long ago with only so much of the impossible.

She was polishing the frame now — it was the third of December, or perhaps the fourth; the days had begun to slide like wet plates — and she could hear Mr. Ashgrove upstairs, walking the corridor outside his sister's room. Back and forth. Back and forth. He had been doing it since dawn, and before dawn too, if the boards were to be believed, and the boards were always to be believed. The house told her everything through its timbers. That was part of the arrangement. That was what she'd traded for.

She heard him stop. She heard the door of Ysolde's room open, and she set down her cloth and went to the kitchen and put the kettle on, because there was one conversation in this world she dreaded more than any other, and she had known since the boy arrived that it was coming. Sixty years is a long time to hold a thing behind your teeth. Her jaw ached with it some mornings.

The kettle had just begun its low complaint when he came down.

He looked like his grandfather. That was the cruelty of it. Edmund Ashgrove had the same long face, the same way of standing slightly inside doorways as though asking permission of them. She had caught herself twice already calling him *sir* in Edmund's voice, and once — unforgivably — reaching up as if to smooth his collar the way she used to when Edmund was young and frightened and the house was hungry.

"Mrs. Vell." Yara Ashgrove stood at the kitchen threshold with his hands in his pockets, and she saw that he had been crying, or close to it, and that he was angry about being close to it. "I want to ask you something, and I'd take it kindly if you didn't lie to me."

"I'm an old woman," she said. "Lying takes strength I don't have."

"You're not old."

He said it flatly, without accusation, and the words went into her like a splinter going in deep: clean, painless at first, all the damage on the way out.

"The ledgers," he said. "Ysolde's handwriting in the ledger, dated after she died. The lamps burning with no power on the line. And you, Mrs. Vell. I found the staff records in the solicitor's box. Agnes Vell, housekeeper, engaged 1964. There's a photograph." He took it from his pocket then, a small black-and-white thing gone soft at the corners, and laid it on the scrubbed table between them. A young woman stood on the back steps in an apron, squinting against the sun, one hand raised against the camera. She might have been thirty. She might have been twenty-five.

It was her own face looking back at her, younger than she felt, which was to say younger than she was, which was to say a lie sixty years deep.

"Sit down, Mr. Ashgrove," said Mrs. Vell.

* * *

She poured the tea because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. He watched her do it. He was polite enough not to speak until she'd sat across from him, and she loved him a little for that, and hated herself for loving anyone in this house, because love was the hook the place baited everything with.

"My mother died when I was nineteen," she began. "Consumption, they called it, though it was 1963 and nobody called anything consumption anymore except this valley, which calls things whatever it likes. My father drank. There was nothing for me here but service, and service there was — the Ashgroves took girls from the village, always had. It was considered good work. Steady work." She smiled, and felt the smile sit wrong on her face, like a picture hung crooked. "Nobody ever asked why the positions never turned over. Nobody asked why no housekeeper ever retired, or married, or moved to town. We came, and we stayed, and the village forgot we'd come at all. That's the first thing the house does, Mr. Ashgrove. It makes you easy to forget. You'll want to remember that."

"Ysolde wrote me letters," he said. "For years. I got none of them."

"I know. I posted some of them myself." She said it and watched the blow land, and did not look away, because she owed him that much at least — to be struck by him honestly. "I'm sorry. I was told to. Not by your family. By the house. You understand I don't mean someone stood over me with instructions. I mean the work simply arranged itself. The letters would go into my basket to post, and then I'd be doing something else, and the basket would be empty, and I wouldn't remember setting them aside. Days later I'd find them in the drawer with the candles, and by then it seemed too late to explain, and after a while it seemed — " She stopped. She drank her tea. "After a while it seemed normal. That's the second thing the house does."

"What's the third?"

"The third is the song."

The kitchen clock ticked. Outside, the wind came down off the fells and pressed its flat palm against the windows, and somewhere below the floor — below the cellar, below the well shaft the crew had opened and Brann had ordered covered again with planks and prayer — something hummed half a bar of a lullaby and let it go.

"Every housekeeper before me learned it," Mrs. Vell said. "You learn it the way you learn the layout of the rooms, just by living here. A lullaby. Old, older than the family's money. The singers sing it — that's the Ashgroves' burden, one per generation, and God keep you ignorant of the details, sir, because the details are worse than the shape of it. But the servants hummed it too. While we worked. Sweeping, scrubbing, carrying coals. Humming while you work is natural, isn't it? It passes the time."

"And it isn't natural here."

"It's payment here." She set her cup down with a small precise click. "I hummed for eleven months. I was young and I liked the tune, that's the truth of it, it's a lovely tune, it gets into you like warmth into stone. And I noticed things, the way you notice things slowly, so each one can be explained away on its own. I noticed my hands didn't chap anymore in winter. I noticed the gray coming in at my temple stopped, and then went backwards, strand by strand. I noticed that Betsy Halloran, who'd been cook here forty years, had the same face in 1970 that she had in the photograph on the stairs. And I noticed that I couldn't remember my mother's voice anymore. Or her face. Or whether she'd died in March or May. Or — " her breath caught, and she rode past it, "— or whether I'd ever had a father at all, or invented him out of the smell of beer and wet wool. The house took the humming and gave me youth, and it paid for the youth out of my memory, and it took the memories nearest the song first, because the song was where I'd been spending."

Yara had gone very still. Stillness, she thought, was the Ashgrove inheritance; grief ran in that family like water finding level.

"Stop humming," he said. "That's the answer. Just stop."

"Yes, sir. I stopped." She folded her hands on the table, and he looked at them — she saw him look — at skin that should have been seventy-one years weathered and was instead the smooth, unremarkable hands of a woman of perhaps fifty, frozen there, held, like fruit preserved in spirit. "I stopped in the spring of '65 and I haven't hummed a note in sixty years. I don't sing in church. I don't sing at funerals. When the wireless plays music I leave the room. And the aging stopped. All of it. I am fifty-three years old and I have been fifty-three years old since the winter Ysolde and Yara Ashgrove were four years old."

"That's not safety," he said quietly. "That's a display case."

"No, sir. It isn't even that." She laughed, and the laugh came out bitter and small. "You'd think it would be. Youth forever, in exchange for a few songs you didn't want to sing anyway. But here's what I learned, and learn it quick, because you're spending nights under this roof. The house doesn't stop taking when you stop paying. It stops *giving*. The debt doesn't die; it sleeps. Every year since, it has taken its interest some other way. Small things. I lose a day sometimes. Whole weeks go missing and come back with edges worn off, like coins passed through too many hands. I have served six decades in this house and I could not tell you what I did in 1988. There is a hole there, sir, smooth-sided, like a hole bored in wood. And last month — " she hesitated, and found she had to make herself say it, "— last month I lost my sister. Not to death. To the hole. I know I had a sister. There's a photograph of us on a beach. I remember the beach. I do not remember her name, or her face, or one single hour of her, and I sat on my bed with that photograph and I understood that the house has begun collecting again, because the singer is dead and the account is unsettled, and when an account is unsettled the house balances it however it can."

"Against you."

"Against whoever's nearest the flame." She looked at him straight then, and let him see all of it, the whole sixty years of it, the preserved woman with the bored-out years. "I stayed because leaving never seemed possible. That sounds like cowardice and maybe it is. But you try, sir. You try to walk down the drive with a suitcase. I've done it eleven times. Eleven times I've found myself back in this kitchen with the kettle on and no memory of the gate, the lane, the road. The house doesn't lock its doors. It just makes the world outside feel like a room you've already dusted."

The wind pressed at the windows again. Above them, faint through the ceiling, the pipes in the walls shivered — that half-melody, rising, stopping, always stopping before the end, and Mrs. Vell saw the boy's head turn toward the sound with an ache on his face that she recognized, because she'd seen it on every Ashgrove she'd ever served. They all loved the song. That was the trap's whole architecture. You could not fight a thing you loved.

"Don't hum it," she said. "Whatever you hear, however it calls you, however much it sounds like her — and it will sound like her, sir, it will wear her voice like a coat — do not hum along. The moment you give it a note, you've given it a handhold. And it climbs."

"Mrs. Vell." His voice had dropped. "My sister died mid-verse. What happens to a binding that's unfinished?"

And there it was. The question behind all his questions, the one he'd carried up from the churchyard in his chest like a coal.

She could have lied. Sixty years of practice; lying would have been easy as breathing. Instead she looked at this boy with his grandfather's face and told him the truth, because pity is just truth delivered gently, and she was so tired, and the holes in her were getting wider.

"I don't know, sir. No singer has ever died mid-verse. They die at the end, always at the end, that's the design of it. Whatever's holding the lights on in this house is holding them with a broken grip." She rose, gathered the cups, needing her hands busy. "Which means either the house will find a way to finish the song — and it has a new singer sleeping under this roof — or the grip fails altogether, and everything the song has been keeping, keeps itself."

She carried the cups to the sink. Behind her, Yara Ashgrove said nothing, but she heard him breathing, quick and shallow, a man doing arithmetic he didn't want finished.

"And sir," she added, her back still to him, her voice as steady as she could make it, "there's one more thing you should know tonight, while the roads are still frozen and your mind is still your own. The ledger entries in your sister's hand. The lamps. The warm sheets in her room. I've served this house sixty years and I know its habits, and those aren't echoes, and they aren't tricks. Something in this house is *finishing her sentences.*"

Behind him, down in the dark of the cellar stair, very softly, the pipes began to hum — and this time, for the first time in sixty years, Mrs. Vell heard them carry the melody *past* the bar where it had always stopped.
