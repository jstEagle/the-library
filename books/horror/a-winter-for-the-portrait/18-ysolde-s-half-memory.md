## 18. Ysolde's Half-Memory

The diary had gained a new line every night since he'd arrived, and for eleven nights Yara had refused to read them.

That was the discipline. Mrs. Vell had been clear about it in her fragmentary, fading way—*don't read what she writes, Mr. Ashgrove, not until you know what reading costs*—and so each morning Yara stood in his sister's room with the diary lying on the warm sheets like a patient thing, and each morning he looked at the fresh ink without touching it, the way you look at a wound you've decided not to pick. The lines were short. They were always short. From across the room he could see that much: a sentence, maybe two, in handwriting he knew better than his own, because it was his own, mirrored. Twins write alike. Twins write alike, and the house knew it, and the house used it.

On the twelfth morning he broke.

He would like to say it was weakness. It wasn't, quite. It was arithmetic. Mrs. Vell had lost another decade overnight—she'd called him Edmund at breakfast, set a place for Edmund, wept briefly and without apparent understanding when he told her his name—and Ysolde's fragment, the thing of her that still moved and wrote and waited, was dying with the unbinding, bar by bar, the same way the housekeeper's memory was going. Every night he delayed was a night the ink might stop. He had come to Ashgrove Hollow to bury his sister. He was not going to stand in her bedroom and watch her die a second time out of caution.

So he sat on the edge of the bed that was not his, on sheets that held the heat of a body that wasn't there, and he opened the diary to the first new line, and he read what his dead sister had written through the walls of the house that had eaten her.

*Five notes rising, then hold. The rest tomorrow, if you sing.*

Below it, in the same hand, smaller, almost shy:

*You know the one. You've always known it.*

Yara sat very still. The radiator ticked. Somewhere below the floor, in the pipes, the half-lullaby hummed its eternal unfinished phrase and stopped, as it always stopped, at the place where the ending should be.

*The rest tomorrow, if you sing.*

She was bargaining. His sister—whatever was left of her, the sediment of her, the half-memory the house had kept like a pressed flower between its floorboards—was trading him the final verse one bar at a time, and the currency was the first verse, the opening Mrs. Vell had taught him with her eyes closed and her hands flat on the piano lid, the one he had sworn he would never sing alone.

Except he already was singing it. He had been for six nights. That was the thing about the Hollow: it did not ask twice, and it did not ask at all, really. It arranged. It had arranged the cold in his room until the only warmth in the house pooled in the music room. It had arranged the silence until the only sound that eased it was the opening phrase, rising, patient, the musical equivalent of a held-out hand. He had told himself he was practicing. He had told himself it was for the funeral he still hadn't managed to hold. And every night at eleven he had sat at the piano with his hands flat on the wood and sung the first verse into the dark of the house, and the house had brightened around him like a lung filling, and he had gone to bed warm and hating himself in a way that was starting to feel almost comfortable.

Now the diary told him what the singing was buying. And it was buying exactly what he wanted most in the world.

*The rest tomorrow, if you sing.*

His sister was selling him the ending of the song. And the price was the beginning. And he was already paying it.

* * *

He went down to the music room at eleven that night the way a man goes to a meeting he has agreed to without ever saying yes.

The room behind the cellar wall was dustless and warm and wrong in the way that only perfectly preserved things are wrong. The piano sat with its lid open. The sheet music stood in its rack—Ysolde's score, the one that ended at the same bar line every copy in the house ended at, the wall where five generations of Ashgroves had stopped writing because the last verse lived only in mouths and memories and blood. The lamp burned. The lamps always burned. No one paid for the light; someone paid for everything.

Yara sat. He put his hands flat on the piano lid, the way Mrs. Vell had shown him, and he noticed—because he was starting to notice everything now, the way you notice the current only once you're in the river—that his hands went there without being told. Flat palms. Fingers spread. The posture of a man steadying himself, or of a man being held down.

He sang the first verse.

It was not a performance. Mrs. Vell had been explicit about that too, in the days when she still had the decades to be explicit with: *you don't sing it, Mr. Ashgrove, you give it. There's a difference, and the house knows the difference, and it will take whichever one you offer.* He gave it. The notes rose out of him the way warmth rises out of a stone that has been in the sun all day—not chosen, just released. The first verse was simple. That was the trap's elegance, he was beginning to understand. A lullaby. A thing a mother would sing. A thing a twin would know by heart before she knew what hearts were for.

The house took it. He felt it go—felt the walls drink, felt the warmth redistribute, felt the lamp above the piano burn a shade steadier. Somewhere upstairs Mrs. Vell, if she still had the years to feel anything, would have felt it too: the bargain renewed, the meter running.

And when the last note of the verse settled into the floorboards, the house answered.

It came up through the pipes first—that half-melody, Ysolde's lullaby, the one that always stopped short—and then, for the first time, it went past the place where it always stopped. One bar. Five notes, rising, then held. The exact phrase from the diary, sounded out in the plumbing like a child practicing at the far end of a long house, shy and halting and unmistakably hers.

Yara sat with his hands still flat on the lid and let the held note hang in the pipes, and he did not cry, because he had learned in the last two weeks that the Hollow drank that too, or wanted to, and he was done giving it things for free.

"Tomorrow," he said to the empty room. His voice sounded very small against all that listening. "One bar a night. That's the deal. I sing, you write. And Ysolde—" he stopped, because the name did what it always did, it opened a door in him that the house was standing right behind. He made himself finish. "Ysolde. If this is you. If any of this is you and not just the house wearing you—I'm coming. One bar at a time. I'm coming to the end."

The pipes were silent. The lamp burned. The warmth lay against his back like a hand.

He did not sleep. He sat in his sister's room until dawn with the diary open on his knees, and at some hour when the windows were still black he watched the ink arrive—not written, there was no pen, no hand, the page simply darkened the way a photograph develops—and the new line said:

*Good. Don't stop. It hears you better than I do.*

* * *

The bars came. One per night, faithful as frost.

Yara learned the shape of the ritual within three nights and stopped pretending he hadn't. Eleven o'clock, the music room, hands flat, first verse given. The house drank. The pipes answered. And the next morning the diary held a new line—not words anymore, mostly, but notation, scrawled in that mirrored handwriting, a single bar of the final verse rendered in Ysolde's quick slanted hand as if she were copying it off a chalkboard only she could see. He transcribed each bar into the back of his own notebook, and by the end of the second week he had eleven bars of an ending that did not exist on paper anywhere else in the world, and the eleven bars did not make sense, and he loved them anyway.

That was the part he hadn't expected: how beautiful it was. He had braced for horror. He had not braced for the melody itself, which unspooled from the first verse the way a river unspools from its spring—inevitable, widening, gathering everything the opening had promised. Each new bar made the previous ones more inevitable. Each morning he sat on his sister's bed and read the night's line and heard, in his head, all eleven bars in sequence, and the sequence ached. It ached the way her letters had ached, the unfinished ones he'd carried across the ice in his coat. It ached like something written by someone who knew she was running out of time.

He caught himself humming it on the fourth day. Standing in the east hall, in front of the portrait of the woman who aged backward, he caught the melody in his own mouth—bars one through seven, the ones he had, the ones that weren't the binding, weren't the contract, were just music—and he shut his mouth so hard he bit his tongue.

Because that was the question, wasn't it. That was the question Mrs. Vell had gone senile trying to warn him about, back when she still had the words for it. Where was the line? The first verse bound. He knew that now; he could feel it, the small daily withdrawal, the way his own warmth seemed a little thinner each morning, the way his reflection in the hall mirror looked, some days, like a man ten years older than the man who'd stepped off the ferry. But the final verse—the verse that ended it, the verse that would free Lissandre Ashgrove from her frame and free the house from its hundred-year appetite—did the final verse bind too? Or was it only the singing that bound, the giving, the act itself, regardless of what was sung?

*You don't sing it, you give it. There's a difference, and the house knows the difference.*

And if the difference was in the singer—if the binding lived in the posture, the flat hands, the closed eyes, the surrender—then what was he doing, night after night, training himself into that posture? What was he practicing?

He asked the diary. He wrote it himself, in his own hand, in the margin of the page, the first time he had ever written to her instead of reading: *Is the last verse safe to sing?*

The answer came at dawn, and it was one line, and he read it forty times:

*Nothing in this house is safe. But I only have so many nights left, and neither do you. Bar twelve tomorrow. Keep singing.*

* * *

Mrs. Vell found him in the music room on the fifteenth night, and for a few minutes she was entirely herself.

He knew it the way you know a break in clouds—suddenly, completely, and with the knowledge that it would not hold. She came down the cellar stairs with a candle she didn't need, in a house where no lamp had ever gone out, and she stood in the doorway of the dustless room and looked at him at the piano with an expression he had not seen on her face before. It took him a moment to name it. It was guilt. Sixty years of it, arriving all at once in a face that had forgotten how to carry it.

"You're taking the verse from her," Mrs. Vell said.

"She's giving it to me."

"That's what I said." She came into the room and set the candle on the piano, absurdly, next to the lamp, two flames that had nothing to do with each other. "She gives. That's how it's always done. The singer gives and the house gives back and everyone calls it a bargain because calling it a bargain is kinder than calling it what it is." She looked at the score on the rack, at the bar line where five generations had stopped. "Your sister sat where you're sitting. Every night for three years. And every morning she was a little grayer and the house was a little warmer and I brought her tea and told myself it was an honor to serve." Her hands were shaking. "Do you know what I remember, Mr. Ashgrove? I remember everything I watched and nothing I felt. That was the trade. They took the feeling out so I could keep doing it. Sixty years of funerals and I remember the dates."

"Mrs. Vell—"

"She's rationing herself." The old woman's voice dropped, went flat, went true. "You understand that. The fragment. Whatever the house kept of her. She's spending it one bar at a time, and when the verse is done, she's done. There won't be a Ysolde in the walls anymore. There won't be a line in the diary. You're buying the ending of the song with the last of your sister, and she's paying it gladly, because she's your twin, and that's what twins do, and the house has always known it. That's why it's always twins." She picked up her candle. "The house doesn't want singers, Mr. Ashgrove. It wants the ones who love them. Singers come from somewhere. The house just has to make sure the somewhere is full of people who'll pay."

She went up the stairs. At the top she paused, and for one moment the senility took her back, and she said, in a small puzzled voice, "Edmund? Is Edmund coming to supper?" and then she was gone, and the candlelight went with her, and Yara sat alone in the warm dustless room with eleven bars of his sister's last gift and the understanding, finally whole, of what they cost.

* * *

He almost stopped that night.

He wants to be honest about that, in the account he keeps in his own head, the ledger no one will ever audit. He sat in Ysolde's room with the diary closed under his hand and he decided—actually decided, with the clean cold clarity that comes at three in the morning in a house that is listening—that he would not sing. He would not spend her. He would find another way. There had to be another way: the blood score the house whispered about, the first singer's remains behind the innermost door, some copy, some loophole, some mercy in the architecture that five generations had missed. He would not trade what was left of his sister for the tool to free the woman who'd helped take her. He would sit in the dark and be cold and keep what remained of Ysolde whole, even if whole meant unfinished, even if unfinished meant the fragment faded in the walls like breath off a window, because some things you don't spend, you keep them, that's what keeping means—

And then he thought about her letters.

The unfinished ones in his coat, the ones she'd written in the months before she died, the ones he'd read so many times the folds had gone soft as cloth. *I found where the music lives,* she'd written. *I'm going to finish it, Yara. I'm going to end it, and then I'll come home, and we'll be done with all of it.* She had gone into the house on purpose. She had gone in singing, with her eyes open, to end a hundred years of it, and she had died mid-verse, and the house had kept the piece of her that knew the ending the way it kept everything: not out of cruelty, maybe. Out of conservation. Out of the same terrible thrift that kept the lamps burning and Mrs. Vell young and the portrait's hands freshly painted. The house wasted nothing. Not light, not warmth, not youth, not the dead.

And the fragment in the walls—the half of his sister the house had pressed between its floorboards—was offering him the one thing she had died trying to deliver.

One bar a night. If he sang.

He went down at eleven. He put his hands flat on the lid. He hated the house, and he sang, and the house drank, and the pipes answered with bar twelve, and upstairs, in the morning, the diary's line said:

*Halfway. Don't grieve me yet. Grieve me at the end, all at once, properly. That's what I'm saving it for.*

He read it standing in the gray light of her room, in the warm sheets' warmth, and he understood that she had thought this through—that the fragment was not a ghost fumbling in the dark but Ysolde, the whole Ysolde, the planner, the one who'd counted the doors and learned the song and walked into the house with a plan, and her plan had a shape, and the shape required him, and the cost was written into the first bar and the last.

Below the line, in smaller ink, almost an afterthought, almost a whisper:

*Also—tell the men in the yard to stop digging. They've found the first door. They don't know what the others are yet. I do.*

Yara went to the window. Down in the frozen yard, in the blue light before dawn, Brann's crew had already gathered around the well shaft, and the humming was coming up out of the ground again, and the men were standing very still, the way people stand when a sound has gotten inside them and started giving instructions.

They were not going to stop digging. And the diary had said *doors*—plural—and Yara stood at the window with his sister's handwriting drying on the page behind him and understood that while he had been learning the song bar by bar, the house had been teaching the ground to open.
