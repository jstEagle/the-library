## 20. Seven Singers

The family Bible lived in the study, on the shelf behind the desk where Grandfather Ashgrove had kept his ledgers and his whiskey and the two things he never let anyone touch at once.

Yara had found it there as a boy, on the one summer he'd been allowed to visit—allowed being a generous word for the fortnight Ysolde had begged for him and Mrs. Vell had pretended not to hear the begging. He remembered it as an enormous book, black leather gone soft as cloth, its spine cracked into segments like something that had been bent back too many times to ever straighten again. He had opened it then looking for pictures. He had found only names.

Now he carried it to the kitchen table, set it down beside Brann's photographs—the crew's foreman had brought them up from the pit that morning, prints still curling with cold—and told himself he was doing this because it was practical. Because names were data. Because if seven doors had been sealed from the inside, each with a name carved into the oak, then someone in this family had been keeping records, and records were how you beat a thing. You found its arithmetic. You learned what it cost per head.

He was not doing it because of the way the carved letters had looked in Brann's flashlight. He was not doing it because the seventh name had been *Ashgrove*.

He poured himself two fingers of the caretaker's rye, didn't drink it, and opened the Bible to the flyleaf.

* * *

The births began in 1789. That much matched the parish records Ysolde had once photographed for a school project—the only school project she'd ever done on the family, and she'd abandoned it halfway through, telling him the archives smelled like "a room where someone died reading." He'd laughed at the time. He thought about it now with his hands flat on the table, steadying himself against nothing.

The register wasn't a normal family record. Normal families recorded marriages, deaths, christenings, the occasional scandal noted in a later hand with disapproval. This one recorded pairs.

Every generation, entered side by side, in columns ruled off in faded red ink:

**1789 — Maren & Mairead Ashgrove. Born same hour, same night.**
**1812 — Edric & Edeva Ashgrove. Born same hour, same night.**
**1836 — Corwin & Corliss Ashgrove. Born same hour, same night.**
**1861 — Aldous & Alva Ashgrove. Born same hour, same night.**
**1887 — Lissandre & Lisbet Ashgrove. Born same hour, same night.**
**1913 — Edmund & Edith Ashgrove. Born same hour, same night.**
**1940 — Gareth & Gwenna Ashgrove. Born same hour, same night.**
**1968 — Tobias & Tabitha Ashgrove. Born same hour, same night.**

And at the bottom, in his mother's handwriting, smaller, almost apologetic:

**1994 — Ysolde & Yara Ashgrove. Born same hour, same night.**

Nine generations. Nine pairs. Every single entry the same formula, copied down like a recipe, like a law of nature: *born same hour, same night.* Not twins in the casual way people said it—not siblings who happened to share a womb. Twins in the way a lock and key are twins. Twins in the way a question and its answer are.

Yara sat very still and listened to the house breathe around him. The pipes hummed their half-lullaby somewhere under the floor, always stopping before the end, and for the first time he heard it not as his sister's melody but as a metronome. Something counting.

He turned the page.

* * *

The deaths were recorded too. Of course they were. The deaths were the point.

Beside each pair of births, in the same red-ruled format, the register noted which twin had died first, and how, and who had buried whom. And Yara read down the column with the rye untouched at his elbow and felt the floor of his own history tilt beneath him like ice going out.

**Maren Ashgrove, d. 1831, aged 42. Buried by her sister.**
**Mairead Ashgrove, d. 1859, aged 70.**

Maren had died first. Maren had been buried by her sister. And Mairead—the surviving twin—had lived another twenty-eight years alone in the Hollow before following her.

**Edric Ashgrove, d. 1851, aged 39. Buried by his sister.**
**Edeva Ashgrove, d. 1878, aged 66.**

Edric first. Edeva buried him. Edeva lived on.

**Corwin Ashgrove, d. 1874, aged 38. Buried by his sister.**
**Corliss Ashgrove, d. 1901, aged 65.**

Corwin first. Corliss buried him. Corliss lived on.

It went like that all the way down, generation after generation, the pattern so clean it looked less like a family history than a machine diagram. One twin died young—in their thirties, their early forties, always, without exception, decades before any natural span would have collected them. The other twin buried them. And the survivor lived on, long past reason, long past appetite, aging slowly or sometimes—he checked the dates twice, three times—barely at all, until the next pair came of age and the whole cycle turned over again like a wheel finding its notch.

Gareth died at forty-one; Gwenna buried him and lived to eighty-nine. Tobias died at thirty-seven; Tabitha buried him and lived to ninety-one. His grandfather Tobias. His grandmother Tabitha, who had held him once, when he was four, and whose hands had been warm as bathwater and whose face had been—

Whose face had been smooth. Whose face had been far too smooth for ninety-one, and he had thought, even at four, even without words for it, that there was something wrong with a grandmother who looked younger than his mother.

He put the glass down carefully, because his hand wanted to shake and he would not give the house the satisfaction of watching it.

One singer. One witness. Every generation, one twin went into the ground early and the other stood over the grave holding the spade, and then the survivor stayed, and stayed, and stayed, fed on something, kept by something, until the next pair of children born the same hour on the same night grew old enough for the house to choose between them.

And the choice, every time, every single time across two hundred years, had fallen the same way.

The one who died was the one who sang.

He knew it the way you know your own name—suddenly, totally, without needing proof. He knew it because the doors below the music room had names carved in them, and because Brann had photographed every one, and because he laid those photographs out now along the edge of the Bible, seven doors in a row, and read them against the register like a man matching a key ring to a wall of locks.

*MAREN* — the oldest door, deepest down, oak gone nearly to stone.
*EDRIC.*
*CORWIN.*
*ALDOUS.*
*EDMUND.*
*GARETH.*
*TOBIAS.*

Seven doors. Seven singers. Seven first-dying twins, every one of them, sealed into rooms beneath the cellar by the sisters and brothers who loved them—who must have loved them, who had sung them down and shut the door and carved the name so no one would forget, because forgetting was the one thing the house could not survive.

And the survivors' names weren't on any door. Mairead. Edeva. Corliss. Alva. Lisbet. Edith. Gwenna. Tabitha.

The buriers. The witnesses. The ones who walked out over the ice, or tried to, or stayed instead, growing strange and smooth-faced and long-lived in the house's warm breath, humming a song they'd promised never to finish.

Mrs. Vell had stopped humming sixty years ago and hadn't aged a day since. He'd heard her confession through the kitchen door and thought it was about her bargain, her private cowardice, her small trade of memory for safety.

It wasn't private. It was the job description. She was Tabitha's role. She was every survivor's role, stretched across generations until the original woman's name wore off her. The non-singing twin didn't leave. The non-singing twin became the house's hands. Became the keeper. Became, eventually, furniture with a heartbeat.

And the singing twin—

Ysolde had died mid-verse. Ysolde had come back here two years ago, alone, telling him it was to settle the estate, telling him not to follow, and she had picked up a song that ran in the family like blood type, and the house had begun to drink.

* * *

He got up because sitting still had become unbearable, and walked the length of the kitchen, and stood at the window looking out at the frozen lake with his breath fogging the glass, and made himself say it inside his own skull in plain words, no poetry, no escape:

We were never the exception.

All his life he had believed they were. That was the story he and Ysolde had built together, the twin mythology, the fortress of it: *everyone else in this family is haunted, but we got out. We're the ones who left. We're the ones Mother smuggled away from the Hollow before the house could count us.* They had built their whole identities on the gap between themselves and the names in this book. Ysolde had become a woman who fled inheritance the way other people fled creditors—changing cities, changing numbers, never singing so much as a note in public, flinching from choirs, from hymns, from doorways that echoed. He had become a man who never asked why. Who took her flinching at face value because asking would have meant learning, and some part of him had known, even as a child, that learning was the trap.

They hadn't escaped the pattern. They *were* the pattern. Ninth in a line of nine, born the same hour on the same night, entered in the same red-ruled columns, waiting since 1994 for the house to decide which of them would sing and which would hold the spade.

And the house had decided. Of course it had decided—it had probably decided before they were born, before their mother fled, maybe before the foundation stones were cut. It had waited twenty-some years with the patience of architecture, and then it had drawn Ysolde back with letters and debts and the soft gravitational pull of a place that owns part of your name, and it had started feeding.

She died at thirty. Thirty. Younger than Maren, younger than Edric, younger than any of them. She hadn't even lasted a full verse.

Because she'd fought it. He understood that now too, with a clarity that felt like a burn. The others had sung—they'd taken up the song as duty, as inheritance, as the thing you did the way you harvested what the land gave. They'd lasted years, decades even, giving the house what it drank at a rate the body could survive. Ysolde had refused. Had starved it. Had tried to leave the binding half-done, half-sung, a melody with no ending, and the house had simply taken faster, the way any starving thing takes, until there was nothing left of her but what fit in an urn.

An urn full of river silt and a stranger's wedding ring.

His stomach turned over and he gripped the counter. Even her ashes had been a forgery. Even her death had been a transaction conducted in someone else's currency—a ring belonging to Lissandre, the resident, the fifth-generation twin who had never died at all, who had been kept instead, kept and kept and kept while her sister Lisbet's name went on no door and her youth went down the house's throat.

Wait.

He went back to the table. Went back to the page. **1887 — Lissandre & Lisbet Ashgrove.** And below it, in the deaths: **Lisbet Ashgrove, d. 1919, aged 32. Buried by her sister.**

Buried by her sister. But Lissandre hadn't buried anyone. Lissandre was upstairs in a frame, aging backward, a hundred and thirty years old and younger than Yara. So who had written *buried by her sister*? Who had stood at Lisbet's grave?

He flipped forward, hunting, and found it—an addendum, squeezed sideways into the margin in a cramped antique hand: *Lisbet interred by the household. Lissandre remains within.*

Remains within. Four words, written by someone who had known exactly what that meant, recording it the way you'd record a change of address.

Lissandre was the break in the pattern. The one generation where the singer hadn't died—where the house, or the family, or Lissandre herself, had found a way to stop the wheel halfway round. Keep the singer. Don't let her spend out. Freeze the account. And the price of that frozen account was everything after it: a house running on stolen light, a keeper who couldn't age, seven doors below the cellar and an eighth being dug, and now, a hundred years late, the bill arriving in the mail with his name on the envelope.

Or not his name. He looked at the last line of the register again, his mother's small apologetic handwriting, and saw what he should have seen the first time.

**1994 — Ysolde & Yara Ashgrove.**

Ysolde's name first. In every other entry, the singer's name came first. Maren before Mairead. Edric before Edeva. Corwin, Aldous, Edmund, Gareth, Tobias—all singers, all listed first, all dead first. Their mother hadn't known what she was writing. She'd just put the names down in the order they arrived, ten minutes apart on a winter night, and the order was the order.

Ysolde had always been first. First named, first chosen, first spent.

And he—Yara, second-listed, second-born, the one who got to live—was Tabitha. Was Gwenna. Was Edith and Lisbet and every smooth-faced survivor in this book. His role was already written. It had always been written. Bury the singer. Stay. Keep the lamps lit. Hum but never finish. Grow old in the house's warm breath until the next pair of children arrived the same hour on the same night, and then stand in the doorway of the music room and teach them the opening bars.

That was what he was for. That was what he had *always* been for. Every choice he'd thought he made—the schools, the cities, the career, the careful distance from his own family's gravity—had just been the long way around to this kitchen table, this Bible, this column of red-ruled names with his own at the bottom of it.

Identity collapsed into ritual. He felt it go, quietly, the way a bone goes—not a snap but a settling, the structure admitting what it had always been.

* * *

He didn't know how long he stood there. Long enough that the light through the window changed from white to blue. Long enough that the rye went warm and untouched and he poured it down the sink without tasting it, because drinking felt like something the house would enjoy watching.

Then he picked up the photographs of the doors again, all seven, and looked at them properly this time—not at the names but at the carving itself. And he noticed what grief had kept him from noticing that morning.

The letters were carved from the inside.

Brann had said it about the first door and Yara had filed it away as one more impossible detail in a house full of them: *sealed from the inside.* But standing here, with the register open, he finally understood what it meant, and understanding it was worse than any ghost.

The singers had shut themselves in.

Not murdered. Not entombed by weeping siblings. They had walked down the stairs, each of them, generation after generation, carrying whatever they carried—Maren with her candle, Tobias with his sheet music—and they had pulled the door closed behind them and driven the bolts home from within, and the ones outside had only carved the names afterward, knelt at the oak like mourners at a headstone, and climbed back up into the light to keep the lamps burning.

Which meant the doors weren't prisons.

They were choices. Recorded, repeated, inherited choices. Two hundred years of Ashgroves walking down into the dark on their own feet, and the register didn't call it sacrifice or suicide or duty. The register just wrote the dates, same hour, same night, and left the space beside them blank for whoever came next to fill in.

Yara looked at the bottom of the page, at his mother's handwriting, at the blank space after *1994 — Ysolde & Yara Ashgrove,* and understood that the space was waiting for him. That the book was waiting. That the house had been waiting longer than he'd been alive for him to pick up a pen and write the ending of his own line.

From below the floor, faint through the joists, the pipes changed.

The lullaby—the half-melody, the fragment that had hummed through the walls every night since he arrived, always stopping before the end—stopped. And in the silence where its ending should have been, he heard, very clearly, very patiently, a new sound.

Not humming. Not water.

A pen, scratching.

Somewhere in the house, someone was writing in the family Bible. And the sound was coming from the direction of the study, where no one was.
