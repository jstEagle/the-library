## 23. Brann's Name on a Door

Brann had been digging holes for thirty-one years and he'd learned early that the ground kept its own books. Every job was a ledger: what went in, what came out, what the earth decided to hold back. He liked numbers because they didn't lie. Forty feet of overburden meant forty feet of overburden. A cubic yard of spoil weighed a ton and a half, give or take, and a man who respected those figures went home with all his fingers.

The Hollow had stopped respecting figures around the time they found the first door.

He stood at the lip of the new shaft that morning with his coffee going cold in one fist and watched Denny and Okafor work the winch below. The music room behind the sealed oak door had been catalogued, photographed, argued over for two days now, and Ashgrove—the thin one, the twin—had spent most of it down there alone with a flashlight and a face like a man reading his own obituary. Brann didn't blame him. Brann had seen the piano. He'd seen the sheet music going back five generations, the spines of the folios soft as old bread, every one of them ending at the same bar line like a sentence cut off mid-word.

What bothered him wasn't the room. Rooms were just rooms. What bothered him was the dust—or the lack of it. A chamber sealed for a century should have been grey. That one had been clean as a chapel on a Saturday night.

"Foreman." Okafor's voice came up the shaft, flattened by the cold. "You want to come look at this."

Brann looked at his coffee. Looked at the sky, which was the color of dishwater and getting lower by the hour. Then he clipped on the harness and let them lower him into the hole, because that was the job, and the job was the only thing between him and thinking too hard about anything else.

* * *

The eighth door was under the music room floor.

They'd found it because the humming wouldn't stop. That was how Okafor put it, anyway, and Brann had heard it himself by then—a thread of melody coming up through the spoil heap, through the tarps, through the cab of the idling excavator, faint as a radio two rooms away. It got into your back teeth. It made you want to dig. Three of his crew had been working past shift without being asked, shoveling in the dark with their breath steaming, and when Brann told them to knock off they'd looked at him like he'd spoken a foreign language.

So they'd pried up the flagstones beneath the piano, and there it was: another door. Oak and iron, same as the first, but this one smaller—child-sized, almost—and this one unsealed. The latch sat open against its plate like a tongue lolling out of a mouth.

And carved into the wood, fresh enough that the curls of shaving still lay in the dust at its foot, were five letters:

**BRANN**

He crouched in front of it for a long time. The others waited. Nobody said anything, which was its own kind of noise.

"That's not funny," Denny said finally.

"No," Brann agreed. "It isn't."

He touched the letters. The wood was warm. Not sun-warm, not pipe-warm—warm the way a hand is warm, blood-warm, and the grain of the oak had grown around the carving the way bark grows around a nail left in a tree years ago. Except the carving was new. He could smell the sap in it. He could see where someone—not someone, something, some pressure from inside the wood itself—had pushed the letters up through the surface like bruises rising.

Behind the door was darkness and a smell of cold stone and, underneath that, something sweeter. Beeswax. Old roses. The way a church smells an hour after a wedding.

"We're done here," Brann said. He stood up so fast his knee cracked. "We're done. I'm pulling everyone off this site tomorrow morning if the road lets us, tonight if it doesn't."

Okafor opened his mouth. Brann pointed at him—not angry, just final—and Okafor closed it again.

But on the way up the ladder, Brann caught himself doing something he hadn't done since he was a boy: he was counting. Doors, rungs, breaths. Counting was what you did when the world had stopped adding up, and the world had stopped adding up the moment a dead woman's handwriting had shown up in a caretaker's ledger, and now his own name was growing out of a door forty feet underground like a second set of fingerprints.

His name. Not Ashgrove. Not Vell. **BRANN**.

The doors below the music room had names carved on them—he knew that much; the crew had reported seven of them, each sealed from the inside, each with a name worn smooth by time. Ashgrove names. Generations of them. His name should not have been among them. His name belonged to a flat outside Inverness and a divorce finalized in March and a daughter who called him on Sundays. His name belonged to invoices and insurance certificates and the laminated card in his wallet that said who to notify.

It did not belong on a door.

* * *

That night he slept in the site trailer because the inn had flooded—of course it had flooded; everything in this valley flooded now except the house on the hill, which stayed dry and lit and warm as a held breath—and he dreamed, and in the dream he was humming.

He woke at three in the morning with the taste of the melody in his mouth the way you wake with the taste of a word you can't place. He was lying on top of the blankets, fully dressed, boots on, and his lips were still moving. He pressed them together. The hum kept going for a second without him, like a fly trapped in glass, and then it stopped.

He got up and drank water straight from the tap and did not look at the window, because the window faced the house, and the house was looking at him. He could feel it the way you feel a dog watching you from across a field. Every lamp in every window burning. No wires running to it. No meter. No bill ever paid, Mrs. Vell had told him cheerfully, in sixty years.

He thought about his daughter, Aoife, nineteen, studying nursing in Glasgow. He thought about her name and how it would look carved in oak.

Then he thought about nothing at all for a while, which was a mercy, and when the nothing ended it was dawn and the kettle was boiling and he had no memory of filling it.

* * *

"You've been sleeping down here?" Ashgrove asked him that afternoon.

They stood at the edge of the excavation, both of them looking down at the little door with its open latch. Ashgrove had gone greyer since the morning. There was a tremor in his hands that hadn't been there a week ago, and Brann recognized it, because he'd seen the same tremor start in Mrs. Vell whenever the humming got loud.

"One night," Brann said. "Roads are out. Nothing to be done."

"You were humming."

It wasn't a question. Brann turned to look at him properly. The man had his collar up against the wind and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady, and underneath the steadiness was something that looked horribly like recognition.

"How would you know that?"

"Because I hear it through the walls at night," Ashgrove said. "And last night it was in two parts. Harmony." He smiled, and it was the worst smile Brann had ever seen on a living face. "I don't sing, Mr. Brann. I gave that up before I arrived. So either the house has learned a new trick, or you do."

"I don't know any songs."

"Nobody knows this one. That's rather the point." Ashgrove crouched at the shaft's edge, careful, deliberate, the way a man moves near a snake. "May I tell you what I think is happening? You can tell me I'm wrong. I'd like that, actually. I'd very much like to be wrong."

Brann said nothing, which Ashgrove took as permission.

"There are seven doors under the music room. Seven singers, one per generation, going back to 1891. Each one sealed from the inside—which means each one walked in and shut themselves away, or was shut in, and the house has never once needed to force anyone. It doesn't force. It invites." He nodded at the eighth door. "And now there's an eighth, and it isn't sealed, and it has your name on it. Freshly. Like a place setting at dinner."

"It's a joke," Brann said. "One of my lads having a laugh."

"With sap still wet in the grain? With the wood grown over it?" Ashgrove shook his head slowly. "Mr. Brann, I've spent two weeks learning how this house works, and the one thing I can tell you with certainty is that it does not joke. It keeps accounts. And somewhere in the last week, an account opened with your name on it."

"Why me? I'm nobody here. I'm a contractor. I dug a well."

"You dug *down*." Ashgrove's voice dropped. "Every one of those doors was buried deeper than the last. The house stacks its singers like sediment. And you're the one who keeps reaching it. You're the one whose hands have been on the shovel every time the ground gives something up." He paused. "Also—you've been hearing the song. Haven't you. Through the ground. At night."

Brann wanted to say no. The word was right there. But his throat did something complicated instead, and Ashgrove read it like a gauge.

"The song finds people who will carry it," Ashgrove said quietly. "Ysolde carried it. My sister. She died of it—mid-verse, whatever that means, and I'm beginning to understand exactly what it means. And now the house needs a singer, and it's casting about, and it has apparently decided that the man who keeps opening its doors is a candidate." He stood, knees popping. "Get off this property, Mr. Brann. Whatever it costs. Break contract, eat the penalty, drive through the flood if you have to. Because that door isn't waiting for permission. It's waiting for you to get tired."

* * *

Brann tried.

He got the whole crew together that evening in the trailer and laid it out plain: roads flooded, trucks dead, but there was a track over the moor that a four-by-four might manage, and come first light they'd walk out if they had to, and every man would be paid in full plus hazard, and he'd personally burn the contract if the company balked. They listened. They nodded. Okafor said "right, boss" in the tone of a man agreeing to a weather forecast.

And then, at ten o'clock, Brann came back from checking the fuel drums and found six men sitting in the trailer in the dark with the lamps off, listening.

Not talking. Listening. Heads tilted, eyes half-closed, faces slack and young-looking in a way that made his stomach turn over. The humming was coming up through the floorboards—from the well shaft, from the doors, from wherever the house kept its throat—and it had words now, or almost-words, syllables that rose and fell like breathing, and his crew were leaning toward it the way plants lean toward light.

"Denny." His voice came out rough. "Denny. Outside."

Denny blinked at him like a man surfacing. "Boss?"

"Outside. All of you. Now."

They filed out, confused, apologetic, and the humming thinned behind them, aggrieved, like a conversation interrupted. Brann stood alone in the dark trailer with his heart going hard and understood two things with total clarity.

First: he could not save these men by standing between them and the sound. The sound went through him like he was a window.

Second: he had been humming along. The whole time. Under his breath, in harmony, without deciding to. His lips were moving right now.

He clamped his jaw shut and went out into the snow and counted his crew twice, like sheep, like a rosary, until the shaking stopped.

* * *

He slept in the excavator that night, cab locked, engine off, radio on static because static was the only thing that drowned the melody. Somewhere past midnight he surfaced from a shallow doze to find his own hands resting on the steering wheel in the position of a pianist's hands—curved, poised, fingers spread over invisible keys—and he snatched them back into his lap and sat in the dark breathing hard while frost feathered the windshield.

In the morning there were boot prints around the machine. Small ones. Bare feet. They circled the excavator twice and ended at the driver's door, where the snow showed the clear print of a palm pressed flat against the glass, fingers long, patient, at rest.

He didn't tell Ashgrove. He didn't tell anyone. He walked down to the shaft instead, alone, because there are things a man has to look at himself, and he stood over the eighth door and made himself read his own name.

The carving had deepened overnight. The letters stood proud of the wood now, half an inch, sharp-edged, and around them the oak had begun to figure itself into something else—knots rearranging, grain flowing—so that if you squinted, the door's face resolved into a likeness. A broad man. Heavy shoulders. A jaw like a shovel blade.

It was a good likeness. It was, God help him, a flattering one. The carver had given him dignity. The carver had given him the face he'd have wanted at a funeral.

The latch hung open. Behind the door, the sweet cold smell of beeswax and roses had strengthened, and beneath it now was something else, something that took him a moment to place because he'd only smelled it twice in thirty-one years of digging: a room that had been closed a very long time, waiting to be aired. A room with furniture under sheets. A room prepared.

Prepared for him.

"Right," Brann said aloud, to the door, to the house, to the humming that had started up again somewhere below his boots, gentle as a mother at a crib. His breath smoked and his voice cracked and he said it anyway, because a foreman's word was the one thing he'd never yet broken. "No. You hear me? I've got a daughter. I've got a number to call on Sundays. I'm not your singer. I'm not anybody's singer. Find yourself another fool."

The humming paused.

Just for a heartbeat—one clean silence, the kind that means attention—and then it resumed, softer than before, and Brann felt it settle into his chest like a coal into ash, and he knew, with the professional dread that had served him faithfully for thirty-one years, that the pause had not been disappointment.

It had been a note taken. An entry in a ledger.

He climbed out of the shaft and went looking for Ashgrove, walking fast through the falling snow, and he found the thin man standing at the east door of the house with an envelope in his hands and an expression Brann had last seen on the face of a man reading a verdict.

"Ashgrove," Brann said. "Your house has started keeping books on me. I need to know how this ends. I need to know *now*."

Ashgrove looked up, and behind him, in the dark of the hall, every lamp was burning, and above the stair hung the portrait of a woman who was, Brann could have sworn, younger today than she had been yesterday.

"That," Ashgrove said, "is exactly the question I've been trying not to ask."
