## 35. The Last Verse

The house was quieter than it had ever been.

Yara noticed it the way you notice a toothache stopping—the absence louder than the ache had been. For weeks the Hollow had hummed through its pipes and floorboards, a low sustained note beneath everything, the sound of an engine idling, of something waiting to be fed. Now that note was gone. The silence underneath it was enormous.

Lissandre's portrait hung empty in the east hall. Not gone—the frame still held her, the canvas still stretched on its stretcher bars—but empty, the way a room is empty after someone has finally left it. The paint had gone gray and flat overnight, a woman-shaped smudge where a woman had been. He had signed her name at dawn—*her* name, Lissandre Ashgrove, in his own hand, because the deed had never asked for his—and he had felt the house exhale around him like a lung letting go of air it had held for a hundred and thirty years.

The papers were signed. The resident was free. And now there was only the song.

He stood in the music room behind the cellar wall, the one Brann's crew had breached, the one that had never gathered dust. The piano sat in the center of the room with its lid open, and above it, pinned to the wall with rusted pins, hung the blood score—the first singer's final testament, copied out in brown-black strokes that no longer rearranged themselves when he looked away. They had stopped moving last night, after Mrs. Vell died in the chair by the kitchen stove with her hands folded and her face, for the first time in sixty years, old.

*Sing it as an elegy,* she had told him, gripping his wrist with fingers gone suddenly papery and light. *Not a contract. A contract is sung with your eyes closed and your hands on the keys, so the house can hold them. An elegy is sung with your eyes open and your hands at your sides, so nothing can hold anything. You give the song its ending. You don't give it yourself.*

*How do I know the difference?* he had asked.

She had smiled with all her new-won years showing in her face. *You'll know. Grief knows the difference. Grief has always known.*

Now he stood before the piano and did not touch it.

The fragments were all in him. Three nights of Ysolde's handwriting crawling across her diary page by candlelight—one bar per night, paid for by his voice holding the first verse open like a door held open against wind. Two verses learned whole: the first from Mrs. Vell, the second from the diary, the third from the blood score that had finally consented to be read. And the fourth—the ending, the verse Ysolde had died mid-way through, the bar line where every score in five generations simply stopped—that lived in half-memory, in the shape of a melody his sister used to hum while washing dishes, always trailing off, always turning away from its own conclusion as if it had seen something at the bottom of itself it didn't want to meet.

He had it. All of it. He could feel the complete song sitting in his chest like water finding its level.

The question was what it would cost to let it out.

---

He thought about singing it wrong on purpose.

The temptation was small and reasonable and he examined it honestly, the way you examine any cowardice: sing only three verses, close the book, walk away. Let the song stay unfinished forever. But he had watched what unfinished did. Unfinished was Ysolde dying mid-verse with the binding torn open. Unfinished was the thing in the well shaft wearing her voice, asking to be sung home. Unfinished was a wound the house kept picking at because no one would let it scar.

A song without its ending isn't silent. It's just hungry.

And besides—he understood this now, standing in the warm dead quiet of the music room—it wasn't only the house that needed the ending. It was him. He had come here carrying an urn full of river silt and a stranger's ring, and every day since, the grief had had nowhere to go. It had pooled in him like meltwater behind a dam. The funeral he'd planned had been a lie; the burial he owed hadn't happened yet. There was a verse for that. There had always been a verse for that. Five generations of Ashgroves had built a machine out of mourning and called it duty, but underneath the machinery, underneath the doors sealed from the inside and the singers who aged so a portrait could stay young, there was a true thing: someone loved someone, and someone died, and someone had to say so out loud.

That was all the song had ever been, before they made it into a contract.

He could give it back.

---

He opened the piano lid anyway—not to play, but because the score lay inside, weighted under the metronome, and he wanted the first singer's blood-strokes facing him while he sang. A witness. That was the other thing Mrs. Vell had said: *never alone in the room.* But everyone who could have witnessed was gone or fled—Brann over the ice two days ago with his crew and his truck and his name freshly carved into a door he'd refused to look at, Mrs. Vell into whatever comes after sixty borrowed years. The house was unbound. Whatever heard him now would hear him as a listener, not a keeper.

Except one.

He set the urn on top of the piano. River silt and a wedding ring, and somewhere in the silt, he had decided to believe, whatever was truly left of his sister—the part that had hummed off-key, that had bitten her nails during exams, that had written him letters she was forbidden to send and hidden them in a music room for twenty years hoping he'd find them. The letters he'd found. The stack of his own childhood letters, returned unopened, he had burned in the kitchen stove the night before, and the fire had burned green for a moment and then ordinary, and he had wept at the ordinariness of it.

"You're going to hear this," he told the urn. "However much of you is in there. You started it. You finish hearing it."

The silt said nothing. But the pipes, for the first time since he'd arrived, gave a single soft knock—like a knuckle on wood, like *yes*—and went still.

---

He began with the first verse.

Eyes open. Hands at his sides. He sang it the way Mrs. Vell had taught him and then deliberately not the way she'd taught him—eyes wide, watching the room, watching the frost ferns on the little window, watching his own breath cloud in air that was growing cold now, cold for the first time, the house spending its last warmth nowhere, hoarding nothing. His voice found the notes and the notes found the walls and the walls did not drink them. That was the difference. Every other time he had sung, the sound had seemed to sink into the plaster, taken, receipted. Now the room gave it back. The verse came off the stone and returned to him changed slightly, the way words change when you hear them spoken back, and he understood that this was what an elegy was: a song that returns.

The second verse rose out of the first the way the second verse always had, in the diary, in the pipes, in the structure of the house itself—load-bearing, foundational. Singing it, he saw the Hollow as it had been, the vision Mrs. Vell had promised and worse: every lamp lit, every door its true number, a household full of voices, children on the stairs, a woman at this very piano with ink on her fingers writing down a melody that would outlive her by seven generations. He saw what they'd had. He saw what they'd traded. He sang the verse through without flinching and the vision dissolved like breath off glass, and the room was small and cold and real again, and real was enough.

His hands wanted the keys. He noticed the wanting the way you notice a current trying to take your feet—innocent, gentle, patient. The piano sat there with its lid open like a mouth, and some old grammar in the house said *hands flat, eyes closed, belong.* He kept his arms at his sides and his eyes open and sang the third verse straight into the piano's throat without giving it his hands.

The third verse fought him.

Not loudly. Nothing in this house did anything loudly anymore. But the melody thickened as he sang it, grew syrupy, sweetened itself, tried to become the kind of tune you'd sway to, the kind you'd close your eyes for. The temperature dropped another degree and his breath plumed white and in the white breath, briefly, he saw shapes—doors multiplying in the hall above, the multiplication he had once unleashed by singing alone, the punishment architecture. But the doors didn't open. They shivered at their thresholds like dogs called off, and faded, and he understood: the house couldn't punish him anymore. It could only remember punishing him. It was running on habit, a machine spinning down, and even its threats were becoming memory.

He sang the third verse to its end. Nothing stopped him. Nothing had ever been able to stop him; that had been the lie at the center of everything, that the inheritance was compulsory. It had only ever been consent, dressed up as fate.

---

Then the pause.

Every score ended here. Every singer had stopped here. The bar line where the ink ran out, where the blood ran out, where Ysolde's voice had run out mid-word on a night in October with the well crew still forty feet above her. He stood in the pause and felt it the way you feel the edge of a diving board—not the water below, just the plank's small tremble under your heels.

In the pause, the house made its last offer.

It came gently, which was how it always came. The cold lifted. A warmth touched the back of his neck like a hand, like a mother's hand, like Ysolde's hand on the back of his neck when they were nine and he'd lost the school race and cried about it. The pipes breathed their lullaby up through the floor, complete now, waiting, and underneath it a voice that was almost hers said, without words, in the language of warmth and belonging that the Hollow spoke fluently: *you don't have to end it. Stay unfinished. Stay with us. Unfinished, you can stay forever.*

Forever. The word the house had been selling since chapter one of his life, since the first letter returned unopened, since the first lamp lit with no one paying for it. Forever was the product. Everything else—the warmth, the light, the youth, the unaging housekeeper, the portrait woman beautiful at any age she chose—forever was what all of it was *for*, and the price had always been the same: one singer per generation, paid in full, aging in the dark so the house could shine.

"Ysolde is dead," Yara said aloud, to the warmth, to the pipes, to the whole listening architecture. "You killed her with arithmetic. She sang and you spent her. And I am not going to stay in the house that ate my sister because it has learned to wear her hand on my neck."

The warmth withdrew. The pipes went silent. And in the silence, faintly, from everywhere and nowhere—from the silt, he chose to believe—a sound like a laugh, short and wet and familiar, the way his sister had laughed when a joke landed badly and she loved it anyway.

*Get on with it,* the laugh said.

He got on with it.

---

The final verse came out of him the way a splinter comes out—not easily, but completely, and with an awful relief on the far side of the pain.

He had feared he wouldn't know it. He had spent three weeks terrified that the half-memory would fail him at the threshold, that he'd stand at the bar line with his mouth open and nothing there. But the moment he leaned into it, the melody was simply *there*, whole, the way Ysolde's face was whole whenever he closed his eyes—as if the missing piece had only ever been waiting for permission. She had carried it halfway. Halfway was where twins lived; they had spent their whole childhood finishing each other's sentences, and this was the last sentence, and he finished it.

He sang it with his eyes open.

He sang it with his hands at his sides, and when the wanting came back—the deep tidal pull toward the keys, toward the closing of eyes, toward the sweet surrender of contract—he sang *through* it, gave the pull no purchase, offered the house a voice and nothing else. The notes climbed. The verse turned where every version of it had always turned away, and instead of turning away it turned *around*, the way grief turns around when you finally face it, and became something he had no word for except *homecoming*. It was a lullaby. It had always been a lullaby. Somewhere under five generations of binding and bargaining and blood, the first singer had written a song to put something to sleep—not to imprison it, he realized, not to bind it, but to soothe it, to sing a frightened thing down into rest—and everyone after her had mistaken the cradle for a cage and moved in.

He sang the verse as a cradle.

He sang it for Lissandre, a century dying slowly behind paint. He sang it for Mrs. Vell, who had traded her memories for safety and gotten neither. He sang it for the first singer whose fingers were still on the keys in the innermost dark. He sang it for Brann, who had run rather than be carved, and who would carry the humming south with him and have to decide, every night for the rest of his life, whether to pass it on. He sang it for the seven names on the seven doors and the eighth name that would never be carved now.

And last, longest, quietest, he sang it for Ysolde.

For the sister who had known about the music room and never told him, because telling him would have made it his. For the sister who had died mid-verse so that the ending would exist for someone to find. For the sister whose ashes were river silt, whose ring belonged to a stranger, whose body the house had spent down to sediment keeping its lamps lit—and who had still, from inside whatever the house had left of her, crawled back bar by bar to hand him the way out.

The last note was low. It was the note the plumbing had always stopped short of, the note under the floor of every melody the house had ever hummed, and when he reached it he did not hold it long. He let it go the way you set down something heavy at the top of a stair—carefully, and then all at once.

The note ended.

The song was finished.

---

For a moment, nothing. The music room stood around him, cold and plain and dustless no longer—he saw, with strange tenderness, that dust had begun to settle on the piano's shoulder, fine and gray and ordinary, the first dust the room had allowed itself in a hundred and thirty years.

Then, above him, through the floors, through the cellar wall, through the whole stacked architecture of the Hollow, something began to happen that he had no choice but to follow.

The lights were going out.

Not failing—*finishing*. Lamp by lamp, room by room, in an order that felt less like collapse than like a housekeeper walking the halls at the end of a long party, dousing each flame with a fingertip and a murmured goodnight. He heard it more than saw it: the faint electric sigh of filaments cooling, the tick of brass cooling in their fittings, a darkness moving through the house above him floor by floor like a tide going out.

Yara picked up the urn. It was lighter than it had been—he was nearly sure of it, though he knew how impossible that was—and the wedding ring inside it had gone to dull gray metal, its stone fallen to common glass, its work done.

"Well," he said to it, because his sister had hated sentiment and loved understatement. "That's that."

Above him, the last lamp in the east hall—the one that had burned for decades with no one paying for it—guttered once, twice, and went out, and in the sudden total dark of Ashgrove Hollow, Yara Ashgrove stood holding his sister's remains and listened to the house begin, at last, to count its doors.
