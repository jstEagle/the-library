## 1. Dead Air

The studio at 2 a.m. is a small warm country with one citizen.

Ines Galloway knew every inch of it the way a lighthouse keeper knows his rock: the foam padding gone amber at the corners where decades of hands had leaned, the mixing board's faders worn to a shine like river stones, the coffee mug—chipped, black, printed with a station logo from before he was born—that had not been washed since 2019 and had achieved, he suspected, a kind of geological permanence. Outside the glass of the control room window, the hallway lights were off. Outside the building, Halloway Bend lay under its first real snow of the season, three inches of it, blue-white under the streetlamps, muffling the town so completely that when Ines cracked the exterior door for his smoke break at half past one, the silence had weight. It pressed on his eardrums like water.

*The Night Desk*, his show was called. Two until five, six nights a week, on WQBR, 1340 AM, five hundred watts of signal that reached, on clear nights, all the way to the county line and maybe past it. The format was simple because the hour demanded simplicity: soft music, local news read slow, and then, from two o'clock, the phones open. *You're up late,* he'd say. *So am I. Tell me what's keeping you.*

It was a good show for a town like this. Halloway Bend had four hundred souls in winter and a long tradition of insomnia. Farmers whose bodies still ran on schedules their farms no longer kept. Widowers. Nurses coming off shifts. Kids driving the loop between the gas station and the reservoir because there was nothing else to drive. They called about their hips, their dreams, their dead husbands' cardigans that they couldn't give away. Ines listened. That was most of the job, listening, and he was good at it because his grandmother had trained him in it without ever meaning to—had sat him on her kitchen counter as a boy and taught him that a person telling you something true will always pause in the same place, just before the hard part, like a swimmer at the edge of cold water.

The red ON AIR light burned above the door. Ines leaned into the microphone.

"You're listening to The Night Desk on 1340. It is—" he checked the clock, an old habit even though the clock was digital and always right, "—2:04 in the morning, and it is snowing, properly snowing, the kind that means business. Lines are open. If you're awake, you're already one of us."

He cued up a song—something old and unhurried, a woman's voice over brushed drums—and watched the phone board while it played. Line one lit almost immediately. He let the song run halfway through, then took the call.

"Night Desk, you're live."

Static first. Not the clean static of a bad connection but something denser, wetter, like wind moving through bare trees. Then a voice, and the voice was a woman's, middle-aged or older, with the flattened vowels of the county.

"Is this the radio man?"

"It is. Who have I got?"

A pause. He heard breathing, slow and careful, the way people breathe when they're holding themselves together by counting.

"My name's Delia Marsh," she said. "I live out past the grange hall. The white house with the green door."

"I know it," Ines said, and he did; everyone knew the Marsh place; old Amos Marsh had kept bees there until the year the bees left all at once, which was a story Ines had never gotten to the bottom of. "What's keeping you up tonight, Delia?"

Another pause. Longer. He recognized it—the swimmer's pause, the edge of the cold water—and he settled back in his chair and waited, because rushing people at this hour was a sin against the whole profession.

"I went to a funeral last Tuesday," she said.

"That's a hard thing. I'm sorry."

"No," she said. "No, you're not understanding me." Her voice didn't rise. If anything it got quieter, more precise, the way voices do when they're carrying something fragile across a long distance. "I want to tell you about the funeral, and I want you to listen, and then I want you to tell me if I'm crazy. Because my daughter says I'm crazy, and the doctor says grief does strange things, and I have been sitting in my kitchen for six days trying to decide which of them is lying to me."

"Take your time," Ines said. "I'm not going anywhere."

"The funeral was at the Methodist church," Delia Marsh said. "Reverend Okafor did the service. There were maybe forty people. The flowers were white chrysanthemums and some dried things somebody brought from a garden, lavender, I think, because the smell of them got into everything. The casket was oak. Not veneer—real oak, because the family paid extra, I remember the funeral director saying so to the son in the vestibule, thinking nobody could hear. There was a picture of her at the front, framed in silver, from maybe twenty years ago, when her hair was still dark. She's wearing a blue dress in the photograph. Somebody chose that photograph who loved her, because it was a good one. She looked kind in it."

Ines's hand had stopped moving. He hadn't noticed it stop. He'd been reaching for his coffee and now the mug sat untouched, steam rising off it in a thin gray thread that bent toward the microphone as though it too wanted to listen.

"Go on," he said.

"The church was cold," she said. "They always keep it cold. And the pews are the old ones, the ones that bite you in the back if you sit wrong, and I sat in the third row on the aisle because I'm family-adjacent, you understand, I'm not family, but I've known her forty years. Forty-one. The organist played 'Abide With Me' and got the tempo wrong, too fast, and everybody sang anyway, and it came out sounding like marching, and I thought, well, she'd have laughed at that. She hated a dirge."

The static on the line breathed in and out. Ines realized he was leaning forward, both elbows on the board, the way he never let himself lean, because leaning meant you'd lost the professional distance that made people able to say things at 2 a.m. that they couldn't say at noon.

"Delia," he said gently, "whose funeral was it?"

And here was the thing—the thing he would turn over and over in the days afterward, the detail that would make him dig out his grandmother's notebooks and sit under the attic bulb until dawn. When she answered, there was no hesitation at all. No dramatic beat, no theatrical reveal. She said it the way you'd say your own address.

"Mine," she said. "It was mine. I was in the casket, Mr. Galloway. I was in the oak casket in the blue dress they picked out, and I could hear everything, and I couldn't say a word of it, and then Tuesday last I woke up in my own bed with the sheets pulled up neat, and my daughter found me standing in the kitchen at dawn making coffee, and she screamed, and now everyone says I'm confused, and I am not confused. I am the only person in this town who isn't."

For a moment Ines said nothing, and the silence went out over the airwaves of Halloway Bend like a held breath.

Prank, he thought. The word arrived promptly, wearing its uniform, doing its job. Prank. It was nearly Halloween week; college kids home for break liked to call; there had been the incident two years ago with the man claiming to be trapped in the grain elevator. He ran the checklist automatically, the way his grandmother had taught him to check a story: details, motive, consistency. And the details were—God help him—the details were exact. Reverend Okafor had indeed been at the Methodist church last Tuesday; Ines had seen the notice in the weekly paper, a name he hadn't recognized, a service at eleven. White chrysanthemums were what the flower shop in Corning stocked in November because they were cheap and lasted. Oak caskets, real oak, cost extra; anyone local would know that. But the organist's tempo, the lavender, the photograph in the silver frame—

Anyone could invent those. That was the trouble with exact details. They proved nothing except that someone had taken care.

"Delia," he said, and kept his voice level, warm, the broadcaster's voice, the one that lived in a separate room from whatever his chest was starting to do. "Tell me the name on the notice. The name in the paper."

There was a silence on the line that had texture to it, like felt.

"You know I can't say it," she said. "That's the whole point. That's why I'm calling you instead of anybody else. You put names on the air, Mr. Galloway. You say them out loud and they stay said. I need somebody to say mine."

"Try me."

"Say it and see what happens," she said, and for the first time her voice cracked—not with fear, he thought, but with something worse, something like hope. "That's what I keep coming back to. Say it and see. Because I've said it to my daughter and she cried and hugged me and called me Mama and then wouldn't look at me the rest of the day. I've said it to Doctor Pruitt and he wrote a prescription. I've stood in the cemetery and said it at my own headstone and the ground didn't so much as shiver. But the radio goes everywhere. The radio goes into every kitchen in this county. So I'm asking you, radio man. Say my name on the air and we'll both find out what I am."

Ines looked at the ON AIR light. Red, steady, faithful. Five hundred watts. Every kitchen, every truck cab, every nightstand with a clock radio glowing its green numbers into the dark. Somewhere out there, forty people had sat in biting pews last Tuesday and sung "Abide With Me" too fast.

He thought about his grandmother again, suddenly and completely, the way you think of a person when the house makes a sound. *A person telling you something true pauses before the hard part.* Delia Marsh had paused twice, and both times it had been before the truth, not after it. Liars paused after.

"Here's what I'll do," he said. "I'll take a break. I'll play a record. And when I come back, if you're still on the line, we'll talk it through—slowly, all of it, every detail. Fair?"

"Fair," she said, and the word came out relieved and broken at once, the word of a woman who had been waiting six days for someone to stop telling her she was grieving and start telling her what she was.

He queued the record without looking at what it was—an instrumental, piano, rain-slow—and hit the mute on the caller channel. For ninety seconds he sat in the little warm country of the studio and let himself feel what he was feeling, which was cold. Not dread exactly, not yet; dread was a luxury that required belief. This was older than dread. This was the feeling of standing in his grandmother's kitchen at seven years old and watching her go silent mid-sentence, staring past him at the window, at the orchard, at nothing, for a full minute, and then turning back and saying, brightly, *Now, where were we?* as if the minute had never happened. As if the thing at the window had agreed to wait.

The record ended. He unmuted the line.

"Delia? Still there?"

"Yes." Closer to the phone now, he thought. Or closer to him. Hard to say with sound.

"Walk me through Tuesday morning," he said. "Before the service. What happened?"

"I woke up dead," she said simply. "That's the only way to put it. I woke up and I knew it the way you know you've got a fever—everything was the same and everything was different. My hands worked. The light came through the curtains the way it always does, that gray November light. But there was a quiet in the house that wasn't the regular quiet. It was—" she searched, and he waited, swimmer's edge, swimmer's edge— "it was respectful. Like the house was in mourning. Like the house knew before I did."

"And then?"

"Then I got dressed," she said, "and I went to my own funeral, and nobody saw me, and I stood at the back next to the coat rack, and I listened to Reverend Okafor talk about me, and he got most of it right, and then—" 

She stopped. Not the swimmer's pause. Something else. Something that sounded, Ines thought, like a person listening to a second voice underneath their own.

"Delia?"

"He said my name," she whispered. "At the graveside. He said, *we commit her body to the ground.* And when he said it, all forty of them bowed their heads, and I felt it. I felt every single head bowing, like a wind going through me. Like being counted." A breath. "Mr. Galloway, have you ever been counted?"

"I don't—"

"Because that's what it was. Being counted. And then it was over, and everybody drove away, and I walked home along the road in my good coat, and the snow started, and I thought, well. Well. Now what."

The studio around Ines had gone very still. He was aware of absurd specifics: the coffee mug's steam had stopped; the VU meter needles rested at zero even though her voice was coming through loud; somewhere down the hall a radiator ticked like a patient clock. He should cut to break. He should screen this, should have screened it, should be doing a dozen professional things, and instead he heard himself ask the question he'd been circling for ten minutes:

"Delia. Whose name was in the paper? Last Tuesday. Just tell me that."

"Yours isn't the only show in town," she said, "but yours is the only one that listens. That's what my husband used to say about you. He's buried up at Fairview, third row, under the cedar. You did his memorial announcement in 2019. You said his name right. Nobody says Hollis names right anymore, but you said his right."

The word went through Ines like a needle finding an old scar. *Hollis.* His grandmother's kitchen had a window facing the orchard road, and beyond the orchard road there had been a property, once, that everyone called by no name at all—the place with the dead apple trees, the capped well, the field that grew thistles taller than a fence. He had asked about it exactly once, as a boy, and his grandmother had gone to the window and been silent for a full minute, and come back and said, *Now, where were we?*

"Delia," he said carefully, "what does the Hollis place have to do with any of this?"

The static swelled. For a moment it wasn't static at all—it was many voices, far off, layered, murmuring the way a congregation murmurs, and underneath the murmur something that might have been his own call sign, his own voice, played backward and slowed.

Then it was just one voice again, tired and gentle.

"Put it back," Delia Marsh said.

"Put what back?"

"Put it back," she repeated, with terrible patience, the patience of someone reciting instructions given to her by something larger. "That's all we're asking. That's all any of us are asking. Put it back, and we can rest, and you can sleep, and the snow can be only snow." A pause, and then, softer, almost kindly: "You don't know yet. That's all right. You'll find it. It's in your grandmother's house, and it has always been in your grandmother's house, and we will call every night until you find it, Mr. Galloway, because you're the one who puts names on the air, and names are the only coin we have left."

The line clicked dead. Not faded—clicked, like a lamp going out.

Ines sat in the red light of the ON AIR sign with his hand still resting on the fader, and outside the glass the hallway stayed dark, and outside the building the snow fell on Halloway Bend with that weighted, listening silence, and the clock said 2:31, and the phone board lit up again—line two, line three, line four, all at once, softly, like candles being carried into a room.

He looked at them for a long moment.

Then, because he was his grandmother's grandson, and because the alternative was to get in his car and drive until the tank was empty, Ines Galloway leaned into the microphone and said, in his steadiest voice, "Night Desk. You're live. Who have I got?"
