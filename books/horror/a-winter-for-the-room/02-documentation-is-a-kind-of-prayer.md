## 2. Documentation Is a Kind of Prayer

My grandmother kept notebooks the way other people kept saints' medals—on her person, always, close to the skin of the household. They lived in a wooden crate beneath the hall table, and every one of them was the same: a school composition book, marbled covers gone soft at the corners, the pages filled in her small, upright hand. She wrote down everything. Telephone calls. Weather. What the doctor said about my grandfather's heart, and what my grandfather said about the doctor, which was less charitable. The exact wording of apologies. The exact wording of insults. She believed, or said she believed, that a thing written down could not be unsaid, could not be smoothed over by memory's slow thumb, could not be denied later by the person who'd said it or by the person who'd heard it.

*Documentation is a kind of prayer,* she told me once, when I was nine or ten and asked why she bothered, why she didn't just live her life instead of writing it. She was at the hall table with her fountain pen, and the light through the window made her look like a photograph of herself. *You say the words twice,* she said. *Once to the air, and once to the page. And the page keeps them.*

I thought of her that morning, the morning after the first call, standing in my kitchen with a composition book I'd bought at the pharmacy on the way home from the station. I hadn't planned to buy it. My hand had simply reached for it off the rack, the way a hand reaches for a light switch in a familiar dark room. Marbled cover. Blue-lined pages. I stood at my counter with coffee going cold beside me and wrote across the top of the first page, in block capitals because my own handwriting felt too intimate for what I was about to do:

**CALL LOG — OCTOBER — 2 A.M. SHOW**

And underneath, in smaller letters, because I couldn't help myself, because the habit was in my blood the way the radio voice was in my blood:

*Caller 1. Female. Elderly or affecting elderly. States name as Margaret Ellery. States that she attended her own funeral "last Tuesday." Describes: the church, the lilies, the coat her sister wore, the hymn (Abide With Me, sung too fast). Ends: "put it back."*

I read it over three times. Then I did what my grandmother would have done, what she had trained into me across a hundred drowsy childhood mornings when I'd answer the phone for her and she'd stand there waiting until I repeated the message back word for word: I said it aloud, to the kitchen, to the air.

"Put it back."

The refrigerator hummed. The radiator ticked. Outside, the first hard frost of the season was silvering the grass, and the light came through the window the color of old ice. Nothing answered me. Nothing had to. The words were on the page now, and the page keeps them.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the only thing I have left to offer anyone, and I learned that late: I did not believe, that first morning, that Margaret Ellery had been dead when she called. I believed I had been pranked. I believed it the way you believe the floor will hold you—not as a thought, but as a condition of standing upright. A prank. Someone in town with a scanner and a cruel streak and a good ear for the way old women say *lilies*. It happens. It had happened to me before, lesser versions of it—teenagers breathing into the line, a man once who claimed to be my dead grandfather and got the man's name wrong. You develop a callus. You screen, you deflect, you move to the next caller with your voice smooth as poured cream, and afterward you go home and drink a beer and forget.

But I transcribed it anyway. That's the part I couldn't explain, even to myself, until later. If it was a prank, why write it down? Why do it in her notebook, in her format, in her block capitals? Why stand in my kitchen saying the words aloud a second time, once to the air and once to the page?

Because the ritual was the only handhold I had. Because the call had frightened me in a way that lived below thought, in the body, in the same place where you know to flinch from a hot stove, and when the body is frightened the hands reach for what they know. My hands knew the composition book. My hands knew the transcription. My hands had been doing it, in one form or another, since I was nine years old.

So I wrote it down. And the page kept it.

* * *

The next night's show was the longest two hours of my life, and I have since had considerably longer ones, so I say that with the authority of hindsight.

I did everything right. That's what I kept telling myself, all through the broadcast, like a man reassuring himself the rope is tied to something. I opened with the weather and the birthday announcements the way I always did. I played the first hour of requests—Patsy Cline, some Roy Orbison, a gospel number an old man calls in for every single week and has called in for so long that his request is less a song than a heartbeat, a proof of continued existence. *This one's for Harold on the Mill Road,* I said, *who tells me every year that he's been listening since before I was born, and who is, I suspect, lying, but lying in a way I respect.* The lines stayed quiet. The board stayed dark. It was, by the standards of the show, a dead night.

I remember thinking, with something that was almost relief: *good.* Let it be over. Let it have been one call, one prank, one cruel joke that would fade by the weekend like frost off a windshield.

Then, at 2:14, line three lit up.

I should describe, for those who have never sat before a board, what a call actually is, technically. It's a light. It's a switch. It's a number in a queue. It is, in every measurable sense, a machine doing what machines do. But when line three lit up that night, I felt it the way you feel a hand settle on the back of your neck—gently, without malice, but without asking.

I let it ring through twice. Professional habit. Then I opened the line, and I said, "You're on the air with Ines Galloway, it's two-fourteen in the morning, who's this?"

"It's Walter," the voice said. "Walter Pruitt. Out past the feed store."

I knew Walter Pruitt. Everyone in Halden County knew Walter Pruitt, or had known him, which is the sentence I want you to notice. He'd run the feed store for thirty years, then his son had taken it over, and Walter had become one of those men who materialize at the co-op and the diner and the church basement, telling the same stories with diminishing detail. He had a voice like gravel poured slowly, and he had died—this was the thing, this was the fact I was holding at arm's length with both hands—Walter Pruitt had died eleven days ago. Heart, in his truck, in the parking lot of the IGA. I had read his obituary on air. I had read it in my radio voice, the smooth one, the cream-poured one, and I had said *he'll be missed by all who knew him,* which is the sentence we use when we don't know what else to say about a man.

"Walter," I said. My voice did something on the way out—cracked, or caught, or both. I smoothed it. "We—huh. We ran your obituary a while back. Nice turnout, I heard."

"I was there for it," Walter said, agreeably. "Cold day. They had me in my good suit. The one with the brown checks." He paused, and I heard him shift, the creak of a chair, and the sound was so ordinary, so *furniturized*, that my hands went cold on the board. "You played my song the other night. The Orbison."

"I play it most weeks."

"You do. I liked it." Another creak. "Ines, I don't want to take up your time. I know you got a show. I just wanted to say it was a good run. Fifty-one years on that road, and I never once minded the winters. You tell people that. You write it down, if you're the writing kind. I never once minded."

"Write it down," I repeated. My mouth was very dry. "Walter, are you—"

But the line had gone to the soft hiss of an open connection with nobody in it, and then the light on line three went out, and the board was dark, and the song that followed my voice out of the speakers was Roy Orbison again because the automation didn't care what hour it was or what was sitting in the studio with me.

I didn't say anything else on air for twenty minutes. I played music and I stared at the board and I did the thing where you press your thumbnail into your finger to confirm you're awake. Then, because the alternative was to sit there dissolving, I pulled the composition book out of the desk drawer—yes, I'd brought it to the station; yes, I'd already started carrying it the way she used to—and I wrote:

*Caller 2. Male. Walter Pruitt. Deceased 11 days (heart, IGA parking lot — obit ran 10/3). Describes his own funeral, his good suit, brown checks. Says he "never once minded the winters." Asks me to write it down. Does not say "put it back."*

I looked at that last line for a long time. The absence of it. And then, because my grandmother's training ran deeper than fear, I said it aloud to the empty studio, once to the air: "He never once minded the winters."

The page kept it.

* * *

By the fourth night I had stopped pretending to myself that I was transcribing evidence for any practical purpose. I wasn't going to the police with a composition book. I wasn't going to a priest, because Halden County's priest was eighty-one and I loved him and I could not do that to him. I was transcribing for the same reason my grandmother had transcribed everything for sixty years: because when the world stops making sense, the notebook is the one thing that still does. The words go in. The page keeps them. Two plus two. The frost on the grass. The light at 2 a.m. that is the same color as the light at 2 a.m. has always been. As long as I was writing, some part of me was still a person to whom things could be written down.

Caller three came on the fifth night. Her name was Delia Marsh, and she had been my fourth-grade teacher.

I want you to understand what that did to me. Margaret Ellery I had known only as a name on the prayer list in the church bulletin. Walter Pruitt I had known as a fixture, a voice, a man at a distance. But Delia Marsh had taught me cursive. Delia Marsh had kept me after school one February because she'd noticed—I never learned how—that my coat had a broken zipper, and she'd fixed it herself with pliers and a paperclip, and she'd never once mentioned it, and I had thought about that paperclip for thirty years without knowing I was thinking about it until that moment, sitting in the studio with her voice in my headphones.

"Ines Galloway," she said, and she said it the way she used to take attendance, a little amused, a little formal, as though my name were a small pleasant surprise. "You always did have a good radio voice. I told your grandmother that. I told her, that boy reads aloud like the words are glad to be in his mouth."

"Miss Marsh." My hand had found the composition book without my telling it to. The pen was already moving. "How—"

"Hush now, I only have a minute, and I want to spend it right." I heard her settle, that particular creak, the same creak Walter had made, and some cold part of my mind noted it: *the same chair. They're all sitting in the same chair.* "I wanted to tell you I heard the show. All of it. Every night since—" she paused, and the pause had a shape, and I didn't ask it to define itself. "You're doing fine, Ines. You're doing better than fine. You listen. That's rarer than people think. Most folks just wait for their turn to talk."

"Miss Marsh, I have to ask—"

"You don't have to ask anything." And her voice gentled further, if gentling was possible, until it was barely a voice at all, more a warmth in the headphones, a hand on the back of a nine-year-old's neck, straightening his collar. "You just keep your book. Keep your book and keep your voice. That's all anybody's ever asked of you, and you'll know when they ask for more."

"Put it back?" I said. I don't know why I said it. It was the phrase from the first call, the phrase I'd written at the top of the second page and underlined twice, and it came out of me the way a question comes out of you in a dream, where you already know the answer.

But Delia Marsh didn't say yes. She made a sound that might have been a sigh, if sighs could be fond. "Nobody's angry with you, Ines. I want that written down too. Write that nobody is angry. That's the part everybody gets wrong in the stories." Another pause, and this one was longer, and I could hear something underneath it—not static, not breathing, just a kind of depth, the way a well has depth. "We just want the room back. That's all. We want the room."

The line went soft. The light went out.

*We want the room,* I wrote, and my pen pressed so hard it nearly tore the page. *Caller 3. Delia Marsh. My fourth-grade teacher. Deceased 2 years (I attended the funeral; I read the obituary; I wore the brown coat with the broken zipper I have never gotten fixed). Says nobody is angry. Says "we just want the room back." Did not say "put it back." But I asked her, and she did not say no.*

I sat in the dark studio after the show, with the board dark and the automation murmuring its 4 a.m. gospel hour to an audience of insomniacs and truckers and the dead, and I read back over the three entries. Margaret Ellery: *put it back.* Walter Pruitt: *write it down.* Delia Marsh: *we want the room.*

Three dead voices. Three gentle ones. That was the thing I kept circling back to, the thing I couldn't make fit any shape of haunting I'd ever heard of: none of them were angry. If the dead of Halden County were going to come back through the phone lines of a 2 a.m. radio show, you'd think—wouldn't you?—that some of them would have grievances. Some of them would have scores. Walter Pruitt's brother-in-law had cheated him out of six acres in 1987 and everyone knew it. Delia Marsh had been passed over for the teaching award three times by a principal who took credit for her curriculum. Where was the anger? Where was the reckoning?

Instead there was only this terrible mildness. This patience. They spoke to me the way you speak to a child who has done something wrong without knowing it—no blame, no heat, just the gentle, awful courtesy of people waiting for you to figure it out yourself. *Put it back. Write it down. We want the room.* As if I were a boy who had borrowed something and forgotten to return it, and the whole dead county was standing in the doorway of my life, hands folded, not accusing, just *waiting*, and the waiting was worse than any accusation could have been.

I said the entries aloud before I closed the book. All three. Once to the air, once to the page, once to the dark studio, because by then I was doing it three times, because the ritual was growing the way rituals do, adding a bead here, a verse there. *Put it back. He never once minded the winters. We just want the room.* The words hung in the studio's dead air, in the acoustic foam and the red ON AIR light and the smell of old carpet and cold coffee.

And then I noticed something in the book. I had noticed it, I suppose, the way you notice a smell before you can name it—subconsciously, over several days, a wrongness accumulating below the threshold of attention. I flipped back to the first entry. Margaret Ellery. Then Walter. Then Delia. Three entries, three nights, my block capitals marching down the blue-lined pages.

The handwriting was changing.

Not much. Not enough that anyone else would see it. But the letters were leaning, very slightly, to the right. The capitals were getting smaller, more upright, more *particular*. The pen pressure was lighter, the strokes finer, and there—on the third page, in the middle of *we just want the room back*—there was a small flourish on the letter *r* that I have never in my life made. A little loop, like a bird's foot. Like a signature.

It was my grandmother's handwriting. It was coming into my hand the way a tide comes into a harbor, an inch a night, patient, unhurried, certain of the shore.

I closed the book. I sat with my palms flat on the cover, feeling the marbled cardboard, cool and slightly damp, as though the notebook had been sitting outside in the frost. And in the silence of the studio, in the dead air between songs, I heard—very faintly, very far down, the way you hear water under a bridge—the automation feed stutter, and for half a second, under the gospel hour, under the hymn, I heard a chair creak.

The same chair.

I did not sleep that day. I sat at my kitchen table as the sun came up silver and hard over the frost, and I took my grandmother's photograph albums down from the shelf in the hall—her albums, the ones I'd inherited with the house and never once opened, because grief is a door you prop shut and walk past every day—and I carried them to the table, and I set them down in the cold morning light, and I reached for the first one.

Because if something was writing through my hand, I wanted to know what else of hers I had inherited. And because Delia Marsh had said *you'll know when they ask for more,* and I could feel it coming, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. Something in this house knew more than I did. Something in these pages had been waiting for me to open them since 1974.

The album cover was green, cracked like old paint, with *FAMILY* in gold letters half flaked away. I opened it. The first photographs were from the fifties: my grandparents young and squinting in front of a car, my mother as a child on a porch step, a dog mid-leap after a ball that had been thrown just out of frame. Ordinary. Sunlit. Safe.

I turned the pages toward the back, toward the seventies, toward the winter I'd been told about only in fragments—*the hard one, the one we don't talk much about, the one your grandfather nearly didn't—*

And there, in a photograph dated August 1974, in my grandparents' kitchen, at a table I knew, in a room I knew, stood a wooden chair.

A chair that did not belong to my family. A chair I had never seen in that kitchen, in any photograph, in any memory. Unoccupied. Plain-backed. Facing the camera.

And closer to the lens than anything else in the room.
