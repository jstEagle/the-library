## 6. What Grandma Kept

The attic stairs were behind a door my mother had painted shut sometime before I was born, and I knew this the way you know things about houses that have nothing to do with logic—by the way the hallway narrowed as I approached it, by the way the light from the window fell wrong there, pooling in a shape like a held breath. The paint had been white once. Now it was the color of old teeth.

I stood in front of it for a long time with the crowbar I'd taken from the garage, and I thought about how my grandmother had died in this house eleven years ago, in her bed, on a night in December so cold the pipes burst one after another down through the walls like small arms breaking. I thought about how she'd spent her last week asking to be carried up those stairs, and how my mother had refused, and how Grandma had smiled at that refusal—not bitterly, not sadly, but with something I'd only now begun to understand was relief. As if the attic staying closed was part of some arrangement. As if she'd signed something too.

"Put it back," Ruthie had said, and the other callers after her, each voice softer than the last, each one ending the same way, the way you end a prayer or a request you've made so many times it has worn smooth as river stone.

I set the crowbar's flat edge against the seam of the door.

The house did not want me to do this. I want to be clear about that. The house resisted in the way old houses resist—not creaking dramatically, not groaning in any way a horror movie would recognize, but in the smaller language of refusal: the air thickening, the hallway light dimming by degrees, the floorboards under my feet going strangely warm, like something beneath them had turned over in its sleep. I swung anyway. The paint cracked. The door gave all at once, easier than it should have, swinging inward into darkness that smelled of cedar and dust and, underneath both, faintly and impossibly, of well water.

Cold air came down the stairwell and touched my face like fingers.

* * *

The attic was bigger than the house should have allowed.

That's the only way I can put it. From outside, from the street, the Galloway house is a modest two-story with a shallow roof pitch—you could stand in our driveway and see that there couldn't be more than four feet of headroom up top. But when I climbed the last stair and straightened, my head cleared the rafters by a full foot, and the space ran back farther than the footprint of the house, farther than the lot line, into a dimness where the single bulb at the top of the stairs made no difference at all. I told myself it was an optical illusion. I told myself a lot of things that night.

Boxes everywhere. Trunks. Furniture under sheets, sheeted shapes standing around the edges of the room like patient relatives waiting their turn to speak. And dust—dust in a way that felt deliberate, laid down over everything in a fine gray snowfall, undisturbed, except—

Except for a path.

A narrow path, maybe two feet wide, swept clean by decades of someone's walking, running from the top of the stairs straight back into the dark, between the sheeted furniture, to a low trunk at the far wall. The dust on either side of it rose in soft drifts against the boxes like snow against a fence. Someone had walked this path often. Recently. My grandmother had been dead eleven years.

I walked the path because the alternative was standing at the top of the stairs forever, and because some part of me—the part that had transcribed three dead voices into a notebook in my own careful hand—understood already that I was expected. That this was less a search than an appointment.

The trunk was cedar, iron-banded, unlocked. Of course it was unlocked. A lock would have meant my grandmother believed in doors, and I was beginning to understand that she had believed in them the way you believe in a dam: knowing full well what's behind it.

Inside: winter clothes gone gray with age. A christening gown folded with terrible care. A shoebox of Christmas cards tied with ribbon. And beneath all of it, wrapped in a flour sack discolored with damp, a book.

It was a ledger. Not a farm ledger, not a store ledger—those have columns, printed lines, a merchant's tidy soul. This was a plain bound book, cloth-covered, the cloth gone soft as skin at the corners, and inside it was nothing but names. Names in ink, in pencil, in a child's careful capitals, dozens of them, page after page, and every single name crossed out. Struck through with a single firm horizontal line, the same pressure, the same angle, the same hand. My grandmother's hand. I knew it from the recipe cards she left me, from the birthday cards she never sent but always wrote. She had crossed out every name in the book with the steadiness of someone performing a duty, and the dates beside each struck name ran backward through the decades—1961, 1953, 1948, 1937—until I reached the pages near the end and the handwriting changed.

Dated December 1974.

Here the entries stopped being just names. Here they had annotations, small and clinical, in my grandmother's tight script: *paid in full*. *agreed terms met*. *witnessed*. One entry read simply: *the girl, delivered Dec 21, terms honored, all parties satisfied.* All parties. I read that phrase four times before my eyes would move on, and each time the word *delivered* sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

And then the last written page—and the tear.

Someone had torn out the next leaf carefully, close to the spine, leaving only a ragged edge and, caught in the gutter of the binding, a fragment: the tail of a name, *—ollis*, and below it, half a letter, the beginning of a capital G. My grandmother's initial. Her signature, witnessing something, torn away.

I sat down on the cold floorboards with the ledger open across my knees, and the attic breathed around me, and somewhere below me—I want to say below, though the sound seemed to come from everywhere at once, from inside the walls, from inside my own ears—I heard water. Slow. Deep. Rising.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for my legs to go numb. Long enough for the light at the top of the stairs to change from night-dark to the darker dark of very late night, the hour my show used to fill, the hour the dead called in.

I brought the ledger down with me. It seemed important not to leave it up there, in the path, in the clean-swept lane that led to it like an aisle. I carried it against my chest the way you carry something that might wake.

In the kitchen I set it on the table and turned on every light in the room, and then, because that wasn't enough, I turned on the television with the volume muted, just to have its blue flicker moving on the walls. I made coffee I didn't drink. I opened my notebook—the transcription notebook, the ritual notebook, the one habit I kept from her—and I began to copy the ledger into it, name by struck-out name, date by date, because documentation is a kind of prayer, because if I wrote it down in my own hand then it belonged to the world of things that can be written down, and things that can be written down can be survived.

I was on the fourth page when I noticed the photographs.

I'd left the digitized scans open on my laptop from the night before—the albums, the family record, the images where the chair first appeared. I hadn't looked at them since I found it. Now, glancing up from the ledger, I saw the screen and froze.

In the earliest photo—Easter, 1975, my mother aged six on the front lawn—the chair stood at the edge of the frame, half-cropped, wood pale as bone. In the next, Thanksgiving 1981, it had moved to the middle distance, standing in the yard behind my grandmother's shoulder. By the nineties it was indoors, in the corner of the kitchen in shot after shot, unoccupied, patient. In the photo from my high school graduation it stood directly behind my seated figure, close enough that its armrest nearly touched my shoulder.

I had noticed all of this before. What I had not done—what I had somehow, without deciding to, avoided doing—was look at the most recent folder. The folder labeled THIS MORNING, because I had photographed the kitchen yesterday on a whim, testing the new scanner, and the file had synced to the cloud while I slept.

There were three photos in it. I had taken them seconds apart, panning across the empty kitchen: counter, window, table.

In the third photo, at my kitchen table, in the exact spot where I take my meals, where I write my scripts, where I sat copying dead names into a notebook at this very moment—there was a chair.

Wooden. Plain-backed. Pale as bone. Unoccupied.

I looked up from the screen at the actual table, the real one, the physical one, expecting of course to see nothing, expecting the relief of ordinary air.

The chair was there.

Not reflected. Not rendered. There, in the room, under the kitchen light, casting a shadow on the linoleum, close enough to touch. It had not been there when I came downstairs. I would swear that on anything you asked me to swear on. And yet here it sat, drawn up neatly to my table as if invited, as if it had always been coming and had merely arrived at the hour appointed for it, and the worst of it—the detail that took the last of my composure and ground it fine—was that it fit. Its height matched my table exactly. Its wood was the same honeyed oak. It looked like it had been built for this room, for this spot, for me, the way a key looks like it was cut for a lock.

My phone rang. The studio line, forwarded, the number only listeners had.

I let it ring four times. Then I answered, because answering is what I do, because the microphone of my life has always been live even when no one was broadcasting, and because some ruined, faithful part of me still believed that if I listened—if I truly listened—there might be mercy in the hearing.

Static first. Then water, faintly, the sound of deep slow rising. Then a voice—not Ruthie's, older, a man's, frayed at the edges like cloth rubbed thin.

"You found the book," he said. He sounded tired in the way the dead are tired, which is to say completely, permanently, without hope of rest. "Good boy. Good. Now count."

"Count what?" My voice came out small in the big lit kitchen.

"The names. Count them out loud. We'll wait." A pause, and then, gently, the gentleness that frightened me worse than anger ever could: "We've waited fifty years, Ines. We can wait while you count."

I looked at the ledger. At the notebook. At the chair, drawn up to my table, waiting its turn.

"There's a page missing," I said.

The static changed. All around me, in the walls, under the floor, in the well-deep dark beyond the windows where the frost was hardening on the glass, I heard it—the sound of many voices drawing breath at once, a sound like the tide pulling back, like the whole drowned congregation of the town inhaling together.

"We know," the dead man said. "Find who kept it. Mothers keep things. It's the one sin we forgive them."

The line went dead. The refrigerator hummed. Snow had begun to fall outside the window, fat and silent, filling the yard, erasing the paths.

And the chair sat at my table, patient as inheritance, and I understood at last what my grandmother had left me—not the house, not the recipes, not the radio voice—but the debt. The debt and the chair to sit in while I paid it.

I picked up the phone again and dialed my mother. It rang, and rang, and rang.

On the ninth ring, she picked up, and before I could speak she said, in a voice flat and quiet and fifty years old:

"You went into the attic."
