## 1. The Long Road In

The bus had been climbing for two hours when the trees began to close in, and Rafael Oyelaran understood that she was no longer going somewhere so much as being swallowed.

She watched it happen from the window seat, third row back, the one she'd chosen because it was far enough forward to see the road and far enough back to be forgotten. The pines came down to the shoulder like a crowd leaning in. Snow had started an hour ago—not falling so much as arriving, fat unhurried flakes that seemed to have been waiting for her specifically—and already the world beyond the glass had gone soft and white and featureless, as if someone were erasing the landscape behind them faster than they could cross it.

"You'll want to get off at the marker," the driver said.

He was a heavy man with a red face and a wool cap he never removed, and he had not spoken since the last village, forty minutes back. Rafael turned. "The marker?"

"Stone post. Old milestone." His eyes stayed on the road. "Can't miss it."

"Is there no stop? A shelter?"

"There's the school." He said it the way you'd say *there's the well* or *there's the drop-off*, a fact of geography with weight to it, and then—she would think about this later, turning it over the way you turn over a stone to see what's underneath—he looked away. Not out the window. Away. Down at his own hands on the wheel, knuckles pale under the skin, as though the road ahead of them held something he had made a private arrangement never to look at again.

Rafael followed the line of the road to its end, where the trees parted around a rise of dark ground, and saw Hollowmere for the first time.

It was smaller than the listing had suggested. Smaller than anything had suggested. A scatter of grey stone houses huddled along a single street that curved downhill toward a frozen mere, their chimneys standing up against the sky like fingers. And above the village, set back on its own shelf of land with the woods rising black behind it, stood the school.

It was a handsome building. That was the strange thing. Two storeys of pale stone, tall windows, a slate roof furred with snow, a bell tower with a weathervane turned to face the wind coming off the fells. It looked like a school from a painting, a school from a hundred years ago, kept and cared for. Lights burned warm in the lower windows. Someone had shovelled a path from the gate to the door, the flagstones dark and wet against the white.

And yet the driver would not look at it. She checked twice, three times, watching him in the reflection of the windscreen—the mirror there gave her a clean view of his face, or most of it, the cap brim cutting off the top—and his gaze stayed fixed on the middle distance, on the snow, on nothing, with the fixed discipline of a man who had trained himself over many years. It wasn't fear exactly. Fear would have been easier to name. It was more like the look of someone passing a house where something had once happened, and everyone knew what, and nobody said.

Rafael knew that look. She'd worn it herself for twelve years.

* * *

Here is what Rafael Oyelaran had learned by the age of thirty-one: that the world has rooms in it where you must become very small and very quiet, and that if you do it well enough—if you make yourself a piece of furniture, a shadow on the wall, a thing too dull to notice—you can live through almost anything.

She had learned it in the winter she was nineteen, in a rented room in a town whose name she did not say anymore, in a house where something moved inside the walls at night. Not rats. Rats have a sound, a scurry, a purpose. This was slower. Deliberate. A dragging patience, floor by floor, always stopping outside her door, always waiting, as if listening for her to give herself away. She had spent eleven weeks in that room telling herself every noise had an explanation—pipes, expansion, the old bones of an old building settling—and she had survived by perfecting a discipline that had since become her whole personality: she made herself unremarkable. She dressed in colours that didn't argue. She spoke softly and only when spoken to. She arrived early and left late and never, ever drew attention, because attention was a door and doors opened both ways.

It had worked. That was the terrible joke of it. Invisibility worked. She had walked out of that town in March with her life intact and a habit so deep-set it might as well have been bone, and she had built a career on it—good teacher, quiet teacher, the one who never caused trouble, never asked questions, never stayed anywhere longer than two years. Twelve years, nine schools, a CV that read like a woman running from nothing in particular.

And now this. Hollowmere Primary. A one-class village school at the end of a road the buses barely served, advertised in a journal she suspected nobody else had read, offering a salary generous enough to make her suspicious and a cottage included, which should have made her more so.

Wary hope. That was the honest name for what sat in her chest as the bus groaned up the last hill. Hope, because the letter from the headmaster—Corwin Hale, written in careful copperplate—had contained a sentence she'd read twenty times: *We are a small community, Miss Oyelaran, and we keep ourselves to ourselves, but we look after our own.* Everyone wanted to belong somewhere. Even her. Especially her. But wariness, because she had learned to read the silences between sentences, and this letter was mostly silence.

"Marker's here," the driver said.

He braked harder than he needed to. The bus sighed, settled, and stood ticking in the cold. Outside the window, a stone post leaned out of the drifts, its carved numbers worn to shadows. Beyond it, a lane branched off toward the village, and the school sat above everything with its lit windows, patient as a held breath.

"Is there no closer stop?" she asked. "The gate?"

"This is where I stop." He was already reaching for the lever to open the door. Then, without quite meaning to—or so it seemed—he added, low, not looking at her: "You're the new teacher."

"I am."

A silence. Snow ticked against the glass. Somewhere behind them, far back down the white road, a bird called once and stopped, as if corrected.

"They'll tell you it's the consolidation," the driver said. "When you ask about the desks."

Rafael went still. "What desks?"

But the driver's face had closed like a drawer. Whatever had loosened his tongue had been momentary, a splinter working free, and now it was back in deep. He nodded at her bag. "Off you get, miss. Last run before the weather turns proper."

"What weather?" she said. "It's already snowing."

He finally looked at her then—one glance, quick, almost apologetic—and what was in his face was not unfriendliness. It was pity. Brief and professional, the way you'd pity a passenger boarding a boat in weather everyone else could read better than she could.

"It's always snowing here," he said. "From November to March. You'll get used to it." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to himself: "Or you won't."

The door folded open and the cold came in like a hand.

* * *

Rafael stepped down onto a road that had never heard of gritting. The snow took her boots to the ankle at once, soft and soundless, and the cold was different from any cold she knew—it had a texture, a pressure, like walking into water that had opinions about her. Behind her the bus door hissed shut. She turned to watch it go, and saw the driver's eyes flick up once to the school on its rise—a single involuntary glance, quickly punished—and then the bus pulled away, taillights shrinking down the white road until the snow closed over them and the sound of the engine faded into that enormous quiet.

And what a quiet it was. Rafael stood in the middle of the road with her suitcase and her satchel and listened to it. Not silence—silence is empty—but quiet, which is full. Full of held things. The kind of quiet in a house after an argument, or in a classroom before an exam. The village below her smoked gently from its chimneys and made no other sound at all. No dogs. No radios. No children, though it was half past three on a weekday and even a dying village should have had children in it somewhere.

Above her, the school bell tower stood against the grey sky, and the weathervane creaked once, turning, as if checking on her.

She shifted her grip on the suitcase and started up the lane.

The walk took ten minutes and taught her three things. First, that the snow here fell straight down, without wind, in a way that felt less like weather than like intention. Second, that every house she passed had its curtains drawn against the afternoon dark, though behind several of them she caught the sense—no more than a sense—of movement, of watchers declining to be seen watching. Third, that the lane bent as it climbed in a long slow curve, so that the school disappeared and reappeared, disappeared and reappeared, each time seeming slightly larger, slightly nearer, as though it were approaching her rather than the reverse.

At the gate she stopped.

The sign read HOLLOWMERE PRIMARY SCHOOL in letters that had been repainted recently enough to show brush marks over older letters beneath. Rafael tilted her head. Underneath the fresh white paint, if you knew how to look—and she did; twelve years of reading rooms will teach you to read walls—you could make out the ghost of a longer name. Something had been scraped off and painted over. *Hollowmere Primary School and ———*. An infant wing, probably. A nursery annex. Villages consolidated their schools all the time; the headmaster's letter had mentioned consolidation twice, in the tone of a man reciting a script.

Beyond the gate, the path led up between banks of untouched snow—except that it wasn't untouched. Small footprints stitched across it, dozens of sets, all of them identical, all of them the same size, all of them leading *up* the path toward the school and none leading back down. Child-sized boots, size after size after size, pressed deep and crisp, as if a crowd of children had climbed to the school that morning and none had ever left.

Rafael stood at the gate a long moment, letting the cold press against her face, and told herself the reasonable things. That snow fills in. That prints going down would fill first, lying open to the sky while the upward ones sheltered under the tree line. That villages were poor and buses were rare and children walked home in batches, hours apart. Every noise has an explanation. She had survived nineteen by believing that, and thirty-one by pretending she still did.

She pushed the gate. It swung without a sound, oiled, maintained, expected.

Halfway up the path, she noticed the last detail, the one she would carry into the school with her like a stone in her shoe: among all those small footprints, one set was different. Larger. Adult. A single line of adult boot-prints, also heading up, also never returning down—and they ended, halfway between the gate and the door, in the middle of the path, in the open snow, as cleanly as if the walker had been lifted off the earth mid-stride. After that, only the small prints continued.

Rafael looked at the school door. Warm light leaked around its edges. Somewhere inside, faintly, she thought she heard a piano—someone practising scales, patient and slow, though the headmaster's letter had said nothing about a music teacher.

She tightened her grip on her case, squared her shoulders into the shape of an unremarkable woman arriving to teach, and went up the path, stepping carefully around the place where the footprints stopped.

Behind her, at the gate, the snow was already filling in.
