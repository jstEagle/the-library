## 1. The Appraisal

The gravel drive announced Xanthe Ashgrove's arrival with a sound like slow applause, and Hollowmere House stood at the end of it, waiting to be measured.

They killed the engine of the county-issued hatchback and sat for a moment with both hands on the wheel, the way they always did before a job — a small ritual of transition, one life folded away so another could unfold. In the passenger seat: the surveyor's case, the laser measure, the clipboard with its pre-demolition assessment forms. In the glovebox, where it had lived for three years now: a photograph they never looked at.

"Two days," they said aloud, to no one. "In and out. You've done worse."

The house did not answer. Houses never did. That was rather the point of them.

Hollowmere was Georgian in its bones, Regency in its pretensions, and ruinous in its extremities — a three-storey block of grey limestone with a portico like a set jaw and windows that had been boarded since before Xanthe was born. Ivy had colonized the east wing so thoroughly it looked less like growth than like the house being slowly swallowed by something patient. The demolition order sat in Xanthe's satchel, stamped and countersigned. Sixty years empty. Structural failure throughout. Site slated for a distribution warehouse by spring.

Xanthe got out, shouldered the case, and felt the particular loneliness of their profession settle over them like a coat. Surveying was work done alone, in places where no one else wanted to be, among the evidence of other people's lives. They had chosen it deliberately, at twenty-three, for exactly those reasons. Other people's houses held no ambushes. No one's mother appeared in a doorway asking why they hadn't called. A derelict property could not disown you.

The front door was unlocked, which the file said it should not be. Xanthe noted this on the clipboard — *entry point unsecured; recommend site fencing* — and told themselves it was vandals, or the council, or simple bureaucratic sloth. There were always explanations. Xanthe had built a career, and arguably a personality, out of finding them.

Inside, the air was cold in a way that felt curated. Not stale, not damp — cold the way a cellar is cold, or a church. Their breath ghosted in the hall as they clicked on their headlamp and began, professionally, to measure.

* * *

The problems started in the morning room.

Xanthe worked methodically: exterior first, then room by room, wall by wall, the laser measure chirping its little confirmation tone like a bird that knew only one song. The exterior footprint of Hollowmere ran sixty-two feet along the south face. Xanthe had walked it twice, tape and laser agreeing both times.

The morning room occupied the southeast corner. Its interior dimensions came back at nineteen feet by seventeen.

That was fine. That was normal. Subtract walls, allow for the stairwell void — except the stairwell was on the north side, and when Xanthe measured the remaining ground-floor rooms and summed them against the exterior, the arithmetic came back wrong by eleven feet. Eleven feet of floor that existed inside the house but not outside it.

They re-measured. Same number. They checked the laser against the steel tape. Same number. They stood in the middle of the morning room with dust motes drifting through the headlamp beam and did the calculation a third time, slowly, the way you'd check a lock you were sure you'd already locked.

Eleven feet.

"Historic remodelling," they said to the empty room. "Extensions. Something's been built over." Old houses grew like teeth, awkwardly and in the wrong directions. There was an explanation. There was always an explanation, and Xanthe wrote it down — *possible undocumented additions; verify against original plans* — and moved on to the drawing room, where the discrepancy was fourteen feet, and then the library, where the shelves ran deeper than the exterior wall allowed, and by late afternoon Xanthe stopped writing down the discrepancies because there were too many, and started writing down everything instead, in the narrow margins, in handwriting that grew smaller and more private as the light failed.

*Library interior exceeds envelope by 14'2". Re-checked 3×.*

*Corridor on second floor is longer walking east than walking west. (Did not re-measure. Did not want to know if it repeated.)*

*A door in the west passage opens onto a linen cupboard. Cupboard interior: 6' deep. Exterior wall behind it: 9" thick, followed by open air.*

Xanthe had surveyed two hundred and eleven properties in nine years. They had found wells under kitchens and a human skeleton under a patio (turned out to be Victorian, turned out to be fine). Never once had they found a building that disagreed with itself.

"You're tired," they told their own reflection in a smoky mirror above the library fireplace. The reflection looked tired. It also looked, briefly, like it was standing slightly further back in the room than Xanthe was. They turned around. Empty room. Dust. The explanation arrived promptly, obediently: angle of the glass, old silvering, warped mercury backing. They wrote it down. Writing things down made them true, or near enough.

Outside, the sun went down behind the ridge and took the temperature with it.

* * *

Xanthe had booked three nights at the Rose and Crown in the village, but the village was four miles off and the forecast was turning, and the file authorized on-site accommodation for remote surveys. There was a caretaker's flat attached to the service wing — dry, wired, provisioned with a camp bed and a kettle some previous contractor had left behind in 1998, according to the log. Xanthe unpacked there while the wind arrived in earnest, moving through the dead trees around the house with a sound like paper being sorted.

They ate standing up, pasta from a pouch, and called no one, because the list of people who would have picked up had gotten short enough to memorize. Their mother, who did not pick up. Their father, whose number changed every time he moved in with someone new, and who had not given Xanthe the latest one. And Dev — but Dev was three years dead, and the calls Xanthe still composed to him in their head at two in the morning, thumb hovering over a contact card that would never again light up, were the closest thing they had left to prayer.

Estrangement, Xanthe had learned, was not an event. It was a climate. You didn't get thrown out of a family in a single storm; you drifted, degree by degree, until one day you realized you were speaking a different language in a different country, and the people who were supposed to be your weather had become merely distant news. The last thing Xanthe's mother had said to them, four Christmases ago, was *I don't know what you expect me to call you.* Not cruel, exactly. Just a door, closing at its own speed.

Xanthe washed the pouch down the drain of the little sink and did not think about any of this, which took considerable effort, and then went to work by lamplight transcribing the day's figures into clean tables, because numbers were honest even when houses weren't, and honesty was a kind of company.

The noises started at eleven.

First: a settling groan from somewhere overhead, long and structural, the kind old buildings make when the temperature drops. Normal. Xanthe noted it without looking up.

Then: footsteps. Above the caretaker's flat, where there was no floor above the caretaker's flat — the service wing was single-storey, Xanthe had measured it, the ceiling was the roof. The steps crossed the width of the wing, unhurried, and stopped directly overhead.

Wind in the eaves. Branches. Something loose in the gutter, shifting with the gusts. Xanthe listed the explanations the way a customs officer lists declarations, and signed off on all of them, and kept writing.

At midnight, the footsteps came again, and this time they were joined by a second set, lighter, quicker, almost playful, and the two sets paced each other across the ceiling — one heavy, one light, heavy-light-heavy-light — like a game of follow-the-leader played by something that had all night and no body.

Xanthe put down the pen.

Here is what they wanted to do: pack the case, drive the four miles to the Rose and Crown, sleep behind a door with a chain, and return in daylight with a colleague and a very boring attitude. Here is what they actually did: stood up, took the headlamp, climbed the flat's internal ladder to the loft hatch, and pushed it open, because nine years of professional composure had calcified into something close to compulsion, and because — though they would not have admitted it, would not have written it in any margin — the alternative was lying alone in the dark listening, and Xanthe had spent enough of their life alone in the dark listening.

The loft space was empty. Rafters, insulation, a draught moving through the gable vent. No footprints in the dust. Of course no footprints.

But the dust itself was wrong, and it took Xanthe a long moment, crouched in the beam of their own lamp, to articulate why. It wasn't undisturbed. It was *arranged* — swept into two parallel lines running the length of the loft, spaced precisely the distance apart of a pair of walking feet, as if the dust had been tidied after the fact. As if whatever had walked up here had cleaned up after itself, considerately, the way you would if you expected company.

Or the way you would if you wanted the next visitor to know exactly where to look.

Xanthe crouched there until the cold got into their knees, and then climbed down, latched the hatch, and lay on the camp bed with the lamp off and their eyes open, running the rationalizations on a loop like a screensaver. Animals. Draughts. A house coming apart at the seams makes sounds; a house coming apart at the seams shifts its own dust. Two hundred and twelve properties. Nothing has ever happened to you. Nothing is going to happen to you.

Sleep came eventually, thin and shallow, and broke just before dawn with a sound from the main house — not a groan this time, not footsteps, but something with structure to it, deliberate, almost formal. Three knocks. From below.

From beneath the floor.

Xanthe lay still in the grey light, heart going hard, and reached for the usual explanations and found the shelf bare. Then, distantly, from the direction of the cellars, came a different sound entirely — a low, sustained exhalation, like hinges remembering how to move, like a door that had been shut a very long time taking its first breath.

The excavation crew wasn't due until eight.

Xanthe got up anyway, and dressed, and picked up the clipboard, because writing things down was the only ritual they had left that had never lied to them.
