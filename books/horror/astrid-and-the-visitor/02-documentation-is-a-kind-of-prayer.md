## 2. Documentation Is a Kind of Prayer

The kitchen table became an altar by lamplight.

Zephyrine Okafor laid everything out the way he always did: drop cloths first, then the evidence, arranged in chronological order of discovery. The plastic specimen jars went on the left—three of them, each holding droppings he had collected from the Delacroix attic with gloved hands and a pair of forceps normally reserved for wasp nests. The camera sat in the center, its memory card already copied twice. His field notebook lay open to the right, pages squared to the table's edge, because even his notes deserved to stand straight.

He washed his hands before touching any of it. He always washed his hands first. There was a sequence to these things, and the sequence mattered, and if he followed the sequence then the world stayed in its proper shape: small problems, solvable problems, problems with Latin names and product numbers and invoices that could be mailed on Friday.

*Specimen 1–3,* he wrote. *Rodentia? No. Too large for Rattus norvegicus. Largest I've handled in 14 years of practice. Diameter consistent with... what?*

He held jar two up to the light. The dropping inside was dark, dense, almost woody, and it was wrong in a way his training could not name but his body understood. It was too big. Not raccoon-big or possum-big. Big the way a man's would be big, if a man left such things in attics and ate whatever a farmhouse offered.

He set it down carefully, as if it might hear him thinking.

The photographs came next. He connected the camera to his laptop with the cable he kept coiled in a drawer, neat as a sleeping snake, and waited for the files to load while the kettle worked itself toward a whistle behind him. Forty-one images. He'd taken forty-one images in that attic, and he knew without looking that most of them were of the same thing: the handprints on the rafters.

They loaded onto the screen one at a time, and Zephyrine made himself look at each one fully before moving to the next. That was part of the discipline. You didn't flinch past evidence. Evidence was only frightening when you refused to measure it.

The prints were pressed into the soft pine of the rafters like fossils in clay. Five fingers each. Human proportions—he had put his own hand against rafter number four, the third print down, and the match had been exact. Not close. Exact. Same span, same finger length, same shallow valley where a thumb meets the palm. He had photographed his own hand beside the print, ruler included for scale, and now the image sat on his screen showing him something impossible with perfect, clinical clarity.

And they had been warm. He hadn't written that down yet. He'd stood in that dusty dark with his palm against hundred-year-old wood and felt heat coming off the print like heat off a living thing, like pressing your hand to the flank of a sleeping animal, and he had told himself: old houses hold warmth strangely. Insulation pockets. Sun through the roof.

He typed: *Surface temp of impression noticeably elevated relative to surrounding wood. Possible explanation: [none found].*

His cursor blinked after *[none found]* for a long time. Then he deleted the bracketed words and wrote *investigating,* which was a lie, because he wasn't investigating anything. He was sitting very still in his kitchen trying not to think about how the warmth had felt familiar.

The kettle screamed. He got up, made tea he wouldn't drink, and came back to find the screen had gone to sleep. In the black mirror of the monitor he caught his own reflection—fifty pounds heavier than the boy he'd been, gray threading his beard, reading glasses pushed up into his hair—and for half a second the reflection looked like someone waiting to be recognized.

He woke the computer and kept working.

* * *

There was a photograph of Zephyrine Okafor at age nine, and in it he is standing slightly outside the frame of his own family.

It hung in Adaeze's hallway now, but he remembered the day it was taken: the church picnic, 1989, everyone arranged on the church steps in rows. His mother's arm around Adaeze. His father beaming. And Zephyrine at the end of the second row, half-turned toward something out of frame, his mouth open mid-sentence, talking—to no one, the photographer must have thought, or worse, not noticed at all. When you looked at the picture quickly, there was a gap in the row where he stood, a seam in the family, and only when you looked longer did you find the boy folded into it.

He had been an easy child to overlook. Quiet where Adaeze was loud, careful where other children were careless, brown-skinned and skinny in a town where the founding families were pale and broad and seemed to take up more space than physics allowed. The Okafors had come to Hollowmere in 1974, his father chasing work at the grain depot, and thirty years later the town still pronounced their name wrong at the pharmacy, at the bank, at the funeral of his father, where the pastor had called them *the Oak-fords* over the open casket and no one corrected him.

A child learns early whether the world has room for him. Zephyrine learned there wasn't, and so he did what overlooked children do. He built a companion out of the leftover material of himself.

Wren.

He hadn't spoken the name aloud in decades, and even now, alone in his kitchen with the lamp humming, he tested it silently, the way you test a tooth that used to hurt. *Wren.* A small bird. A small boy. Seven years old, the way Zephyrine had been seven years old, wearing clothes Zephyrine had described for him—a corduroy jacket, mismatched buttons—and saying things only because Zephyrine had said them first.

*Counting keeps things true,* Wren used to say, because seven-year-old Zeph had said it first, crouched under the porch steps with a spiral notebook, counting fence slats, counting crows, counting the seconds between lightning and thunder. *If you write it down, it can't pretend it didn't happen.*

That was the whole theology of it. That was the prayer.

Because that's what the notebooks had been, though he'd never used the word. After the funeral—his father's, when Zeph was eleven—the notebooks multiplied. Measurements of the backyard fence. Sketches of every house on Corvus Lane with the number of windows counted and recounted. Lists of birds seen from his bedroom window, dated, cross-referenced to a library book about migration patterns. While Adaeze grieved loudly and healthily, crying at school, breaking dishes, being held, Zephyrine sat in his room recording the world with the desperate thoroughness of a man taking dictation from God, and the world, so documented, became bearable. If it was written down, it was real. If it was real, it couldn't leave him the way everything else did.

His mother found the notebooks after she died—his mother, years later—and Adaeze kept them in a box. Nineteen of them. Zephyrine had never gone back through them. Some archives you keep sealed.

But he remembered the last one. He remembered the summer he turned thirteen, when Wren stopped coming, or when Zephyrine stopped needing him—or those are the same event viewed from different angles—and the final entry in the final notebook read, in a child's careful print:

*Last day. He said he'd wait. I said don't wait. He said counting keeps things true. I said goodbye.*

He had closed the notebook and never opened another one for six months, and when he finally did, the entries were about insects and infestations and square footage, professional, dry, safe. Documentation stopped being a prayer and became a job. Or seemed to. Standing in his kitchen at midnight, measuring a handprint on his laptop screen with a digital ruler, Zephyrine understood that the job had been the prayer all along, said daily, in a smaller voice.

* * *

At 1:40 in the morning, his sister let herself in with her own key, because Adaeze Okafor had never once in her life waited to be invited anywhere.

"You're up." She stood in the kitchen doorway in her nursing scrubs, hair wrapped, hospital lanyard still around her neck, smelling of antiseptic and the cold outside. Her eyes went to the table—the jars, the notebook, the laptop glowing with rafters—and narrowed. "That's the Delacroix place."

"Follow-up documentation," Zephyrine said. "Routine."

"Nobody documents routine at two in the morning, Zeph." She crossed the kitchen, poured herself the tea he'd abandoned, drank it cold without wincing, and pulled out a chair with her foot. She looked at the specimen jars the way she looked at lab results she didn't like. "What am I looking at?"

"Droppings. Oversized."

"From what?"

"That's the question I'm being paid to answer."

Adaeze set the mug down and turned the laptop toward herself. She scrolled slowly. She was four years older than Zephyrine and had spent those four years learning to read people the way he read evidence, and he watched her face do what faces did when they met the Delacroix attic photos: confusion first, then the effort of rational explanation, then the failure of it, then something older underneath, something that lived in Hollowmere's groundwater.

She stopped on the image of his hand beside the print.

"Okay," she said quietly. "Whose hand is that?"

"Mine."

"And the other one?"

"Also mine. Pressed into the wood next to it. For scale."

Adaeze looked at him for a long moment. "Say that again slower."

"The print matches my hand exactly, Ade. Span, length, everything. Which means either some animal has evolved a human hand, or—" He stopped. He heard himself about to say *or a man crawled through that attic on all fours leaving warm prints in the wood,* and the sentence refused to finish, because finishing it would make it real, and if it was real he would have to write it down, and if he wrote it down—

"If you write it down, it can't pretend it didn't happen," Adaeze said.

Zephyrine went cold. "What did you say?"

She frowned. "Grandma used to say that. About her garden ledger. *Write it down, chérie, then it can't pretend it didn't happen.* Why, you remember it too?" She waved the question away before he could answer, because Adaeze always had somewhere else to go. "Never mind. Look—I came by because Ruth Delacroix called the clinic today asking odd questions. Asking if we'd ever heard, medically speaking, of a child talking to somebody who isn't there. Persistent. Weeks. Maisie's got herself an invisible friend, and Ruth wanted to know if that was normal or if that was *Hollowmere* normal."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere down the street a dog began to bark—not frantic, just reporting, the way dogs report things humans can't see.

"There are stories," Adaeze said. "You know there are stories. Grandma told them every October until Mama made her stop. About the founders. About the tithe. About the thing that counts the town." She turned the laptop back toward him, and the handprint glowed between them like a wound. "I'm a nurse, Zeph. I believe in blood cultures and bedsores and nothing else. But I grew up on Grandma's knee same as you, and this—" she tapped the screen, once, hard, "—this smells like the old stories. The ones where people get counted."

"I'm a pest controller, Ade. I'm going to go back Thursday, set traps, identify the animal, invoice Mrs. Delacroix, and go home."

"To what?"

"What?"

"When you're done identifying it. Then what? You think it's a rat? You've already ruled out rats. You ruled them out the moment you jarred that." She nodded at the specimens. "So you're going to identify something that doesn't have a name in any manual you own, and then you're going to write it down, because writing it down is the only way you know how to feel safe. And I'm telling you, little brother—" her voice dropped, and it was Grandma's voice suddenly, or near enough, "—some things, you write them down and they know they've been seen. Some things have been waiting a long time to be seen. You want to be the one who looks?"

Zephyrine said nothing. On the screen, the cursor blinked in the empty field where the species name should go.

Adaeze stood, kissed the top of his head the way she had since he was small, and took her mug to the sink. At the doorway she paused.

"Maisie draws her friend," she said. "Ruth mentioned it. Draws him over and over. Little figure, corduroy jacket, mismatched buttons."

The kitchen went very quiet. Even the refrigerator seemed to hold its breath.

"Kids copy cartoons," Zephyrine managed.

"Mm." Adaeze didn't turn around. "Thursday, then. Set your traps. Write your notes." She pulled the door shut behind her, and through it, softer, almost to herself: "Just don't count anything you're not prepared to answer for."

* * *

He worked until nearly four.

He printed the best photographs and taped them into the notebook, because paper records survived power failures. He measured each print three times and recorded the measurements three times, and the numbers agreed with themselves, and the agreement gave him no comfort at all. He catalogued the droppings by weight and dimension. He wrote a paragraph of hypothesis—structural settling, thermal anomaly, possible hoax by a previous tenant—and read it back and heard it sound exactly like what it was: a man praying in Latin he didn't believe.

Near the end, his pen slowed. He found himself writing without deciding to, the old automatic handwriting, the seven-year-old's careful print surfacing through thirty years of professional shorthand:

*Item: the prints were warm.*
*Item: the warmth felt like a hand I have held before.*
*Item: I do not want to know whose.*

He stared at the last line. His hand hovered over it, pen ready, ready to strike it out, to redact, to keep the record clean and therefore keep himself safe.

But that was the rule, the whole rule, the only rule he had ever trusted: *if you write it down, it can't pretend it didn't happen.*

He left the line in. Closed the notebook. Turned off the lamp.

In the dark, the house settled around him with small wooden sounds, and Zephyrine Okafor lay awake listening to them, and it occurred to him—arriving gently, terribly, like frost forming on a window—that he had spent his whole life believing documentation was a shield. A wall between him and the unmeasured dark.

But a record is also an invitation. Every name in a ledger knows it has been read. Every photograph knows it has been taken. Somewhere out past the edge of town, in a farmhouse attic, something had pressed its hand into the wood of Hollowmere and held it there, patient as scripture, waiting a long, long time for someone to come along who would write it down.

Waiting, maybe, for him specifically.

He slept at last near dawn, and dreamed of a porch, and of a small figure sitting on the steps with its back to him, corduroy jacket, mismatched buttons, perfectly still, keeping count.
