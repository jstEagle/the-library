## 9. Hollowmere Remembers

The moon was three nights from full when Odile Fontenot began making her calls.

She did not use the telephone. Telephone calls could be overheard, subpoenaed, remembered wrong by the wrong ears. Odile had lived eighty-one years in Hollowmere, seventy-nine of them knowing exactly what the town was, and she had learned that the only safe way to deliver a warning was to stand in a person's doorway and say nothing at all—just look at them until they understood.

The first door she stood at was Earl Boudreaux's, at the hardware store on Cane Row. Earl was restocking boxes of ten-penny nails when the bell over the door chimed, and he looked up expecting a customer and found instead Odile's small, dry face beneath her gray bun, watching him with eyes like river stones.

"Closing early this week," she said. Not a question. Not quite an order either. Odile Fontenot did not give orders; she gave weather reports. You didn't argue with weather.

Earl set down the nail box. His hands were big and blunt and had built half the fences in the parish, and they were suddenly unsteady. "Moon coming in pretty," he said.

"Moon coming in heavy," Odile corrected. "You'll want your shutters drawn by six. And Earl—" She paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and for a moment she looked older than her years, older than the town itself. "You'll want to be home."

He closed at four that afternoon. He told his wife it was slow. It wasn't slow. There were three trucks in the lot when he turned the sign, men who needed feed and fencing wire before the weekend, and he watched their faces fall as the lock clicked, and none of them said a word about it. They just got back in their trucks and drove away, because they understood too. That was the thing about Hollowmere. Nobody ever had to explain anything twice. The explanations had all been given a hundred and thirty years ago, and everyone had inherited the summary.

* * *

Renée Thibodeaux ran the diner, or the diner ran itself through Renée, which amounted to the same thing after forty years. On Tuesday she took down the specials board and wrote in its place, in her careful cursive: *CLOSED AT DUSK UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE.*

Dale Mercier came in for coffee anyway, because Dale came in every morning of his life, and he sat at the counter stirring a cup he wasn't drinking while Renée wiped down surfaces that were already clean.

"You heard anything," Dale said. It wasn't really a question. It was the Hollowmere version of *I'm scared, tell me I'm wrong.*

"I heard the Delacroix girl's got herself an imaginary friend," Renée said. She kept wiping. The cloth made small circles on the laminate, around and around, like something being buried. "Same as there used to be. Way back."

"There hasn't been one since—" Dale stopped himself. He stirred his coffee. Outside the window, the light was doing something strange, going gold and thick the way it did in October when the season turned over in its sleep. "My granddaddy used to say you don't name things that come around on their own. Says naming's an invitation."

"Well." Renée folded the cloth. Unfolded it. Folded it again. "Somebody already named it, didn't they. Long time ago. And somebody else answered to it."

They let the silence sit between them, and the silence was old and comfortable and terrible, like a dog that had been sleeping in the house so long nobody remembered when it arrived.

At two o'clock, Renée flipped the sign. A car pulled into the lot as she did—a stranger's car, passing through—and she watched through the glass as the driver read the sign, frowned, and pulled back out onto the highway without stopping. Good, she thought. Keep driving. This town has never once been worth the detour.

* * *

Zephyrine noticed it first at the gas station.

He'd been going to Dupré's Fuel & Feed since he was sixteen, since old man Dupré used to let him run a tab against the promise of his first paycheck, and in thirty years not a single clerk had failed to meet his eye. But when he came in Wednesday for diesel and a fresh box of nitrile gloves, the young man at the register—young Castile boy, third generation—looked at the counter, at the pump display, at the wall of tobacco behind Zephyrine's shoulder, anywhere but at his face.

"Thirty-one fifty," the boy said to the register.

Zephyrine paid. He waited for the usual small talk—the weather, the Saints, the price of feed. Nothing came. The receipt printed and the boy slid it across the counter like a man feeding something through the bars of a cage.

Outside, Zephyrine stood by his truck in the flat white afternoon light and felt the shape of what was happening settle over him. He had spent his whole life being overlooked—that was the family condition, the Okafor inheritance, invisibility passed down like a coat too big for any of them—but this was different. Being overlooked was passive. This was active. People were *not-looking* at him, deliberately, the way you don't look at a hole in the floor until the boards are replaced. They knew where he'd been. They knew what he'd photographed. Word traveled in Hollowmere the way water traveled under it: silently, everywhere, arriving all at once.

He drove past the church and saw the notice board out front. Pastor Guidry had taken down the announcement about the fall potluck and put up a single sheet of paper in its place, the letters large and blocky:

**EVENING SERVICES SUSPENDED UNTIL AFTER THE HARVEST. PRAY AT HOME.**

Pray at home. As if home were safer. As if anything in Hollowmere had ever been contained by walls.

* * *

In the pharmacy on Bell Street, Marguerite Sonnier counted pills behind the counter and listened to her mother-in-law, who was ninety-four and sharp as a fishhook, describe the moon.

"It'll be red at the bottom first," the old woman said. "Like it's bleeding into us instead of us bleeding into it. My mama saw the last bad one. Nineteen sixty-one. She said the frost came up out of the ground in circles, perfect circles, like someone had set plates down on the fields overnight."

"And then?" Marguerite asked, though she knew. Every child in Hollowmere knew the shape of the story even if no adult would ever finish telling it. The story always stopped at the same place, mid-sentence, the way a hymn stops when the singer forgets the words are a confession.

"And then it was spring," her mother-in-law said, "and the Boudoin family had moved away in the night, dishes still in the cupboard, and nobody ever asked them why. Because asking is how it gets in, baby. Questions are doors."

Marguerite filled the prescriptions. Sedatives mostly, that week. More than usual. She noticed these things; it was her job to notice. Half the town was quietly medicating itself against something it refused to name, and she rang up the little white bags with a smile she'd practiced for decades, and inside she was counting too—not pills but days. Three. Two. One.

* * *

At the elementary school, Maisie Delacroix's teacher, Miss Alma, taped construction-paper moons to the windows and tried to teach fractions, and the children kept looking outside.

It was the light. Everyone remarked on it, strangers and locals alike, that particular October gold that made Hollowmere look like a photograph of itself, nostalgic for something that hadn't happened yet. But the children weren't looking at the light. They were looking past it, toward the tree line beyond the playground fence, and Miss Alma caught at least six of them doing it in a single hour, heads turning in unison like a field of sunflowers tracking something that wasn't the sun.

"Drawing time," she announced, louder than she meant to, and put paper in front of them because busy hands couldn't point.

Maisie Delacroix drew a figure. Small, neat, patient. She drew it standing under a tree, and she gave it kind tired eyes, and when Miss Alma leaned over her desk and asked, gently, *who's that, sweetheart?*, Maisie smiled up at her with a serenity that did not belong on a seven-year-old face.

"He counts everybody," Maisie said. "He says soon he won't have to count anymore. He says it's almost finished."

Miss Alma straightened up. Around the room, eleven other children kept drawing, heads bent, crayons whispering. None of them had looked up. None of them needed to. Later, alone in the empty classroom, she would take down every construction-paper moon and throw them away, and she would not be able to tell anyone, including herself, why her hands were shaking.

* * *

Odile Fontenot saved Zephyrine for last.

Not because he mattered least. Because he mattered most, and Odile believed in approaching the center of a thing slowly, the way you approach a snake you intend to pick up.

She found him Thursday morning at the county archive annex, or rather she was waiting outside it when he came out, sitting on the bench in her good coat with her hands folded on her cane, a woman who had clearly been there long enough to make the building uncomfortable.

"Mr. Okafor." She said his whole name, both barrels of it, and watched him flinch slightly at the sound. When was the last time anyone in this town had said his full name aloud? "Walk with me."

They walked. Down Bell Street, past the shuttered diner, past the pharmacy with its lights burning at noon behind drawn blinds. The town had gone quiet the way a body goes quiet—not asleep, but listening.

"You've been in the archive," Odile said.

"Yes."

"And the Delacroix well."

It wasn't a question, and he didn't insult her by pretending. "Yes."

Odile nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed a diagnosis she'd already read. Ahead of them, the water tower stood against the pale sky with HOLLOWMERE painted on its side, the letters faded to the color of old bone.

"My grandmother told me stories," Odile said. "Her grandmother told her. In this town we don't pass down truth, Mr. Okafor. We pass down instructions. Close early. Draw the shutters. Don't answer if something calls your name after dark. And above all—" she stopped walking, and turned to face him fully, and her eyes were the flattest, oldest things he'd ever seen on a living face— "above all, don't go looking for the count. Because the count looks back."

"The ledger," Zephyrine said quietly. "You know about the ledger."

"I know my family has kept a piece of paper in a tin box for five generations, and I know that piece of paper is the reason my people ate in bad years and buried their dead in good ones, and I know that whatever is written on it, I was told never, ever to read it aloud." Her knuckles whitened on the cane. "And now a pest controller has been crawling through attics and wells with a camera, taking pictures of the one thing this town survived by not photographing."

"They have a right to know—"

"They have a right to *survive*," Odile said, and her voice cracked like green wood. "That's the only right this town has ever honored. You think silence is cowardice? Silence is load-bearing, Mr. Okafor. You pull it out and you find out real quick what the whole structure was resting on."

Down the street, a screen door opened and closed. Someone had been standing on a porch, watching them, and now pretended they hadn't been. Further off, a dog began to bark at nothing, then thought better of it and stopped.

"Harvest moon's Saturday," Odile said. "Come see me Friday. Before dark." She turned and walked away, small and straight-backed down the middle of the empty street, and called back over her shoulder without turning around:

"And Mr. Okafor—whatever you've figured out about who's been keeping the count all these years—" 

She paused at the corner. The wind moved through the town, lifting dust and dead leaves, carrying the smell of cold coming.

"—ask yourself why it never once knocked on *your* door."

Then she was gone around the corner, and Zephyrine stood alone in the street with the question settling into him like a splinter working toward the heart—because he already knew the answer, didn't he. He'd known since the porch. Wren had never needed to knock on his door.

Wren had always had a key.
