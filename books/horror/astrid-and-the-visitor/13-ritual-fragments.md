## 13. Ritual Fragments

The kitchen table of Zephyrine's apartment had not seen a meal in eleven days. It had become a workbench instead, and by the third night of the new moon's absence—when the dark was deepest and the hunger, he suspected, was patient rather than quiet—it held everything he owned that mattered: the tithe ledger wrapped in a pillowcase to keep the damp off it, photocopied pages from the county archive, his grandmother's Bible with its pressed flowers and its marginalia in her careful hand, and forty-one photographs of handprints on rafters, arranged chronologically like stations of a cross.

He had stopped sleeping. Sleep was where Wren lived now, or where Wren had always lived, and Zephyrine could no longer tell the difference between dreaming of him and being visited. So he worked. Documentation was a kind of prayer, and prayer was the only thing standing between him and the understanding that kept trying to arrive whole.

He would not let it arrive whole. He made it come in pieces.

* * *

The ledger opened flat now, its spine broken from handling. One hundred and thirty years of names, one per harvest, each entry followed by the same two words in different hands across generations: *paid* or *carried*. He had transcribed every line onto index cards, because transcription was how he thought, because the act of copying slowed the horror down to a speed he could survive.

Paid names outnumbered carried ones four to one. Paid names were drifters, mostly—the 1961 entry, *Elias Marsh, paid*, sat beside a note in a later hand Zephyrine had learned to dread: *the Fontenot girl saw him at the stones before. Handled.* Carried names were children. His own name was there, in his grandmother's handwriting, dated 1994: *Zephyrine Okafor, carried.*

Carried. Deferred. A debt postponed with interest accruing somewhere in the dark.

But the ledger told him *who*. It did not tell him *how*. For that he needed the covenant, and the covenant was Odile Fontenot's now, locked in her kitchen drawer, and she had let him photograph only three pages before closing the drawer with the finality of a woman shutting a coffin lid.

He spread the three photographs out under his desk lamp.

The first page was preamble—flowery Victorian language about prosperity and posterity, the kind of thing founding families wrote when they wanted their crime to sound like stewardship. The second page was the operative text, and he had read it so many times the words had worn grooves in him:

*"…and on the night when the harvest moon stands full above the stones, the count shall be kept open by the giving of a name freely offered, and the offering shall be received at the place of standing, and the debt shall sleep until the next reckoning. Should the count be left unclosed, the hunger shall not sleep, nor distinguish, nor cease."*

Freely offered. At the place of standing. On the night of the full harvest moon.

And the third page—the one he'd photographed last, the one Odile had watched him take with an expression he still couldn't parse—was a fragment of instruction, half eaten by water damage:

*"…the giver must stand within the ring and speak the name into the count, and the receiver—"*

The rest was gone. The receiver what? Received the name how? What happened at the moment of transfer?

He sat back and looked at the ceiling of his apartment, which was mercifully blank, which had never grown handprints, though some nights he checked anyway, flashlight in teeth, because checking was also a kind of prayer.

Wren spoke only in phrases Zephyrine had invented. That was the rule, the terrible rule he'd confirmed over three decades of absence and three weeks of presence. Every word out of that small mouth was vocabulary Zephyrine had authored at seven years old, sitting alone in the Delacroix cornfield with nothing but his own loneliness for company. Which meant Wren couldn't explain the ritual even if he wanted to. The riddles weren't evasion. They were a cage—a creature who could only speak in the language a lonely child had built for him, trying to communicate something enormous through a vocabulary of maybe two hundred words.

*Keep the count,* Wren said.
*Counting is caring,* Wren said.
*A book needs an ending or it isn't a book.*

Zephyrine pulled a legal pad toward him and wrote the phrases out, all of them, every utterance he'd collected since the porch. Then he did what he always did with evidence. He looked for the pattern.

* * *

At two in the morning, Adaeze let herself in with her spare key and found him surrounded by paper.

"You look like a man losing an argument with a library," she said. She set down a bag of jollof rice she'd brought without asking, because asking would have meant accepting that this was a visit and not an intervention.

"I'm close," he said.

"You said that Tuesday."

"Tuesday I had the ledger and the covenant. Tonight I have the ledger, the covenant, and Wren's sentences." He tapped the legal pad. "Adaeze, listen. *A circle is a door both ways.* That's one of his. I invented it—I remember inventing it, I was explaining why we couldn't leave the chalk circle we drew in the yard, I was seven and full of nonsense. But it's true here, isn't it? The stones form a circle. The ritual happens inside a ring. A circle is a door both ways."

She stood over him, arms crossed, and he watched her decide between fear and love. Love won, the way it always did with Adaeze, arriving dressed as anger.

"And what goes through the door?"

"That's what I don't have yet. The receiver clause. The water damage ate it." He flipped to the archive photocopies. "But the county records mention a surveyor in 1903 who mapped 'antiquities' for the historical society. If anyone drew the stones, drew the site—"

"Hollowmere has a historical society?"

"Hollowmere has a filing cabinet in the library basement and a woman named Mrs. Pruitt who guards it like a dragon. She's eighty-six and she remembers my grandmother." He stood, knees cracking, and realized distantly that he hadn't eaten since yesterday. "There's something else. The ledger stops in 1994. My year. After me, nothing—no paid, no carried, no entries at all. Thirty years of blank pages."

"So the debt's been accruing."

"Compound interest," Zephyrine said. "And the covenant says if the count is left unclosed, the hunger doesn't sleep, doesn't distinguish, doesn't cease. Doesn't distinguish, Ade. Not between founders and outsiders. Not between guilty and innocent. When the moon rises full, if nobody closes the count—"

"Then it takes everyone." She said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "That's why they're all closing early. That's why Odile gave you the page. They know."

"They've known for thirty years. They just hoped it would keep waiting." He gathered his notes with hands that were steadier than he felt. "I need the surveyor's map. I need to know exactly what stands at those stones besides stones."

"Zeph." She caught his sleeve. "Whatever you find—there's no version of this where you walk away clean. You understand that? Even if you run tonight, drive until the gas light comes on, the ledger has your name in it. Carried means carried. It'll follow."

He looked at her and, for once, didn't reach for reassurance. "I know. There's no clean exit. There's just a choice of debts."

* * *

Mrs. Pruitt met him at the library basement door at opening time, keys already in hand, as though she'd been expecting him for years.

"Okafor," she said. "Your grandmother used to bring me gooseberries. Sit where I can see you."

The cabinet yielded the surveyor's folio—water-stained, brittle, magnificent. Inside, a pen-and-ink rendering of the Delacroix land as it stood in 1903: farmhouse, well, orchard rows long since gone wild, and beyond the back pasture, drawn with the careful hatching of a man recording something he didn't understand, a ring of seven stones. The surveyor had labeled them in a small, uneasy hand: *Standing stones, origin unknown. Locals will not approach. Ground within the ring shows no frost in winter; grass green year-round.*

Green year-round. No frost. Something fed there, even then.

And beneath the stones, in a cross-section sketch the surveyor had apparently added as an afterthought—or perhaps as a warning—was a shape. A cavity. A hollow beneath the ring, like a root cellar, like a mouth.

Zephyrine stared at it until the lines blurred.

"A book needs an ending or it isn't a book," he said aloud.

"Pardon?" said Mrs. Pruitt.

"Nothing." He photographed the map from six angles, thanked her, and walked out into a morning gone strangely gold, the light thickening toward October.

* * *

He assembled it that afternoon, all the fragments laid end to end, and the picture that formed was simple enough to be unbearable.

The ritual required three things: a name freely given, spoken inside the ring of stones, on the night of the harvest moon. The receiver—the hollow beneath the stones, the hunger itself, the thing that had once been a forgotten child before Hollowmere forgot it harder—took the name into the count, and the debt slept another generation. The founders had done it with drifters and strangers and, at least once, with a girl who saw too much. They had outsourced their salvation to people nobody would miss.

But the ledger had been blank for thirty years. The hunger had been fasting. And a fast ends.

If no name was given, it wouldn't take one. It would take all. The covenant was explicit: *nor distinguish, nor cease.* Seven hundred souls in Hollowmere, and the hunger beneath the stones would move through them like fire through a dry field, and it would start, he suspected, with the children—the isolated ones, the lonely ones, the ones Wren had always been able to reach. Maisie Delacroix first, probably. Maisie, who drew him with such love.

Zephyrine sat at his workbench table and understood, finally, with the grim clarity he'd been postponing for weeks, that there was no report he could file, no archive he could expose, no newspaper that could print this truth in a form that would save anyone. Knowledge wasn't a shield here. Knowledge was just the price of admission to the choice.

The choice: gather the five descendant families and finish what their ancestors started—find a name, give it freely, close the count. Or refuse, and let the town pay the arrears of a hundred and thirty years of borrowed time.

Or a third thing, which had been sitting at the edge of his vision for days now, patient as Wren on the porch steps. A ledger with his name already in it. *Carried.* A debt with his signature pre-written, waiting only for the date.

He looked at the index card with his own name transcribed in his own careful hand, and he did not pick it up, and he did not put it away.

Outside, the first leaves came down in the wind. Nine days to the full moon.

He reached for the phone to call Odile Fontenot, and his hand was shaking, and he made himself dial anyway, because documentation was a kind of prayer, and prayer, sometimes, was a call to assembly.

"Odile," he said, when she answered. "We need to get the families together. All five of them. Before the moon."

A long silence on the line. Then, quietly: "I wondered which choice you'd make. Come at dusk. And Zephyrine—bring the ledger. It's time it went home."
