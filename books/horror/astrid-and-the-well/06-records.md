## 6. Records

The basement key lived on a hook in the janitor's closet, behind a mop that had given up its purpose sometime during the previous administration. Wendell took it down on a Tuesday, after last period, when the hallways had emptied of children and the building had settled into its after-hours creak. Nobody stopped her. Nobody asked. In Hollow Creek, it seemed, a teacher could go anywhere she liked as long as she smiled while doing it.

The stairwell down smelled of cold concrete and old paper, a smell that in another life would have been comforting to her—the smell of archives, of proof. Here it just smelled like a place where things were kept so they could be forgotten.

She'd told herself it was about Eli. That was the official reason, the one she'd rehearsed on the drive in, the one she'd give if anyone asked: a student had stopped attending, no forwarding records, no withdrawal paperwork, and a responsible teacher checked the enrollment history before filing a truancy report. It was even true. But it wasn't the whole truth, and she knew it wasn't, and the basement stairs took her down anyway.

Because Eli's file had bothered her in a way she couldn't shake. Not the absence of records—the *shape* of the absence. When she'd asked the district office for his transfer documents, the clerk on the phone had gone quiet for three full seconds, a silence with a specific texture, the texture of someone deciding how much to say. Then: "We'll mail those out." They had not mailed anything. It had been nine days.

The basement was one long room bisected by support beams, lit by bare bulbs on pull-chains. Shelving units ran along the walls like the ribs of something large and patient. Boxes of yearbooks. Boxes of standardized tests from decades when tests were printed on paper. And against the far wall, in a row of green archival binders with cracked spines, the enrollment ledgers.

One hundred and forty years of children, give or take.

She pulled the most recent volume first—2010 to present—and carried it to the worktable under the one window, a slit of glass at ground level that let in a gray blade of afternoon. She opened it to the current year and ran her finger down the roster until she found the line.

*Okonkwo, Elias. Gr. 6. Enrolled 9/02. Status: TRANSFERRED — 10/14.*

Transferred. Not withdrawn. Not moved. Transferred, as though some receiving school somewhere had signed for him, as though a desk in some other town had been held open for a boy who had stopped existing on a Tuesday.

There was no destination listed. There was a stamp, though—a small purple stamp, faded, the kind schools used before everything went digital. Wendell tilted it toward the light. It read: *HOLLOW CREEK CONSOLIDATED — RECORDS COMPLETE.* Records complete. What a phrase. What a tidy little coffin of a phrase.

She sat back. Her heart was doing something she didn't like, a slow heavy knock, and she made herself breathe the way she told anxious students to breathe—four in, hold, four out—while she turned back through the pages of the current ledger. Looking, though she hadn't let herself name what for, until she found it.

*Delacroix, Marguerite. Gr. 4. Status: TRANSFERRED.* Year: 2014.

Four years ago. No destination. Same stamp.

She pulled the older volumes off the shelf one by one and stacked them on the table until the stack wobbled. 2000 to 2010. 1990 to 2000. The spines went back and back, the handwriting shifting from ballpoint to fountain pen to a copperplate so fine it looked engraved, the paper changing from acid-yellowed to brittle brown to something soft as cloth.

She started with the oldest and worked forward, and she kept a sheet of legal paper beside her, and she wrote down every TRANSFERRED she found, and by the third ledger her hand had stopped shaking, which frightened her more than the shaking had. Shaking was human. What she was doing now was arithmetic.

*Hollow Creek Consolidated. Transfers without destination:*

1886 — Aldous Pike, age 9
1896 — Serafina Marchetti, age 11
1906 — Thomas and Ruth Ellery, ages 8 and 7
1916 — James Vane, age 10
1926 — Odette Carrow, age 12
1936 — Henry Boone, age 9
1946 — Lena Marsh, age 8
1956 — Peter Ishikawa, age 10
1966 — Daniel Reyes, age 11
1974 — *no entry*
1984 — Grace Whitlock, age 9
1994 — Simon Achebe, age 10
2004 — Caleb Vane, age 11
2014 — Marguerite Delacroix, age 8

Fourteen names across a hundred and forty years. One every ten years, like clockwork, like a bill that came due on a schedule. And every single one of them, every year, ended in a four or a six—no. She looked again. She made herself look again, because the first thing a good researcher learns is that the eye wants patterns and will invent them out of noise.

But it wasn't noise.

Every year ended in a 4. 1884. 1894. 1904. She'd misread the first two in her haste—the ledgers themselves were organized by decade, and the transfers clustered at the end of each volume, and she'd grabbed the wrong page. She went back, carefully, name by name, and corrected her list, and when she was done the column of years read like a countdown that had never learned to stop:

1884, 1894, 1904, 1914, 1924, 1934, 1944, 1954, 1964, 1974, 1984, 1994, 2004, 2014.

Every ten years. Every year ending in four.

And 2024 was this year.

* * *

She sat with that for a long time. The window-slit of light moved maybe an inch across the table. Somewhere above her, the building's heating system clicked on and off, on and off, patient as a pulse.

1974 had no entry. She'd noticed it the first time and told herself it was an anomaly, a gap, a year the town got lucky. But now, with the full column in front of her, the gap glared. She went back to the 1970–1980 volume and paged through it twice, three times, checking the transfers section, checking the marginalia, checking the back pages where withdrawals and deaths were recorded. Nothing for 1974. No transfer. No withdrawal. No death certificate filed through the school.

But there was something else. In the back of the volume, on a page titled *MISCELLANEOUS CORRESPONDENCE*, someone had paper-clipped a letter, and the clip had rusted into a small orange bloom, and the letter itself had gone the color of tea.

It was from the district office, dated November 1974, typed on onionskin:

*Re: Ishikawa, W. — We show no enrollment record for the above-named student for the current term. Please forward enrollment documentation or confirm withdrawal to district office by Dec. 1.*

Wendell read it four times.

*Ishikawa, W.*

Her own surname. Her father's surname—Peter Ishikawa, age 10, transferred, 1954, no destination, her uncle or her father's cousin or some branch of the family tree that had been pruned and the stump sanded smooth. And *W.* Wendell. Wendell Ishikawa, who had been seven years old in 1974, who had lived in a town two hundred miles from here, who had—

Who had lost her sister that year.

The memory arrived not as a memory but as a pressure, a headache behind the eyes, the shape of a thing she had spent forty years not thinking about. Her sister. Six years old. Gone in the autumn of 1974, and the police had searched the creek, and the town had searched the woods, and her mother had stopped speaking above a whisper for the rest of her life, and Wendell had—what? What had seven-year-old Wendell done? She had played with an imaginary friend that year. Astrid. She remembered Astrid the way you remember a fever, vivid and wrong. And then the family had moved, and the imaginary friend had faded, and her sister had become a photograph on a mantel and a hole shaped like a girl.

The district had written to ask about *her* enrollment. In November. The month after her sister vanished. Her parents had pulled her out of school—or been asked to—or—

She put the letter down very carefully, the way you put down something that might go off.

* * *

The sensible thing—the thing any reasonable person would do—was to stop. Photocopy the page, take it to the principal, take it to the sheriff, take it out of town to a state investigator, and let institutions handle institutions. Wendell had been a teacher for nineteen years. She believed in institutions the way you believe in a bridge you've driven over ten thousand times: not with passion, but with habit.

But the principal had smiled at her when she'd asked about Eli, a smile like a hand laid flat on a table to show it was empty, and the clerk at the district office had gone quiet for three seconds, and nobody in this town had yet said a single true thing to her without being made to.

So she did the unreasonable thing. She kept reading.

She went back through the ledgers with new eyes, and once she knew to look, the pattern had texture. The transfer years weren't just marked—they were *managed*. In the weeks before each transfer, attendance across the whole school dipped. Not dramatically. A flu here, a family trip there. As if the town, without coordinating, without ever saying a word to one another, collectively held its breath. In 1954, three separate families had taken their children out of school "for harvest" in October—families that, she confirmed in the census box two shelves over, had no farms.

In the 1934 volume, in the margin beside Henry Boone's transfer line, in pencil, in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the page, someone had written a single word: *paid.*

She photographed it with her phone. She photographed everything—every transfer line, every stamp, the letter from 1974, the column of years on her legal pad. Her phone's storage filled and she kept photographing, and the flash strobed in the dark of the basement like something trying to signal.

Near the end of the 2014 ledger, tucked in the binding, she found a carbon copy of a form she didn't recognize. Not a district form. No letterhead. Just a title, centered, in a font that looked older than the paper it was printed on:

*LEASE RENEWAL — ACKNOWLEDGMENT OF TERMS.*

Most of the text was illegible, the carbon faded to ghosts, but the signature line at the bottom was clear, and the name signed there was clear, and Wendell read it and felt the temperature of the basement drop in a way that had nothing to do with the pipes.

*E. Vane, Executor for the Original Families.*

Edmund Vane. She knew the name from the historical society's plaque outside the library—*Edmund Vane, 1841–1919, Founder and First Selectman.* A man who had been dead for a century, signing a form for a transfer that happened in 2014.

Or: a man whose name was still being used, the way a company uses a founder's name long after the founder is dust. The way a lease gets renewed by whoever holds the office. The way—

The way a dying matriarch might sign, if she were the last one left.

Cora Vane. The name surfaced from her second week in town, from the grocery store, from the way people said it: *the Vane house, old Miss Vane, she won't last the winter.* Wendell had filed it away as local color. Small towns always had their dying matriarchs, their houses with histories.

But small towns didn't have ledgers with a child's name in them every ten years, on schedule, for a hundred and forty years, stamped *records complete* with no destination, and a signature of a founder who refused to stop founding.

* * *

She climbed the stairs at nearly six, and the hallway lights had switched to their night cycle, and her own shadow walked ahead of her down the corridor, long and doubled on the polished floor.

At the top of the stairs, she hung the key back on its hook behind the dead mop, and her hand was steady, and that steadiness felt like the most horrifying thing she'd learned all day. Because she understood, suddenly, what the pattern demanded of her—not just to see it but to *do the math*, and the math was simple, and she had done it in the basement without letting herself finish it, and now, in the empty hallway, with the heating clicking its patient pulse, she finished it.

2014. Plus ten.

This year. This autumn. This month, maybe. Eli had transferred in October of a year ending in four, and Marguerite Delacroix had transferred in October of 2014, and the schedule had never once, in one hundred and forty years, missed a payment.

And there was a girl in her fourth-period class who drew a fenced wellhead in the margins of her worksheets without ever looking down at her own hand. Marisol Reyes—Reyes, her own name, a name she'd chosen for herself at twenty-two when she'd needed one that wasn't attached to a vanished sister and a silent mother, a name she'd picked out of the air, or thought she had.

Marisol had drawn the wellhead forty times. Wendell had counted.

She turned off the hallway light and stood in the dark, listening to the building breathe, and made herself say it in words, because things said in words could be acted on:

"The next name is due. And I know whose it is."

Down the hall, in the dark classroom where Marisol sat in the third row, something in the silence seemed to lean in and listen.
