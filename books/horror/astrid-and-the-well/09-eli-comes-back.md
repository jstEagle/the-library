## 9. Eli Comes Back

He was standing at the bus stop on Monday morning like he'd never left.

Wendell saw him from the crosswalk—small, still, in a jacket that looked two sizes too big now, as if the week had shrunk him. The fog hadn't burned off yet and Eli stood inside it the way a nail stands inside a wall: driven in, flush, part of the structure. Other kids gave him a wide berth without seeming to know they were doing it. They curved around him like water around a stone.

She'd called his house twice last week. She'd filed the paperwork, sent the truancy letter, sat through Principal Hargrove's smooth little speech about *family circumstances* and *some things we don't pry into here, Ms. Reyes*. She'd told herself it wasn't her business, and had failed to believe herself for six straight days.

Now she crossed the street faster than she meant to.

"Eli."

He turned. That was the first wrong thing. Ten-year-old boys don't turn like that—slowly, all at once, shoulders and head moving together, no flick of the eyes first. He turned the way a security camera turns.

"Hi, Ms. Reyes," he said. His voice was fine. His voice was completely normal, and somehow that was worse than if it had been ruined.

"You're back."

"Yes ma'am."

"Where were you?"

And here was the thing she would turn over for nights afterward, the thing she'd examine from every angle like a stone with something coiled underneath: he didn't lie. He didn't say *we went to my grandma's* or *I was sick*. He just looked at her with those pale, washed-out eyes and said, "I don't know yet."

Then the bus came, doors folding open with a hydraulic sigh, and he got on, and she stood in the fog watching through the smudged glass as he took a window seat and put his forehead against the cool of it and closed his eyes like a man conserving himself for something long.

* * *

In class he was worse than absent.

The other children had absorbed his return the way soil absorbs rain—no comment, no curiosity, just a general settling. When Wendell said, "Class, Eli's back with us, let's make him feel welcome," twenty heads nodded in unison and nobody smiled, and Marisol Vega, second row by the window, did not look up from her notebook at all. Her pencil kept moving. Wendell caught a glimpse of it later: spirals. Page after page of tight, concentric spirals, drawn smaller and smaller toward a center that never arrived.

Eli knew all the answers. That was the second wrong thing. Last month he'd been a middling student, bright but restless, always losing his place in the reading. Now when she called on him he answered without looking at the board, without looking at his book, in a flat reciting voice, correct every time, as though the questions were being read to him somewhere just behind his ears.

At recess she watched him through the classroom window. He didn't play. He stood at the edge of the blacktop where the grass began, facing the tree line, hands in pockets, and after a while she realized he was facing not the trees specifically but the low ground beyond them—the dip in the land where, according to the old plat map she'd found in the library, the original town well had been sunk before they capped it and built the iron fence.

She went out to him. The cold came up through her shoes.

"Eli. You want to tell me what happened?"

He considered this seriously, the way you'd consider a math problem. "I was somewhere wet," he said finally. "But I wasn't drowning. It's hard to explain. There was a sound like a throat clearing, but very slow. Like it took all night to clear." He shrugged. "I'm probably not supposed to talk about it."

"Who told you that?"

"The water," he said, reasonably, the way you'd say *the teacher* or *my mom*. Then he blinked, and some of the boy came back into his face for a moment—confusion, fear, a kid waking up in a room he doesn't recognize—and he said, quietly, "Ms. Reyes, can I ask you something? Did I miss anything? While I was gone. Did anybody—" He stopped. Started over. "Is everybody still here?"

"Everybody's here," she said.

He nodded, satisfied, and walked back toward the swings, and Wendell stood on the cold blacktop with her heart going like a trapped bird, because she understood that he hadn't been asking about the class.

* * *

The desk was the third thing.

Fourth period, silent reading, she was circulating the rows and she saw it: marks on the desktop near his elbow. At first she thought gum scars, old graffiti. But they were fresh—the wood pale where the finish had been dug away—and they weren't letters or names.

They were rings.

Concentric circles, scratched over and over until the grooves went dark, ring inside ring inside ring, and at the center of each one a small deep point, as if he'd been trying to drill through to whatever lived under the wood. There must have been thirty of them. Some were neat. Some were frantic, overlapping, gouged so deep the grain had splintered up around them like flesh around a wound.

"Eli," she said softly.

He didn't stop. His hand kept moving, the point of his compass—he'd taken it from his pencil case, she realized; he'd done this with a compass—tracing another circle with the mechanical patience of a clock hand.

"Eli." She put her hand over his.

His skin was cold. Not autumn-cold. Cold like a jar taken from the back of a fridge, cold like something that had been stored. Under her palm she felt the tremor running through his arm, a fine continuous vibration, and when he looked up at her his eyes were wet and wide and for one second entirely, terribly present.

"I can't get them clean," he whispered. "I keep washing and they won't come off. They're under the skin. Look."

He held up his hands.

She looked. And there was nothing—his hands were chapped and ordinary, a boy's hands, a healing scrape on one knuckle—but he stared at them with such absolute conviction that she felt the stain anyway, felt it the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue, a shape just below the threshold of seeing. Dark rings around his fingers. Water-marks. The kind a bathtub leaves.

"They rise at night," he said. "When I'm asleep they climb up past my wrists. My mom thinks I'm drawing on myself. I'm not drawing on myself." His voice cracked, dropped to almost nothing. "Ms. Reyes, am I still there? Part of me? Sometimes I think I only came back halfway. Like a coat left on a hook. Like most of me is still down where it's wet, and what's sitting in class is just the part that could walk."

The bell rang. The other students began packing up, chairs scraping, the ordinary noise of an ordinary afternoon flooding back in, and Eli snapped his compass shut and sat with his hands folded on the ruined desk like a child at a funeral, and Wendell stood beside him unable to speak, unable to touch him again, because she was afraid of what her own skin would feel.

* * *

She drove to his house after school. A narrow clapboard place on Corbett Street with a rusted swing set in the yard and a pickup truck up on blocks. His mother, Dana Pruitt, opened the door before Wendell knocked—she'd seen the car pull up—and Wendell watched the hope drain out of the woman's face in real time, replaced by a tired, practiced civility that Wendell was beginning to understand was Hollow Creek's native language.

"Ms. Reyes. Come in, I'll put coffee on."

"Dana, I'm not here about attendance. I'm here because Eli isn't okay."

Dana Pruitt held the door wider. Inside, the house was clean and dim and smelled of bleach—not the pleasant lemon kind, but the industrial kind, the kind you buy by the gallon. Wendell noticed the smell before she noticed anything else, and then she noticed everything else: the plastic sheeting folded in the corner of the living room. The bucket and mop by the stairs. The towels stacked on the banister, dark-stained even after washing, and Dana following her gaze and quickly turning away.

"He wets the bed," Dana said. "He's ten. It happens."

"It's not urine," Wendell said quietly. "Is it."

It wasn't a question, and Dana didn't treat it as one. She sat down heavily on the couch arm and pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes, and for a moment she looked ten years older than she was, and Wendell thought: *she knows. Whatever this town knows, she knows some version of it, and she's alone with it.*

"He came home Thursday," Dana said. "Just walked up the drive at four in the morning. Dry. Clean. Shoes not even muddy. I asked him forty times where he'd been and he says—I don't know yet. Who talks like that? What does that even mean?" Her hands came down. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and frightened. "My grandmother used to say the creek keeps what it catches and gives back what it doesn't need. I used to think that was just something old people said."

"What else did your grandmother say?"

"That some years are thirsty years." Dana laughed, a small broken sound with no humor anywhere in it. "And then she'd spit over her shoulder, like that does anything. Look—Ms. Reyes. You seem like you actually care, which around here is either brave or stupid, I haven't decided. So I'll tell you what I tell myself. He's home. He's eating. He goes to school. Whatever happened, it's over, and if I don't say that out loud every morning I will lose my mind, and my son needs me not to lose my mind." She stood, arms crossed, done. "You should go home before dark. Everybody says that too."

* * *

That night Wendell sat at her kitchen table with the enrollment ledger open in front of her and a pot of tea going cold, and she made herself look at the pattern again, because looking away had started to feel like complicity.

Every seven years, going back through the decades: a name, followed by *transferred*. No forwarding school. No address. Just the word, in different secretaries' handwriting across 140 years, always tidy, always final. She'd copied the names onto an index card. Eleven names. Eleven children who had come back—or hadn't—to houses where mothers scrubbed dark stains out of sheets and grandmothers spat over their shoulders.

A knock at the door.

Not a knock—a tapping. Three fingers, light, patient, at exactly the height of a person standing on her porch steps. Wendell's whole body went cold before her mind caught up, and then she was moving, through the dark hallway, hand on the deadbolt, telling herself it was a neighbor, a student, anyone.

Astrid stood on the porch in the porch-light's yellow cone, unchanged, ageless, the same girl from thirty years ago with the same ribbon in her hair, and her face was the worst part—not mysterious, not eerie, but *afraid*, the plain animal fear of someone watching a car slide toward the edge of a cliff.

"You talked to him," Astrid said. It wasn't a question either. Nothing tonight was a question. "The Pruitt boy. You touched his hands."

"How do you know that?"

"Because he's a door now, Wendell. They bring them back half-open, so the rest of us can see through." Astrid glanced past her, into the house, at the ledger lying open on the kitchen table, and something in her expression collapsed inward. "You found the book. Oh, you shouldn't have found the book."

"Astrid—"

"Don't." The name landed between them like a dropped glass. Astrid flinched from her own name the way she always did, stepping back off the step into the fog, and her voice came thinner from the gray. "Listen to me. He scratches circles because circles are how it counts. Rings in the wood, rings in the water, one ring for every year it waits. He's not sick and he's not haunted. He's a receipt, Wendell. The Well gave him back to show the town it *can* give things back." A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quiet enough that Wendell had to lean out into the cold to hear it. "Which means next time, it won't have to give anything back at all."

The fog swallowed her. Not gradually—completely, between one blink and the next, the way a lamp goes out.

Wendell stood on the porch a long time, hugging her arms against the cold, listening to the empty street. Down at the end of it, past the fence line, past the dark dip in the land, she could just make out the iron fence around the old wellhead, a thin black lattice against the gray.

And beneath the wind, beneath the far-off hum of the highway, she heard it: a sound like a throat clearing.

Very slow.

Like it had all night.
