## 10. Town Meeting

The notice was stapled to the door of the Hollow Creek General Store, and it was printed on cream cardstock with a tasteful serif font, which somehow made it worse.

**QUARTERLY TOWN MEETING — ALL RESIDENTS WELCOME. GRANGE HALL, THURSDAY 7 PM. AGENDA: ROAD MAINTENANCE, HARVEST FUND, OLD BUSINESS.**

*Old business.* Wendell stood in the cold outside the store, her breath fogging, and read the words three times. Old business. As if Eli's empty desk were a line item. As if a hundred and forty years of missing children could be folded into the minutes between road maintenance and the harvest fund.

She went inside and bought coffee she didn't want, mostly because the woman at the register, a broad-faced woman named Dill whom Wendell knew only as the mother of one of her fourth-graders, looked like she wanted to be asked about it.

"Town meeting Thursday," Wendell said, casual as she could make it.

"Everybody comes." Dill counted change with the unhurried competence of someone who had never once been robbed. "You'll want to come too. New in town, it's good to be seen."

"Will the council be there?"

Something moved behind Dill's eyes—not fear exactly. More like the flicker of a person deciding which version of the truth to hand a stranger. "Council's always there. It's their meeting, near enough." She slid the change across. "You teach at the school now, don't you. Ms. Ishikawa."

"I do."

"Kids like you." Dill said it as a fact, and then, quieter, almost to herself: "Be good if you came. Be good if everybody came." She turned to the next customer, and the conversation was over, sealed shut like a well cover.

Wendell walked home through the fog with the cardstock notice in her pocket, and all the way up the hill she could feel it against her hip like a summons.

* * *

Thursday came gray and windless, the fog sitting on the town like a lid on a pot. The Grange Hall had been built in 1911 according to the plaque by the door, and it smelled of wood polish and decades of potluck steam. Folding chairs in rows, a folding table at the front with a water pitcher and five glasses, a banner over the stage that read **HOLLOW CREEK: ROOTED AND GROWING** in letters that had gone the color of weak tea.

Wendell arrived early and sat in the back row. She had spent the day drafting what she would say, revising it in the margins of her lesson planner between arithmetic and spelling, and by evening she had it down to three sentences. She'd learned, over years of school board meetings in three different districts, that you got one clean shot at a room. After that, they stopped listening and started defending.

The room filled. That was the first thing that unsettled her—how completely it filled. Nearly two hundred people, and she recognized faces from her classroom, from the store, from the church with the leaning steeple. Families sat together. The Hollises, whose daughter Dana was in her third period, occupied the same row as the Brandts and the Okonkwos and a gray-haired man Wendell had seen every morning sweeping the sidewalk in front of the bank. There were no strangers here. There was only her.

At seven exactly, the five council members took the stage. Wendell knew three of them by sight: Mayor Alden Prentiss, a soft-bodied man with a barbered silver mustache and the patient smile of a man who had never lost an election because no one had ever run against him; Councilwoman Ruth Okonkwo, whose son was in Wendell's first period and who had once brought Wendell a tin of shortbread with the word *WELCOME* piped on it in icing; and Councilman Harlan Voss, who ran the hardware store and whose handshake always came a half-second too late, as if he were deciding whether to extend it.

They ran the meeting the way she imagined their grandfathers had run it. Road maintenance: the culvert on Miller Road would be cleared before the rains. Harvest fund: the Vane estate had again declined to contribute, and the council had again voted to respect the estate's wishes. A laugh line about Cora Vane's stinginess that got a warm, easy chuckle from the room, and Wendell felt her skin prickle, because she had stood in that dying woman's house and seen the calendar on the wall with its days crossed off like a countdown, and there was nothing funny in it at all.

Then Prentiss said, "Now. Old business," and the room's warmth changed temperature. It didn't go cold. It went *still*—the stillness of people settling in, of an audience that knows the show.

And Wendell stood up.

Her chair scraped, and two hundred heads turned with the smooth synchrony of a single animal. She walked down the aisle with her three sentences in her fist, and she was surprised to find her voice steady.

"My name is Wendell Ishikawa. I teach at the school. I've been here six weeks." She let that sit. "Six weeks ago, my class had twenty-two students in it. Now it has twenty-one. Eli Marsh stopped coming to school on a Tuesday, and when I asked about him, I was told he'd moved. His family hasn't moved. Their truck is still in their yard. Their lights are still on at night." She turned to face the council, and she made herself look at each of them. "And I've been in the school basement. I've read the enrollment ledgers. Every few years, going back a hundred and forty years, a child is listed as *transferred*—and no one can tell me transferred to where. I'm asking this council, in front of this town: where did Eli Marsh go?"

Silence.

It was not the silence of guilt, which is fidgety and hot. It was not the silence of denial, which is loud and fast. It was the silence of a room full of people who had expected this question the way you expect rain in a wet season—and who had already decided, long ago, what they would do when it came.

Ruth Okonkwo looked at her hands. Harlan Voss looked at the wall. And Mayor Prentiss looked at Wendell with an expression she would spend a long time trying to name, and never quite manage: pity, yes, but pity with a foundation under it, the way a doctor pities a patient whose diagnosis he has delivered a hundred times before.

"Ms. Ishikawa." He said it gently. "Why don't you come up here, so we don't have to shout."

"I'm fine where I am."

"Of course." He folded his hands on the table. "Eli Marsh is at home. He's been unwell. There's a sickness that moves through children in this valley sometimes—not often, but it comes, and when it comes, the families keep the child close, and the school understands, and the child comes back when he comes back. You'll see him soon, I expect. I hear he's improving."

"That's not what his mother told me. His mother told me he was fine on Monday and gone on Tuesday and that she wasn't permitted to discuss it."

"Mothers say all kinds of things when they're frightened." Prentiss's voice never rose. It was the voice of a man talking a spooked horse down. "And you've been reading old ledgers in a damp basement. Those books are full of clerical errors. *Transferred* was a catchall term—children sent to live with relatives, children whose families left in the night owing money. It meant nothing sinister. It meant bookkeeping."

"A hundred and forty years of bookkeeping," Wendell said. "Every disappearance in a year ending in the same digit. What are the odds of that, Mayor? Because I did the math, and I teach math, and the odds are—"

"Ms. Ishikawa." It was Ruth Okonkwo who spoke, and her voice cracked on the second word, and Wendell stopped. The councilwoman lifted her head, and her eyes were wet, and she was looking at Wendell the way you look at someone standing on a ledge. "Please. Sit down."

"I'm asking a simple question."

"You're asking it in the wrong room." Okonkwo's voice dropped to almost nothing. "You're asking it in front of the wrong people. There are children in this room."

And there were—Wendell saw them now, scattered among the adults: Dana Hollis with her braids, a Voss boy picking at the hem of his shirt, half a dozen others, all of them sitting unnaturally still, all of them looking at the floor. They had known. She understood it in one cold drop. *The children always know. That's chapter five of my own life now. Marisol with her drawing. The silence around her desk.*

"Then let the children step outside," Wendell said, "and tell me the truth."

Prentiss sighed, and the sigh had the weight of genuine weariness behind it, and that was the thing that frightened her most—not menace, not threat, but the bone-deep tiredness of a man who had given this speech before, maybe to her predecessor, maybe to her predecessor's predecessor, and would give it again.

"You want the truth," he said. "All right. Let's give her the truth, Ruth, since she's asked for it in front of God and everybody." He gestured at the hall around them, at the banner, at the tea-colored letters. "Look around, Ms. Ishikawa. What do you see?"

"A town," Wendell said. "A town that loses a child every few years and calls it bookkeeping."

"A town," Prentiss said, "where no one has died of hunger since 1889. You know what was here in 1889? Nothing. A failed logging camp and a graveyard. The famine of that winter killed thirty-one people, and the ones who survived did it by doing something they were ashamed of, and they swore they'd never be that hungry again. And they never were. Not in the flood of '36, when every town in this valley lost people and we lost shingles. Not in the fire of '61, when the blaze came down the ridge and turned at our boundary like it had hit glass. Not in the flu, not in the drought, not in any of it." He spread his hands, and his voice stayed gentle, and that was the horror of it, Wendell thought, the gentleness. "You've read the ledgers. Read the county records too, sometime. Read the newspapers from the towns around us. Hollow Creek has been lucky for a hundred and forty years, and luck like ours isn't luck. It's a purchase. And everything has a price."

"Children," Wendell said. "The price is children."

"Sometimes," Prentiss said. "Rarely. Once in a generation, and the families that give—" he caught himself, and Wendell saw the word he'd swallowed, *give*, replaced by nothing at all, "—the families involved are cared for. They want for nothing. Their names are on the good plots in the cemetery. Their businesses survive every hard year. And the child—" for the first time his voice wavered, just slightly, "—the child isn't dead, Ms. Ishikawa. We don't know what the child is. But Eli Marsh came home, and Eli Marsh will grow up, and in twenty years Eli Marsh will sit on this council and understand what I understand now, which is that the alternative to our arrangement is the famine of 1889, and I have seen the photographs from 1889, and I will not preside over that. Not for anything you say tonight. Not for anything."

The room had not moved. Two hundred people, and not one of them had looked away from the mayor, and not one of them had looked at Wendell with anger. That was the part she hadn't prepared for. She had rehearsed for anger, for denial, for someone calling her a liar or an outsider or worse. What she got instead was compassion. Dana Hollis's mother was looking at her the way you look at a grieving woman at a funeral. Harlan Voss shook his head slowly, sadly, a man watching a car go over a cliff he'd tried to warn the driver about.

"Ms. Ishikawa," Prentiss said. "You lost someone yourself. We know that. It's in the record of why you left your last district. And I think—I honestly think—that grief is doing your thinking for you. You came here looking for a monster because you met one once, and this town looks foggy and old and strange, and so you've decided we're it." He paused. "We're not it. We're just people who pay a bill. You pay yours, wherever you're from. We pay ours. The only difference is we know what ours costs, and we've made our peace, and we'd be grateful—grateful—if you'd let us keep our peace."

Wendell stood in the aisle and felt the full weight of what he was saying settle over her, and underneath it, worse, the full weight of the room's agreement. Justice, she understood then, was not being denied. It was simply not applicable. You cannot prosecute a town for a lease it signed before your grandmother was born. You cannot shame people who have already done their shaming, privately, generation after generation, and folded it into potlucks and road maintenance and harvest funds. There was no audience here for her outrage. There was only a room full of people who pitied her, and a mayor who spoke of children's prices in the tone of a man discussing a water bill, and somewhere out in the fog, a fenced wellhead that she now understood was not a relic.

It was a meter. And it was running.

"Thank you for your honesty, Mayor," Wendell heard herself say, and her voice sounded far away, calm, someone else's voice. "One more question, then I'll sit down."

Prentiss inclined his head, magnanimous in victory. "Of course."

"When is the next payment due?"

The stillness that went through the room this time was different. Colder. Quicker. A flinch moving through two hundred bodies like wind through wheat. Ruth Okonkwo closed her eyes. Harlan Voss's hand found his knee and gripped it.

And Mayor Alden Prentiss smiled his patient smile and said, "There's no schedule, Ms. Ishikawa. It's not a subscription. It's a covenant, and covenants keep their own time." He picked up his water glass. "Now, if there's no further old business—"

Wendell sat down. She sat through the vote on the culvert and the discussion of the harvest fund and the closing benediction, and she did not hear a word of it, because she was doing the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do since the basement, since the ledgers, since the years ending in the same digit.

Every disappearance, a generation apart. The last one—she'd found it in the third ledger, the entry that had made her hands go cold—was 1974. A girl, age seven. Listed as *transferred*.

Fifty years. A generation.

Which meant the ledger's next line was already waiting to be written. And there was a girl in her third period who drew wells in the margins of her arithmetic and whispered about a thirsty year, and whose name, when Wendell got home and pulled the class roster from her bag with shaking hands, she read and reread under the porch light until the letters blurred.

*Marisol Vega. Age nine. Enrolled this September. Family: recent arrival.*

Recent arrival. No roots. No family name on the good plots. No councilman's son to protect her.

The fog pressed against the windows of her house all night, and Wendell sat at her kitchen table until three in the morning, and somewhere in the dark, from the direction of the old wellhead on the hill, she thought she heard—very faintly, very patiently—water moving.
