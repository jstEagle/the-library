## 1. The House That Counts Wrong

The bus from the coast let me off at the bottom of Haldenmark's only hill at four in the afternoon, which in late November this far north might as well have been midnight with ambitions. The driver didn't so much stop as slow down long enough for me to get off, and then he was gone, taillights shrinking up the road like a rumor being withdrawn.

I stood there with two suitcases and a document box and my grandmother's death certificate, which I had not yet managed to read all the way through without my throat doing something unscheduled, and I looked up at the village that had raised my mother and, at one remove, me.

Haldenmark in winter looks like a photograph of itself. That was my first thought, and I want to be honest about it, because a folklorist's first thoughts are supposed to be observations, and that one was. The houses were dark timber under white roofs, the snow unbroken except where someone had walked to the church and back, and the whole valley sat in that blue hour silence that photographers chase and villagers simply live inside. Smoke rose from chimneys in perfectly straight columns because there was no wind. It was beautiful. It was also, I noted, doing that thing beautiful places do, which is daring you to find them sinister.

I was thirty-four years old, I had a doctorate in comparative folklore, I had spent a decade professionally collecting the songs people sing to keep the dark at manageable size, and I want to be clear that at no point on that first evening did I think anything was wrong. Grief does its own set dressing. When your grandmother dies and you inherit a house at the edge of a valley that never learned to need you, everything looks slightly like a stage set waiting for a play you haven't been cast in. That's not a haunting. That's just being an orphan with luggage.

I let myself in with the key she'd mailed me years ago in an envelope with no note, on the principle, apparently, that a key requires no explanation. The house smelled the way it had always smelled: birch smoke, wool, and the faint camphor ghost of a chest that had been locked since before I was born. The radiators ticked. Somewhere a pipe knocked, twice, and stopped.

"Hello, Bestemor," I said to the empty kitchen, because I am the kind of man who talks to empty kitchens, and then I put the kettle on, because I am also the kind of man who believes tea is a load-bearing structure.

* * *

The cataloguing began the next morning, as the light came up gray and sideways through the frost-ferned windows. I had promised the solicitor an inventory: furniture, effects, anything of value, anything of record. What I had actually promised myself was three days of being busy in the only place my grandmother had ever been entirely herself, and if being busy was also a way of not feeling anything, well. Grief and productivity have always been close cousins in my family. We come from people who sing while they dig.

Her house was a museum of a life conducted with great care and no waste. Books on the shelves she'd actually read — spines cracked at the same pages, marginalia in a hand like bird tracks. Knitting in progress on the third chair from the stove, needles still crossed mid-row, which stopped me for a moment because it looked less like an abandoned project than an interrupted sentence. The kitchen table with its oilcloth. The good dishes behind glass. The radio she'd listened to at volume six, never seven, never five.

And the photographs. The photographs were everywhere, the way they are in houses where someone has lived for eighty years: walls, stairwell, the landing, the shelf over the stove. My mother as a girl, gap-toothed on a sled. My grandmother young and squinting into a sun that no longer visits this valley in November. Weddings, confirmations, christenings. Whole decades of Haldenmark faces, most of them dead now, all of them looking at the camera with the particular solemnity of people for whom a photograph was an event and not a reflex.

I catalogued them in batches, the way you do. Frame, dimensions, subject, approximate date. It was on the third batch, I think — the stairwell photographs, the older ones — that I noticed the chair.

Here is what I noticed, precisely, because precision is the only prayer I know: in a photograph dated 1911, a confirmation group posed on the church steps, eleven children and a pastor, and at the far left edge of the frame, half-cropped, the arm and back of a wooden chair. No one sat in it. No one stood beside it. It was simply there, at the margin, the way a dog or a bucket might be, except that it was a chair, and chairs imply a sitter, and the sitter was not in the picture.

I noticed it the way you notice anything in an old photograph: not at all, and then completely.

"Prop," I said aloud, to the stairwell. "They brought a chair out for the group and nobody used it." This is a reasonable explanation. Photographers did stage furniture; formal sitters did get posed with chairs whether they sat in them or not; edges of old photographs are full of cropped nonsense. I wrote *chair, cropped, incidental* in the inventory column and moved on.

On the landing there was a wedding photograph from 1938. My grandmother's sister, veil like a net of frost, the whole family arranged in a semicircle on the vicarage lawn. At the left edge — and I want to say I checked the right edge too, and the right edge was clean — the top rail and one turned post of a wooden chair, cropped by the frame, empty.

Different chair. That was the thing my hand noticed before my head did, the way your hand knows a staircase in the dark. The 1911 chair had a plain flat rail. This one had a rail with a carved wave in it, or a rope pattern, something twisted. Different chair, same position, same emptiness. Same margin of the frame, as if the photographer had been told, every time, *leave room at the left*.

I sat down on the landing step with the photograph in my lap for longer than an inventory requires.

There are explanations. I want to be fair to the explanations, because I have spent my professional life among people who leap from a crooked picture to a demon, and I have built a small career on being the man who says *actually, that's dry rot*. So: old cameras cropped unpredictably. Families reused poses. Chairs were expensive and heavy and got carried out to lawns for photographs and left at the edges by tired children. A village with seven founding families might have a habit — a *tradition* — of including a chair in formal pictures. Villages are full of habits that look like superstitions and are really just the sediment of some practical decision made in 1840 and forgotten by 1900.

I wrote *chair, cropped, recurring* in the inventory, and I did not write *tradition?* next to it, and the fact that I did not write it is the first dishonest thing I did in that house, though it was not the last.

* * *

By the third day I had worked through the sitting room and the good parlor, and I had developed a system: I'd hold each photograph up to the window light, note the date, note the subjects, and then — without deciding to, the way you don't decide to check a bruise — look at the left edge.

The chair was in forty-one photographs.

I know the number because I counted, and I know that counting is its own kind of symptom. Forty-one photographs spanning 1904 to 1998. Studio portraits, farmyard tableaus, a christening in the church itself, a funeral gathering outside a door I didn't recognize, my mother's graduation, my grandmother at seventy holding a cake. In every single one, at the left margin, cropped to greater or lesser degree: a wooden chair. Empty.

And here is where the reasonable explanations began, one by one, to do what reasonable explanations do when you lean on them, which is to shift their weight and settle lower into the ground.

The studio portraits were the problem. A studio portrait is composed. A studio photographer controls every inch of the frame; nothing enters it by accident; a chair at the edge of a studio portrait is a chair someone *put there*. I found one from 1923 — my grandmother's brother, aged nine, stiff in a sailor suit against a painted backdrop of a lake he had never seen — and there at the left edge, cropped at the seat, the turned post and rail of a chair. The same chair. I knew it was the same chair the way you know a voice on the telephone. The rail had the carved twist. The wood in the photograph had the pale, unweathered look of a thing kept indoors.

A chair does not attend studio sittings. A chair does not follow a family across sixty years and four photographers and two counties. A chair is not a *mascot*.

Unless it is included on purpose. That was the next explanation, and it was a good one, and I clung to it the way a man clings to a door in weather: the family included the chair deliberately. Some memorial. Some tradition. A chair for a dead relative, set at the edge of every family picture — plenty of cultures set places for the dead at tables; why not at photographs? It would be a custom, a tender one, the kind of thing my grandmother would never have explained to a grandchild because it was simply the water she swam in.

It was a good explanation. It accounted for everything except the fact that in ninety-four years of photographs, across weddings and christenings and studio sessions, not one living member of my family — not one — had ever mentioned it. Not my mother, who talked the ears off every relative she had. Not my grandmother, who in the last decade of weekly phone calls had told me, among other things, the correct way to salt herring, the name of every horse the Brenner family had owned since 1950, and a recurring dream about a door in a hillside. She told me everything. She told me things I did not want to know. She never once said *you'll notice the chair*.

And there was one more thing. I found it on the third evening, in the last album, the one from my own childhood. A photograph of me, aged six, on my grandmother's knee in this very kitchen, both of us laughing at something outside the frame. At the left edge, cropped at the legs: the chair. The pale wood. The twisted rail. And I sat there with my own small laughing face in my hands and I understood, with the cold specific clarity that I would later learn to distrust, that I had *seen this photograph before*. Many times. On this same wall. As a child, I had looked at it, at summer visits, at Christmas visits, and I had never once seen the chair.

That's not remarkable. Children don't see chairs at the edges of pictures; children see themselves. The eye goes where the love is. I told myself that, sitting in the dark kitchen with the frost ferns going blue outside, and it was true, and it settled nothing, because the question was not whether child-me had failed to see the chair.

The question was why adult-me, a professional observer of images, a man who has spent ten years looking at photographs of ritual objects and noticing what's cropped out of them, had looked at this same photograph on this same wall two years ago — I had visited, I had catalogued nothing, I had eaten her bread and recorded her singing and left — and had not seen it then either.

The eye goes where the love is. Fine. But something had also, it seemed, been deciding where my eye was allowed to go.

* * *

I did what I always do when a house gets too quiet, which is put on a recording and let other people's voices fill it. My grandmother's house had a shelf of my old field recordings — cassettes, then minidiscs, then the little hard-drive recorder I'd graduated to — from every summer I'd spent here collecting. I put on a tape from 1998: Marta Brenner, forty then, singing the harvest lullaby in her kitchen while her daughter did dishes, the water running under the melody like a second river.

I know that song. I've transcribed it twice. It has eleven lines, a rising third on *sove nå*, and a verse structure that repeats with variations. I could sing it in my sleep, and possibly have.

I was half-listening, cataloguing the good parlor's remaining photographs — the chair, the chair, the chair, a whole congregation of the chair — when my pen stopped.

Underneath Marta's voice, under the dishwater and the kitchen ambience, there was a rhythm. Not in the song. Under it. A slow, regular sound, soft as a knuckle on wood, in a pattern that didn't match the meter of the lullaby at all. I turned the volume up. The rhythm didn't change. It sat underneath the music the way a current sits under ice — patient, unhurried, entirely indifferent to what was happening above it.

Scraping. That's what I'd have called it in a field report. *Low, periodic scraping or knocking, source unidentified, likely structural.* Pipes. Old houses knock; old tapes hiss; old recordings pick up the building settling around the microphone. I have written that sentence in a dozen reports about a dozen villages, and it has almost always been true.

I played the tape through to the end. The rhythm never stopped, never matched the song, never matched the dishes, never matched anything. It just went on, at its own pace, like something counting.

I took down another tape. 2003. My grandmother singing the same lullaby for me, her voice older, in this kitchen, at this table.

Underneath her voice: the rhythm. Same tempo. Same pattern. Ten years apart, two different rooms, and the same patient counting underneath.

I sat in the good parlor with forty-one photographs of an empty chair on my lap in a stack, and a tape hissing to a close, and the light going out of the sky the way it does here, all at once, like a held breath released. The radiators ticked. A pipe knocked, twice, and stopped.

I am a folklorist. I do not believe in hauntings; I believe in customs, in sediment, in the things villages do for reasons they've forgotten. I believe in chairs carried out to lawns and left at the edges of pictures. I believe in pipes.

But I wrote nothing in the inventory that night, and I left the good parlor photographs face-down on the table, all forty-one of them, in a stack, like a deck I didn't want dealt to me again. And as I climbed the stairs to bed, past the 1911 confirmation and the 1938 wedding and the 1998 kitchen, I caught myself doing something I have never done in any field site in ten years of work.

I said, quietly, to the stairwell, to the house, to whoever keeps the books here:

"I'm only here to sell the place."

The house said nothing. The house was very good at saying nothing. And underneath the floorboards, faint and patient and entirely unbothered by my presence — as if my presence were the one thing here that had never once been in question — something began, very softly, to scrape.
