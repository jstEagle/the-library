## 4. The Count Is Off

Astrid had a system for old houses, and the system had never once lied to her.

Measure the perimeter. Count the windows against the tax records. Walk every room, note every door, and — this was the part she'd learned from a grizzled surveyor named Hollis back in her first year, a man who smelled of pipe smoke and cynicism — count the doors *twice*. Once going up, once coming down. Old houses settled. Old houses lied. But they lied consistently, and consistency was the whole game.

"Eleven doors upstairs," she murmured, tapping her stylus against the clipboard. "Eleven windows on the second floor. That tracks."

The upstairs hallway of the Hollowell farmhouse was long and dim, lit by a single bulb on a pull chain that swayed when there was no draft to sway it. The wallpaper — faded yellow roses on cream — had been hung by someone with more patience than skill, the seams slightly drunk. Dust lay on the floorboards in a fine, even gray, except along one line down the center of the hall, where the dust was disturbed, as though something small had walked back and forth for a very long time.

She told herself it was mice. She told herself a lot of things in that house. The house, she suspected, was telling itself things about her.

She started her second count at the top of the stairs, stylus raised.

One — the linen closet, door swollen shut, smelling faintly of cedar and something sweeter underneath, like overripe apples.

Two — the master bedroom, its four-poster bed draped in a sheet like a patient awaiting surgery.

Three — the small room with the crib, which she had not yet been able to make herself enter. She'd stood in its doorway that morning and felt the temperature change against her face, warm as an open oven, and had written *structural note: heat source?* in her neat surveyor's hand, knowing full well there was no heat source. There was never a heat source.

Four — the child's room with the toys.

She paused at that one. She had catalogued it that morning: tin soldiers in ranks, wooden blocks stacked by color, a rocking horse facing the door. All of it dusted. All of it *arranged*. She'd written *prior occupancy? recent?* and moved on, because moving on was a professional skill and she was a professional.

Five, six, seven — three narrow bedrooms in a row, empty, their windows looking out over the fields where the corn stood too straight, too evenly spaced, like an audience.

Eight — the bathroom, claw-footed tub, mirror with a hairline crack running through it that she could have sworn had been a different shape that morning. Longer. More like a seam.

Nine — a sewing room, bolts of fabric gone stiff as tree bark.

Ten — a room she had no notes on, which troubled her, because she had notes on everything. Small. A bed. A window. A child's drawing pinned to the wall, crayon on butcher paper: a house with a smiley face, and stick figures in the windows, and one stick figure standing outside on the lawn, waving.

She had not written anything down about that drawing. She had looked at it for a long time — how long, she couldn't say — and then she had been in the hallway again, counting.

Ten.

She frowned. Stylus hovering. She'd had eleven on the way up.

"Come on, Ashgrove," she said aloud, because saying things aloud in empty houses made them ordinary. "You counted a closet twice. Happens to everybody."

Except she never counted closets twice. That was the thing about her system. It had never once lied to her.

She started again at the stairs, walking slower this time, touching each doorframe as she passed, the way Hollis had taught her — *the hand remembers what the eye skips, girl.* One. Two. Three. The warm door of the crib room breathed against her palm and she snatched her hand back and kept moving. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten.

Eleven.

She stopped.

The eleventh door had not been there a moment ago. She was nearly certain of it — nearly, because certainty had started leaking out of her sometime around the hallway that measured longer on the return trip, and now it pooled somewhere in her stomach along with the cold. It was a plain door, white paint gone gray at the edges, with a porcelain knob the color of old milk. It sat between the sewing room and the room with the drawing, in a stretch of wall that, according to her own measurements — she checked, flipping back through the clipboard with fingers that wanted to shake — was eleven inches of solid plaster.

Eleven inches. She'd measured it that morning. She'd written it down. *Wall section, 11 in., no openings.*

The door had a hairline crack in its paint, running top to bottom, like the mirror.

"Okay," Astrid said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, and she was briefly, absurdly proud of it. "Okay. You're a door. Doors open, doors close, doors do not *condense out of drywall* because a woman with a clipboard is having a bad afternoon."

From downstairs, faintly, came the sound of August humming. He'd been humming all evening — some tuneless, looping melody that never resolved, the way children's songs don't. It should have been comforting. It was, a little. That was the vertigo of it: every time the house did something impossible, she found herself wanting to go find August, to stand next to his small solid reality the way you'd stand next to a lit fireplace. And every time she remembered that his small solid reality was itself the largest impossibility in the building, the comfort curdled.

She put her hand on the knob.

It was warm. Not oven-warm like the crib room — warm like a hand that had been holding it. Warm like someone on the other side had been waiting, palm pressed to the wood, for a very long time.

She turned it.

The door opened onto a bedroom — a girl's bedroom, 1950s by the wallpaper, pink roses and a border of jumping rope children. A vanity with a lace runner. A bed with a chenille spread, white with pink pom-poms, turned down neatly. A pair of saddle shoes by the door, laces tucked inside, the way polite girls were taught. Everything was clean in the way that nothing in this house was clean — no dust, no gray film, no years. It was clean the way a photograph is clean. Preserved. *Kept.*

"Hello?" Astrid said.

The room did not answer, but the light in it did something wrong — it dimmed at the edges, gently, like a breath drawn in, like the room was gathering itself to speak.

And in the vanity mirror, there was a girl.

Astrid's first thought — her professional, clipboard-brained, desperately rationalizing thought — was *reflection.* She was standing in the doorway; the girl stood in the room; the mirror showed her. Except Astrid was wearing a gray blazer and her hair was coming loose from its clip and she was holding a stylus like a small stupid sword, and the girl in the mirror was wearing a dress — a proper 1950s dress, yellow with white collar, the kind with a full skirt that needed a crinoline — and the girl was not holding anything at all. The girl was standing with her hands clasped in front of her, the way children stood when adults were photographing them. And the girl was looking directly at Astrid, which reflections do, technically, but not like this. Not with *focus.* Not with seventy years of attention behind the eyes.

Astrid stepped into the room. The floorboards did not creak. That was the detail that undid her more than anything else — the silence of her own footsteps, as though the room declined to acknowledge her weight, her presence, her *reality*, and was reserving all its attention for the girl.

The girl stepped out of the mirror.

There was no flash, no ripple, no horror-movie shimmer. She simply was on one side of the glass and then the other, the way a thought moves from the back of the mind to the front. She was perhaps ten years old. Her hair was curled and pinned. Her socks had lace at the tops. Her face was the face of a child who has been polite for a very, very long time, and is at the very end of polite.

She opened her mouth.

No sound came out. Astrid watched her try — watched the small throat work, the lips shape something, watched the girl's whole body lean into the effort the way you lean into a door that's stuck. Nothing. Not a whisper. The silence where her voice should have been was the loudest thing in the house.

The girl pointed — urgently, desperately — at her own mouth, then at Astrid, then at the wall behind her.

And Astrid, who had spent her career reading buildings, reading the grammar of rooms, understood.

*Help me.*

The girl's lips formed it slowly, twice, three times, exaggerating each shape the way you'd speak to someone deaf, the way you'd speak across a great distance. *Help. Me.* Her eyes were wet. Her hands, still clasped, had gone white-knuckled. Behind her, in the mirror, the bedroom was beginning to change — the wallpaper fading, the roses graying, the chenille spread going thin and translucent, like a photograph left in the sun.

"Who are you?" Astrid asked. "What's your name?"

The girl's mouth worked. *Edie.* Maybe. The shape of it, two syllables, soft. Or maybe it was *Eddie*, maybe a boy, maybe Astrid's ears were inventing things now too — but no, the girl was nodding, nodding hard, *yes, yes, that's it, you have it,* and she was pointing again, past Astrid this time, down the hall, toward the stairs, toward the ledger she didn't know about yet, toward something.

Then the girl went still.

Her eyes moved past Astrid's shoulder. Whatever she saw there, it drained the desperation out of her face and replaced it with something worse: resignation. The practiced, weary resignation of a child who has done this before, who has reached the edge of the photograph before, who knows exactly how the light behaves when the room is gathering itself to close.

"Wait — no — hold on—" Astrid turned, as if the hallway could be argued with, as if she could stand in the gap and physically prevent what was coming. "August! August, I need you up here, I need—"

The humming downstairs stopped.

The wall began to seal.

It didn't slam. It didn't crash. It did the gentlest, most terrible thing imaginable: it *healed*. The doorway's edges softened and flowed, plaster creeping over wood like wax over a candle flame, paint smoothing, the hairline crack in the door's face narrowing to a thread, to a hair, to nothing. The girl in the yellow dress pressed both palms flat against the closing seam — Astrid saw her fingers there, small and lace-socked, splayed against the gap — and her mouth made one last shape, wide and silent, and it wasn't *help me* anymore.

It was *remember.*

Then the wall was a wall. Smooth, cream, hung with drunk yellow roses. Eleven inches wide. No openings. Warm to the touch, faintly, like a hand that had recently been pressed against it from the other side.

Astrid stood in the hallway with her stylus hanging loose in her fingers and her breath coming in short, sharp, professional-unease-shaped pieces.

She did the only thing she knew how to do. She counted.

She counted the doors. Ten. Ten doors, where there had been eleven, where there had been twelve, where there had — she was almost sure, she was *almost* sure — been ten at the start. She counted the windows: eleven. She counted again, walking the hall end to end, hand on every frame, and got ten, and then, on the final pass, without the number changing, without anything changing, she got the distinct and unshakable sense that she was being counted *back*. That somewhere in the walls, in the warm places, in the rooms that kept their own books, a tally was being kept of her. Of her blazer, her loose hair, her small stupid stylus, her eleven inches of certainty. That the house had been doing inventory since the moment she'd walked in with her clipboard, and it was very thorough, and it did not make the mistakes she made.

The house didn't count wrong.

*She* counted wrong. That was the trick of it. The house was exactly, perfectly, horribly right, and it simply showed different people different truths — showed her ten doors when it wanted ten doors, twelve when it wanted her to see the girl, and nothing at all when it wanted to keep something hidden. It wasn't a building with secrets.

It was a building with *occupants.*

She thought of the toys in the fourth room, dusted and ranked. The rocking horse facing the door. The stick figure on the lawn, waving. She thought of the disturbed line in the dust down the center of the hallway, small footprints going back and forth, back and forth, for years, for decades, a child pacing a corridor no one else could see the full length of. She thought of seven — why seven? where had seven come from? — and pressed the thought down before it could press back.

"August," she said, and her voice cracked on the second syllable, and she hated it, and she let it crack, because there was no one up here to be professional for except a wall with a girl inside it.

She took the stairs too fast, one hand skimming the banister, and found him in the front hall, sitting on the bottom step with his worn overalls and his gap-toothed grin, swinging his legs, which didn't reach the floor. He looked up at her and his grin faltered — just a flicker, just at the edges — because he was good at reading her, he always had been, even when he'd been nothing but a voice in her mother's garden thirty years ago.

"Astrid," he said. "You went upstairs."

"I went upstairs," she agreed. Her voice was doing something strange — too light, too bright, the voice of a person holding a door shut from the inside. "August. How many doors are there on the second floor of this house?"

He didn't answer right away. He looked at his hands. He swung his legs. And when he looked up at her again, his eyes were the oldest thing she had ever seen on a nine-year-old's face.

"How many did you count?" he asked.

"That's not an answer."

"How many," he said gently, "did it *let* you count?"

The humming started again, low and tuneless, and Astrid realized with a cold, settling dread that it wasn't coming from August at all. It was coming from the walls. From all of them. From upstairs, where a girl in a yellow dress was pressed against the inside of eleven inches of plaster, keeping time.

The house, patient as a field, was counting along.
