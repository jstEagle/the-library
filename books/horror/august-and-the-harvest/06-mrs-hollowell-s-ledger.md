## 6. Mrs. Hollowell's Ledger

The cellar door was the only door in the house that hadn't lied to me yet.

I want to be clear about that, because by hour six of the Hollowell appraisal I had stopped trusting doors on principle. The pantry had shown me three shelves and then five. The hall upstairs had gained a doorway like a man gaining a shadow. Even the front door — a perfectly ordinary door with a perfectly ordinary brass knob — had seemed, when I touched it at noon, faintly warm, as though it had been pleased to see me.

But the cellar door under the stairs was heavy, honest oak, swollen in its frame the way cellar doors get, and when I shouldered it open it resisted me with the pure dumb stubbornness of wood and hinges and nothing else. It was almost a relief. Here, finally, was a house part that was only a house part.

"Appraiser's privilege," I said out loud, because talking to myself had become a habit somewhere around the third impossible hallway, and because the alternative was admitting how quiet the house had gone since August went to "tidy up," which is what he'd said, cheerfully, thirty years after his own death or disappearance or whatever it was, and wandered off down a corridor that I had decided not to watch him go down.

The stairs were stone, worn into shallow bowls by generations of boots. My flashlight found cobwebs first, then jars — rows and rows of them on plank shelves, preserves in colors that made no agricultural sense. Amber, yes. Ruby, fine. But there was a jar of something the color of dusk, and one that caught my light and held it a half-second too long before giving it back, like a handshake that lingers.

I wrote *cellar: shelving, original, good condition* in my report notes, and my handwriting came out smaller than usual.

* * *

Cellars are supposed to smell like earth and old apples and the ghost of onions. This one did, all of those, but underneath it was another smell I couldn't place until I could, and then I wished I couldn't. It was the smell of a room where someone has been waiting a long time without moving. Paper and dust and patience.

The workbench ran along the far wall beneath a window no bigger than a brick — the kind of window you put in a cellar so that if the house ever fell down around you, you'd have a hope of seeing sky. On the bench: a tin cup, a whetstone, a coil of twine, and a book.

Not just any book. A farmer's ledger, the old-fashioned kind, cloth-bound and fat, its spine cracked into a fan of soft ridges from decades of being opened to the same pages. The cover had once been green. It was now the color of moss on the north side of things.

I should have left it. I know that now. Appraisers don't read private papers; we note their existence, we assign them a line item, we move on. But my hand was already on it, and the thing I've learned about this house — the thing I keep learning, the way you keep learning the same bruise is there by pressing it — is that everything in it is exactly where it wants to be found.

I opened it to the middle, because that's what you do, and the middle of Mrs. Hollowell's ledger was a harvest record for 1938, and it was beautiful, in the way that careful handwriting is always a little bit beautiful even when it's telling you something terrible.

*Wheat, 41 bu/acre — best since '29.*
*Corn, standing full, no blight.*
*Orchard heavy. Cider put up: 14 gal.*

And beside the yields, in the same steady hand, a column of names.

*Thomas H., b. 1931*
*Ruthie M., b. 1933*

Ruthie M. had a single line drawn through her. One clean stroke, corner to corner, the way you cross out an item on a list once it's been handled.

I turned back a page. 1937. A thinner year — drought numbers, half-yields, the handwriting pressed harder, the pen digging little trenches in the paper. No names crossed out. Two names added.

I turned forward. 1940: a good year, generous columns, and one name struck through. 1943: excellent yields, two names struck. 1949: the best year in the book, every crop singing off the page, and three names crossed out in a row, the ink of the third still somehow looking wet, though that was impossible, though everything about this house was impossible, and I stood in a cold cellar holding a dead woman's accounts and understood, with the slow sick clarity of ice forming, what I was reading.

It wasn't a ledger of harvests with names beside them.

It was a ledger of prices.

* * *

Here is where I should tell you I slammed the book shut and ran. I didn't. That's the part that shames me later, in the daylight, when I tell this story — and I do tell it, eventually, to exactly one person, who never once makes me feel foolish for it. I didn't run. I sat down on the cold stone step and I read, because I am an appraiser, and appraising is just the polite word for wanting to know what things cost.

I worked backward through the decades, page by page, and the pattern held like a fence holds. Good years, crossed-out names. Thin years, nothing owed, nothing taken — the house going hungry, the family going hungry together, which apparently was the honest arrangement. The bargain wasn't cruelty. That was the worst part. It was arithmetic. Feed me in the good years and I'll make more good years. And what I eat, in the good years, is —

Well. Children. Written in a farmwife's careful hand, dated, recorded, accounted for. Not hidden. Filed. Whatever Mrs. Hollowell had felt about it, she had felt it with excellent penmanship.

I found Edie in 1954. *Edith Hollowell, b. 1946.* Her name wasn't crossed out. It was written twice — once in her mother's hand, and once again below it, in different ink, bluer, younger, pressed so hard the paper had dented. Someone had come back to this page and written her name again, over and over in tiny crowded lines, filling the margin, like a girl practicing her signature, or like a girl making sure she still existed on paper somewhere.

*Edith. Edith. Edith. Edith.*

I closed my eyes for a second. When I opened them, the cellar window showed a sky gone the color of the strange jam.

* * *

There were more ledgers. Of course there were. They lived in a crate under the bench, spines fanned like organ pipes, one per generation, and I told myself I'd check just the last one, just to confirm the pattern ended, just to confirm the house had stopped, sometime, sometime surely —

The final ledger was newer. Seventies cloth, sun-faded orange. The handwriting changed hands halfway through — a rounder script, a man's, then back to a woman's — and the yields got worse as the years went on, the soil tiring, the bargains getting more expensive. By 1968 the entries were mostly wishes. *Maybe next year. Maybe if we ask proper.*

1971 stopped me cold.

*Wheat failed. Corn failed. Orchard bare. Asked for more than we had. Took the boy instead.*

No name. Just *the boy.* And beneath it, in that same desperate hand, a postscript crammed against the edge of the page, the writing slanting downhill like something running:

*Sent him out. Gave him to the Ashgrove child. She plays with him. He will remember the way in. Forgive us. Forgive us. Forgive us.*

The cellar got very quiet, the way a room gets quiet when it's listening to find out what you'll do with what you know.

August. Gap-toothed, overalled, patient August, who had waited thirty years on a porch because he was keeping an appointment he never agreed to. Who wasn't my imaginary friend at all. Who was a message — a letter written in the shape of a little boy and mailed to a nine-year-old girl, because children answer letters from other children when they would burn a letter from a dead farmwife.

They had used him. The Hollowells had taken a boy — *took the boy,* their own words, their own pen — and sent him out into the world as bait dressed as a friend, so that someday, someday, someone would come back and open the door.

And I had come back. Clipboard, flashlight, professional skepticism and all. I had walked in through the front door like a parcel addressed and delivered.

I set the ledger down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be sleeping.

* * *

That's when I saw the last page.

It was torn — not ripped in anger but cut, cleanly, with a blade, the way you remove a page you mean to keep separate. But the stub remained, and the impression of the writing had pressed through onto the page behind it, mirror-reversed, faint as breath on glass. I tilted my flashlight and read it backwards, letter by letter, my lips moving, my heart doing something I refused to call knocking.

One word survived legibly. One surname, pressed deep enough to outlast the cutting.

**Ashgrove.**

Below it, in the same entry, the ghost of a date. This year. This autumn. This harvest.

I stood in Mrs. Hollowell's cellar with forty years of crossed-out children stacked in a crate beside me and my own family name waiting at the bottom of the column like a bill that had finally come due, and I understood the shape of the whole terrible machine at last. Every generation invited something in. Every good year had a price. And the invitation I'd spoken as a lonely nine-year-old rhyming on a porch step — the game I thought I'd invented, the friend I thought I'd dreamed — hadn't been rescinded. It had been *renewed*, the way you renew anything: by showing up. By walking in. By taking inventory of a house that was, all along, taking inventory of me.

Somewhere above me, floorboards creaked — the ordinary kind, I told myself, the honest kind — and through the little brick window the sky had gone the deep bruised gold that comes right before the sun gives up.

I took the ledger. All of them. Evidence, I told myself. Documentation. Line items. I tucked them under my arm like firewood and started up the stairs toward whatever August wanted to show me, and behind me, in the dark, very softly, something began counting.

It got to eleven. Then twelve.

Then it started over, slower, the way you count when you're giving someone time to come down.
