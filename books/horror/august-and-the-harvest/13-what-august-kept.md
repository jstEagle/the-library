## 13. What August Kept

August had kept a great many things for thirty years, and he was proud of every single one of them.

He kept the marbles in the tin under the third-floor eave, all nine of them, polished weekly with his sleeve. He kept the porch swing oiled so it didn't squeak when Astrid came home — because she would come home, he'd always known it, and you don't want to greet somebody with a squeak. He kept the rhyme. *One for the door and two for the wall, three for the friend who answers when you call.* He kept it the way other people keep their own names.

But there were things he hadn't kept. Things the house had taken back, or things he'd never been given in the first place, and tonight — with Astrid sitting on the nursery floor among seven sleeping children and one warm empty bed — those missing pieces started rattling around inside him like marbles in an empty tin.

"I waited," he said. It wasn't the beginning of what he wanted to say, but it was where his mouth decided to start. "I waited so long I wore a groove in the porch boards. See?" He held up his heel, though the groove was in the board, not him, and that was the sort of mix-up that happened to him more lately than before. "Thirty years. That's a lot of Saturdays."

Astrid looked at him the way grown-ups look at things they're afraid to touch. "August. How old are you?"

"Nine." He said it quick, because it was true, because it was the only answer he had. Then, slower: "Except I'm not. Am I."

* * *

Here is what August knew, and here is what August was, and the two lists did not match up, which is a terrible thing to discover about yourself at any age but especially at nine-forever.

He knew the house. Every board, every door that counted wrong, every place the walls breathed in and out. He knew the children upstairs — not their names, mostly, except Edie, who had taught him checkers and lost on purpose and told him once, very seriously, *you're not like us, little one, but don't let the house hear me say so.*

He knew Astrid. He knew her laugh at six years old, her scab collection at seven, the summer she turned eight and stopped believing quite so hard and he'd felt himself go thin as tissue paper. He knew the day she left — the moving truck, the cardboard boxes, her mother's hand pulling hers down the gravel drive while August stood on the porch and waved until his arm ached in a way arms shouldn't ache, being made of whatever he was made of.

And he knew — this was the newest knowing, the one still warm from the oven — that he could not remember arriving anywhere. Not ever. There was no first day. No mother, no father, no before-the-porch. Other children had befores. Edie talked about hers sometimes, in the dreaming hours: a kitchen that smelled of bread, a dog named Biscuit. August had a porch. That was all. A porch and a rhyme and a girl named Astrid, and he had always assumed that was just how imaginary friends came packaged.

Tonight, sitting cross-legged on the nursery floor while the house breathed its slow harvest-breath around them, he finally asked the question out loud.

"Astrid. When you made me up. What did I look like? Before you saw me, I mean. In your head first."

She frowned. "You looked like you. Gap teeth, overalls, cowlick. You always looked like you."

"But where'd the picture come from?" He leaned forward. This mattered. He could feel it mattering the way weather matters. "Kids make friends out of stuff. Cartoons. Neighbors. Bits of everybody. What bits was I?"

Astrid opened her mouth. Closed it. And August watched something move across her face like a cloud crossing a cornfield — slow, wide, and taking all the light with it.

"I don't know," she whispered. "I never asked. You were just... there. From my first memory of this house, you were on that porch."

"That's funny," said August, and laughed his gap-toothed laugh, except it came out wobbly. "Because that's funny, because — see, I've got a picture too. I've had it my whole whole whole life, tucked behind everything else, and I never looked at it straight on because looking at it straight on felt like a rule I'd break. But tonight the rules feel soft. Tonight everything feels soft as old apples." He took a breath, which he didn't strictly need, and let it out, which helped anyway. "Want to know what's in the picture?"

* * *

The picture was this:

A boy in overalls standing at the end of a lane, holding a paper bag with his supper in it. Except it wasn't supper. August didn't know how he knew that, but he knew it the way you know a dream isn't about the thing it's about. The bag was a message. The boy was a message. Somebody had folded him up careful, like a note passed under a schoolroom desk, and sent him down the lane and out into the world to find one particular girl.

His name had been August Hollowell.

The knowing arrived all at once, the way the house's doors arrived — not gradually, but suddenly present, as if they'd always been there and you'd simply miscounted. August Hollowell. Born 1962. Taken 1971. Nine years old.

"Oh," said August. His voice sounded far away, like it was coming from the end of the hallway that grew longer on the return trip. "Oh, that's — Astrid, that's my name. My real-real one. Not the game one."

"You're shaking," Astrid said. She reached for his hand. Her hand was warm and real and it fit his exactly right, which made sense now, didn't it, because hands that fit are hands that remember.

"The house gave me to you," he said. The words tumbled out faster now, tumbling like the marbles he'd kept so carefully, spilling everywhere. "That's what the picture means. That's why there's no before. I didn't get made up, Astrid. I got *sent*. Nineteen seventy-one, the year they took me — the house wrapped me up small and young and sent me down the road to the next family, the next generation, because that's how it works, that's how it's *always* worked—"

"Slow down." But her eyes were huge and wet and she wasn't breathing right either.

"It keeps a boy," August said. "Every so often. For interest, like Edie says, like the ledger says. And then it sends him out as a friend. An imaginary friend. To some little kid whose family will someday, someday, someday come back to this house." He tapped his own chest, twice, the way you knock on a door. Gentle. Polite. The way you knock when you're the letter and not the mailman. "Somebody has to open the door from the outside, Astrid. That's the rule. The house can't fetch. It can only *wait*. So it mails itself a key."

"And the key is you."

"And the key is me." He smiled, because smiling was the only tool he'd ever really owned, and even now — especially now — it worked. Wobbly, but it worked. "I'm not your imagination, Astrid Ashgrove. I'm your *mail*. Thirty years late, but the post office here runs different."

* * *

They sat with that a while. The nursery breathed around them. One of the sleeping children murmured something about rain.

It should have been a relief, August thought. Finding out you were real. Real boys had mothers. Real boys had birthdays, actual ones, with cakes. Real boys got to grow up and grow hair on their faces and complain about their backs. Somewhere in him a small voice was cheering, *see, see, you were never pretend, you mattered, you counted* —

But counting, in this house, was a complicated thing to celebrate.

"I remembered wrong," he said quietly. "All this time. I thought you made me because you were lonely after your mama died. I thought I was yours. Your invention. Your — your *present*." He picked at a thread on his overall knee. "Turns out I was the house's present. Wrapped in your loneliness so you'd keep me. So you'd carry me out of here and grow up and get a job appraising houses—" he said the big words careful, they were new-ish to him, "—and someday walk up these steps with a clipboard and count the doors."

"August—"

"Eleven doors," he said. "Twelve. Ten. You counted them today and the house let you, because the house has been waiting thirty years for somebody to count. That's the appointment. That's what I kept. Not the porch swing. Not the marbles." He looked up at her, and his eyes were doing the hot thing, the thing eyes do that he'd only ever seen other people's do. "Me. I was the appointment. I was the envelope. And you—" his voice cracked clean through, "—you opened it."

Astrid pulled him against her, and August let himself be held, and it was confusing, being held, because half of him was nine years old and had waited thirty years for exactly this, and the other half was a stolen boy from 1971 who understood, now, what the warm empty bed beside Edie's meant. Whose bed it had been. Who had slept in it for one night before the house folded him up like a letter.

"Astrid," he said into her shoulder. "There's something else. In the picture. At the bottom."

She pulled back to look at him.

"There's writing on the bag. Like a label. Like the address part of a letter." He squeezed his eyes shut and looked at it straight on, finally, breaking the rule, letting himself see it. "It says: *Deliver to the one who rhymed. She'll come back. Make sure she counts.*" He opened his eyes. "And underneath, smaller, in different handwriting — older handwriting, Edie-handwriting — it says: *Tell her we're in the walls.*"

The house exhaled. Downstairs, somewhere, a clock that had no business working ticked past eleven.

Astrid stood up. And August — hollowed out and refilled and more real than he had ever been — watched her wipe her face with the heel of her hand and pick up Mrs. Hollowell's ledger like it weighed something, like it *owed* something, and he thought: *there she is. There's the girl who rhymed. There's the one they mailed me to.*

"Then we tell them," she said. "All of them. By name."

August nodded. But deep in the picture behind his eyes, in the corner he hadn't looked at yet — the corner he was saving, or maybe hiding — there was one more line of writing, and it was in his own handwriting, and it said: *one delivery per envelope.*

He decided not to mention that part yet.

Not yet.
