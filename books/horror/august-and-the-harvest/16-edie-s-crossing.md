## 16. Edie's Crossing

The ledger weighed less than a Bible and cost more than a Bible, if you counted in the right currency. Astrid carried it up from the cellar with both hands, the way you carry a sleeping animal that might wake angry.

*Edith May Hollowell, b. 1946. Taken 1954.*

The name sat on the page in faded iron-gall ink, in her father's careful farmer's hand, right beside the yield for that year — *corn: best in county* — as if a girl were a bushel and a peck and something you could weigh.

Astrid climbed the stairs to the attic nursery with the ledger open against her forearm like a waitress balancing a tray she was afraid of.

August trailed behind her, three steps down, which was as close as he could bring himself. He'd been quiet since the supper he shouldn't have eaten. Quiet the way a room is quiet after someone stops talking mid-sentence.

"You don't have to come," Astrid said, without turning around.

"I kept every appointment for thirty years," August said. "I'm not missing this one."

It wasn't quite humor. It was wearing humor's coat, though, and Astrid decided to let it pass, because if she stopped and looked at his face too long — at the way his eyes had gone slightly wrong since dinner, like a photograph left in the sun — she would lose her nerve, and nerve was currently the only tool in her kit that still worked.

The attic stairs creaked in a pattern. She'd noticed it hours ago: seven creaks going up, six coming down. The house always kept one step for itself.

* * *

The nursery was exactly as she'd left it an hour before, which is to say it had rearranged itself into something worse. Seven beds stood in a row beneath a sloped ceiling papered with fading wallpaper of rabbits pulling turnips from the ground. The beds were never made — Edie had told her that through the mirror, in a voice like wind through a keyhole — because made beds meant someone expected you to get back in them, and getting back in them was the whole trick.

Six children slept. Six small chests rose and fell in a rhythm too slow, too synchronized, like instruments all tuned to one distant note. Their faces were smooth. No dreams moved behind their eyelids; only the house's dream, whatever that was. Corn, maybe. Rain. A bargain being renewed forever in a language without words.

Edie's bed stood empty at the end of the row. The sheets were warm. Astrid had checked twice, pressing her palm flat against them, half expecting cold and getting instead a warmth so specific it made her chest ache — the warmth of a body that had just stepped out of bed to go stand somewhere and wait.

"Which one do I—" Astrid began.

"The ledger," August said. He had stopped at the top of the stairs, gripping the rail with both hands, knuckles pale. "Read the whole line. Not just her name. My ma used to say names are just handles. You have to lift by what's written under."

Astrid looked down at the page. Beneath *Edith May Hollowell*, in smaller script, cramped into the margin like an afterthought:

*Taken for the drought year. Third of five. Liked to whistle through grass. Afraid of cellars. Her father could not look at her after.*

"Oh," Astrid said softly. Because there it was. Not a handle. A whole girl. A girl who whistled through blades of grass and feared the dark under the floorboards and whose last living image of her father was the back of his head, turned away.

"She can hear you," August said. "She told you that. Through the glass. She said the house lets us hear anything true."

Astrid cleared her throat. It felt absurd, standing in a dead nursery at two in the morning, reading a farm ledger aloud like scripture. Then she remembered the walls breathing. She remembered the knock matching her heartbeat. Absurdity had left the building some hours ago and taken the furniture with it.

She read.

"Edith May Hollowell. Born June the ninth, 1946, to Walter and Lorraine Hollowell, third of five." Her voice wobbled on *five*. Four other children, and the ledger had chosen the third. Or Walter had. She didn't let herself think about which. "Taken in the drought year, 1954, so that the corn would come. She liked to whistle through grass. She was afraid of cellars." Astrid's throat closed. She forced it open. "Her father never forgave himself, but forgiveness wasn't hers to give, and he never asked for it anyway. He just turned away. That was his sin, and it was a big one, and it wasn't hers."

The nearest sleeping child — a boy in overalls decades out of fashion — sighed in his sleep. The sound went through the room like a stone through a pond.

"Edie," Astrid said. And then, because August had said the house traded in truth and she was done trading small: "Edie, I know your name. All of it. Every part they wrote down and the parts they were too cowardly to write. You were a person. You whistled. You were scared of cellars and you got put in one anyway, and that was never a fair trade, and I am here to tell you the trade is void."

The warm sheets at the end of the row rustled.

Nothing else happened for a moment, and in that moment Astrid felt the particular terror of having shouted into a well and heard nothing. Then the sheets folded back on their own, neatly, the way a polite guest makes a bed, and the air above the mattress thickened, and shimmered, and came apart like fog burned off by a sun that wasn't there.

Edie stood up out of nothing. Out of seventy years of nothing.

* * *

She screamed.

Astrid had braced for many things. A whisper. A thank-you. Some ghostly gratitude, soft and cinematic. What she got was a sound like every held breath between 1954 and now leaving one small body at once — a scream with seventy years behind it, high and cracked and human, human past any argument, the scream of a nine-year-old who had been promised she could go home after the harvest.

Astrid dropped the ledger and caught her. That part was instinct. Edie was solid — solid and cold and shaking, small enough that Astrid could feel every rib, dressed in a cotton dress printed with strawberries that had been bright once and were gray as ash now.

"It's me," Astrid said, uselessly, into her hair. "It's — my name's Astrid. I'm the one from the mirror. You're awake. You're awake, you're awake—"

"He said *after the harvest*," Edie gasped. Her voice was rusty, a door unused. "He said I could go home *after the harvest*. Papa said. He shook on it. He shook on it and then he wouldn't look at me—"

"I know." Astrid did know; the ledger had told her, and Edie had told her, and the house had told her in its own way, which was to keep the girl's bed warm for seventy years as if warmth were an apology. "I know, honey. Your papa was a coward and a liar and the thing he bargained with was worse than either. But none of that is yours. Do you hear me? None of it stuck to you."

Edie pulled back and looked at her. Her eyes were brown and enormous and wet, and behind the terror in them was something older and sharper, something that had spent seven decades doing arithmetic in the dark.

"You woke the others' names too?" Edie said. "In the book?"

"No. Just yours. I only had the nerve for one."

"Good," Edie said, and the sharpness in her eyes came fully forward, and Astrid understood suddenly that this child had survived the house by becoming, in some small private way, harder than the house. "Because waking one means the house has to fill the bed. That's the grammar. One out, one in. Edie told you—" Edie stopped, frowned at herself, corrected. "I told you. Through the glass. It keeps count. It hates being uneven."

Astrid felt the floor change under her feet.

Not metaphorically. The floorboards tilted — a degree, no more, the way a ship settles — and somewhere below them, deep in the bones of the house, something shifted its weight like a large animal turning over in a stall.

"August," Astrid said.

But August was already looking at the doorway.

* * *

The seventh bed was making itself.

Sheets pulled taut. Pillow plumped by invisible hands, or by hands that preferred not to be seen. The rabbits on the wallpaper seemed to lean toward it, turnip baskets swinging.

"No," Astrid said. "No, absolutely not. Nobody is getting in that bed. We are leaving. All three of us, out the door, down the stairs, and if the stairs disagree we will argue with them."

"Astrid." August's voice was very gentle. Gentle the way a doctor is gentle. "You knew the rule. Edie told you. Waking one costs one."

"It costs *nothing*," Astrid snapped, and hated how loud it came out, how much it sounded like fear wearing anger's clothes. "It costs nothing because I say it does. I am the appraiser of record on this property and I am telling you the collateral is insufficient—"

She was babbling. She knew she was babbling. Edie took her hand, small and cold, and squeezed it, and somehow that shut her up better than any argument.

"It won't take one of the sleepers," Edie said quietly. "Not tonight. They're already its property. Taking one of them would be moving money from one pocket to another. It wants new money."

The house exhaled through the nursery. The temperature dropped four degrees in the space of a breath. Downstairs, a door closed — politely, firmly, the way a bank teller slides the drawer shut when your transaction is concluded.

And Astrid saw it, then, with the horrible clarity that had been arriving all night in installments: the ledger in the cellar with her own surname as the final entry. *Collateral.* The invitation she'd spoken at nine years old, never rescinded, accruing interest across three decades. She had walked into this house thinking she was the professional. She had been the payment plan all along.

One child freed. One child owed.

"It's not taking me," Edie said. There was steel under the rust in her voice now. "I've paid seventy years. I'm paid in full. If anyone comes to collect on me, I'll bite them."

Despite everything — despite the tilting floor and the self-making bed and the door downstairs closing like a verdict — Astrid laughed. It came out ragged, but it was real. "You're terrifying, Edie Hollowell."

"I had a lot of time to practice," Edie said.

Then they both turned to look at August, because the house had turned to look at him first.

He stood at the top of the stairs with the rail gripped in both hands, and the light from the hallway behind him made him translucent at the edges, and Astrid realized with a lurch that he had been translucent for hours and she had been refusing to see it. Since supper. Since the food that smelled like every childhood summer. Since obedience began its slow work of erasure.

"August," she said. "No. Whatever it's suggesting — no."

"I'm the lawful substitute," he said. His voice was steady, and that was the worst part, the steadiness, the way he said it like a boy reciting a rule of a game he'd mastered. "That's the grammar. Edie taught you the grammar. I was taken in 1971. I belong to the count. I've always belonged to the count. I just — got lent out. To you." He smiled, and it was the gap-toothed grin from the porch, except one tooth had gone missing since dinner, and Astrid's heart went with it. "You were supposed to come back and open the door. You did. Best appointment I ever kept."

"August, listen to me—" Astrid started toward him, and the hallway behind him lengthened. She watched it happen. Three extra feet of corridor slid into existence like a drawer opening, putting him just out of reach, patient and smooth, a house buying time.

"One condition," August said — to her, but louder, the way you say things when you want the landlord to hear. "If it takes me, it leaves her alone. Both of them. The sleepers stay sleeping until they're woken proper, by name, like she was. That's the deal I'm offering. Somebody write it down." His eyes found Astrid's. "Somebody keep a ledger."

"Don't you dare," Astrid said. "Don't you *dare* make a bargain in this house, August, that's how all of this started, that's the whole—"

"I know how it started," August said. "I *was* how it started. For somebody. Maybe that's how it ends, too. Things going back where they came from, but backwards this time." He held out one hand to the empty seventh bed, casual as a boy offering a seat on a porch swing. "Well?" he said, to the house, to the dark, to the vast patient thing behind the wallpaper. "Do we have terms?"

The house went very still.

Stillness, Astrid was learning, was its yes. Stillness was how it signed.

Downstairs, the front door unlocked itself with a click like a pen touching paper. Somewhere in the walls, the rhythmic knocking — the one that matched her heartbeat — stopped. Matched something else now. Something slower. A boy's pulse, keeping time.

Edie's grip tightened on Astrid's hand until it hurt.

"Take the ledger," Edie whispered urgently. "Get the others' names. Wake them fast, one after another, before the house balances its books again. And Astrid—" the girl looked up at her, seventy years old and nine years old at once, "—when it comes for him, don't let it take how you remember him. That's the interest it charges. It doesn't want the body. Bodies it has plenty. It wants to be *forgotten correctly*."

The seventh bed's pillow dented, gently, as if someone had just sat down.

August looked at Astrid one last time from the end of the lengthening hall, and mouthed the words she'd taught him herself, thirty years ago, on a porch in summer:

*Kept the appointment.*

Then he lay down, and the sheets drew up over him with terrible tenderness, and his eyes stayed open and fixed on hers as the house began, softly, to hum — a lullaby in no language, counting backward from one.
