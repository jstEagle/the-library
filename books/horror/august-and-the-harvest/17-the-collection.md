## 17. The Collection

The house stopped breathing for a moment, and I knew it was thinking about me.

I have lived inside its thinking for thirty years, so I know the difference between the house breathing and the house deciding. Breathing is the walls going in and out like a sleeping dog. Deciding is when everything goes still, and you can hear the wallpaper listening. It was listening now. Downstairs, Astrid was holding Edie's hand, and Edie was crying the way you cry when you haven't cried in seventy years — all at once, like a jar of pickles that finally got opened — and the house had gone quiet as a held breath.

It wanted a child. That was the rule. One out, one in. Edie's name had come off the ledger, so another name had to go on, and there were only two names left in the whole house that weren't written down anywhere.

Hers. Or mine.

I sat on the top stair with my knees pulled up to my chin, the way I used to sit when I was small and waiting for Mama to call me in from the fields, except Mama never called me in from the fields because Mama was already inside the walls by then, or some part of her was, and I didn't know that yet. I was eight years old in 1971. I am fifty-two years old now, if you count the years I spent being a pretend boy in a real girl's summer, which nobody does, but I do. I count everything. That's what the house taught me before it taught me anything else: counting is how you know what's missing.

And what was missing, right now, was time.

* * *

I found Astrid in the kitchen, because the kitchen was where the house let us be, now. The floors tilted toward it all night like a spoon tilting toward a mouth. The table was still set from supper — six plates, seven chairs, one chair for every child who ever slept upstairs and one extra for the thing behind the walls, which never ate but liked to watch. Astrid stood at the counter with the ledger open in front of her and her finger on the last line, and she was doing that thing she does when she's scared, which is get very organized about it. She'd made a list. Of course she'd made a list. Even at nine years old, she made lists. *Things August likes: frogs, strawberry pop, the smell of cut grass.* I found that list once, folded up in her treehouse, and I kept it for thirty years. It's the only thing I own.

"It's going to take one of us," she said without turning around. "Isn't it."

"It's going to try," I said.

"August." She turned around then, and her face did the thing grown-up faces do, where they try to be brave and mostly succeed and the part that doesn't succeed is the eyes. "There has to be another way. There's always another way. I appraise property for a living. There's always a loophole, an easement, a—"

"Astrid." I said her name the way you set down something heavy. "Sit down. Please. I want to tell you a story, and I want you to listen to the whole story before you start fixing things. You always want to fix things. That's my favorite thing about you and right now it's going to make this harder."

She sat. She put her hands flat on the table like she was bracing against weather. Edie came and stood in the doorway, small and gray in her 1950s dress, and I was glad, because this story belonged to her too.

* * *

Here is what I remember about being real.

I remember the smell of the barn in June, which is hay and oil and something sweet underneath that I never could name. I remember my brother Thomas teaching me to whistle through a blade of grass and me spitting on myself instead, and him laughing so hard he fell off the fence. I remember Mama singing while she shelled peas, and how the peas went *tink tink tink* into the bowl like tiny bells. I don't remember Mama's face. Isn't that funny? I remember the peas and not the face. The house took the face first. They always take the faces first, because a face is the biggest thing a memory has, and the house eats big things first, like a kid eating the frosting.

My papa farmed this land, and his papa before him, and every one of them signed the same paper in the same cellar, and every one of them thought they were smarter than the last one. Papa signed in '71 because the corn was dying and the bank was coming and there were four mouths to feed, and he signed it in the kitchen, at this table, maybe in this very spot where Astrid is sitting, and he cried while he did it, and I know that because I watched him from the stairs, and I thought: *Papa is crying, so it must be okay, because papas only cry about important things.*

The thing came that night. Not through the door. Through the *idea* of the door. And it looked around our kitchen the way you look around a bakery, and it said — and I will hear this voice until I stop existing, whatever stopping means — it said, *"One good year for every name. Interest accrues."*

Papa thought it meant money. Papas always think everything means money. It meant us.

Thomas went first. He just wasn't there one morning, and his bed was made, and nobody in our family made beds, so we knew the house had done it. Then it was my turn, and here is the part I need you to understand, Astrid, because it's the whole point of everything:

They didn't take me to punish Papa. They took me to *spend* me.

Because a taken child doesn't stay a child. A taken child gets shaped. The house poured me out like batter into a mold, and the mold was a boy named August, an imaginary friend, and it carried me — carried *itself*, wearing me — three miles east to a little yellow house where a girl had just lost her mama and was playing alone in the yard, talking to nobody, making up friends because the real ones couldn't fill the hole. And the house set me down in that hole like a cork in a bottle.

You didn't invent me, Astrid.

You *received* me.

"I was the message," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle like a boy's voice does, and I hadn't cried in thirty years and suddenly it was all trying to come out at once. "The house sent me. Because houses can't walk, but invitations can. It planted me in your summer like a seed, and it waited for you to grow up and become somebody who appraises old farmhouses, and then it waited for you to get the Hollowell file, and then it waited for you to drive out here. Thirty years of waiting. Do you know how patient a house can be? A house has nothing but time. A house IS nothing but time."

Astrid's hands had come up over her mouth. Above them, her eyes were shining and furious and broken, all three at once, which is a hard thing for eyes to do but hers managed it.

"The games," she whispered through her fingers. "The rhyme. *Step through the gate, the gate swings wide—*"

"You invited me in. That part was true. But the house wrote the invitation and mailed it to you postage-due, and you paid it without knowing, because you were nine, and nine-year-olds sign anything if the signature is a game." I swallowed. My throat felt full of corn husks. "Every child in this house was delivered somewhere. Edie was sent to a boy in Cedar Falls in 1958. The Kessler twins went to two sisters in Omaha. We're not ghosts, Astrid. We're *mail*. And mail only works if somebody eventually opens the door."

Edie spoke from the doorway, quiet as dust settling. "He's telling it true. I watched them pour him into you. I've watched every pouring since 1954. It's the only show this house puts on."

"So why," Astrid said, and her voice went hard, appraisal-hard, the voice she used on contractors who lied about water damage, "why are you telling me this NOW? What changed?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. That was the whole question sitting on the table between us like a seventh plate.

What changed was Edie. What changed was that her name came off the ledger and the ledger needs a name and the house has been circling Astrid all night like a fly circles a window. But that wasn't the answer I gave, because it wasn't the truest one.

"What changed is I remembered my mama's face," I said. "This morning. When Edie screamed, when seventy years came out of her, something shook loose in me too, and I remembered her face, Astrid. I remembered it clear as the kitchen window. And you know what? The house noticed. The house knows I'm leaking. A worn-out memory isn't worth keeping. A worn-out boy—" I smiled at her, and I felt the smile wobble, and I let it wobble, because she deserved the wobbling truth more than a steady lie. "A worn-out boy is just a boy again. And the house can't use a boy. It can only use a message. So the message is delivered, and the boy is leftover, and leftovers—" I spread my hands. "Well. You've seen the harvest suppers. Nothing in this house goes to waste."

"No," Astrid said. She stood up so fast her chair fell over. "No. Absolutely not. We un-invite it tonight, we do the rhyme backward, we—"

"And if it doesn't work?" I asked. "If the rhyme needs a name on the ledger to balance, the way it always has? Are you going to bet Edie's freedom on *maybe*?"

She didn't answer. Outside, the sky was going the color of a bruise, and the house exhaled around us, slow and satisfied, and I felt it start to reach.

* * *

So here is what I did, and I did it quick, before I could be a coward about it, because I have been a coward for thirty years in small ways and I refused to be one in the big way.

I walked to the center of the kitchen, where the floorboards made a circle that was warmer than the rest of the floor, and I said, out loud, in my plainest voice:

"I'm offering myself. Lawful substitute. One child out, one child in — that's the rule, and rules are the only holy thing this place has ever respected. Take me. Take the original. I was yours first anyway; everybody else is just a copy."

The house went still. Then the walls did something I'd never felt them do in thirty years.

They *purred.*

"Oh, August," Astrid said, and it came out half sob and half scold, and God, she sounded exactly like her mother must have sounded, and I treasured it even as it broke me. "Oh, you stubborn, stupid, wonderful—"

"Let me finish," I said. "I have terms. Houses respect terms. It's in the ledger, look it up." I pointed at her, finger shaking, and I made my voice steady because this was the important part, this was the part I'd been rehearsing on the stairs while the house listened. "One term. One. You remember me *right*. Not as imaginary. Not as a game you played when you were sad. As stolen. I was a real boy named — my name was—" and it caught in my throat, the old name, the house had kept it locked so long, but Edie was watching me and her eyes said *say it, say it, names are how we wake,* and so I said it. I said my true name out loud for the first time since 1971, and the walls flinched, and Astrid's lips moved, repeating it, learning it, filing it away in that list-making heart of hers.

"That name goes on no ledger," I told her. "That name goes home with you. You tell people about me. You tell them a boy was stolen from this farm in 1971 and hidden inside a lonely girl's summer, and you tell them his name, and you say it angry, because justice for the Hollowells was never coming from any court. Courts don't count doors. You're the only judge this house ever feared, Astrid Ashgrove, and your verdict is *remembering*. Swear it."

"I swear," she said, and her voice didn't wobble at all, and I believed her completely.

The floor under my feet began to warm. Not kitchen-warm. Throat-warm. The circle of boards glowed faintly, like a stove lid, and up through the cracks came the smell of every childhood summer at once — cut grass and strawberry pop and rain on hot dirt — the house's oldest trick, its sweetest bait, and I'll be honest with you, whoever finds these words: I wanted it. For one whole second I wanted to sink into that warmth and be a kept thing forever, safe and sweet and never lonely again.

Then I heard Astrid say my name — my *kept* name, August, the name she gave me even though she didn't give me, the name that was mine anyway, the only thing I ever chose — and the wanting broke like ice on a pond in March.

"August." She crossed the room and grabbed my hands, and her grip was strong, appraiser-strong, a woman who hauled ladders and measured rooms for a living. "Between three and four. The thirteenth door. If I go in there — if I find the thing behind the house — can I take you back? Is there a version of the rules where I get you back?"

I looked at her. Nine years old in a treehouse, thirty-nine years old in a haunted kitchen, and both of them the realest person I ever knew.

"There's a version of the rules where anything happens," I said. "But Astrid — listen — the thing behind the house wears shapes. Parent-shapes. Mama-shapes. Papa-shapes. It will offer you your mother's face, and it will sound so kind, and you have to remember that kindness is the wrapper, not the candy." I squeezed her hands back, hard, memorizing them. "And when you speak the rhyme backward — when you get there, and you will get there — leave room in it. Leave a gap. A door-sized gap."

Her brow creased. "A gap for what?"

The clock in the hall began to strike. Not midnight. Later than midnight. The hour when the house's arithmetic got strange, when eleven doors became twelve became ten, when the impossible door swung open on its hinges of dark.

"For whatever walks out of it," I said, and smiled my gap-toothed grin, and stepped onto the warm circle, and let the harvest take me.

The last thing I saw was her face, furious and hopeful and utterly unwilling to forget.

Good, I thought, as the warmth closed over me like water. *Remember it angry, Astrid. Remember it stolen. And leave the door open wide.*

Somewhere above me, muffled by sixty years of wall, a clock ticked past three.
