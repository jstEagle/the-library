## 6. The Family Out Ahead of the Count

The call came in at 2:47 in the morning, which was the hour when Kestrel Amberdale no longer trusted their own ears.

They'd learned to work the dead hours carefully. Coffee measured to the gram so the tremor in their hands wouldn't show on the fader. The board lights dimmed to amber so the studio felt less like a box and more like a hearth. *The Night Line* ran from midnight to four, and for eleven years Kestrel had filled those hours with the particular warmth that small-town radio does best: requests, confessions, the low murmur of a voice that sounds like it's sitting on the edge of your bed. Lately the warmth had gone thin. Lately the calls had been coming from people with dates attached to them, and Kestrel had gotten very good at not doing the arithmetic on air.

The screener board lit. Line two. The caller ID field, which Kestrel had long ago stopped looking at because it had started showing things like the chapel's defunct number and, once, their own childhood landline, read only a string of zeros.

They let it ring twice. They always let it ring twice now. It felt like knocking.

"Night Line, you're live with Kestrel."

"Good evening, dear." The voice was old and papery, the kind of voice that had spent decades being patient with children. A woman's voice, warm at the edges, worn in the middle. "Is this the Amberdale boy? The radio one?"

"I'm the Amberdale, yes." Kestrel kept the smile in their voice the way you keep a hand on a door in wind. "And you're calling from—"

"Hallowmere, of course. Birch Row. The blue house with the crooked gate." A small laugh, like paper turning. "Though I suppose the gate is straighter now. They fixed it after the funeral."

Kestrel's hand was already moving toward the dump button. It was a reflex now, a professional tic, and they hated that they could feel it in their shoulder before the words even finished. "Ma'am, we do get a lot of prank calls this time of—"

"Edith Marsh," the woman said, gently, the way you say a name to someone who's forgotten it. "You used to sit on my porch steps in the summer and I'd give you lemonade with too much sugar in it because your grandmother said you'd go to sleep otherwise and your grandmother liked a house that slept." A pause. "You had a gap in your teeth. You whistled when you said *s*. I told you it was a gift and you believed me, because children believe old women about everything except the important things."

The dump button sat under Kestrel's finger like a stone.

They didn't press it. That was the thing they would think about later, at four in the morning and at four in the morning for many mornings after: they didn't press it, because the lemonade was true. Nobody could have looked that up. Nobody had ever looked it up, because Kestrel had never told anyone, because it was nothing, because it was a porch and a summer and a gap-toothed whistle, and things that are nothing don't make it into the public record.

"Mrs. Marsh," Kestrel said, and heard their own voice go carefully level, the voice they used for grief calls, the voice that was a hand extended over dark water. "When did you pass?"

"Thirty years ago, come the first frost." No hesitation. No performance. The dead, Kestrel was learning, did not perform. It was only the living who needed to. "I've been meaning to call for a while, if you'll forgive me. The lines are crowded these days, as I expect you've noticed."

"I—" Kestrel's mouth was dry. Somewhere in the rack, the ON AIR light burned its steady red, and eleven years of professionalism sat on their chest like a hand. "I've noticed."

"You've been very kind to them. The others. Cutting them off, mostly, but kindly." A rustle, as if she were settling into a chair that wasn't there. "I understand. It's a hard thing, believing. Harder than dying, in my experience. Dying is very easy. You just get lighter."

Kestrel looked at the clock. 2:51. Thirteen minutes to the top of the hour, to the weather, to the station ident, to the ordinary machinery of a world that still worked. They could ride to that. They could ride anything to that.

"Mrs. Marsh, I have to ask. The others who've called—the ones I've—" *cut off*, they didn't say. "Why now? Why all at once?"

"Because the moon is coming, dear."

"The moon comes every month."

"Not that one." And here the papery voice changed. Not colder—warmer, if anything, the way a stove is warm when you're standing too close to it. "The harvest moon. The big one, low and orange, that comes up over the ridge like it's looking in a window. You'll know it when you see it because you won't be able to look away. Nobody in this valley ever could." A breath. "It's coming early this year. The twenty-ninth. Mark it on your calendar, though I expect you won't need to. Your body will mark it for you."

"Coming for what?" Kestrel heard themself ask, and hated the smallness of their own voice, the child's voice underneath the broadcaster's.

"For what it's always come for. The count." Edith Marsh said it the way you'd say *the mail* or *the weather*. "The door under your chapel, dear. The one the men dug up. It opens when the moon is full on the harvest, and what's behind it comes out to collect, and it has been so patient, and it is so very hungry, and it has waited a long, long time because the Amberdales kept it fed and kept the count honest. That was the family's work. That was always the family's work."

The studio was very quiet. Kestrel became aware of the sound of their own blood, a slow tide in their ears.

"Mrs. Marsh. I think you have the wrong family. I'm a radio host. I don't—there's no chapel work. There's no count. My grandmother ran a gardening segment."

"Your grandmother ran this show," Edith said, not unkindly, "and before that she ran the ledger, and before that her mother ran it, all the way back to the first Amberdale who came up this valley with an iron door and a guilty conscience. You are the last of them, dear. Do you understand what I'm telling you? There's only you left now, and one other, and the count is nearly full."

"There's another?"

"Wren. Your cousin. She'll come, I expect. She always knew, that girl. Knew and hated it, which is the honest way to know a thing." A pause, and when Edith spoke again her voice had the careful gentleness of someone delivering a diagnosis. "But that's not the important part. The important part is this, and I need you to listen, because I can only say it plainly once and then the line will start to pull me back. The door opens on the harvest moon. And every Amberdale must be out of Hallowmere before it does. Out of the valley entirely. Over the ridge, past the county line, and don't stop for anything that calls your name on the way, because it will know your name better than you do."

"Why?" Kestrel's finger had finally found the dump button and pressed it, and nothing happened, and the red light stayed red, and the line stayed open, and Edith Marsh went on as if the silence of the machinery were just another kind of listening. "Why does it matter if I'm here?"

"Because you're not food, dear. You're the *key*." The warmth went out of the voice then, all at once, like a window closing. "The family isn't the lock's keeper. The family is the lock. It was never fed *by* the Amberdales. It was fed *around* them, and it is full, and when it is full it will want what it has always wanted, which is the last of the line, walking down the cellar stairs on their own two feet, with the moon on their back, to open what was sealed from the inside. It cannot take you. It can only *invite* you. So it will spend the next weeks making Hallowmere into an invitation. And Kestrel—" the papery voice frayed now, thinning, going the way of bad reception, of distance, of thirty years, "—it is very good at invitations. Ask your grandmother. Ask her why the tape ends where it ends. Ask her why her chair is still warm—"

The line clicked to dial tone. Clean, ordinary, municipal dial tone, the most mundane sound in the world, and Kestrel sat in it for a long moment before they realized their hand was shaking too hard to fade the music in.

They faded the music in. They read the weather—rain by Thursday, clearing toward the weekend, a lie so comfortable it should have been criminal—and at 4 a.m. they signed off with the same eleven words they'd used eleven hundred times, *this has been the Night Line, sleep well, Hallowmere*, and they sat in the dark studio and laughed.

They laughed for a while. It was a real laugh, at first, ragged and disbelieving, the laugh of a person who has just been told the plot of a bad movie by a woman who has been dead since the first frost of 1994. Edith Marsh. They could look it up. They would look it up. There would be a headstone on Birch Row behind the blue house with the straightened gate, and the dates would say 1919–1994, and Kestrel would stand there in the morning light and feel the last of the dread drain out of them, and they would go home and sleep for twelve hours and never again take a call from a line of zeros.

That was the plan. The laugh carried them all the way to the parish register.

* * *

St. Brigid's had been deconsecrated in the eighties and sold twice and was now, absurdly, a wedding venue, but the records had stayed in the vestry because nobody had ever wanted them enough to move them. Kestrel knew the caretaker, a sleepless widower named Halloran who listened to the show every night and had once dedicated a request to his wife's memory in a voice that had cracked Kestrel's heart straight down the middle. Halloran let them in at 6:40 in the morning without asking why, because Halloran had the specific courtesy of the grieving, which is to say he never asked anyone anything.

The registers were in the back cabinet. Baptisms, marriages, deaths. Leather spines gone soft as cloth, the oldest ones written in a hand like iron fence-work.

Kestrel told themself they were looking up Edith Marsh. That was the story they were telling, and they almost believed it, the way you almost believe you're only checking your phone.

Edith Marsh was there, of course. Birch Row. 1919–1994. Cause of death recorded in the old shorthand: *heart, in her sleep, a blessing*. Kestrel stood over the page and felt the dread begin, very slowly, to drain out of them, exactly on schedule, exactly as planned, and they were actually smiling—actually smiling, in the grey morning light of a deconsecrated vestry that smelled of dust and lily petals from Saturday's weddings—when their eye caught the facing page.

The death register for the current year was not full. Hallowmere was a dying town in the demographic sense, but its paperwork was sparse; there were perhaps nine entries, most of them elderly, one of them Dov Hale's crew man who'd walked into the hill, entered in Halloran's careful hand as *lost, presumed*.

Kestrel's name was the last entry.

Not Kestrel Amberdale the broadcaster. Not a mistake of a similar name. *Kestrel Wren Amberdale*—the full legal name, the name on their birth certificate, the middle name their grandmother had insisted on and their mother had resented—written in the register's careful columns in ink that was not dry.

Date of death: the twenty-ninth.

The harvest moon. The date Edith Marsh had given them, forty minutes ago, on a line of zeros.

Kestrel stood very still. Outside, a wedding party was arriving early for photographs, and their laughter came through the old windows bright and terrible, and somewhere down the hill the excavation crew would be arriving at the chapel with their machines and their bravado, and the town was waking up, all of it, the whole ordinary machine of Hallowmere grinding into its morning, and none of it—none of it—knew that the last of the Amberdales was already dead on paper.

They touched the ink. It smudged. It was real. It was wet, almost, as if the register had been written in minutes ago, as if the hand that wrote it was still in the room.

There was no hand in the room. There was only Kestrel, and the smell of lilies, and the entry beneath their own, which had not been there a moment before, which they were certain had not been there, which read, in the same wet careful hand:

*Wren Amberdale — date of death: pending.*

Kestrel laughed again. It came out wrong this time. It came out like something breaking.

They backed away from the register, and their heel caught the cabinet, and in the silence that followed they heard—faintly, from below, from under the floor, from under the hill, from wherever a sealed iron door kept its patience—a sound that might have been the building settling, and might have been a hymn, and might have been a voice counting.

The twenty-ninth. Nine days.

Kestrel Amberdale walked out of St. Brigid's into the flat white morning, got in their car, and sat with their hands on the wheel trying to remember how to drive, while somewhere out past the ridge, on a road they didn't know yet, a car with a lapsed deaconess in it was already pointed at Hallowmere, coming to drag the last of the family out of the valley—whether the valley, or the family, was willing or not.
