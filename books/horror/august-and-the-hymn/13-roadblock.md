## 13. Roadblock

The duffel bag was packed before midnight. Wren had done the packing — she packed like a woman who had rehearsed this her whole life, which, Kestrel was beginning to understand, she had. Two changes of clothes each, the hymnal wrapped in a dish towel, a flashlight, a box of matches, and nothing else. "You don't take things from a house that's counting you," Wren said, when Kestrel reached for the framed photograph of their grandmother in the hallway. "You don't give it more of you than it already has."

Kestrel's hand hovered over the glass. In the photograph, Gran stood behind the Night Line's old broadcast desk, one hand on the fader, smiling like a woman mid-sentence.

"Leave it," Wren said again, gentler now.

Kestrel left it.

They took Kestrel's car — the old Volvo with the rust blooming along the wheel wells — because Wren's truck had been sitting outside the Hallowmere Motor Lodge for two days and its tires were flat in a way that wasn't flatness. Kestrel had looked at them that morning and looked away quickly, the way you look away from a dog that's been hit. The tires weren't punctured. They were *deflated*, deliberately, like lungs exhaling.

"Route 9," Wren said, folding the county map on her knees as Kestrel pulled out of Amberdale Lane. "North through the valley, then west at the reservoir. Forty miles to Corben Falls. There's a bus from there to anywhere."

"And if Route 9 doesn't work?"

"Then we try Route 9 again until it does." She said it without looking up. "Roads are roads, Kess. They go where they go. Whatever's happening here is happening *here*. The second we're out of the valley—"

The words died in her mouth because they crossed the town line marker — the chipped granite post with HALLOWMERE cut into it, moss filling the letters like something growing in a wound — and the radio came on by itself.

Kestrel hadn't touched it. The ignition hadn't even woken the stereo; the display stayed dark. But the speakers produced sound anyway: a low, sustained tone, almost a note, almost a voice humming behind a closed door. It lasted four seconds. Then silence.

Neither of them spoke for a mile.

* * *

Route 9 climbed out of the valley through stands of birch gone gold early, too gold, the color of candle flame rather than leaf. The road was empty. That was the first wrong thing. At nine on a weeknight there should have been logging trucks, teenagers racing the curves, at least one lost tourist braking hard for deer. Instead the asphalt ran ahead of them pale and vacant, the centerline dashes flickering past in the headlights like a pulse being counted.

Wren watched the odometer. "Corben Falls is thirty-eight miles out," she said. "I want to see the mileage match the map."

For eleven miles it did. Kestrel let herself believe, in the small superstitious way she'd permitted herself since the parish register — since seeing her own name entered in a ledger under *deceased*, in her grandmother's handwriting, dated next spring — that maybe the town's reach ended where its gravel did. Maybe the count was a local ordinance. Maybe the dead only had jurisdiction where they'd been buried.

At mile twelve, they passed the old Hollis farm.

Kestrel knew it was the Hollis farm because she'd passed it ten thousand times in her life, because its collapsed silo leaned against the barn roof like a drunk against a doorframe, because the mailbox said HOLLIS in faded reflective letters. She knew it, and yet the road kept going past it, and then the road bent left where it had always bent right, and then the road went down into a stretch of valley she did not recognize, and then—

"There," Wren whispered.

Ahead, catching their high beams full in the face: the chipped granite post. HALLOWMERE. Moss in the letters.

They had driven twelve miles and arrived back at the town line from the opposite direction.

Kestrel pulled over. Her hands were steady on the wheel, which frightened her more than shaking would have. She put the car in park and sat with the engine idling and the headlights washing the granite post and the dark woods beyond it, and she felt the shape of the thing clearly for the first time — not a haunting, not a curse in the storybook sense, but an *arithmetic*. A ledger balancing itself. You could no more drive out of a count than you could walk out of your own name.

"Again," Wren said.

"Wren—"

"*Again.* We have four days until the moon. I am not spending them inside the parish lines arguing about it."

So they turned around, and they drove Route 9 north again, and this time Wren navigated by watch as well as odometer, calling out landmarks — the Hollis farm at minute nine, the reservoir turnoff at minute twenty-two — and at minute twenty-two there was no reservoir turnoff, there was only more road, climbing, descending, and at minute thirty-one the granite post swam up out of the dark again, patient as a tide.

The third time, they tried the reservoir road itself. The fourth time, the fire road over Tanner Ridge. The fifth time, Kestrel drove south instead of north, toward Millbrook and the interstate, reasoning aloud — reasoning like a woman building a case she could hold, the old habit, the last habit — that whatever loop gripped the northern roads might not have bothered with the southern ones.

The granite post met them at the bottom of Miller's Hill, standing in the headlights like a man waiting to be recognized.

On the sixth pass, Kestrel noticed the fuel gauge.

It had been three-quarters full when they left Amberdale Lane. Six loops of thirty-odd mountain miles should have emptied it twice over. The needle sat at three-quarters. It had sat at three-quarters all night. She stared at it while the engine idled, and Wren followed her eyes, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that the gauge wasn't already saying: *the car isn't burning anything. The car isn't going anywhere. Nothing here is being spent except you.*

"It's feeding us back to itself," Kestrel said. Her voice sounded strange in the sealed dark of the car, flattened, like a call coming through the board. "Gas, miles, roads. It's not keeping us in. It's keeping us *fresh*."

Wren opened the hymnal on her knees, found the page she wanted — the tally page, the arithmetic page — and held it so the dashboard glow lit the columns of marks. "Then we stop trying to outrun it," she said. "We think. There has to be a way the family got out before. Gran's generation, great-aunt Odile's. Somebody left. The count expects Ambers *gone* — the caller said so. Gone means possible."

"Or gone means dead," Kestrel said. "Gone means collected. We saw the register, Wren. My name's already in it. Maybe the only exit it honors is the one it wrote."

Wren didn't answer. Outside, somewhere up the black slope of the hillside, faint as memory, someone began to hum.

It was the tune. Of course it was the tune — the hymn Dov Hale's whole crew had dreamed, the melody that lived on the hymnal's final page, the harvest song whose verses were instructions. But out here, unaccompanied, unhurried, drifting down through the birches, it didn't sound like instructions. It sounded like a lullaby sung to something enormous that was nearly asleep, and nearly was doing a lot of work in that sentence.

Kestrel killed the headlights. The humming stopped.

"Drive," Wren said quietly. "Not the roads. Just drive."

* * *

They drove the town itself after that, slow circuits of streets Kestrel had known since childhood, because if the valley wouldn't release them, at least the motion was something, at least the wheels turning was a kind of refusal. And it was on the third circuit, passing the shuttered gas station on Foundry Street, that they came upon the patrolman.

He stood at the intersection of Foundry and Church, beside his cruiser, which was parked at an angle with its light bar glowing softly — not flashing, just glowing, a blue wash like moonlight through water. He wore the uniform. He wore the smile. Kestrel knew him. Everyone in Hallowmere knew Deputy Arlen Coyle, or had known him, until the ice went out of the reservoir in April and took him with it, and they dredged for six days and found his hat.

His hat was on his head now.

"Oh God," Wren breathed. "Oh, Kess, don't stop—"

But Kestrel was already stopping, because Arlen Coyle had stepped into the road and raised one gloved hand in the universal gesture — *come ahead* — and some deep animal part of her brain insisted that you did not drive through a police officer, even a drowned one, even one smiling at you with water still moving gently behind his eyes.

She rolled down the window. The night air came in cold and green-smelling, pondweed and iron.

"Evening, Miss Amberdale." His voice was the voice from the phones — that particular flatness underneath the warmth, like a recording played slightly too slow. "Nice night for a drive."

"Arlen." Her mouth was dry. "You're dead."

"Yes'm." He said it the way he'd have said *yes'm* to *you're working late*. No grievance in it. No horror. Just fact, worn smooth. "Since April."

"What do you want?"

"Well, that's the thing." He leaned down toward the window, and up close she could see that his skin had the soft, swollen look of things long soaked, and that his smile never moved, never varied, was painted on by the same patience that had smoothed everything else about him. "Been getting calls. From below. Asking after you Amberdales specifically. Whether you're comfortable. Whether you're staying." He straightened up, gestured broadly at the town around them — the sleeping houses, the dark chapel on its hill, the sky where the moon was fattening toward full behind a scud of cloud. "And I get to tell 'em: yes. Yes, they're staying. Everybody's staying. Nobody leaves Hallowmere before the harvest. That's the rule, and the rule's older than me, and I was old when I drowned."

"We're not staying," Wren said from the passenger seat, low and hard. "We're leaving. Tonight. However many times it takes."

Arlen Coyle turned his head toward her with the smooth deliberation of a weathervane finding wind, and smiled wider, and the smile was the worst thing yet because it was *kind*. "Ma'am," he said, gently, the way you'd speak to a child insisting the stove wasn't hot, "the count doesn't need you to stay. The count just needs you here when it closes. Where would you even go?" He spread his hands at the road, the hills, the whole stitched-shut night. "Every road in this valley is a throat, and every throat leads down. You've seen." He tipped his hat brim — the hat they'd dredged six days for — and stepped back from the window. "Go on home now. Get some sleep. Moon's only four days off, and you'll want your strength for the singing."

"I'm not singing anything," Kestrel said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly, walking backward toward his cruiser, blue light pooling around him like spilled water. "But it'll sing you. It always does. Ask your gran."

The cruiser's door shut. The blue light went out all at once, like a held breath released, and the intersection was empty, and there was no tire track in the frost on the asphalt, none at all, as though the cruiser had been set down from above and lifted away the same way.

Kestrel drove. Not home — not yet — but around, faster now, both hands strangling the wheel, and Wren sat rigid beside her with the hymnal clutched to her chest like a shield that had just proven very thin.

"He knew about Gran," Kestrel said finally. "Ask your gran. He said *ask your gran*."

"Your gran's been dead nineteen years."

"Yeah." Kestrel's eyes were on the road, but she wasn't seeing it. She was seeing the photograph on the hallway wall, the one Wren wouldn't let her pack — Gran behind the broadcast desk, hand on the fader, smiling mid-sentence. And beneath that image, surfacing now like a body coming up through dark water, was another one, older, half-buried: the attic of the chapel house, the summer they cleared it out, the shapes under dust sheets, boxes tied with twine, and among them — she was almost sure, she was suddenly desperately sure — a machine. A reel-to-reel. Heavy, brown-faced, its tape reels furred with dust, labeled in Gran's handwriting in a way she hadn't thought about in years and couldn't now stop thinking about.

She'd assumed it was old shows. Archives. Dead air on tape.

"Turn around," she said. "The chapel. The attic. There's something up there I have to hear."

Wren looked at her. "Hear what?"

"I don't know yet." Kestrel swung the Volvo onto Chapel Hill Road, and above them, through the breaking clouds, the moon rose fat and yellow and one night closer, and the needle on the fuel gauge, she noticed, had not moved. "But Arlen Coyle just told us Gran left instructions. And I think she left them on tape."
