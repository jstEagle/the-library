## 14. Grandmother's Tape

The attic stairs were the kind that punished you for hesitating.

Kestrel climbed them anyway, one hand on the rail that had gone soft with rot, the other holding a flashlight whose beam kept stuttering like a nervous witness. Behind them, Wren's footsteps followed — slower, heavier, as if she were climbing into a confession booth rather than a crawlspace above a dead chapel.

"You said the sexton's records were up here," Wren said.

"I said they *might* be up here." Kestrel ducked under a beam furred with gray dust. "I also said I might be wrong about everything lately, so adjust your expectations accordingly."

The attic was a country unto itself. Moonlight came through a single round window at the gable end, filtered through sixty years of grime until it fell on the floorboards like weak tea. Boxes in ranks. Furniture under sheets that had become shrouds. A child's rocking horse with one eye worn blank. The air tasted of paper and pigeon and something older underneath — something mineral, like the inside of a bell.

Kestrel moved down the rows, opening lids. Hymnals by the dozen. Funeral registers going back past the founding of the town, their entries fading from iron gall ink to pencil to nothing. And then, in the corner farthest from the window, beneath a sheet that slid off like it had been waiting, a steamer trunk with brass fittings gone green.

The initials on the lid stopped Kestrel's hand midair.

**E.A.**

Edith Amberdale. Their grandmother.

"She hosted the Night Line," Wren said quietly. She'd come up beside them without making a sound, which was a thing Wren did that Kestrel had forgotten and now remembered all at once, unpleasantly. "Before it was the Night Line. It was called *The Hour Between* then. I told you that."

"You told me a lot of things at three in the morning over cold coffee. Forgive me if my retention was imperfect."

Wren didn't rise to it. That was the worst part of her these days — she'd stopped rising to anything, the way you stop flinching from a sound you've heard your whole life. She knelt at the trunk and lifted the lid, and the smell that came out was camphor and dust and, faintly, impossibly, cigarette smoke. Edith had quit smoking before Kestrel was born.

Inside: folded vestments gone the color of old bone. A photograph of a woman with Kestrel's jawline standing at a broadcast microphone, laughing at something off-camera. And beneath the cloth, wrapped in a wool blanket that crumbled at the touch —

A reel-to-reel recorder. A portable Sony, the kind with the handle and the hinged lid, its casing scuffed silver. One reel still mounted on the spindle, half played-through, the tape wound loose and slack.

Kestrel stared at it for a long time.

"Sixty years in an attic," Wren said. "It won't play."

"Everything else in this family refuses to stay buried. Why should the electronics be different?"

They carried it down the stairs together, because neither of them wanted to be the one holding it alone.

* * *

The chapel's back room still had power — Vetch's excavation crew had run an extension line for their floodlights, and someone had left the junction box live. Kestrel found an outlet behind a stack of folding chairs, wiped the plug prongs with a sleeve, and set the machine on a table scarred by generations of coffee rings and coffin-corner dents.

The belt was cracked. The capstan was gummed. But when Kestrel turned the take-up spindle by hand, it turned, and when they thumbed the switch, the motor caught with a sound like a throat clearing after decades of silence, and the reel began to turn.

Hiss first. Then a hum. Then a voice.

*"—and if you're just joining us, it's ten past eleven on the night of September the twenty-eighth, 1961, and this is Edith Amberdale, keeping the light on for you here on The Hour Between, broadcasting from the little chapel on the hill where, I'm told, the acoustics are better than they have any right to be."*

Kestrel sat down slowly. The chair complained. They barely heard it.

The voice was their grandmother's — but not the grandmother Kestrel had known, the small fierce woman who'd smelled of lavender and menthol and died when Kestrel was nine. This was her younger, her voice still carrying the river-silt of some accent long since sanded away, warm and wry and utterly in command of the dark the way only a person alone in a building at midnight can be.

*"We've got lines open, friends. You know the number. And I know what you're going to say — Edith, it's late, Edith, nobody's listening, Edith, why do you do this show out of a chapel instead of a proper studio. Well. The chapel has good bones. And sometimes, on nights like tonight, I think the building itself likes to listen."*

A pause on the tape. Long enough that Kestrel glanced at the reels to make sure they were still turning.

*"First caller. Go ahead, you're on the air."*

Static. Then a man's voice, thin and wet, as though he were speaking through water or through a wall thick with earth:

*"Edith? Edith, is that you? They said — they said if I called this number—"*

*"This is Edith. Who am I speaking with?"*

*"It's Tomas. Tomas Reyes. From the mill road."*

On the table between the speakers, Kestrel's hands went still. They knew the name. Everyone in Hallowmere knew the name; there was a bench by the reservoir with a small brass plate, TOMAS REYES, 1931–1961, DROWNED, and children had dared each other for generations to sit on it after dark.

*"Tomas,"* Edith said, and her voice did something remarkable — it didn't change pitch, didn't soften into pity or sharpen into fear. It simply made room. *"Tell me what happened."*

*"It was the water, Edie. It wasn't cold like I thought it would be. That's the thing I keep trying to tell people and nobody — it was warm. Like it was glad to have me."*

*"I believe you."*

*"And there's a door, Edie. Under the hill. I can see it from here. There's marks on it, counting, and every time somebody goes in the ground the count gets longer, and Edie, it's nearly full. It's nearly full and your name's on it. Your whole name. I can read it plain as print."*

Silence on the tape. Kestrel realized they had stopped breathing and started again.

*"Tomas,"* Edith said at last, *"how many marks?"*

*"More than last harvest. Fewer than next."*

*"Thank you for calling. Rest easy now."*

*"Will you sing it, Edie? Will you sing the verse?"*

And here the tape did something Kestrel would spend the rest of the night turning over — Edith laughed. Not nervously. Warmly. The way you laugh at an old friend's joke.

*"You know I can't, Tomas. Not yet. Ask me again when the moon's right."*

The line went to dial tone. Edith filled it with patter — *"Well, folks, that's the hour between for you"* — but her patter had a hairline crack in it now, and Kestrel heard it because they'd spent twenty years putting exactly that kind of crack in exactly that kind of patter, and you always hear your own work in someone else's mouth.

* * *

The tape ran on. Caller after caller. A woman describing her own funeral with mild irritation at the flowers. A child asking Edith whether dogs could find their way under the hill. A voice that was nothing but wind and, underneath the wind, a hymn sung in a register no human throat owned, which Edith talked over quickly and professionally, the way a weatherman talks over a storm warning.

And threaded through it all, growing clearer as the night deepened on the recording, a second sound. Low. Rhythmic. At first Kestrel took it for the building settling, the chapel's old bones ticking in the autumn cold.

Then they realized it was keeping time.

"Is that—" Wren leaned toward the speaker.

"The door," Kestrel said. "That's the sound the crew described. Knocking. Except it's not knocking."

It was counting. Slow, patient, each impact spaced like a heartbeat at rest, and every few minutes it paused — paused exactly as long as a caller spoke — and then resumed, one beat further along than before.

*"—and that's all the time we have tonight, friends,"* Edith said, and her voice had changed. The warmth was still there but stretched thin over something else, something that sounded almost like relief. *"The moon's coming up. I can see it through the window, and it's the color of a harvest apple, and I promised somebody a long time ago that when the count got full, I'd stop talking and start singing."*

Kestrel's skin tightened. *"Ask me again when the moon's right."*

*"To whoever's listening to this someday — and somebody will be, because Ambers never throw anything away, including their mistakes — I want you to know it isn't a punishment. Everybody always thinks it's a punishment. It isn't. It's a job. Somebody in this family has to hold the door, and hold it singing, or everything the door holds comes out unsung, and unsung things don't go back to sleep. My mother did it. Her mother did it. We keep the count honest. That's all any of us ever did."*

A pause. On the recording, faintly, the knocking stopped.

*"Oh,"* Edith said softly. *"There it is. Full."*

Chairs scraped on the old tape — the microphone being moved, set down, left running. Footsteps crossed a wooden floor. A door opened, and through it came a rush of cold air recorded sixty-two years ago, and beneath the air, rising, a hymn — the same one from the wind-voice, but sung now in Edith's warm low alto, steady, unafraid, getting fainter with every step.

Down the stairs. Toward the cellar. Toward the door with her name on it.

The tape hissed. The hymn faded to almost nothing. And then, in the last audible seconds, Edith's voice came back once, close to the microphone she'd left running, close enough that Kestrel felt she was speaking directly into the room with them, directly into their ear across six decades:

*"If you're hearing this, love, then I didn't finish the verse. Or I finished it and it wasn't enough. Either way — check the book. Check the last page and the page before it. Your great-grandmother tore something out and I never asked her why, and I have regretted it every night since. Find the missing verse. Sing it inside. Don't do what I did. Don't just hold the door."*

A breath.

*"End it."*

The tape ran on for eleven more seconds — Kestrel counted them, staring at the spinning reels — nothing but hiss and the distant, dwindling hymn, and then a sound like a great iron breath drawing shut, and then silence, and then the take-up reel clicked empty and the machine kept turning, playing the shape of a voice that no longer existed.

* * *

Neither of them spoke for a while. Outside the round window, the sky was doing the thing it had been doing all week — bruising toward the east, fattening the moon a little more each night, the harvest moon swelling like something gestating.

"The page before the last page," Wren said finally. Her voice was rough. She had the hymnal out of her bag already, the grandmother's hymnal, the one she'd carried three hundred miles. Her thumb moved along the gutter of the binding near the end, and Kestrel watched her face change as her fingers found what they found.

The stubs. Five of them, cut close to the spine with a razor or a knife, long ago. The ghost of a verse, excised.

"She said torn out," Kestrel whispered. "Not lost. Torn."

"Why would she tear it out?"

"Because of what it does." Kestrel stood, and their legs felt borrowed, unreliable, a puppet's legs. "Or because of what it costs. Edith sang the verses she had. She held the door. She said so herself — hold it singing. And it worked for sixty years, right up until a construction crew with a county contract cracked the seal." They looked at the dead machine, the slack tape, the small brass window where the last of their grandmother's voice had spun itself out. "She's still down there, Wren. Whatever's left of her. Holding. And the thing she's holding against has been taking calls on my show for two weeks, learning our voices, learning the town's grief, getting fat on the ratings."

Wren was quiet. Then, carefully, she smoothed the hymnal open to the final page and laid her palm flat beside the cut stubs, the way you'd lay a hand on a wound.

"There's a way to get it back," she said. "Paper remembers. Ink remembers. If part of that verse exists anywhere — another printing, another copy, another hand that copied it out before the blade came down —"

"Where?"

Wren looked up, and in the light of the fattening moon through the dirty glass, her face was the grandmother's face from the photograph, the same jaw, the same stubborn set.

"The deacony archive," she said. "In Marlow. Where I used to serve. Where I swore the oaths I broke." She closed the hymnal gently, like closing an eye. "We leave tonight. Before the moon finishes filling. Because Kestrel — I counted the stubs five times, and there are five verses gone, and the knocking on that door downstairs has been one beat short of full since Tuesday."

From below them, up through the floorboards, through the chapel's good bones, faint and patient as ever:

One knock.

And then, for the first time in sixty-two years of recorded and remembered nights —

Two.
