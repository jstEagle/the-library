## 18. On Air

The transmitter shed smelled of copper and rain. Wren held the bolt cutters like a rosary she'd finally learned to pray with, and when the padlock gave — one sharp crack that echoed off the water tower — neither of us breathed for a second, waiting for the town to notice.

The town didn't notice. The town was standing in its yards.

I'd seen them through the windshield on the drive over: Mrs. Calloway from the pharmacy, barefoot in her begonias; the Ostrander twins in their pajamas, hands at their sides, faces tilted up toward a moon still twenty minutes below the ridgeline. Not asleep. Not awake. Standing the way wheat stands before a combine comes through. I had wanted to stop the car and shake them, and Wren had put her hand flat on my arm and said, "You can't wake them by touching. You wake them by telling."

So we were going to tell.

The back door of the station was chained, but the window beside it hadn't been replaced since the Nixon administration, and Vetch's paranoia had never extended to glass. I went through first, elbow through the pane, blood threading down my wrist — I barely felt it. The lobby was dark except for the ON AIR sign in Studio B, burning red like a coal that wouldn't die. It had been burning since Vetch locked me out three days ago. Someone — something — had been keeping the light on.

"Kestrel." Wren's voice behind me, low. "The board's live."

She was right. I could hear it before I saw it: a hum out of the monitors, the faint hiss of an open line. The console sat in the dark like an animal waiting to be fed, every fader pushed up, every channel open, though no one sat in the chair. On the screen above the board, the automation log scrolled its innocent record:

*3:12 AM — CARRIER ACTIVE. NO OPERATOR.*

It had been broadcasting silence into Hallowmere all night. Or what sounded like silence. When I leaned close to the monitor speaker I heard it — under the hiss, under everything — a hymn, sung so low it might have been my own pulse. The same four notes Dov's crew had dreamed. The same four notes from the last page of Grandmother's hymnal, the ones that weren't instructions but appetite.

"It's been warming them up," Wren said. "All night. Getting the town's mouth ready for the harvest."

"Then let's give them something else to chew on."

I dropped into the operator's chair. The leather was warm. That was the detail that nearly broke me — not the dead callers, not the register with my name printed under DECEASED in faded ink, not the door with our name carved in iron. The chair was warm, as if someone had just gotten up from it, as if Vetch had only stepped out for coffee and would be back any minute to fire me again. I put my hands on the board anyway. My hands knew this desk better than they knew my own face. Eleven years of nights. Eleven years of talking Hallowmere to sleep.

Wren took the guest chair, the hymnal open on her knees, the torn edge of the missing verse showing pale as a scar.

"You don't have to do the whole truth," she said. "You could just tell them to stay indoors. Keep it small."

"No." I keyed the mic. The red light bloomed brighter, greedy. "Small is how we got here. Small is forty-one calls I logged and didn't believe. Small is a door I drove past every day of my childhood." I looked at her. "Big, Wren. We go big or the hill goes big first."

She nodded once. Deaconess to the end, even lapsed. Especially lapsed.

I pressed the button.

"This is Kestrel Amberdale," I said, "and for once — God help me, for the first time in eleven years — everything I'm about to say is true."

* * *

The words came easier than I expected. That frightened me most of all, how easy it was, once you stopped polishing. I told them about the funeral caller in week one, the woman who described her own casket spray — white chrysanthemums, her sister's handwriting on the card — and how I'd cut her off and called it a prank because believing her would have cost me something I wasn't ready to pay. I told them about the man who asked me to apologize to his daughter, and how the records showed he'd been nine days drowned, and how I pulled the log at 4 a.m. with shaking hands and counted forty-one calls from the dead in fourteen days.

I told them about the excavation behind the chapel. About Dov Hale hitting iron instead of stone. About a door sealed from the *inside*, hinges fused, wax rubbed across it revealing one word cut deep enough to survive centuries: AMBERDALE. Ringed by tally marks. Hundreds of them.

"That's my name," I said. "My family built that chapel. My family built that door. And my family has been feeding whatever waits behind it for longer than this town has had a name. Every mark on that iron is a soul. Your souls. Your mothers, your sons, the girl who used to lifeguard at the quarry — I've taken your calls, I've played your requests, and while I kept you company at night, my family's debt was being paid with your people."

Wren's hand found my knee. I hadn't realized I'd stopped breathing.

"And Marlowe Vetch knew." The name came out of me like a splinter leaving flesh. "He found the ratings. Dead-callers hour, biggest audience in county history. He ordered me not to investigate. He rebranded this show without my consent. He made your grief into inventory. And three days ago he met something in the basement of this very station — something on a dead line that offered him the biggest signal in three counties if the show never stopped and the Ambers stayed in town. He said yes. He's probably listening right now, wherever he is, and I want him to hear this: your signal is a leash, Marlowe. You're not the broadcaster. You're the bait."

The phone lines lit up.

Not one line. All of them. Line one, line two, the hotline, the request line, even the old business number nobody had called since the fax machine died. The board lit like a Christmas tree, like a runway, like a congregation raising hands. And beneath the ringing — beneath it, woven through it — the whole town seemed to exhale. Through the studio's double glass I could see out across the parking lot to the street beyond, and the people standing in their yards were turning. Slowly, all together, faces coming down off the moon, turning toward their kitchen windows where their radios glowed, toward porch speakers, toward the little transistor in the Ostrander twins' hands.

They were waking up. They were listening.

"The harvest moon rises in nineteen minutes," I said. "When it clears the ridge, the count completes — unless it doesn't. There's a verse missing from the hymnal. Torn out, years before either of us was born, because it doesn't feed the thing below. It closes the count. It ends it. And it requires an Amberdale — the last Amberdale — to sing it inside the threshold, willingly, at moonrise." I laughed, and it came out cracked and real. "That's me. That's always been me. Grandma Edith walked down those same stairs in 1961 and the tape cuts out mid-sentence, and I used to think that was tragedy. Now I know it was a relay. She carried it as far as she could, and now it's my leg of the race."

Line one blinked. I punched it open without thinking, eleven years of reflex.

A voice, soft and papery: "Kestrel? It's Alma Pruitt."

My stomach dropped. Alma Pruitt had taught piano to half the town. Alma Pruitt had been buried — I remembered now, I'd attended, I'd given the eulogy because her family asked — eleven days ago.

"I'm not calling to haunt you, honey," she said, and there was a smile in it, tired and warm, the voice of a thousand Tuesday lessons. "None of us are. We tried fear first because fear gets attention. But you're telling the truth now, and the truth unties knots. I'm calling to say goodbye."

"Alma—"

"Tell Ruthie her scales were never as bad as she thought. Tell her the metronome was set too fast on purpose, to build her patience." A pause, and when the voice came back it was thinner, already receding, a tide going out. "And Kestrel — sing loud. We'll hold the door frame steady from our side."

The line clicked to dial tone.

And then they came, one after another after another, and I stopped counting and just opened every line at once and let the board carry them. A man who'd died in the mill collapse in '94, saying goodbye to a brother who still blamed himself — *it was the cable, Danny, it was never you*. A young woman drowned in the reservoir, telling her mother where she'd left the good scissors, telling her the grief was allowed now, telling her *put it down, Mama, it's heavy and I'm fine*. Old Tom Vasser, dead thirty years, laughing through static: *the count's honest arithmetic, girl, but arithmetic can be closed*. Caller after caller, and each goodbye landed in Hallowmere like a stone dropped into still water, and through the glass I watched the town come alive with weeping — good weeping, the kind that unclenches — porch lights flicking on, doors opening, people falling into each other's arms in their yards, grief moving through the streets like weather breaking after a season of drought.

"They're not haunting," Wren whispered beside me, tears cutting clean lines down her dusty face. "They never were. They were holding the grief hostage until someone told the truth about why."

"Silence feeds it," I said. "Every secret was a mark on the door."

Vetch's voice came through the monitor channel then — faint, warped, coming up out of the building's bones rather than any microphone. *"You're stealing my audience, Amberdale."* The words dragged, doubled, like two men speaking in one throat. *"I have a contract. Three counties. You think your little confession beats a signed—"*

I reached over and pulled the master fader on the basement feed all the way down. The voice cut to nothing. Somewhere below us, in the dark under the studios, I heard — or imagined — screaming that had no more wire to travel on.

"Fourteen minutes," Wren said. She stood, hymnal clutched to her chest, and for the first time since she'd arrived uninvited with our grandmother's book under her arm, she looked afraid for me instead of afraid *of* me. "Kestrel. Once you start down those stairs, there's no version of tonight where I get my cousin back."

"There's a version where nobody else loses anybody," I said. "That's the trade. I did the math a long time ago; I just didn't know I was doing it." I keyed the mic again, and my voice went out over Hallowmere calm and clear, the way I'd talked a thousand insomniacs through a thousand bad nights. "Listen to me, all of you. Lock nothing. Fear nothing after tonight. Go to your neighbors. Say the goodbyes you were given. And if you pass the old chapel on the ridge road in the next hour, don't look at the door — look at the sky. Watch the moon come up over a valley that owes it nothing. That's the show. That's the last broadcast of the Night Line, and it's the only one that ever mattered."

I took off the headphones. Set them on the board, neatly, the way you'd close a book.

Wren was holding the door to the stairwell, holding it the way Dov Hale would hold the outer seal — open, steady, weeping without shame.

"Grandma Edith's tape ends mid-sentence," she said. "Finish it. Finish the sentence, Kestrel."

Below us, deep under the chapel hill, something patient turned its many borrowed faces toward the sound of an Amberdale coming to keep an appointment.

I picked up the hymnal, and together we went down.
