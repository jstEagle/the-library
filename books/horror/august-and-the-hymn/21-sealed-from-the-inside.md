## 21. Sealed From the Inside

Wren had never believed her hands would shake like this. Not at her mother's funeral, not when she signed the papers that unmade her from the deaconate, not even the night she found the hymnal's final page and understood what it was asking. Her hands had always been steady. Steady hands, her grandmother used to say, are the only inheritance worth having.

They shook now, and she let them.

She stood at the top of the chapel cellar stairs with the door held wide — Dov's hands under the iron lip, his arms trembling with a weight that had nothing to do with pounds, his face gone the color of candle tallow — and she watched her cousin descend into the dark with a hymnal pressed to their chest like a shield that was also a wound.

Kestrel didn't look back. That was the worst of it, or the best. Wren had rehearsed a hundred goodbyes in the drive over, in the weeks since the roads had stopped pretending to leave town, and not one of them had survived contact with the actual moment. Kestrel simply went, the way you go downstairs to answer a phone you already know is ringing for you.

The moonlight came down the stairwell behind them, thin and white and wrong, sliding along the stone like water finding a channel it had always known. Wren watched it pool at the bottom, watched it climb the far wall, and she understood — with the terrible clarity that had been her curse since childhood, since the summer she'd found her grandmother's notes and read them by flashlight under blankets while the rest of the house slept — that the light was not illuminating the cellar.

It was arriving. On schedule.

"Go," Dov said through his teeth. Not to her. To the door, or to himself, or to whatever was holding him upright. His knuckles were white where they gripped the iron. "Go, go, go—"

She went. She took the stairs backward, one at a time, her hand on the cold wall, because she could not make herself turn away from the dark below until the last step forced her to. At the bottom, small now, Kestrel stood in the pooled moonlight with their back to her, and the thing that had worn generations of Amberdale faces was rising to meet them — she couldn't see it, not exactly, but she could see the light bending around something, the way heat bends above summer asphalt, and she could hear it.

Not a voice. A congregation. A low, patient hum, like a hymn sung by people who had forgotten the words but not the tune, sung so long the forgetting had become part of the song.

Kestrel opened the hymnal. Wren saw the torn page — the verse she'd found, the missing one, the closing verse, *the one that ends the count* — held in Kestrel's hand, and then Kestrel began to sing.

* * *

Wren had heard the hymn a thousand ways in her life. Sung properly in the old chapel before the roof went, mumbled by her grandmother over dishwater, hummed by aunts at bedsides, and — since the excavation — rising out of the hill itself some nights, out of the ground, out of the water mains, out of the fillings in her teeth. She had thought she knew every version of it.

She had never heard it sung like this.

Kestrel's voice was not good. It had never been good; family talent had pooled elsewhere, in the hands, in the stubbornness. But the verse didn't want good. The verse wanted true. And Kestrel sang it true — flat notes and cracked breath and all — and the cellar took the sound and did something to it that Wren felt in her sternum, in her molars, in the soles of her feet through her boots. The hum from below faltered. The congregation-stutter. The patience, for the first time in two hundred years of tally marks, wavered.

The words were simple. That was the horror and the mercy of them. Wren had spent weeks believing the missing verse would be some incantation, some locked grammar of the old tongue. It was not. It was a harvest song, the same as the others, except the others were instructions for feeding and this one was a receipt. *What was given is returned. What was counted is closed. Let the table be empty and the chairs be folded and the family be finished, and let the hunger learn to keep its own door.*

*Let the family be finished.*

Wren stood at the top of the stairs with the door in her hands now — Dov had let go, had fallen back against the wall, half-hollowed, his lips still moving with a tune he'd never learned — and she held it open with arms that shook, and she listened to her cousin finish the family, verse by verse, and she grieved.

That was the thing no one warned you about sacrifice: it doesn't feel noble from the outside. It feels like arithmetic. One cousin, weighed against a town. One name, crossed off against forty-one callers and a hillside of the fed and a valley full of sleeping people standing in their yards with their faces turned up like sunflowers. Wren had done the math a dozen times and the math always came out the same, and the math never once made it hurt less. The math just made the hurt honest.

Kestrel reached the last line.

*Let the hunger learn to keep its own door.*

The hum stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, the way a heart stops, the way a clock stops, the absolute silence of a thing that had been making noise so long the noise had become the shape of the world. Wren's ears rang in the vacancy. Below, in the pooled light, she saw Kestrel lower the hymnal, saw their shoulders drop — not defeat, she would know that shape anywhere, it was the shape of someone setting down a weight they'd carried so long they'd forgotten it was heavy — and then Kestrel turned, finally, and looked up the stairwell at her.

Wren would carry that look for the rest of her life. It wasn't afraid. It wasn't sad, exactly. It was the look of someone who has finally, after a whole life of brittle professional detachment and insomnia and refusal to believe anything that couldn't be held, held something. And found it lighter than expected.

Kestrel smiled. Mouthed something — she read it off their lips through the moonlit dark, two words, and she would not be sure until weeks later, until the phone call, which of the things they might have said it actually was — and then stepped back, out of the moonlight, into the dark that had been waiting for an Amberdale for two hundred years, and was, at last, fed.

The door moved.

Not pushed. Not pulled. It swung, on hinges that had been fused for two centuries, smooth and unhurried, like a door closing behind the last guest of a long dinner party. Wren felt the air of it pass her on the stairs, warm, smelling of turned earth and candle smoke and — she would swear this to no one, ever — her grandmother's kitchen, bread and lavender and the particular dust of old hymnal paper.

The iron met the frame.

And then it fused. Wren watched it happen and her mind refused it and she watched it anyway: the seam of the door sealing itself, the metal flowing like wax under a thumb, the hinges drinking themselves back into the slab, until there was no door at all. There was only a wall of iron, featureless, unbroken, the kind of wall that looks like it had never been anything else. As if the excavation crew had dreamed it. As if the whole town had dreamed it.

She put her hand on it. The iron was warm, then cooling, then — by the time she found her voice — simply cold. Stone-cold. Earth-cold. The cold of a thing that had never been warm.

And the tally marks were going.

She saw them go. The carved rings of marks, the hundreds of them, the arithmetic of two centuries of feeding — they were smoothing from the inside out, each groove rising shut like a scar healing in fast-forward, the iron erasing its own ledger. She traced one with her fingertip and felt it close under her touch, a seam knitting, and then there was nothing under her fingertip but blank metal.

The count was closed. The count had never been opened. Both were true, and only one of them mattered, and Wren stood in the cellar stairwell with her palm flat against blank iron and wept the way you weep at funerals for people who died of old age, full of years, full of peace — grief and relief in the same breath, indivisible, indistinguishable, one thing wearing two names.

Behind her, Dov made a sound. She turned. He was sliding down the wall, his hands empty now, his lips still, the hymn gone out of him like water out of a cracked cup. He looked up at her with eyes that were his own again, mostly, and said, in the voice of a man who had spent a month being a tool and was only now remembering he was a person:

"It's done?"

"It's done," Wren said.

He nodded, and put his face in his hands, and cried like a foreman, which is to say quietly, and only for a minute, and then never again where anyone could see.

* * *

They came up out of the chapel into a town waking.

That was the only word for it. All the way down Chapel Hill, in yards and doorways and parked cars with their doors open, the people of Hallowmere were stirring out of the standing sleep, blinking, looking at the moon — still up, still full, still harvest-gold, and utterly ordinary now, just a moon, just rock and light, with nothing arriving on schedule beneath it. A woman two doors from the church stood in her rose bed in her nightgown, looked down at the dirt on her knees, and laughed — a startled, embarrassed, wonderful laugh, the laugh of someone waking from a dream they can't remember and don't need to.

The phones, Wren thought. By tomorrow the phones would just be phones. She didn't know how she knew it. She knew it the way she knew the hymn.

She walked down through the town with Dov trailing her, and the night smelled like autumn and nothing else, and somewhere a dog barked at the moon for the ordinary reasons dogs bark at moons. At the bottom of the hill she stopped and looked back once. The chapel stood black against the stars, derelict, unremarkable, the hill behind it just a hill. If she hadn't known, she would never have guessed there was anything under it but earth.

That, she understood, was the point. That had always been the point. The best door is the one that looks like a wall. The best ending is the one that looks like it never began.

She still had Kestrel's hymnal. Kestrel had left it on the cellar floor — no. Wren stopped in the middle of the empty street. Kestrel had handed it to her, at the top of the stairs, before descending. She was certain of it. She had been certain of the opposite an hour ago. The memory sat in her mind like a coin on its edge, and she let it be, because some things you don't knock on to check if they're solid.

Inside the cover, in pencil, in Kestrel's slanting on-air scrawl, someone had written a note that had not been there that morning:

*Take the night shift. Somebody should answer.*

* * *

She didn't cry again until Thursday.

She got through the funeral first — small, at the edge of the Hallowmere cemetery, a stone with a name and two dates and no body, because there was no body, because there had never been a door. Half the town came. Dov came with his whole crew, and stood at the back with his cap in his hands. Marlowe Vetch did not come; Vetch was in the county hospital, and what was left of him — the doctors used words like *episode* and *breakdown* — did not speak, except once, on the second night, when a nurse heard him say clearly, to the empty corner of the room: "You promised me signal." The nurses wrote it off. Wren did not.

The station had gone quiet after that night. The transmitter still ran; the license still stood; but nobody had hosted the Night Line, and the dead had stopped calling, all at once, the way they'd gone quiet before the end — and this time they had stayed quiet, because there was no one left below to do the calling, and no count left to count.

On Thursday, in the empty studio, Wren sat down in the chair that had been her grandmother's and then Kestrel's, and put on the headphones, and opened the fader, and said into the dark of the microphone: "This is the Night Line. Lines are open."

She cried then, finally, alone in the booth with the ON AIR light glowing red and steady, crying for a cousin who had walked downstairs so a town could wake up, crying for a grandmother who had done the same and been erased for it, crying for a family that had been saved by ending, which is the only way some families can be saved. Grief and relief, indivisible. She let them be one thing. She let the song be finished.

The phones stayed quiet. Mostly.

Because on her last night — three weeks in, when she'd begun to believe that quiet was simply what the rest of her life would sound like — at 3:07 in the morning, with the moon a thin ordinary crescent and the coffee gone cold in the cup, one line lit up.

She stared at it for a long time. The rule she'd made for herself sat on the desk in her own handwriting: *some doors you don't knock on again.* But the light was patient, and patience, she knew better than anyone alive, was a thing you could inherit in either direction.

She opened the fader.

"Night Line," she said.

And the voice that came through was calm, and tired in the way of someone who has finally slept, and it was Kestrel's, and it said only:

"Count's closed."

Then the dial tone, soft as a door swinging shut, and the light going out, and the booth so quiet Wren could hear the transmitter humming its one long note out into the dark over Hallowmere, where the town slept, and slept, and slept.
