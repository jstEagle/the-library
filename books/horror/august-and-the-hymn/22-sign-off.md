## 22. Sign-Off

The studio smelled different when it was hers.

Not better — that was the first thing Wren had to admit, sitting in Kestrel's chair with the board's faders worn smooth under her fingers — just different. Kestrel had kept a tin of ginger candies in the desk drawer and a coffee mug shaped like a tombstone, both of which she'd thrown out in the first week, and both of which had somehow reappeared by the third, as if the station itself had opinions about what belonged here. She'd stopped fighting it. The mug sat at the corner of the console now, chipped and grinning its little skull grin, holding pens instead of coffee. Some inheritances you don't get to refuse. You only get to decide what to fill them with.

"Night Line," she said into the mic, the way she'd said it every night for six weeks. "This is Wren Amberdale. It's eleven-oh-three, the fog's coming up off the valley like it always does this time of year, and the lines are open."

She'd kept the show's bones but changed its flesh. No more dead callers — or rather, no more *hunting* for them, no more leaning into the static the way Kestrel used to lean, hungry for proof. If someone called, they called. Mostly they didn't. Mostly it was Mrs. Aldous wanting to know if anyone had seen her cat, and teenagers requesting songs at two in the morning because Hallowmere had exactly one radio station and nothing else to do after dark, and old men who wanted to talk about the weather the way other men talked about their wives: with a tenderness that embarrassed them.

The phones were just phones now. That was the phrase she used, privately, like a prayer she didn't quite believe. *Just phones.* The word *mostly* lived right behind it, where she kept all the things she couldn't say on air.

Because mostly wasn't always.

* * *

The town had come back wrong and then slowly, grudgingly, right. In the weeks after the harvest moon, people walked around Hallowmere like sleepwalkers who'd woken mid-stride and decided not to mention it. Dov Hale told her once, over bad coffee at the diner, that he still dreamed about the hymn sometimes — but now when he woke, he couldn't remember how it went, and that forgetting felt like being paid back something he'd never owed. He'd taken his crew off the chapel job permanently. The county had filled the excavation with gravel and poured a slab over it, and the slab already had moss on it, which was impossible in October, but Wren had learned to stop filing impossibilities under *later.*

Vetch was gone. Not dead — though for a while the town had argued about it, the way towns argue about anything that lets them avoid the truth. He'd simply left. Sold the station to a holding company whose paperwork led nowhere, packed whatever he had left after the dark finished with him in the cellar, and driven east. Someone said they saw him in a diner outside Corvallis, sitting alone, mouthing along to a song nobody else could hear. Wren chose to believe he was somewhere with a big signal and an empty room, broadcasting to no one, forever. It was kinder than the alternative, and kindness was a discipline now. She practiced it daily.

And Kestrel —

Kestrel was a door that had closed from the inside.

Wren had spent the first weeks furious about it. Properly, biblically furious, the kind of anger that has scripture underneath it whether you believe anymore or not. Her cousin — the last of them, the one who was supposed to run, the one she'd crossed three states to drag out by the collar — had stood in front of a microphone and told a whole town the truth, and then walked down into the dark and sung the verse that wasn't in any hymnal anymore, and shut the door, and ended the family line with their own name on the tally. And hadn't even left her a note. Just a broadcast. Just a voice on tape saying *I'm sorry, Wren. Sing the last page for me sometime,* and then the sound of footsteps going down, and then nothing.

She'd sung it. Once, in the chapel, alone, at noon when the light was honest. The hymnal's final page, the torn verse reconstructed from memory and from the way the paper ghosts showed where ink had been lifted. It had sounded thin without a second voice. Everything did, now.

But fury is a fuel that burns out, and what was under it, when it cooled, was worse and better at once: peace. A haunted, unearned, infuriating peace. The count was closed. The tally marks had smoothed to blank iron. Whatever patient hunger had worn generations of Amberdale faces down there in the cellar dark was fed its final meal and sealed behind a door that had fused like it had never been opened — and the price had been the last person on earth who shared her blood.

Some nights she thought that was a fair trade. Most nights she thought it was a theft. All nights, she thought about calling the station before she'd taken it over, just to hear the dial tone, just to know the line was still there.

Then she'd taken the station over, and the line was hers, and she couldn't call it anymore without it ringing in her own ears.

* * *

Six weeks in, she'd built new rituals around the old ones. She opened the show with the fog report because Kestrel had, and because the fog in Hallowmere deserved coverage; it came up off the drowned valley like something exhaling, and some nights it carried a smell like wet iron and candle smoke, and she reported that too, in her flat deaconess voice, and the town found it comforting. Honesty as liturgy. She'd learned that from a dead woman's reel-to-reel and a cousin who'd finally stopped lying on air.

She signed off at four a.m. sharp. Kestrel had drifted past four, chasing the hour when the phones got strange. Wren closed the window early. Doors, she'd learned, were best shut on purpose, by your own hand, before something else shut them for you.

On her last night — she'd announced it two weeks prior, told the town she was moving the show to Sundays only, then to once a month, then to a taped rerun, then to nothing, a slow fade so the silence wouldn't feel like abandonment — she did everything the same. Fog report. Requests. Mrs. Aldous called about the cat, who had been dead since August and whom Wren gently informed was probably sleeping in the sun somewhere warm, and Mrs. Aldous had thanked her and hung up, and neither of them had acknowledged what either of them knew. That was the arrangement now. The town had made its peace with the thin places the way you make peace with a drafty window: you stop pretending you can't feel it, and you stop trying to seal it, and you put a chair near it because sometimes the cold feels like company.

At 3:47 a.m., thirteen minutes before sign-off, with the fog pressing white against the studio glass and the transmitter humming its low faithful note, Line One lit up.

Wren looked at it for a long moment.

It wasn't the light itself. Lights happened. Wrong numbers happened. But 3:47 was the minute every clock in Dov Hale's camp had stopped, the night the outer seal cracked, and she was not a woman who believed in coincidence anymore — coincidence was just a word people used for patterns they were too tired to read.

She let it ring four times. Then she pulled the fader.

"Night Line," she said. "You're on."

Static first. But not the hungry kind — not the deep, wet, listening static she remembered from the tapes, from the weeks when the dead had queued up like penitents. This was ordinary static, the kind that lives inside every wire, patient and dumb and harmless. And under it, a voice.

Calm. Unhurried. Familiar in the way a scar is familiar.

"Count's closed."

Wren's hand went to her mouth. The board lights glowed green and steady around her, and the fog stood against the window like a congregation, and she could not have spoken for a thousand pounds of money.

She made herself speak anyway. Deaconess discipline. Voice level, hands steady, heart going like a bird against glass.

"Kestrel?"

A pause. Long enough that she almost convinced herself she'd imagined the timbre, that it was a kid prank-calling, that grief had finally done what grief does and dressed itself up as a voice on the phone. Then:

"Count's closed," the voice said again, gentle, the way you'd repeat something to someone waking from anesthesia. Not annoyed. Not cold. Calm the way the bottom of a lake is calm. "You can take the chair down off the desk now, Wren. You don't have to guard it anymore."

"I know," she whispered, and then, louder, into the mic, because some things needed to be said on the record: "I know it's closed. I checked. I check every week. The door's blank, Kestrel. There's nothing written on it. There's nothing *under* it. You did it. You —"

"Wren." Almost amused now. Kestrel's voice, the on-air voice, the one that had talked a whole town through the worst night of its life. "Breathe. I'm not calling to haunt you. I'm calling because the line was open. That's all this ever was, in the end. Open lines." A breath of static, soft as a turned page. "Tell Dov he can sleep. Tell the town the harvest goes unclaimed, and tell them to plant something in the spring. Something that grows tall. They'll know why."

"The hymnal's on my shelf," Wren said, and heard her own voice crack down the middle like river ice. "Your page. I kept your page."

"I know." And there — there it was, buried in the calm, a warmth so small and so enormous that it nearly took the rest of her voice away with it. "Sing it on Sundays. Loud enough to reach the gravel lot. I liked the fog reports, by the way. You're better at this than I was. Don't argue. It's my last night to be right about something."

Wren laughed, and it came out broken, and she let it. "This isn't fair. You don't get to do the goodbye thing. That's my — we were supposed to —"

"I know what we were supposed to do." Quiet now. The static breathing around the voice like tide around a stone. "We were supposed to leave together, and I was supposed to keep lying, and the town was supposed to keep listening, and the thing below was supposed to keep waiting with our faces on. None of that happened. That's the whole miracle, Wren. None of it happened." A pause. "Some doors you don't knock on again. So I won't. This is me not knocking. One time. Last time."

"Don't hang up yet."

"I have to. Sign-off's at four. You taught me that." And she could hear it, actually hear it, the smile in it. "Take care of the mug. It knows more than it says."

The dial tone came then — clean, ordinary, the most beautiful ugly sound in the world — and the light on Line One went dark, and the studio was so quiet Wren could hear the fog moving against the glass like a hand laid flat.

She sat there until 4:00 a.m. exactly. Then she leaned into the mic, and her voice held, because she made it hold, because that was the family gift and the family curse and she would carry it however she had to carry it:

"That was the last call, Hallowmere. For those just joining us: the count is closed. The harvest goes unclaimed. Plant something tall in the spring." She swallowed. "This is Wren Amberdale, signing off. Goodnight, Kestrel. Goodnight, everybody. The lines are closed."

She faded the music in — the old hymn, the one with the missing verse, hummed low under the strings where only she and maybe one listener in the dark would know what was absent — and she took the tombstone mug down off the corner of the console, and she washed it, and she set it on the shelf beside the hymnal, facing outward, keeping watch.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the valley for the first time in weeks, and beneath the gravel and the moss and the blank iron door, something that had waited a very long time was, at last, simply quiet.

Mostly.

She locked the studio door behind her, twice, out of habit — and then, smiling at herself in the dark glass, unlocked it again.

Some doors, after all, you left open on purpose.
