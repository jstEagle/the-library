## 5. Tomas Comes Down the Hill

The road up from the valley had a way of making a man feel he was being swallowed.

Tomas Vasquez drove it with both hands on the wheel, the way his father used to, knuckles pale against the leather, as though the car might otherwise drift off the verge and into the hedgerows that leaned close like gossips. Wren sat beside him with her headphones on, drawing in the sketchbook she never let him see. Nine years old and already she'd learned to keep her interior rooms locked. He wondered where she'd learned it. He wondered if it was in the blood, the way the flinch was in the blood—the small involuntary tightening he felt every time the road bent past the standing stone at the parish boundary, the one shaped like a tooth.

He hadn't been back in eleven years. He had told himself, all the drive from London, that Hollowmere was just a place now, drained of everything but its inconvenience: a house to sell, a solicitor to sign, a daughter who needed the country air for a fortnight while the flat above his office was repainted. That was the story. He had rehearsed it so many times it had begun to sound true, which was, he understood dimly, how all his family's stories worked.

The village appeared the way it always did—not gradually but suddenly, as if it had been holding its breath behind the hill and decided, at the last moment, to exhale. Grey stone, grey slate, a grey-green river going about its business without enthusiasm. The pub. The church with its squat tower. The shop with its hand-lettered sign advertising eggs and ice. And everywhere, threaded through the ordinary afternoon like wire through bread, the sense of being watched by people who were very careful not to look.

He parked outside the estate gates because the gates themselves hadn't opened in years; the mechanism was seized with rust and something else, something that made the iron cold even in August. Wren pulled off her headphones and looked up.

"That's our house?"

"It's a house I own," Tomas said. "There's a difference."

She considered this with the grave attention she gave everything lately, then said nothing, which was worse than any question. She had started doing that in the spring—going quiet at odd moments, her head tilting slightly, as though listening to a radio only she could receive. The pediatrician had used words like *developmental phase* and *nothing to worry about*, and Tomas had nodded along, because nodding along was the Vasquez inheritance too, older than the house, older than the name.

They walked up the drive through weeds that came past their knees. The east wing had come down three winters ago—a slow collapse, no fire, no storm, just one morning a great exhale of dust and the roofline changed forever—and the rubble had sat there since under tarps that the wind had peeled back like bandages from a wound nobody wanted to treat.

Except somebody was treating it now. He heard them before he saw them: the ring of a trowel against stone, a generator's idle stutter, voices kept low the way voices are kept low in hospitals and churches.

A heritage survey crew. He'd had the letter—he remembered signing something, or meaning to sign something, in the fat folder of papers his solicitor sent before any sale. *Preliminary archaeological assessment of the collapsed east wing.* He'd assumed it meant paperwork. It apparently meant men in hi-vis vests kneeling in the bones of his family's house, lifting stones out of the dark one by one.

"Oi." His voice came out harder than he intended. "This is private property."

The foreman straightened up. A broad man, sunburnt, with the particular weathered cheerfulness of someone who spent his days persuading the ground to give things up. "Mr. Vasquez? Didn't expect you till Thursday."

"You have me today. What is this?"

"Survey. Council requirement before sale—heritage clause. We're just cataloguing, mate. Nothing invasive." The foreman wiped his hands on his thighs. "Well. Not much invasive."

"Not much." Tomas looked past him, at the trench they'd cut into the collapse, at the dark opening in the earth where the wing's ground floor had folded down into whatever lay beneath it. Cellars. He knew there were cellars. He had been told once, as a child, never to go into the cellars, and had been told it in the tone that made a boy understand the instruction was load-bearing, that some part of the house's honesty rested on his obedience.

He had obeyed. He had left at eighteen and never gone down, and now here were strangers with torches, peering into the family dark like it was a job site.

"I want it filled in," he said. "Today. All of it. Backfill the trench, put the stones back, and send your invoice to my solicitor."

The foreman blinked. "Filled in? We're two days from—"

"Two days from what?"

The foreman's mouth opened and closed. Around him, the crew had gone still in that particular way workmen go still—not stopping, exactly, but slowing until the stillness became deniable. One of them, a young woman with a dust mask hanging around her neck, was looking at the trench instead of at him, and her expression was the expression of someone listening.

"There's a door down there," the foreman said finally, and laughed, and the laugh didn't hold. "Old one. Stone. You'd know it, presumably, growing up here."

Tomas knew it. He knew it the way you know a face from a photograph you were shown too young—inverted, wrong, belonging to a dream rather than a memory. A door. He could almost see it: grey stone, iron bolt, and—

And the bolt on the wrong side.

He didn't know where that detail came from. He had never seen the door. But the knowledge arrived whole and cold, the way the flinch arrived when the car passed the standing stone, and he understood that his grandfather or his father or some patient ancestor had pressed this into him the way you press a seal into wax: *the door is shut from within, and you must never wonder by whom.*

"Fill it in," he said again, quieter.

"It's a scheduled assessment, Mr. Vasquez. There's process. There's—" The foreman rubbed the back of his neck. "Honestly, there's a couple of the lads who wouldn't mind going home early. Bad air down there. Old houses breathe funny."

*Old houses breathe funny.* Tomas looked at the man and saw that this was a kindness dressed as an excuse, an offered ladder, and hated him for it, and wanted to climb it.

"Then find the process," Tomas said, "that lets you leave."

* * *

The woman was waiting for him at the fence line when he came back down the drive with Wren trailing behind, sketchbook hugged to her chest.

"You're the heir." She said it not as a question but as a diagnosis. She was perhaps forty, sharp-faced, with the sunburn and scuffed boots of someone who walked a lot of fields, and a recorder in her hand—a proper field recorder, foam windshield, the works. "Odile Vasquez."

"No relation," Tomas said.

"That's what everyone says." She smiled, and it was a good smile, quick and wry, and he distrusted it immediately, the way you distrust anything beautiful found near a ruin. "I've been in the village three years. Recording songs. Folk material. Your family comes up in quite a lot of it."

"Does it."

"The ballads do. There's one fragment about a door shut from within. I'd love to ask you—"

"No."

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"And the answer's no." He shifted Wren behind him without meaning to—a small movement, half a step, his hand finding her shoulder. He saw the woman see it. Her eyes went to Wren, lingered a moment too long, and something in her face changed, softened, sharpened; he couldn't tell which and didn't want to know.

"She's tired," Tomas said. "We're leaving."

"It's four in the afternoon."

"We're leaving anyway."

He walked. Behind him he heard her call out—not chasing, just sending the words after him like stones skipped across water: "The knocking starts around nine! You'll hear it! Everyone pretends they don't!"

He kept walking. His hand was tight on Wren's shoulder, tighter than he'd realized, and she said, "Ow, Dad," and he loosened it and apologized and hated himself in the low, familiar way.

* * *

The cottage his solicitor had arranged was at the far end of the village, last house before the fields, rented from a landlady named Mrs. Tredwell who met them at the door with keys and a face like a closed ledger. She showed them the rooms quickly, competently, touching nothing longer than necessary. On the kitchen windowsill there was a line of salt, coarse and white, laid out neat as an altar offering.

"Spillage," she said, before he could ask.

"From what?"

"From the bag." She handed him the keys. "Dinner's not included. The tap runs brown first thing; let it clear. And Mr. Vasquez—" she paused at the door, and for a moment her professional blankness slipped and he saw underneath it something old and tired and sorry, "—if you hear anything after dark, it's the pipes. This whole village has bad pipes."

She left before he could answer.

Wren claimed the back bedroom, the one overlooking the garden and, beyond the garden wall, the long slope of the estate grounds rising toward the broken silhouette of the house. She unpacked her sketchbook on the bed and drew with her door open, which she never did at home, and Tomas stood in the hallway watching her and felt the strange double vision that had haunted him since the drive: she was his daughter, ten years old, ink on her fingers; and she was also a Vasquez child sleeping under a Vasquez roof, and the last time a Vasquez child had done that—

He didn't finish the thought. Finishing thoughts was how his father had died, according to his mother: not of anything the doctors could name, but of thinking too hard in this house, in August, alone.

* * *

At nine o'clock precisely, the knocking began.

It came up through the floor. Not from the walls, not from the pipes—Mrs. Tredwell's pipes ran visibly along the ceiling of the bathroom, and they ticked and groaned in their own language, arrhythmic, metallic. This was different. This was patient. Three knocks, a pause, three knocks, a pause, steady as a pulse, steady as a metronome, coming from below the floorboards, below the foundations, below the earth itself, as though something down there were tapping on the underside of the world to check whether anyone was home.

Tomas sat on the edge of his bed with the lamp on and listened.

He had heard it before. That was the thing he had spent eleven years successfully not remembering, and now it came back with the door-knowledge, whole and cold: he had heard it as a boy, on the visits he'd been brought on each August, lying in this same cottage or one like it, hearing the knocking and asking his father what it was, and his father had said *pipes*, and young Tomas had accepted it because children accept the stories adults need them to accept, and the acceptance had calcified over the years into genuine forgetting, which is the mercy families build for their children and the curse they build for themselves.

Three knocks. Pause. Three knocks.

He got up. He told himself he was getting up to make tea, and he made tea, and he stood in the kitchen drinking it with the light on, and the knocking continued beneath his feet, unhurried, unoffended, as though it had all the time in the world, as though it had been counting for centuries and would count for centuries more.

Counting.

The word arrived uninvited. He set the mug down. Where had that word come from?

He went to Wren's room. Her door was open a few inches, lamplight spilling out, and he eased it wider and looked in and his heart performed a small, terrible maneuver.

She was asleep. She was asleep and murmuring, lips moving, and her fingers—her right hand, resting on the quilt—were tapping. Three taps. Pause. Three taps. In perfect time with the knocking beneath the floor, the way a singer keeps time with a song only she can hear.

"Wren." He crossed the room and took her hand gently and the tapping stopped, and the knocking below stopped too, a beat later, like something correcting itself.

Her eyes opened halfway. They were unfocused, dream-heavy, and she smiled at him with such simple, unguarded affection that his chest cracked open.

"Dad," she said. "She says you came back just in time."

"Who says?" His voice was very calm. He was proud of how calm it was. "Who says that, sweetheart?"

But she was already sinking back down, already gone, her breathing lengthening into sleep, her hand slack in his. He tucked the quilt around her and sat in the chair by her bed for a long time, watching the window, where the curtain breathed in and out with a draft that shouldn't have existed in a sealed room on a still August night.

Below the floorboards, after a while, the knocking resumed. Softer now. Almost apologetic.

Three. Pause. Three.

Tomas did not sleep. At some point he took out his phone and searched the village for the folklorist—Vasquez, Odile, the woman at the fence—and found nothing useful, only a university page listing her fieldwork: *Hollowmere song cycle project, ongoing.* Ongoing. As though the songs were still being sung. As though anyone here sang anything twice.

Near dawn the knocking stopped altogether, and in the silence that followed—the first silence he'd known in this village—Tomas understood two things with perfect clarity.

The first was that filling in the dig would change nothing. You cannot bury a door that opens inward.

The second was that he had brought his daughter back to Hollowmere in August, and somewhere below the floor of the world, patiently, without malice, something had begun counting—and it had counted her the moment she crossed the parish boundary, the moment her small feet touched Vasquez ground.

In the morning, he would call the foreman. In the morning, he would be reasonable, procedural, cold; he would perform indifference the way his father had, the way his grandfather must have, wearing it like a coat over the terror.

But tonight he sat by his sleeping daughter and listened to the quiet, and waited, with a father's helpless arithmetic, for the next knock.
