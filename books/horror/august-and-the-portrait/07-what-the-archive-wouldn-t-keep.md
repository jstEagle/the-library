## 7. What the Archive Wouldn't Keep

The county records office sat forty miles east of Hollowmere, in a market town whose name Odile had already forgotten by the time the bus let her off. It was that kind of place—built of the same honeyed stone as everywhere else in the county, with a war memorial and a bakery and a church spire that leaned, very slightly, like a man listening at a door. She had chosen it precisely because it was ordinary. Three years in Hollowmere had taught her that the extraordinary kept its accounts in dull rooms.

The office occupied the ground floor of a converted granary: high windows, green-shaded lamps, the smell of dust and cold iron and old glue. A radiator ticked somewhere behind the shelves like a small patient heart. Odile signed the visitors' book—*O. Vasquez, folklorist, research purposes*—and felt the familiar small thrill she always felt entering an archive, the sense of standing in a room where everything was dead but nothing had quite stopped speaking.

The archivist who came to meet her was a thin man in his sixties named Mr. Pell, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and the particular gentleness of someone who has spent his life handling things that fall apart.

"Vasquez," he said, when he read her request slip. He said it the way people in Hollowmere said it—not the way you say a surname, but the way you say *weather coming*. "The estate papers. You're not the first."

"No?"

"There was a historian, oh, must be fifteen years back now. And before him a genealogist. They both came asking after the founding documents." He smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "The county's records for Hollowmere are... incomplete. You'll find we're honest about that, at least."

He led her back through the stacks, past folio boxes labeled in fading copperplate, to a reading table beneath the tallest window. The light there was good—north-facing, even, kind to old ink. He set a grey archival box on the table with both hands, the way you'd set down something sleeping.

"Hollowmere parish, 1640 through 1800," he said. "Manorial records, tithes, the enclosure awards. The founding deed itself would have been under 'Land Grants, Hollowmere.' I'll warn you now—" and here he paused, and Odile watched him decide, consciously, to finish the sentence, "—you may find that particular folder lighter than expected."

"Lighter how?"

"Torn out," Mr. Pell said simply. "Clean away. Whoever did it knew what they were doing. Cut close to the spine, no ragged edges. That's not vandalism. That's surgery."

Odile opened the box.

The documents inside were beautiful in the way only very old paperwork is beautiful—vellum gone the color of weak tea, iron-gall ink faded to the brown of dried blood, wax seals cracked like frost on a puddle. She worked slowly, the way she'd been trained, turning each leaf with clean dry hands, photographing what mattered, noting reference numbers in pencil. Sheep counts. Boundary disputes. A petition about a collapsed well. The ordinary sediment of a village's life, layer upon layer, and threaded all through it, the name Vasquez—Vasquez as landholder, Vasquez as justice of the peace, Vasquez as churchwarden, Vasquez signing, always signing, in a hand that grew more confident down the centuries.

And then, near the bottom of the box, the gap.

She felt it before she saw it—the folder for land grants was visibly thinner than its neighbors, its contents shifted toward the front like furniture rearranged around an absence. She lifted the remaining leaves out one by one. A surveyor's memorandum. A copy of a copy of a boundary agreement. And then nothing, just the empty back half of the folder, and along the inner margin where the missing deed had once been sewn in, the stubs of the binding threads, cut short.

But paper remembers. Paper is the most vindictive of materials; it holds impressions the way skin holds pressure. When Odile tilted the last surviving leaf against the window light, angling it just so, she saw them: the ghost-embossing of writing from the page that had once lain pressed against this one, transferred over centuries of damp and weight into the fibers themselves.

Not enough to read. Enough to know the missing page had been written on heavily, densely, top to bottom—and that it had contained, repeated at intervals down its length, a word or phrase of four or five letters, appearing perhaps twenty times.

A list, she thought. Something written over and over. Names?

She photographed it anyway, knowing the camera wouldn't catch what her eyes barely could. Then she turned to the rest of the box, because scholars are scavengers, and scavengers know that when the meal is stolen, the crumbs remain.

It was in the tithe records that she found it. Not the tithe rolls themselves—those were present, mundane, grain and lambswool and shillings—but in the margins of a lease renewal from 1712, where some clerk with a fine italic hand had copied out the terms of an older obligation for reference. Most of the marginal note concerned rent and rights of common. But at the bottom, cramped into the last inch of space as if the writer had hoped the words would be too small to be read by anyone who came after:

*"...and the said yearly observance to continue without fail, viz. the tithe of names, renewed each August, as by covenant sworn and entered in the Great Deed (since withdrawn)."*

*Since withdrawn.* As if the deed had merely stepped out of the room.

Odile sat very still. Outside the tall window, pigeons crossed the pale sky. The radiator ticked. Somewhere in the building a door closed with a sound like a held breath released.

*A tithe of names.* Every tenth thing belonged to God—that was the old arithmetic of tithes, the tenth lamb, the tenth sheaf. But this wasn't a tenth. This was renewed *each August*, whole and entire, a debt that never diminished because it was never meant to be paid off. A covenant. And covenants, in the old legal language, required two parties, both named.

She thought of the lullaby Mrs. Aldery had sung for her once and never twice. *The second time invites.* She thought of the knocking beneath her floorboards, patient as a metronome. She thought of a door sealed from the inside, buried under a collapsed wing, and a village that went quiet in August the way a household goes quiet around a sickbed.

You don't seal something in, she thought, unless you're afraid it will answer if you call. And you don't bind it with a song split into pieces unless—

Unless the song is the contract. Sung aloud, witnessed, renewed. A verbal deed. And every contract has a signature line.

"Found what you were looking for?" Mr. Pell stood at the end of the table. She hadn't heard him come back. His face was carefully neutral, but his eyes went to the open folder, to the margin, and something moved behind them—recognition, and beneath the recognition, a tired old fear, the look of a man seeing weather he'd been promised.

"I found a reference to a document that isn't here," Odile said. "The founding deed. Do you know when it was removed? There should be a withdrawal record—a ticket, a stamp, something."

"Oh, there'd be a record," Mr. Pell agreed. "If it had been done properly." He came closer, looked down at the marginal note without touching the page, and read it the way you read something you've read before. "There isn't one. I've checked. I checked fifteen years ago, when the historian asked, and thirty years before that when my predecessor was asked the same question by the genealogist. No withdrawal ticket. No stamp. The deed was cut out sometime between 1911 and 1934—we can date it by the rebinding—and nobody in this office will ever tell you who did it, because nobody knows, and the ones who might have known took the answer with them."

"Then whoever took it—"

"Was from Hollowmere." He said it flatly, not unkindly. "Or was paid by someone from Hollowmere. Or was frightened by someone from Hollowmere. Miss Vasquez—" and here he paused again, and she realized he was deciding whether to say her name at all, and what it cost him to say it. "Miss Vasquez. You have the same surname as the family in these papers. Coincidence, I expect. Folklorists collect odd surnames. But I'll say this once, and then I'll forget I said it, and you'd be wise to do the same. In fifty years at this trade, my predecessor taught me one rule about Hollowmere. We don't lose documents. Documents leave. And they only ever leave going one direction."

"Which direction is that?"

"Downhill," said Mr. Pell. "Toward the house."

* * *

She stayed another three hours. It was stubbornness, mostly, and partly the scholar's superstition that the archive always keeps one more thing than it admits to. She went through the churchwardens' accounts and found, in August entries across four separate decades, identical small payments: *for salt, for the parish, 2s. 6d.* Salt bought in bulk, every year, in the month when the counting came. She found a burial register with a page torn out—not cut, torn, hurriedly, the stub still rough—and the register resuming the following spring with no explanation of the gap. She found, in a bundle of correspondence from the 1880s, a single letter from a Vasquez to the county surveyor declining, in terms of glacial politeness, to permit any inspection of the estate's cellars, on grounds of "structural danger to visitors, which we are unwilling to see visited upon strangers."

*Which we are unwilling to see visited upon strangers.*

By closing time the light through the tall windows had gone the color of pewter, and the green lamps made little islands on the tables, and Mr. Pell stood waiting by the return shelf with his hands folded, watching her box the documents with the tenderness she'd learned from him.

"You'll want copies," he said. "I can arrange that. Standard fee."

"Mr. Pell. The marginal note. Will you photograph it officially? Get it into the catalogue? If the deed's gone, that note is the only surviving evidence of the covenant's terms—"

"No."

It came fast, faster than anything he'd said all day, and she saw that he'd known she would ask, had perhaps been dreading it since she signed the visitors' book.

"It's not catalogued, it won't be catalogued, and I'd take it kindly if your own notes didn't cite this office." He took off his glasses and polished them on his sleeve, a long unnecessary business. "You think I'm being obstructive. I'm being *kind*. Fifteen years ago the historian published. A footnote, six lines, in a journal nobody reads. Cited this office by name. Within a month he'd written to me asking whether we'd had any trouble—break-ins, noises, letters. He'd started hearing counting. Under the floor of his study, he said. In a town two hundred miles from Hollowmere." Mr. Pell put his glasses back on. "He stopped publishing. The counting stopped. Draw whatever conclusion you like. I draw mine."

"That's not—"

"Scholarship, Miss Vasquez, is a kind of naming." He said it gently, almost apologetically, the way you'd explain gravity to a child who wanted to fly. "You write a thing down, you give it an address. You publish it, you give it a road. This office has spent three hundred years making sure that particular document has neither." He walked her to the door, unlocked it against the closing time, and stood holding it while the evening air came in, cool and smelling of rain on stone. "Copy what you need. Take it home. But understand what you're carrying. You're not taking away information. You're taking away a debt record. And debts," he said, "have a way of finding their readers."

* * *

The bus back west left at seven, and Odile took it, and watched the country fold itself into dusk around her—hedgerows going black, farm lights kindling one by one like slow thoughts. She had her notebook on her knee and the photocopied pages in a folder, and she should have been transcribing, organizing, doing the work. Instead she sat with Mr. Pell's sentence turning over in her mind like a stone in a stream.

*Scholarship is a kind of naming.*

Three years she had told herself she was a recorder, a witness, a neutral instrument. Tape and notebook and careful citation. The songs no one would sing twice—she had preserved them, hadn't she? Given them permanence, given them an audience beyond the singers who refused to repeat them. That was the work. That was the good work.

But preservation was repetition. Her recordings were second singings, archived and indexed, playable infinitely. *The second time invites.* What had she been inviting, all this while, with her patient microphone and her footnotes? And the deed—cut out, carried downhill, hidden where the thing it bound could keep an eye on its own leash. And she was riding west toward Hollowmere with photocopies of the covenant in her bag, and tomorrow she would transcribe them, and file them, and cross-reference them, and build the thing scholars build: a complete, legible, permanent record.

An address. A road.

The bus climbed the last hill and Hollowmere appeared below in its bowl of dark fields, a scatter of lit windows, the church spire leaning its slight attentive lean. From this distance the village looked peaceful, gathered, warm. She knew better. She knew about the salt on her windowsill and the pub that emptied and the evenings when the knocking came up through her floorboards, regular as breathing, patient as interest accruing.

That night, in her rented room, she spread the photocopies on the bed and began to transcribe the marginal note into her notebook in her own hand, and stopped halfway through, pen lifted, because she understood suddenly what she was doing. She was copying the covenant out. Word for word. Making her own copy of the deed the archive wouldn't keep.

Every deed needs a signature.

Her pen hovered over the page. Below the transcribed clause, in the space where a clerk would enter the parties bound, she wrote—because the scholar in her demanded completeness, because the record must be accurate—*Covenant referenced: Vasquez estate, Hollowmere. Terms: tithe of names, renewed each August. Surviving evidence: this note.*

And then, because the form of it demanded it, because three years of training had taught her that a document without attribution is worthless, she signed and dated it, in her neat archival hand:

*Recorded by O. Vasquez.*

She looked at her own name on the page for a long time. The lamp hummed. Beneath the floorboards, faint and punctual, the knocking began—seven strokes, a pause, seven strokes—as though something below had heard the pen scratch and was acknowledging receipt.

In the morning she tried to call Mr. Pell to ask about the burial register gap, the torn page, the dates. The office number rang and rang to a dead line tone. She called the main county switchboard. The records office, the operator said, was closed for inventory. She called again that afternoon. Closed. She called a third time at four o'clock, and a young voice answered, polite and baffled: no, there was no Mr. Pell on staff, no, there hadn't been for years, was she sure she had the right office?

Odile stood in the phone booth on the village road with the receiver warm against her ear and the photocopies heavy in her bag, and understood that the archive had done what archives do best.

It had kept what it wanted, and let the rest go.

And downhill, in the dark under the collapsed east wing, something had just learned her name.
