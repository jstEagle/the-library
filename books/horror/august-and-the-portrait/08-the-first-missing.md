## 8. The First Missing

The man's name was Declan Ferro, and for three days no one in Hollowmere said it aloud.

Odile learned it the way she learned everything in that village—sideways, by indirection, from a woman at the bakery who spoke of "the poor fellow from the crew" while her hands kept working the dough as though kneading were a form of prayer. The name itself arrived later, folded inside a police report Odile read over the shoulder of a constable who pretended not to notice her reading it. *Declan Ferro, 44, of Marlowe Green. Last seen Tuesday evening, leaving the site.*

Last seen. As if seeing were something you could lose your grip on.

She had met him once, the week before. He'd been leaning against his truck in the grey light before the shift started, drinking tea from a thermos lid, and he'd nodded at her across the fence line with the easy friendliness of an outsider—the only person in Hollowmere besides herself who didn't belong to its silence. "You're the one doing the song recordings," he'd said. "My mum used to hum things she wouldn't explain. Said they were just for keeping." He'd laughed. "Funny what people keep."

*Just for keeping.* She had written the phrase down that night in her field journal, underlined twice, and now the ink seemed to have darkened on the page, as though the words had continued drying long after she'd closed the book.

His truck was still at the site when they found him missing—parked neatly where he'd left it, engine cold, doors locked from the inside. That detail traveled through the village like a current through water, touching everyone, surfacing nowhere. Locked from the inside. Odile heard it four times in two days, always in the same hushed register, always with the same small pause before *inside*, and never once followed by speculation. In the city, that pause would have filled with theories within the hour. Here it simply stayed open, a held breath the whole village was keeping together.

The doors were locked from the inside, and there was no one inside to lock them.

* * *

She went out to the site on the third morning, walking the lane past the collapsed east wing with her recorder in her bag though she had no intention of using it. Some instinct told her that turning it on here would be a transgression she couldn't name, the way you don't photograph certain funerals.

The police tape sagged between the fence posts, already weathered, already half-forgotten. Beyond it the excavation sat open to the sky like a wound someone had decided not to dress. The stone door was still there at the bottom of the trench, bolted from its buried side, and someone had thrown a tarp over it—not covering it, exactly. More like averting their eyes. The tarp had slipped at one corner, and beneath it Odile could see the edge of the stone, pale as a tooth.

Declan Ferro's truck stood forty meters off, dew on the windshield, a high-visibility vest folded on the passenger seat.

She stood at the fence a long time. The morning was warm for late August, but the air coming up out of the trench moved against the wind, she noticed. It came up slow and steady, like breathing.

"You shouldn't be here."

The foreman. Rourke, the one who'd joked about bad air. He looked worse than the last time she'd seen him—grey around the mouth, eyes rimmed red in a way that suggested not crying but the refusal of it, grief dammed up behind the face until it backed into the blood.

"Neither should any of you," Odile said. "But you're all still working."

"We're not." He said it flatly, without heat. "Crew walked off yesterday. All of them. Union's calling it a hazard dispute." He looked at the tarp the way a man looks at a dog that has bitten someone. "It's not a hazard dispute."

"Then what is it?"

Rourke was quiet for so long she thought he wouldn't answer. When he did, his voice had dropped into the register she'd come to think of as the Hollowmere voice—the one below whisper, the one people used after dusk.

"Mr. Ferro asked me a question Tuesday night. Walking back to the trucks. He said—" The foreman's jaw worked. "He said, *what's the counting for?* He'd heard it. Three nights running, he said. Under the ground. Like someone tallying." Rourke's hands were shaking, and he shoved them into his pockets to hide it, which hid nothing. "I told him I didn't know what he meant. And that was true, Mr.—that was true the way most things are true here. I knew exactly what he meant. I just don't know what it's *for*. Nobody knows what it's for. We only know you don't ask."

"He asked."

"He asked." Rourke nodded slowly. "And now he's gone, and his doors are locked from the inside, and there's no search party because—" He stopped himself. Looked past her, down the empty lane, at the village below with its chimneys standing straight and its windows shuttered against a bright afternoon. "Because nobody here will say his name. And I've watched enough to know that's not callousness. They're frightened of something. And whatever it is, it listens better than any police force ever will."

He turned and walked back toward the site office, and Odile stood alone at the fence line with the wind at her back and the breath of the earth in her face, and understood that she had just been told more truth than anyone in Hollowmere had given her in three years—and that it had cost a stranger everything to buy it.

* * *

There was no search.

She kept waiting for one the way you wait for thunder after lightning. Posters, volunteers, dogs, a cordon, a press conference—any of the machinery a town produces when one of its own goes into the ground unaccounted for. Instead there was nothing. The constable filed his report. The crew left. The bakery woman put out a plate of the spiced buns Declan Ferro had bought every morning, set it in the window facing the road, and took it in each night untouched, and this—Odile realized with a chill that started in her teeth—this was the search. This was the memorial. A plate of buns and a closed mouth.

On the fourth day she cornered Mrs. Aldery, her landlady, in the kitchen, and asked her directly: why is no one looking for him?

Mrs. Aldery was salting the windowsill again. She did it every evening now, had done it since the excavation began, laying down a white seam along the wood with the unhurried precision of ritual. She didn't stop to answer.

"Because looking is calling," she said finally. "And calling is naming. And naming—" She smoothed the salt with one fingertip, an absolution. "You record songs, Miss Vasquez. You know what a name does in a song. It calls a thing present. It makes it stand up in the room." She wiped her hands on her apron. "That poor man is lost. But he isn't lost the way a child loses a glove. And if we go shouting his name across the fields and down the old wells and into the dark places, we aren't searching. We're *inviting*. Do you understand me?"

"No," Odile said honestly. "I don't."

Mrs. Aldery looked at her then, really looked, and there was something in her face Odile hadn't seen before—not the polite village blankness but something rawer, older, almost tender. Pity, maybe. Or recognition.

"No," she agreed softly. "Not yet. But you're closer than you were, and that frightens me more than I can say politely, so I'll say it impolitely: stop getting closer."

* * *

The vigil happened anyway, because grief is a pressure and even Hollowmere could not contain all of it.

She heard about it the way she heard about everything—a scrap, a murmur, a time and no place, which meant the place was understood: the churchyard, after dark, the night before the month turned. No notices. No announcement. Just knowledge moving house to house the way the knocking moved, quiet and thorough.

Odile went. She told herself it was fieldwork, and the lie tasted familiar in her mouth; three years of telling herself the counting was pipes had made lying to herself nearly effortless, nearly native. She brought no recorder. Whatever happened tonight would happen unwitnessed except by her memory, and she suspected that too was part of the design—that some things survived only in flesh, passed mouth to mouth, so that no machine could ever hold them and play them back into the world.

The churchyard was full.

That was the first shock: how many came. Forty-some figures standing among the leaning stones in the blue dark, no candles—she noticed that immediately, no candles, no lanterns, nothing that made light—and dressed in ordinary clothes, as though they had simply wandered out of their houses and forgotten to go back in. She recognized shapes rather than faces: the baker woman, the constable, Rourke standing apart near the lych-gate with his arms crossed like a man holding himself shut. Near the yew tree, unmistakable even in shadow, Tomas Vasquez stood with his daughter's hand in his. Wren looked small and pale and awake in a way children should not be awake at that hour. When Odile drifted closer, she saw the girl's lips moving. Not talking. Counting. Silently. Her fingers twitching through the numbers against her father's palm.

Tomas felt Odile's presence and turned. In the dark his face was mostly absence, but his voice, when it came, was pitched below the whisper-line, below courtesy, in the register of confession.

"She's at thirty-four," he breathed. "Last night. Thirty-four."

"Thirty-four," Odile repeated, and the number sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. Forty souls in Hollowmere. She had counted them herself last spring for a census of tradition-bearers, a dry academic exercise that now read like a prophecy she'd typed up herself. Forty residents. Thirty-four and climbing.

"Not here," Tomas said, and it wasn't clear whether he meant the conversation or the vigil or the village entire. His hand tightened around his daughter's small fingers. "God help us, not here."

No one spoke above a whisper.

That was the second shock, and it endured: the whole assembly held to that sub-whisper register for the full hour, forty people mourning a man whose name none of them would say, and the silence they wrapped around their grief was not emptiness but structure—it had walls, a roof, a discipline, like the sealed door with its bolt on the far side. An old man began, very quietly, to hum. Two notes, three, a fragment of melody Odile knew from her recordings—one of the refused songs, the lullaby that invited—and a dozen voices hushed him at once, gently, the way you'd hush a sleeper talking in a dream, and the humming stopped, and the silence resumed, deeper than before.

Mourning is a kind of naming. She understood it now, standing among them, feeling the vigil's shape from the inside. To grieve loudly is to gather the dead close, to speak them into the room, to make them present at the table of the living. Every funeral she'd ever attended had been a summoning—loving, communal, safe. But here, in this soil, with this thing below counting the living each August, a summons was not a comfort. It was an address. You did not call the lost home when home had a hole in its floor.

So they mourned in the negative. They mourned by withholding. Forty people stood in a churchyard loving a stranger in absolute silence, and the silence was the largest sound Odile had ever heard.

Near the end—she couldn't have said what marked it, some shift in the quality of the dark—an elderly woman at the front began to sway, very slightly, and Odile realized with a start that it was the singer from her first recording, the lullaby woman, the one whose hands had shaken as she said *the second time invites*. The old woman's lips moved without sound. Her eyes were closed. And though no note escaped her, Odile found she could *hear* the song anyway, could feel its contour in the pressure of the night air, the way you feel a train through the soles of your feet before the rails sing.

The old woman was singing the second verse. Silently. To herself. Had perhaps been singing it silently every night for sixty years, keeping the other half of something alive in her body like a coal banked in ash.

Then her eyes opened and found Odile across the churchyard, unerring, as though the dark were glass to her, and she looked at Odile for a long, long moment—and nodded. Once. Slowly. An invitation, or a surrender, or a sentence being passed.

The vigil ended the way fog lifts: imperceptibly, and then everyone was simply walking home, alone or in silent pairs, folding back into their houses, sweeping the salt aside to let themselves in and laying it fresh behind them.

* * *

Odile did not go straight back to her room. She walked the lane past the site, past the sagging tape and the tarped door and the abandoned truck with its locked doors, and stopped at the fence, and stood in the dark listening.

At first: nothing. Wind in the yews. A dog somewhere, thinking better of barking.

Then, beneath her feet—faint, patient, perfectly rhythmic—the knocking. Not pipes. Not settling stone. It rose through the soles of her boots and up the bones of her legs like a pulse that wasn't hers, and she counted without wanting to, the way Wren counted, the way the whole village counted in its sleep.

Thirty-five.

Her breath went out of her. She gripped the fence rail. Thirty-five. One more than the vigil. One more than Wren's lips had shaped against her father's hand. Somewhere between the churchyard and this fence line, in the space of an hour, the count had grown by one—and Odile stood alone in the dark trying to make the arithmetic mean anything other than what it meant.

Below her feet, the knocking paused. Then, gently—almost kindly, the way Mrs. Aldery laid salt, the way the old woman had nodded across the graves—it knocked thirty-six times.

And stopped, as if satisfied. As if it had noticed her noticing, and wanted her to know it had.

Odile walked back to her room without running, because running felt like answering, and lay awake until dawn with her field journal open on her chest, on a page where she had written a single line:

*It isn't counting the living. It's counting down.*

In the morning she would pack her recorder, her notebooks, three years of refused verses, and take the bus to the county records office. Because somewhere in the torn paper trail of the founding family there was a reason for all of this—a tithe, a term, a clause—and she was done pretending she didn't need to read it.
