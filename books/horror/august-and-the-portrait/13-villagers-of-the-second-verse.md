## 13. Villagers of the Second Verse

The knocking had reached the church.

Odile noticed it first as a wrongness in the silence between hymn practice and evening prayer—a rhythm under the floorboards, patient as a metronome, counting something she could not see. Old Mrs. Aldous, dusting the lectern, did not look down. The vicar, locking the vestry, turned his key with exaggerated care, as if noise itself might be an invitation. Nobody said *it's in the church now*. Nobody had to. Hollowmere had stopped pretending the knocking was pipes three weeks ago; what remained was a village-wide agreement to behave as though silence were still possible, still theirs to keep.

But Odile had come to the church for a different reason. She came because of a list.

Three years of fieldwork had given her names: eleven singers, scattered through Hollowmere and its outlying farms, each of whom possessed a fragment of song they would perform exactly once and never again. She had catalogued them the way you catalogue anything you're afraid of—neatly, at arm's length, in a notebook whose margins had slowly filled with her own increasingly frantic annotations. And over those three years she had begun, without admitting it to herself, to map them. The lullaby Mrs. Tregowan wouldn't repeat. The harvest-round from the Hollis brothers that broke off mid-phrase, both men going pale in unison. The keening tune old Sorrel hummed while shelling peas, four bars only, her hands never stopping, her eyes never lifting.

Eleven fragments. Eleven singers. And every one of them, when pressed, gave the same answer in the same flat voice: *I don't know where I learned it. I don't know how it ends.*

Tonight she was going to stop being polite about it.

* * *

She found Mrs. Tregowan first, because Mrs. Tregowan was the oldest and the oldest were always the least able to keep running. Her cottage sat at the end of Latchet Lane, behind a wall of dry-stone so low it seemed apologetic, its garden a riot of late-summer herbs gone leggy and wild. Rosemary, Odile noted. Mugwort. Vervain. A border planted by someone who remembered why such borders were planted.

The door opened before she knocked.

"I thought you'd come sooner," Mrs. Tregowan said. She was small and dry as a wren's bone, her white hair pinned in a knot so tight it looked structural. Her eyes were the pale washed-out blue of very old china. "Come in, then. Mind the cat."

The cottage smelled of woodsmoke and dried lavender and, beneath both, faintly, of salt. There were saucers of it on every sill, and Odile felt something in her chest loosen slightly—the landlady's silent ritual, then, was not one woman's eccentricity but a grammar the whole village spoke.

Mrs. Tregowan poured tea without asking and set the cup before Odile with a click that sounded final, like a bolt sliding home.

"You've been collecting," the old woman said. "Three years. You think we didn't notice? A girl with a machine, asking us to sing things twice." She wrapped her own hands around her cup but did not drink. "We noticed, Miss Vasquez. We just hoped you'd get bored."

"I'm not bored."

"No." Something almost like approval moved across the seamed face. "You're not. That's the trouble with your sort. The curious ones. The ones who can't leave a lock alone once they've found it." She looked up, and her eyes were suddenly terribly clear. "You've been to the records office."

It wasn't a question. Odile set down her cup. "The deed is torn out. But there was a marginal note. *The tithe of names, renewed each August.*"

"Margaret Ashcombe wrote that," Mrs. Tregowan said. "1791. She was the last one who tried to write it all down properly, and look what happened to her page." She drank at last, grimacing, as if the tea had gone bitter in the interval. "Paper remembers too well. That's why we sing instead. A song can be broken into pieces. Paper can't."

"Then tell me about the pieces."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire settling, and beneath it—faint, muffled, as though rising through the garden soil itself—that soft irregular knock. Knock. Knock-knock. Mrs. Tregowan's eyes flicked toward the floor and back, and Odile saw her decide something, saw the decision cost her.

"You have the fragments," the old woman said. "All of them?"

"I believe so. Eleven."

"Twelve." Mrs. Tregowan closed her eyes. "There are twelve. Mine is the last one. The ending." She opened her eyes again, and they were wet, which frightened Odile more than any of the knocking ever had. "And no, girl, I will not sing it for you tonight. Not yet. First you'll understand what you're asking for, and you'll ask anyway, or you won't. But you'll do it knowing."

* * *

What Mrs. Tregowan told her took until nearly midnight, and Odile filled nine pages of her notebook, her handwriting deteriorating from careful fieldwork script to something jagged and urgent.

The founding family had not built the seal alone. That was the first thing. The Vasquezes had bound the thing below, yes—but a binding sung by one throat belongs to one throat, and one throat can be cut, or bought, or simply die. So the founders had done something cleverer and crueler. They had taken the binding-song apart like a garment picked off a scarecrow, and given a piece to every household in the new village. A verse here. A phrase there. The Hollis line kept the round; the Tregowans kept the ending; the vicarage kept, of all things, a descending scale that everyone assumed was just a psalm tone. Twelve households, twelve pieces, and a law laid down beside them: *never sing it whole, never teach it whole, never let any one soul hold more than their share.*

"Why?" Odile asked, though she already knew, and hated that she knew.

"Because of what happens when it's whole." Mrs. Tregowan's hands were folded now, knuckles like river stones. "A binding needs an anchor. Something living, freely given, woven into the last line. That's the price of the craft—it always was. The founders knew that whatever they sang down there would need a name inside it to hold fast. So they split the song so that no name would ever be in it. Each piece alone is harmless. Pretty, even. A lullaby. A harvest round. Sing them separately, once a year, in different houses, and the seal holds on borrowed breath—no anchor required, because no one has offered one." Her mouth twisted. "And if anyone ever does sing it whole—if some fool assembles it and gives it a voice from beginning to end—then that voice becomes the anchor. That singer goes into the count. Willingly. Permanently. In place of everyone else."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard ticked, and both women ignored it with the practiced grace of people who had spent lifetimes ignoring things.

"That's why you won't sing twice," Odile said slowly. "It's not superstition. Repetition is assembly. If two people's fragments overlap—if someone hears enough of the pieces—"

"Then the song starts putting itself together in their head, whether they mean it or not." Mrs. Tregowan nodded. "Songs want to be whole, girl. It's their nature. Every time I sang you my verse, I fed the thing in your memory a little more. Why d'you think we all refused you? Not rudeness. Arithmetic." She leaned forward. "How much of it do you have in your head right now? Honestly."

Odile looked down at her nine pages. At the melodies transcribed in her neat notation, the phrases cross-referenced, the overlaps marked. Three years of work. Three years of quietly, professionally, assembling a weapon she hadn't known was a weapon.

"All of it except yours," she said. "I could sing it now. I've caught myself humming it. I didn't know what it was."

"I know you didn't." For the first time the old woman's voice softened. "That's how it takes the curious ones. It doesn't chase. It waits to be finished." She rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and went to the dresser, and from behind a row of chipped teapots she took a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with string gone grey with age.

"My grandmother taught me my piece when I was seven," Mrs. Tregowan said, untying the knot with fingers that trembled—not, Odile understood now, from age alone. "She made me swear I'd never give it to anyone whole-hearted. Never to anyone who'd use it. And I've kept that oath sixty years, and I have hated keeping it, because oaths like that are how the thing below stays fed and the village stays quiet and everyone gets to pretend they're innocent." She laid the oilcloth bundle on the table between them but did not open it. Inside, Odile could see the shape of paper. Old paper. "My granddaughter left in March. My son's boy won't come back. I'm the last Tregowan, and when I die the ending dies with me, and then the seal fails anyway, and everything I protected by cowardice comes up through the floor regardless."

"So why not just let it fail?"

"Because there's a difference," Mrs. Tregowan said fiercely, "between losing and surrendering. Listen to me now, Miss Vasquez, and listen well, because I'll say this once." She gripped Odile's wrist, and her grip was stronger than her frame suggested, strong as a root. "If you take my verse and sing the cycle whole—and you will, I can see it in your face, you've already decided, you decided the day you heard the knocking and stayed anyway—then you will be the anchor. Not Tomas Vasquez. Not the child. You. Your name goes into the last line and your voice goes into the count and there is no undoing it, no clever third option, no trick. Do you understand what I'm offering you? I'm offering you the rope and the knot both."

Odile's throat was tight. Outside, the knocking had stopped, and somehow that was worse.

"And you'll teach me anyway," she said. It wasn't a question either.

Mrs. Tregowan studied her for a long moment—the pale eyes moving over her face as if reading a text written there long ago. Then she began, slowly, to unwrap the oilcloth.

"Fury and gratitude," the old woman said. "That's the proper order of feeling for this. Fury first, at us, at all of us—for splitting it, for hiding it, for letting generations of strangers carry pieces of a curse like it was a casserole for a fete. Gratitude second, if you've got any left, for the ones who tried." The paper inside was vellum, brown as autumn, covered in a hand like bird tracks. "My grandmother wrote hers down against the day. Said a song should be allowed to be finished by someone who chooses it, not just lost by everyone who feared it." She smoothed the vellum flat with both palms. "So. Fury and gratitude. Have you got both?"

"Yes," Odile said, and found it was true. She was furious—at the polite silences, the salt left without explanation, the eleven singers who had watched her for three years and said nothing while the song assembled itself in her skull like a splinter working toward the heart. And underneath the fury, inextricable from it, a fierce terrible thankfulness for this dry fierce woman unwrapping sixty years of fear on a scrubbed kitchen table.

"Good." Mrs. Tregowan pulled her chair around, shoulder to shoulder with Odile's, close enough that their arms touched. "Then we begin. And we begin with breathing, because the last verse is sung on one breath, and you'll need all of yours."

* * *

They worked until the candle guttered. The melody was simple, which made it monstrous—seven notes, descending, the kind of tune a child could carry, the kind that lodges behind the sternum and hums itself back at 3 a.m. The words were older than the village, older than English perhaps, and Mrs. Tregowan translated them line by line in a voice gone rough:

*A door shut from within is a promise kept.*
*A name given freely is a lamp left lit.*
*Count what remains, and count it kindly.*
*Count what remains, and let it sleep.*

"The last line changes," Mrs. Tregowan said quietly. "Every anchor makes it their own. That's the giving. You don't just sing your name—you make the ending yours, so the thing below knows it holds a person, not a placeholder." She looked at Odile sideways. "You'll want time to choose your words. Don't rush it. The song knows a lie."

Odile copied the verse into her notebook, then, after a moment, tore the page out and burned it in the grate, watching the notation curl and blacken. Some things should live in only one head at a time.

When she stepped out into the lane near two in the morning, the air smelled of turned earth and, very faintly, of old hymns. Down the hill, the Vasquez estate stood black against the stars, and she could see the excavation site's single floodlight burning over the open door like a votive nobody had the courage to extinguish.

Behind her, from the cottage, Mrs. Tregowan's voice floated after her—not the binding verse, but something lighter, almost tender, a blessing or a farewell:

"Practice your breathing, girl. August ends Friday."

Friday. Two days. Odile stood in the empty lane with her heart hammering out a rhythm she refused to count along to, and began, deliberately, to breathe.
