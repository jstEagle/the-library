## 14. Salt on Every Sill

The salt arrived on a Tuesday, in the back of Harold Petch's flatbed, fifty-kilo sacks of it, coarse and grey as crushed ice. Tomas watched from the study window of the estate house while the men of Hollowmere carried it door to door in their arms like something newborn, and he understood that whatever Odile had learned in the county records had already traveled through the village by its own routes—faster than any phone call, faster than speech, the way bad news moves through water.

By evening every sill in Hollowmere wore a white line. His own included. Someone had come to the estate house while he was at the dig site filling in what could be filled in, and laid a careful unbroken ridge along each windowsill, tucking the corners with a precision that felt less like superstition than like masonry. Wren stood at her bedroom window looking down at it.

"Who put this here?"

"A neighbor," Tomas said.

"It's not for keeping things out," Wren said. She was ten and said things like this now, flatly, the way she reported the weather. "It's for keeping things *in*. That's what she told me."

He didn't ask who *she* was. He was saving that conversation for a night when he had more courage than he owned, which meant, practically speaking, never.

* * *

The silence came next. It was announced without being announced—no notice in the post office window, no word from the parish council—but by Thursday it was law. After dusk, no one spoke above a whisper. No one sang. Radios went dark; televisions were turned to mute behind drawn curtains. The pub closed at sundown for the first time in its history, and Harold Petch, who had missed perhaps forty evenings there in sixty years, locked its doors at half past eight and walked home whistling until he remembered himself and stopped mid-note, the tune dying in his throat like a bird hitting glass.

Tomas learned the rules by breaking them. On the first night he called out to Wren up the stairs—"Wren, bed!"—and heard, in the pause that followed, the whole street listening. Mrs. Aldous next door opened her front door and looked at him across the dark with an expression he would think about for weeks: not anger. Pity. The look you give a man who has spoken in a library, or laughed at a funeral.

After that he whispered too. He whispered to his daughter goodnight, and she whispered back, "Thirty-six tomorrow, Daddy," and he lay awake doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. Thirty-six. He had counted the village himself, twice, walking the lanes with a notebook like a census-taker: thirty-nine souls, counting him, counting Wren, counting the folklorist who had begun leaving her tapes on his kitchen table with annotations in pencil, as if she were already distributing an estate.

The knocking came to his house that same night. Not below—not under the floors where it had always lived, patient as plumbing—but at the walls. Three sides of the house, moving clockwise, unhurried. *Knock. Knock. Knock.* A rhythm like someone learning a language by tapping it out.

Wren slept through it. Tomas sat on the edge of his bed with the salt line glowing faintly white in the moonlight and understood, with the clarity that comes only at three in the morning, that the thing was not trying to get in. It was taking attendance.

* * *

It moved house to house through the following week, and the village tracked its progress the way coastal towns track weather. The knockings visited the Aldouses' on Monday, the Petch cottage Tuesday, the farmhouses up the lane Wednesday and Thursday, and always the pattern was the same: first beneath the floors, then the walls, then—this was new—the doors themselves, a slow polite rapping at the latch, as though a caller had come all this way and only wanted asking in.

And it was learning. That was the word Odile used, sitting at his kitchen table with her headphones around her neck, her tape recorder between them like a third guest. "The old accounts describe it as fixed. Bound to the ground under the estate. But listen." She played him a recording from her first autumn in Hollowmere: the knocking, distant, arrhythmic, almost random. Then one from June: steadier. Then July: measured, deliberate, four beats and a rest, four beats and a rest. "It's found the meter," she said. "It's been listening to us for three hundred years, and now it's practicing."

"Practicing what?"

She didn't answer, but she looked at the stack of tapes on the table—the song cycle, the halves of it, the refused second verses—and Tomas saw her answer in the way her hand rested on them, protective, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping child.

The iron came after the salt. Old horseshoes nailed over lintels, iron keys buried under thresholds, the blacksmith's great-granddaughter digging a family heirloom poker out of an attic and driving it into the garden soil like a stake. None of it worked, or none of it worked visibly, which in Hollowmere amounted to the same thing. The knocking passed through iron the way sound passes through glass. But the village kept adding defenses the way a drowning man keeps swallowing, because to stop trying was to admit what the trying was for.

* * *

The child went missing on the last Friday of the second week of August.

His name was Iestyn Pryce, seven years old, red-haired, the son of the couple who ran the campsite down by the stream. He had been seen at four o'clock riding his bicycle past the church. At six his bike was found standing upright in the lane outside the Vasquez east gate, kickstand down, handlebars turned toward the estate, as neatly parked as if he'd be right back.

There was no search party. Tomas waited for one the way you wait for thunder after lightning, standing in the lane as the light failed, and it did not come. The Pryces' lights burned all night. Neighbors brought food and left it on the step without knocking—no one knocked anymore—and by morning the village had arranged itself around the absence like water around a stone. Mrs. Aldous took in the grandmother. Harold Petch closed the pub indefinitely. And when Odile asked, gently, terribly, at the vicarage gate, why no one was searching, the vicar's wife answered in a whisper:

"Because calling his name is how they lose them properly."

Tomas heard it secondhand and then heard it again firsthand, at the vigil, which was not called a vigil. Thirty-eight people stood in the churchyard at dusk, silent, not praying aloud, not singing, not weeping where anyone could hear, and the silence had a texture to it, a pressure, like the inside of a held breath. Wren held his hand and whispered, "Daddy, everyone's being very quiet so the lady can't learn their names."

"And yours?" he whispered back. "Has she learned yours?"

Wren looked up at him with her mother's grey eyes and said, "She's had mine since before I was born. That's what the book is."

* * *

The ledger. He thought about it constantly now. He had found it in the grandfather's study two weeks ago—generations of Vasquez names entered each August in a hand that deteriorated down the decades, the list shortening as the family thinned, and this year's page blank except for two entries inked in something that was not ink, WREN and TOMAS, letters like wet clay pressed by a thumb. He had burned nothing. He had dared not even tear the page, because tearing felt like answering, and answering felt like accepting terms.

But the vicar had records too. Everyone knew it. St. Mere's kept a parish register going back past the founding, and Odile had become convinced—I saw it in her, Tomas thought, the way conviction looks on a person who has run out of other explanations—that somewhere in those pages was the original tithe, the founding arithmetic, maybe even the whole song written out in some curate's careful hand before the villagers thought to split it and scatter it and refuse it.

On Sunday morning she went to the vicarage with her recorder and her notebook, and Tomas went with her, because he could not bear to let her go alone into any room in this village anymore.

Reverend Llewellyn met them on the porch. He was seventy-three and had held the living for forty years, and Tomas had always assumed his mildness was emptiness, the way you assume a still pond is shallow. He stood aside to let them in, poured tea nobody drank, and listened to Odile make her case in a low urgent voice—the deed, the marginal note, the tithe of names renewed each August, the count reaching thirty-seven—and then he rose, without hurry, and went to the vestry, and came back carrying the parish registers in both arms like an infant.

For one wild moment Tomas thought he was going to hand them over.

Instead the reverend carried them through to the back garden, to the incinerator the diocese had installed for confidential papers, and began, methodically, feeding the pages into it.

They were old pages. Vellum, some of them, soft as cloth. They caught slowly, reluctantly, and the reverend helped them along with a poker, and the smell was appalling—three centuries of baptisms and marriages and burials going up in a greasy black smoke that rose straight up in the windless air like a column marking the spot.

"What are you doing?" Odile's voice cracked on the last word. "Those are irreplaceable. Those are *the record*—"

"The record is the problem, Miss Vasquez." He did not turn around. His voice was steady, almost kind. "You think names are just words. You've spent three years collecting them—songs, singers, stories. A fine scholarly habit. But a name given freely is a door, and a name written down is a door with the key left in it. Every baptism I ever recorded, every marriage, every burial—I wrote these people into the world. Made them findable." He fed another gathering of pages to the flames. "I have spent forty years writing this parish down. I will not spend my last ones handing Him the index."

"Him," Tomas said. "You know what it is."

"I know what it isn't." The reverend finally turned. His face in the firelight was calm in a way that frightened Tomas more than terror would have. "It isn't a devil. Devils bargain. This is older than bargaining. It counts, and what it counts, it keeps, and the only mercy our founders found was to make itself hard to read—to split the song, seal the door from within, leave the ledger blank. Deniability, Miss Vasquez. Ignorance as architecture. And you—" he nodded at her recorder, at the bag of tapes, at the notebook full of transcribed verses, "—you have spent three years building the thing a map of everything we spent three hundred years refusing to know."

Odile had gone very pale. "The song can hold the seal. If it's completed—if someone sings it whole—"

"Then someone becomes the anchor." He said it flatly, without drama, and looked at her a long moment, and something passed over his face that might have been grief. "Yes. We know how it ends. We've always known. Why do you think no one would sing the second verse twice?"

The fire crackled. A scrap of vellum lifted on the heat, spiraled, and landed at Odile's feet—a corner of a page, a fragment of faded brown ink, a few words legible before the charred edge: *...and the ninth verse returns to the first, and the singer's own name closes the circle, freely given, and the door...*

The rest was ash.

The reverend looked down at it, and at her, and his calm finally broke—not into anger but into something exhausted and ancient. "Take your fragment," he said quietly. "God forgive me, take it. But understand what you're holding, girl. You aren't reading a recipe. You're reading a resignation letter."

He went inside and shut the door, and the smoke rose over Hollowmere all afternoon, and that night—for the first time—the knocking did not stay below or at the walls or at the doors.

It came, softly, patiently, to the windows. All of them. House after house after house, working its way up the lane in the dark, four beats and a rest, four beats and a rest, and Tomas lay awake beside his sleeping daughter counting the houses between the church and theirs, doing the terrible arithmetic, knowing it would reach them before dawn—

—and it did, at the sill where the salt lay unbroken, a gentle rapping, almost tender, like a knuckle on a nursery door, and beneath it, so faint he might have dreamed it, a voice like wet clay learning to speak:

*"Thirty-eight."*

One short of the number of souls in Hollowmere. One short of Wren's count. And in the morning, when he went down the hill to tell Odile, he found her already awake, already packing her tapes, already writing in her notebook with a hand that did not shake, and pinned under the lamp on her table was the burnt vellum fragment and beneath it a fresh page on which she had written out, in her own careful hand, the first nine verses of the song—and after the ninth, a space.

A space exactly the length of one name.
