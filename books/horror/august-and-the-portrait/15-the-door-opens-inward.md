## 15. The Door Opens Inward

The knocking stopped at dawn on the twenty-ninth of August, and that was worse.

For three years Odile had catalogued the sounds of Hollowmere the way other scholars catalogue birdsong: the pipes that weren't pipes, the owls that kept no owl's hours, the patient arithmetic beneath her floorboards. She had learned its rhythms the way you learn a neighbor's footsteps—well enough to know when they falter. And this morning the house was silent in a way that felt less like peace than like held breath. The kettle's whistle seemed too loud. Her own pulse seemed an intrusion.

She was standing at the window with her tea going cold when she saw the men from the excavation crew walking up the hill toward the Vasquez estate—not trudging, as they had for weeks, but moving with the quick, loose stride of people summoned. None of them looked at one another. None of them spoke. They moved like iron filings drawn across a table by a magnet beneath it.

Odile set down the cup without drinking and followed.

* * *

The east wing of the estate had been collapsing for a century, a slow surrender of slate and timber into the hillside, so that now it resembled less a ruin than a wound half-healed over. The heritage crew's trenches cut through the green skin of it: surveyor's tape fluttering between stakes, a generator humming where it shouldn't have been needed, the smell of turned earth rising from the cuts like breath from a mouth.

They were all gathered at the trench nearest the old foundation line when she arrived, and none of them told her to leave. That alone told her everything. For three weeks the foreman—a broad, cheerful man named Aldous Peake who joked about bad air and older ghosts—had waved her back behind the fence with the easy authority of someone protecting his crew from liability. This morning he glanced at her, said nothing, and stepped aside to make room.

The door stood open.

Not broken. Not forced. *Open*—swung inward on hinges that three hundred years of burial should have fused solid, revealing a passage that sloped down into the hill's black interior. The stone door itself was thick as a church altar, banded with iron gone to orange lace, and Odile remembered the photographs she'd taken of it two weeks ago, when it had still been sealed: the bolt mechanism on the buried side, the impossible hardware, the way her own reflection in the image had stood with her mouth open mid-word, though she could not remember speaking.

Someone had guarded it. She knew that too; she'd heard Tomas arrange it through gritted teeth, two men from the village taking shifts in a caravan at the fence line, paid from what remained of the estate's accounts. She found them now at the edge of the group, sitting on an upturned bucket, sharing a thermos neither drank from. One of them—the younger, a Peake cousin with a boxer's flattened nose—was staring at his own hands.

"Tell me," Odile said quietly.

He didn't look up. "Nothing came out. That's what I keep saying to myself. Nothing *came out*. It opened inward." He said it twice more under his breath, like a man testing whether a floor would hold him. "It opened the wrong way."

The older guard took up the thread without being asked, in the flat voice of a man reciting evidence he wished he could burn. "Quarter past four. We both heard it. Not a creak—that door hadn't moved since before our grandfathers' grandfathers, it should have screamed like a banshee. It didn't. It moved like—" He searched for the word and settled on one that made the younger man flinch. "Like it was polite. Like something opening a door for a guest. We had the lamps on it inside ten seconds. Nothing there. Cold air coming up the slope, steady, like a draft off a cellar, except there's no cellar that deep and no tunnel we haven't dug through solid rock to find."

"Did you go in?"

"No," both men said together, and then the younger one added, with terrible precision: "Mr. Vasquez is down there now."

* * *

Tomas had brought a lantern, which struck Odile as either very brave or very foolish, and standing at the threshold watching him she could not decide which. He was perhaps eight meters along the passage, the light throwing his shadow huge and trembling against walls that swallowed illumination the way dry ground swallows rain. Wren was not with him—he'd left her with Mrs. Tallow at the vicarage, and Odile had seen him check the locks on the vicarage windows himself, twice—but he carried the girl's presence with him anyway, in the set of his shoulders, in the way he kept glancing back toward the daylight as if measuring how long it would take to reach if he ran.

"Tomas." She kept her voice level. Sound behaved strangely here; her word went out ahead of her and came back wrong, softened, as if the passage were listening politely and choosing not to repeat it.

He didn't turn. "You shouldn't be here."

"Neither should you."

"I'm the heir." He said it like a curse. "It's mine. All of it. The debts too." Then, because the dark seemed to demand honesty the daylight never got from him: "I dreamed about this door last night. I've never seen it before today and I dreamed about it. I dreamed I was on the *inside*, Odile, and someone was knocking, and I unbolted it because I thought—" His jaw worked. "Because I thought it was my grandfather. Because that's what you do. You answer a knock."

The cold air breathed past them both, up the slope, out into the August morning. It smelled of turned earth, of roots cut in springtime, and beneath that—she caught it again, the same note she'd caught in the first photograph, faint as memory—of old hymns. Of paper and candlewax and congregations long dissolved. It was the smell of a church closed for two hundred years that someone had nonetheless been singing in.

"Tomas," she said. "The walls."

He raised the lantern higher, and she saw why he hadn't turned around.

* * *

The tally marks began a meter inside the threshold and did not stop.

They covered every surface the lantern could reach: fine lines scratched into stone, thousands upon thousands of them, grouped in fives with the fifth stroke crossing the four, grouped in tens, in forties, in arrangements she recognized with a scholar's cold delight and a human being's rising nausea. Some were ancient, worn nearly smooth, their edges rounded by time or touch. Some were shallower, sharper. And some—

Some were wet.

The stone glistened with them, scores so fresh the displaced dust still hung in the grooves, and when Odile touched one—she could not afterward explain why she touched it, any more than she could explain the mouth open mid-word in her photograph—her fingertip came away damp and grey, smelling of grave-soil and, unmistakably, of salt. The marks were not old. The marks were not even finished. As she watched, in the lantern's unsteady light, she could have sworn the newest group gained a stroke—four lines becoming five—and she made herself stand very still and count her own heartbeat until the certainty passed, because certainty of that kind was a door of its own, and she had no intention of opening it.

"How many," Tomas said. His voice had gone somewhere flat and far away. "How many do you think."

Odile did the arithmetic she was built for, the arithmetic that had been her refuge for three years and had become, this August, the shape of her damnation. "If they're counts of forty—if each group is a full village—" She stopped. Started again. "There are more marks on these walls than there have been days since Hollowmere was founded. More than there have been *Augusts*."

"It's been counting longer than us," Tomas said. Not a question. "It's been counting since before there was anything to count."

"The founding deed was torn out of the county archive." She heard her own voice doing what it always did, retreating into citation, footnotes as a barricade. "But the margin note survived. *The tithe of names, renewed each August.* Renewed. Tomas—it doesn't say the tithe was paid once. It says renewed. Every year. For three centuries. And every year, someone stood down here and marked it off."

"Or something stood down here," Tomas said, "and marked *us* off."

The lantern guttered. Neither of them moved. In the recovered light the fresh marks shone wetly, and Odile saw now what she had refused to see a moment before: the newest groups were not arranged in fives. They were names—or the bones of names, tallies that bent and doubled in ways that suggested letters rather than numbers, and one of them, low on the wall near where the passage bent down into deeper dark, was small. Child-sized. Recent enough that a bead of moisture still clung to the final stroke like a tear that refused to fall.

WREN.

Tomas saw it at the same moment she did. What came out of him was not a shout; it was quieter and far more frightening—a sound like a beam settling, like something structural giving way. He lunged for the wall and then stopped himself an inch short, hand trembling in the air, because some animal wisdom in him understood that touching it might be answering it.

"She hasn't counted in two nights," he whispered. "Two nights, Odile. I thought—I thought it meant it was over. I thought we'd won something."

"Or it means it's done counting her," Odile said, and hated herself for the scholarship of it, the accuracy, the refusal to lie even now. "Or it means it knows her name well enough that it doesn't need to learn it anymore."

* * *

Above them, at the threshold, the daylight had begun to change.

It was not yet noon, but the light coming down the slope had thinned to the color of late October, and the crew had drawn back from the trench mouth in a silent crescent, and Aldous Peake—who had joked about bad air for three weeks—stood with his cap in his hands like a man at a graveside. Odile climbed up beside him and looked out over Hollowmere, laid out below in its shallow valley: the crooked lanes, the pub with its doors shut at midday, the church spire, the salt lines white on every sill like a village wearing mourning bands it couldn't speak of. Forty souls last spring. Thirty-nine now, and everyone pretending the missing man had simply driven off in his locked truck to start a new life, and no search ever mounted, because mourning was a kind of naming and naming was a kind of invitation.

From the valley rose no sound at all. No dogs, no tractor, no children. The whole village was holding the breath the house had held that morning, and Odile understood, standing between the buried door and the silent town, that the boundary they had all lived behind—the boundary between above and below, between the counted and the counting—had not been breached this morning.

It had been *abolished*. Formally, with ceremony, the way borders are abolished: not with violence but with paperwork, with a door swinging open on oiled hinges after three hundred years, with a bolt drawn back from the inside by something that had decided, at last, that the wall between them was a formality it no longer wished to observe. The cold air breathed steadily up out of the earth and washed over the grass and the fences and the salt-silled windows of Hollowmere, and everywhere it passed, Odile saw the same thing happen: curtains twitching shut, one by one, street by street, like eyes closing against a light that wasn't light.

"We can't fill it in," Peake said beside her. It wasn't a question or a proposal; it was a surrender. "We talked about it. Concrete, steel, the lot. Danny wanted to." He nodded at the younger guard, still sitting on his bucket below. "Then Danny stood watch last night, and this morning he wouldn't say why, but he told me filling it in would be like—" Peake's throat moved. "Like putting your hand over a mouth while it's telling you something. It doesn't stop the talking. It just makes it angry."

Behind them, down in the passage, Tomas's lantern moved slowly along the tally-marked walls, a small gold fish swimming in black water, and Odile thought of the ledger she had not yet seen but knew existed—knew with the certainty of the converted—generations of Vasquez names entered each August in ink and duty. The door had opened inward. The bolt was on the inside. Everything about this place, every lock and law and lullaby, had always been designed by the living to hold something *in*—and every one of those designs had been built backwards, and they had all known it, and they had sung in halves for three hundred years rather than say it aloud.

The song cycle lay in fragments across her desk in the rented room: thirty-one verses recorded, refused, repeated only once, scattered among singers who would not sing them whole. Tonight she would spread them out and do the work she had been avoiding since the first shaking hands in a kitchen three years ago. Tonight she would assemble it.

Down in the dark, faint as a pulse, the walls gave off their smell of turned earth and old hymns, and Odile realized the hymns were not old at all.

Somewhere beneath her feet, patiently, in a voice made of everyone it had taken, something was practicing the descant.
