## 21. Inheritance, Revised

The letter from the estate agents arrived on a Tuesday, thin as a hymnal page, and Tomas read it standing in the kitchen with Wren's porridge going cold behind him.

*We note your instruction to withdraw the property from the market,* it said, in the flat courtesy of people who had never heard anything knock. *May we ask the reason? There is a buyer prepared to meet your asking price.*

He thought about what he might write back. *Because the ground under this house is not mine to sell.* Because he had spent thirty years running from Hollowmere and one August learning that you cannot sell what was never yours — that the estate had been holding him all along, the way a jaw holds a tooth, and the only honest thing left to do was stand still and be useful.

He wrote: *Family reasons.* It was true enough. Every reason in Hollowmere was a family reason; that was the whole trouble.

Wren came down in her socks, hair unbrushed, and looked at the letter over his arm without asking. She had stopped sleep-counting in September. She slept like a stone now, heavy and dreamless, and every morning Tomas checked her breathing anyway, an old habit already worn smooth as a river pebble.

"Are we staying?" she asked.

"We're staying."

She nodded, unsurprised, and ate her porridge, and outside the window the first proper frost of October lay on the garden like salt someone had finally swept into order.

* * *

The east wing was a ruin no architect could love: roof fallen into the rooms it used to shelter, walls furred with moss, the excavated doorway at its heart still shored up with timber and caution tape that no one in Hollowmere had ever taken seriously. But it had good bones, his grandfather's ledger said somewhere between entries of names — good bones, deep cellars, dry stone. And it had the door.

Tomas stood in front of it on a grey morning with a builder's report in one hand and Odile's field notebooks stacked under his other arm, and he made himself look at it properly for the first time since August.

It was closed. That mattered more than he could explain to anyone who hadn't been there. The bolt sat on the far side, where it had always sat, where generations of Vasquez hands had driven it home from below — but the door itself was shut, seated in its frame like a word swallowed, and the cold that had breathed out of it that last night was gone. The air around it smelled of nothing but old stone and rain.

He had considered filling it in. Concrete, rebar, the works. He'd gotten as far as pricing it.

Then he'd played the tape. *FOR WHOEVER STAYS.* Her voice, warm and slightly too close to the microphone, explaining with a folklorist's patience exactly why a wall would fail where a memory might hold: because the thing below did not answer to masonry. It answered to being known. For three hundred years it had been known only in fragments, in halves of songs sung by frightened people who refused to finish them, and the refusal had been the crack in everything. What kept the seal now wasn't stone. It was the song, sung whole, remembered by name.

*Keep it alive,* she'd said. *Not as a binding. As a memory. Memory is just a binding that loves you back.*

So instead of concrete, Tomas hired builders.

* * *

They came from two villages over, which was deliberate. Hollowmere men would have worked in silence, and silence was the disease here, not the cure. The outsiders asked cheerful ignorant questions — *what's this room for, then, what's the load-bearing situation* — and Tomas answered them all plainly, except about the door, about which he said only: "That stays. Reinforce the frame, don't touch the stone."

The foreman, a broad woman named Ilsa with sawdust permanently in her eyebrows, squinted at it. "Listed?"

"Older than listing."

She shrugged the shrug of a woman paid by the day and built around it. By November the east wing had a new roofline, damp-proofing, wiring run through conduits that hummed faintly when the wind caught them — and Tomas had learned to hear that hum and not flinch, which felt like progress of a kind.

The sign went up in December, painted by hand because printed signs looked wrong against the house:

**THE HOLLOWMERE ARCHIVE**
*Field Recordings · Oral History · Local Song*
*Open by appointment*

Underneath, smaller, because he'd argued with himself for a week before allowing it:

*In memory of Odile Vasquez, who gave her voice to keep ours.*

He had never known her middle name. He'd gone looking for it in her papers and found nothing — she'd signed everything O. Vasquez or simply Odile, as if she'd been paring herself down for years, getting ready to fit through some narrow place. So the plaque carried only what he had, and he visited it most mornings with coffee he didn't drink, the way other men visited graves.

Except it wasn't a grave. That was the thing he kept having to remind himself. There was no body, no marker, no legal death — just a woman who had walked down to an open door singing and had not walked back up, and a village that could not say her name above a whisper for months, and then, slowly, could.

* * *

The singers were the harder part.

There were four of them left who knew their halves: Mrs. Aldery with her lullaby fragment, the Fenn twins with the walking verse they'd learned from their mother, and old Bram Tallow, ninety-one, who knew more of the song than anyone suspected and had never sung three bars of it in company. When Tomas had first approached them — hat literally in hand, standing in Mrs. Aldery's kitchen while she pretended to be busy with a kettle — the room had gone so quiet he could hear the fire settling.

"You want us to sing it," Mrs. Aldery had said, "into a machine."

"Into a recorder. Into writing. Into whatever will outlast us." He'd laid Odile's notebooks on her table, the transcriptions with their careful rests and breath-marks, three years of a woman's patient listening. "Someone already finished it. Someone already paid. The least we can do is stop pretending we don't know the tune."

Mrs. Aldery had looked at the notebooks a long time. Her hands, which shook whenever she sang even a verse, were steady on the table's edge.

"The second time invites," she'd said. "That's what my mother told me, and hers before her. But your Odile — she sang it whole, didn't she. All of it. And the invitation went *down*, not out." She'd turned to face him then, eyes wet and furious and relieved all at once, an expression he would see often in the coming year and never get used to. "You're telling me the danger was never in the singing. It was in the hoarding."

"I'm telling you what happened," Tomas said. "I'm not clever enough to tell you what it means."

* * *

The first session was in February, in the archive's finished reading room, with a fire lit and tea going and four elderly singers arranged in chairs like witnesses at an inquiry — which, Tomas supposed, was exactly what they were. He ran the recorder himself. He'd learned the machine from Odile's notes, her neat instructions in the margins: *level check first, let them settle, never ask for a second take unless they offer.*

Mrs. Aldery sang first. Just the lullaby fragment, the one Odile had recorded three years ago in this same village, the song the old woman had performed once and then refused with shaking hands. Now she sang it twice through, deliberately, her voice cracked and true, and when she finished the second time she sat very still, waiting, and nothing in the room changed except that everyone in it was crying a little and pretending not to be.

"That's the first time in sixty years I've done that," she said. "Twice." She wiped her eyes with a handkerchief pressed flat as a document. "And the floor didn't open. Write that down, boy. *The floor did not open.* Let them know in fifty years that we noticed."

Bram Tallow went last. He'd brought his own chair. He sang the verses no living person had heard complete — the counting verse, the naming verse, the long descending line that Odile's tapes had only ever caught in pieces through walls and keyholes — and his voice was a ruin, sanded down by nine decades, but he knew every turn of it, and halfway through the third stanza the other three singers, without any signal Tomas could detect, joined in on the harmony their grandmothers must have shared.

The room filled with a sound Hollowmere had forbidden itself for three hundred years.

Tomas sat with his finger on the record button and his heart going like a fist on a door, and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like her hand on his shoulder, that this was the work. Not the roof, not the sign, not the archive itself. This: testimony given aloud, together, in daylight, with tea going cold in the cups. Justice built outside any court, from the only evidence that had ever mattered here — voices, freely given, refusing to be silent anymore.

When the last note settled, Bram looked straight into the microphone, the way Odile's notes said he never had before, and said: "For the record. We knew. All of us, all along, we knew, and we let it be paid for by whoever it fell on. That's the verse nobody wrote down, and it's the truest one I've got."

* * *

Spring came late that year, and then all at once.

The village changed the way ice changes — imperceptibly, then everywhere at once. It started with the children, because children have no memory of prohibitions, only of tunes. Wren taught the descant to two girls from the school bus, humming it over seatbacks, and within a month half the under-twenties in Hollowmere could sing the child's part without knowing what it had been for. Then the pub choir — which had existed for decades singing nothing riskier than sea shanties — asked Mrs. Aldery, shyly, if she might teach them something local.

Tomas attended that first rehearsal without announcing himself, sitting at the back with a pint he didn't touch, certain the whole thing would collapse the moment anyone reached the second verse.

It didn't. They sang it. Twenty-odd voices in the back room of the Lamb and Flag, singing the binding-song's second verse at conversational volume, slightly ragged, entirely unafraid, while outside the windows the April dusk came down soft and ordinary and nothing knocked anywhere in the village at all.

He walked home afterward through streets he'd hated as a boy and found he was hearing them differently. A dog barking. A television through a window. Someone practicing the descant badly in a bathroom. The ordinary noise of the living, uncounted, unafraid.

At the top of his own stairs he paused, out of habit, listening for the knocking that had lived beneath his rented room in those first weeks — the sound he'd logged as pipes, as settling, as anything but what it was.

Silence. The good kind. The kind that was just absence, not attention.

* * *

By summer the archive had visitors. Folklorists first — word travels fast in that small academic world, and a complete regional song cycle documented from four independent sources was the sort of thing that got cited. Then historians, then a documentary crew who stayed three days and left with eleven hours of footage and, Tomas noted with satisfaction, no story at all, because the truth was too strange to compress and too quiet to sell.

Odile's colleagues came, some of them. A woman named Prentiss who had co-edited a journal with her, who stood in the reading room among the boxed tapes for a long time and then said, quietly: "She wrote to me once, three years ago. Said she'd found something in Hollowmere worth finishing. I told her to be careful with *finishing*. Academics always mean it as a compliment." She'd laughed, and it had broken in the middle. "She finished it, didn't she. In her own way."

"She did," Tomas said.

Prentiss endowed a small stipend in Odile's name — for young researchers, for fieldwork done *properly*, with consent and credit and the names of singers preserved alongside their songs. The first recipient arrived in June: a nervous graduate student who reminded Tomas so sharply of Odile's early tapes — the same careful questions, the same habit of listening harder than she talked — that he had to step outside and stand in the churchyard for ten minutes until his throat worked again.

Wren, meanwhile, had appointed herself the archive's youngest employee. She shelved tapes, labeled boxes in enormous careful letters, and interviewed villagers with Odile's own recorder held in both hands like something hatched from an egg. She had her grandmother's ear, Tomas realized — the same instinct for which silences were important. She collected the pauses too.

"Dad," she said one evening, feet swinging off a chair too tall for her, "when I'm grown up, can I do what she did? Go places and write down what people remember?"

"You can do better," Tomas said. "You can do it while everyone's still alive to thank."

* * *

On the last night of August, one year on, the village did something no generation of Hollowmere had dared: they sang the cycle complete, outdoors, after dark.

It had been Mrs. Aldery's idea, proposed with the terrifying mildness she reserved for her most radical suggestions. "A memorial," she'd said. "Not a vigil. Vigils are for the dead and afraid. This is for the living and grateful. On the green. Whole song. Everyone who wants to."

Forty-one people came. Tomas counted them himself, twice, and then made himself stop counting, because some arithmetic belonged to the past. They stood in loose rings around the war memorial with lanterns — electric ones, Bram had insisted, *no flames, we're commemorating, not summoning* — and when Mrs. Aldery raised her hand the whole village drew one breath and began.

The lullaby first, in her cracked voice and then everyone's. The walking verse from the Fenn twins. The descant rising from the children like birdsong over water. Verse by verse the song assembled itself in the air above Hollowmere, whole for the first time in three centuries, sung by forty-one living throats in the open dark — and the final line, the line that required a name, they sang as she had asked it be sung: not as an offering, but as a remembering.

*Odile. Odile. Odile.*

Her name, spoken by a village, carried no weight at all. That was the miracle of it. Names were only heavy when they were currency. Spoken in gratitude, they weighed nothing, and rose.

Tomas stood at the edge of the rings with Wren's hand in his, and he did not cry until the very end, when the last chord faded and the night stayed exactly as ordinary as it had been — crickets, wind in the beeches, a car passing on the far road — and he understood that this, this unbearable mildness, this was what peace sounded like in a place that had forgotten its own name for it.

Wren tugged his sleeve. "Dad. Listen."

He listened. Crickets. Wind. And underneath — so faint he might have invented it, so gentle it could only have been kindness — something keeping time with the last echoes, down where the ground met its own deep dark, humming the melody back.

Not counting. Singing along.

Wren smiled up at him in the lantern light, and Tomas held his daughter's hand, and above them the August stars came out over Hollowmere like witnesses signing a page that was, at last, finished — though far away, in the reading room of the archive, Odile's oldest tape recorder sat with its reel turning slowly to a stop, and on the label, in handwriting no one in the village recognized, fresh letters had begun to spell a single word:

*Thank*
