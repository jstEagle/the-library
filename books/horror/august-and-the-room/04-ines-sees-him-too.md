## 4. Ines Sees Him Too

Ines Delgado had a folder under her bed, and the folder was the most important thing she owned, more important than her rock collection and more important than the tooth she'd lost in March that Tía Paloma said was worth a dollar from the tooth fairy even though Ines was pretty sure the tooth fairy wasn't real either.

The folder was blue with a white cat on it, except the cat had been colored over so many times it looked like a black cat now, or maybe a shadow of a cat. Inside were forty-one drawings. She knew because she counted them every night before bed, the way Mami counted the silverware when it came out of the dishwasher, the way Papi checked the locks twice, the way everybody in this house counted things like counting could keep something out.

Maybe it could. She hadn't decided yet.

The drawings were all of August.

She'd never met August. That was the thing nobody understood when she tried to explain it, which she had stopped doing, because the last time — three weeks ago, at dinner, with spaghetti — Papi had put his fork down very carefully and said, *Mija, where did you hear that name?* in a voice like the name was a bug he didn't want on the table. And Mami had laughed, but her laugh came out wrong, like a hiccup wearing a costume, and she'd said, *Kids make up friends, Tomás, remember your cousin's kid with the—* and then they'd both talked really fast about something else while Ines sat there knowing, in the way kids know things, that she had just stepped on something invisible and important.

So she stopped saying his name out loud. But she kept drawing him, because he kept showing up, and you can't just not draw somebody who keeps showing up. That would be rude. And also — this was the part she never told anybody — the drawings weren't really hers. Not exactly. Her hand did them, sure, the same way her hand brushed her teeth, but the pictures arrived already finished somewhere behind her eyes, and all she had to do was let them out through the crayons. It was like taking dictation, except for pictures. Tía Paloma would know what that word meant. Tía Paloma read big books with no pictures and stayed up late talking into a microphone to people who couldn't see her, which Ines thought was maybe the loneliest job in the world, and also the best one.

August looked the same in every drawing because he was the same in every visit. Corduroy overalls, the brown kind with the little cord ridges she made by drawing lots of close-together lines. A smile too wide for his face, which she used to think was just how she drew him until she tried to draw him smiling smaller and her hand wouldn't do it. It was like asking your elbow to bend backward. His smile was a certain size. That was just a fact about him, like how the sky is up.

He never got older. She'd figured out early that grown-ups get older because their faces do math you can see. August's face didn't do any math.

* * *

Tonight was Thursday, which meant Mami worked late at the clinic and Papi fell asleep in front of the TV with his mouth open, and Tía Paloma did bedtime. Ines liked Tía-Paloma-bedtime best. Tía Paloma did voices for every character and never skipped pages even when the book got boring, and she didn't rush the part where you lie in the dark and talk, which was honestly the best part of any day, better than recess, better than ice cream, because in the dark-and-talk part you got to ask real questions and nobody said *go to sleep.*

But tonight Ines didn't want to talk. Tonight she wanted to show.

"Wait here," she said, and slid off the bed, and pulled the blue folder out from under it, and carried it back like it might bite her if she moved too fast.

Tía Paloma sat up against the headboard. She had her radio-host voice even off the air, low and warm, like everything she said was being recorded by someone kind. "What've we got?"

"You can't tell Papi."

Paloma went still. Ines watched her aunt's face do a thing adults did where they smiled with their mouth and got serious everywhere else. "Okay," Paloma said. "I'm good at secrets. It's basically my whole job."

"It's not a bad secret."

"I know." And the strange thing — the thing Ines would think about later, lying awake — was that Tía Paloma said *I know* like she actually did know, like the secret had already knocked on her door too.

Ines opened the folder on the quilt.

She started with the early ones, the easy ones. August standing in the yard. August sitting on the porch steps. August waving. Tía Paloma turned each page slowly, and her breathing changed on page six, a small catch, like a stitch pulled tight.

"When did you draw these?"

"Every night. Mostly. Sometimes two in one night if he comes twice."

"Comes where?"

"Here." Ines pointed at the window. The curtains were open a hand's width, and past them the yard was going purple with evening, and the porch light was on because Mami always left it on now, every single night, since Abuela died. "He sits on the porch sometimes. He doesn't come inside. He says he's waiting for permission, but I don't know permission for what."

Tía Paloma's hands had stopped moving. "Ines. Look at me. When did he start coming?"

"Around when we moved in. Before school started." Ines shrugged, because it felt like a hundred years ago and also yesterday, the way time worked when you were nine. "He said he knew the house already. He said he'd been keeping the lights on."

The room got quiet in a specific way, the way it got quiet when the refrigerator stopped humming and you realized the hum had been there the whole time.

"Tía?"

"Keep going," Paloma said. Her voice was still warm but there was something under it now, like a stone under a blanket. "Show me the rest."

So Ines showed her the rest, and this was the part she'd been scared of, not because of August but because of what came with him.

Because after the first ten drawings or so, the house started appearing in them. And the house in the drawings was wrong.

It was their house — she always got the shape right, the porch, the crooked chimney, the elm tree Papi kept threatening to cut down — but every window had faces in it. Not drawn-faces, not smiley-face faces. Faces like the ones you saw when you pressed your cheek against a cold window from inside and looked out: pale, pressed, watching. Dozens of them. Every window, upstairs and down, attic and cellar, filled to the glass with people looking out, and none of them were anybody Ines knew, and all of them looked patient, the way you look when you're waiting for a bus you're absolutely certain will come.

She hadn't drawn those faces on purpose. They came with the pictures, the way shadows come with lamplight.

Tía Paloma turned to one of the window drawings and held it under the lamp for a long time. Her thumb moved toward one of the faces and stopped, hovering, not quite touching.

"That one," she said quietly. "Right there. Second floor, third window." Her finger trembled, just barely, the way a tuning fork trembles after the sound is done. "That's my grandmother's room."

"You knew her?"

"I lived in this house when I was your age." Paloma said it like a confession, like a coin dropped down a well. "For one summer. One summer and then—" She stopped. Started again. "And then I left, and I never came back, and I don't know why I never came back, except that some part of me must have known not to."

Ines considered this. Kids were good at considering things; adults forgot that. "You ran away from the house?"

"I ran away from everything. That's a longer story." Paloma set the drawing down carefully, face-up, like it was sleeping. "Ines, the faces in the windows. Have you ever seen anyone in the actual windows? Here? For real?"

"No." Then, because lying to Tía Paloma felt like lying to the radio, which felt impossible: "Not in the windows. But sometimes at night the hallway is longer than it should be. If I get up for water. It takes more steps. I counted. Eleven steps normally, and some nights it's twelve, and the twelfth step sounds different, like the floor is thinking about it."

Paloma closed her eyes. When she opened them they were shiny in the lamp light, and she reached over and pulled Ines close, and Ines let her, because nine was an age where you could still be hugged without it being weird, and she suspected that window was closing faster than the hallway was growing.

"Okay," Paloma said into her hair. "Okay. New rule. New rule starting tonight: no getting up for water alone. You knock on my door. Any hour. I don't care if I'm on air, I'll mute the mic."

"You'd leave your listeners?"

"My listeners will survive." Something flickered across her face when she said it, quick as a moth, gone before Ines could look at it straight. "One more thing, mija. The boy. August. What does he talk about?"

Ines flipped to the back of the folder, to the newest drawing, the one from last night, the one she'd almost not let out through the crayons because it had scared her even as it arrived.

It was the house again, windows full of faces, but this time August stood in front of it, on the porch, and above the house she'd written — because the pictures lately came with words attached, arriving whole, like captions on a movie — in her own careful nine-year-old letters:

**HE IS ALMOST DONE COUNTING.**

"What does he talk about," Ines repeated. "Numbers, mostly. He asks me how many people live here. He asked me that the first day, actually, before anything else. Like it was the most important question in the world. How many people sleep in this house. And I said four, because it's me and Papi and Mami and him—" she nodded at Paloma, "—and he smiled his smile, the whole-size smile, and he said, *Four now.*"

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere downstairs a door eased shut on its own weight, slow and soft, the way doors did in old houses, the way they'd been doing all week.

"*Four now,*" Tía Paloma repeated. She said it the way you'd repeat a diagnosis.

"And then last night," Ines said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted, "he asked me to draw the house with everyone in it. Everyone who lives here. All four of us, in the windows, so he could check his work. He said checking your work is how you know you're done."

She turned the page. There it was: the house, and in the windows now only four faces, and she knew them the way you know your own handwriting. Papi in the kitchen window. Mami in the one over the sink. Herself in her bedroom window, small and pale, pressing her palms to glass that wasn't there.

And in the fourth window — the big one at the end of the upstairs hall, the one that faced the road, the one that was always cold no matter what season it was —

Tía Paloma's breath stopped.

"—that's you," Ines said. "I didn't mean it to be. It just came out that way. You were already in the house in the picture before I ever drew you in the house in real life. He said the house remembers people who are coming before they come. He said the count likes to be ahead."

Paloma took the drawing in both hands. Outside, the purple had gone fully dark, and the porch light threw its yellow cone onto the steps, and Ines — without meaning to, without deciding to — looked at the window of the room they were sitting in, reflected faint and ghostly in the dark glass, and for half a second, less than half a second, the reflection showed a fifth shape standing behind them by the door, small, in corduroy, smiling its whole-size smile.

When she blinked, it was just the dresser and the laundry basket.

"Tía," she whispered. "Is August real?"

Paloma was quiet for a long moment. Downstairs, the grandfather clock that had been broken since before they moved in ticked once, deliberately, like a man clearing his throat.

"He was real to me first," Paloma said finally. "Thirty years ago. Which means, mija—" and here her arm tightened around Ines, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for the dead hours of her radio show, the voice that could hold anything, "—he isn't imaginary at all. Imaginary things stay where you put them. He's something else. Something that lets children think they invented him, because children have permission to invite things that grown-ups would bar the door against."

Ines looked down at the drawing in her aunt's hands. At her own small face in the paper window. At the words underneath, which had arrived last night already finished, already true:

**HE IS ALMOST DONE COUNTING.**

"How many does he need?" Ines asked.

Paloma stared at the cold window at the end of the hall — staring through the wall at it, Ines realized, staring at it from memory — and when she answered, she answered like someone reading a number off a ledger she'd hoped was buried.

"One more than four."
