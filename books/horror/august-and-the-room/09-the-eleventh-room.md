## 9. The Eleventh Room

**Paloma**

The drawings started showing up on the refrigerator like weather reports.

Marisol pinned them there with fruit-shaped magnets — Ines's crayon houses, her horses, her lopsided suns — and for the first week they were ordinary, the way nine-year-olds are ordinary: bright, repetitive, slightly unhinged in their confidence. Then the houses changed.

Every house had every window filled with faces.

Not stick-figure smiles. Faces with eyes that followed you across the kitchen if you let them, which Paloma made a point of never doing. She'd stand at the counter with her coffee and keep her gaze fixed on the toaster, on the window, on the slow drip of the faucet, anywhere but the refrigerator, because she was thirty-eight years old and she knew what it meant when a child drew something they shouldn't be able to see.

She told herself it was a phase. She told herself a lot of things lately. That the cold door was a draft. That the hallway being one step longer at night was her medication. That August sitting on the porch at dusk, unchanged after three decades, wearing the same corduroy overalls with the same grass stain on the left knee, was a hallucination she could live inside if she just didn't look directly at him.

The drawings got harder to ignore on a Tuesday, when Ines drew a house with twelve rooms.

Paloma counted eleven rooms in this farmhouse. She'd counted them her first night here, back when counting was still a comfort instead of a symptom — eleven rooms, one of them always cold, and the feeling, wordless and certain, of twelve people breathing in a house where only four lived.

She stood at the refrigerator that Tuesday morning holding Ines's drawing by its corner, the way you hold something dead, and counted the rooms in the picture. Twelve. The twelfth room sat at the end of a hallway Ines had drawn longer than any hallway in the actual house, and it had no furniture in it, no bed, no toys. Just walls covered in small dark marks, row after row, like writing.

Like names.

"Ines," Paloma said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because underneath it everything was screaming in a register only dogs and dead radio callers could hear. "Honey, what's this room?"

Ines looked up from her cereal. She had Tomás's dark eyes and Marisol's stubborn mouth and, this morning, an expression Paloma recognized from mirrors: the careful blankness of someone deciding how much truth the grown-up can handle.

"That's the new room," Ines said.

"There's no room there."

"I know." She said it the way you'd say *I know the sky is blue*. Patient. Slightly sorry for you. "It's not there yet."

*Yet.* Paloma set the drawing down very carefully, as though it might go off. "What's on the walls?"

"Names." Ines spooned more cereal into her mouth. Milk dripped down her chin and she wiped it with her sleeve, an ordinary child doing ordinary child things, and somehow that made it worse. "Thousands of them. I tried to read them but they're all smushed together except the empty part."

"The empty part?"

"At the bottom." Ines frowned, concentrating. "There's space. Like when you're almost done coloring and there's just a little bit of white left." She looked up, and her voice dropped into the confiding whisper children use for important things. "I think it's waiting for somebody."

* * *

**Ines**

Tía Palomo asked a lot of questions about the new room, and Ines didn't mind, because Tía Palomo was the only one who asked the right ones.

Mamá looked at the drawings and said *how lovely, mija* and pinned them up next to the others, including the ones with all the faces in the windows, which Mamá either didn't notice or was pretending not to notice, and Ines had learned that pretending was a thing whole families could do together, like a game nobody admitted to playing. Papá looked at the drawings for exactly two seconds and then went out to the barn to fix something that wasn't broken. But Tía Palomo looked at them the way Ines looked at them. Like they mattered. Like they were *true*.

Tía Palomo also talked to the radio at night. Ines heard her through the wall sometimes, low and fast, saying numbers. Ines pretended not to hear that, too. Everybody in this house was pretending something. It was practically the family hobby now.

The new room was hard to draw. That was the thing nobody understood — Ines didn't *make up* the pictures, she took dictation. Her hand did what it did and afterward she looked at what was there, same as everybody else. The new room kept changing. Some days the hallway to it was long. One day it was full of doors, all of them shut, and she'd drawn little cold-blue lines around each doorknob before she knew why, and then at breakfast Tía Palomo had gone white as milk and left the table, and Ines had felt bad about that without knowing what she'd done.

Today the room was clear. Today she could see it all the way to the bottom of the wallpaper.

She drew it after school, at the kitchen table while Mamá cooked and the radio murmured its afternoon songs, and she worked slowly because the names deserved care even if they were smushed. When she finished, she held the paper at arm's length and checked it against the feeling in her chest, the true-feeling, and it matched. Good drawing. True drawing.

That night, bedtime took forever. Mamá did the tucking and the water and the one-song limit, and then the hall light went off, and the house did its nighttime sounds — the tick of cooling wood, the wind worrying the eaves, and under it all, faint, the sound Ines thought of as the house swallowing. A soft, wet click, deep in the walls, like a throat.

Ines lay in the dark and thought about the empty space at the bottom of the wallpaper.

Here was the thing she hadn't told anybody, not even Tía Palomo: the room wasn't in the drawings because she imagined it. The room was in the drawings because the room wanted to be seen. She could feel it wanting. It pressed against the back of her eyes like a sneeze. And tonight, lying in her bed with the hall light off, she realized something else, something that made her stomach fold over:

She knew where the door was.

Not in the drawing. In the *house*. At the end of the real hallway, past the bathroom, there was a stretch of wall that was warmer than the rest of the wall, and everyone walked past it every day and nobody ever looked at it, the way you don't look at the spot on the ceiling, and Ines had never looked at it either until tonight, when she couldn't stop looking at it through the floor, through the dark, through the wallpaper of her own mind.

The room was there. It had been there the whole time. It was just shy.

Or it was waiting.

Ines got up. Her feet were cold on the floorboards and she didn't put on slippers because slippers made noise and this felt like a time for quiet. She crept down the hall, past Papá and Mamá's door, past the bathroom, past Tía Palomo's door — where a thin line of light showed under the door, and behind it, the murmur of the radio and Tía's voice, counting something — and stopped at the warm stretch of wall.

Up close, it wasn't warm. It was warm the way a hand is warm. Alive-warm.

"Hello," Ines whispered, because Mamá raised her polite.

The wall didn't answer, but the warmth spread, and the wallpaper seemed to shift the way water shifts when something moves underneath it, and Ines reached out and touched it with one finger, the way you touch a dog you don't know yet.

* * *

**Paloma**

Paloma heard the floorboard outside her room — the third one, the one that creaked, the one she'd catalogued along with every other sound this house made — and she was moving before her brain caught up, radio still murmuring dead air in her hand, bare feet on cold boards, pulling open her door onto a hallway lit by moonlight and nothing else.

Ines stood at the far end, small in her pajamas, one finger pressed flat against the wall.

"No," Paloma said, and the word came out of her like a shot. "Ines. *Don't.*"

But Ines had already touched it, and the wall was already changing, and Paloma's legs carried her down the hallway faster than she'd moved in weeks, faster than sense, her hand closing around her niece's shoulder and pulling her back, back, away —

Except the wall didn't stop.

Where Ines's finger had been, the wallpaper was peeling itself open like a healing wound in reverse, curling back in neat spirals, and behind the wallpaper there was no plaster, no studs, none of the honest guts of a house. There was another wall. And that wall was papered in names.

They ran in rows. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, written in hands of every kind — copperplate and scrawl, pencil and ink, some in languages Paloma couldn't read, some so old the letters had degraded into claw-marks. They covered the hidden wall floor to ceiling, dense as rain. And at the bottom, at a child's eye level, there was a patch of clean paper maybe two feet wide.

Empty. Waiting. Exactly as drawn.

"Oh God," Paloma breathed. "Oh, God, no."

"Tía?" Ines's voice was very small. Her finger was still extended, and on the pad of it, faint as a bruise rising, a mark was appearing. Ink. Welling up out of her skin like a tattoo being written from the inside.

A letter. Then another. Paloma grabbed her niece's hand and turned it toward the moonlight and read it upside down and felt the world tilt sideways and keep going.

*I-n-e-s*

Half-formed. The last letter unfinished, the stroke trailing off like a sentence interrupted, like a name caught mid-saying. Like a count that had found what it was looking for and paused, savoring it.

"It writes me," Ines said. She wasn't crying. That was the worst part. She said it with the mild interest of a child reporting a science experiment. "It knows my name."

"Listen to me." Paloma crouched, took Ines's face in both hands, forced those dark Tomás-eyes to meet hers. "Names have power here. You don't give yours. Not to the wall, not to the room, not to anyone who asks. Not even politely. Especially not politely. Do you understand me?"

"Why does it want my name?"

Because the house counts souls, and it counts through children best, and you were always the intended audience, and I brought this thing to your doorstep the summer I was seven years old and I have been running from it for thirty years and it has finally stopped chasing me and started shopping. Paloma said none of it. She said: "Because it's greedy. And we are going to be smarter than it."

Behind her, the hidden wall rustled.

She turned. The rows of names had shifted — she was almost sure they'd shifted — settling like birds on a wire rearranging themselves, and in the empty space at the bottom, the clean paper had grown a faint outline. A border. A frame, waiting for its picture. And above the empty space, in handwriting Paloma recognized with a sickness that started in her teeth and went all the way down — round, careful, a child's best cursive, *her* cursive, aged thirty years — a single line had appeared:

*Room for one more.*

"No," Paloma said aloud, to the wall, to the house, to whatever was listening through the wallpaper. Her voice cracked and she let it. "No. Not her. You don't get her. You never get her."

The hallway light clicked on.

Tomás stood at the other end in his boxers and a T-shirt, hair wild, face gray. Marisol was behind him, gripping his arm, and neither of them asked what was happening, because the wall was right there, open, breathing its thousands of names into the dark, and some things explain themselves.

"What is that," Tomás said. Not a question. A man demanding the universe take it back.

"The eleventh room," Paloma said, and then, because the lie had run out of road: "The twelfth. Depending on how it counts." She pulled Ines tight against her side, felt the girl's heartbeat hammering against her hip, fast as static. On Ines's finger, the half-written name was fading — the house pausing, patient, a collector who knows the auction isn't over. "And it's started writing Ines in."

Marisol made a sound Paloma had only heard once before, at their mother's funeral, a sound with no words in it at all.

Tomás stepped forward, jaw set, hand rising — to tear the wallpaper down, to punch the wall, to do something large and male and useless — and Paloma caught his wrist.

"Don't touch it," she said. "Whatever you do. Don't give it your name, don't speak your name near it, don't touch it. It's a ledger, Tomás. It's been keeping accounts in this house longer than the Delgados have owned it, and tonight—" her voice steadied, hardened, found the broadcast cadence she used at 1 a.m. when the dead called in and the living needed her calm, "—tonight it opened a new page."

Down the hall, behind them, the wallpaper rustled again, and in the space at the bottom, beneath the child-cursive warning, a fresh line of ink began to bleed through the clean paper, letter by patient letter, spelling out a date.

Three days from now.

The night of the new moon.
