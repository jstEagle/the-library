## 14. Rooms Going Dark

Tomás woke on the third morning to a house that had lost its hallway.

He knew it the way you know a face in a photograph that's been retouched — not by what was missing but by what had been moved to cover for it. The door of the bedroom he shared with Marisol opened directly onto the kitchen now. No corridor. No runner rug Ines had picked out at the flea market, blue with yellow stars. No coat hooks, no mirror, no little table where Marisol kept the bowl for keys. Just bedroom, then kitchen, as if the house had always been built that way, as if the stars rug had never existed.

"Marisol," he said.

She was already sitting up. She'd been sitting up, he realized, for some time. "I counted," she said. "Last night before bed I counted from here to Ines's room. Eleven steps." Her voice was flat and careful, the voice of a woman carrying something breakable across ice. "This morning it's four."

The kitchen smelled like coffee someone else had already made. Paloma stood at the counter with her back to them, and when she turned, Tomás saw she hadn't slept either. There were notebooks stacked beside her elbow — her notebooks, the ones he'd found, the ones he'd almost thrown into the yard along with her. He hadn't thrown them. He hadn't thrown anything. He'd stood in the driveway holding them and then carried them back inside like a man returning a stone to a cairn because the mountain seemed to want it there.

"The bathroom's gone," Paloma said. "It was there last night. This morning there's a wall where the door was, and the wallpaper is old. Older than us. It's like the room never—" She stopped herself. He watched her decide not to finish the sentence, out of mercy or out of fear; with Paloma those had become the same gesture.

Ines sat at the table drawing. She drew every waking hour now, fast and focused, a foreman keeping a crew on schedule. Crayons wore down to nubs by noon. Today's page showed the farmhouse from above, its rooms laid open like a dollhouse — and Tomás counted the boxes of it twice, because counting had become the family religion, and got nine where there should have been eleven.

"Papá," Ines said without looking up. "Don't count out loud. It hears counting."

* * *

Marisol kept the ledger nobody had asked her to keep.

It wasn't a notebook like Paloma's. It was the back of an envelope from the electric company, and on it, in her small square handwriting, she wrote down what the house took and when. She told herself it was practical. Inventory. If they were going to fight this thing — whatever this thing was, and she still flinched from naming it even in her own head — they would need to know its appetite.

*Tuesday: guest bath. Wednesday: hall closet (Mamá's coats were in it). Thursday: the stairs to the cellar — not blocked, just gone, a wall of plaster smooth and warm as skin.*

Her mother's coats. That was the entry that made her press the pen so hard it tore the envelope. The closet hadn't been emptied. The coats hadn't been moved. The room itself had simply stopped agreeing to exist, and everything in it went with it, and somewhere in the folded dark of the house her mother's camel coat hung in a closet that was no longer anywhere, and Marisol could not stop picturing it, could not stop reaching for the grief the way your tongue finds a broken tooth.

Because that was the other thing. The thing she hadn't told anyone.

The baby monitor still sat on the kitchen shelf, though Ines was nine and hadn't needed it since she was three. Marisol had kept it out of sentiment — Mamá had bought it, one of the last things she bought, laughing that Marisol worried too much. And three nights ago, in the hour when the house went quiet in a way that wasn't peace, the monitor had filled with static, and through the static, clear as a phone call, her mother had said:

*Mija. Leave the light on for me.*

And Marisol — God help her, God forgive her — had gone to the porch and turned the light on, and stood under it crying, feeling *held*, feeling for the first time since the funeral that her mother was near instead of gone. She'd left it on every night since. She knew — she knew with the cold part of her brain, the part that read Paloma's notebooks at 3 a.m. while everyone slept — that the light was a door propped open. She knew the voice on the monitor was not her mother the way a photograph is not a person. She knew, and she did it anyway, because the house had found the softest room in her and moved in, and some nights the only thing between her and the bottom of everything was that yellow bulb burning over the porch like a candle in a church window.

She wrote none of this on the envelope. Some inventory you keep in the body.

On Friday morning she woke and the pantry was gone. Not empty — *gone*. A wall where the shelves had been, and the wall was warm, and when she put her palm against it she felt, faintly, unmistakably, a pulse. Slow. Patient. Like something sleeping off a large meal.

That afternoon she caught Ines standing in the middle of the living room, turning in a slow circle, frowning at the ceiling.

"Mija?"

"There used to be a window there," Ines said, pointing at blank wall. "The morning window. Where the light came in on my drawings." She looked up at her mother, and her eyes were dry and terribly adult. "Mamá, is the house eating itself?"

Marisol knelt and held her daughter and did not answer, because every answer she owned was a lie, and Ines had passed the age of believing lies about houses.

* * *

Saturday, they tried the car.

Tomás had loaded it the night before — gas can, water, blankets, Ines's crayons and paper, Paloma's notebooks, Marisol's envelope, his own tools, because a man needs to bring something he knows how to use. They'd agreed on dawn. Nobody slept much anyway; sleep had become a gamble against waking to fewer rooms, and the house had started collecting faster, greedy now, cheated, the way Paloma described it after August left — *it takes rooms instead of souls, it's closing its hand*.

They walked out together at first light, all four, because nobody would say it aloud but nobody would let anyone walk out alone either. The porch. The gravel drive. The truck sat where Tomás had parked it, gray in the gray light, and his heart lifted — stupidly, gratefully — at the sight of it, ordinary and real, dented door and all.

He drove. Marisol beside him, Ines between them, Paloma twisted around in the seat watching the house shrink in the rear window.

The drive took four minutes. Tomás knew it took four minutes because he'd driven it a thousand times, to town, to church, to the school where Ines used to wait by the fence. At four minutes the road should have met the county highway with its guardrail and its mailbox painted like a cow.

Instead the truck crunched onto gravel, and the farmhouse stood in front of them.

Porch light on. Front door closed. Same angle, same distance, same crooked shutter on the east gable that Tomás had been meaning to fix for six years.

Nobody spoke. Tomás put the truck in reverse and backed down the drive until the house slid out of the mirrors, then turned and drove forward again, faster, jaw set, hands at ten and two, a man doing arithmetic with his foot on the gas.

Two minutes. Three. Four.

Gravel. Porch. Door.

The third time, Marisol reached over and put her hand on his arm. "Tomás."

"I know how far it is," he said. His voice came out strange, squeezed. "I have driven this road my whole life. I know how far it *is*."

"It isn't a road anymore," Paloma said quietly from the back seat. "It's a throat. We're in it."

They sat in the idling truck in the dooryard, engine ticking, and Tomás stared through the windshield at the house that had eaten his road, and something in him finished breaking — cleanly, like a bone setting wrong — and began, in the same motion, to heal into a different shape.

* * *

He found Paloma on the porch that evening, sitting where August used to sit, which he suspected she didn't realize.

For a long time he just stood in the doorway. Then he came out and lowered himself into the chair across from her, the one with the broken slat he'd never fixed, and the two of them watched the light Marisol insisted on leaving burning — and he saw Paloma look at it, and look away, and say nothing, and he understood that she knew about the monitor, or guessed, and was extending to his wife the same terrible courtesy the rest of them extended to each other now.

"I owe you an apology," Tomás said.

Paloma shook her head. "You don't—"

"I found your notebooks and I read enough of one page to decide my sister was insane, and I stood in the driveway deciding whether to put my family in a car and leave you standing in it." He kept his eyes on the light. It was easier than her face. "Thirty years, Paloma. You lived with this alone for thirty years because the one time you tried to tell people, they did exactly what I did. And then you come here to be safe, and the safe place turns out to be the mouth of it, and the first thing we do is nearly make you homeless twice."

"You believed me eventually," she said. "Some people never get the eventually."

"That's not forgiveness, that's arithmetic."

"No," Paloma said, and now she looked at him, and her eyes in the porch light were tired and steady and older than thirty years could account for. "But it's a start. And Tomás — I need to tell you something, because you're the one who'll do the math when it matters. The count was at eight when August left. It drops when a soul leaves freely. But the house doesn't forgive a debt; it refinances one. Every room it eats is a room it thinks it owns. And it's accelerating because it's angry, and it will keep accelerating until moonset, and moonset is tomorrow night."

"Then we leave before moonset."

"How?" she asked gently. "Your road is gone."

"Ines," Tomás said.

Paloma went very still.

"She draws rooms," he said. "Rooms that don't exist anymore. Marisol saw her walk into the pantry wall yesterday and come out holding a jar of Mamá's peaches. Ninety seconds, maybe less, and then the drawing closes. But ninety seconds is a hallway. Ninety seconds is a road, if she draws it long enough." He heard his own voice steadying as he said it, the skeptic discovering that belief, once installed, worked like any other tool. "My daughter is nine years old and she's the only architect this house can't fire."

From inside, faintly, came the sound of crayon on paper — fast, relentless, a girl building exits out of wax and will.

"And if it doesn't hold?" Paloma asked. "If the house learns to eat her drawings too?"

Tomás looked at the porch light, burning faithful and fatal over the door, and thought of the voice on the monitor, and of his wife's face when she thought no one could see her listening to it, and of the envelope in Marisol's pocket with its list of losses, and of the pulse in the pantry wall, slow and patient and getting hungrier.

"Then we run out of house," he said, "before we run out of family. And I've decided which one I'm saving."

Inside, the lights flickered — once, twice, in sequence, front to back, like something walking through the rooms taking attendance.

And then, from the kitchen shelf, thin and tinny through nine years of dust, the baby monitor clicked on all by itself, and a voice that was and was not Marisol's mother said, kindly, warmly, in the tone of a woman reading a bedtime total:

*"Nine."*
