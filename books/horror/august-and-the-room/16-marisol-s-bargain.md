## 16. Marisol's Bargain

Marisol had learned to tell time by the baby monitor.

Not the clock on the stove, which had started running backward sometime last week, and not her phone, which lost signal the moment it crossed the porch. The monitor. It sat on the kitchen counter where Ines used to sleep, though Ines was nine now and hadn't needed a monitor in years, and every night around two a.m. it filled with static that wasn't static — it was breathing, slow and patient, like something very large asleep in the walls.

And sometimes, under the breathing, her mother's voice.

*Marisol. Mija. It's cold in here.*

She'd stopped telling herself it was interference. Interference didn't know her name. Interference didn't hum the lullaby from the house in Oaxaca, the one about the woman who wove birds out of thread, the one Mami sang with her whole chest while shelling peas at the sink. Interference didn't say *you always were my careful girl* in exactly the tone Mami used when Marisol checked the locks twice and Tomás laughed at her for it.

Mami had been dead thirty-one days. Marisol knew because she counted everything now. Days since the funeral. Rooms left in the house — eleven this morning, ten by dinner. Steps in the hallway, which changed depending on who walked it. She counted because counting was the only thing that still behaved.

The others thought she was holding up well. That was the joke of grief: everyone watches the widow or the daughter to see if she'll crack, and so you learn to be smooth where they're looking and come apart where they aren't. Paloma came apart at night; Marisol heard her through the wall, talking low to someone who never answered. Tomás came apart in the truck, sitting in the driveway with his forehead on the wheel. Ines came apart into her drawings, page after page of a house with too many faces in the windows.

Marisol came apart at the cold door.

* * *

It was the door off the back hall, the one that had been ice against Paloma's palm since the day she arrived, the one Tomás swore felt fine on his side. Marisol had tested it herself, both sides, three times, because she was her mother's careful girl. Warm wood facing the kitchen. Cold as a grave lid facing the hall. The house couldn't even keep its lie straight anymore.

Tonight she stood in front of it in her robe, bare feet on floorboards that had gone soft as loam, and she said it out loud.

"I'm offering."

The door did nothing. Of course it did nothing. Doors don't answer. But the cold coming through the seam at the bottom thickened, pooled around her ankles like water finding its level, and somewhere below the floor — or above the ceiling, the geometry wouldn't commit — something turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to pay attention.

"My name is Marisol Delgado," she said, and her voice held steady, because she'd practiced. "Née Reyes. Daughter of Lucía Reyes, who died on the ninth of last month, who is in this house somewhere because I hear her on the monitor every night." Her breath fogged. In July. "I'm offering myself. Freely. Take me and let them go — Tomás, Ines, Paloma. Let them walk out and keep walking."

The cold climbed her shins. Not painful. Worse than painful — *welcoming*. Like sinking into a bath drawn by someone who loved you. Somewhere far off, faint as a hymn through a hospital wall, she heard the count tick over in her bones.

Nine.

"No."

Paloma's voice, from the mouth of the hallway.

Marisol didn't turn around. "Go back to bed, Paloma."

"You're doing what I was going to do." Now footsteps, quick and furious, and then Paloma's hand closed around her arm — thin fingers, radio-host fingers, gripping harder than Marisol would have guessed. "I've been sleeping in my clothes for two nights waiting for one of us to try this. Get away from the door."

"It accepted me." Marisol finally looked at her. Paloma's face was gray in the dark, eyes huge, hair wild from the pillow. "Did you feel that? Nine. It took the offer. It's already—"

"It *started* to. Offers can be withdrawn before moonset. August told me. There are rules, Marisol, and we finally know some of them, and the first rule is nobody gives this house anything else." She pulled. Marisol planted her feet.

"Let go of me."

"Not until you step back from the door."

"Why?" And there it was, the crack running through her voice, the smooth surface splitting right down the middle. "Why should I? You want to know why I'm standing here, Paloma? Because I heard my mother's voice and I *answered*, and you know what that makes me? It makes me the same as you."

Paloma flinched like she'd been struck.

"That's right." Marisol turned fully now, shaking, cold climbing past her knees and she barely cared. "Three weeks of calls from dead people and you said *nothing*. You sat at our table eating my cooking knowing the house was counting us like spoons, and you kept your little secret because your secret was more comfortable than my family's lives. So don't stand there telling me about rules. I learned duty from a woman who buried two children before me and got up every morning anyway. My duty is to my mother. She is alone in there—"

"She is *not in there*." Paloma's voice cracked across the hallway like a whip, and then dropped, ragged: "Marisol. Listen to me. I have talked to the dead every night for three weeks. Do you know what they all have in common? Every single one of them was counted by a house like this one. They stayed. They answered voices they loved, and the count closed around them, and now they're wallpaper." Her breath hitched. "There is a room upstairs with thousands of names on the walls. Your mother's name might be on it. But your mother isn't speaking to you from inside this house. This house is wearing her voice like a coat, because grief is the easiest door, and you left yours wide open."

The cold reached Marisol's waist. She could feel it listening. Loving her. That was the horror of it, the thing she'd never be able to explain to anyone who hadn't stood here: it didn't feel like a trap. It felt like being wanted.

"Mami hummed the bird song," she whispered.

"I know." Paloma's grip gentled but didn't release. "Because I told you what she sounded like. Three nights ago, in the kitchen, crying over the dishes. You told me about the peas, about her chest, about the weaving. Everything the voice has said to you, you gave it first." A beat. "That's how it works. That's how it worked on me when I was seven years old and so lonely I invented a friend and invited him into this house. I fed it August. You're feeding it Mami."

Marisol's legs went. Not all the way — Paloma caught her, both of them half-collapsing against the warm side of the cold door, and Marisol realized she was sobbing, great ugly gulps she hadn't allowed herself at the funeral because someone had to make the casseroles and sign the cards and hold Tomás up.

"I just want to hear her again," she said into Paloma's shoulder. "Is that so much? Thirty-one days. I just—"

"I know."

"And if it's a lie, then she's really gone, and if it's real, then she's trapped, and either way—" A wet, furious laugh. "Either way I lose her twice."

Paloma held her. The cold pressed against their backs like a crowd pressing toward a stage, patient, certain the show would go on.

* * *

They ended up on the kitchen floor, of all places, backs against the cabinets, sharing the blanket off the couch because neither of them wanted to be in a room alone. The monitor hissed on the counter. Neither of them looked at it.

"I'm sorry," Marisol said eventually. "For what I said. About your secret."

"Don't be. It was true." Paloma turned her mug of untouched coffee in her hands — she hadn't drunk any in days; none of them trusted swallowing anything the house had touched. "I told myself silence was protection. It was cowardice with good posture. If I'd broken it the night I arrived, you'd have had weeks more to prepare."

"We'd have thought you were crazy."

"Yes. But crazy and warned beats sane and surprised." She almost smiled. "Tomás is coming around. The hallway grew a step for him. He believes now, he just hates it."

"He's good at hating things he can't fix."

"He gets it from somewhere." Paloma glanced at her. "Your mother, probably. Getting up every morning anyway."

Marisol laughed — cracked, but real. Then quiet settled, the bad kind, the kind with a shape, and she asked the question she'd been carrying since the hallway.

"The name I offered. Is it still... is it written down somewhere? Even if I take it back?"

Paloma was quiet long enough that the answer arrived before she spoke it.

"There's space at the bottom of the wallpaper. Fresh space. Ines saw it." A pause. "A name appeared when she touched it. Half-formed. Hers."

Marisol closed her eyes. "So it keeps a ledger and it drafts children."

"It counts drawings now too. Ines's drawings. That's why the count jumped." Paloma set the mug down. "Which means we have until moonset tomorrow night, maybe less, and one way out that the dead on my switchboard swear works. A name freely given satisfies the count. One name. One person stays, the rest walk free."

"So it comes down to who loves the others enough to be wallpaper."

"It was always going to come down to that. I just intend to make sure it's me."

"Paloma—"

"Don't." For a second Marisol saw the seven-year-old under the radio voice, the lonely girl who'd invented a friend because no one else came. "You have a daughter. I have a sister-in-law who makes casseroles and checks the locks twice. The math isn't hard."

"The math," Marisol said slowly, "is exactly what my mother would have hated. She didn't raise children who do arithmetic with their lives. She raised children who find the third option." She pushed herself up off the floor, joints aching, resolve hardening into something with edges. "So we find one. Tonight. Before moonset. You call your dead people, I'll talk to Ines about her drawings, and between the four of us and whatever August is, we find a third option."

Paloma stared at her. "You believe that's possible?"

"I believe my mother got up every morning for sixty years without a guarantee of anything." Marisol pulled the blanket off them both and folded it, because folding things was how she prayed. "Get your notebook. We start now."

But as she crossed the kitchen, the monitor breathed — and this time it wasn't her mother's voice, and it wasn't static.

It was August. Calm, warm, terribly kind:

*"Thank you for taking your name back, Marisol. It made room for mine."*

And down the hall, the cold door swung open onto darkness that hadn't been there before — a darkness with a doorway's shape, waiting politely, like a guest who'd finally been invited.

Paloma went white. "That's not possible. He's outside. He's been outside for thirty years—"

The front porch light, visible through the kitchen window, flickered once and went out.

Then came back on.

Then went out again — and stayed out, and in the glass of the dark window, faint as breath on a mirror, Marisol watched a figure in corduroy overalls walk *out of the reflection* and into the hallway behind them, smiling his too-wide smile, saying:

*"The count likes lit houses. But it loves open doors."*
