## 5. First Ringing

Freya woke at 2:47 a.m. without knowing why.

The cottage was cold in the way Hollowmere was always cold—not the honest cold of a drafty room but something more deliberate, as though the chill had been measured out by hand. She lay still under the blankets Ivo had left stacked on the chair by her door when she moved in three weeks ago, and listened to the house settle its bones.

Then she heard it.

At first she thought it was her own pulse, thick in her ears. Then she thought it was wind—Hollowmere's wind had a voice, a low moaning through the chimney that she'd learned to sleep inside of like a shell held to the ear. But this was not wind. This was round. This was bronze.

This was a bell.

She sat up. The sound came from the direction of St. Aldhelm's, down the lane and past the frozen duck pond, and it rang with the slow, patient gravity of a bell being rung by someone who knew exactly what they were doing and had done it for a very long time. Not an alarm. Not an accident. A tolling. One stroke, then a silence long enough to hold a whole prayer, then another stroke.

Freya counted. She couldn't help counting; it was what she did with things that frightened her, she made them into data. Eleven strokes. A pause. Eleven strokes again.

The bell of St. Aldhelm's had been melted down in 1911. She had read the record herself, had run her gloved fingers over the ledger entry describing the sale of the metal, eleven pounds of bell-bronze cast into eleven small hand bells, one for each child lost to the winter. The great bell was slag now. It had been slag for a hundred and thirteen years. There was nothing up in that tower but an empty yoke and pigeons and the cold.

And yet.

She got up, dressed in the dark with the clumsy speed of someone who has decided not to give herself time to reconsider, and opened her front door.

* * *

The village stood in its doorways.

That was the first thing—the sheer completeness of it. Every house on the lane had a figure silhouetted against its own interior light, or against no light at all, standing on thresholds with their heads bowed. Old Mrs. Tarnow in her nightgown and coat. The Postlethwaites together, his arm around her shoulders. A young family Freya didn't know yet, the father holding a child who was not crying, who was not asking questions, who stood with the terrible docility of a child who has been taught this posture since before he could walk.

Nobody looked up. Nobody spoke. The bell tolled and the village bowed and the frost on the windowsills caught the moonlight like salt.

Freya stood in her own doorway, and understood, with a lurch, that she was the only one whose head was raised.

It felt obscene. It felt like the moment in a funeral when everyone kneels and you are still standing, fumbling for the right page. She bowed her head too—not out of reverence, she told herself, but because standing upright suddenly felt like shouting in a library—and watched her own breath bloom and fall, bloom and fall, keeping time with the bell.

Eleven strokes. Pause. Eleven strokes.

Three full cycles. She counted all of them. On the last stroke the sound did not fade the way sound fades, thinning into distance; it stopped, cleanly, the way a door closes. As if something had finished saying what it came to say.

Then the village moved. Doors shut, one after another down the lane, soft and sequential, like a wave folding back into the sea. Within two minutes the street was empty. Within five there was no evidence anything had happened at all except the cold, which seemed, if anything, deeper than before—a cold with an aftertaste.

Freya stayed in her doorway until her feet went numb. No lights came on anywhere. No voices. Hollowmere lay under the moon like a photograph of itself.

She went inside and wrote it down. Time of onset: 3:01 a.m., approximately. Duration: nineteen minutes. Pattern: eleven strokes, pause, repeated three times. She wrote it in her notebook in her careful teacher's hand, and then she sat looking at the page, and then she added a line beneath:

*Everyone knew. No one will say.*

* * *

In the morning, the schoolroom was warm.

Ivo had been at the boiler before dawn—she could tell by the wet boot prints half-melted on the corridor flagstones, already going grey at the edges—and the radiators ticked with the contentment of iron doing its work. Freya stood in the doorway of her classroom with her coat still on and let the warmth find her face, and hated how much she needed it, and hated the small voice that said: *this is why they pay the price. This warmth. You are drinking it too.*

"Miss Oyelaran."

Sefu Halloway stood at the classroom door. She was seventy if she was a day, straight-backed as a churchwarden, with a face like a carved doorpost—something ancient and load-bearing. In her hands she carried a covered dish.

"I brought you breakfast," Sefu said. "You'll be tired. The nights are long here."

"The nights are—" Freya stopped. "Did you hear it? Last night?"

"Hear what?" said Sefu Halloway, pleasantly, and set the dish on Freya's desk.

There it was. The word spoken so smoothly, so kindly, that for one vertiginous second Freya doubted her own ears, her own clock, the nineteen minutes of her own numb feet. *Hear what.* Two words, and the whole night folded up and vanished like a letter burned.

"You were awake," Freya said. "Everyone was awake. I saw the whole village in their doorways."

Sefu looked at her then, and her eyes were not unkind. That was somehow worse. They were the eyes of someone watching a person walk toward a cliff edge with a picnic basket, deciding how much to say and when.

"It's an old custom," Sefu said. "On certain nights, we keep a quiet hour. Out of respect. It does no harm, and it does some good." She smoothed her apron. "You're new, Miss Oyelaran. New people hear things the rest of us have learned not to listen to. It passes. Let it pass."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then you'll be one of us properly." Sefu smiled, and the smile reached her eyes, and Freya realized with a small internal horror that the smile was genuine—that Sefu Halloway meant it as welcome, as inclusion, as warmth—and turned to go. At the door she paused.

"The children," she said. "When they come. Teach them well and don't teach them anything else. That's all any of us ever wanted for them."

She left the breakfast dish. Freya never learned what was in it. She couldn't eat it.

* * *

She tried the pub at lunchtime, which was a mistake she should have seen coming.

The Green Man at midday held six men and the landlord, all of whom found urgent business elsewhere within four minutes of her arrival, departing with their pints half-drunk in a dispersal so smooth it must have been practiced. The landlord, a broad man named Garrick with forearms like hams, wiped the bar and answered her questions with the flat courtesy of a man defusing something.

"Lovely morning," Freya said.

"Aye."

"Quiet night, though."

Garrick's cloth stopped moving for exactly one second, then resumed. "Nights are quiet here," he said. "That's the way we like them."

"I heard the bell."

The cloth kept moving. His eyes did not. "St. Aldhelm's hasn't rung since before my grandfather was weaned," he said. "No bell up there. You'd know that if you'd read your parish history, miss, and I'm told you're a reading sort of woman."

"I know there's no bell up there. That's rather my point."

He leaned on the bar then, close enough that she could smell yeast and coal smoke, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Look. You seem decent. So I'll say this once, and it's not a threat, it's advice, the same advice I'd give my own daughter. Whatever you heard, unhear it. Whatever you saw, unsee it. There's folk in this village who've buried children and grandchildren and great-grandchildren in that churchyard, and every one of those funerals happened in a village that kept its quiet hours. You want to ask why the graves are so old and the village is so alive, you go ahead and do your sums. But don't ask us out loud. Asking out loud is how you get answers."

"Is that a threat?"

"No, miss." He straightened up, and resumed wiping the bar, and his face went pleasant and closed as a shop shutter. "That's arithmetic."

* * *

She went to the churchyard in the afternoon because she had nowhere else to put herself, and because Garrick had said *do your sums*, and sums were the one thing she trusted.

The churchyard was small and crowded, stones leaning together like conspirators, and she walked the rows reading dates the way other people read tea leaves. And the pattern was there, plain as print, once you looked. A scatter of deaths across the decades, ordinary and unremarkable—and then clusters. Winters with five names. Winters with eight. 1911 itself, a thicket of small stones, names she recognized from the register: Ashdown, Bellamy, Carrow, Edith Hale. Children's dates of death all within six weeks of each other, all in the hungriest winter the parish ledger described.

And then, in every cluster-winter since—1927, 1943, 1963, 1988—the same thing. Deaths in the cold months, bunched like birds on a wire. And in between, nothing. Decades where nobody in Hollowmere died at all. Not of age, not of accident, not of the cancers that take everyone everywhere eventually. Whole twenty-year spans in a village of two hundred souls with a single burial date between them.

People didn't live like that. People didn't die like that. Unless—

Unless something was being paid, and something was being spared, on a schedule.

Freya stood among the leaning stones with her notebook open and her pen motionless, and felt the shape of the truth move underneath the facts like a fish under ice. She couldn't see it. She could see where it was.

Behind her, gravel crunched. Ivo stood at the lychgate with his cap in his hands, and his face was grey.

"Don't write that down," he said.

"Ivo—"

"Not here. Not anywhere they can find it." He came through the gate, quick and low, glancing back down the lane the way a man checks for weather. "You've been to the pub. You've talked to Sefu. You've done your sums in the churchyard on the same afternoon. By tonight they'll know everything you know, and they'll start deciding what to do about you, and I'm asking you—" his voice cracked, just slightly, just at the seam— "I'm asking you to come home and lock your door and let me explain some things before anyone else gets to you first."

"They won't hurt me."

"No," Ivo agreed. "They won't hurt you. That's not what they do." He looked at the clustered children's stones, and then away from them, fast, the way you look away from a wound. "They'll just stop seeing you. And the thing in the cold, Miss Freya—it only takes what no one is seeing."

The bell had rung eleven times, three times over, for a class that had been dead for a hundred and thirteen years. The village had bowed. The village had closed. And now the only person in Hollowmere willing to stand in a doorway with his head up was the caretaker, holding his cap, begging her in a whisper to come home before dark.

Freya closed her notebook.

"Explain on the way," she said. "It's nearly four. I want to be indoors before the light goes."

Ivo nodded, and pulled his cap on, and as they passed through the lychgate together, far off across the white fields, from the direction of the scrapyard where a hundred and thirteen years of rusted bell-bronze lay in a melted lump behind a wire fence—

—or from somewhere that only sounded like it—

—one soft stroke rang out, clear as a finger tapped on glass.

Just once. Just a greeting.

Ivo's hand found hers in the dark of the lane, and gripped it, and did not let go.
