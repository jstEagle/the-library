## 6. The Frost Writes

The frost came on a Tuesday, which felt obscene. Disasters, Freya had always believed, should have the decency to arrive on significant days—solstices, anniversaries, the dark of the moon. Not a gray Tuesday in late November with the radiators ticking and the smell of chalk dust and Ivo's boiler breathing warmth up through the floorboards like a sleeping animal.

It started at the corner window, the one nearest Edith Marsh's empty desk.

Freya noticed it during morning break, standing with a mug of tea gone cold because Hollowmere's kettle took eleven minutes and their attention had wandered. The frost on that pane wasn't frost. Frost, proper frost, made ferns—branching, fractal, indifferent, the same pattern whether it formed on a farmhouse window or a dead man's eyelash. This was different. This was *legible*.

Letters. Half-formed, slanting, as though written by a hand learning to write again after a long convalescence. The strokes were made of ice-crystal so fine it looked like breath on glass, and they did not quite resolve into words. They resolved into something worse: the *beginnings* of words. An E that might become EDITH or ELLEN or EMILY. A long vertical stroke waiting to decide what it would carry.

Freya set down the tea. Walked to the window. Put one finger against the glass.

Cold came through the fingertip like a question.

Behind them, the classroom door opened and closed—the small sounds of children filtering back in from the yard, nine living children who had been assigned to Freya's care by a village that would not explain why there were twenty-two desks' worth of names in the registers and only ever eleven chairs. Freya turned back to the class with the practiced brightness of a first-term teacher and did not look at the window again until home time.

By then, the writing had spread.

* * *

All four windows now carried it. The frost had crept along the frames overnight—or over the day; Freya could not say when—and each pane held its own fragment of script, the letters larger near the corners where the cold pooled, smaller toward the centers, as if the writer were running out of room or patience or *glass*. Freya stood alone in the classroom at four o'clock, coat still on, bag still hanging from one shoulder, and read what could be read:

Window one: **ED**

Window two: **ITH M** — no. Look again. **I TH M**. Or was the M an upside-down W? Was anything here actually language, or was Freya's own hungry mind doing what minds do, finding faces in clouds, names in static?

Window three: a single letter, repeated, overlapping itself like a signature practiced on a schoolroom slate. **A. A. A.**

Window four: nothing legible at all, only a dense rime of crystal that caught the last of the light and threw it back in tiny prisms, beautiful the way certain wounds are beautiful.

"Right," Freya said aloud, to the empty room, in the crisp voice they used for register call. "Evidence."

Because that was the thing. That was the whole thing. Freya Oyelaran had come to Hollowmere with two suitcases and a method. The registers didn't add up? Photograph them. The headteacher's warnings didn't make sense? Write them down, date them, cross-reference them. Eleven chairs warm at seven a.m. with no one in them? Measure the temperature, note the time, file it under anomalies pending explanation. Documentation was a kind of prayer, Freya had told themselves on the bus from Inverness, watching the road close behind them like a mouth shutting. Not prayer in the kneeling sense—prayer in the archivist's sense. The belief that what is faithfully recorded cannot wholly be lost. That naming a thing is a way of keeping it safe. That the truth, written down honestly enough times, becomes load-bearing.

So: evidence.

The phone camera was old but adequate. Freya shot all four windows from three angles each, wide shots for context, close-ups for detail, a ruler from the supply cupboard laid against the glass for scale, because scale mattered, because *method* mattered, because if you were going to be the person who believed impossible things you had better be the person with the best records in the building. The flash fired white against the frost. In the little screen, the letters glowed like something lit from inside.

Twelve photographs. Freya checked each one twice, backed them up to the cloud with the village's grudging dial-up wheezing through the upload like a man climbing stairs, and went to bed feeling—for the first time since the bell rang at 3 a.m. three weeks ago, since the whole village stood in its doorways with bowed heads while a melted bell rang in a scrapyard—feeling *steady*. Feeling armed.

They woke at six to a silence of a particular quality, and knew before they reached the classroom.

* * *

The windows were clear.

Not frosted—clear. Wiped, somehow, polished, the panes so clean they seemed thinner than glass ought to be, and the November morning lay flat and gray beyond them, ordinary as porridge. Freya stood in the doorway for a long moment with the phone already in hand, thumb already moving, opening the gallery.

Twelve photographs.

The first showed a classroom. Empty desks, a ruler propped against a window, gray light. No frost. No letters. Glass like water.

The second: the same. The third: the same. Freya scrolled faster, then slower, then zoomed in until the pixels broke apart into grain, hunting for a ghost of crystal, a smear, a compression artifact shaped like hope. Nothing. The photos had been taken—Freya remembered taking them, remembered the flash, remembered the cold coming through the glass into the fingertip like a question—but they recorded a world in which nothing had happened. Every image was blank glass. Every image was Tuesday.

The cloud backup, when it finished syncing, matched perfectly.

That was the detail that undid them. Not the loss—the loss was almost a relief, almost funny, the universe playing the archivist's oldest joke: *your evidence was never evidence.* It was the thoroughness. Whatever had written on the glass had also reached into the phone, into the cloud, into the small faithful architecture of backups Freya had built precisely against moments like this, and edited everything. Consistently. Completely. As though the frost had never been. As though the only place it had ever existed was memory—which is to say, nowhere that anyone else could check.

Freya sat down at their own desk, the teacher's desk, and put their face in their hands.

This was the terror, they understood then, and it was not the frost and not the letters and not even the bell. The terror was this: that from this morning forward, every strange thing Freya witnessed in Hollowmere would happen in the one country to which no one else held a passport. They could photograph the bell ringing; the photo would show a scrapyard. They could record Sefu Halloway's oblique warnings; the recording would hold only wind. They could bring the whole village to stand before these windows and the village would look at clean glass and look at Freya with pity, and the pity would be the cruelest thing yet, because it would be *correct*, according to every instrument human beings had ever agreed to trust.

To be disbelieved is bad. To be disbelieved by your own evidence—to reach for the proof you carefully made and find it smiling back at you with a blank face—that is a different order of exile. It means the madness isn't coming. It means the madness has already arrived, and it has excellent administrative skills.

"Morning, miss."

Freya looked up. One of the nine—Tam Yelland, eight years old, perpetually cold, perpetually cheerful about it—was hanging up his coat. He glanced at the windows without interest.

"You'll want to wipe them again," he said. "Mum says the glass sweats somethin' awful this time of year."

"Wipes them?" Freya heard their own voice from a great distance. "Who wipes them?"

"Ivo does." Tam shrugged, settling into his seat, pulling out his pencil. "Every morning. Before anyone's up. Mum says his dad did it too, and his dad's dad." He frowned slightly, the frown of a child encountering an adult's oddness. "Don't you know that?"

Freya did not know that. Freya thought of Ivo at the boiler, feeding it through nights when fire shouldn't burn, tending it like a vigil, saying only that the village kept the cold *polite*. Polite. And every dawn, before the village woke, Ivo Almeida walked the school perimeter wiping clean glass that someone or something had written on in the night, and had said nothing, and had asked nothing, and had moved eleven chairs back into their places each evening with the same silent diligence.

The children filed in. Freya took the register. Nine living names, answered in nine living voices. And then, because their hand moved before their courage could stop it, Freya read out, softly, the first name on the 1911 list—the name the frost had been trying to spell, the name that began with E and ended, Freya was suddenly certain, in *ith*—

No answer. Of course no answer. Only the radiators ticking, and outside the wiped-clean windows, the first snow of the season beginning to fall, straight down, unhurried, like something that had all the time in the world.

At lunchtime, Freya found Ivo in the boiler room and said, "Show me your cloth."

He looked at them for a long moment. Then he reached into his coat pocket and set it on the boiler's warm iron lip: a square of gray flannel, worn soft as skin, damp at the center.

It smelled, very faintly, of winter.
