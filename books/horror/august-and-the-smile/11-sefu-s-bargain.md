## 11. Sefu's Bargain

The invitation came on a Tuesday, folded into Freya's postbox like a moth with its wings pinned shut. Heavy cream paper, handwritten in ink the color of weak tea:

*You are expected at Halloway House, Thursday, four o'clock. Wear something warm. — S.H.*

No question mark anywhere. Not even in the word *expected*. Freya read it three times at the kitchen window while the frost on the glass spelled and unspelled itself in the corner of her vision, half-letters, half-hope, and understood that in Hollowmere an invitation was not a request. It was a summons with good manners.

She thought about not going.

She thought about it for exactly as long as it took to remember the twelfth desk, and the girl in it, and the way her own pen had moved across the register—*present*, she had written, *present*, the small clean corruption of it—and decided that whatever Sefu Halloway wanted to offer, Freya needed to hear it with her eyes open. You could not refuse a bargain you hadn't let yourself understand.

* * *

Halloway House sat at the top of Church Lane, the last house before the fields went white and featureless. It was older than the church, older than the school, built of stone the color of old bone, with windows so thick with condensation from the inside that the whole house seemed to be breathing. Smoke rose from its chimneys in a perfectly straight column, unbothered by wind that was bending the bare oaks double.

Sefu opened the door before Freya knocked.

She was smaller than Freya had imagined—everyone was smaller up close, in Hollowmere, as if the cold compressed people toward their centers—but she filled the doorway anyway, in the way that certain women fill any space they occupy. Eighty if she was a day, hands like tree roots, eyes dark and unhurried and absolutely without mercy. She wore wool the color of coal and a silver brooch shaped like a bell.

"Miss Oyelaran." She said the name carefully, the Oyelaran round and whole, no fumbling. "You've been eating. Good. Come in before the cold learns your name."

The hall of Halloway House was warm. Not boiler-warm, not Ivo's faithful fire-warm, but warm in a way that felt *administered*, like a drug in the bloodstream. Freya's cheeks stung as the blood came back into them. The walls were hung with photographs going back generations, and Freya caught herself cataloguing them the way she catalogued everything now—*prayer, documentation, evidence*—and noticed that in every photograph, in every decade, the same eleven or so children appeared at the edges of the frame. Never centered. Never quite in focus. Standing at the boundaries of the picture like guests who had not been told to leave.

"They don't come out in the photographs," Sefu said, watching her look. "They come out in other things." She didn't say what things. She led Freya through to a parlor where a tea service waited on a table under a lamp, and the tea was already poured, and Freya understood that Sefu had known to the minute when she would arrive.

"Sit."

Freya sat. The chair was deep and swallowed her and she hated how good it felt.

"You've been to the archive," Sefu said.

It wasn't a question either. Freya wrapped her hands around the teacup. "I've been to the archive."

"And you found the page about the bell. Eleven little bells for eleven little children. Very moving. Very true, as far as it goes." Sefu lifted her own cup and drank, and Freya noticed her hands did not tremble at all, which in a woman of that age, in a village like this, seemed less like health than like discipline. "As far as it goes," Sefu repeated. "The trouble with archives, Miss Oyelaran, is that they only keep what people were willing to write down. The truth is usually what got burned off the edge of the page."

"The ledger," Freya said. "The page after the bells."

"Mm." Sefu set down her cup with a click. "And what do you think was on it?"

Freya had thought about nothing else for a week. She had lain in her bed with the frost writing its half-names on the window and turned the burned page over in her mind like a stone. "I think," she said slowly, "that the bell wasn't melted down to mourn the children. I think it was melted down to *pay* for something. And I think the something is still here."

For the first time, something moved in Sefu's face. Not surprise. Recognition—the look of a woman hearing a pupil finally pronounce a word correctly.

"Yes," Sefu said. "That's near enough."

* * *

The tea was black and bitter and Freya drank it because refusing felt like a move in a game she didn't yet know the rules to, and Sefu talked, and what she said was this:

"In the hungry winter—1911, but the winter didn't care about our calendar—the fever came through the village. You'll have seen the graves. Forty-one of them, all inside four months. It was taking a family a week. Whole houses went dark. And the people here did what desperate people do. They didn't pray *for* anything. They prayed *to* something. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." Sefu's voice was flat and even, a woman reciting a recipe. "They opened a door. Not a door in a wall. A door in the *weather*. And they asked what was on the other side of it to come in and do for the fever what the fever was doing for them."

"And it did," Freya said.

"The fever stopped inside a fortnight." Sefu smiled, and it was the worst smile Freya had ever seen, because it was genuinely warm. "Cold kills fever. Everyone knows that. We simply knew it *better*. And the price—" She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "The price was the interest. Eleven children, taken over that winter, gentle as a bank draft. Not stolen. *Collected*. And the village, being still alive, being still grateful, did what sensible debtors do. They renegotiated. They made it formal. A covenant. A renewal. Signed, every generation, by those who keep the village."

"The Mothers' Circle."

"The Mothers' Circle," Sefu agreed, as if Freya had confirmed a dinner reservation.

"And the bell rings because—"

"Because a covenant is a contract, and a contract must be witnessed. The bell was the witness. We melted it into eleven small bells, one for each child, to *bind* the witness to the debt—so the thing we invited would always know its collateral by name." Sefu's eyes came up and fixed on Freya, and the warmth went out of the parlor by a degree. "The big bell ringing at night means the contract is being *read*. It means the renewal is coming due, Miss Oyelaran, and the reader is getting impatient."

"The twelfth desk," Freya said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"Ah." Sefu nodded slowly. "You've met her. Edith. Yes. That's the impatience. That's the pen being put in your hand before the paper is put under it." She leaned forward. "Now. Let us stop circling. You are a clever woman and I am an old one and the fire is warm and neither of us wants to waste the afternoon. Here is what I have to say to you."

* * *

What Sefu had to say was this:

"The Circle is nine women. It has been nine women since 1911. We sign. Our mothers signed. Our daughters will sign, if there are daughters, and if there is a village. We sign and the cold stays *polite*—you've heard that phrase, I think, from the Almeida boy—and polite cold is the difference between a village and a graveyard. We sign and our children live. We sign and the school stays open, the bus road stays open, the shops sell to you, the doctor comes when you call. We sign and everything in Hollowmere continues to work, because everything in Hollowmere *is* the covenant. The warmth. The bread. The road. The boiler. All of it is the contract wearing clothes."

She let that sit. Outside, the wind found the corner of the house and worried at it, patiently, like a dog with a bone.

"You are not being asked to believe anything," Sefu went on. "Belief is for churches and we burned most of ours a century ago. You are being asked to *sign*. A name. Your name, in the register, alongside ours. And in exchange—" she counted it off on root-hard fingers, "—belonging. Safety. A place here that no one can take from you. And Ivo."

Freya's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

Sefu watched her with those unhurried dark eyes. "Yes. I know about you and Ivo. The whole village knows; the village knows everything, it's the one advantage of a fishbowl. He is the last of his line and he has fed that boiler since he was nine years old, and he is tired, Miss Oyelaran, tired down to the rivets, and he will die at that furnace the way his father did and his grandmother before him, unless—" she spread her hands, "—unless the covenant is renewed by new blood. Unless someone outside the old families takes up the pen. Then the burden spreads. Then he might have a life. He might have *you*. He is too decent to ask you for it. I am not decent. I am the one who asks."

The tea had gone cold in Freya's cup. She set it down and heard the click of porcelain and thought: *this is how it's done. This is how it's always done. Not with knives. With tea.*

"And if I don't sign," Freya said.

Sefu's expression didn't change, but the room did. The lamp dimmed by a shade, or seemed to. The photographs on the wall seemed, for a moment, all to be facing Freya.

"Then you will be a guest in Hollowmere," Sefu said, "and not a resident. And you have seen how the village treats guests in winter. The road closes. The shop forgets your order. The classroom locks. And the cold—" she paused, and for one flickering moment, behind the matriarch, behind the keeper of the covenant, Freya saw something that looked almost like grief, or maybe just exhaustion, eighty years deep, "—the cold stops being polite. It learns your name. And then one day there will be a twelfth line in a register, and a warm chair, and a new teacher arriving on the last bus before the roads close, and she will be told never to teach after dark. And she won't listen either. They never listen. That's why the Circle is nine women and not ninety. The ones who listen are already sitting at this table."

"You're telling me refusal is suicide," Freya said.

"I'm telling you," Sefu said gently, "that in Hollowmere, refusal *feels* like suicide. Whether it is—that depends on things older than me. But I will tell you what I tell every daughter of this village when the pen is put in her hand: the cold does not hate you. It doesn't hate anyone. It is only *hungry*, the way winter is hungry, the way debt is hungry. And hunger does not distinguish between the guilty and the innocent. It distinguishes only between the fed and the unfed." She pushed the pen across the table. It was an old pen, bone-handled, and Freya recognized it suddenly from the archive photographs—the signing pen, the covenant pen, the pen that had been in the hands of nine women across a century. "The renewal is in ten days. The solstice. Think until then. That is a courtesy I am not obliged to extend."

Freya looked at the pen.

She thought of Edith's voice like snow settling. *Present.* She thought of eleven warm chairs. She thought of Ivo's hands, scarred and steady, feeding a fire that wasn't for warmth. She thought of the photographs, the children at the edges of every frame, waiting to be let back in.

She thought about how easy it would be. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that kept her awake for the rest of that winter: how *easy*. One signature. Warmth, safety, belonging, Ivo's hand in hers, a whole life arranged like a gift. All she had to do was agree that eleven children should stay in the cold so that she could stay out of it. All she had to do was become the village.

That was the trap, she understood now. Not the threat. The *offer*. Power never came for you with a knife. It came with tea, and it made refusal feel like stepping off a cliff, and it let you tell yourself, for the rest of your comfortable, complicit life, that you'd had no choice.

Freya stood up. Her legs were shaking. She made them hold.

"Thank you for the tea, Mrs. Halloway," she said. "I'll see you at the solstice."

She left the pen on the table.

Behind her, in the warm parlor of Halloway House, Sefu Halloway said nothing at all, which was the loudest thing anyone in Hollowmere had ever said to her.

* * *

The walk down Church Lane took four minutes in daylight and eleven that evening, because the wind had turned and was coming *down* the lane toward her, against the geography, and it carried something in it—faint, rhythmic, almost like a voice counting.

Freya let herself into her lodgings and found the frost on the window had finished a word for the first time. Not a half-name. A whole one. Written in the corner of the glass in letters of perfect, delicate lace, small as a signature:

*Ivo.*

She stood in the cold room with her coat still on and her heart going like a trapped bird, and understood that she had not been the only one offered a bargain that day.

And that the frost, whatever else it was, kept excellent records.
