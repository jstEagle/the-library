## 15. The Twelfth Pupil

I have been counting the nights since the teacher refused to sign.

I am good at counting. I was good at it in 1911, when Miss Aldridge made us do sums on slate because paper was for the war effort, and I am good at it now, when the nights are the only thing left to measure. Forty-one nights. Forty-one times the frost has carried me up the lane to the teacher's window, a little closer each time, the way you approach a horse you don't want to spook. Forty-one nights of standing in the dark with my breath making flowers on the glass, watching Freya Oyelaran not sleep.

They see me. I know they do. The first nights they pulled the blanket up like a shield, which was silly — blankets are for cold, and I am not cold, I am *before* cold, I am the thing cold is made from — but they looked at me, and looking is more than the village ever did. The village looks through me. The village has had a hundred years of practice.

Tonight I am close enough to touch the glass. I don't touch it. Touching leaves marks, and marks frighten people, and I have spent a century learning the difference between being seen and being feared. They are not the same. Nobody ever told the village that.

The teacher's lamp is on. It is always on now. They sit at the little table with the 1911 register open in front of them, and they have been reading it the way I once watched my father read the Bible — not for the words but for what's underneath the words, the way you read a letter from someone who is already dead.

My name is the first one on the list.

*Edith Halloway.*

Sefu Halloway's great-aunt, though she has never said so aloud, and I have stood in her kitchen on winter nights long enough to know she keeps my photograph turned face-down in the drawer with the tea things. Not out of shame. Out of the same instinct that makes you not look down when you're on a high ledge. I am the ledge.

The teacher looks up. Looks at the window. And this time — this time, after forty-one nights — they do not look away.

They stand. They cross the small room. They open the window, and the cold pours in like water finding its level, and Freya Oyelaran, who has been afraid of me since the first night and afraid of the village since the first morning, says, in a voice that shakes only a little:

"You'd better come in. You're letting the cold out."

I almost laugh. A hundred years, and the first thing anyone says to me is a joke about draughts.

I come in. I sit at the table, because sitting at a table is what I know how to do. My chair does not creak, because I weigh what snow weighs, which is nothing and everything. The register lies open between us, and I do not look at it, because looking at it is like looking at the sun through a hole in your hand.

"Edith," the teacher says. Not a question. They read it off the page, but they say it like a name, not an entry, and I have to look down at my hands for a moment, at the frost blooming and fading in the whorls of my fingers.

"You say it right," I tell them. "Most people say it like a fact. You said it like a person."

"I've been saying it to myself for weeks." Freya's breath fogs. The room is going white at the edges, frost climbing the inside of the window in slow ferns, and they don't seem to mind, or they've decided not to mind, which is braver. "The register. I've had it since autumn. Eleven names. I've read them a hundred times in my head. Why does it matter how they're said?"

"Everything here is about how things are said." I fold my hands on the table, a schoolgirl's posture, muscle memory from a body that melted into the ground in the spring of 1912 when they finally dug deep enough. "That's the whole trick of this place. The covenant, the renewal, the bell. It's all words. Invitations are words. Debts are words. The village made a bargain in sentences, and sentences can be unmade in sentences, and they have known that for a hundred years and they have chosen not to know it."

Freya goes very still. Teachers do that — I remember it from Miss Aldridge, the way they stop moving when the lesson arrives, as if stillness could hold the moment open. "Unmade how?"

"You already know." I nod at the register. "You've known since the night you found the burned ledger. You've known since the children came back and sat down and waited for you to teach us. We weren't haunting you, Miss Oyelaran. We were *enrolling*."

* * *

Let me tell it properly, because no one has ever asked me properly, and the dead are owed their testimony the same as the living.

In February of 1911 the fever came up the valley with the thaw that never finished thawing. Eleven of us took it in the space of three weeks, and the doctor was a day's ride away and the roads were snowed shut, and the village did what villages do, which is everything wrong for the right reasons. There was a woman then — Sefu's grandmother, though the Circle has rewritten even that — who knew the old words, the ones from before the church, from when the cold was a neighbor and not a nuisance. And she said: the fever is a fire in the blood, and there is a fire older than blood, and one fire can put out another if you invite it in.

So they invited it. The Frost. Not the weather — the weather is just the Frost's coat, hanging on a hook. The thing itself. They opened the schoolhouse door at midnight and read the invitation aloud, all of them, every soul in Hollowmere, and it came in politely, the way it does everything, and it put the fever out like a thumb and finger pinching a wick.

And then it looked around the warm room at all the living people and said, in the way frost says things, which is not in words: *interest.*

Eleven of us were already sick. We were the cheapest coin in the room. It took us that night, gently — I remember it gently, I want that written down somewhere, I remember the cold coming into the fever and the fever going and both of them feeling like the same mercy — and it kept us. Not our bodies. Our *names.* It wrote us into its own register, and it has held us at the threshold ever since, the way you hold a pawn ticket. Conscious. Waiting. Watching our village renew the lease every generation because the alternative is to let it roam, and a roaming Frost takes more than eleven.

"We're collateral," I say to Freya. "That's the word Ivo's grandfather used, when he was dying and talkative. Collateral against the debt. As long as it holds us, it holds something of itself back. It can't take the whole village while it's holding a piece of it. We're the reason Hollowmere is merely cold instead of dead."

Freya's pen is in their hand. They've been writing since I started, and I watch the pen move, and something in my chest — the place where a chest used to be — aches at the sight of it. "The children who come to the classroom. The eleven desks."

"Us. On the nights the Frost lets us off the leash, which is the nights it wants something. It parades us. It's showing you its collateral, the way a lender does. Look how valuable. Look how well kept. Sign the renewal and they stay comfortable." I look at the window, at the ferns of frost, and I let myself be angry for one breath, because I have earned one breath of it a century. "We are not comfortable. We are *kept.* There's a difference and it's the width of a grave."

"And the twelfth desk." Freya's voice has gone careful. "The girl who answers roll."

"Me. When it wants me to be the twelfth, I'm the twelfth. When it wants me at my own desk, I'm at my own desk. It likes me at the twelfth desk. It likes the arithmetic of it. Eleven taken, one returned, and the returned one is still its own — it thinks it's clever with numbers." I unfold my hands, fold them again. "It is not clever with numbers. It has never once counted the cost of being counted."

Freya looks up sharply at that. Good. They're quick. They've been quick since the bus, I think — the village says so, says it like an insult, *the new teacher asks too many questions* — and I have watched them turn their quickness into a kind of prayer, documenting everything, photographing the frost, writing it all down. It failed them, the photographs. Of course it failed. The Frost is older than silver and chemicals; it does not consent to be evidence.

But a voice. A voice is different.

"Read the register aloud," Freya says. Not asking. Testing the sentence in their mouth like a coin bitten for gold. "That's what you came to tell me. Forty-one nights at my window to tell me to read a list of names."

"To read it *properly.*" I lean forward, and the lamp flame leans with me, and I make myself gentle, because what I say next is the heaviest thing in this room and I will not have it dropped. "Not to yourself. Not silently, not skimming, not for the archive. Aloud. Each name, spoken whole, in the room where we were taken, and meant — meant the way you mean a prayer, the way you mean a summons. Every name you speak, it loses its hold on. A name read back into the world is a name it no longer owns. That's the flaw in its arithmetic. It took our names as payment, but names are the one currency that can be *withdrawn* by the person who spends them. Or spent on their behalf." I nod at the register, at the eleven names in Miss Aldridge's fading copperplate. "You'd be spending them for us."

The frost on the window has stopped climbing. The whole room is listening. I can feel it at the threshold of the door and under the floor and in the lamp's small trembling light, and I speak a little faster.

"And in exchange it voids the invitation. All of it. The covenant, the renewal, the whole hundred years of polite cold. The debt is called in and the collateral is released and the Frost has nothing left to hold it at the threshold, so the threshold stops meaning anything. It goes. Or it doesn't go, and it takes the village in a single night instead of a single century — no one knows which, not even it, I think. It has never been refused before. Refusal is the one experiment Hollowmere has never run."

Freya is quiet for a long moment. The lamp hisses. Somewhere down the lane a dog is barking at nothing, at the thing under the nothing.

"And the reader," Freya says at last. "The register. What happens to the person who reads it?"

I could lie. I have had a hundred years to get good at lying and I have practiced none of it, because the dead have no one to lie to. And there is something in this teacher's face — the same something that made them not sign at the renewal, that made them stand in the snow at Ivo's boiler shed and ask *why* until Ivo nearly wept — that deserves the truth the way the ground deserves the seed.

"The Frost will want a name in return," I say. "Eleven for one is a bargain from its side. It will demand the reader's name, spoken into the pattern, and a name given is a person taken, and the person taken is kept the way we were kept — or worse, because it will be *angry*, and I have seen it angry once, in 1947, when a Halloway boy tried to burn the ledger, and what it did to the east field has never grown right since."

Freya closes the pen. Sets it down. Looks at the register with an expression I recognize, because I wore it in 1911, on a slate, doing sums: the look of someone who has finally got the answer and wishes the question had been different.

"So it's the eleven of you, or it's me."

"It's the eleven of us and possibly you." I make my voice as kind as frost can be, and frost can be very kind; it was kind to me at the end and I have never forgotten it. "Or it's the eleven of us and no one, and Hollowmere keeps its bargain and keeps its silence and keeps us, forever, and in a hundred years there is another teacher at this window and another girl at the glass, and you will be a photograph turned face-down in a drawer. That is the other arithmetic. The one the village has always chosen."

Freya stands. Walks to the window. The frost ferns part around their breath like a crowd.

"Edith Halloway," they say — my name again, said like a person, said like a key turning — "how long do we have?"

"The solstice," I say. "The longest night. It will finish its signature then — the pattern it's been writing on every window in the village since you refused to sign. When the pattern is complete, the covenant closes itself, and there is no reading anything aloud ever again, because there will be no one left in Hollowmere with a voice the cold hasn't kept. You have until the sun turns. Four nights."

Freya turns from the window, and their face is terrible and calm, and I think: *this is the one. This is the one who will do it.*

Then the lamp gutters, and the frost on the window writes a single word — not a name, not yet, just a warning in the old angular script, and I know it, I know its handwriting the way you know your jailer's boots on the stair — and the word is *BOILER.*

And down the lane, in the shed where four generations of Ivo's family have fed the fire that keeps the thing at the threshold, the light goes out.
