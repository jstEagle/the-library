## 16. Ivo's Choice

The boiler had a voice. Four generations of Almeidas had learned to listen to it the way farmers listen to weather—by feel, by habit, by the small grammar of knocks and sighs that meant *all is well* or *all is not*. Ivo's grandfather had called it the old man in the cellar. His father had called it nothing at all, because his father did not believe in naming things you intended to serve until you died.

Ivo crouched beside it on the last night of its life and put his palm flat against the iron.

It was warm. It was always warm. That was the point of it, and also the lie.

"You know what I'm going to do," he said.

The boiler ticked. Steam moved through pipes that ran under the schoolhouse floor and out beneath the village square, a network older than any map in the parish archive, older than the church that no longer had a bell. His great-grandfather had laid those pipes—or so the story went—with hands cracked from frostbite, working through the hungry winter while the fever took the children one by one and something else stood at the edge of the lamplight, patient as snowfall, waiting to be asked in.

The fire wasn't for warmth. Freya had guessed that weeks ago; he'd seen it in her face when she finally understood what he was telling her, standing in the boiler room with coal dust on her sleeves she'd never bothered to brush off. The fire was a fence. The fire was the word *no*, spoken continuously, in the only language the Frost respected. Every shovelful was a sentence in a conversation his family had been having for a hundred and thirteen years without once stopping to breathe.

And tonight he was going to stop breathing.

He took his hand off the iron. It left a print in the dust, five fingers, already fading.

* * *

He had told her plainly, because he was not a man who could do things any other way. It was the fourth of December and the light had gone out of the sky by three o'clock, and they had stood in the doorway of the schoolhouse where eleven desks sat empty and one desk—the twelfth—sat emptier still, since Edith had stopped coming to the window and started coming to the glass, closer each night, her breath flowering on the pane like a signature.

"I can let it die," he'd said. "The boiler. If I stop feeding it, the Frost comes all the way to the threshold instead of waiting at the gate. The village will panic. They'll come for you faster, or they'll come begging. There's no way to know which."

"And you?" Freya had said. "What happens to you?"

"Nothing happens to me. That's the curse of it. Almeidas don't freeze. We just keep the fire." He had almost laughed. "My father said it like a boast. Took me thirty years to hear it as a sentence."

Freya had looked at him then with an expression he couldn't file anywhere—not pity, not admiration, something rawer—and said, "Ivo, if you do this, there's no taking it back."

"That's rather the idea."

Now, alone in the cellar, he found he wanted to say more than he'd said. He wanted to say it out loud, to the boiler, to the dark, to the man his father had been and the boy he himself had been at nine years old, learning to shovel coal before he'd learned long division. He wanted to say: *I am tired. I am forty-one years old and I have never slept a whole night through, because somewhere in my body there is a clock that wakes me every four hours to check the gauge, and I did not choose this clock, it was installed in me the way the pipes were installed under the village, and I am done.*

He didn't say any of it. He just banked the fire low—one last act of tenderness toward the machine, because even betrayals deserve courtesy—and climbed the stairs, and did not lock the boiler room door behind him, because locking it would be a kindness to his own resolve and he deserved none.

* * *

The cold came honestly at first, the way cold does when it has permission. By midnight the temperature in the schoolhouse had dropped six degrees. By two it had dropped twelve. Ivo sat in his cottage across the lane with his coat on and his boots laced, watching the thermometer he'd nailed to the porch post thirty years ago, and felt the strangest sensation of his life, which was relief.

Not peace. Relief. The way a man feels setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying because he'd been carrying it since before he could walk.

At 2:40 the frost began.

He saw it first on his own windows—not the ordinary ferning of a hard night but the deliberate geometry underneath it, the pattern beneath the pattern, lines straightening themselves out of the chaos like soldiers falling into formation. He had watched this happen at the edges of things his whole life: the corners of cellars, the far side of mirrors, the inside of the boiler room door where no breath could reach. The Frost wrote when it was close. It had been writing for a hundred years and nobody in Hollowmere had ever let it finish a sentence.

Tonight it had room.

By three the patterns covered his windows entirely, and they were not half-formed anymore. He recognized letters now—had recognized them since Freya showed him the photographs that refused to develop, since Edith's name bloomed on the classroom glass in strokes fine as hair. The Frost was spelling something across the whole of his cottage, and though he could read only fragments, the fragments were enough: a name, repeated. A date. And beneath them, in characters larger than the rest, a word he chose not to translate, because some invitations should remain grammatically ambiguous as long as possible.

Across the lane, the schoolhouse windows were lighting up too. Not electric light. A pale glow from within, like moonlight stored in glass, and behind it—shapes. Small ones. Seated.

They're back, he thought. All eleven. She's reading to them.

Or she will be. Tomorrow. At midnight. On the longest night, when the covenant came due and the village gathered to sign its renewal and one teacher from away had promised, quietly, over tea neither of them drank, to stand up in front of God and Sefu Halloway and everyone and refuse.

His job—his only remaining job, the one he'd assigned himself the day he understood what she meant to do—was to make sure the Frost didn't take the schoolhouse before she got there.

* * *

The screaming started around four.

It came from the direction of the square, thin and human and multiplied, and Ivo was moving before he'd decided to move, boots on frozen ground, breath tearing white behind him. The village had woken to find its world rewritten. Frost on every window, yes—but also frost indoors, climbing wallpaper, feathering across table tops, tracing the rims of sleeping children's beds. The pipes that ran warm under the square for a century had gone cold as drainpipes. In the Halloway house, old Sefu stood in her kitchen watching ice build on the inside of the kettle.

They were all in the street. Dozens of them, wrapped in blankets, holding lamps that guttered, and every face turned when Ivo came running, because every face knew whose fault this was.

"The boiler," someone said. Bram Teller, red-faced, furious, his voice cracking on the second word. "The boiler's dead, Ivo. The school's freezing. The whole line's freezing. What have you *done*?"

Ivo stopped in the middle of the square. The lamps made a ring around him, and beyond the ring the darkness was pressing in like water against a dam, and he understood suddenly that this was the moment his family had dreaded for four generations—not the failure of the fire, but the accounting after.

"I let it go out," he said.

The silence lasted three seconds. Then the square erupted.

He let it wash over him. Coward, traitor, madman—the words arrived in no particular order and he declined to rank them. Sefu Halloway pushed through the crowd with the unhurried authority of a woman who had signed her name to something terrible and slept well afterward, and stood in front of him, and looked at him the way you look at a door that used to be a wall.

"You know what's coming now," she said. Not a question. Her breath hung between them, and where it hung, frost formed—instantly, unnaturally, little crystals spinning in the air like the village's hundred-year secret made visible.

"Yes," Ivo said.

"Then you know we'll sign without you. We'll renew tonight, early, all of us, and the thing will be invited deeper than it ever was, and your name—" she leaned close, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle, which was worse than shouting, "—your name will be first on the new register."

"It isn't on any register," Ivo said. "That's the point. None of ours are. Only theirs. Eleven names from 1911, held hostage so we'd keep paying rent on our own lives." He raised his voice then, let it carry past the lamps into the dark, because there was one listener he actually wanted to reach. "You want to know what we've been doing all this time? We haven't been keeping the cold out. We've been keeping the *children* in. The fire doesn't protect us. It imprisons them. It has always imprisoned them."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere a baby cried and was hushed too quickly.

Sefu's face went through several weathers. "You've been talking to the Oyelaran woman."

"I've been talking to the truth. It took longer to get an appointment."

A few people laughed—a short, shocked, involuntary sound, immediately strangled. Ivo would remember that later: that even on the worst night of the village's history, somebody laughed at him, and that it sounded like hope trying to remember how.

"Go home, Ivo," Sefu said. "Light your stove. Pray. Tomorrow we fix what you've broken, and if the Oyelaran woman is wise, she'll be on whatever road is still open by dawn."

"She won't," Ivo said. "Neither will I."

He turned and walked out of the ring of lamps, and the cold closed behind him like a hand.

* * *

He did not go home. He went to the schoolhouse.

The door was locked—he'd locked it himself, days ago, on the village's orders, part of the siege—but locks had never been the point of doors in Hollowmere, and this one opened at his touch as if it had only ever been waiting for permission. Inside, the cold was total and alive. His lamp flame bent sideways, toward the classroom, as if being pulled.

Every window blazed with frost. The patterns had finished themselves here first; he could see that now. Whole sentences, whole paragraphs, written in a hand older than handwriting, covering the glass floor to lintel. And in the center of the room, faint as breath on winter air, the shapes of the eleven sat at their desks, more solid than he had ever seen them, their outlines sharpened by the very cold that held them.

At the twelfth desk, a girl turned her head.

Edith. Pale as paper, punctual as grief. She looked at Ivo with eyes that had been nine years old for a hundred and thirteen years, and she did not speak, and she did not need to. The question was in the room itself, in the pressure of the dark against the walls:

*Why are you here?*

"To keep watch," Ivo said aloud. His voice sounded enormous and absurd. "Until she comes. Until midnight. Nobody gets to this room before she reads those names. Nobody. Not the Frost, not the village, not Sefu Halloway with a pen in her hand and a renewal in her pocket." He set his lamp on his old caretaker's desk by the door, the desk his father had used, and his grandfather, and sat down in the chair behind it like a man taking up a post he should have occupied decades ago. "Four generations of Almeidas kept the fire so the debt wouldn't come due. Well. It's due. And I'm the collateral now, and I say we pay it differently."

Edith watched him. Slowly—so slowly he might have imagined it—the frost on the nearest window began to recede from the frame of the door, clearing a path, or perhaps simply making way.

Outside, the bell rang.

Once. Soft. Impossible—a bell that had been slag in a scrapyard since before his grandfather was born—and the sound of it went through the schoolhouse like a finger drawn down a spine, and every pattern on every window trembled, and in the square he heard the villagers cry out, all of them at once, a single animal sound of a people realizing the lease was up.

Ivo put his hands flat on the desk and waited.

Midnight was nineteen hours away. The cold was at the threshold. Freya Oyelaran was somewhere across the village with a register full of names and a promise she intended to keep, and the Frost was writing its final signature across the town, letter by letter, window by window, and Ivo Almeida—last of his line, breaker of boilers, traitor to a hundred years of polite silence—sat down to hold the door.

He thought about what he would say to her when she came. He rehearsed it in the dark, the plain words, the true ones, the ones he'd carried since autumn: *I let the fire die for you. Not for the village. Not for the children, even. For you, because you looked at me like I was more than a man who feeds a furnace, and I would rather burn once, freely, than smolder forever on schedule.*

He'd say it at midnight. If there was a midnight. If there was a her.

The frost on the doorframe crept a little closer, patient as snowfall, and began, very gently, to spell his name.
