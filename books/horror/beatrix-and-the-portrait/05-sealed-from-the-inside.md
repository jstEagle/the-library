## 5. Sealed From the Inside

The door was open.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was the last thing I had left that week. On Tuesday the door had been shut — oak, iron-banded, and sealed in a way that made no sense to anyone with a working knowledge of how doors work. The hinges were on the inside. The latch was on the inside. There was no handle on our side at all, just a flat plane of wood gone black with fifty years of soil, sitting upright in a trench at the bottom of an excavation like it had been buried standing up, waiting for someone to knock.

Nobody had knocked. Nobody had needed to. Marisol had touched it once — I'd watched her do it, palm flat against the grain, the way you'd touch a horse you didn't trust — and the ground had exhaled around us, a long sigh of warm air that smelled like birthday cake going stale. And then every one of us had heard our own name come up out of the dirt. Not shouted. Not whispered. *Spoken*, the way your mother says your name when she's found something you broke.

We'd called it a day after that. Danny had called it several days, actually, from the parking lot, in language I won't reproduce here.

But Wednesday morning we came back, because that's what crews do — they grumble, they drink bad coffee, and they come back — and the door stood open like a held breath finally let go.

"It wasn't us," Danny said immediately, hands up, as if the door were a cop and he was drunk. "I want it on record. Nobody opened anything."

"Record's noted," Marisol said. She was already walking toward it, which was very Marisol. Twenty-two years running excavation crews and she had exactly two speeds: thorough and faster-than-thorough.

"Marisol." I said her name the way you say *stop* without wanting to spend the word. "Let's look first."

"Look at what? It's a door, Theo. It's either a door or it's a hole, and both of those are my department."

She was right, which was the problem. Everything about this job was technically my department — appraisal, survey, the whole paper trail — but the ground itself belonged to her, and I'd learned a long time ago not to argue jurisdiction with a woman who owned a backhoe.

Behind the door there was a stairway.

That's the sentence I keep trying to make boring, and it keeps refusing. Behind the door there was a stairway: poured concrete, handrail intact, descending into dark at an angle that suggested a basement. Except the Aldergate lot had no basement. I had the drawings. I had the 1961 permit file, the soil composition report, the utility maps. The ground beneath this house was dense clay and glacial till down to bedrock, forty feet of it, geology so uncooperative the original builder had given up on any foundation deeper than six feet. There was no room under this lot for a stairway the way there is no room inside a brick for a hallway.

And yet.

Danny's flashlight went down the stairs and came back wrong. That's the only way I can put it. The beam should have hit concrete at twelve feet; instead it kept going, and going, and the light got softer as it went, like it was falling into wool, and somewhere down there it illuminated what looked unmistakably like wallpaper. A floral pattern. Faded rose.

"Basements don't have wallpaper," Danny said.

"This isn't a basement," Marisol said, and clipped her radio to her collar, and started down the stairs.

* * *

I've replayed the next four minutes maybe nine hundred times since, and every version ends the same way, because the ending isn't negotiable. In some versions I grab her arm. In some versions I go first. In all of them, she goes anyway, because Marisol Reyes does not take orders from men who file paperwork for a living, and because — I believe this now, though I didn't have the vocabulary for it then — the house wanted her, and the house had been practicing patience for half a century.

She radioed down at intervals. Professional habit. "Ten feet, solid footing. Twenty feet, walls finished, drywall, somebody hung pictures down here. Thirty—" Static. Then, clear as a phone call: "Theo, you're not going to believe the square footage."

Then nothing.

We gave it five minutes. Then ten. Danny shouted down the stairwell and his voice came back to us doubled, once in his own pitch and once in a higher register, a girl's register, echoing him a half-second late like a translation nobody asked for.

At minute fourteen, her radio crackled on by itself.

It was playing a lullaby.

Not a recording of one — or if a recording, then one pressed through decades of dust, warped and warbling, a music box melody with a woman humming underneath it, off-key, tender, tired. It drifted up the stairwell and out across the trench where the three of us stood holding shovels like weapons, and I understood, with the calm certainty that arrives right before everything stops being calm, that the radio was not playing *to* us.

It was playing *for* her. Down there. Wherever she was.

"Marisol!" I yelled it down the stairs until my throat tore. "MARISOL REYES!"

The lullaby paused. Just for a moment. Long enough for a voice — small, polite, terribly young — to say, from thirty feet below the clay of a lot I had appraised at $340,000:

"He's sleeping. You'll wake him."

Then the lullaby resumed, and the door at the top of the stairs swung shut on its own, soft as a bedroom door at naptime, and the hinges — I checked, I put my fingers on them with shaking hands — the hinges were on the outside now.

* * *

We sat in Danny's truck for an hour. Nobody spoke much. The engine ticked. Somewhere in that hour I did the thing I do, the thing that has kept me employed and sane and alone for most of my adult life: I reached for the rational explanation and found the shelf empty.

Here is what I had. A missing girl, 1974, harvest moon, case cold before I was born. A portrait withdrawn from the county archive by the victim's own family, two days after she vanished. A door hinged from the inside, buried upright in clay, now open, now closed, its hardware migrating like birds. A crew member with twenty-two years of experience and a daughter in college, gone down a staircase that cannot exist, leaving behind a radio playing songs for children.

Here is what I did not have: any explanation that would survive contact with a police report.

"We call the cops," Danny said finally. "Right now. State police, county, whoever. People dig people out of holes all the time. There's protocols."

"There are," I agreed. "And the protocol starts with 'describe the entrance,' and I describe a door that opens inward into forty feet of solid clay, and then what?"

"Then they think we're insane."

"They'll think worse than that." I was looking at the trench through the windshield. The door sat at the bottom of it, ordinary as anything, and I hated it with a feeling that had stopped being fear and become something more personal. "They'll think we did something to her. Two guys, a missing woman, a story about haunted real estate. Danny, I'm the one who hired her onto this site. My name is on the permit."

He was quiet for a while. Then he said, quietly, the way you say things when you've been thinking them longer than you want to admit: "You heard what it said. *He's sleeping.* Like there's somebody down there. Like it's got — company."

"I heard."

"You've been reading those files. The Aldergate girl. Beatrix." He said the name carefully, like it might answer. "You think that's her down there. Fifty years, and she's still—"

"Don't."

"Why not? Somebody has to say it." He turned to face me, and he was scared, genuinely scared, but underneath the fear was the thing I liked best about Danny Okafor, which is that he runs toward the truth even when it's ugly. "Because here's what's eating me, Theo. We opened it. Us. Marisol put her hand on that door and the whole yard breathed, and whatever was waiting down there — it was waiting for *someone*. And we showed up with a backhoe and a work order and we dug it free. So it's not the cops' problem. It's ours. We opened it."

I wanted to argue. God, I wanted to argue — I had spent my whole life arguing, professionally, with tape measures and zoning codes and appraisers who fudged comparables, and argument was the tool I used on every problem, the crowbar you slide under reality to pry it open and see what's true.

But there was no crowbar for this. Because he was right.

The house had taken Marisol the way it took Beatrix Aldergate. That was the sentence forming in me, cold and complete, while Danny talked. It keeps what it loves. Mrs. Okonkwo hadn't said those words yet — that came later, over tea I never drank — but I already knew their shape, because I'd seen the house's taste in the files: it loved a nine-year-old girl enough to eat her, and it loved her family enough to help them hide the crime, and now it had met a crew of strangers and decided, within a week, whom it wanted to keep.

It had chosen Marisol. Thorough, faster-than-thorough Marisol, who touched doors without knocking.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. Here's what happens tonight. Nobody goes near that trench. Nobody works alone. Tomorrow morning I drive to the county archive and I pull every document that exists on this property, and I find out what's down there and why it wants us. And then—" I stopped, because the *and then* was a cliff edge. "And then we close it. Whatever it takes. We opened it, so it's ours to close."

Danny nodded slowly. Then he frowned and picked up his camera from the seat between us — he'd been shooting interior photos all week, documentation for the appraisal — and thumbed through the roll to check his battery, and stopped.

"Theo," he said. His voice had gone strange. "When did you take these?"

"What?"

"There's — hold on." He scrolled. Scrolled again. Turned the screen toward me.

Every frame was the same room — the front parlor, shot from different angles, Tuesday afternoon, timestamps in neat digital orange. And in every single frame, standing in the corner behind the spot where Danny must have been standing when he pressed the shutter, was a girl.

Nine years old. White dress. Hair combed flat. Hands folded.

In the last photo, timestamped 4:47 p.m., she was looking directly into the lens.

And she was smiling the way you smile when someone you've been waiting for finally, finally walks through the door.
