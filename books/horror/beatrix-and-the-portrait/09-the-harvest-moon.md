## 9. The Harvest Moon

The moon came up over the county records office like a coin someone had flipped and never bothered to catch.

I was doing sixty through a residential zone, which for me is the moral equivalent of kicking a nun. I'm the guy who reads the terms of service. I'm the guy who returns the extra change. And here I was, running a red light with a county vehicle and a head full of a nine-year-old girl asking me why love always looked like a locked door.

The archive closed at five. It was 6:40. That was fine, because I wasn't planning on using the front door. I was planning on using something worse: my credentials.

Here's the thing about appraisers that nobody outside the business knows. We have access. Not glamorous access — nobody writes thrillers about the county assessor's shared filing system — but access. Every property record, every deed, every estate inventory, every item of seized or surrendered personal property tied to a parcel. And the drawer where Beatrix Aldergate's portrait had been sleeping since 1974 was catalogued, I'd learned that afternoon, under *Aldergate Estate — Contents, Unclassified, Restricted*. Restricted meant a key card. Key cards meant the county had issued me one eleven days ago with a smile and a lanyard.

They'd handed me the key to her cage and called it onboarding.

I parked on the grass, because the lot was full and I'd stopped caring about tire marks. The building was the usual municipal brutalism — poured concrete, narrow windows, the architectural conviction that nothing inside should ever be comfortable or, ideally, seen. The harvest moon sat fat and orange behind it, and the light coming off it was the color of something healing wrong.

My card opened the side entrance. The hallway lights were on timers and gave up on me twice; I walked the middle stretch in the dark, past the break room, past the microfilm cabinets, down to Records Storage B, where the air was cold and smelled like paper slowly becoming dust.

The drawer was steel, waist-high, third bank from the left. The label read ALDERGATE in someone's faded, apologetic handwriting, as if even the typist had wanted to be somewhere else.

My card beeped. The lock thought about it. For one horrible second I imagined it refusing — the house reaching all the way out here, into the county, into the drawer, holding shut what it loved — and I felt my stomach drop the way it does when a measurement comes out different the second time.

Then the drawer slid open with a squeal, and there it was.

I'd seen the photograph in the file. This wasn't the photograph. This was the portrait — oil on canvas, maybe sixteen by twenty, small enough to hold like a grievance. Beatrix Aldergate, age eight or nine, in a yellow dress, sitting very straight with her hands folded in her lap the way children do when they've been told to. The painter had been good. Too good. He'd caught the thing in her eyes that adults spend their whole lives learning to hide, which is the understanding that the people smiling at you are deciding something about you right now.

The frame was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not radiator-warm. Warm like a hand you've been holding. I stood in the cold storage room with a dead girl's portrait warming my palms and I thought, *fifty years, and she's still waiting for someone to pick her up.*

"Okay, Beatrix," I said, out loud, because the dark seemed to want it. "Okay. I've got you. And then I let you go. That's the deal. That's the whole deal."

The portrait didn't answer. But the warmth pulsed once, gentle, like something settling, and I chose — deliberately, the way you choose to believe a tape measure — to read that as *yes.*

* * *

I broke three traffic laws on the way back and invented a fourth.

The house saw me coming. I knew it before I turned onto Aldergate Road, because the streetlights went out one at a time ahead of me, like the dark was rolling out a carpet, and the moon — which had been behind me the whole drive — was suddenly in my windshield, huge, directly over the lot, close enough that I could see the seas on it like bruises.

The house was sealing.

I don't know how else to describe it. The front door was gone — not locked, not boarded, *absent*, the doorway filled with a smooth gray surface like skin grown over a wound. The windows were going the same way, one by one, the glass clouding and thickening. The siding had the look of flesh that's decided to keep something in. Danny's truck was still in the driveway, driver's side door hanging open, and Danny himself was standing in the yard throwing gravel at the front door and screaming my name, and the gravel was bouncing off nothing, off the smooth gray nothing, and the house didn't even have the courtesy to make a sound.

"Theo!" He saw me and his whole body changed, relief and fury fighting for the same muscles. "Theo, it started ten minutes ago — Marisol's in there, the stairway's closing, I heard it — Theo, it's *eating the stairs*—"

"Then we go in now." I was already out of the car, portrait under my arm, a can of lighter fluid and Danny's road flares from the crew kit in a plastic bag. "Where's the opening?"

"Side wall. It opened the side wall when it heard your car — Theo, why would it *open* for you—"

Because it wanted the portrait. Because the house had spent fifty years keeping what it loved, and the one thing it loved more than any of us was the girl in the yellow dress, and I was carrying her home. The house wasn't sealing us out. It was sealing us *in*, with her, forever, the way it had sealed in everyone it ever wanted.

It had misunderstood the assignment. It had misunderstood everything.

"Stay behind me," I said, which was stupid, because I had a painting and he had a flashlight, but he did it anyway, and I've thought about that since. Loyalty weighs more than truth. Danny had told me that, back when we were arguing about calling the police, and I'd called it cowardice. Standing at the wound in the side wall, with the moon coming down like a verdict, I finally understood it was neither. It was just what people do for each other. The house never learned it. That was the whole horror of the place, distilled: it knew how to keep, and it never learned how to stay *for* someone, only to keep someone *from* leaving.

The wall opened as we reached it. Of course it did. It wanted us in.

We went in anyway. There's a difference, and I held onto it like a handrail all the way down.

* * *

The stairway was still there — half of it. The lower steps had gone soft and gray, dissolving upward like a sugar cube in slow water, and the rooms below were dimming, the 1974 light — that warm, amber, home-movie light — guttering at the edges like a film burning in the projector. Somewhere down there a girl's voice was counting. *Forty-one, forty-two, forty-three.* She'd been counting to keep from crying for fifty years, and she was at forty-three, and she would have been at forty-three forever.

We ran. The stairs held under us out of what I can only assume was courtesy, or hunger, or both.

The heart of the house was the room where it had all happened. I knew it the way you know a room from a nightmare you've never had: low ceiling, no windows, walls that breathed. Marisol lay on the floor in the center, on her side, knees drawn up, asleep in the way that isn't sleep. Her face was smooth. Too smooth. Fifty years of memory had been sanded off her, and what was left was a face with nothing behind it, a face the house was keeping the way it kept everything — perfectly, and therefore dead.

And Beatrix was there.

She stood at the edge of the amber light, nine years old, in the yellow dress from the portrait, and she was also not nine years old, and I don't have the language for it, so I won't pretend. Her eyes were the same. That was the unbearable part. Eight years old in the painting, nine years old in 1974, fifty-some years old in the dark, and the eyes never changed: the eyes of a child waiting to be told what was going to happen to her.

"You came back," she said.

"I came back." My voice cracked on the second word and I let it. "Beatrix. I need you to listen, because I'm only going to say this once, and it's the most important thing anyone's ever said to you, and everyone who should have said it is dead now, so it's me. It's a stranger with a road flare. I'm sorry about that."

She tilted her head. The house around us held its breath. I could feel it — the walls leaning in, the ceiling lowering a half inch, the whole structure straining to hear, to keep, to make this moment last forever.

"They took you," I said. "Your parents. It was 1974, the harvest moon, and your mother was dead, and your father couldn't stand it, and they decided — they *decided*, Beatrix, it was a choice, it was always a choice — that keeping you was the same thing as loving you. It isn't. It never was. They were wrong. It was cruel, and it was selfish, and it was wrong, and you did not deserve it, and there was never anything wrong with you that needed keeping."

The counting had stopped.

"They hid your picture so nobody could ever let you out. Fifty years. And this house learned the lesson too well. It keeps things because keeping is all it was ever taught." I knelt, and set the portrait on the floor between us, and poured the lighter fluid over it in a slow figure eight, and my hands were shaking and I didn't try to hide that either. "So here's the truth, and then here's the door, and you can do whatever you want with both."

I struck the flare.

"I'm not going to keep you," I said. "Nobody is ever going to keep you again. You're free to go."

The portrait caught with a sound like a gasp. Fire ran up the yellow dress in the painting, and the painted girl's folded hands seemed to open — I know what fire does to oil paint, I know about blistering and darkening, and I am telling you her hands *opened* — and Beatrix made a sound I will hear until I die, a sound like fifty years of held breath leaving a small body all at once.

The house screamed.

Not through the walls. The walls *were* the scream — every surface at once, the whole structure howling as the thing it had loved most for fifty years walked out of its hands. The amber light died. The breathing stopped. Marisol's eyes flew open and she sat up screaming her mother's name, her mother's actual face, remembered, returned, and Danny was down the last intact steps and holding her, and the ceiling came down in pieces that were dust before they landed.

"Out," I said. "Everybody out. It's letting go."

We ran up through a house that was falling out of itself, walls slumping like a puppet with cut strings, and the gray skin over the front door split open in front of us like it had been waiting to, and we came out into the yard under the harvest moon, all three of us, and turned around, and watched.

The Aldergate house did not collapse. It *released*. Room by room, wall by wall, it let go of its shape the way a fist lets go of what it's holding, and by the time the moon cleared the rooftops there was nothing left but a foundation trench and a door lying flat in the dirt.

I picked up the door and turned it over.

The hinges were on the outside now.

Behind me, Marisol asked Danny where she was, and Danny started to answer, and I walked to the edge of the lot and stood in the cold with my empty hands and my credentials and my zero, and that was when I saw the small bare footprints in the fresh dirt, leading away from the ruin, toward the road, toward town, toward everywhere.

They went all the way to the property line.

And then they stopped.
