## 1. The Village That Sings Once

The bus to Hollowmere ran twice a week, and only if there were passengers, and only if the driver felt like it. I learned all of this from a laminated notice in a station the size of a garden shed, and I learned it from a man who told me the schedule the way other men tell you their ex-wife's phone number: accurately, and with a certain grim satisfaction.

"You'll want to get off at the cross," he said. "There's no stop. Just get off at the cross."

"What cross?"

"You'll see it." He looked at my equipment cases the way you look at a dog that's been in the river. "Folk music, is it?"

"Folk songs. I'm recording them. There's a project—"

"Mm," he said, which in the local dialect apparently meant *I have heard a sentence and I am declining to participate in it.*

I want to be clear about my expectations when I arrived, because I have since had a great deal of time to revise them, and revision is easier when you can point to what you originally thought. I expected a dying village. I expected suspicion — folklorists are a suspicious breed to the suspicious, and I had learned to introduce myself as a "researcher" or, when lying felt tiring, as "nobody important." I expected the usual arithmetic of rural decline: three pubs become one, one becomes a memory, the church roof held up by faith and scaffolding. What I did not expect was that the village had already decided, collectively and in advance, that I was not going to hear what I had come to hear. That decision had been made the way tectonic plates are made. Slowly. Deeply. Without anyone alive remembering the original event.

But I'm ahead of myself. That first day, I was only tired.

* * *

The cross was exactly where the driver's tone had promised it would be: a leaning stone thing at the junction of two lanes, furred with lichen, its carved face worn down to a suggestion. Someone had recently tied a strip of red cloth to it. Someone had also recently untied one, or it had fallen, because a second strip lay in the grass beneath, dark with rain, like something shed.

I stood there with my two cases and my rucksack and my letter of introduction — an actual letter, on actual paper, from a professor who had written it in 1987 and died in 2003, because that is the kind of connection my career had produced — and I waited to be met, or not met, and was not met.

So I walked. Hollowmere announced itself the way shy animals do: first a sound, then a smell, then, reluctantly, a shape. The sound was a dog, barking at nothing with the professional commitment of a dog who has been doing it for years. The smell was woodsmoke and wet stone and, underneath, something sweeter and older that I couldn't place and would spend a long time not placing. The shape was the village itself: grey houses hunched along a single street, a church with a squat tower, a shop whose window displayed, with heartbreaking optimism, four tins of soup and a birthday card.

The letter of introduction was addressed to the parish council, which no longer existed in any legal sense, and to a Mr. Aldous Crane, who had been dead for eleven years, and to "the keeper of the village hall," a title I suspected had been retired along with the hall. I had come anyway. I want that on the record too. I had a reason for coming that I told no one, and the reason was a house — the Crane house, my inheritance, empty seven years, mine by a document I had signed in a solicitor's office without reading past the figures. I had come to record the songs, yes. But I had also come because I had spent seven years being very carefully invisible, in cities where invisibility is a service provided free of charge, and something in me had begun to suspect that invisibility was not the same as safety. That it might, in fact, be the opposite of safety, worn as a coat.

You can see I was already halfway to the truth. I just didn't know it yet. Nobody ever knows it yet. That's the whole trick.

* * *

The inn was called the Drowned Bell, which is either a wonderful name for a pub or a terrible one, depending on how much you think about it. I thought about it a lot, later. That first evening I thought about it only long enough to appreciate that the bar had eight stools, four of them occupied, and that conversation stopped when I entered with the precision of a needle lifted off a record.

"I've got a room," said the man behind the bar. Not *we have rooms.* Not *let me check.* A room. Singular. As if the question of whether I'd stay had been settled some time ago, and the only variable left was which bed.

"Tam Weller," he added, wiping a glass that did not need it. "You'll be the Crane girl, then."

"Vesper Crane. And I'm not—"

"Your grandmother's house." He set the glass down. "It's been aired. Mrs. Petch did it. You'll want to light a fire the first night regardless. Old houses hold damp."

"Old houses hold a lot of things," I said, aiming for charming and hitting, I think, merely strange. Tam looked at me for a moment with an expression I would later learn to read as *the village's entire opinion of outsiders, compressed* — a kind of polite, bottomless non-commitment — and then went back to his glass.

I ate a pie that tasted of gravy and duty. I asked, casually, about the songs. Did anyone still sing the old harvest rounds? The wassails? My professor's notes from 1987 described Hollowmere as "an unusually rich repository," and I said so, and the word *repository* landed on the bar like a dead bird.

"There's singing at Christmas," said a woman at the end, not unkindly. "Carols. The normal kind."

"And the rest of the year?"

"People got other things to do."

I looked around at four people in an empty pub at seven in the evening and decided, generously, not to pursue the arithmetic. I went up to my room, which was clean and cold and had a mirror above the washbasin in which I looked, I noted, exactly as tired as I felt. No more, no less. I remember noticing that. I remember being pleased by it, in the small way you're pleased when the world agrees to behave.

* * *

Maren Odell I found the next morning, because everyone in the village, when asked who knew the old songs, said "not me" and then, after a pause, "Maren Odell maybe, but she's not right," and the consistency of the *not right* interested me more than the refusal.

She lived at the far end of the street in a house with a garden gone to seed in the beautiful, deliberate way that only very old gardeners achieve — everything allowed, nothing tended. She was on her step when I came up the path, as if she'd been waiting, though her face said she'd been waiting to tell someone to go away.

"You'll be the Crane one," she said.

"Is it the letter? Did Tam—"

"Tam doesn't read letters. Tam reads faces." She looked at mine. "You've got her chin."

"My grandmother's?"

"Your grandmother's." She said it the way you'd identify a feature on a map. *Here be the chin. Here be trouble.* Then, to my astonishment: "You'd better come in. It's cold on that step and I'm not saying it twice."

Her kitchen smelled of bread that had been baked and bread that was being baked, and of dried herbs hanging in bundles from the ceiling like small hanged men, and I sat where she pointed, at a table scrubbed so many times it had gone the colour of bone, and I did not get out my recorder. I want that understood: I had learned, over seven years and one failed marriage and a career that consisted mostly of being politely not-listened-to, that the recorder comes out when the room forgets it exists. Not before.

So we talked. About nothing. About the weather, which had opinions. About her knees, which had stronger ones. About the garden, where the beans had failed and the roses had not, "though they've no business doing either, in that soil." I let two hours go past like water through fingers, and I watched her, and I will admit that part of what I was watching for was the thing my professor's notes called *the Hollowmere cadence* — a lilt he'd transcribed in the margins and never explained, as if he'd expected whoever read the notes to already know.

I heard it first when she talked about the roses. Her voice went up at the end of the sentence and hung there, unresolved, like a note waiting for the next one, and then came down in a way that was almost but not quite a melody. It was the shape of a song worn thin by being spoken for so many years.

"Maren," I said, "my grandmother used to sing. Didn't she."

The room changed. Not dramatically — the kettle didn't stop, the light didn't shift. But something in the air between us reorganized itself, the way a flock of starlings does, all at once and with no visible signal.

"Everyone used to sing," she said.

"The harvest song. The one about the last sheaf." I had it from the notes: *The Last Sheaf, Hollowmere variant, words lost, melody fragmentary.* Four bars of melody and a question mark. "There's a version where the sheaf is carried through the village and everyone—"

"I know the one."

"Could you—"

"No."

She said it without heat. That was the strange thing, the thing I turned over that night and many nights after. There was no anger in it, no embarrassment, none of the shyness that usually guards an old song. It was the *no* you use for questions that have already been answered, long ago, by someone with the authority to answer them.

"Maren—"

"I said no, girl." She stood, and her knees cracked like green wood, and she turned her back on me to deal with the bread, which did not need dealing with. "My mother sang it. Her mother sang it. My grandmother sang it at the harvest home in — what year would that be — and the whole village sang, all together, like one voice with a hundred throats. And then we stopped. That's all there is. We stopped."

"Why did you stop?"

The silence went on long enough that I counted my own heartbeats in it. Nine. Ten.

"Because you only sing a song once," she said, to the bread, to the window, to the garden. "That's the rule here. You sing it once and then you let it go. You don't call a thing twice."

"That's not how songs work."

"That's how *our* songs work."

And then — and I have replayed this moment so many times that I no longer trust my memory of it, which is a sentence I never thought I'd write about a Tuesday — she turned around, and looked at me, and something in her face did a thing I can only describe as *losing an argument with itself.* Curiosity. That's what it was. Seventy years of refusal, and underneath it, a girl who had liked to sing.

"Sit still," she said. "And don't you dare write it down while I'm doing it."

I sat still. I did not dare.

She sang four bars.

That's all. Four bars of the Last Sheaf, the melody my professor had chased a question mark around, and I will tell you it was the most beautiful four bars I have ever heard, and I have heard a great many, including some I had no business hearing. It was in a mode I couldn't name, with a turn at the end of the second bar that folded back on itself like a hand closing, and her voice was old and cracked and absolutely certain, and the kitchen, the bread, the hanged herbs, the light — everything held still around it as if the room were listening too.

*The last sheaf stands in the furrow's end,*

*and no one knows whose hand it was—*

She stopped.

She stopped the way a clock stops. Not trailing off. Not forgetting. Stopped, mid-word, her mouth still open on the *was*, and the colour left her face in a single tide, from the cheeks down, like water finding a drain.

"Maren?"

She sat down. Not in the chair — that would have required intention. She sat down the way a marionette sits down when the hand above it flinches. Her hands were flat on the table and I could see the tendons standing in them, and her eyes had gone to the window, to the garden, to some point past both.

"You sang it," she whispered. "I sang it. I sang it *again.*"

"You sang it once. I asked, you said no, and then—"

"Forty years." Her voice had gone thin and high, a wire pulled tight. "Forty years I haven't sung that, not a note of it, not in my head even, I *hummed round it*, do you understand, I hummed *around* it the way you walk round a hole in the floor—" She looked at me then, and what was in her face was not fear of me, exactly. It was fear of having been caught. Of having been *seen* doing it. "Get out."

"Maren, please, I only wanted—"

"*Get out of my house.*" And she stood, and her strength came back all at once, the way it does when the body decides fear is a kind of fuel, and she had me at the door with a hand between my shoulder blades before I had properly decided to go.

I was three steps down the path when she said my name.

"Vesper."

I turned. She was in the doorway, one hand on the frame, and the colour had come back to her face, which somehow made it worse, because now she just looked like an old woman, and old women are allowed to look frightened without the world ending.

"Your grandmother," she said, "was the last one to sing it whole. You should know that. You should know it before you go knocking on any more doors."

And she shut the door. Not slammed. Shut, with the care of someone closing a box on something.

* * *

I walked back through the village in the flat grey light of early afternoon, and I did the thing I always do, the thing that has kept me alive and lonely and employed: I made a list of explanations.

She has dementia, and the song unlocked something, and the fear was confusion. Possible. She has a heart condition and overexerted herself. Possible. She's simply eccentric, and the whole village is simply eccentric, and villages are like that, and I have read too many of my professor's marginalia. Possible. Probable, even. The most likely explanation, by a comfortable margin, was that an old woman had frightened herself over nothing, because that is what old women in isolated villages do, and folklorists who forget this end up writing books about ley lines.

I got back to the inn. Tam was behind the bar, wiping a glass that did not need it.

"Maren Odell sang for me," I said.

I don't know what I expected. A raised eyebrow. A joke. What I got was worse. What I got was Tam setting the glass down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and looking at me with an expression that was not surprise.

"Did she," he said.

"Four bars. Of the harvest song. And then she stopped and threw me out."

"Ah," said Tam.

*Ah.* Not *good Lord.* Not *she did what?* Just *ah*, in the tone of a man confirming a diagnosis he'd already read on the chart. He picked the glass back up. He began to wipe it again.

"Your room's made up," he said. "Supper's at six. There's no supper after seven."

And I stood there in the empty bar with my list of explanations in my hand, and I crossed out the top three, and I wrote at the bottom, in the handwriting I use when I don't want to admit a thought is mine:

*They all knew she might. They all knew what happens when someone sings it twice. They were waiting to see.*

That night I unpacked the recorder, and set it on the table in my room, and did not turn it on. The house my grandmother had left me stood dark at the end of a lane I hadn't yet walked, holding its damp, holding whatever else old houses hold. And in the wall — I told myself it was the pipes, and it was the pipes, it was certainly the pipes — in the wall, very faintly, something hummed four notes.

The turn at the end of the second bar. The fold, like a hand closing.

I did not hum back. I want that on the record too.

I lay in the dark and did not hum back, and in the morning I would tell myself I had dreamed it, and I would almost believe it, and that — the *almost* — was the first crack in the armour, though I didn't hear it then. Cracks never sound like anything, at first. That's the whole trick of them.
