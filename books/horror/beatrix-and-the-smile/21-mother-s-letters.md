## 21. Mother's Letters

The letters were in the piano.

I want to be precise about that, because for two days I had torn the house apart looking for anything my mother might have left me—crawled the attic with a torch clenched in my teeth like a burglar with a conscience, pried up floorboards that had never hidden anything but dust and one very indignant mouse, gone through her clothes, her books, her sewing basket, even the cold ashes of a fireplace she hadn't used since before I was born. Nothing. The house kept its secrets the way Maren kept her verses: you could ask all you liked, but it would only give them up when it decided you were ready.

Then I sat down at the piano to rest my feet, and the bench made a sound no bench should make—a soft, papery sigh, like something exhaling after holding its breath for thirty years.

I lifted the lid. Underneath the seat cushion, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string gone brittle as old celery, lay a bundle of letters. Forty-one of them. All addressed to *V.* Not Vesper. Just *V*, as if even on paper she couldn't quite bring herself to say my whole name—as if the name itself were something flammable.

My mother's handwriting. I'd forgotten I remembered it, and then there it was, slanting across the envelopes like a row of birches leaning out of a wind, and my chest did something complicated and unhelpful.

I carried them to the kitchen, because the kitchen has always been where this family does its hardest thinking, and because the mirrors in every other room had started showing me things I hadn't done yet—a broken cup that morning, still whole on the shelf; a red door I didn't recognize; and once, horribly, a figure on the stairs wearing my coat, standing exactly where I would stand eleven seconds later. I had taken to avoiding the stairs on principle. You can't be ambushed by your own future if you refuse to use the staircase. It's not a strategy. It's a lifestyle.

The kitchen mirror over the sink showed only the present tense, which by now felt almost nostalgic.

I untied the string. The oilcloth smelled of camphor and time. And I read.

* * *

The earliest letter was dated March 1974, six months before I was born.

*V—if it is a girl we will call her Vesper, and if it is a boy he will never forgive us, so let us hope. I am writing these because there are things I cannot say aloud in this village without someone counting the words afterward. You know how they count.*

I sat back. She was writing to me before I existed. Writing to a maybe-daughter as though I were already listening, which—given what this house did with time, given what the mirrors had become—I was no longer entirely willing to rule out.

*I went to the undercroft today. Thirteen chairs, thirteen names in the ledger, and Tam's grandmother walking me through it all with that patient voice they use, the one that makes refusal sound like bad manners. They have chosen the vessel. They will not tell me who. They say it is not my business until the solstice, and then it will be everyone's business, and isn't that lovely.*

I turned the page with fingers that had begun, without consulting me, to shake.

*It is the baby. It is you. Eleven weeks from being born and they have already spent you. "A vessel must be innocent," Edith's mother said, as if innocence were a currency and infancy the mint. As if the whole point of a child is that she cannot yet say no.*

The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere above me—the attic, or the room above the attic, or whatever room the house keeps up there for its own purposes—something shifted its weight. I have lived with those footsteps for seven years in another city, and I had told myself they were pipes, raccoons, the settling of an old building, the ordinary creak of a world too full of weight to hold still. Sitting in my mother's kitchen reading my mother's handwriting, I understood that I had spent seven years practicing not knowing things, and that I had gotten very good at it, and that the skill had been inherited, along with the house.

*I said no.*

The next letter was dated three days later, and it was short, and the pen had pressed hard enough to tear through in places.

*I said no, and the room went quiet the way snow goes quiet, which is to say completely and with menace. Old Crane rules: the ritual must be sung whole or not at all, and every living descendant must lend their voice, and the vessel must be freely given. Freely. So I have made myself the flaw in their arithmetic. Let them find another vessel. Let them wait.*

*They will not wait. That is the thing I understand now that I did not understand when I agreed to come back here and raise you among them. They will not wait, and they will not choose again, because choosing again means admitting the first choice was a choice, and this village would rather drown than admit it can swim.*

*So it will go unsung. And the thing beneath will stay unbound and unshaped, and it will get hungry, and the hunger will leak. I know what I am doing, V. I want that written down somewhere besides my own guilty heart: I know exactly what I am doing, and I am doing it anyway, because you are eleven weeks old and I love you more than I love the shape of the world.*

*Love reframed as the original sin—that was the thought that arrived, uninvited, in my mother's own wry cadence, as if she'd left it between the lines for me like a tip under a pillow. Every horror story I'd collected in seven years of folklore fieldwork had a moment like this: the revelation that the monster was a decision, made by someone who meant well, at a kitchen table much like mine.*

I read through the night. There were forty-one letters, and they spanned nineteen years, and they charted the slow erosion of a woman who had saved her daughter and damned everything else, and who knew it, and stayed anyway.

*1976. The dogs won't go past the well anymore. I told the vet it was a fox. He looked at me like he knew it wasn't.*

*1981. Children are humming it in the schoolyard. A version, anyway—corrupted, the way children corrupt things, all the dangerous parts worn smooth into nonsense syllables. Edith confiscated a girl's copybook. I could have kissed her and I don't even like her.*

*1987. Your father asked me last night why we never sing at Christmas. I said I don't like carols. He accepted this. Your father accepts everything; it is his single talent and I married him for it. Sometimes I think the only person in Hollowmere who doesn't know what I did is you, and I intend to keep it that way until keeping it that way stops working.*

*1993. I heard it try your name today. In the wall, behind the pantry. It tried it in my voice first, and then in yours, pitched high, the way you sounded at five. I put my hands over my ears and sang nothing at all, very loudly, and it went away. Nothing at all is the only song I allow myself. It is the only one that doesn't cost anything except everything else.*

That was the sentence I had to put the letters down for. I got up and made tea I didn't drink and stood at the window watching the square, where the smiling man had stood all the previous night without moving, and I thought about my mother standing in this same kitchen with her hands over her ears, singing silence at the walls for nineteen years, and I thought about my own seven years of small noises—the footsteps, the humming, my name tried in voices like a thief trying keys—and how I had explained every one of them away with the diligence of a scholar and the desperation of a daughter.

She hadn't been hiding the truth from me out of shame. She'd been hiding it the way you hide a lit match from a child: because the truth was burning, and I was made of paper.

* * *

The later letters changed. Her handwriting loosened; the sentences shortened, as if she were rationing her strength between the writing and the walls.

*1999. It knows I'm tired. That's the ugly truth of it—it waits for tired the way wolves wait for dark. Last week I caught myself humming. Four notes of the harvest song before I strangled it. Four notes, V, and the water in the taps shivered. I haven't slept properly since. This is what my refusal bought: not peace, just a slower war, fought in whispers, forever, and the bill forwarded to my daughter.*

There it was. Not accusation—accounting. She was doing sums on the page, and the sums came out against her, and she signed them anyway.

*2001. If you are reading these, then either I am dead or I have finally run, and we both know I will never run, because running is a kind of singing too—it leaves a trail of noise. Forgive me. Or better: don't forgive me. Forgiveness lets the forgiver off easy. Understand me instead. Understand that I looked at a room full of good people asking me to spend my baby, and I chose you, and I would choose you again tomorrow, and next week, and at the end of the world. Everything that has gone wrong here grew from that choice like ivy from a crack, and I watered it daily, and I regret nothing, and I regret everything, and both of those are true, and I am so tired.*

*The ritual needs every voice. That was always its genius and its cruelty—you cannot bind a hunger alone; you can only starve it together. One missing voice and the whole song collapses into a lullaby. I am the missing voice. I am the reason it sleeps badly. When I die, sing nothing. Promise me. Sing nothing, and stay small, and stay elsewhere, and let the village keep its half-memory and its quiet dread. Small noises, V. Live among small noises and never once look up.*

And there it was—not just my mother's secret but my inheritance entire: the seven years of invisibility, the apartment in a city where nobody knew my name, the career built on recording other people's songs because I could not bear to hear my own. I had thought I was fleeing Hollowmere. I had been obeying her. Every year I'd spent not looking, not singing, not returning, had been the last verse of her refusal, sustained across distance like a note held past endurance.

She hadn't doomed the village in 1974 and then died. She had doomed it slowly, deliberately, lovingly, for twenty-seven years, one day of silence at a time. And she had drafted me into the silence without telling me I'd enlisted.

I should have been angry. Some part of me filed the anger away for later, the way you file documents you'll need for a hearing. But what I felt mostly, sitting in the grey light of a kitchen whose mirror watched me with the patience of a held breath, was the terrible tenderness of being loved in a way that broke things. She had weighed an entire village against an infant and chosen the infant, and the infant had grown into a woman who now had to weigh everything back.

The last letter was dated nine days before her death.

*V. It is nearly time and I can feel it learning my outline, the way a coat learns a shoulder. I have one request and one confession. The confession: sometimes, at night, I want to sing. Not the ritual—just anything, a scale, a hymn, the stupid jingle from the flour advertisement. I want to open my mouth and let sound out like a bird from a hand. That is how it gets you, I think. Not with terror. With the longing to stop being careful.*

*The request: if it ever comes for you—and it will, eventually, because I have made you the shape of its patience—do not do what I did. Refusal was right for me because I had something to refuse for. But a refusal held too long turns into a wall, and walls keep things in as well as out. If the time comes when the song must be sung, sing it. Find the verses. Finish it properly, whole, with every voice it needs—including the ones it doesn't deserve. Don't starve it the way I did. Shape it. Give it a form it can wear so it stops trying to wear us.*

*And if you can't forgive me, then use me. Use my refusal as the map of everything not to do. That is the only inheritance I have to leave you besides the house and the piano bench, and I suspect the house comes with the undercroft, and God help you, sweetheart, I hope you never find the undercroft.*

I laughed. It came out wrong—too loud in the empty kitchen, cracked down the middle, closer to its opposite than laughter usually gets. Too late, Mum. Found it Tuesday. Thirteen chairs. All facing the glass.

I gathered the letters, squared their edges, and held the bundle against my sternum like a compress on a wound. Above me, the house shifted—one deliberate footstep, then another, crossing the ceiling toward the stairs, unhurried, familiar, patient. It had been pacing up there my whole life. It had been pacing since 1974. It was pacing now because it had heard me laugh, and it liked the sound, and it was coming down to see whether I'd do it again.

I looked at the kitchen mirror. My reflection looked back—on time, for once, obediently, faithfully—and then, in the corner of the glass, behind my reflected shoulder, the reflected stairwell door stood open. The actual door behind me was shut. I checked. I'm thorough about doors now; it's part of the lifestyle.

In the mirror, at the top of the stairs, something stood wearing my mother's cardigan.

It raised one hand—not a wave, not a threat. The gesture people make when they're about to speak, when they've been waiting years for the room to go quiet enough.

I turned around. The hallway was empty. The door was shut. The house breathed.

When I turned back to the glass, the stairwell was empty too, and my reflection was smiling—warmly, sadly, with unmistakable approval—and I had not told it to.

I set the letters down on the table, opened the ledger to the scraped-out thirteenth line, and picked up a pencil, because my mother had given me instructions, and instructions require materials, and verse four demands that the singer name what she is willing to give up.

I had a list. It had started forming the moment I read the word *sweetheart* in her handwriting, and it was longer, and heavier, and more expensive than anything the founders had ever written down.

I sharpened the pencil, and began.
