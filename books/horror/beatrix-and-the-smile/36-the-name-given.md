## 36. The Name Given

The name left me the way a tooth leaves: not all at once, but with a root.

I had written it on the scraped line of the ledger two nights before solstice—*Vesper Crane*, in my own hand, because the verse demanded a freely surrendered name and I suspected that even my handwriting would be audited—and for those two nights I could feel it thinning. Not gone. Thinning. Like frost on a window you breathe on and wipe away and it comes back furred at the edges, less each time. I would reach for myself the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue and find only the shape of where it had been.

*Vesper.* Evening star. My mother had chosen it because I was born at dusk, she'd said, though her letters suggested she chose it because names are doors and she wanted mine to face away from whatever was under the house. It hadn't worked. Doors never do. They only decide which direction you fall through them.

Now, in the thirteenth chair's shadow—I sat in the first; the thirteenth belonged to the thing that wore my smile—I finished the seventh verse.

It is not a long verse. That is the cruelty of it. Six verses of architecture, of naming and turning and hollowing, of a village singing through cracked lips while its dead songs rose out of the ground like heat off summer stone—and then seven lines, short as a shopping list, that required nothing but the one thing no ritual can take from you.

You have to hand it over yourself.

---

The singing had been going for what felt like hours and might have been eleven minutes. Time in the undercroft had stopped pretending to be time. The thirteen mirrors around us showed thirteen rooms, none of them this one, all of them exactly on schedule—a kettle boiling in a kitchen I didn't recognize, rain starting against a window somewhere above us, Edith's empty classroom with the chalk still resting mid-word where she'd dropped it to come sing. The mirrors had become honest. That was the whole point, I understood now. Not prophecy, not haunting. Honesty, imposed by force on something that had spent centuries lying about what it was.

The village sang behind me. Raggedly. Wonderfully. Tam's voice carried the third line of every verse because he had learned, in nine days, to do the one thing he'd sworn his whole life he couldn't: sing in front of other people. His voice was terrible. It was also load-bearing. Halfway through verse four, when the double on the church steps had thrown the false fifth verse down into the square like a net, it was Tam who held the melody while the rest of us found our way back to it, bellowing like a man arguing with a river, and the net tore.

Edith's voice had failed an hour ago—or a minute—or during verse two; the clocks were all liars now. She stood beside my chair with her hand on my shoulder, barely remembered by her own students, barely remembering herself, and she mouthed the words she could no longer voice. Her lips moved like a woman praying in a language she'd been forbidden to study.

And in the thirteenth chair, the smiling man waited.

He had lost the smile. That was Maren's verse doing its work—the sixth verse, the smuggled one, the true one, sung last because unmaking must always come after making or it has nothing to unmake. When I'd reached the final note of it, the double on the church steps had stopped mid-false-verse, her borrowed throat closing around the song like a fist, and across the square the smiling man's face had simply... emptied. The smile drained out of him the way color drains from a photograph left in the sun. What remained was worse than any face: a hunger with no features at all, a shape like the space where a person should be, leaning forward in its chair with the terrible patience of something that has waited since before chairs.

It didn't want to be worshipped. It didn't want to be freed. It wanted what every founder had known and every ledger entry had circled without saying:

It wanted a name. A bounded shape. Something to be *in*.

My mother had refused to give it mine in 1974, and the village had paid for her love in memory ever since—the songs sung once, the faces half-known, the slow erosion of everything that makes a place a place instead of a location. She'd saved me and unmade them, and both things were true, and I had hated her for three days and forgiven her in the fourth, which is roughly the standard rate of exchange between mothers and daughters.

But a debt deferred is not a debt forgiven. And I was the last Crane. And the line on the ledger was already written in my hand.

---

"Vesper." Edith's mouth shaped it. No sound came out, but I read it, and for a moment—that moment—I knew exactly who I was. Every memory lined up like choristers: the university library where I'd hidden for seven years, the lover whose name I'd already fed to verse four, the smell of my mother's kitchen (gone too; verse four had taken it early, greedy), Maren's hands shaking around a scrap of burning paper, the first lagging mirror in the bathroom of a house I'd inherited like a sentence.

Then I opened my mouth and began the seventh verse, and the choristers started leaving the loft one by one.

*"What is given is given whole,"* I sang, and my voice sounded strange to me—not because the verse changed it but because I was already becoming someone listening to a stranger. *"What is named is named alone. Take the word and not the flesh, take the sign and not the stone—"*

The mirrors all turned. Not physically—they couldn't; they were bolted into the walls by men dead three hundred years—but every reflection in every one of them rotated toward me at once, thirteen rooms pivoting like thirteen eyes, and in the room that was almost mine, the one that flickered when I sang verse two, a figure stood up from a chair.

Not my figure. Not yet. A suggestion of one. An outline waiting for ink.

*"Take the evening, leave the day,"* I went on, and my mother's face went—I felt it go, felt the memory slide out of me like a splinter drawn clean, and I grieved it even as the verse demanded I keep singing, grief and song in the same breath, which is, I think, the entire human condition compressed into one impossible technique. *"Take the star and not the sky. Let the hunger have a door—"*

The thing in the thirteenth chair rose.

It didn't walk toward me. Walking requires legs and intention and a direction, and it had spent so long being none of those things that it simply *arrived*, the way cold arrives under a door, standing before my chair with its featureless face inclined toward mine, and I understood that we were both doing the same thing: waiting for the last line.

*"And let the singer be the song."*

---

There is no way to describe what it feels like to have your name taken except to say that it feels like being read aloud by someone else.

The name went first. *Vesper* peeled away and I watched it go—not saw, exactly, but registered the absence the way you register a clock stopping in a house you've lived in forever: a silence shaped exactly like a sound. Then *Crane*, and with it the founders, the ledger, the house, the obligation, the whole long inheritance I'd walked into believing I was visiting. Then the smaller things attached to the name like burrs: the way teachers used to say it when I'd done well, the way my mother said it when I'd done badly, the way I'd whispered it to myself in seven years of deliberate invisibility, a charm against being perceived.

All of it, gathered up.

The thing took it gently. That was the worst part. I had braced for theft and received instead something like gratitude—an attention settling over me, vast and careful, the way a hand settles over a moth to carry it outside. It did not take my face. It did not take my body. It took the *word* for me, and the world's memory of the word, and left the rest sitting in the chair like an empty coat.

And then it settled.

I felt it happen in the mirrors before I saw it. Thirteen panes sighed—there is no other word for it, a glassy exhalation, the sound a window makes when it finally sits true in its frame after years of rattling. In the first mirror, the kitchen kettle boiled exactly when the kettle boiled. In the second, the rain struck the glass in perfect time with the rain. Room after room snapped into honesty, reflections arriving precisely when they should, neither late nor early, neither ahead nor behind, and in the last mirror—the one that had shown the undercroft occupied—the outline of a figure filled itself in, slowly, like frost forming on glass in reverse.

It looked like no one. That was the gift of it. It looked like whoever needed to be reflected, a presence wearing the mirrors the way I had once worn my name: lightly, provisionally, as a courtesy to others.

The hunger had a shape now. Bounded. Livable alongside. Exactly what the thirteen founders had intended, before fear made them write *banish* in the margins and their descendants mistake the margin for the text.

The thing turned its new, unformed face toward me—one last look, I thought, though it had no eyes and I had, by then, very little left to be looked at with—and if a hunger can be gentle, it was. Then it stepped backward into the glass the way a swimmer steps off a ledge, without drama, without splash, and the undercroft was only a room again. Thirteen chairs. One empty. One holding a woman who was trying very hard to remember why she was sitting in it.

---

The singing stopped. Of course it did. Songs end; that's what makes them bearable.

Tam's voice gave out mid-breath and he stood in the square above us—he and the others had crowded down the undercroft stairs at some point, or perhaps they'd been there all along; my grip on sequence was loosening along with everything else—staring at me with the expression of a man trying to recall where he'd put his keys.

"Do you—" he started, and frowned. "Do you know her?"

"She's the folklorist," someone said uncertainly. "Isn't she? The one passing through."

"The one recording the songs," another voice offered, kinder, reaching for me the way you reach for a fact you're sure you own and finding the shelf bare.

They were trying. I could see them trying, feel their minds scrabbling at the smooth place where I used to be, and I loved them for it and it changed nothing. The name was gone from them too. Not hidden—*gone*, the way a removed tooth leaves a gap the tongue returns to obsessively and finds nothing. I was a woman in a chair. A stranger who had arrived for the solstice. Perhaps a friend of Edith's; Edith was nodding slowly, her hand still on my shoulder, and her face was doing something complicated—grief and recognition fighting through fog, the single page of her that refused to blank.

"You did it," she whispered. She shouldn't have known there was an *it*. But Edith had always kept what she wasn't supposed to keep. "Whatever your name was. You did it."

*I did,* I almost said, and then realized I didn't know what *I* referred to anymore, and laughed instead—a small, cracked sound that echoed off thirteen honest mirrors and came back exactly on time.

Maren was dead. I knew that the way you know weather: the knowledge arrived without a name attached to it, grief with the label torn off. There would be a grave. There would be, I somehow understood, a second marker beside it that no one could explain, because the village would remember a ritual and a solstice and a debt paid, and the debt's creditor would be a blank line in a ledger.

That was the bargain. That had always been the bargain. The founders' handwriting changed halfway through the ledger because the truth was too expensive to write plainly: *sung whole or not at all*, and *whole* meant everyone, including the one who sings the ending, and the ending costs the singer her name and the world's receipt of it.

I had known. I had written my name down anyway. There is a version of me—in the memories that remained, anyway—who wants to claim that as courage. The truer version knows it was simpler than that. Someone had to be the last verse. My mother wouldn't let it be me at one year old. So it was me at thirty-four, by choice, at dusk, in a room full of people who would forget me by morning.

Grief and completion. Same breath. I'd been practicing for weeks without knowing it.

---

We came up out of the undercroft into dawn.

The square was wrecked in the ordinary ways—scuffed cobbles, a shattered shopfront or six, the church steps swept clean of the double, who had dissolved when the false verse died in her throat, folding down into nothing like a dress slipping off a hanger. The villagers stood blinking in the early light, and already they were changing. I watched it happen in real time: shoulders dropping, faces unknotting, a man in a doorway suddenly whistling a harvest tune and then whistling it *again*, frowning at himself, whistling it a third time, weeping a little without knowing why.

The songs would come back. All of them, sung twice, sung three times, the old hoarded verses pouring out of people who no longer remembered why they'd been rationing them. That was part of the settlement too. The thing in the mirrors kept its shape; the village got its music back; and the ledger got its final line, written in a hand that no longer belonged to anyone.

Tam found me at the edge of the square. He looked at me a long moment with his terrible kind face, and I watched him wrestle the question—*do I know you?*—and lose, gracefully.

"You'll be wanting breakfast," he said, "if you're staying on. We've rooms." A pause. "Though I can't for the life of me think why anyone comes to Hollowmere."

"Neither can I," I said, and my voice was a stranger's voice, and it was mine, and both of those facts were going to take some getting used to.

He nodded, satisfied, and turned away, and I stood alone in the morning with no name, no reflection, no past that anyone could verify, holding the only possessions that had survived the transaction: a bag of recordings, a body, and a future.

In the dark glass of the shattered shopfront, the street reflected perfectly. Carts, rooftops, smoke, Edith walking toward the school with her chin up. Everyone accounted for.

Everyone except the woman standing right there, whom the glass declined, politely and permanently, to show.

I touched the cold surface once—goodbye, or hello, I honestly couldn't say which—and turned toward the house on the hill, where my bags were packed, and where tomorrow I would begin the strange work of packing up a life's recordings for a village that would play them back and weep.

Behind me, in the shopfront glass, the street went on reflecting, exactly on time.

And in the deepest pane, almost too faint to see, something settled—patient, bounded, home at last—and waited, the way mirrors do, for the next face to arrive.
