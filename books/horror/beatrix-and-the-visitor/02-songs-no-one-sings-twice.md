## 2. Songs No One Sings Twice

The first singer was a woman named Beryl Hodge, who kept the post office and had known Marguerite's grandmother for sixty years. She agreed to be recorded in her back room, among the tins of loose tea and the smell of dust burning off an electric heater, and she chose a song she called "The Mere's Lullaby."

"It's not really a lullaby," Beryl said, adjusting the small recorder Marguerite held out to her as though it might bite. "It's older than that word. My mother sang it wringing laundry. You'll want it slow."

Marguerite wanted everything slow. That was the whole discipline—capture the tempo before it evaporates, note the ornament, the bent third, the places where breath replaces sense. She had spent eleven years recording songs in danger of dying, and she was good at it, and being good at it had always felt like a kind of rescue.

Beryl began.

Her voice was thin but true, and the melody did something Marguerite's trained ear couldn't immediately file: it descended where it should have risen, twice, like something walking down steps into water. The words were dialect-heavy, half of them glossed in Marguerite's notebook with question marks. *Lay you down, lay you down, the mere is deep and wide—*

Then: *And none shall wake, and none shall weep, for the one below the—*

Beryl stopped.

Not faltered. Stopped, cleanly, mid-word, the way a machine stops when someone lifts the needle. Her mouth stayed open around the syllable she hadn't sung. Then she closed it, swallowed, and said, "There's tea in the pot if you want some," as if the last four seconds hadn't happened.

"Beryl—you cut off. Right at 'below the.'"

"Did I." It wasn't a question. She poured tea with steady hands and didn't look at the recorder.

"You've sung this your whole life, I'd guess. Do you always stop there?"

"I don't sing it often." Beryl set the cup down harder than necessary. "Look, love, it's just how the song goes now. Everyone leaves that bit out. Has done since—" She paused, calculating something, and whatever number she arrived at made her jaw tighten. "Since before you were born, I expect. Ask someone else if you want the ending. Though I wouldn't."

*Though I wouldn't.* Marguerite wrote it down anyway, because that was the job, and because writing things down was how she kept the world from sliding.

* * *

By the end of the week she had seven recordings and seven silences.

The ferryman's daughter—a mute young woman everyone called Wren, who communicated in chalk on a slate hung from her neck—wrote out the verse instead of singing it, then stopped writing at the same line, chalk hovering, and underlined what came before three times as if to say: *this much is safe.*

The choir master at the chapel sang two verses of a harvest round and went quiet at "the one below the water," then laughed at himself, embarrassed, and said he supposed he'd forgotten the rest, except his eyes said he hadn't forgotten anything.

Old Mrs. Petch got further than anyone—she actually sang the word *water*—and then sat trembling with her hands flat on the table like a woman steadying herself against weather, and said, "Turn that thing off, dear. Turn it off and don't play it back tonight."

"Why not tonight?"

"Because songs are doors," Mrs. Petch said, "and some of them only open from the inside."

Marguerite walked home along the mere road in the last grey light, playing the recordings back through one earphone, listening for the pattern she already knew was there. Seven singers. Six different songs—lullaby, harvest round, a christening wassail, two fragments of a ballad no one would name, a skipping rhyme children still used without knowing what they were using. And every single one broke at the same seam. Not the same words, exactly—the phrasing shifted across versions—but the same referent. *The one below the water. The quiet one under. The guest beneath the mere.*

Fifty years of villagers editing their own folklore in unison, without discussing it, without apparently knowing they were doing it. That wasn't tradition. That was suppression so old it had fossilized into tradition.

She should have been thrilled. This was the kind of find careers were built on—a living taboo, intact and functioning. She drafted three emails to colleagues in her head as she walked.

But her hands were cold, and the earphone kept catching a sound underneath Beryl's voice on the recording, a low tone below the melody that Marguerite hadn't heard in the room. She told herself it was the heater, or tape noise, or the recorder's cheap microphone handling bass badly.

She played it again anyway. The tone was still there. It rose slightly, near the cut-off point, like something inhaling.

She took the earphone out and walked the rest of the way in silence, and did not look at the mere, and noted in her journal that she had not looked at the mere, which was itself becoming a strange thing to note.

* * *

Tam Weller was ninety-one and lived in a cottage at the end of Church Lane with a garden gone to bramble and a door that opened before Marguerite finished knocking.

"I heard you've been collecting," he said. He was small and dry as a fence post, with pale eyes that didn't track quite normally—one on her, one slightly off, watching something over her shoulder out of lifelong habit. "Come in, then. Mind the cat. Or don't; he minds himself."

Inside smelled of woodsmoke and paraffin. Tam poured two glasses of sloe gin without asking and sat down across from her with the air of a man who had been waiting all week.

"You've found the hole in the songs," he said.

"You know about it."

"I know about most things in Hollowmere. It's a village built on knowing and not saying." He drank. "So ask me your questions, girl, and I'll answer the ones I can."

"Why does everyone stop at the same line?"

"Because they were taught to stop. Not by people. By the stopping itself." He saw her face and almost smiled. "You're a folklorist. Tell me—what happens to a song that gets interrupted at the same place, by everyone, for fifty years?"

"The interruption becomes part of the song. The silence gets notated whether you mean it to or not."

"There you are. Now tell me why a whole village would agree to break its own music."

Marguerite turned her glass. "Something happened here. Something the songs used to describe, and the description became dangerous."

"Careful," Tam said softly. "That's closer than most get."

"My grandmother collected these same songs. In 1974. There are notes in her study—I found the box yesterday. Half the pages are torn out."

The cat, which had been asleep in the hearth, stood up, arched, and left the room without touching the floor more than twice.

Tam watched it go. Then he looked at her properly, both eyes, for the first time.

"Your gran was Agnes Underhill," he said. "Best collector this county ever produced, and the last person who understood what she was holding. You want my advice? Don't read what's left of those notes after dark, and don't read what's torn out at all, because torn pages aren't missing, girl. Torn pages are *kept*. Somebody tore them out to keep them somewhere, and things that get kept get kept for reasons."

"You're frightening me, Mr. Weller."

"Good. Fear's the only honest instrument you'll bring to Hollowmere." He leaned forward. "I'll tell you one thing free, because you've got her chin and she once talked me out of drowning myself, though that's another story. You think those songs are broken. They're not. A song with a hole in it isn't broken—it's *waiting*. Every one of those verses everyone cuts off, they fit together. Lullaby, wassail, skipping rhyme, the lot. Stitch them end to end and the holes make a shape."

"What shape?"

"A whole song. One song, split into pieces and scattered through fifty years of harmless little tunes so no one would ever sing it complete." His pale eyes held hers. "And here's the part you won't like, Marguerite Underhill. Some songs aren't records. Some songs are contracts. Sung whole, by the right throat, in the right place—they don't describe anything. They *do* something. Your gran knew that better than anyone alive, and if she tore pages out of her own field notes, it's because she was trying to keep the contract from ever being read aloud again."

Marguerite's mouth had gone dry. "Sung by the right throat. What does that mean? Whose throat?"

Tam refilled her glass instead of answering, and his hand, she noticed, was not entirely steady.

"Go home," he said. "Read your gran's notes in daylight. Count the torn edges—count them carefully, because the number matters. And when you've done that, come back and tell me what number you got, and I'll tell you whether you should have counted."

* * *

The house was cold when she let herself in, colder than October justified, and the hallway smelled faintly of pond water though the windows had been shut for months.

She told herself it was the drains.

In the study, Agnes's field notes waited in their acid-free box where Marguerite had left them, squared neatly, the way her grandmother had squared everything. Marguerite put on the desk lamp, made tea she didn't drink, and opened the folder in full afternoon light slanting through the dirty window—daylight, as instructed, though the light was going amber at the edges and would not last.

The surviving pages were meticulous. Dates, informants' initials, melodic transcriptions in Agnes's small upright hand. The same songs Marguerite had collected, in versions with *more* verses—verses no living singer would give her—and every one of those extra verses circled in red ink, with annotations in the margins. *Do not record. Do not repeat. Do not sing to test.* And beside one verse, in handwriting pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper:

*IT ANSWERS WHEN NAMED.*

Marguerite sat very still.

Then she counted the torn edges, because Tam had told her to count them, and because she was her grandmother's granddaughter and obedience-through-curiosity was the family disease.

Eleven pages had been removed. Eleven ragged stubs, each torn close to the spine, each torn by a careful hand—except the last one, which had been torn fast, in panic, leaving a jagged triangle of paper still attached. On that triangle, in Agnes's hand, two words survived:

*—her name—*

The lamp flickered. Outside, the light had failed without her noticing, and the window had become a dark mirror showing her the study behind her: the desk, the box, the chair.

Marguerite turned in the actual room.

The wooden chair stood against the wall where no chair had stood that morning. Child-sized. Plain-backed. Facing the desk. Facing her.

She had catalogued every object in this house over the past week for the estate inventory. She could have sworn an oath, itemized and notarized, that this chair did not exist.

She looked at it for a long time. It did not move. It simply sat there with the terrible patience of furniture, and the smell of deep water strengthened, gently, like something below the floorboards turning over in its sleep.

On the desk, her recorder—switched off, batteries out since morning—clicked softly on.

From its small speaker, in Beryl Hodge's thin true voice, came four seconds of lullaby, stopping clean at *the one below the—*

Then silence.

Then, from somewhere beneath the floorboards, or beneath the village, or beneath the world, something hummed the next line.
