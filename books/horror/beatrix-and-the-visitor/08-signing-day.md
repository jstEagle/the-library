## 8. Signing Day

The solicitors came at nine, and the mere came with them.

Marguerite heard the cars first—two sets of tires hissing up the lane through water that had no business being on the lane at all. She had woken to a sound she couldn't name, a low wet breathing under the floorboards, and when she pulled back the curtain the garden was gone. Not flooded. *Replaced.* The rosebeds her grandmother had tended for sixty years stood submerged to their hips in still black water, the roses nodding above the surface like women bending to drink. The lawn was a mirror. And down at the bottom of it, faint as a coin dropped in a well, the pale shape of something that might have been a chair.

"Edith," Marguerite said aloud, and ran for the phone.

* * *

Edith Crane received her in bed, propped on pillows the color of old teeth, with the sale papers squared neatly on the blanket tray before her as though she'd been waiting for them the way other dying people wait for a priest. Her hands were translucent now. You could see the clock of her pulse ticking at the wrist.

"They're early," Edith said. "The solicitors."

"The mere's early too." Marguerite set down the kettle she'd carried up and didn't pretend otherwise. "It's in your garden, Edith. It's in the lane. There are fish—I think they're fish—swimming past your kitchen window."

"There always were." Edith smiled with half her mouth. "You just weren't meant to see them yet. That was part of what Agnes bought, you understand. Time. Fifty years of you not looking back, and the mere keeping its distance, and everyone in this village forgetting your face five minutes after you left the room. It was a good bargain. It held." Her eyes went to the window, where the light had gone the green of deep lake water though the morning outside was grey. "It's not holding today."

"Why today?"

"Because I'm the last signature, girl. Agnes is dead. Tam's name was never on anything that mattered. When I sign, the house passes out of Underhill hands for the first time since before there was a mere worth naming. And the arrangement lives in these walls." Edith laid one transparent hand flat on the papers. "Dissolve the house, dissolve the terms. Whatever's been waiting politely at the edge gets to stop waiting."

* * *

The solicitors arrived at the door at ten minutes past nine, which was wrong, because Marguerite had heard their tires at nine exactly, and she understood then that time in Hollowmere had begun moving the way shadows move—forward, but not only forward.

There were two of them: a young man with a briefcase and an older woman whose smile never quite reached the muscles around her eyes. They dripped on the mat. Neither remarked on the fact that they were soaked to the knees, or that the hallway behind them glistened as if the sea had come up the drive to see them in.

"Mrs. Crane?" the woman said. "We're so sorry for the weather. Shall we proceed?"

They proceeded. That was the horror of it—the ordinary machinery of it all, the reading aloud of clauses, the initialing of pages, while the smell of deep water filled the house. It came up through the floors the way heat comes up from a cellar: cold, mineral, ancient. Marguerite stood by the parlor door and watched dust motes drift upward instead of down. On the wall, the photographs had changed overnight. In every one, the wooden chair sat closer than it had ever sat before. In the christening picture from 1974, it was no longer at the mere's edge at all. It was in the church. It was in the front pew. It was empty, and every head in the photograph was turned very slightly toward it, except her grandmother's, and her grandmother was looking directly out of the frame at Marguerite with an expression she had spent fifty years refusing to read.

*Don't look back,* Agnes's face said. *Whatever happens today, don't look back.*

* * *

The visitor arrived without arriving.

One moment Marguerite was alone by the door; the next there was a presence at her shoulder like a change in air pressure, a coolness against her cheek as if she'd turned her face toward an open window over black water. She did not look. She had learned that much. But she could feel it standing beside her—small, child-height, patient—and she could hear, beneath the solicitor's droning recitation of covenants and boundaries, a sound like someone practicing breathing.

It had gotten better at that. At being almost a person. Last week it had arranged her childhood slippers by the hearth, toes pointing out, the way she'd left them when she was seven. Yesterday it had hummed three notes of a lullaby she'd never been taught. This morning, when she passed the hall mirror, there had been two figures reflected where there should have been one, and the smaller one had its hand raised—not grasping, not threatening. *Waving.*

"Miss Underhill." The older solicitor was looking at her. "You're named in the estate as next of kin witness. If you could countersign page four."

"I—" Marguerite's pen was in her hand. She had no memory of taking it out. The visitor's coolness pressed gently against her shoulder, guiding her toward the table, courteous as an usher. *This way. This is your place. You've always had a place here; you simply couldn't see it.*

"Don't," Edith said. It was the sharpest word Marguerite had ever heard her use, and it cost her—a cough that bent her double, that brought up something dark. When she straightened, her lips were stained. "She doesn't sign. Nobody signs until we've finished what Agnes started. Read your own papers, Mrs. Ashworth. Clause eleven. The boundary holds *while the house remains in trust*—and there's a trustee yet to be appointed. Or did you leave that page in the car?"

Something moved across the solicitor's face. For a heartbeat the professional mask slipped and underneath it was something older and wetter and glad, and then the mask came back down and she said, smoothly, "Of course. We'll need the trustee's declaration before completion can occur."

"And you won't get it," Edith said, "because the trustee hasn't decided yet."

* * *

Afterward—after the solicitors withdrew to their dripping car, after the door closed on them—Marguerite sat on the stairs with her head in her hands while the house breathed around her.

"How long do I have?" she asked.

Edith's voice came thin from the bedroom above. "Until I die, or until I sign. Whichever comes first. And I'm told the signing is scheduled for tomorrow morning, and I'm told—" a wet, rattling laugh— "that I may not last the night regardless. The mere knows. It's rising to meet me. Half of Hollowmere's lanes are underwater already. Old Tam says the ferryman's daughter saw lights down there, rows of them, like windows."

Marguerite climbed the stairs. In the doorway of the bedroom she stopped, because the visitor was there before her—sitting, actually sitting, in the chair beside Edith's bed, small hands folded in its lap, watching the old woman with an expression of such naked, terrible longing that Marguerite's chest cracked open down the middle.

It wanted to be family. That was the whole of it. Fifty years of learning to sit in photographs, warming a chair, mimicking the habits of a child it had never been allowed to be, and all of it—all of it—was the wanting of a place at a bedside, a hand held, a name spoken in love rather than in contract.

"It isn't fair," Marguerite said quietly. "What they did to you."

The visitor turned its head. She still could not describe the face—it slid away from description the way water slides from glass—but she felt its attention settle on her like a hand, gentle and cold and full of something that had waited so long it had forgotten any other shape.

"You were promised a place," Marguerite said. "And they gave you a chair in pictures instead. A name in a box under the floor. They fed you paper and called it a meal." She knelt slowly, bringing herself down to its height, and the smell of deep water rose around them both like a tide. "I'm going to fix it. Not the way they would. Not with another trade. Properly."

Edith made a sound. Marguerite looked up. The old woman was holding out the torn ballad verses—the ones Tam had pieced together, the ones the found family had decoded over three nights of candlelight and bad tea—and her eyes were bright and afraid.

"The last verse needs a name given freely," Edith whispered. "Not taken. Given. That's the part the village never understood, and it's why the bargain's been rotting for fifty years. Everything taken rots. Only what's given freely holds." Her fingers trembled on the paper. "Tomorrow, when they come with the pen—if you mean to do this, girl—you'll have hours, not days. The mere will take the garden by dawn. Maybe the house."

Outside, the light failed all at once, though it was barely noon. Down in the drowned garden, far below the surface of the black mirror, the pale shape of the chair was no longer faint.

It was near the top now. Rising.

And beside Marguerite, patient as winter, the visitor took her hand—its grip cool and careful, a child learning the weight of a hand it had never been allowed to hold—and waited, politely, for what it believed it was owed.
