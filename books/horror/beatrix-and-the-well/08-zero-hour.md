## 8. Zero Hour

Dana Kesh had spent twenty-two years in construction and she knew the sound a job made when it was about to go wrong. It wasn't the crash or the shout—it was the quiet before, the way men stopped whistling, the way the radios carried nothing but breath. Hollow Creek had been making that sound for six days now, ever since the frost began blooming on windows in July, spreading outward from the well site in rings so perfect they looked drawn with a compass.

She was at her sister's house when Petey reached zero.

Not her sister—Petey's sister, Marisol Vane, who lived three streets over in a yellow clapboard house with a porch swing that hadn't swung since 2019. Dana had gone there because half the crew had gathered there, the way people gather when something is wrong and no one will say what it is. They'd brought casseroles nobody ate. They'd brought beer nobody opened. They'd brought Petey himself, who had been counting for eleven days, backwards from some number only he could see, his lips moving through conversations like a man praying in a language he'd never learned.

It had started small. A number under his breath while he tied rebar. Then during lunch, between bites of sandwich. Then all the time—a low, constant murmur, like a faucet someone couldn't quite shut off. *Four hundred and two. Four hundred and one.* He didn't remember doing it. That was the part that hollowed everyone out. You'd ask him about it and he'd look at you with honest, frightened eyes and say, *Doing what?* And then, thirty seconds later: *Three hundred ninety-eight.*

The town had seen this before, in a way. The dying counted—that was old knowledge, whispered knowledge, the kind of thing hospice nurses knew and everyone else pretended not to. But the dying were supposed to count. That was the arrangement of things. The living were exempt.

Until they weren't.

* * *

Marisol's kitchen smelled of coffee gone bitter on the warmer and the cinnamon candles she burned year-round. Seven people crowded the room: Dana, two crew members, Marisol's neighbor, the pastor's wife, an old man named Cobb who claimed he'd been at the well site the day they pulled the door out of the ground. And Petey, sitting at the head of the table in a chair that had belonged to his father, hands flat on the wood like a schoolboy waiting to be called on.

He was down to single digits.

They could tell by the rhythm. For days the numbers had come in long strings, unhurried, almost lazy. Now they came slower, spaced apart, each one landing like a footstep on a stair you couldn't see the bottom of.

"Nine," Petey said, mid-sentence, interrupting himself. He'd been telling Marisol about a fishing trip from 2011, something warm and ordinary, and then: "Nine."

Nobody spoke. The refrigerator hummed. Outside, the street was empty—not summer-empty but *empty*-empty, the way a street looks when every curtain has shifted just enough to let someone watch.

"He doesn't have to sit here," Marisol said, for maybe the fortieth time. Her voice had gone thin over the eleven days, worn down to wire. "We could take him to St. Mary's. To the county hospital. Somebody there would know—"

"Somebody there would know what?" Cobb said. Not cruelly. Just tiredly, the way you correct a child who keeps asking if the storm will miss the house. "You want some resident to put electrodes on his head? You think this is in his *head*, Mari?"

"It's in his mouth," Marisol said. "That's where I hear it."

"Eight," said Petey.

Dana watched him. She'd hired him four years ago off a work-release program, a skinny kid with a laugh too big for his chest, and she'd never once regretted it. He was the first one up on cold mornings. He remembered everybody's kids' names. He had a tattoo of a lighthouse on his forearm that he'd gotten at nineteen because, he said, somebody ought to keep a light somewhere.

Now he sat with his hands flat and his eyes clear and his mouth full of numbers, and the worst thing—the thing Dana kept circling back to like a tongue finding a broken tooth—was that he wasn't afraid anymore. He had been afraid the first week. Now he was calm. Calm the way Mr. Ellery had been calm, if the stories were true, the old man who'd counted down from forty-one in the hospice ward and smiled when he hit zero before his heart quit.

The dying smiled. That was the other piece of old whisper-knowledge. Nobody talked about what they smiled *at*.

"Seven," Petey said.

"Stop him," the pastor's wife whispered. "Somebody make him stop. Give him something to chew. Make him sing. Interrupt the—"

"Interrupt it with what?" Cobb asked. "You got a verse that beats whatever's under this town?"

And that was the sentence that broke the room open, because until then they had all been carefully not saying *under this town*, had been carefully not saying *the door*, had been carefully not saying *we cut it open and it opened inward, onto stairs, going down farther than anything we poured foundations for*. Dana had led that descent herself. She had stood in the dark below with a flashlight beam that seemed to bend, looking into thirteen alcoves cut into stone, twelve of them holding shapes she still couldn't describe without her hands going numb. Two of the four who'd gone down hadn't come back right. The town had locked them out of their own houses. She hadn't stopped them from doing it.

"Six," said Petey.

Marisol started crying, quietly, the way she did everything, folding a dish towel against her mouth. Her husband had left for his brother's place in Cedar Falls on day three and hadn't called back, which wasn't abandonment exactly—lots of people had left, or tried—but which felt like it anyway.

Dana stood up. She didn't know why. Some instinct told her to stand, the same instinct that had told her, standing over that stone door with its iron ring fused solid, *don't*. She'd ignored it then. She wouldn't ignore it now.

"Petey." She put her hand on his shoulder. His skin was cool—not corpse-cool, but cellar-cool, root-cellar cool. "Look at me, kid."

He looked at her. His eyes were fine. That was the horror of it; his eyes were completely, ordinarily fine, brown and tired and fond of her the way they'd always been.

"When you get where you're going," she said, and heard how it sounded, and said it anyway, "you hold on to your name. You hear me? Peter Vane. You keep saying it. Whatever else is in your mouth, you make room for that."

Something moved behind his face. Not fear. Recognition, maybe—or the memory of recognition, like a word on the tip of the tongue.

"Five," he said. And then, softer, almost tenderly, as if correcting a child: "*Five.*"

* * *

He reached zero at 4:17 in the afternoon, though afterward no one in that kitchen would agree on the time, because every clock in the house said something different and none of them said 4:17.

What Dana remembered was the silence.

Eleven days of counting, and then zero, and then nothing—and the nothing was worse. It dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and everyone flinched from the splash that never came. Petey sat perfectly still. His lips stopped moving. His chest rose and fell. He blinked. He turned his head toward the window, toward the light coming gold and wrong through the frost-ferned glass, and he smiled.

He smiled the way the dying smiled. Dana knew it instantly, bone-deep, the way you know a face in a funeral photo.

"Petey?" Marisol's voice cracked down the middle. "Petey, baby, say something. Say my name. Say—"

Petey turned to her and said, in a voice that was not his voice, "There now, sweetheart, don't fuss. I've got you."

It was their mother's voice. Dana had never met Mrs. Vane, dead eight years, but she saw Marisol take it like a physical blow, saw her grab the edge of the counter, because there was no mistaking it—the cadence, the little upward lilt, the *sweetheart* worn smooth by decades of use. Petey had been nine when she died. Whatever lived in his mouth now had not learned that voice from recordings.

"No," Marisol said. "No, no, no—"

"Hush," said Petey, in their father's voice this time, gravel and tobacco. "Hush now. Everything's paid up."

Then, in a woman's voice none of them knew, high and formal and old-fashioned, with vowels shaped by another century: "Is the vessel present? We were promised the vessel."

The kitchen went cold. Not figuratively. Dana felt it move across the backs of her neck like a hand, and the coffee on the warmer stopped steaming, and the frost on the window spread a quarter inch toward the center of the pane, all its ferns leaning inward like grass bending under wind.

"The vessel," the strange voice repeated, patient as arithmetic. "The withheld one. She is still above us. Tell her the account stands. Tell her the interest has compounded. Tell her—"

And then Petey's own voice surfaced, briefly, drowning: "*Mari—Mari, I can't—I'm still in here, I'm still—*" and then it went under again, and the smiling resumed, and Dana did the hardest thing she had done in twenty-two years of hard things.

She took Petey gently by the arm, and Marisol by the elbow, and she got them both out of that kitchen and into her truck, because whatever was speaking through him wanted something delivered, and she was not going to let it be delivered by a boy who was still, somewhere inside, holding on.

*Hold on to your name,* she'd told him. She was starting to understand that some doors you can't unopen, but you can decide who walks through them.

* * *

By nightfall, Hollow Creek understood that it was alone.

It happened in pieces, the way bad news always does. The county line was forty minutes east; the sheriff's office in Harlow had taken Dana's first call on day two and sent a deputy, who had driven to the well site, stood looking at the frost for ten minutes, driven home, and filed a report about vandalism and mass hysteria. Her second call today had rung differently. Not busy. Not voicemail. It rang, and rang, and then—there was no other word for it—it rang *backward*. A rising tone instead of a falling one, a bell being pulled up out of water instead of struck. She'd held the phone to her ear for a full minute, listening to that impossible ascending ring, feeling it in her fillings, before she hung up.

Across town, other people were getting the same thing. Phones ringing backward. Texts sending as blank gray rectangles. The one radio station out of Harlow playing music that sounded normal until you noticed the songs were all oldies, all decades stale, the DJ's patter looping every eleven minutes. The state road crew had cone-and-barriered Route 9 at the county line—for repaving, the sign said, though no equipment had come, and when Cobb drove out to look, the barriers were already furred with frost.

"They're not coming," the pastor's wife said that evening, in the community hall where maybe sixty people had gathered, which was most of the town, which was everyone who hadn't fled and everyone who'd tried and found the roads strangely, politely impassable. "Nobody's coming. We called everybody. We are—" She stopped, because finishing the sentence meant living in it.

"We're on our own," Dana said, so nobody else had to.

The hall smelled of dust and old basketball games and the pine cleaner the janitor used twice a year. Someone had set up coffee. Someone had brought a box of candles, though the electricity worked fine; the candles were for morale, or against something, nobody said. On the wall hung the banner from last year's Founders' Day: HOLLOW CREEK — 150 YEARS, EST. 1874, SURVIVING TOGETHER. Someone had laughed when they saw it, a short ugly laugh, and then apologized.

Dana stood at the front with the ledger under her arm—the founders' ledger she'd pulled from the historical society archive, the one with the column labeled REMAINDER, the one with her own family name appearing eleven times down the years like a stain that wouldn't wash out. She hadn't shown it to anyone yet. She didn't have to. Word traveled in a town this size faster than frost.

"There's a nurse," said old Ruth Calloway, from the third row, in a voice like dry leaves. Ruth, who ran the historical society and whose family name appeared in the ledger more times than anyone's. "Montrose. August Montrose. She's been asking questions. About Adaline Voss."

"Voss," someone repeated. "The one in the photographs."

"The one in the photographs," Ruth agreed, and looked straight at Dana, and Dana felt the ledger grow heavy as a headstone under her arm.

Outside, through the hall's tall windows, the first stars were out, and beneath them the town lay quiet—not sleeping-quiet, but listening-quiet, sixty households lying awake in the dark, and in the walls, in the pipes, in the space between the studs where insulation should have been, the counting had begun. Not one voice. Dozens. Half the town counting in its sleep, murmuring down through the numbers in a slow communal descent, and the sound rose through the summer night like heat off asphalt, converging, everyone falling toward the same bottom of the same stairwell.

Dana stood in the community hall with a dead boy's smile still printed on the inside of her eyelids and a message she hadn't delivered yet burning a hole in her pocket:

*Tell her the account stands.*

She looked out at the faces of her neighbors, at the candle flames standing perfectly straight in air that should have been moving, and she understood, with the terrible clarity of a foreman reading a load-bearing wall, that whatever came next would not be survived by hiding.

Someone had to finish what the founders started. And the frost, blooming in concentric rings across every window in Hollow Creek, had already written the name of who it wanted.
