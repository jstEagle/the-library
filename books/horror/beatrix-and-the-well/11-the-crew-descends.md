## 11. The Crew Descends

Dana Kesh had spent twenty-two years in construction and she knew the sound a building made when it was settling—wood ticking, concrete sighing, the small honest noises of things finding their level. Hollow Creek was not settling. Hollow Creek was holding its breath.

She stood at the edge of the excavation pit at six in the morning, coffee going cold in her gloved hand, and looked down at the door.

It hadn't changed overnight, which somehow made it worse. Stone the color of old teeth, fitted so precisely that a knife blade couldn't find the seams, and that iron ring fused to the face of it like it had grown there. Sealed from the inside. She'd said those words to the county engineer on the phone three times now and each time he'd gone quiet in a way that wasn't confusion but recognition, and then he'd changed the subject to liability insurance.

The frost was still there too. July frost, blooming up out of the ground around the door's edges in feathered whorls that never melted, never spread, just sat there like something waiting to be acknowledged.

"Boss." Petey stood behind her, and when she turned he was looking at his own boots. "You're really doing this."

"You don't have to come."

"I know." He swallowed. "That's why I'm coming. If I stay up here I'll just stand around counting my fingers or whatever the hell this town does now." He tried to laugh and it came out wrong, thin as wire.

Three volunteers. That's what she'd asked for and that's what she'd gotten, though *volunteer* was doing generous work with two of them. Petey, because fear was worse than cold for him now. Marcus Odele, because his sister had started counting in her sleep and he'd decided—if you could call the flat look in his eyes a decision—that knowing was better than not knowing. And Tom Brandt, who was sixty-one years old and had poured foundations all over this county and who had come to her office the night before without being asked and said only: "My grandfather helped pour the cap over that well in '54. He wouldn't talk about it for thirty years. I want to see what shut him up."

And August Montrose, who was not coming, and who had stood in Dana's field office trailer until midnight telling her exactly why nobody should.

*You don't open it. You especially don't open it with fire. It sealed itself from the inside—that means it chose to close. Things that choose to close have reasons.*

Dana had heard her out. Had watched the nurse's hands shake around a paper cup of terrible coffee while her voice stayed perfectly level, which told Dana more than the words did. Then she'd thanked her, walked her to her car, watched her taillights go down the access road, and gone back inside to lay out the cutting torches.

Because here was the thing Dana couldn't say to August Montrose, couldn't say to anyone: she'd read the ledger. Eleven Keshes in that column marked *Remainder*. Her name was written into this hole in the ground eleven times before she was ever born, and if there was a debt sitting under Hollow Creek with her family's name on it, then by God she was going to be the one who walked down and read the terms out loud. Not because she was brave. Because the alternative was standing in her kitchen every night listening to half the town count backwards through the walls and pretending she didn't recognize her grandmother's voice among them.

Hubris, maybe. Her ex-husband would have said so. But courage and hubris lived in the same house and shared a wall, and some mornings you couldn't tell which one was talking until you were already moving.

"Gear check," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Torches, tanks, spare regulators. Ropes. Lights—three per person, minimum. Nobody goes anywhere alone, nobody touches anything, and if anybody hears counting—" she paused, hating herself for what she was about to say, "—you say your own name. Out loud. As many times as it takes."

"Why your name?" Marcus asked.

"Because it's yours," Dana said. "And right now that's the only thing in this town we're sure belongs to us."

* * *

The torch lit with a hiss that echoed off the pit walls, blue-white flame bending slightly toward the stone, which Tom noticed first and pointed out with a grunt: flame doesn't bend unless air is moving, and nothing should be moving down there.

The seal resisted.

Not the way stone resists—not dumb mass, not heat absorption. Dana had cut through vault doors and ship hulls and once, memorably, a decommissioned bank floor full of rebar laid like a practical joke. Stone got hot, glowed, gave way. This did not get hot. The flame washed against the surface and the surface drank it, and the temperature gauge on the torch climbed past numbers Marcus didn't like, and the stone stayed gray and cold and patient.

"It's not working," Petey said. His voice had that stretched quality it got lately, the one where you could hear him listening underneath his own words.

"It's working," Dana said. "Keep cutting."

"How do you know?"

"Because it's *paying attention* now."

She couldn't have explained how she knew that. But the frost around the door's edges had begun to move—slowly, the way ice shifts on a pond before it gives—and the feathers of it were rearranging themselves into lines, and the lines were arranging themselves into rows, and the rows looked uncomfortably like columns in a ledger.

Tom took the second torch. They worked in tandem, tracing the outline of where a seam might be, and after forty minutes of screaming metal and sweat freezing on their brows—the pit was cold, cold enough that their breath hung like ghosts—they found it. A seam. Hairline-thin, running vertical along the left edge, and where the flame touched it the stone finally, grudgingly, began to glow a dull red that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than the surface.

Then the radios started.

All four of them, clipped to their harnesses, crackled at once—a soft chorus of static that resolved, the way it always did now, into voices. Counting. Faint, overlapping, dozens of mouths saying numbers in unison like a congregation, and the numbers were going *down*, and Dana recognized one of the voices and hated herself for recognizing it: Mrs. Aldous from the pharmacy, who'd sold her cough drops two weeks ago and been fine, been normal, been alive in all the ordinary ways.

"Say your names," Dana barked. "Now."

"*Dana Kesh,*" she said, loud, into the static. "*Dana Kesh.*"

Petey said his. Marcus said his. Tom said his, twice, calm as reading a grocery list, and the counting in the radios stuttered—actually stuttered, like a needle skipping—and went quiet.

The seam glowed brighter.

"We're almost through," Tom said. "Another inch of depth and—"

He stopped. They all stopped, because the door moved.

Not much. A shudder, deep in the stone, transmitted up through their boots and into their teeth. The kind of movement you feel in a building when something enormous on another floor sets down something heavy. Dust sifted from the pit walls. The frost-lines around the door flared white and then, all at once, went dark—not melted, *dark*, the way a screen goes dark, the way an eye closes.

"It knows we're here," Marcus whispered. "August said—it knows, it's been paying attention the whole—"

"The whole time, yeah." Dana wiped her face with the back of her glove. Her hand was steady. She made it steady; that counted for something. "Everybody take five minutes. Water. Check your lights."

Petey didn't take five minutes. Petey took thirty seconds, sat down hard on a rock, put his head between his knees, and said, in a small voice with no performance left in it at all: "I can't do this, Dana. I'm sorry. I thought—I thought I could, but it's in my head already, it's been in my head since the site, and if I go down there it'll finish me. I'll hit zero. I can feel it waiting for me to hit zero."

Nobody laughed. Nobody told him to man up. That era of this crew was over.

"Tom," Dana said quietly, "walk him up. Take the truck keys."

"I can walk myself," Petey said, and then he was crying, ugly and open, a grown man sobbing on a rock in a July morning cold enough to see his breath, and he got up and hugged Dana fast and fierce, smelling of torch smoke and fear-sweat, and climbed the ladder out of the pit without looking back at the door. Which was, Dana thought, the smartest thing anyone had done all week.

Tom hesitated at the ladder's base. Sixty-one years old, torch scarred hands, grandfather silent for thirty years.

"You sure about this, boss?"

"No," Dana said. "But I'm sure about the alternative, and it's worse."

Tom nodded slowly. Then he surprised her—he reached out and pressed his palm flat against the door, the way August Montrose had described doing, and held it there for a long moment. Whatever he felt, his face recorded none of it.

"My granddad used to say the founders built this town on a handshake," he said. "Never said who with." He turned and climbed.

Which left two.

* * *

Marcus and Dana stood before the door with the torches guttering, and the seam they'd cut glowed faintly red, a hairline of ember in the gray.

"One more pass," Dana said. "Right along the line. Full pressure."

They cut together, twin flames converging, and this time the stone gave—not breaking, but *opening*, the way a lock opens, the way something designed opens. The seam widened. Cold air breathed out of it, and it was wrong air, old air, air that tasted of iron and snow and something underneath both, something mineral and vast and aware, and the hairs on Dana's arms rose beneath her jacket.

The door swung inward.

No groan of hinges. No resistance. It simply opened, smooth and silent as a held breath released, onto darkness.

Stairs. Wide ones, cut from the same tooth-colored stone, descending at a gentle grade into the earth. The light from their lamps reached perhaps thirty feet down before the dark thickened and refused it. The stairs kept going past the reach of the light, and kept going, and there was no sound of them ending.

"That's not possible," Marcus said. His lamp shook. "The town's foundations—the bedrock table, the survey—we're maybe forty feet above the water table here. Those stairs go below it. They go way below it."

"Surveys measure what people built," Dana said. "This wasn't built by people. Or it was, and they weren't measuring."

She tested the first step with her boot. Solid. Cold radiating up through the sole. She clipped her rope to the anchor Tom had set at the pit's edge, paid out slack, and looked back once—at the ladder, at the gray sky, at the ordinary world with its trucks and its thermoses and its July—and then down, into the stairwell, where the darkness had texture now, a depth like water.

"Say your name again," she told Marcus.

"Dana Kesh," he said.

She almost smiled. Almost. "Close enough."

They descended.

Twenty steps. Fifty. A hundred, and the entrance above was still visible, a rectangle of gray daylight no bigger than a playing card. Two hundred, and it was gone, and the air had gone colder, and their breath plumed silver in the lamplight, and the walls had begun to change—the rough-cut stone smoothing, then dressing itself in long horizontal bands, and in the bands there were marks. Grooves. Rows of them.

Dana ran her glove along one band and felt notches. Regular, deliberate, evenly spaced.

"Notches," Marcus said hoarsely. "Like tally marks."

"Or numbers," Dana said, and didn't say the rest: *counting down.*

At three hundred steps the stairs ended—not at a bottom, but at a landing, and the landing opened onto a corridor, and from somewhere ahead of them, far down in the dark, came a sound that stopped Dana's heart and restarted it sideways.

Breathing. Slow, even, patient as a held breath let out over centuries.

And beneath it, faint and clear and horribly familiar, a single voice—not the chorus from the radios, just one voice, dry as paper—saying:

"...four. Three. Two."

A pause. Long enough for Dana's blood to freeze in her veins.

Then, gently, almost kindly, in a voice that sounded the way her own name looked written in an old ledger:

"One is waiting for you, Dana Kesh. Come and see the arithmetic."

Marcus grabbed her arm. In the lamplight his face was the color of the frost outside, and behind him, at the top of the stairs they'd descended, the rectangle of daylight had gone out—as if the door, all the way up there in the July morning, had quietly closed itself behind them.
