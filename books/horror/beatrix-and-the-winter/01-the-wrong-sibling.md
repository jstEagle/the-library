## 1. The Wrong Sibling

The night bus to Hollowmere ran once a week, and it was the kind of bus that made you understand why.

Zephyrine had the whole back seat to themselves, which should have been a comfort and instead felt like being set aside. The driver—a broad man with a mustache that seemed to be doing most of his emotional labor—had taken their fare without looking up from a crossword puzzle. Eleven across, *provincial gloom*, four letters. Zephyrine wanted to tell him it was *mere*. They didn't. Twenty years away from a place teaches you to keep small observations to yourself, like coins you're saving for a vending machine that no longer exists.

They watched the dark fields slide past the window, stitched together by the occasional streetlight, and rehearsed what they would say at the funeral.

*I'm sorry I wasn't there.*

No. Too much about themselves. Funerals were not the venue for confessions; funerals were where you swallowed them whole and let them sit in your chest like a swallowed stone, which was, Zephyrine supposed, the entire traditional purpose of funerals.

*Beatrix, I—*

But Beatrix wouldn't hear it. That was the thing about dead sisters. They were the one audience you could never perform for again, and so every apology drafted for them was really just a letter of resignation from your own conscience.

Twenty years of silence. Not a falling-out—Zephyrine would have preferred a falling-out, honestly, because those came with dialogue, with scenes, with the satisfying architecture of blame. No, theirs had been a slow evaporating. A missed call returned three weeks late. A birthday card that arrived after the birthday, then no card at all. Beatrix had stayed in Hollowmere because Beatrix stayed in Hollowmere the way rivers stay in valleys—it was simply the shape she'd settled into—and Zephyrine had left the way some water leaves: as fog, as rain somewhere else entirely.

And now Beatrix was dead, and Zephyrine was on a bus with a broken reading light and a seat that hummed like a hive, going home to bury the only person who had ever known both halves of their name.

*Zephyrine.* Their mother had found it in a book of storm names and thought it was lovely. *Beatrix* had come from their grandmother, who believed names should be sturdy enough to lean on. Between the two of them, the twins had been saddled with a gale and a fortress, and Zephyrine had always privately thought they'd gotten the worse end. Nobody leans on a wind. A wind is just weather that happens to you.

The bus shuddered around a bend, and there it was.

Hollowmere did not appear so much as condense out of the dark. First the water tower, leaning its old tired lean against the sky. Then the church spire, then the long low line of rooftops along the mere itself, black water holding whatever light the town offered and giving none of it back. Zephyrine's chest did something complicated—not grief exactly, more like an old injury remembering itself when the weather changed.

The bus sighed to a stop outside the Hollowmere General Store, which still had the same hand-painted sign, though the *E* in GENERAL had faded into a ghost of itself, so the sign now read G NERAL, which struck Zephyrine as accidentally honest.

"End of the line," the driver said, and then, glancing up at last, squinting: "Evening, Miss Ravensworth."

Zephyrine opened their mouth to correct him and then closed it again. Of course he'd assume. There was only one Ravensworth left in Hollowmere. Only one Ravensworth anyone here had seen in twenty years.

"Thank you," they said instead, and stepped down onto the street, and the cold came up through their shoes like the ground had been waiting for them personally.

* * *

The town was asleep, mostly. But not entirely.

A dog barked twice from somewhere near the mill and then stopped mid-bark, as if someone had hushed it. A light burned in the window of the house on Corben Street—the Aldous house, Zephyrine remembered, though the name surfaced from twenty years down and might have belonged to anyone. And standing under the lamp outside the store, as if he'd been placed there by a stagehand who hadn't been told the scene was over, was old Mr. Vetch.

He looked terrible. He had always looked terrible, in fairness—Vetch had the kind of face that suggested it had been assembled during a shortage—but now he looked terrible in a new way, thinner, grayer, like a photograph of himself left too long in a window.

"Well," said Vetch, and smiled, and the smile was the worst part, because it was warm. Warmth from Mr. Vetch was like warmth from a radiator in an empty house: technically present, deeply wrong. "You came back."

"For the funeral," Zephyrine said. "Yes."

"Of course, of course." He nodded several times, more than the sentence required. "We've all been so glad. Knowing you were coming. It's been—" he searched for the word and landed on one that didn't fit, "—*settling*."

"I'm sorry," Zephyrine said, "do we know each other well enough for settling? Last time we spoke I was eleven and you told me my kite was going to land in the mere, and it did."

Vetch laughed. It was a good laugh, actually, rusty but real, and for half a second the warmth stopped being wrong. "You'll want to get up to the house," he said. "It's late. You shouldn't stand about."

"It's my house," Zephyrine said. "I can stand about in it."

Something crossed Vetch's face then—quick, gone before Zephyrine could name it, though later, much later, they would learn its name was *pity*. "Sure," he said. "Course you can." He touched the brim of his cap, turned, and walked off toward Corben Street, and Zephyrine noticed that he walked on the far side of the lampposts, keeping to the dark between them, like a man crossing a river on stones.

* * *

The walk up Raven Lane took eleven minutes and cost Zephyrine approximately everything.

Not because anything happened. Because nothing happened, loudly. The lane was exactly as they remembered it—the same crooked fence posts, the same hawthorn hedge grown into a wall, the same gap in the hedge where the twins used to squeeze through to reach the mere—and the sameness pressed against Zephyrine like a hand on the back of the neck. Places are supposed to change when you leave them. That's the deal. You go, they age, you come back to find your childhood home smaller and sadder and painted a color you don't remember, and the disappointment is how you know time is real.

Hollowmere had not honored the deal.

The house stood at the top of the lane exactly as it had stood for two hundred years, which was to say: large, gray-gabled, and patient. Its windows caught the moon. Its chimney stood straight. Somebody had mowed the lawn recently, in neat stripes, which meant somebody had been maintaining a dead woman's grass, and Zephyrine found this somehow more upsetting than the death itself.

They climbed the porch steps. The boards knew their weight. That was the thing about old houses—they kept records.

And that was when Zephyrine saw the wreath.

It hung on the front door, black ribbon and white flowers, fresh, dew still on the petals. Beneath it, tacked neatly to the wood at eye level, was a card in careful copperplate:

**In loving memory of BEATRIX RAVENSWORTH — beloved sister, devoted daughter of Hollowmere. Services Friday, ten o'clock, St. Maren's. Reception following at the house. All welcome.**

Zephyrine read it three times. The words refused to rearrange themselves into sense.

Someone had arranged the funeral. Someone had ordered flowers, printed cards, chosen a Friday, notified the town. Someone had done all the work of mourning while Zephyrine was still on a bus, still drafting apologies, still believing—foolishly, they could see that now—that they would arrive and find a house full of decisions that needed making, tasks that needed doing, grief that needed company. That had been the plan. That had been the entire moral architecture of the trip: *I was too late for her life, but I will not be too late for her death.*

The death had apparently been handled without them. Efficiently. With good penmanship.

Zephyrine tried the door. Unlocked.

Inside, the hall smelled of beeswax and lilies, and a lamp had been left burning on the hall table, beside a stack of mail addressed to Beatrix, sorted, and—Zephyrine saw with a slow, cold turn of the stomach—a funeral program, printed, folded, stacked in dozens. Zephyrine picked one up. On the cover, a photograph of Beatrix at maybe nineteen, smiling the smile she saved for photographs, the one that never reached her eyes. Below it:

*Friday, ten o'clock. St. Maren's Church. Officiant: Rev. E. Tansy. Arrangements by Mrs. H. Calloway, Calloway & Sons, Funeral Directors, est. 1798.*

Est. 1798. The same year the house was built. Zephyrine filed that away without knowing yet why it mattered, the way you pocket a strange coin you find on the ground.

They stood in the hall of their childhood home, holding their sister's funeral program, feeling like a guest who'd arrived after the party. The house creaked overhead in a way that might have been settling timbers and might have been footsteps, and Zephyrine chose, deliberately, consciously, with the full effort of a thirty-four-year-old adult, to believe it was timbers.

"You're here."

Zephyrine spun. Nothing. The hall lamp. The stairs. The long dark throat of the corridor toward the kitchen.

Then, from behind them again—from the direction of the front door, from the porch—

"You're finally here. I was starting to think you'd gotten lost."

The voice was familiar in the way a scar is familiar. Zephyrine knew it before they knew they knew it: a child's voice, bright and slightly sing-song, the voice of someone who had narrated a thousand imaginary expeditions through that very hedge gap.

There was nobody in the hall. Zephyrine was certain of it. They were less certain, with each passing second, why that should comfort them.

They set down the funeral program, picked up their bag, and climbed the stairs to Beatrix's room, telling themselves the sensible things. *Tired. Grieving. Old houses talk.* The room was exactly as Beatrix had kept it—books in crooked towers, a sweater over the chair, a jar of pens on the sill—and on the desk, squared neatly in the center as if waiting, lay a document.

Zephyrine told themselves not to look. They looked.

It was a will. Beatrix Ravensworth's last will and testament, witnessed, notarized, stamped by the county clerk. Everything—the house, the land, the accounts, the *title of resident*, which was an odd phrase to find in a will, written as if it were a piece of furniture—left wholly and completely to her sibling.

Signed at the bottom in Beatrix's handwriting. Dated three days before she died.

Zephyrine stood very still. Outside, the mere lay flat and black under the moon, and the house breathed around them, and somewhere below—on the porch, probably, or in the yard, or nowhere at all—a voice that belonged to no living person said, softly, almost kindly:

"I kept your seat."

* * *

Zephyrine did not sleep. They sat on the edge of Beatrix's bed until the windows went gray, listening to the house make its small sounds, and when the gray turned gold they went downstairs to make tea in a kitchen where two cups had already been set out on the table—one at Beatrix's place, and one, they realized as they stood there with the kettle ticking, at the little stool by the window where no adult had ever sat.

Where a child used to sit, twenty years ago, and talk to someone no one else could see.

Zephyrine looked at the second cup for a long time.

Then they went to the front door, opened it, and stepped out onto the porch—to ask the morning air, the empty lane, the indifferent sunrise, whoever might be listening, one simple question:

"Who's been in my house?"

The porch swing moved, gently, though there was no wind.

And on it, wearing a coat that had been out of fashion since 1998, sat someone who smiled up at Zephyrine with absolute delight and said:

"There you are. I *told* you I'd wait."
