## 12. The Room Opens

Zephyrine had counted the rooms twice that evening, the way you might count your fingers after a bad dream—expecting them all to be there, checking anyway.

The first count said eleven. The second said twelve.

They sat on the edge of Beatrix's bed with the lamp on and their shoes still laced, because a person who has learned anything at all about Hollowmere learns not to get comfortable. The house had gone quiet in the particular way of an animal pretending to sleep. Somewhere below, the floorboards ticked as they cooled, or as they thought, Zephyrine couldn't say which anymore.

At 11:58, they heard a door open.

Not slam. Not creak. *Open*—with the soft, deliberate courtesy of a butler announcing a guest. One long exhalation of hinges, then silence, then the faint smell of pencil shavings drifting up through the floor like a memory with a body attached.

Zephyrine did not go down immediately. They were proud of this. Twenty years ago they would have run straight toward it, the way they'd once run toward every strange sound in this house, calling out *Beatrix? Was that you?* while Beatrix rolled her eyes from the safety of her own bed. Twenty years had taught Zephyrine at least one skill: the ability to sit very still and be afraid in an organized fashion.

They lasted four minutes.

The hallway was dark except for the moon through the landing window, and there—at the end of it, where the wall had been flat and blank since childhood, where the blueprints in Mrs. Calloway's tidy file insisted no door had ever hung—stood a door, wide open, breathing out cold air that smelled of winter mornings and woodsmoke and, underneath it all, faintly, of libraries.

Wren was already standing beside it.

"You're supposed to be watching the stairs," Zephyrine whispered.

"I was." Wren's coat looked damp at the shoulders, though it hadn't rained. "The stairs were fine. This is more interesting."

"It's a door that doesn't exist."

"It's a door that exists *selectively.* There's a difference, and the difference matters." Wren turned to look at them, and in the moonlight their face did something complicated—the expression of someone who has read the last page of a book and is now forced to watch a friend read the first. "You don't have to go in."

"Obviously I don't."

"You especially don't have to go in tonight."

"Wren."

"Yes?"

"Why are you standing in front of it like an usher?"

A pause. Snow began, very lightly, outside the window—fat, unhurried flakes that fell without any wind, straight down, like the sky was being careful. "Because," Wren said, "the last time someone I loved stood in front of this door, she went in. And I want you to know, before you decide, that whatever's in there will be *kind* to you. That's the trap. It isn't cruel. Cruelty you can fight."

Zephyrine looked past Wren's shoulder into the dark beyond the threshold, and the dark looked back with the patience of a well-stocked pantry.

"Stay close," they said, which was not a yes, and both of them knew it was.

* * *

The room was larger than the house.

This should not have been possible. Zephyrine had spent the afternoon with the blueprints, measuring hallways with a tailor's tape and muttering arithmetic like a curse, and the house was what it was—a narrow, stubborn, three-storey thing built by some ancestor who believed windows were optional. But the room behind the impossible door went back farther than the property line, farther than the garden, farther than sense. Its far wall was only a suggestion, a grayness that might have been distance or might have been weather.

And it was full of everything Zephyrine had ever lost.

There was no other way to describe it. A shelf ran along the left wall, and on it, third from the end, sat the green glass marble Zephyrine had lost in the creek at age seven—a marble they had mourned with the full operatic grief available to small children, and here it was, unscratched, catching the light of a lamp that burned somewhere without a cord. Beside it: the left mitten from the pair Beatrix had knitted—badly, with holes—in their one semester of home economics. The right mitten was still in a drawer in Zephyrine's city apartment, four hundred miles away, waiting for a partner that had been waiting here all along.

"Oh," Zephyrine said. It came out very small.

Farther in, a child's bicycle leaned against nothing, its handlebar tassels stiff with frost that never melted. A fountain pen they'd lost on a train in 2011. A library book overdue by nineteen years, *The Complete Poems of Emily Dickinson*, its due-date card soft with age. A photograph of their mother laughing at something off-camera—one of the photographs that had vanished from the family albums after the funeral-that-wasn't-this-funeral, the ones Zephyrine had privately suspected Beatrix of taking and had never, ever asked about, because asking would have meant admitting they'd noticed.

Every object wore a thin skin of dust, like things kept in a house where no one lives but everything is remembered.

"They're not copies," Wren said quietly, from just inside the doorway. They had not come farther. Zephyrine noticed that; they were beginning to notice a great many things about where Wren would and wouldn't stand. "Don't ask me how I know. I just do. These are the actual lost things. The house keeps them."

"Why?"

"Because losing is a kind of giving," Wren said. "And this place runs on gifts."

Zephyrine walked deeper. Their footsteps made no sound on the floor, which was either stone or snow or memory. Here was a scarf their grandmother had crocheted, unraveling at exactly the thread Zephyrine remembered pulling loose at age nine out of pure destructive curiosity. Here was a shoebox, and their hands were shaking before they touched it, because they knew the weight of it, they knew the specific rattle of it—

Inside: drawings. Crayon and colored pencil, executed with the ferocious confidence of a six-year-old. A sun with too many rays. A horse that was mostly legs. And Wren—dozens of Wrens—drawn over and over across a whole summer, tall and thin in a coat much too big, with buttons drawn as spirals because Zephyrine had never figured out how buttons worked and had decided, correctly as it turned out, that spirals were close enough.

In the corner of the oldest drawing, in wobbling letters: *WREN IS MY FREND AND HE IS TALLER THAN THE HOUSE.*

"He was never taller than the house," Zephyrine said. Their voice cracked in the middle like a dropped plate.

"No," Wren agreed softly. "But you wanted him to be. So for one whole summer, from certain angles, he was."

Zephyrine stood there holding their own childhood in a shoebox, and felt the trap close around them so gently they almost thanked it. That was the horror of the room, they understood suddenly—it wasn't the wrongness of it. It was the *rightness*. Every object hummed with authenticity. The house had not fabricated these things; it had *kept* them, curated them, loved them in the way only a hoarder loves, and what could be more seductive to a person who had spent twenty years being invisible than a room that had noticed every single thing they'd ever dropped?

Somewhere in the grayness at the far end, a table waited under a lamp.

Of course it did. Rooms like this always ended in tables.

* * *

The contract lay on a blotter of deep green leather, squared to the table's edges with a precision that felt like a held breath. A fountain pen rested beside it—not the lost one from the train; a better one, heavier, warm to the touch as if someone had just set it down.

Zephyrine read it standing up, refusing the chair, on the principle that chairs were how this sort of thing got you.

It was short. That was the first shock. After days of ledgers and death certificates and wills dated before deaths, Zephyrine had braced for pages of clauses in Victorian prose. Instead the document was barely half a page, written in a hand so elegant it seemed embarrassed to be legal:

*In consideration of residence, the undersigned grants to the House at Hollowmere one (1) name, freely given, together with all rights, memories, and continuities pertaining thereto.*

*The House shall provide, in exchange: warmth, remembrance, and a place at every table forever.*

Below, in the signature line, in ink that was somehow already dry:

***Zephyrine Ravensworth.***

Their own name. In their own handwriting.

Zephyrine stared at it for a long time. The handwriting was perfect—not similar, not close, but *exact*, down to the way they crossed their z's with a little flourish they'd invented at fourteen to annoy a French teacher, down to the slight rightward lean their letters took when they were tired. Whoever had signed this had reached inside Zephyrine's wrist and used their hand.

"That's forgery," they said finally, because the alternative responses were screaming or crying and neither seemed proportionate yet.

"It's pre-filled," Wren corrected, from the doorway, very far away now—or perhaps the doorway was simply moving, the way doorways in this house liked to. "It's been pre-filled for a long time. Since before you arrived, I'd guess. Possibly since you were born. Possibly—" Wren stopped themselves, which was unlike them.

"Possibly what?"

"Possibly since Beatrix signed hers."

The words landed in the quiet room and rang there. Zephyrine picked up the contract, held it closer to the lamp, and saw what they had missed: beneath the elegant text, ghosted through the paper like a watermark, older writing showed through from the reverse side. Clauses. Dozens of them. Generations of fine print soaked through two hundred years of signatures, all of it bleeding inward toward the center of the page like a stain spreading through cloth.

And there, in the middle of the bleed-through, one phrase repeated so often it had worn a groove in the paper:

*freely given, freely given, freely given.*

"The house can't take," Zephyrine said slowly. "It can only receive."

"Now you see why it builds rooms like this one." Wren's voice had gone gentle in the way that made Zephyrine's chest hurt. "It doesn't push anyone through the door. It fills the room with everything you've ever mourned and then it waits for you to *want* to stay. Wanting is consent. Consent is a signature. The pen is almost a formality."

Zephyrine looked at the contract. At the name in their own hand. At the chair they were still refusing, and the lamp burning without a cord, and the green marble on its shelf three hundred feet away, patient as a saint.

And the terrible thing—the thing they would carry out of this room and hate themselves for later—was that for one full, honest moment, they *wanted* it.

Not the trap. Not the feeding. Just... the room. A place where nothing they loved was ever actually gone. A place that had watched them closely enough to keep the left mitten. Twenty years of being the twin nobody could tell apart, the sibling nobody wrote to, the guest the town called by the wrong name—and here was a house that had never once confused them with anyone. It knew their handwriting. It knew their losses. It had kept every single one.

"Get out of my head," Zephyrine whispered. They weren't sure if they were talking to the house or to themselves.

"I don't think it's in your head," Wren said. "I think that's what frightens you. It's just showing you what's already there."

Zephyrine set the contract down. Squared it back to the blotter's edges, out of spite, out of a need to leave one small mark of order in the room. Did not touch the pen.

"I'm not signing," they said aloud, to the grayness at the far end, to whatever listened from inside the walls' long memory. "For the record. Which I know, now, is the only record that matters."

The lamp flickered—once, twice—and for just a moment the light bent strangely, and in the grayness at the room's far end, Zephyrine saw something move. A figure. Tall. Standing among the shelves with its head tilted, the way Beatrix used to tilt her head when she was deciding whether to tell Zephyrine the truth or a kinder version of it.

"Zeph?" the figure said, across all that impossible distance, in a voice Zephyrine had last heard through a phone line full of static, saying *come home, I need to tell you something.*

The lamp went out.

When Zephyrine's eyes adjusted, the room was only a room—smaller, meaner, its far wall a plain twenty feet away, the shelves bare, the shoebox in their hands suddenly just cardboard and old crayon dust. The door stood open onto the hallway. Beyond it, at the top of the stairs, someone was descending slowly, one careful step at a time, wearing Beatrix's nightgown and Beatrix's face and an expression of such heartbreaking relief that Zephyrine's whole body turned toward it like a compass finding north.

"You came back," the figure said. "I've been trying to reach you for *years*, Zeph. Don't you remember? You died. I'm the one who lived."

Behind Zephyrine, in the doorway of the emptied room, Wren said, very quietly: "Whatever it tells you in the next five minutes—hold the shoebox. Hold the shoebox and count the drawings. Count them, and remember which one of us drew them."
