## 13. Which Twin

The figure was standing at the foot of Zephyrine's bed when they woke, and the first thing Zephyrine thought—before the fear arrived, before the cold, before the arithmetic of *how did she get in*—was that Beatrix had never once in her life respected a closed door.

"Bea," Zephyrine said. Their voice came out sanded down, half-asleep, absurdly ordinary. "You're dead. We discussed this. There was a viewing."

"There was a viewing," Beatrix agreed. She was sitting on the end of the bed now—Zephyrine hadn't seen her move, which was either a trick of the dark or a fact he'd have to think about later, in daylight, with tea. She wore the gray coat Zephyrine remembered from the last winter they'd shared, the one with the crooked button Beatrix had refused to fix on principle. "You cried at it. Very moving. You cried at your own viewing, Zeph."

The room was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season. Zephyrine sat up slowly, the way one sits up in the presence of a wasp.

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny." Beatrix tilted her head, and the moonlight through the curtain gap caught her face, and it *was* her face, every freckle in its correct place, the small scar above her eyebrow from the fence incident of 1998. "You always did cry at the wrong things. You cried when Mrs. Pemberton's cat died and you didn't cry at Gran's funeral. Mum thought there was something wrong with you. There wasn't. There was just something wrong with *which one of you* was doing the feeling."

"Beatrix—"

"Think about it," she said, gently, the way you'd talk someone down off a ledge, or into one. "Really think. When did you last see yourself in a mirror and believe it?"

* * *

The house had been quiet all day in the way a held breath is quiet. After the extra room, after the contract with Zephyrine's name pre-filled in that patient, flourished hand, Zephyrine had done the only sensible thing: made tea, checked the locks, and told themselves firmly that haunted houses, like bullies, fed on reaction.

The tea had tasted like pennies. The locks had been locked. And then they had slept, which in retrospect was the house's move, not theirs.

"I died," Zephyrine said now. "Twenty years ago. In this house."

"You fell," Beatrix said. "The stairs. The same stairs, Zeph. You were carrying my school bag because I'd dared you to race me down and you never could resist a dare, you were so *desperate* to be brave for once, to be the twin people noticed." Her voice was soft. Kind, even. That was the worst part. It was Beatrix's kind voice, the one she'd used on injured birds and crying Zephyrines. "And I couldn't tell anyone. Because who would believe me? And because—" she spread her hands, apologetic, terrible, "—because it was so much easier to be you. You were the one they liked. Quiet, obliging Zephyrine. I just had to keep my head down and be invisible for a while, and you'd already taught me how."

"That's not—" Zephyrine's mouth had gone dry. "That's not true. I left. I went to London. I have a flat in London, I have—"

"Do you?" Beatrix asked. "What color is the front door?"

Zephyrine opened their mouth. Closed it. Reached, in the dark of their own memory, for the door of the flat on Marlowe Street, and found—nothing. A blank where a door should be. A flat with no hallway leading to it. A life with no hinges.

"You don't remember," Beatrix said, not cruelly. "Because there's nothing to remember. You've been here the whole time. Twenty years, Zeph, in this house, and the town got tired of looking at a dead girl's twin wandering the halls, so they stopped seeing you, and you told yourself that was a choice. You told yourself you were *private*." She smiled, and it was Beatrix's smile, and Zephyrine wanted to be sick. "You were never private. You were a ghost who hadn't gotten the memo."

* * *

Zephyrine got up. Put the lamp on. The light fell across Beatrix and she didn't flinch, didn't go translucent, didn't do any of the things a decent hallucination would do to give herself away. She just squinted and said, "That's bright," in Beatrix's exact tone of grievance, and Zephyrine's hands started shaking so badly they had to set the lamp down.

"You're the house," Zephyrine said. "You're the thing in the extra room. You're wearing her."

"Am I?" Beatrix stood, walked to the window—walked, with weight, with the little hitch in her left knee she'd gotten falling out of the rowan tree—and pulled the curtain aside. "Then explain this."

She held out her hand. In her palm lay a small brass button, and Zephyrine knew it before they knew they knew it: the button off Zephyrine's own coat, the one lost in 2004, the one they'd mourned privately and stupidly for two decades because Beatrix had sewn it back on crooked the first time and it had been *their* crookedness, theirs together.

The room was full of lost things. Zephyrine had seen them. But the house had never once put one in a hand and said *here, this is yours, remember.*

"Where's Wren?" Zephyrine asked suddenly.

Beatrix's face did something very small and very fast—there and gone, a flicker of the mask slipping—and Zephyrine grabbed onto it like a rope.

"You don't know," Zephyrine said. "The house knows everything I've lost. It knows the stairs and the button and the fence incident. But it doesn't know where Wren is, because Wren isn't *mine*. Wren was never mine to lose." Their heart was going like a drum in a parade. "You're doing very well, Bea. Whoever you are. But you've read my grief and not my love, and those aren't the same book."

"Zeph." Beatrix's voice cracked, and it cracked in Beatrix's way, and Zephyrine hated the house for knowing that, hated it with a purity that felt almost like fuel. "Please. I've been alone in here for twenty years. You're the only one who can hear me. If you sign the papers, if you just *stay*, we can be together again, the way we were supposed to—"

"There it is," Zephyrine said quietly. "The signature. You know, for a house with two hundred years of practice, you're terribly predictable. It's always the signature. It's never *hello, how was the bus*."

* * *

They ended up, absurdly, in the kitchen. Beatrix made tea—made it properly, two sugars in Zephyrine's, milk first in her own, the family blasphemy—and the domesticity of it was the most horrifying thing yet, because the house had learned to do this, had learned the *choreography* of a sister, and choreography was all it was.

"The funeral," Zephyrine said. "Whose body is in the casket, then? If I died twenty years ago. If you've been me. Who did Mrs. Calloway measure?"

"You," Beatrix said. "Sort of. The house keeps what it's given. You've been given to it a long time, Zeph. It just needed a shape to show the town, and the town will see whatever it's told to see. It always has. That's what this place is *for*." She wrapped her hands around her mug. "You think you came back to bury me. You came back to bury yourself. The papers were signed three days before I—before anyone—died, because the house has known the date of your retirement for twenty years. It was just waiting for you to come home and make it official."

"And the letters," Zephyrine said. "In her room. In *my* handwriting."

"Your handwriting," Beatrix agreed. "Written by your hand. Whose hand did you think it was?"

Zephyrine looked down at their own hands, pale on the table, and for one vertiginous second could not swear to them. *Whose hands are these. Whose tea is this. Whose grief am I sitting in.* The kitchen tilted. Somewhere upstairs, a door that should not exist breathed open and shut, breathing.

"No," Zephyrine said aloud, to the kitchen, to the house, to Beatrix's borrowed face. "No. I know what this is. This is the kindest lie you have. This is the one that would work—because of course it would, I've spent twenty years feeling like I was never quite *in* my own life, like I was watching someone else be Zephyrine through glass. You're not showing me a lie. You're showing me my own doubt, dressed up in my sister." They stood. The chair scraped. "But doubt isn't evidence. And you know what I have that you don't?"

Beatrix looked up. For a fraction of a second—less—the thing behind her face looked *hungry*.

"A witness," Zephyrine said.

* * *

Wren was on the porch when Zephyrine threw open the front door, sitting on the swing in the same impossible coat, as though they had never been anywhere else and never would be. The night behind them was enormous and full of stars, and the town below was dark except for one light in Mrs. Calloway's parlor window, burning late, patient.

"Wren," Zephyrine said, and their voice broke on it, and they didn't care. "Tell me I'm alive."

Wren turned. And for the first time in twenty years, Wren looked *sad*—not eerie-sad, not uncanny-sad, but the plain human sadness of someone about to say a hard thing to a friend.

"I can't tell you that," Wren said. "Not because I don't know. Because it isn't mine to tell. That's the whole point of me." They stood, slowly, joints creaking like the swing. "But I can tell you this, Zephyrine Ravensworth, and you can believe it or not: the house lies in the shape of what you fear and what you want, and it has never once, in two hundred years, told the truth about a name. It's wearing your sister because your sister is the softest door into you. Everything it said in there was built to make you doubt the one thing it cannot fake."

"Which is?"

Wren smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from the summers that never happened, and it steadied something in Zephyrine's chest that had been coming loose.

"Whether you were loved," Wren said. "It can list your losses. It can't produce a single person who chose you. I can. I've been waiting on this porch since you were six years old, and I don't wait for ghosts, Zeph. Ghosts don't need waiting for. They're already home." They held out one hand, the same chapped, too-real hand from the first night. "Come inside. There's something you have to know about me, and it's going to cost us both, and we're running out of night."

Zephyrine looked back once, at the dark kitchen window, where a shape stood holding two cups of tea no one would ever drink.

Then they took Wren's hand, and the porch light—unlit all week—came on above them like a struck match.
