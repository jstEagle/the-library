## 19. Witness

The room had stopped pretending to be a room.

Zephyrine stood in the doorway of the extra space—the one that appeared only when unobserved—and felt the walls breathing around them like the flanks of some enormous sleeping animal. The contract lay on the table at the center, Zephyrine's name pre-filled in ink that gleamed wet no matter how long it sat there. The false Beatrix had just finished telling them they'd been dead for years. The house was holding its breath.

And Wren stood behind them, in the doorway, unable to cross.

"You have to understand what I'm about to ask," Wren said. Their voice was the same voice it had been when Zephyrine was seven years old—patient, wry, a little too fond of terrible puns—but there was something underneath it now, something old and tired and terribly kind. "Because once you do it, you can't undo it. There's no version of you after this that gets to be invisible again."

"I haven't been invisible in days," Zephyrine said. "The whole town keeps calling me Beatrix."

"That's not being seen. That's being *misfiled*." Wren smiled, and it cracked something open in Zephyrine's chest to see that smile unchanged after twenty years—the same crooked half-smile, the same coat with the same missing button, as if Wren had been kept in a drawer somewhere between visits and taken out only now, pressed and unwrinkled and exactly as remembered. "Being seen is worse. Being seen means every eye in Hollowmere can find you. Every eye in *this house* can find you. You'd be giving up the one protection you've had your whole life."

"Which is?"

"Nobody looking too closely." Wren stepped closer to the threshold—not crossing it, never crossing it—and their outline flickered like a candle in a draft. "You were the twin nobody picked out of photographs. The one teachers forgot on field trips. The one your own grandmother called 'the other one' at Christmas dinner. I know. I was there. I was the only one who was ever there."

"That's not—" Zephyrine started, and then stopped, because it was true, all of it, and they had spent twenty years building a life in the shape of that absence. Cities were good for people like Zephyrine. Cities lost you professionally.

"The house runs on consent," Wren said. "Every signature, every generation. But a contract needs a witness to be valid. That's what I am. That's what I've always been. The first resident's little girl invented me so she'd have someone to sit with her while her father signed away the family name, and the house bound me into the paper as witness, and I have watched two hundred years of Ravensworths sign themselves into its stomach." Wren's voice did not shake, but it went very quiet, the way voices do when they've been quiet for a very long time. "A witness who is seen can testify. A witness who is loved can testify *truthfully*. And truthful testimony, spoken aloud, by someone the house cannot misname—" they looked at the contract, at the wet-ink name, "—voids it. All of it. Back to the first page."

"And the cost," Zephyrine said. "You said there's a cost."

"You stop being invisible forever," Wren said simply. "To everything. Not just people. To *it*. Once you see me truly and speak for me, the house sees you back. Permanently. Even if we win. Even after it burns. Some part of you will always be standing in its doorway, and it will always know exactly where you are." A pause, and then, gentler: "I would not ask it of anyone. I have waited two hundred years to ask it of anyone. I hoped it would never be necessary."

Outside, down the hill, the town was gathering. Zephyrine could hear them through the walls—polite murmurs, the clink of covered dishes, Mrs. Calloway's voice rising and falling like a hymn sung slightly off-key. The welcome supper. The siege. They had hours until sunrise, and the house had all night, and the contract on the table had rewritten itself three times already, each version more generous than the last. The latest one offered Zephyrine a life in which Beatrix had lived. It offered forgiveness. It offered, in small print near the bottom, *belonging*.

Zephyrine thought about belonging. They thought about how it had been sold to them their whole life as something you signed for—leases, contracts, employment agreements, marriage licenses, all those little papers where you agreed to become legible to someone else. They thought about Beatrix, whose body was under the floor of this room, beneath the boards Zephyrine was standing on, and whose last letter—written in Zephyrine's own handwriting—had said *don't sign anything*.

They thought about a little girl in 1798 who invented a friend because she couldn't bear to watch alone.

"Okay," Zephyrine said. "Tell me how."

* * *

Wren could not enter the room. But Wren could stand in the doorway, and Zephyrine could stand inside it, facing them, and the threshold between them was—Wren explained, with the air of someone reciting terms and conditions they'd memorized out of spite—a kind of table. A place where testimony could be given and received.

"You speak. I witness. The ledger hears it whether it wants to or not."

"Where is the ledger?"

"Calloway's parlor. But it doesn't matter. It's connected to the contract the way a shadow is connected to a foot. Speak here, and it burns there." Wren's eyes—gray, the exact gray of the porch swing's paint, Zephyrine realized, they had been modeled on the porch swing, or the porch swing on them—held steady. "Last chance to walk away. The house would let you. It wants you tired, not trapped. Tired people sign."

"I'm not tired," Zephyrine lied.

"You're exhausted," Wren said fondly. "You've been exhausted since the bus. Good. Exhausted people tell the truth."

Zephyrine turned to face the room. The walls leaned in—not metaphorically; the wallpaper actually tilted, the room drawing itself tighter around them like a fist closing—and the objects on every shelf caught the lamplight and threw it back wrong. Every object Zephyrine had ever lost was here. The red mitten from age six. The library book they'd never returned, *Myths of the British Isles*, overdue fine accruing in some cosmic account. A photograph of Beatrix and Zephyrine at eight years old, arms slung around each other, grinning identical grins—the only photograph in existence where anyone had bothered to label which twin was which, in their mother's handwriting, on the back: *Bea left, Zeph right.*

Zephyrine picked it up. Held it. Put it in their pocket.

Then they looked at Wren.

Not glanced. Not checked. *Looked*—the way you look at a person, the way you look at someone you are about to swear something sacred to. They took in the coat, the missing button, the gray eyes, the smile lines that shouldn't exist on an imaginary friend but did, because Wren had been smiling at a lonely child for years and faces record what they practice. They took in the tiredness underneath. Two hundred years of standing in doorways.

"I see you," Zephyrine said.

The room screamed.

It wasn't sound, exactly—it was pressure, the whole house flexing against the words, the floorboards bowing upward like ribs around a struck heart. The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, in the walls, something vast shifted its weight. Zephyrine's knees buckled and they locked them, and kept looking, and Wren—Wren *brightened*, actually brightened, their edges going from candle-flicker to something solid and warm and unmistakably, permanently real.

"Oh," Wren said, blinking. "Oh, that's—yes. That's what it feels like. I'd forgotten." They laughed, wet-eyed. "Two hundred years, and I'd forgotten."

"Does it hurt?"

"It *itches*," Wren said, delighted. "Like taking off a cast. Keep going. Before it recovers."

Zephyrine breathed in. The air tasted of dust and cold ash and, faintly, of the supper downstairs—roast and gravy and sweet pie, the smell of a town pretending to grieve. They breathed out.

"My name is Zephyrine Ravensworth," they said, and their own name sounded strange in their mouth, like a key fitting a lock that hadn't been opened in decades. "I am the surviving twin. I am not Beatrix. I am not the other one. I am not whoever this house decides I am today. And I am here to give testimony."

The walls stopped leaning. For one moment the room was perfectly, horribly still—the stillness of a thing listening.

"In the year 1798," Zephyrine said, "Edmund Ravensworth built this house on land he did not own, taken from a family named Sorrel, whom he ruined with debts he forged himself. When the Sorrels came to reclaim what was theirs, Edmund invited something into his walls to punish them—to *avenge* him, though he was the thief. He signed the first contract. His daughter, Maren, aged nine, was made to sign as heir. She invented a friend to sit with her while she did it. Her friend has been standing in doorways ever since."

"That's me," Wren said softly. "Witnessed."

"The signers," Zephyrine continued, and the names came—not from memory, because Zephyrine had none of this in memory, but from somewhere deeper, the way a river knows the sea: "Edmund Ravensworth, 1798. Maren Ravensworth, 1811. Tobias Ravensworth, 1834. Esther Ravensworth, who tried to refuse and was made to watch her children go hungry until she signed, 1859. Silas Ravensworth, 1877. Agnes, 1893. Harold, 1916, who signed while his brother bled in France. Ruth, 1940. Gerald, 1963. Peter Vetch—who was not a Ravensworth at all, who tricked a dying woman into signing in his place, forty years ago, and has been hiding behind her signature ever since."

Each name landed like a stone dropped down a well. Each one, somewhere far below, struck something.

"And the victims," Zephyrine said, and here their voice finally broke, and they let it. "The Sorrel family, erased from their own land. Maren, who was nine. Every child taught to call this inheritance love. And Beatrix Ravensworth. My sister. Who found the letters, and asked questions, and refused to sign, and so the house took her body and wore her name and filed her death certificate three days before she died, because the paperwork matters more to it than the person. Because to this house, we were never people. We were *residents*. A title passed down through signatures, not blood. A job opening."

The room shuddered. The shelves rattled. The lost objects—the mitten, the book, decades of small vanishings—trembled in their places like birds before a storm.

"And the thefts," Zephyrine said. "It stole my sister. It stole my name—it gave it away, handed it to strangers in the street, let them call me by her name until I doubted my own face. It stole twenty years of my life by making me so afraid of being unseen that I made myself invisible on purpose, just to have it be my choice. It stole Wren. Two hundred years of a witness nobody saw, bound to a crime scene, keeping minutes for a monster." Zephyrine turned, deliberately, and faced the contract on the table—their name gleaming wetly, patiently, hungrily. "I sign nothing. I witness everything. Take it all back."

Behind them, Wren spoke—one word, formal and ancient and simple:

"*Testified.*"

* * *

Across the dark town, in the funeral parlor on Bellwether Lane, Mrs. Calloway's great ledger burst into flame.

There was no fire in the room. The candles were unlit; the stove was cold. But the ledger burned anyway, from the first page to the last, blue-white and silent, and the names lifted off the pages like sparks—Edmund, Maren, Tobias, Esther, Silas, Agnes, Harold, Ruth, Gerald, the false entry for Beatrix, the pre-filled entry waiting for Zephyrine—each one rising, glowing, and coming apart into nothing. Two hundred years of signatures, unmade. The binding cracked. The cover curled. And where the final page had been, blank paper remained, blank as snow, blank as an unsigned life.

Mrs. Calloway woke in her chair with a gasp, hands pressed to her breastbone, and stared at the ash drifting down through the moonlight like grey snow, and understood—for the first time in forty years of tending the machine—that it was over, or else that it was very much not, and either way she reached for her coat.

* * *

In the room, the contract was screaming.

Not the house—the contract itself, the paper writhing on the table like a live thing, Zephyrine's name boiling off the page in black bubbles. The walls slammed outward and inward. The floorboards under which Beatrix lay groaned like ship timber. Every door in the house opened at once, everywhere, all of them, and through each one poured memory: Christmas mornings, Beatrix laughing, the lake in summer, their mother's kitchen, every happiness the house had catalogued and hoarded, thrown at Zephyrine like nets.

"Hold the testimony!" Wren shouted from the threshold, gripping the doorframe, their newly-solid knuckles white. "It will offer you anything—it's rewriting itself right now—don't read it, don't even look—"

"I'm not looking," Zephyrine said, through their teeth.

Through the nearest door came Beatrix's voice, warm and alive and close: *"Zeph, come on, the water's fine, you absolute coward—"* and Zephyrine's whole body turned toward it like a compass needle, grief hitting like a wave, and they planted their feet and said, aloud, shaking: "That's not her. She's under the floor. I'm going to get her out."

The voice changed tactics. It got quieter. Kinder. *"You could have her back,"* it said, in a voice like their mother's, like their own. *"One signature. One little line. Everyone you've lost, returned. Isn't that worth your name?"*

"No," Zephyrine said.

*"Everyone you've ever—"*

"I said no." And then, because Wren had told them exhaustion tells the truth, and because it was true: "She wouldn't want it this way. She'd want me to burn your house down and complain about the smoke. So that's what I'm going to do."

The contract tore itself free of the table and fluttered up, up toward the ceiling, toward the chimney breast at the room's heart, where—Wren had told them, and now they could feel it, a heat like a buried sun—the original 1798 contract slept inside the chimney, the root of the whole rotten tree. The copy fled toward its original like a child toward a parent.

"After it," Wren said. "Zephyrine. *After it.*"

Zephyrine ran.

Behind them, Wren held the door wide—not as a resident, not as a guest, but as a witness released, two hundred years of standing in doorways finally meaning something—and the light of them poured down the hallway ahead like a lantern, and the house began, in every stolen voice it had, to scream.

* * *

The chimney room was the oldest room, though the blueprints denied it existed. The hearth was big enough to stand in. The original contract glowed behind the bricks like a coal that had never gone out, and above it, scratched into the mantel by a nine-year-old girl's fingernails two hundred years ago, someone had written: *I'M SORRY I SIGNED. — M.*

Zephyrine stood before it, catching their breath, the copy of the contract circling overhead like a panicked bird.

"Maren," they said to the scratch-marks, gently. "I know. I'm sorry too. It ends tonight."

From the darkness of the hall behind them, many voices at once—the undertaker's, Vetch's, Beatrix's, their own—said, in perfect unison:

"*You cannot.*"

Zephyrine reached into the hearth.
