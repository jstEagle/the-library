## 21. Burn Before Sunrise

The chimney breathed.

That was the only word for it. Zephyrine stood in the parlor with the original contract clutched against their chest—paper gone soft as skin from two hundred years of being handled by people who wanted something—and felt the hearth inhale behind the grate. A slow, patient pull of air, like a sleeping thing drawing breath through its nose.

"You're going to have to open me," Wren said from the doorway. "The flue's been shut since 1953. It doesn't like being opened."

"Nothing about this house likes anything," Zephyrine said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt, which was a small mercy and probably the last one they'd get tonight.

"It likes being fed," Wren said. "It's liked that since before either of us existed. That's not affection, though. That's just appetite wearing affection's coat." They crossed the room, and their boots made no sound on the boards—they never had, not even when Zephyrine was seven and had decided this was charming rather than horrifying—and knelt at the hearth. The grate resisted them. Wren put both hands on the iron and looked back over their shoulder with an expression Zephyrine had never seen on that face: effort. Strain. The face of someone doing something costly.

"Witnesses can't act," Wren grunted. "That was the binding. We observe, we remember, we testify—but we don't touch. Every time I move something, it takes a piece out of me."

"Then stop—"

"No." The grate shrieked open. Cold air rushed down the flue, and then, immediately after, warm air rushed up, and the temperature in the room dropped ten degrees in the space of a heartbeat, because the house had noticed what they were doing. "I've spent two hundred years watching people be polite to it. I'd like to spend one minute being rude."

Zephyrine almost laughed. That was the thing about Wren that twenty years hadn't dulled—the way humor lived in them like a pilot light, still burning under all that strangeness. *You made me up,* Zephyrine thought. *Or you made yourself up, or the first resident made you, but somewhere in there is a child who needed a friend, and that child gave you jokes.* It seemed important to hold onto that. It seemed like the kind of thing the house would try to take first.

The walls began to lean.

Not metaphorically. Zephyrine watched the plaster bow inward above the mantel, slow as bread rising, and heard the studs creak in the walls like knuckles cracking. Somewhere upstairs, a door opened—the extra door, the one that appeared only when unobserved—and began to come down the stairs at its own pace, unhurried, the way Mrs. Calloway walked.

"We don't have long," Zephyrine said.

"We were never going to have long." Wren stood, brushing soot from their knees—that same grey coat, buttoned to the throat, unchanged since childhood except for everything. "Do you have it?"

Zephyrine unfolded the contract. In the grey pre-dawn light leaking through the curtains, the ink moved. Not shimmered—*moved*, crawling across the page like insects disturbed under a stone, rearranging clauses, renumbering promises. Zephyrine could read fragments: *...the undersigned consents... the house shall keep... justice for the drowned girl, justice for the burned field, justice for* —and then the words curdled into appetite, into *keep, keep, keep*, the mandate rotting down the page like fruit.

"It's trying to renegotiate," Zephyrine said.

"It's always renegotiating. That's how it got so fat." Wren glanced at the window. Beyond the curtain, shapes stood in the yard—the town, gathered, patient, smiling. They had been there since dusk. They did not shift their weight. They did not speak. Once, around two in the morning, Zephyrine had pulled the curtain back an inch and old Mr. Vetch's granddaughter had waved, sweetly, with fingers that bent the wrong direction, and Zephyrine had understood that the wave was not friendly. It was *inventory*. "Burn it, Zephyrine. Before the sun gives them courage or the dark gives the house teeth. Whichever comes first."

Zephyrine held the contract over the hearth.

And the house screamed.

* * *

It did not scream with one voice. That would have been bearable. It screamed with every voice it had ever stolen, and it had stolen so many—two hundred years of last residents, of neighbors who signed, of visitors who lingered too long, of a twin sister whose body lay beneath the floor of a room that shouldn't exist. The scream came down the chimney and out of the walls and through the floorboards and it was Beatrix laughing, Beatrix calling *Zeph, Zeph, wait up*, Beatrix at nine years old daring Zephyrine to eat a worm on a bet; and it was their mother singing off-key in the kitchen; and it was Mr. Vetch young and terrified saying *sign it, sign it, please, I have children*; and underneath all of it, older than all of it, a voice like wet gravel and cold iron that said:

**MINE.**

"They weren't yours," Zephyrine said. Their hands shook. The paper trembled in the updraft. "None of them were yours. You were invited to make things *right*, and you made them *yours* instead."

**THEY CONSENTED.**

"They consented the way drowning people consent to water." Zephyrine struck the match. The first one broke. The second one caught—a small, ordinary flame, sulfur-smelling, human-sized, and somehow that was the most defiant thing in the whole house. "There's a difference between a signature and a surrender, and you never learned it, because learning requires wanting something other than your own hunger."

The match touched the corner of the page.

For one moment, nothing happened. The flame licked at two-hundred-year-old paper and the paper refused it, the way the door refused to be seen, the way the ledger refused to show its final column. Then Wren stepped forward and put one hand flat against the hearthstone and said, in a voice that filled the room without being loud—

"I saw it given. I saw Elias Ravensworth sign in 1798, weeping, for his daughter who drowned in the well while the neighbors watched and did nothing. I saw him invite this thing to punish them. I saw it accept the invitation and then stay past its welcome, the way guests do when no one is brave enough to say go home. I *witness* it. And a witness's word is older than any signature on any page."

The paper caught.

* * *

Fire is supposed to be orange. This fire was every color the house had ever eaten. Zephyrine watched the contract burn and saw, in the flames, brief flickers of everything the signatures had purchased: a harvest that never failed, a sickness that skipped the signed-for households, a funeral in 1911 where nobody grieved because grief had been included in the terms. And beneath that, deeper in the burn, the cost: faces in windows, names worn smooth, a town that couldn't tell two twins apart because it had stopped looking at people altogether and started looking at *residents*.

The screaming changed key.

It went from rage to bargaining so smoothly it would have been funny if it weren't happening inside Zephyrine's own skull. The walls stopped leaning and started *offering*. The hallway stretched toward the kitchen, and the kitchen was suddenly Zephyrine's happiest memory rendered in perfect detail—rain on the window, cocoa, Beatrix across the table arguing cheerfully about whether owls could be embarrassed—and the house poured warmth through it like honey.

*Stay,* said the kitchen, in Beatrix's voice. *Stay and she's alive. Stay and none of this happened. Stay and you never left her.*

"That's not her," Zephyrine said, walking. Walking was the hardest thing they had ever done. Each step toward the hearth was a step away from the kitchen, and the kitchen knew it, and the kitchen turned up the volume—Beatrix's laugh, Beatrix's terrible puns, Beatrix at fourteen defending Zephyrine from the kids at the bus stop with a ferocity that had made Zephyrine feel, for the only time in their life, completely safe.

*You'll be alone,* the house said, dropping the disguise for one honest second, and the honesty was worse than the lie. *You'll walk out of here with nothing. No name anyone gave you. No place that keeps you. You'll be visible to everyone and kept by no one.*

"Yes," Zephyrine said. "That's called being a person."

They reached the hearth. The contract was half-consumed, the moving ink dying in segments, each clause curling black and releasing—not smoke, but *sound*, tiny compressed screams of every promise breaking at once. Zephyrine fed the rest in, page by page, and the fire took it gladly now, hungrily, as if the flames had been waiting two hundred years for permission.

The house came apart politely.

That was the horrible part, afterward—the politeness. No collapse, no explosion. Just doors closing themselves gently throughout the house, one after another, like a hotel at the end of the season. The extra room, coming down the stairs, reached the bottom step and simply wasn't there anymore, and the staircase was one step shorter than it had been a moment ago, and no one would ever count the rooms and find one too many again. The wallpaper faded from its hungry pattern to plain, tired, honest floral. Somewhere upstairs, the letters in Beatrix's desk—all those pages in Zephyrine's handwriting describing lives neither twin had lived—slid quietly into blankness.

But the voices didn't stop. The voices went on screaming, and Zephyrine realized, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the house was burning out of the chimney and into the sky, and every stolen voice was leaving with it—going *home*, maybe, or going nowhere, but going. Beatrix's laugh rose through the flue last of all, and it didn't sound angry. It sounded relieved. It sounded like someone setting down luggage they'd been carrying so long they'd forgotten it wasn't theirs.

Then the fire settled into ordinary embers, orange and modest, and the house was just a house, drafty and old and empty, and dawn was coming up grey-gold over Hollowmere.

* * *

Wren was still standing at the hearth, hand on the stone, and they were *fading*.

Not dramatically. Not like a ghost in a film. More like a photograph left in a window—still entirely themselves, still Wren, but lighter at the edges, as if the world had finally remembered they were optional.

"The binding's broken," Wren said. They sounded surprised, and pleased, and a little frightened, which was such a human combination that Zephyrine's chest hurt. "Witnesses are bound to the contract. Contract's ash. Which means—" they flexed their fingers, experimentally, and for the first time in two hundred years, their boots made a sound on the floorboards. A small scuff. They looked down at it like it was a miracle. "Which means I'm not a witness anymore. I'm just..."

They stopped. Zephyrine waited. Outside, the shapes in the yard were dissolving with the dark—Mrs. Calloway's smile going last, lingering like frost in a shadow—and the front door stood open, and beyond it the road ran out of Hollowmere toward anywhere, and the grave in the churchyard, Zephyrine knew without checking, was empty, had always been empty, because there had never been a body, only a title looking for a holder.

"Just a person?" Zephyrine offered.

"Just a person," Wren agreed. They picked up the poker and set it back in its stand, carefully, the way you handle something that belongs to a life you're only just joining. "I don't entirely know how to do that. I've never done it before. I'm told it involves breakfast."

"It does," Zephyrine said. "Frequently."

"And you? You signed nothing. You're on no register. Calloway's ledger is ash, the deed burned with the rest, and as far as any record anywhere is concerned, there are no Ravensworths and never were." Wren tilted their head, that familiar birdlike tilt, unchanged across twenty years and two centuries. "So who are you?"

Zephyrine thought about it. Really thought—the way they hadn't let themselves think in years, because thinking about it meant feeling the shape of the hole where an answer should be. The town had called them Beatrix. The papers had called them resident. The house had called them *mine*. Their parents, kindly, distractedly, had mostly called them *the quiet one*.

"I don't know yet," Zephyrine said, and found that it didn't frighten them. That was the strange gift of the whole terrible night: once you'd refused a house, a town, and two hundred years of inherited appetite, an unanswered question stopped being a threat and became what it had always been—an opening. "But I'd like to find out somewhere with better weather. And breakfast. Come on."

They walked out together through the front door, past the empty yard, past the house that was only a house now, breathing nothing. Behind them the sun cleared the ridge and lit the windows gold, ordinary glass, reflecting only morning.

Ahead, the road.

"So," Wren said, falling into step, hands in the pockets of that impossible coat. "About these owls. Can they be embarrassed? Because I've had two hundred years to form an opinion—"

"Wren."

"—and I feel very strongly that they can—"

"*Wren.*" But Zephyrine was laughing, actually laughing, for the first time since the night bus rolled into Hollowmere, and the sound carried down the empty road ahead of them like something being born.
