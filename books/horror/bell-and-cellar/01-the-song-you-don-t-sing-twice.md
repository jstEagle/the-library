## 1. The Song You Don't Sing Twice

The rain found Gideon Winterbourne three miles before Hollowmere did, and it did not introduce itself politely.

It came down the moor in a gray wall, flattening the heather, turning the cart track into a brown river, and reducing his carefully oiled field recorder—a wax-cylinder phonograph of the finest make, wrapped in oilcloth and cradled against his chest like a firstborn—to the status of a very expensive anxiety. He had walked from the last railway stop since noon. By four o'clock he could no longer tell whether his boots were wet because of rain or because they had simply given up on the concept of dryness altogether.

He was still composing a letter of complaint to the Ordnance Survey—*Dear Sirs, I have consulted your map and wish to report that the road marked "passable" is a slander against the word "road"*—when the village appeared out of the murk all at once, as if it had been standing there the whole time waiting for him to notice.

Hollowmere sat in a shallow bowl of hill, a scatter of slate roofs and low stone walls around a green, with a church at one end whose bell tower leaned very slightly, like a man listening hard. Lamps were being lit in the windows. Not all at once—he noticed that even through his exhaustion and dripping state. One lamp, then another, then another, in an order that seemed deliberate, though he couldn't yet say what the order was.

A woman was standing in the doorway of the inn, arms crossed, watching him come down the lane.

She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with iron-gray hair pinned back and the particular stillness of someone who has stood in many doorways watching many things come down that lane. She didn't wave. She didn't call out. She waited until he was close enough to be spoken to at a normal volume, which told him something about her before she said a word.

"You'll be the folklorist," she said.

"Gideon Winterbourne." He attempted to remove his hat, discovered it had fused to his head, and settled for tipping what remained of its shape. "I wrote ahead—"

"Aye. Three letters." She stepped aside to let him into the hall. "You write a great deal, do you?"

"It's my trade."

"Mm," she said, in the tone other people used for *it's my disease*.

The inn was called the Bell, and it was warm inside in the way that old stone buildings manage—not cozy so much as *holding its heat close*, like a cat that will permit you near it but not touch it. A fire burned low in the parlor. The smell was woodsmoke, tallow, bread, and under it something faintly mineral, like the inside of a cave or a cellar that has been swept every day for a hundred years.

"I'm Maren Quill," the woman said. "I keep the Bell. You'll want the room at the back. It's dry, mostly, and the window doesn't rattle if you don't open it."

"That sounds like luxury beyond my budget."

"Your budget's fine. You're paying for the room, not the window." She took his coat without asking, held it at arm's length, and hung it by the fire with the expression of a woman assessing a drowned animal. "Supper's at seven. Bread, stew, cheese if you've earned it."

"How does one earn cheese?"

"Ask me again Thursday."

He liked her immediately, in the way one likes weather that has stopped actively trying to kill you. He followed her down the hall, cataloguing as he went—it was an occupational habit, this constant noting, and he'd long ago stopped apologizing for it. The floorboards were scrubbed pale. The doors were all closed. Every single door along the corridor stood shut, and he had the odd impression, passing them, that they were shut *in order*.

"The village expects me," he said. "I hope. I sent word I'd be recording songs. Ballads, lullabies, work songs—whatever people will give me. There's very little collected from this valley. It's a gap on the map, musically speaking."

Maren stopped at the back room and set her hand flat against the door before opening it, which he might have thought nothing of, except that she did it the way one touches a horse's neck before saddling.

"There's a reason for gaps on maps," she said.

"Usually it's just distance."

"Usually." She pushed the door open. The room was small and clean: bed, washstand, chair, a candle already lit. Another lamp, he noted. Lit already, before he arrived. "You can stay. I'm not turning a soaked man out into that. But you'll want to hear how things are done here, Mr. Winterbourne, before you go knocking on doors with your little machine."

"I'm listening."

She looked at him for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below, a log shifted in the grate.

"We don't sing songs twice here," she said.

Gideon blinked water off his lashes. "I'm sorry?"

"When someone sings you a song—one time. You listen, you take your notes, and you don't ask them again. Not that evening, not the next day, not ever. If they offer it again, that's their affair. But you don't ask." She said it plainly, the way she might say *we don't put the milk in the pantry, the mice*. No drama in it at all, which was precisely what made it dramatic. "You can write that down in your little book if you like. Rule one of Hollowmere."

"May I ask why?"

"You may ask," said Maren. "That's free too. I won't answer tonight, mind. Ask Cobb, if it troubles you. He's the sexton. He likes talking about old things, and he's past the age where anyone listens to him, so he'll talk your ear clean off." She turned to go, then paused in the doorway with her hand on the frame—the same flat-palmed touch—and looked back at him with something that wasn't quite a smile. "You seem a decent sort. Decent sorts last longer here."

"Longer than what?"

"Than indecent ones," she said, and went to see about his supper.

* * *

The stew was excellent, the bread was warm, and the cheese, it turned out, required no earning whatsoever—Maren brought it unasked, which Gideon chose to interpret as a good sign rather than a bribe. He ate alone in the parlor, because the taproom emptied the moment he entered it. Not rudely. Nobody left mid-drink or made a show of it. They simply finished their pints over the following ten minutes and drifted home through the rain, nodding to him politely on the way out, one after another, like a tide going out.

By half past eight he had the parlor to himself and the fire to himself, and he got out his notebook and began the day's entry in his small, upright hand:

*Arrived Hollowmere, 4:15 pm, in weather of biblical temperament. Innkeeper: Maren Quill, keeper of the Bell, dispenser of cheese and warnings. Local custom: no song sung twice. Reason unknown. Village lamps lit in sequence (order TBD). Doors closed in sequence (order TBD). Population friendly in the manner of people who are friendly because it is expected, not because they mean it.*

He paused, pen hovering, and added:

*Or possibly they mean it and are afraid to show it. Distinguish tomorrow.*

He heard Tansy before he saw her—a quick light tread on the stairs, and then a girl of perhaps twelve came around the corner carrying an armload of kindling nearly as large as herself. She had dark hair escaping its braid in every direction and the ferociously competent air of a child who has run a household since before she could reach the shelf where the salt lived.

"You're the song man," she said, dumping kindling by the hearth with a crash.

"I'm a folklorist. Which is—"

"Someone who collects songs instead of stamps. Maren explained." She crouched and began building the fire up with the speed and economy of long practice. "Tansy. I live here. Well, I live here now. Before I lived at the Harrows', and before that—" She shrugged, a whole biography compressed into a single lift of the shoulders. "Are you really going to write our songs down? In a book? So they don't get lost?"

"So they don't get lost," Gideon agreed. "That's the whole idea. Songs are like birds, you know. Beautiful, but they fly off if nobody writes down where they nested."

Tansy considered this. "Our songs don't fly anywhere," she said. "They stay right here. That's the trouble with them."

Before he could ask what on earth she meant, Maren's voice came down the hall—*Tansy, the kindling, not the whole tree*—and the girl rolled her eyes with magnificent discretion and vanished, leaving Gideon alone with a fire that was burning much better than he'd built it and a sentence rattling around his skull like a coin in a jar.

*They stay right here. That's the trouble with them.*

He wrote it down. He wrote everything down; it was the only prayer he knew.

* * *

He woke once in the night, and it took him a long moment to understand why.

Not a sound had woken him—that was the strange part. It was the *stopping* of a sound. Like a carriage passing your window and falling silent, except no carriage had passed. The room was dark, the rain had gentled to a whisper against the glass, and the silence had a texture to it, a freshly-broken quality, as though something had been going on and had been switched off the instant his attention touched it.

He lay still and listened.

At first: nothing. Wind, rain, the settling of old beams. And then, gradually, underneath it—or woven through it, or perhaps it had been there all along and he was only now hearing it the way you only see stars once your eyes adjust—

Humming.

It was coming through the walls. Through several walls. Faint, low, wordless, in no voice he could identify—more felt than heard, a vibration in the plaster as much as a sound in the air. Not one hummer but many, scattered through the inn and, he suspected, out into the village beyond, up and down the lane, behind all those closed doors. All of them humming the same thing. The same slow phrase, rising and settling, rising and settling, like breathing, like a tide, like something enormous asleep.

And it was beautiful. That was the thing that undid him. He was a collector of forty years' standing—well, eleven years' standing, but he liked to speak of himself in the voice of his future biographers—and he knew within four bars that he had never heard its like. It had the shape of a lullaby and the weight of a hymn, and it moved in a way that melodies weren't supposed to move, leaning always toward a note it never quite reached, so that the whole song felt like a hand reaching for something just out of grasp. His throat tightened. His eyes stung. He thought, absurdly, of his mother, dead these twenty years, of a kitchen he could barely remember, of being small and safe and sung to.

He sat up slowly. He reached for the notebook on the bedside table, moving with exaggerated care, trying not to make a sound, trying not to break whatever spell carried it.

The moment his intention firmed—*I am listening to this, I am taking this down*—the humming stopped.

All of it. Everywhere. At once.

Not fading. Not trailing off house by house, voice by voice, the way any natural chorus must. *Stopped.* Cut clean, like a door closing on a room full of singers. The silence that followed was so total and so sudden that his own heartbeat sounded like drumming, and he sat in the dark with his pen uncapped and his heart hammering, feeling exactly like a boy caught eavesdropping at a keyhole.

For a long time, nothing. Then, distantly, a clock struck two, unhurried, indifferent, counting something else entirely.

Gideon lay back down. He did not sleep so much as hover above sleep, and in the gray hour before dawn he dreamed—though *dreamed* seemed too soft a word—of a stone door set into a hillside, and of a hand on the far side of it, pressing flat against the granite, patient as stone itself.

He woke with the phrase of the humming still in his ears. He wrote it down from memory, every interval he could hold onto, and stared at the notation for a long while.

Then he noticed the strangest thing of all, the thing that made the hair rise on his forearms in the cold morning light: the melody resolved. On paper, in ink, in the honest arithmetic of notes, it ended exactly where it had always been reaching. Complete. Settled. Safe.

But no one had hummed it that way. Every voice in Hollowmere, singing through their walls in the dark, had refused—had *practiced refusing*—that final note.

As if the song were only safe unfinished.

As if finishing it were a door, and everyone in the village knew better than to open it twice.

Downstairs, he could hear Maren moving about, and Tansy's quick feet, and the ordinary sounds of an ordinary morning. He dressed, pocketed his notebook, and went down to earn his breakfast, telling himself the whole way that a folklorist's job was questions, and he would begin with the sexton.

He did not yet know that the question he should have asked was the one Maren had answered before he'd thought to wonder: *why not twice?*

He would learn. Hollowmere had been keeping count a long time, and now, without either of them knowing it, so had he.
