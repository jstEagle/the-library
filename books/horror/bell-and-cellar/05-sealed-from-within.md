## 5. Sealed From Within

The rain had finally stopped, which in Hollowmere was less a change of weather than a change of mood. The whole village seemed to exhale—chimneys smoking again, gutters chuckling, the moor steaming like a kettle taken off the fire. Gideon Winterbourne woke in his room at the Bell and Cellar with the particular contentment of a man who had slept under a good roof after three wet days on the road, and with the particular unease of a man who had done so while listening to something he could not name.

He came downstairs to find Maren polishing the bar with the ferocity of a woman punishing it for something.

"Sleep well?" she asked, without looking up.

"Very. Though I'll confess I heard bells in the night. Thirteen times, I counted."

The cloth stopped moving.

"Old bell," Maren said. "Cracks in odd weather."

"Thirteen is an odd number," Gideon agreed pleasantly, "but not usually the sort a cracked bell produces."

Maren looked at him then—a long, level look, the kind a lighthouse gives a ship—and said, "Eggs are on the table. Eat them before they judge you."

It was, he was learning, her way of ending a conversation without appearing to end it. He ate the eggs. They were excellent, and they judged him not at all.

* * *

The walk up to the excavation took forty minutes along a path that had been a sheep track until last week and was now a churned brown scar. The government crew's tents stood at the lip of the cut like pale mushrooms that had decided, against all advice, to grow on a hillside. Gideon had made himself known to Halloran two days prior—the engineer was brisk, competent, and possessed of the cheerful conviction that every problem in the world was a drainage problem wearing a costume—and Halloran had waved him through with the air of a man granting permission to a harmless bird.

"You're the song fellow," Halloran had said. "Well, there's precious little singing here. Just mud and granite. Come have a look if you like, though I can't imagine what a folklorist wants with a hole."

What a folklorist wanted, Gideon could not entirely have said himself. He told himself it was professional curiosity: the villagers' silence around the dig had a texture he recognized, the texture of a story everyone knows and no one will tell. He had spent fifteen years collecting songs from places that had forgotten why they were afraid of music, and Hollowmere's fear had a shape. It had edges. And now those edges had been exposed by a steam shovel.

The door sat in the side of the cut like a held breath.

That was the only way Gideon could think of it, standing before it that morning with his notebook damp in his pocket. A slab of grey granite, perhaps seven feet tall, set flush into the hillside as though the hill had grown around it the way a tree grows around a fence post. An iron ring hung from its face, fused solid with rust—or with something older than rust, something that had flowed into every joint and seam like candle wax, sealing the door to its frame so completely that the boundary between door and stone was nearly imaginary.

Nearly. Not quite. Because the door was a door. Something about it insisted on being a door the way a locked piano insists on being an instrument.

"Been staring at it all week," said one of the workers, a young man named Petch with forearms like hams and eyes that hadn't slept properly since Tuesday. "You're the learned one. What d'you make of the marks?"

Gideon stepped closer.

The tally marks covered the lower half of the door in neat rows, carved deep and confident, five strokes and a diagonal slash, five strokes and a slash, row upon row upon row, climbing the stone like ivy made of arithmetic. They were old—the edges softened by a century of rain—but not ancient. Not prehistoric. Not folklore.

They were bookkeeping.

"They're counts," Gideon said slowly. "Someone kept count here. Nightly, I'd guess, by the regularity." He ran his fingers over a row. The grooves were clean, deliberate, unhurried. Whoever had cut them had done it the way Cobb lit the lamps: as a duty, performed properly, night after night after night.

"How many would you say?" Petch asked.

Gideon did what he always did when a question mattered: he took out his notebook and began to work. He measured a representative section, counted the rows per foot, extrapolated upward with the fussy precision of a man who trusted numbers more than he trusted most people. The mathematics took him twenty minutes, kneeling in the wet grass, tongue between his teeth.

"Roughly thirty-six thousand marks," he said at last. "If one mark per night—" He stopped.

Petch went pale in a slow, interesting way, like milk turning.

"That's near enough a hundred years," Gideon finished quietly. "Give or take."

"A hundred years," Petch repeated. "And then they stop?"

"Not stop." Gideon looked again at the final row, low on the door, where the marks ended. The last stroke was incomplete—a single vertical line begun and abandoned, its groove shallowing out mid-carve as though the hand making it had simply... gone elsewhere. Mid-thought. Mid-motion. "They end mid-stroke. Like a sentence interrupted."

Behind him, someone dropped a shovel. Nobody picked it up.

* * *

He copied the marks anyway, because copying was what he did, because the notebook was the only place in the world where he felt competent. Row after row of small vertical lines marched across his pages, and somewhere around the four-hundredth mark he became aware of a presence at his shoulder and turned to find Cobb.

The old sexton stood with his hands folded in front of him like a man at a funeral—which, Gideon thought, was more or less his permanent condition. But today there was something new in his face. Not the practiced blankness of the village. Something rawer. A man deciding whether to speak.

"You've been counting my village," Cobb said.

"Your village has been counting itself," Gideon said. "I'm only writing it down."

Cobb grunted, which from Cobb was applause. He lowered himself onto a flat stone with the elaborate care of the elderly, and for a long moment the two of them regarded the sealed door together, the way men regard a grave they both knew was coming.

"My grandmother saw this door closed," Cobb said at last.

Gideon's pen stopped.

"She was a girl. Eight years old, maybe nine. She remembered it because it was the last night anyone sang in Hollowmere." His voice was flat, recitation-flat, the voice of a man who had rehearsed a truth so long it had worn smooth. "There was a bargain made. Before her time, but she knew the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a room you've never entered, from where the light falls under the door."

"What kind of bargain?" Gideon asked.

"The kind you keep or you don't." Cobb's hands tightened on his knees. "Long ago—longer than the door, longer than the church—this valley belonged to something that wanted counting. That's the oldest way I can put it, and it's the truest. It wanted to be counted. Attended to. Named nightly, like a child checked on in the dark. And the founders, being practical people, made terms instead of running, because you cannot run from a thing that lives under your floor."

"And the terms were?"

"One man goes below." Cobb said it simply, the way you'd say *one man pays the bill*. "One man goes below, of the founder's own blood, and keeps the count from the inside. Every night, a mark. Every night, the thing beneath is answered, attended, satisfied. And in exchange—" he gestured upward, at the moor, at the smoke rising gently from Hollowmere's chimneys, at the entire green and ordinary world— "the rest stay above. Safe. Unbothered. Left alone to plant turnips and raise children and complain about the weather."

Gideon found his mouth had gone dry. "For how long?"

"For as long as the count is kept honest." Cobb turned and looked at him, and his eyes were very old and very tired. "One keeper lasts a lifetime, boy. Then another must go down. That's the shape of it. That's the whole shape of it. One below so the rest stay above, and the count keeping the bargain honest from both sides."

The wind moved through the cut, and the iron ring on the door creaked—just slightly, just once, like a throat clearing.

"Your grandmother," Gideon said carefully. "She saw the door closed. Who closed it?"

"The last keeper's brother. From outside." Cobb rose, joints crackling like green kindling. "But the keeper marked the door first. Marked it right up to the last night. My grandmother said the whole village heard him counting behind the stone, clear as a bell, right up until—" he nodded at the unfinished stroke— "until he didn't."

"Until he what?"

"Until whatever happens, happened." Cobb straightened his coat with two sharp tugs. "And then the count stopped, and the village stopped counting too, and everyone agreed—without ever saying it aloud—that some silences are load-bearing. You don't pull at them. You build your life on top and you don't ask what holds the floor up."

He began to walk away, then paused.

"You asked Tansy to sing her lullaby twice," he said, without turning. "Don't do that again. Songs here aren't songs. They're answers. And you don't give the same answer twice unless you're asked twice—and nothing down there ever stops asking."

Then he was gone, down the path, a small black figure against the enormous indifferent green, and Gideon stood alone before the door with his notebook full of thirty-six thousand little deaths of ink.

* * *

He stayed until the light went long and gold. He told himself it was scholarship. He sketched the door, measured the frame, noted the strange warmth of the granite under his palm—as though the hill had a fever, as though something behind the stone were running hot with patience. He noted the fused iron ring. He noted, though he did not write it down, the sensation that had been growing in him all afternoon: the feeling of being *recognized*. Of standing before a door that knew his knock before he made it.

Walking back through the village at dusk, he passed the houses as the lamps came on—one, then the next, then the next, in their strict and unvarying order—and heard, through open windows, the murmured counts. Doors. Lights. Names. Children reciting their family lines like rosaries, mothers answering, fathers confirming, the whole village performing its nightly arithmetic of survival without knowing—or refusing to know—what sum it was balancing.

At the inn, Tansy met him in the hall, hair damp from the washbasin, holding a candle.

"Maren says supper's ready," she announced. Then, with the terrible directness of children: "You were up at the door all day. Cobb came past looking gray as ash. What did he tell you?"

Gideon considered lying. He was, he reflected, getting worse at it in this village, as though honesty were contagious here, caught from people who had survived a century of it.

"He told me about the bargain," Gideon said. "One below, so the rest stay above."

Tansy nodded, unsurprised, and something in her nod chilled him more than anything Cobb had said. She was eleven. She had known.

"There's a verse of the harvest song nobody sings," she said, in the tone of one sharing a useful household hint. "The fourth one. Maren says if I ever hear it in my head, I'm to come tell her straightaway and not hum it, not even a little." She smiled up at him, sweet and solemn. "Supper's mutton."

She went in. Gideon remained a moment in the hall, listening to the lamps being counted down the lane, each small flame a name, each name a debt, and felt—with no reason he could name, with nothing but the itch of a wrong note in familiar music—that the ledger Cobb had described had a column in it with his family's name at the top.

Winterbourne. The word arrived in his mind unbidden, the way a sneeze arrives, the way a name arrives when you wake from a dream of being called.

He shook it off and went in to his mutton.

Above the moor, unseen, the last unfinished tally mark waited on the stone, one stroke short of complete, patient as arithmetic, as though it had been waiting a hundred years for exactly the right hand to finish it.
