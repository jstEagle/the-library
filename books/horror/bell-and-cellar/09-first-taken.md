## 9. First Taken

Maren Ashdown had kept the ledger of Hollowmere for nineteen years, and in all that time she had never once had to strike a name.

The book itself was older than her tenure—older than her mother's tenure, older than the church roof—bound in leather gone soft and dark as plum skin, its corners worn round by a century of careful thumbs. Each family had its page. Each page had its names. And each night at dusk, when the bell rang, she walked the high street with the book under her arm and marked every door shut, every lamp lit, every soul accounted for. It was tedious work. It was holy work. It was, she had long ago decided, mostly nonsense that happened to be the sort of nonsense that kept a body honest: you counted your neighbors because your neighbors were worth counting.

She had not expected to feel grief over a line of ink.

But there it was, on the Harrow page, third name from the top: *Edmund Harrow*, and beneath it, where his tally should have been, a single stroke drawn through the letters themselves—not crossed out so much as *dismissed*, the way a schoolmistress strikes an error on a slate. She hadn't done it. That was the thing she could not stop turning over, like a stone in the pocket. She had stood at the Harrow gate at dusk with her pencil poised, and the name had already been struck, the graphite gray and fresh as if written seconds before, though no hand but hers had touched the page since morning.

The bell had rung thirteen times that evening. There are twelve households in Hollowmere. Everyone had heard it. Nobody had said a word about it, which was somehow worse than screaming.

* * *

It began, as these things apparently did, with boots.

Not missing boots—Maren would have noticed missing boots, because missing boots meant someone had walked off somewhere, and walking off somewhere was a problem you could solve with a lantern and a bad temper. No. The trouble started smaller than that. It started with supper.

"Edmund didn't come for his loaf," Tansy said, setting the basket down by the hearth. She was fourteen and all elbows and opinions, and she delivered this news with the particular gravity of a girl who understood that bread was not merely bread in Hollowmere; it was a census. "Mrs. Harrow took it herself. She said he'd be home late."

"Late doing what?" Gideon asked. He was at the corner table with his papers spread out, that ink-stained folklorist with the gentle voice and the habit of listening so hard it made you want to tell him things you'd sworn to keep. He'd been with them three weeks now, and Maren had stopped thinking of him as a guest somewhere around the second night, when he'd fixed the wobbly stair without being asked and then blushed as though he'd committed a crime.

"Hedging," Tansy said. "The east field. His father wants it done before the frost."

"Mm." Gideon made a small note. Maren shot him a look—the look, the one that said *some things are not for the record*—and he had the grace to turn the page as if writing nothing at all.

"He'll come home," Maren said, and heard how it sounded, like a spell she was casting rather than a fact she knew. "Harrow men always come home. His grandfather once walked eleven miles through a whiteout because his mother had a goose in the oven."

Tansy smiled. Gideon did not, quite, and Maren found herself irrationally annoyed with him for it. The man had been in the village long enough now to know the rules of the place, and rule one was that you did not let your face do arithmetic in public.

* * *

He didn't come home.

Maren learned it the way everyone learned everything in Hollowmere, which was to say sideways, in pieces, from people who spoke in the passive voice as though events simply happened to themselves. Mrs. Harrow had knocked at the church. Cobb had gone out with a lantern. Cobb had come back without Edmund and without saying why, and his face had been the color of tallow, and he had told Mrs. Harrow something in a low voice that made the old woman sit down very slowly on the church step and fold her hands in her lap like a child called in for scolding.

And then—this was the part Maren could not get past, the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed coal—then nobody went looking.

They were not cruel people. She'd have staked the inn on it. These were the same hands that had raised her barn twice after storms, that had buried her husband with more flowers than the church could hold, that left soup on sills for the sick and coins in coats for the poor. But when dusk came and the bell rang and Edmund Harrow's lamp stayed dark behind its window, the village simply... adjusted. Mrs. Harrow lit her own lamp and one extra beside it, the way you do for a widow, though her son was not dead, or not dead in any way anyone would say aloud. The Hedgerows walked their boundary a little faster. Old Tam Prewitt, who hadn't missed a dusk count in forty years, was seen checking his locks twice.

Nobody searched the moor. Nobody called his name into the dark. Because everybody, it seemed, already knew what Maren was only beginning to admit to herself: that Edmund had not wandered off. That he had been *called*, in the way a debt is called, and that going after him with lanterns and good intentions would be like trying to chase down a bill collector with a casserole.

"We should go up there," she said to Cobb that night, in the churchyard, where he was trimming the yew with shears that shook. "We should at least walk the east field. Shout a bit. Do the ordinary human thing."

Cobb didn't stop cutting. "And find what?"

"A boy! A cold boy, maybe, or a frightened one hiding in a hedge, but a boy we can bring home and wrap in blankets and feed until he's sick of soup!"

"And if the field's empty?" He set the shears down. His hands, freed, shook worse. "If there's nothing there but his hat? What do we do then, Mare? Knock on doors and ask if anyone's seen a man the hill has taken? Ask the hill to give him back?" He looked at her then, and his eyes were wet and furious and ancient. "You know what's up there. You've known since the spade hit stone. We all know. We've just spent a hundred years getting very good at knowing it quietly."

"The count hasn't run in a hundred years," Maren said. "That's what you told me. That's what everyone told me. It ended. Whatever bargain, whatever bell-counting nonsense—it finished, and we got to be an ordinary village with ordinary fears, like weather and debt and whether the Prewitts' dog will ever stop killing chickens."

"It ended," Cobb agreed softly, "because it was paid in full. And debts, Mare—" he picked the shears back up, and the snip of the yew sounded horribly like a tally being marked, "—debts don't end. They just wait for the next due date."

* * *

She told herself she wouldn't go up. She was the innkeeper, not the constable; the village had made its choice, and a woman who ran a house full of other people's suppers didn't get to spend her nights wandering hillsides playing hero. She had a ward to think of. She had a lodger who asked questions like a man sharpening knives. She had a business, and businesses required sleep.

At half past ten she put on her boots.

She told herself it was practical. If Edmund was out there, better she found him than nobody. If he wasn't—if the field held nothing but cut hedgerow and cold stars—then at least she'd know, and knowing was a kind of housekeeping. You couldn't sweep a room you refused to enter. Her mother had said that, though her mother had been talking about grief at the time, and Maren suspected the advice applied here too, which was exactly why she'd tried not to think of it.

The moon was three days shy of full and gave enough light to walk by. The east field lay under a rime of frost that crunched like sugar. The hedgerow was half-done—a neat stretch, then a ragged gap where the work had stopped mid-row, shears (she saw later) lying in the grass with a glove still tucked in one jaw of them, dropped, not set down. Dropped. Her heart did something complicated and painful around that glove.

Beyond the field the moor rose toward the excavation, and Maren followed the cut the government men had begun, that raw brown scar through the turf, because her feet knew the way even while the rest of her pretended they didn't. The drainage trench ran up the slope like a wound, stakes and string marking where Halloran's crew meant to carve their channel straight through the hillside—and there, where the trench met the old outcrop, stood the door.

She had seen it before, of course. Everyone had, from a distance, in the way you see a thing you've agreed not to see. Granite, taller than a man, set flush into the living rock as if the hill had grown around it. An iron ring fused solid with rust—or not rust, the engineers had said, puzzled, some kind of fusion, metal into stone like wax into wax. Scratches across its face, fine and countless, which Halloran had pronounced glacial striations until a geologist friend looked at photographs and went quiet and then went home.

Up close, in the moonlight, the scratches resolved into something else entirely. Not striations. Marks. Tallies. Rows upon rows of them, four strokes and a diagonal, four and a diagonal, marching across the granite in columns, in pages, in years. And Maren stood before them with her breath smoking in the cold and understood, with the dull certainty of a woman reading her own account book, that she was looking at a ledger. That the hill had been counting too. That the count above and the count below were the same count, kept in two hands, the way a shopkeeper keeps the duplicate copy.

The tallies stopped. Partway down the lowest column, mid-stroke—one clean vertical line begun and abandoned, a hundred years old and still looking unfinished. She reached out and laid her fingers on it. The stone was cold, but not moor-cold. It was the cold of a cellar in summer, a held breath, a room underground where nothing has moved in a very long time.

And there, at the base of the door, neatly placed side by side with their toes pointing inward, toward the granite, toward the dark—

Boots.

Edmund Harrow's boots. She knew them the way you know your neighbors' washing on a line: patched at the left heel, laces replaced last spring with good hemp twine she'd sold his mother herself from the drawer behind the bar. They were placed with care. Not thrown, not dropped—*placed*, squared to each other, the way a man leaves his boots at his own door when he's come home for the night. Except the door they faced was not his door, and the home beyond it was not his home, and the toes pointed inward because whoever arranged them wanted it understood, in the plain dumb language of objects, that Edmund Harrow had gone inside.

Maren stood in the frost for a long time. She did not cry, which surprised her; she did not scream, which surprised her more. What she felt instead was worse than either: a terrible administrative calm, the calm of a woman holding a ledger that has balanced itself without her permission. Somewhere below her feet, in the dark under the hill, a column had gained a mark. Somewhere above, a page had lost a name. The sums agreed. The books were in order. And she, Maren Ashdown, keeper of the count, keeper of the inn, keeper of every stray soul who'd ever slept under her roof—she had stood at her gate at dusk and let it happen, pencil in hand, saying nothing, because saying nothing was the oldest habit in Hollowmere and habits were the only religion anyone here still practiced.

"No," she said aloud, to the door, to the hill, to the whole rotten arrangement. Her voice came out small in all that open dark, and she hated it, and she planted her feet and said it again, louder. "No. He was twenty-three. He had a girl in Thistleford and a laugh you could hear across two fields, and he was *twenty-three*, and I will not stand here and call that a payment. Do you hear me?"

The hill, naturally, said nothing. Hills are excellent at silence; they've had practice.

But as she turned to go—as she made herself turn, made herself leave the boots where they lay because taking them felt like theft and leaving them felt like consent and she chose, for tonight, the sin she could live with—the bell in the village below began to ring.

There was no one in the belfry. She knew that the way she knew her own hands. Cobb had locked the tower at sundown; she'd watched him do it. Yet the bell rang on, unhurried, thirteen strokes, and on the thirteenth it did not stop but seemed to hang in the air, one note stretched thin as wire, and in that ringing Maren heard—not imagined, *heard*—a voice underneath it, dry and patient as paper, keeping time.

Counting.

* * *

Gideon was waiting up when she came in. Of course he was. He sat at the corner table with a cold cup of tea and that awful, gentle attentiveness, and he took one look at her face and didn't ask anything, which was somehow more infuriating than questions.

"I found his boots," she said, because she was too tired for strategy and too angry for silence, and anger, she was learning, was the only thing in Hollowmere that hadn't been rationed yet. "At the door. Facing inward. Neat as church." She pulled off her wet coat and threw it over the chair harder than the coat deserved. "And do you know what the worst of it is, Gideon Winterbourne? Not the hill. The hill is just a hill doing what hills do when somebody signs papers on our behalf a century before any of us were born. The worst of it is us. Twelve households heard that bell ring thirteen times and every one of them went to bed. Mrs. Harrow lit an extra lamp and thanked me for the loaf. We didn't search. We didn't shout. We *struck his name* and carried on, because that's the bargain, isn't it—that's what we've all been paying into all these years without ever seeing the paperwork. Safety. As long as it's never your son."

Gideon's face had gone the color of the ledger's old pages. "Maren—"

"And here's the other thing I worked out on the walk home, standing in a frozen field talking to a rock like a madwoman." She leaned both fists on the table. "The count runs in order. Family lines, eldest first—you've seen the pages, you know the order they're written in. Harrows first. Then Prewitts. Then Hedgerows." Her voice caught, and she hated that too, and pushed through it. "Then Ashdowns. And there's only two of us left in the book, my lad, and one of them is fourteen years old and asleep upstairs, and the other one is standing right here telling you that I will burn that covenant, dig up that door, or lie down in front of it myself before I let this village count another soul like coins in a till."

For a moment Gideon said nothing. Then, quietly, in the voice of a man reading something he'd hoped never to read: "Maren. Why do you suppose the letters inviting a folklorist to Hollowmere were signed *Winterbourne*?"

The fire ticked. Upstairs, floorboards settled. And Maren, who had spent her whole life keeping count of other people, felt the number of things she did not know begin, at last, to be counted against her.
