## 13. Second Taken

Tansy knew something was wrong before the bells finished.

She was in the schoolhouse, of all places—sneaking in after hours to straighten the slates Miss Ellery always left crooked, because Miss Ellery said a crooked slate kept the room honest and Tansy said a crooked slate kept the room untidy, and they had argued about it every Friday for two years in a way that Tansy now understood, with a horrible new clarity, had been love wearing an argument's clothes.

The bells rang at dusk. That was normal. The bells rang thirteen times when there were twelve bells, and that was not normal, but it had stopped being not-normal a fortnight ago, and Hollowmere adapted the way water adapts to a stone: by going around it and pretending the stone had always been there.

But tonight the thirteenth ring hung in the air like a struck tooth.

And then the count came, rolling up from the churchyard, from the wells, from under the floorboards where old things slept with their faces pressed to the wood, and every voice in the village answered it. Tansy heard Mrs. Harrow through her shutter, flat as a hymn. She heard Cobb's cracked baritone. She heard Maren downstairs, steady, steady, steady, saying names like she was setting them down gently on a shelf.

Tansy did not answer. She never answered. Maren had told her, the first night the count began again, *you're not on their list yet, love, and we'll keep it that way*, and so Tansy held her name behind her teeth like a coin she refused to spend.

Then the count reached the Ellery line, and paused.

The pause lasted three heartbeats. Tansy counted them against her own chest, which was a stupid thing to do, because then her heart started racing to catch up with itself.

*Ellery,* said the count, from everywhere and nowhere, patient as arithmetic.

No one answered.

The whole village held its breath at once—you could feel it, the silence pressing down on the lane like snow—and Tansy stood in the dark schoolhouse with a crooked slate in her hands and understood, in the way you understand a fall only once you've already left the roof, that Miss Ellery had not come home.

* * *

They found the boots at dawn.

Maren wouldn't let Tansy go—of course she wouldn't; Maren had developed a grip like a mooring rope since the count began—but Cobb went, and Halloran went with him, the engineer walking with his head down like a man following a debt collector, and they came back at first light with their faces the color of porridge.

"Boots," Cobb said, sitting down at the inn's long table without appearing to decide to sit. "Placed at the hillside door. Facing inward." He turned his hat over and over in his hands. "Neat as church."

"What does facing inward mean?" Tansy asked.

Nobody answered her, which meant it meant something terrible.

"It means," Gideon said quietly, from the corner where he'd been sitting all night without sleeping—he had shadows under his eyes deep enough to post letters into—"it means she walked in. Willingly or otherwise. It means the door knows she's accounted for."

"And nobody went after her?" Tansy's voice came out higher than she intended. "Nobody—not one person—walked up that hill and knocked?"

"Tansy—" Maren began.

"There are forty-one adults in this village!" She was standing now; she didn't remember standing. "Forty-one grown people with legs and lanterns and opinions, and not one of them could climb a hill?"

"The last man who climbed that hill uninvited," Cobb said, very gently, "was Josiah Harrow's grandfather. In 1811. We don't speak of what came back. Mostly because it didn't stop coming back for some time."

"Well, I'm not forty-one adults," Tansy said. "I'm one fourteen-year-old girl who is going up that hill right now, and if any of you try to stop me I will bite you, Maren, I'm sorry, but I will, you know I will—"

She made it four steps toward the door.

Gideon caught her around the shoulders, and it wasn't violent, it was barely even firm—it was the grip of a man who had spent a week learning exactly how much force this village considered acceptable and hated every ounce of it—but Tansy was furious and grieving and fourteen, and she fought him like a kettle fights a lid.

"Let me GO—"

"He'll be taken next if you open that door for him!" Maren's voice cracked across the room like a wet towel. "You think the count doesn't see? You think it isn't *watching* how much he matters to us? Every kindness is a ledger entry, Tansy! You'd be handing them his name gift-wrapped!"

That stopped her. Not because it convinced her—nothing would convince her—but because it was true, and truth, Tansy had learned, was the only thing in Hollowmere heavier than grief.

She sagged against Gideon, and Gideon held on, and for a moment the two of them just stood there in the middle of the inn like a pair of fence posts leaning on each other in a wind.

"I liked her," Tansy said, into his coat. It came out small and furious. "She gave me extra ink when I ran out and she pretended it was my imagination. She called my essays 'spirited,' which meant too many exclamation marks, and she read every single one anyway. Even the one about the pig."

"The one about the pig," Gideon repeated, with the careful voice of a man trying not to find anything funny while a girl cried on him.

"It was a *good* essay. The pig had interior motives."

"Interior motives," Gideon said again, and something in his voice wobbled in a way that might have been a laugh or might have been the other thing, and Tansy decided to allow it either way.

* * *

The village did not mourn Miss Ellery.

That was the part that broke something loose in Tansy. Not the taking itself—she was almost getting used to the taking, which was its own obscenity—but the *administrative* quality of everything after. Mrs. Harrow came by the inn with the updated household rolls and asked, in the tone of a woman asking whether you wanted your bread sliced, whether the Ellery house should be shuttered or aired quarterly. The school bell did not ring the next morning. A notice appeared on the schoolhouse door: *Lessons suspended pending arrangements.* Pending arrangements. As though Miss Ellery were a delivery.

"They're not even sad," Tansy said that evening, scrubbing pots with a violence that endangered the pots. "Old Mr. Fennel said—and I quote—'well, she was third in the Ellery line, it stands to reason.' *Stands to reason.* Like weather. Like turnips."

"Fear wears obedience because obedience fits better than grief," Gideon said. He was at the table with his papers, though he hadn't written anything in an hour; Tansy had been watching him pretend. "It's easier to have a rule than a wound. Rules you can follow. Wounds you have to carry."

"That's a horrible way to be wise."

"Yes," he agreed. "I got it from a village full of people counting their doors."

Maren came in from the taproom, wiping her hands, and looked at the two of them—Tansy attacking a pot, Gideon attacking nothing—and something crossed her face that Tansy couldn't read, some arithmetic of her own.

"Tansy," she said. "Come sit a moment."

"I'm busy being angry."

"You can be angry sitting down. It's more sustainable."

Tansy sat. Maren sat across from her and took both her hands, flour and all, and held them the way she used to when Tansy was nine and had nightmares, except now her hands were shaking slightly, which they had never done when Tansy was nine, and that scared Tansy more than the bells had.

"The count took Harrow's boy first because Harrow is the oldest line," Maren said. "Then Ellery. It's going in order, love. Old lines to young. And ours—" her jaw worked—"ours isn't old. I took you in when you were six. Before that you were nobody's, which in Hollowmere means you were everybody's, which means the count can't find your line. It keeps skipping you."

"So I'm safe."

"So you're *unlisted*. Those aren't the same thing, and I don't trust the difference, and neither should you." Maren squeezed her hands. "Which is why I need you to promise me something, and I need you to actually promise it, not do the thing where you say 'fine' and then do whatever you were going to do anyway."

Tansy said nothing, which was, historically, the thing.

"If it comes for me—or for Gideon—you stay inside. You hold the count with Cobb. You don't run to the door. You don't offer yourself. Because here's what I've learned about bargains in this village, sweetheart: they only ever cost the people who love you." Her voice frayed at the edge. "Promise me."

And Tansy, who loved her more than she had ever loved anyone including possibly herself, said: "Fine."

Maren closed her eyes. "That was the thing. That was exactly the thing."

* * *

It happened on the fourth night.

Tansy hadn't planned it. Planning implies thought, and thought had packed its bags sometime around the second verse of the funeral-less non-funeral the village held for Miss Ellery, which consisted of the vicar reading her name off a list in the same voice he used for announcing the collection total.

What Tansy did instead was wait until the house went quiet—Maren asleep, Gideon up late with his papers, the lamps guttered in their proper order—and take the storm lantern and Cobb's spare coat, which smelled like earth and pipe smoke and was far too big, and walk out into the dark toward the hill.

The moon was a fingernail paring. The moor grass whispered the way it always did, like a congregation deciding whether to stand. And the hillside door sat in its cut of granite, iron ring fused solid, tally marks crawling across its face like a scar learning to write, and Miss Ellery's boots were gone now, taken in or taken down, and that absence hit Tansy harder than the boots' presence ever could.

She put her hand on the door.

It was warm. Not sun-warm. Skin-warm. The warmth of something that had been waiting a long time under a blanket and was pleased, in a dreadful patient way, to be touched.

*Tansy.*

Not out loud. Out loud would have been kinder. This came up through her palm, through the bones of her arm, in the voice the count used—the voice of arithmetic, of a ledger that had learned to want things.

*Unlisted,* it said, almost fondly. *We could list you.*

"Don't," Tansy whispered.

*Your teacher is below. She asks after you. She has your essay about the pig. She reads it every night. She says it is spirited.*

And that—that was the cruelest possible thing, because Tansy could *see* it, could see Miss Ellery underground in a corridor of doors with a child's essay in her hands, marking the exclamation marks, and the grief in Tansy's chest turned inside out and became something with teeth.

"Give her BACK."

She hauled on the iron ring with both hands, with her whole furious weight, and the ring didn't move but the door *noticed*, the warmth under her palms sharpening into attention, and somewhere beneath the hill something began, softly, to count upward—

Hands closed on her arms and pulled her back, and Tansy screamed and fought, and it was Gideon, of course it was Gideon, he'd followed her or watched her leave or simply known, the way he seemed to know things about this village that the village didn't know about itself, and she twisted and clawed and drove her elbow back into whatever part of him was nearest, and he grunted and did not let go.

"Let me go, let me go, she's DOWN THERE, she's reading my ESSAY—"

"Tansy. *Tansy.* Listen to me—"

"She asked after me! It said she asked after me!"

"And it lied, or it didn't, and either way the door does not open from out here!" He spun her to face him, and his face was gray in the lantern light, and he was breathing hard, and there was a scratch on his cheekbone where she'd caught him, and he didn't seem to know about it. "Listen. *Listen.* If you go down there, it won't be you visiting her. It'll be you joining her. Do you understand? There is no visiting. There is only the count."

"Then let it count me!"

"No." Quiet. Terribly quiet. "No, because I am not letting this village—or that door—have one more person that I—" He stopped. Swallowed whatever the end of that sentence was.

And Tansy, mid-struggle, mid-rage, tried one last time to wrench free, and in doing so she did the worst possible thing, the thing Maren had begged her not to do, the thing that no one in Hollowmere had done in a hundred years:

She sang.

It burst out of her the way blood bursts—no decision in it at all. The forbidden song, the one every villager knew one verse of and refused forever, the lullaby Tansy had sung once for Gideon's phonograph in the early days when the world still had edges. But grief had broken the seal on her, and what came out was not the first verse.

It was the second.

She had never been taught it. No one alive had been taught it. But it poured out of her whole and perfect, a descending melody, a melody that went *down*, note by note like stairs, words in no language she knew and somehow entirely in the language she knew, and the meaning arrived in her chest along with the tune: *come down, come down, the count is kind, come down and be accounted, come down and be at rest—*

The lantern flame lay down flat.

The grass stopped whispering. Under the hill, the counting stopped—stopped like a clock stopping, like a throat closing—and the warmth in the door went taut as a held breath.

And Gideon—Gideon had gone rigid, his hands still on her arms but gripping now like a drowning man, and his lips were moving. Moving in time with hers. Half a beat behind, catching the melody the way you catch a falling cup, and his eyes were wide and wet and horrified, because the song was writing itself into him through her voice, syllable by syllable, and she could feel it leaving her and landing in him like coins into a well.

"*Stop singing,*" he breathed.

"You stop singing!"

"I can't—it's—I can't *stop*—" His voice cracked on the last note of the phrase, and the phrase completed itself in his mouth, and the door beneath their hands shuddered once, deeply, like a sleeper turning over.

Silence. Real silence, the first Tansy had heard on the moor since the excavation.

Gideon released her slowly. He stood there in the dark with his hand pressed flat over his own sternum, as though checking that the song had stayed down.

"What did I just learn," he said. It wasn't really a question. "Tansy. What did I just learn?"

She was crying now, finally, the rage burned down to ash and the ash still hot. "I don't know. I've never—it just *came*—"

"It's the descent verse." His voice had gone strange and far away, the voice of a man reading his own obituary in a language he'd just learned. "Cobb showed me the covenant. One song to open. One song to close. Everyone assumed the closing verse was lost, or that it was the same verse sung twice." He laughed, a small terrible sound. "It's not the same verse. It was never the same verse. The second verse was never meant to be sung up *here* at all."

"Then where—"

He looked at the door. At the fused ring, the tally marks, the warmth fading slowly out of the granite like a hand withdrawing.

"Below," he said. "It's meant to be sung below."

Behind them, down in the village, a single bell rang. Once. Unstruck. And Tansy felt the sound pass through her chest and settle somewhere beside her heart, and she understood it the way you understand a knock at the door when you already know who's standing there.

The count had noticed the song.

The count had noticed *him*.

* * *

They walked back down the hill together, and Tansy held the lantern, and Gideon walked like a man carrying something breakable inside his ribs—which, Tansy supposed, he was.

At the lane's edge he stopped.

"Tansy. What you did tonight—" He rubbed his face. "You nearly got yourself killed, you disobeyed Maren in the exact manner she predicted, and you handed me the only weapon anyone has found in a hundred years."

"That's the most mixed review I've ever gotten."

"It's the most mixed review you've ever *earned*." But he smiled, briefly, tiredly, and put his hand on her head the way Maren did, and for a moment Tansy let herself lean into it. "Go inside. Hold the count tomorrow. Sing nothing."

"And you?"

Gideon looked back up at the hill, where the door sat warm and patient in the dark, keeping its tallies, holding its rows.

"Me," he said, "I'm going to go learn the rest of my inheritance."
