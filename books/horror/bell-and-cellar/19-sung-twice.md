## 19. Sung Twice

The second verse came out of me the way a stone comes out of a boot—painfully, and with an absurd sense of relief.

I had learned it wrong, at first. That was the trouble with the whole village, if you wanted the truth of it: they had all learned everything wrong, for a hundred years, because the first lesson had been learned in fear and every lesson after was copied from a frightened hand. Tansy's struggle in the schoolhouse lane had knocked the true verse loose from her—the verse meant to be sung *below*, facing the doors, not above them facing the hill—and when she had shouted it at me, furious and grieving and thirteen years old, it had lodged in my chest like a swallowed coal. I had carried it down the corridor of doors, and now, standing in the dark with my great-grandfather's hand hovering an inch from mine, I opened my mouth and let it out.

The sound was not what I expected. I am a folklorist; I have opinions about acoustics; I expected the corridor to swallow the song the way Hollowmere swallowed everything, politely and without receipt.

Instead the corridor *sang back*.

Not an echo. Echoes are stupid things, faithful and late. This was harmony. Somewhere ahead of me, in the rows and rows of doors that lined the passage like a village street buried in a hill, voices took up the verse—thin at first, rusty, the way a pump coughs before it gives water—and then steadier, and then, God help me, *glad*. A hundred years of people who had been taken, and not one of them had ever been allowed to finish the song.

They finished it with me.

The nearest door—oak, iron-banded, with a name burned into the wood that I did not let myself read, because I knew whose it would be—shuddered on its hinges. Not the violent shudder of something trying to get out. The shudder of a thing settling. Of a house at night when the wind dies and the timbers finally relax.

Beside me, my great-grandfather made a sound I will carry to whatever grave they give me. It was not a word. It was the sound a man makes when a cramp he has held so long he forgot it was a cramp finally lets go.

"Gideon," he said. My name, in a voice like paper that had been folded and folded and folded. "You sang it *twice*."

"Someone had to," I said. My voice cracked on the second word, and I decided, as a professional, to blame the acoustics.

He laughed. It was a terrible laugh, dry and disused, and it was also the best thing I had heard in my life. "Your grandmother's mouth when she said that. Exactly her mouth." He turned to face the corridor, and I saw him properly in the lantern light for the first time—not the thing at the drill hole, not the face in the dark that had looked back at Halloran's lowered glass, but a man. Tall, stooped now, in clothes that had been good a century ago and had since become simply *his*, the way old coats do. His eyes were the same color as mine. I have spent my life collecting other people's faces and I had never once expected to meet my own, aged and worn, waiting at the bottom of a hill.

"The doors," he said. "They'll want to close in order. They always did. I kept the count, but the count was never mine, you understand. I was only the hand that held the book."

"Then give me the book," I said.

He looked at me for a long moment. Down here, in the place that had offered me belonging like a fisherman offers bait, I felt the offer stir one last time—the warm pull of *stay, keep the count, be needed forever*—and I felt my great-grandfather feel me feel it, because he nodded slowly, the way you nod at a young man deciding not to take the last drink.

"That's the trick of it," he said. "It only ever asks for what you're missing. It asked me for purpose. It asked the Harrow boy for his father's approval. It asked the schoolmistress for a classroom that listened." He smiled, and the smile was tired and kind and a hundred years old. "It never once asked anyone for something they had."

"Then it's poor at its business," I said, "because I have everything I need, and it's all upstairs."

* * *

We began at the far end.

The corridor ran longer than the hill had any right to contain—I had stopped being surprised by this somewhere around the fourth impossible measurement, in the way you stop being surprised by a dog that opens doors—and the doors stood in it the way houses stand in a lane, some near, some far, each with its name. As we walked, the taken came out of the dark to walk with us. That was the thing I will never be able to make Halloran believe, and I have not tried: they were not ghosts, they were not horrors, they were *people*. The eldest Harrow son had his sleeves rolled up like a man between chores. The schoolmistress had chalk dust still on her fingers, though there had been no chalk down here for a year. They fell into step behind us, patient as furniture, as the plan had described them—and then, as the first verse's echo died and the second verse's work began, they stopped being patient at all.

They started to hope. You could hear it. Hope has a sound, and it is very like a held breath.

The first door closed the way a hymn ends.

I did not touch it. My great-grandfather did not touch it. The song did it—the second verse, the true one, the one that had never been sung because no one had ever come down here to sing it, because the bargain had only ever required the *first* verse, the opening verse, the verse that said *come in and be counted*. The second verse said something else. I have tried since to write it down and I cannot; my hand refuses, and I have decided to respect my hand's opinion. But I can tell you what it *said*, because meaning survives translation even when melody doesn't.

It said: *You are released from the ledger.*

It said: *What was owed is paid, and what was taken is returned, and you are not a number anymore.*

It said: *Rest.*

The farthest door—old, older than the others, its name worn to a shadow—swung inward one last time, and something inside it sighed the way a house sighs when the last guest leaves, and then it shut, gently, with a click like a tongue saying *there*, and the name on it faded the way breath fades off a window. And out of it, or out of the space where it had been, came a person. Not a shade. A person, blinking, straightening, looking at their own hands as if they'd been handed back after a long borrowing.

"Edmund," my great-grandfather said softly. "Edmund Carrow. You were first."

The man—Carrow—looked at him, and then at me, and there was no blame in his face, which I had braced myself for and did not know what to do without. "You're the Winterbourne lad," he said. His voice was rough, unpracticed. "The one they wrote to."

"They wrote in my name," I said. "I only found out last week. I'm sorry it took a hundred years."

Carrow considered this with the unhurried air of a man who had, after all, waited a century and could spare another moment. "Took my family three years to bury my mother," he said. "Some things take the time they take." And he fell in beside us, walking toward the next door, and I understood that he was not following me. He was *helping*. The released were helping release the rest, door by door, name by name, the whole buried village of Hollowmere rising off its knees.

* * *

It took, by my best guess, four hours. By the corridor's reckoning it may have taken a hundred years; time down there had the consistency of porridge, and I have stopped litigating the question. We went door to door. Some names I knew from the count—the Harrows, the Pells, the Vosses, families whose lines still stood above and had spent generations flinching at dusk. Some names I did not know, and my great-grandfather knew them all, because he had kept their count, and keeping a count, I learned, is its own terrible form of love. He said each name aloud as its door closed, not as a tally. As a roll call. As a teacher marking a child safely home.

"Silas Pell."

*Click.*

"Agnes Voss."

*Click.*

"Merrit Harrow."

*Click* — and the eldest Harrow boy, walking beside me with his sleeves still rolled, stopped, and put his hand flat against the closed door of himself, and said, "Tell my mum the boots were my idea. So she wouldn't think I went messy." And then he smiled, and it was a boy's smile, and I promised him, because what else does a man do with a request like that but carry it up the hill like water?

The schoolmistress was last of the taken but one. Her door had a chalk line drawn under the name—her own doing, I suspected, even down here, even in servitude; some people correct errors wherever they find them—and when the door closed and she stepped free of it, she looked at the corridor, at the rows of shutting doors, at the growing crowd of the rested, and she did what schoolmistresses do.

"Right," she said. "Lines, please. Youngest at the front. You—" this to a Harrow cousin who was drifting— "shoulders back. We are going to walk out of this hill in an orderly fashion if it is the last orderly thing any of us does, and I will have no shoving."

And they lined up. God help me, they lined up, and there was something so achingly, absurdly human about it that I had to stop and pretend to adjust my lantern while I got my face back under management.

The last door but one was my great-grandfather's own.

It stood at the corridor's end, apart from the others, and it was smaller than I expected, and it was not shut. It had never been shut. That was the whole shape of the bargain, I understood at last—he had gone below and sealed the door *behind* him, but his own room had no door to close, because the keeper's room was the corridor itself. He had lived a hundred years in the hallway of the dead, keeping their count, holding the book, with no door of his own to close and no verse to sing himself free.

"Yours," I said. "Sing it. You know it now. You heard me."

He shook his head, and his hand found mine, and his grip was cold and dry and strong as a root.

"The count has to finish," he said. "That's the last of the terms. The book closes from inside or it doesn't close at all, and I'm the last name in it. I always was." He smiled at whatever my face was doing. "Don't look like that. A hundred years I've wanted to strike my own name, lad. Do you know what it is to count everyone out of a room and know you're the reason the room exists? Strike it. Strike it for me."

"I'll strike it *with* you," I said, "but you're not staying in the hallway."

"No," he agreed. "I'm not staying anywhere. That's rather the point." He turned to the wall at the corridor's end, where the stone was smooth, and drew from his coat—I never learned where he kept it, and I have decided it is one of the hill's small mysteries, like where the bells keep their thirteenth ring—a piece of chalk worn to a nub. The same chalk, I would have sworn it, the same chalk that had scratched the tally marks into the outside of the door a hundred years ago. He raised it to the smooth stone, where a hundred years of unspoken names waited in his memory, and he began to say them one last time.

Not the count. Not the dusk-count, the frightened one, the one that kept the bargain honest by keeping the village small. He said them the way Maren said grace—each name given its full weight, each one a door, each one *loved*. And as he said each name, he struck it from the stone, and the stone took the strike, and where the chalk fell away the granite shone like water.

The rested stood behind him in their lines, the schoolmistress's orderly procession, and they said the names with him. All of them. A hundred voices that had been furniture for a century, saying one another home.

And I stood beside my great-grandfather with my hand in his, and I said the names too, the ones I knew, and where I didn't know them I said *friend*, and *neighbor*, and *the one who liked the singing*, and the stone took those strikes as gladly as any.

Then there was one name left.

He turned to me. Up close, in the lantern light, with the corridor emptying of doors and the air going soft the way air goes before dawn, he looked less like my ancestor and more like what he had been all along: a man at the end of a very long shift.

"Say it for me," he said. "I've said it every night for a hundred years and it never once sounded like mine."

So I said it. "Edmund Winterbourne."

And he struck it, and the stone rang like a bell—one clean note, the note the village bells had been trying to make all along, the note under all the others—and the corridor let go.

* * *

I will not pretend I remember the walk out with any dignity. There is a version of events, which I have told no one and will tell no one, in which a grown man and a folklorist of some small reputation walked up a passage of closing darkness with a procession of the rested behind him, weeping freely, singing the second verse over and over not because it needed singing but because it was the first song in a hundred years that anyone down there had been allowed to sing *for pleasure*. The schoolmistress kept the lines orderly. The Harrow boy carried the lantern when my arms got tired. My great-grandfather walked beside me the whole way, lighter at every step, until near the end he was less a man than a warmth at my shoulder, and then less than that, and then—

At the foot of the stair that led up to the sealed door, he stopped.

"This is as far as my terms go," he said. "I was the inside of the door, lad. The inside doesn't get to leave. But the *outside*—" he looked up the stair, toward the thin line of lamplight where Maren and Tansy and half the village were holding the count against the dark— "the outside gets to do whatever it likes. Seal it from your side. That's the mercy of it. That's the whole mercy, and it's enough."

"Edmund," I said—his name, the first time I had said it to his face.

"Lad."

"Was it worth it? The bargain. A hundred years."

He was quiet a moment. Down in the emptied corridor, the last of the doors stood open like mouths after a song, and the dark there was only dark now, ordinary dark, cellar dark, the dark of a place where nothing is waiting.

"No," he said. "But the *trying* was. I went down for them, and that was right. I stayed down out of the terms, and that was wrong, and it took a hundred years and a boy with my own stubborn chin to finish it properly. Tell them that. Tell them the bargain was half right and half wrong, like most bargains, and the half that was right was the loving, and the half that was wrong was the counting." He squeezed my hand once, hard, the way men of his generation said everything they couldn't say. "Now sing, Gideon. Sing it twice. And then—" and here his face did my grandmother's mouth again, the family mouth, the mouth I now knew I owned— "then go home. You've got people waiting up. It's the best thing a man can have, people waiting up. I'd know. I've watched it through a door for a century."

And he was gone—not faded, not taken, just *finished*, the way a song is finished, the way a count is finished, the last stroke struck, the book closed.

I climbed the stair. Behind me, the corridor's dark settled into itself like a bed after the sleeper rises. And I sang the second verse one final time, facing the doorway, and the great granite door that had been sealed from within for a hundred years swung shut in front of me—not slammed, not sealed in fear, but *closed*, the way you close a door behind a guest who has finally gone home—and I set my palm against it from the outside, and I felt the stone cool under my hand, and I felt it become, at long last, simply a door. A thing in a hill. Shut, and staying shut, and nothing behind it but earth and roots and the honest dark.

The tally marks on its face—the old ones, ending mid-stroke a century ago—I finished. I had chalk in my pocket; I always have chalk in my pocket; a folklorist without chalk is a fisherman without a hook. One stroke. The count complete. The debt discharged.

Then I put my forehead against the cold granite, and I laughed, and I cried, and I did both in the undignified proportion of a man who has just been orphaned and unburdened in the same hour, and up the hill, faint and clear and *only twelve times*, the bells of Hollowmere began to ring.

They were ringing for me. And this time, every name they rang was a name that had been set free.

I picked up the lantern and went home to the people waiting up.
