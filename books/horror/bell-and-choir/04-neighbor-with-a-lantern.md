## 4. Neighbor with a Lantern

Deacon Hale had kept the church bell of Little Sallow for fifty-one years, and in all that time he had learned exactly three things worth passing on.

First: a bell remembers every hand that ever rung it, and it sulks if you change your grip.

Second: never trust a Tuesday that arrives too quietly.

Third — and this was the one he'd have carved on his own headstone if the stonecutter hadn't charged by the letter — when a Coldharbour comes down the hill, you count them. Twice. Then count again, because the first two counts are liable to disagree.

He was counting now, from his kitchen window, with a mug of tea going cold in his fist and a slice of buttered toast forgotten at his elbow like an abandoned soldier.

The bus didn't come to Little Sallow anymore except on Thursdays, and this was a Wednesday, which meant the figure trudging up from the crossroads with a suitcase and a coat that had seen better decades in a bigger city had walked the whole way from Marleigh. Nine miles. That was grief for you. Grief made people walk places no sensible person would walk, and it made them carry suitcases they should have left behind, and it made them come here, of all places, back to the hill house.

The figure stopped at the gate. Looked up at the house. Looked a long time.

Hale counted.

One Coldharbour.

He waited. Counted again.

One Coldharbour.

"Well," he said aloud, to the toast, "that's either very good or very bad, and I've not yet decided which."

Because there had been three Coldharbours once. He remembered it plainly, the way he remembered bell-ropes and debts: the grandmother, Marta, straight-backed as a fence post; and the twins, Ulyana and Yuliya, running down the hill path every Sunday with their hair braided identically and their boots on the wrong feet half the time, because they'd each insisted on dressing themselves and neither would admit defeat by fixing it. Yuliya always laughed first. Ulyana always laughed longer. Hale had been a younger man then, with fewer aches and more certainties, and he'd thought those two girls would outlive the church itself.

Then one autumn there were two. And then, five years past, there was one. And now the one had come home carrying a suitcase full of whatever you bring when you mean to bury somebody.

The trouble — the whole rotten trouble of it — was that Hale could not, even now, say which twin had survived. He'd looked at the funeral notice in the parish sheet. *Yuliya Coldharbour, beloved granddaughter.* But notices were written by whoever was left, and whoever was left always believed themselves the survivor. That was human nature. That was also, in this particular valley, occasionally wishful thinking.

He drank the cold tea. It tasted like punishment, which he supposed was fitting.

* * *

At dusk he did what he did every night, and had done every night for as long as anyone in Little Sallow could remember, including himself, though the remembering got slippery around the edges these days.

He built a fire in his yard.

Not a bonfire — nothing festive, nothing that would draw a crowd or a constable. A small, disciplined fire, in the old iron basin that had once held flowers outside the church door until the flowers froze solid one February and nobody had the heart to use the basin again. Into the fire he placed the night's offering, which tonight was a bundle of dried rosemary tied with red thread, and a photograph.

The photograph was forty years old. It showed the church fete, 1983, everybody squinting into the sun: the vicar holding a prize marrow, Mrs. Tull with her legendary gooseberry jam, and in the background, half-turned away, a young woman with dark hair who was not looking at the camera because she was looking at something behind it.

Hale had burned this same photograph eleven times. Each morning the fire pit yielded nothing but ash, and each evening, when he went to the drawer where he kept such things, the photograph was there again, slightly more faded, slightly more patient.

"You'll not get me twice," he told it, as he fed it to the flames. "I know what you are. I knew what you were before any of them did."

The fire crackled in a way fires shouldn't — a little too much like speech, a little too much like laughter — and Hale stood with his hands behind his back and watched it until it died, because a man who tended a church bell ought to be good at vigils, and he was, and it was the only talent the valley had ever asked of him.

From up the hill, faint through the trees, came light. The Coldharbour house, waking up. Somebody moving through rooms that had been shut for years, opening curtains, disturbing dust that had arranged itself into patterns over the long vacancy. Patterns, Hale knew, that were not accidental. Dust in that house had opinions.

"God help her," he said — and then paused, because he genuinely did not know whether he meant Ulyana or Yuliya, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

The next morning he took the bell its breakfast.

This is what he called it, and the village had long since stopped finding it quaint: a soft cloth, a tin of oil, a quarter hour of fussing over the great wheel and the headstock and the rope's worn place, where his own palms had polished the hemp to a shine like dark honey. The bell was named Margaret, after nobody living, and she had a voice that carried nine miles on a still day — clear across the valley to Marleigh, where people set their clocks by her without knowing why their bread rose better on Sundays.

Margaret had been silent since the news came. Not rung silent — *silent*, the way a dog goes quiet when something walks past the end of the lane that only dogs can see. Hale had climbed to her that first morning, taken the rope in both hands, and felt her refuse him. Not stuck. Refusing. There is a difference, and a man who has spent fifty-one years with a bell knows it in his wrists.

"Not yet, old girl," he murmured now, oiling the wheel. "There's a funeral to finish first. Then we'll see. Then we'll see whether you ring at all, or whether you've finally gone over to the other lot."

The other lot. He said it lightly, even alone, even to a bell, because saying things heavily gave them weight, and weight was how they got hold of you.

Through the belfry louvres he could see the hill house, and smoke rising from its chimney, thin and gray and wrong. Wrong because the chimney drew crooked — everyone knew that, everyone had always known that, Marta Coldharbour herself had said the chimney smoked sideways on principle — and yet this smoke went up perfectly straight, plumb vertical, like a line drawn by a schoolmaster, like a finger pointing at heaven or at the well behind the house, depending on how you cared to read it.

Hale read it both ways. He was thorough.

* * *

On Thursday the bus came, and with it Mrs. Tull, returning from her quarterly expedition to the supermarket in Marleigh, where she bought things in quantities that suggested she was provisioning a siege. She found Hale at his gate and delivered the week's intelligence the way she delivered everything: at volume, in order of grievance.

"—and the girl's back, of course, you'll have seen, walking from Marleigh like some pilgrim, and she's opened the house, and Enid Prout says there's *two* toothbrushes in the cup upstairs, wet ones, and Enid only went round with a pie out of decency—"

"Enid went round with a pie out of nosiness," Hale said.

"—and the chair, Deacon. The chair at the table. Pulled out. Facing the door. And the girl moved it back against the wall, and next morning it was out again, and Enid says—" Mrs. Tull lowered her voice, which for Mrs. Tull meant the volume of ordinary conversation elsewhere, "—Enid says the girl sat in it. Sat right down in the extra chair and ate her supper there, talking to nobody, and smiled while she did it."

Hale looked at the hills. The light was going long and gold, the way it did before weather, and the churchyard yew threw a shadow clean across the road like a black river.

"What did Enid do?"

"What would you do? She said 'evening' and she left. We're not animals, Deacon."

"No," Hale agreed. "We're worse. We're neighbors."

Mrs. Tull sniffed, shifted her shopping bags, and then — because under the volume and the grievances she had a heart, and it was a decent one, and it troubled her — she said, quieter: "It isn't right, what we let happen to that family. What we *keep* letting happen. You know it and I know it."

"I know it," Hale said.

"And you burn your bit of rubbish every night like that makes up for anything."

"It doesn't," Hale said. "But it keeps my yard mine. There's a difference between guilt and rent, Mrs. Tull, and I pay both."

She looked at him a long moment, this old woman with her siege provisions and her gooseberry-jam pride, and something passed over her face that might have been pity, if pity weren't too proud a word for what the people of Little Sallow allowed themselves.

"The bell will ring soon," she said. "Mark me. Before the thaw's done. And when it does, I'm not answering the door."

"None of us will answer the door," Hale said. "That's rather the arrangement."

* * *

That night the fire in the iron basin got more than rosemary.

Hale had gone into the back room — the room he called the vestry, though it held no vestments, only boxes, and among the boxes one box he did not open, had not opened since Marta Coldharbour's funeral, when she'd pressed it into his hands with fingers like winter twigs and said, *Keep this where the fire can reach it, Deacon, and don't let them read it, and don't read it yourself, you're too honest for your own good* — and he had taken from the box, just taken, not opened, a folded paper that rustled like dry wings.

He held it over the basin a long time. The firelight made the paper glow amber, and beneath the amber, faint as breath on glass, he could almost see the ink shifting. Not writing. Just settling. Getting comfortable.

"You're not getting out," he told it. "You're not getting read. Whatever bargain she cut, it ends with her, and it ends with me standing here like a fool burning the same photograph for forty years."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the sound of it — and he would swear this later, to no one, because there was no one to tell — somewhere in the sound was a syllable. One syllable. It might have been *no.* It might have been his name.

He put the paper back in the box. He put the box back in the vestry. He went out and burned the rosemary and stood vigil until the coals went gray, and above him the stars came out hard and cold, and up the hill the house showed one lit window, and in the lit window, briefly, there were two silhouettes.

He blinked. One silhouette.

He counted again, the way he always counted, twice and then once more for honesty.

One silhouette. Standing quite still. Looking down the hill, he was nearly sure, directly at his little fire, directly at him.

Hale raised one hand — an old man's wave, half greeting, half surrender — and the curtain fell closed, gentle as a wing.

* * *

He slept badly, which was normal, and dreamed of water, which was not.

In the dream he was young again, and it was the year everything changed, and he was standing at the wellhead behind the Coldharbour house with a lantern, because someone had to carry the lantern, that was the whole of his job that night, someone had to hold the light, and he had told himself ever since that holding the light was not the same as doing the deed. Fifty-one years of telling himself that. The dream did not argue. The dream simply showed him his own young hands, steady on the lantern handle, and the light falling down into the dark, and the dark rising up to meet it like something coming home.

He woke before dawn with the taste of well water in his mouth — cold, mineral, sweetish, the way it never was — and lay listening.

The house on the hill was awake. He couldn't have said how he knew; there were no lights, no sounds at that distance. But he knew it the way Margaret the bell knew a hand on her rope. Something over there was stirring, moving through the dark rooms, following something.

Following something *wet*, he thought, and did not know why he thought it, and hated that he thought it.

Hale got up, dressed by feel, and went downstairs. In the kitchen he lit the lamp, filled the kettle, and stood at the window while the sky went from black to bruise. His eyes went, as they always went, to the hill.

And there — pale in the last of the starlight, small against the darker mass of the house — a figure came around the corner of the Coldharbour house and stopped at the low stone wall behind it. Where the well was. Where the cap was, the flat stone that had not been lifted in his lifetime, that Marta herself had sealed with lead and prayer and, he'd sometimes suspected, a certain amount of swearing.

The figure stood at the wall a long moment. Then it stepped over it.

Hale's hand was on the door latch before he knew he'd moved. The kettle began, absurdly, to sing.

He stood in his doorway with the lamplight spilling yellow down his front path, an old man in his coat at four in the morning, torn clean down the middle — go to her, warn her, tell her everything, open the box, pay the debt, damn the village and its silence — or stand here, as he had stood for fifty-one years, holding the light and calling it innocence.

The kettle sang. The figure at the wellhead bent down, out of sight below the wall.

And from behind the capped stone, faint but unmistakable, rising through the ground itself like warmth through floorboards, came humming.

A lullaby. The one Marta used to sing. Every note of it, sweet and slow, drifting up the hill and down again —

and stopping, short and deliberate, one verse shy of the end.

Hale closed his door. He turned off the lamp. He sat in the dark with his heart going like a hammer on an anvil, and he did not sleep again, and when the sun came up he would tell himself he'd imagined it, and he would almost believe himself, and tonight he would build the fire again, and burn the photograph again, and keep the box unopened again.

But he knew — the way he knew bells, the way he knew Tuesdays — that the song had started without permission, and songs like that did not stop for silence.

They stopped for a singer.
