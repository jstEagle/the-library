## 5. What the Well Remembers

The morning the footprints appeared, Ulyana was trying to make coffee in a kitchen that seemed personally offended by the concept.

The stove was one of those old enamel monsters that required a negotiation—match first, then a prayer, then a small act of faith—and the kettle whistled in a key that made the dog next door (three miles next door; this was the kind of place where "next door" was a philosophical position) begin to howl. Ulyana stood there in grandmother's too-large flannel robe, hair uncombed, watching the kettle like it might confess something.

"Fine," they told it. "Fine. You win."

This had become a habit, talking to the house. It had started as a joke during the first week—*nice dust arrangement, Grandma's ghost has hobbies*—and had curdled slowly into something more sincere. Because the house did seem to listen. The floorboards creaked in answer sometimes, a syllable or two behind, like a slow student repeating what you'd said.

And the dust. Ulyana had given up on the dust. They'd swept the parlor twice and both times found it resettled by evening into long, deliberate curves—not drifts, *patterns*, sweeping arcs that radiated out from the corner where the extra chair sat. It looked less like neglect and more like calligraphy. Like someone practicing their signature in a language Ulyana didn't read.

They poured the coffee—it tasted of iron and old pipes, which after two weeks felt less like a flaw and more like terroir—and carried it to the back door to drink while the hills did their gray morning thing.

That was when they saw the footprints.

They led from the back step down across the yard, through the wet grass, toward the tree line behind the house. Bare feet. Small ones. And here was the thing that made Ulyana's coffee hand go still against the doorframe: the prints were *wet*, gleaming dark on the frosted grass, and it had not rained in nine days.

Ulyana knew those feet. You know your own feet. You know your twin's feet better than anyone's, because for the first years of your life the two of you were measured against each other constantly—height marks on the pantry doorframe, shoe sizes compared at every shop, Aunt Renata holding your ankles up side by side like a butcher weighing cuts. Yuliya's feet had been half a size smaller. A source of endless smugness on Ulyana's part, and endless schemes on Yuliya's, including one memorable attempt to stretch her shoes overnight by stuffing them with wet newspaper, which had only succeeded in giving her a week of blisters and a permanent grudge against the *Herald*'s classified section.

Half a size smaller. These prints were half a size smaller.

"Nope," Ulyana said aloud, conversationally, to no one. "No thank you."

But their legs were already moving. That was the thing about grief nobody warned you about: it made you stupid in specific directions. A living person would have gone inside, locked the door, called Deacon Hale, maybe called a priest, possibly called a realtor. Ulyana set down the coffee on the step—carefully, so it wouldn't spill, because even in extremis there is a part of us that minds the small things—and followed the footprints into the trees.

* * *

The path behind the house was one Ulyana half-remembered from childhood summers: a track worn through the birches by generations of Coldharbour feet hauling water, firewood, and complaints. The birches had grown taller. Everything else had shrunk—the way childhood geography does when you return to it, the enormous forest of memory revealed as forty scraggly trees and a ditch.

The footprints didn't waver. They went straight as a plumb line, and they went *downhill*, and downhill meant one thing, because there was only one thing downhill behind the Coldharbour house.

The well.

Ulyana had forgotten the well existed, honestly. Or had performed forgetting, which amounts to the same thing in the ledger of the heart. There are objects in every family's history that everyone agrees not to mention, and the well had been one of theirs. Grandmother had never spoken of it directly, but she'd enforced its absence with the quiet totality of a border checkpoint. No games past the tree line. No exploring "back there." Once, when Ulyana was seven, Yuliya had come back from an unauthorized expedition with her pockets full of interesting gray stones and a story about hearing someone singing down a hole, and Grandmother had gone white as milk and washed Yuliya's mouth out—not with soap, but with well water she'd carried up from the kitchen, which even at seven had struck Ulyana as a strange and terrible transaction. *You don't drink from it. You don't speak of it. What hears you once will learn your name.*

Yuliya had cried. Ulyana had held her hand under the table. And neither twin had gone near the tree line again, and the well had folded itself away into the category of things that were not, and stayed there for thirty years.

Until now.

The clearing opened around them all at once, the way clearings do—one step you're in the trees, the next you're standing in a circle of pale grass with the sky pressing down. The well sat in the middle of it like a punctuation mark at the end of a sentence nobody wanted to finish.

It was capped. That was the first thing. A great round slab of fieldstone and old mortar, domed like a buried skull, with an iron ring set in the top that had rusted to the color of dried blood. Someone had built that cap with care—with *intent*. This wasn't a farmer's afterthought. This was a lid, and lids imply contents, and contents imply a desire to keep them.

The footprints walked right up to the cap and stopped.

Then—they turned around. Facing back the way they'd come. Facing Ulyana.

Standing at the edge of the clearing, Ulyana became aware of several facts in sequence, the way you become aware of individual instruments in an orchestra when the conductor points at each one. Fact one: the footprints ended here, and there were no prints leading *away*, which meant either the walker had left without touching the ground or had never intended to leave. Fact two: the frost on the grass in the rest of the clearing lay undisturbed, silver and perfect, except for a single line of melted green leading from the trees to the cap—a path exactly as wide as a person. Fact three: it was cold, genuinely cold, breath-fog cold, and yet the air rising off the well cap shimmered faintly, the way air does over a summer road.

Something down there was warm. Or something down there was breathing.

"Okay," Ulyana said, and their voice came out steadier than they'd expected, which surprised them, and then the steadiness itself frightened them, because who was doing the expecting? "I'm going to stand here. I'm going to stand here and I'm going to count to ten, and then I'm going to walk back to the house, and I'm going to pretend this is a dream I'm having on the train, which is a thing I do now apparently."

They counted. They got to four.

Because from beneath the cap—muffled by stone and mortar and thirty years of deliberate silence—came a sound.

Humming.

* * *

There is a lullaby every Coldharbour knows. It has no name; Grandmother just called it "the song," the way other grandmothers say "the prayer," with a capital letter you can hear. She sang it every night of their childhood, standing in the doorway between the twins' beds so that one rendition covered both of them, her voice low and unhurried, a melody that wandered in circles the way a lantern beam wanders across a ceiling. It had three verses and a little turn at the end of each one, a descending phrase like footsteps going down stairs, and Ulyana had not thought of it in decades and could nevertheless have hummed it flawlessly under torture. Some songs aren't stored in memory. They're stored somewhere deeper, in the marrow, in the inner ear, in whatever part of us keeps beating without being asked.

The humming under the stone was that song.

It was Yuliya's voice.

Ulyana would have known it anywhere, in any register, through any thickness of stone, across any number of years—including the impossible five that stood between the sister they'd buried and the sister they remembered. Yuliya hummed slightly flat on the third note of every phrase, always had, a tiny sag that music teachers had tried and failed to correct and that Ulyana had privately treasured as proof that perfection was not distributed evenly among twins. The voice below the cap sagged on the third note.

Ulyana's knees decided independently of the rest of them to stop working, and they sat down hard in the frost-wet grass, and did not notice, and did not care.

"*Yuliya*," they said. It came out as almost nothing, a shape of breath. They tried again. "Yuliya. That's—that's not funny. If this is some—some village thing, some hazing, if Hale put a speaker under there—" Even as they said it they heard how it sounded, the desperate arithmetic of a person adding up numbers that refuse to total.

The humming continued. Unhurried. Patient. It moved into the second verse, and Ulyana, sitting in the dead grass with their arms wrapped around their shins like a child at a fire drill, realized their own lips were moving, following along a half-beat behind, harmonizing from the marrow. They clamped their jaw shut. The harmony kept going anyway, faintly, somewhere behind their teeth, and they had the sudden vertiginous certainty that the song was not something they knew but something they *were*, and that it had been waiting in them all these years the way a seed waits, without effort, without opinion.

The second verse ended. The turn descended—footsteps going down stairs.

And then the humming stopped. Right where the third verse should begin.

The silence afterward was worse than the humming. It had texture. It had *expectation*. Ulyana sat in it and understood, with the slow cold slide of a thing becoming undeniable, that the song had three verses, and that the voice below had sung two, and that the stopping was not forgetfulness.

It was a question.

"You want me to—" Ulyana's voice cracked and they hated it and pushed on. "You want me to sing the last verse. Is that it? That's the ask?"

The cap did not answer. But the warmth coming off the stone intensified, gently, like a hand laid over a fevered forehead, and beneath it—so faint Ulyana would spend the rest of the day unsure whether they'd heard it or invented it—came a sound that was not singing at all.

A voice. Speaking. Waterlogged and distant, words arriving the way light arrives at the bottom of a lake, bent and dimmed but arriving:

*"Lya."*

Only children and Yuliya had ever called them Lya.

"I'm here," Ulyana heard themselves say, and their hands were on the stone now, palms flat against the cap, against the warmth, and they had no memory of crawling forward. "I'm here, Yuli. I don't—I don't understand any of this. They buried you. I *saw*—"

*"Cold,"* said the voice below, and it was not an accusation. It was stated the way you'd state the weather, the way you'd mention a headache: a fact endured so long it had stopped being remarkable. *"Lya. It's getting higher."*

"Higher? Higher what, the water?" Ulyana pressed their ear to the stone. Through the mortar, through the dark, they could hear it now that they listened—far down, a soft rhythmic lap, water against stone, patient as the humming had been. Meltwater. Somewhere up in the hills the thaw had begun, weeks early, and all that winter was looking for somewhere to go, and gravity is honest, and honest things flow downhill.

"How high does it get?" Their voice sounded very young to them. Seven years old, standing in a kitchen, watching grandmother wash their sister's mouth. *What hears you once will learn your name.* "Yuli. How high does it get?"

The pause stretched. When the voice came again it was closer, or clearer, or Ulyana had simply begun to hear it the way you hear a radio in another room once you stop fighting it:

*"Sing it down. Sing it down, Lya. Please. Before I forget which verse comes next."*

And then, horribly, gently, the humming began again—from the top, first verse, the way you start over when you fear you won't get another chance—and Ulyana tore their hands off the cap as if it had burned them, though it was warm as skin, warm as a sleeping shoulder, and stumbled backward across the clearing until a birch caught them and they leaned against it and breathed.

The humming did not follow them. It simply continued, circling, circling, a lantern beam crossing a ceiling, stopping each time at the top of the final staircase.

* * *

Ulyana did not remember most of the walk back. They arrived at the back step with wet knees, bark-scraped palms, and the coffee untouched and cold where they'd left it, and they picked it up and drank it anyway, all of it, because the body wants its rituals even—especially—when the world has come off its hinges.

Inside, they did the sensible things. They wrote down everything, timestamped, in the notebook they'd bought for estate inventory, under a heading that started as *WELL — POSSIBLE ACOUSTIC PHENOMENON* and crossed out and rewritten as *WELL — HER VOICE* and finally left as just *WELL*, underlined twice, because euphemism had stopped being available to them somewhere around the third note.

They considered calling Hale. They got as far as lifting the receiver of grandmother's rotary phone—heavy, cream-colored, a fossil that nonetheless had a dial tone, which struck Ulyana as its own small horror—and then set it back down. What would they say? *Your neighbor's well is singing my dead twin's lullaby and skipping the last verse like it's saving room for dessert.* Hale would come over, and he would be kind, and he would look at them with that careful old man's face, and he would tell them nothing, because everything about this village so far had been a conspiracy of gentle omissions, a whole community tiptoeing around a hole in the ground.

No. If there were answers, they wouldn't be in Hale's mouth. Grandmother had been the keeper of this house for sixty years, and keepers keep records. Somewhere in these rooms full of arranged dust and wet toothbrushes and chairs that moved themselves, there was paper. There was always paper. Grandmother had been a woman who labeled her preserves by year and weather; a woman like that did not bind her family to a singing well on a handshake.

Ulyana was halfway down the hall toward the study when they passed the bureau in the front room—dark walnut, brass handles, the one piece of furniture Grandmother had always kept locked, even from the twins, even from the aunts. *That's mine,* she used to say, not unkindly, resting her hand flat on top of it the way you'd quiet a dog. *Everyone's allowed one drawer the house can't open.*

Ulyana stopped. Looked at it. The brass keyhole glinted in the gray light, and from somewhere far behind the house—too far to hear, really, and heard perfectly—came the sound of the second verse beginning again.

The bottom drawer was unlocked.

Ulyana stood in the hallway, heart going like a bird in a box, and reached for the handle.
