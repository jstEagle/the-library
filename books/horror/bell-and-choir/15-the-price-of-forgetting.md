## 15. The Price of Forgetting

The crypt under St. Brannoc's is warmer than you would expect, which is the first kindness I offer anyone. The second is tea. The third—well. The third depends on what they're willing to pay.

I had set out two cups before Ulyana Coldharbour ever knocked on my door, because I had heard them coming for three nights: their footsteps in the churchyard above, pacing the length of the graves like a person measuring a room they hoped not to live in. Grief walks that way. So does arithmetic.

"Sit," I said, when the door opened and they stood there dripping thaw-water onto my floor, looking at me the way people always look at me first—with the polite confusion of someone who has been told to expect a monster and has instead found a thin old woman in a cardigan with a kettle on a brazier. "You'll want to be seated for this. Most people do. It's the standing up afterward that undoes them."

"You're the Choirkeeper," Ulyana said. Not a question. Their voice had the flatness of someone who has spent two weeks being lied to by furniture and photographs and has run out of surprise the way a well runs out of rope.

"I am. And you are half a Coldharbour, though which half is a matter of some dispute." I poured the tea. Chamomile and something older; I keep bees in the summer who visit flowers that don't grow anywhere else. "Drink. What I'm going to show you goes down easier with something warm in you. That's not a figure of speech. It's practically a medical instruction."

They drank. Good. Obedient grief is the easiest kind to teach.

* * *

"Now," I said, setting my cup aside, "you've come about the verse. The final verse. Your grandmother's ledger told you it exists, Deacon Hale told you it has terms, and the well has been telling you, every night, in your sister's voice, that it wants singing. But nobody has told you what it costs, because nobody who knew was brave enough, and nobody who was brave enough didn't know."

"And you know because—"

"Because I invented the price." I smiled, and I felt my face do it, the way one feels a coat button. "Not the song. The song is older than me, older than the village, older than the drowned woman everyone pretends was guilty. Songs like that don't have prices. They have *exchange rates*. Someone has to keep the books. I have kept them for a very long time."

I rose and took the lamp from its hook. "Come and see the accounts."

The back wall of the crypt is stone, and in the stone there are niches—not for ashes, whatever Hale may have told the diocese—and in each niche sits a small object under glass, the way a museum keeps a moth. I walked Ulyana down the row slowly, lifting each lamp-light to each little reliquary, and I told the truth, which is the most expensive thing I own.

"This one," I said, touching the glass over a child's wool mitten, gone grey with age, "belonged to Marta Ilves. She came to me forty years ago with a problem very like yours—a sister below, a verse owed, a house that wouldn't stop setting extra places. She bargained. She sang her sister home and kept her own skin, and she paid me—" I tapped the glass "—her children. Not their lives. Their *weight* in her. She remembers having children the way you remember a coat you once owned. She knows she must have loved them. She can produce no evidence of it. She waves at them across the market square and feels what you feel toward a pleasant stranger who resembles you."

Ulyana had gone very still. Stillness is good. Stillness means the tea is working and the lesson is landing.

"She visits them," I continued, gently, because cruelty without gentleness is just cruelty, and I have never seen the point. "Every Sunday. She brings bread. She is fond of them, in the way one is fond of weather. And here is the thing I need you to understand, Ulyana: she does not grieve them. Grief requires the memory. I took the memory and left the love, and love without memory is just—temperature. A warmth in a room where something used to be."

"That's monstrous," Ulyana said quietly.

"Yes," I agreed. "It is also exactly what she asked for. That's the part people forget when they tell this story later. Nobody in this crypt was robbed. Everyone in this crypt signed."

* * *

Three niches along sat a fountain pen, nib-down, in a bed of velvet gone brown.

"Andrei Voss," I said. "He forgot his name. That sounds small until you try living without it. He came to me a proud man, a schoolmaster, certain he could pay anything because he was certain he was nothing but his mind. He was wrong twice over. I took his name and the name went everywhere—it went out of his letters, off his diploma, out of his mother's mouth before she died. His wife called him 'dear' for eleven years and he answered to it the way a dog answers to a whistle, gladly and without knowing why. At the end he used to stand at his window practicing syllables, trying to find the shape of himself in his own mouth." I looked at the pen a long moment. "He never found it. But he wrote beautifully to the last. Whatever else I am, I am honest about interest rates."

Ulyana's eyes had moved past me, down the row, counting. They were a Coldharbour; they counted. "How many?"

"Fifty-one bargains struck. Forty-eight paid in full. Two ran." I let that sit. "You'll meet neither of them again, so don't waste your fear on it. Running doesn't void the debt. It only changes the collector."

"And the fifty-first?" Ulyana asked. "That's an even number missing. Fifty-one struck, forty-eight paid, two run—that's fifty."

I turned from the wall and looked at them, and I confess I took my time about it, because there is a pleasure in this work that I do not apologize for, and it is the pleasure of finally being *seen*, after decades of being a rumor in a cassock.

"The fifty-first is me," I said. "Or rather—the fifty-first payment is hanging on my face. Come to the mirror. You've earned the whole tour."

* * *

At the far end of the crypt, behind the brazier, there is a mirror in a frame of black oak, and it does not lie, which in my experience makes it rarer than any relic in Rome. I stood Ulyana before it and stood beside them, and I watched them look at me properly for the first time.

My face, as I have already said, is thin. People assume age did that. Age had help.

"Do you see it now?" I asked. "Look closely. Not at what's there—at what isn't. There is no line on this face that was earned by laughing. There is no softness that was put there by being loved. I am not old, Ulyana Coldharbour. I am *empty*. There is a difference, and the difference is that the empty can be filled by anything, and so I am filled with the work."

Their reflection stared at mine. Mine stared back with the mild patience of a portrait.

"My name was Maren Coldharbour," I said, and I felt Ulyana flinch, because the name is in their grandmother's ledger, in the earliest entries, in a hand steadier than the later ones. "Yes. Your grandmother's grandmother's sister. The first of your line to hear the well and think herself clever enough to negotiate with it. The village had drowned a woman in that water and bound her with a song, and the song needed keeping, and the keeping needed a keeper, and I volunteered—volunteered!—because I believed power was a thing you could hold without it holding you." I laughed, a dry sound, like a hymnal closing. "I sang the binding-verse alone, all of it, and I asked the well for the keeper's office, and the well granted it, and the price was everything in me that wasn't useful to the office. My husband. My son—I had a son, I'm nearly sure, there's a niche with his christening cap and I have never once wondered whose it was, which tells you everything. My face, bit by bit, year by year, whenever the work required more of me than the work had. I have been paying myself in installments for ninety years."

Ulyana was quiet. Then, in the flat careful voice they'd been using on the house: "Why show me this? You want me to bargain. This is the sales pitch. Look how terrible the price is—"

"No," I said. "This is the opposite of a sales pitch. Listen to me, child, because I am about to give you the only gift in this room that's free." I turned them by the shoulder to face me, and my hand was cold and they didn't pull away, which I noted with approval. Coldharbours don't startle. "Everyone who has ever stood where you're standing believed they were different. Marta believed her love for her children was structural, load-bearing, couldn't be removed without the whole house falling. Andrei believed his name was the least of him. I believed I was doing God's bookkeeping. We were all intelligent people. Intelligence has never once protected anyone from the well. The well doesn't take your strength or your courage or your cleverness. It takes whatever you are *most made of*, because that's what holds the most water. Do you understand? When you sing that final verse alone—and you will be tempted, oh, you are being tempted right now, every night, in your sister's patient voice—you will not lose something incidental. You will lose the center. And you will walk out of here whole-seeming and hollow-hearted, and you will set a place at your table for someone you cannot remember why you miss."

I stepped back and let them see the whole wall again, all the little glasses, all the little weights.

"So. That is the price of forgetting. Not pain. Pain would be cheap. *Absence.* The well deals in absence the way banks deal in gold."

* * *

We drank the rest of the tea in silence, which suited us both. Ulyana Coldharbour strikes me as a person who does their best thinking in silence, and I do all of mine there.

At the door, they paused. "The two who ran," they said. "What happened to them?"

"They're still running," I said. "One of them is in the city. She buys new photographs every few years, hoping the extra chair won't be in them." I smiled, and felt the smile arrive like weather. "It's always in them. Chairs travel, Ulyana. So do songs."

They went up the crypt steps into the grey afternoon, and I stood listening to their footsteps cross the churchyard—slower now, heavier, a person carrying new arithmetic.

And then I heard the other thing I had been hearing for two days underneath everything, the thing I had not mentioned, because it was not yet mine to mention:

Far up on the hill, behind the Coldharbour house, the well was making a sound it had not made in ninety years.

Not humming. Not the lullaby.

*Cracking.*

Ice letting go of stone. Water rising ahead of schedule, the way debts rise when they smell a settlement close. The thaw had come early this year, and it was coming fast, and somewhere below the roots Yuliya Coldharbour had stopped singing to stay anchored and started singing to come up.

I banked the brazier, took down the ledger, and turned to a fresh page.

There was going to be a fifty-second bargain. I could feel it in my empty face, the way sailors feel weather in old scars.

I only wondered—which twin would sign.
