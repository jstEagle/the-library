## 20. What Rose and What Stayed

Dawn came up gray and gentle over the hills, the way it does after a storm has spent itself entirely, and Ulyana stood at the kitchen window with a cup of tea going cold in their hands and watched the light reach the house for the first time in what felt like years.

The well was dry.

They had gone out to check at first light, boots squelching through mud that had finally stopped trying to swallow them, and leaned over the low stone cap with their heart in their throat. No water. No voice. No darkness looking back with a familiar face. Just a shaft of empty stone going down and down into the earth, smelling of wet chalk and old rope, ordinary as a chimney. A bird had landed on the rim while Ulyana watched—some small brown thing with no opinions about lullabies—and had hopped twice along the stones and flown off again, and that was somehow the most convincing proof of all. The dead do not share their real estate with songbirds.

"Right," Ulyana had said out loud, because the house had gone quiet enough that they could hear their own voice without it harmonizing, and that too felt like a victory. "Right. Good. Excellent."

They had laughed then, a little wildly, standing alone in a muddy yard at six in the morning, and the laugh had echoed off the hills and come back sounding like only one person.

That was the word for it, Ulyana thought now, watching steam curl off the tea. Only one. The house was only-one again. The chair at the foot of the bed had been there when they woke, yes, but it was just a chair—wood and dust and no particular attention in it. The photographs on the mantel showed two children and one empty space, which was the correct and terrible arithmetic of a family that had lost a twin, and not the wrong arithmetic of a house that had been keeping a spare. The dust lay where dust should lie. The toothbrush cup held one toothbrush, dry. When Ulyana had walked through the rooms that morning, opening curtains, the house had creaked in the ordinary way houses creak—timber cooling, pipes settling—and not in the way it had creaked before, which was the way a throat clears before speaking.

It was over. They had won.

Ulyana took a sip of the cold tea and made a face, and put the kettle on, and that was when their right hand set the cup down without being asked.

Not dropped. Set down. Gently, precisely, in the center of the counter, handle turned to the left, the way Yuliya had always turned her cups because she was left-handed and the world had never once been built for her. Ulyana stared at the cup. The cup sat there, smug, handle left. Their hand hung at their side, warm and obedient and no longer entirely theirs.

"Huh," Ulyana said.

The kettle clicked toward boiling. Ulyana made a fresh cup, deliberately, watching their own fingers the whole time like a supervisor who suspected the new hire of theft. The fingers behaved. The tea was fine. Everything was fine. They were alive, the well was dry, the Choirkeeper had screamed herself hoarse—or screamed herself into whatever silence came after hoarse—and the debt had been split down the middle like a loaf between sisters. Half a grave each. Half a song each. No whole soul for the house to eat.

It was only that sometimes, Ulyana was learning, the half of the song that had settled in them had opinions.

* * *

They spent the morning doing the small, dumb, glorious work of the living. They swept the kitchen. They opened every window in the house and let the thaw-smell blow through—wet earth, green things deciding, the far-off sweetness of the hills letting go of winter. They found Grandma's ledger on the desk where it had lain open to its last page, *one verse short*, and Ulyana closed it with both hands and put it in the bottom drawer and weighed the drawer shut with the good iron skillet, and the house made no objection, which Ulyana chose to take as agreement rather than exhaustion.

At noon they ate eggs and toast at the kitchen table, and it was the best meal of their life, and they cried a little into the toast, and that was fine too. Grief and relief, Ulyana was discovering, come from the same tap and you don't always get to choose which one pours.

It was while washing up that the hands did it the second time.

Ulyana was scrubbing a pan, thinking of nothing—blissful, miraculous nothing—when their hands stopped, rinsed themselves, set the pan in the rack, and then reached up and began braiding their own wet hair. Over, under, over, under, in the pattern Yuliya had worn hers every summer of their childhood, the pattern Ulyana had never once been able to manage because their fingers refused to learn it, because it was *Yuliya's* braid, the way her laugh was Yuliya's laugh.

The braid came out perfect.

Ulyana stood at the sink with wet hands and a perfect braid and looked at their reflection in the darkened window. Their own face looked back. Mostly. There was something in the set of the mouth that wasn't quite theirs—something softer at one corner, something that tilted, very slightly, the way a face tilts when it is listening to a joke the other person hasn't heard yet.

"Hi, Yuli," Ulyana said to the glass.

The mouth in the glass didn't answer. The mouth in the glass didn't need to. But the coldness in Ulyana's chest—the one that had been there since moonset, since the verse, since the well had convulsed and the Choirkeeper had screamed that a shared grave was not a grave at all—that coldness shifted. Not painfully. The way a sleeping person shifts. The way someone settles deeper into blankets to stay a while.

Ulyana put a hand flat against their own sternum, over the cold place.

"I know," they said. "I know. You're not gone. I didn't think you were. That wasn't the deal, was it? That was never going to be the deal."

The coldness pressed back, gently, like a forehead resting against theirs.

* * *

In the afternoon Ulyana walked down to Hale's cottage, because there was a debt of a different kind to settle, and because the living owed each other things the dead no longer could.

Hale's neighbor met them at the lane and took off his cap and said the old man had gone in the night, peaceful, in his chair, with the church bell rope still looped over one hand like he'd been holding it the way you hold someone's wrist to feel a pulse. The village was calling it his heart. Ulyana, who had watched him beg forgiveness with his last breath and then smile like a man setting down a sack he'd carried for sixty years, thought the village could call it what it liked. Hale had paid what he owed. He'd paid it in the only coin he'd had left, which was the truth.

They stood a while at his gate. The yard was full of neighbors, awkward and kind, and someone had set out chairs—more chairs than people, because that was what you did in this village, you always set the extra one, and for the first time in Ulyana's memory the extra chairs looked like habit instead of hunger.

"You'll stay on, then?" the neighbor asked. "At the house?"

"I don't know," Ulyana said, and found it was the truth. "I might. Somebody should keep an eye on the well."

"The well's dry, they say."

"Wells refill," Ulyana said. "That's what wells do. It's the whole job description."

The neighbor laughed, uncertain, and Ulyana smiled and walked home along the hill road as the light went long and gold, and if their left hand kept time against their thigh—*one-two-three, one-two-three*, the meter of a lullaby's last verse—well. They let it. You don't slap a hand for keeping time. You especially don't slap that particular hand.

* * *

Evening came down soft. Ulyana lit the stove and made soup and set the table, and here they paused, bowl in hand, looking at the two chairs and the one of themselves.

They got down a second bowl. Set a second place. Spoon, bread, the works. It felt ridiculous and it felt necessary and both of those things could be true at once; Ulyana was getting used to that.

"For the record," they said to the empty chair, "you're getting soup and you're not even going to eat it. This is a terrible arrangement and I want it noted."

The cold place in their chest warmed by one degree. Ulyana counted that as laughter.

They ate. They washed up—both bowls, and their hands did the second one with a tenderness that was not theirs and was not unwelcome. They banked the fire. And then, because there was one more thing, they took Grandma's ledger out of the drawer, moved the skillet, and sat with it at the table under the lamp.

The pages turned themselves to the last entry without being asked. *One verse short.* Ulyana clicked the pen Grandma had kept in the spine—ink still wet, ink always wet in this house—and beneath the old entry, in their own hand, in their own stubborn leftward-slanting script, they wrote:

*Verse paid in full. Two voices. One debt, split. The house ate nothing. Signed, U. Coldharbour—and other.*

The pen hesitated over the last word. Then Ulyana let their hand do what it wanted, and the hand added, in a script that was not quite theirs, rounder, left-handed, a small and careful:

*—and Y.*

The ledger closed itself. Politely, Ulyana thought. Almost shyly. Like a book that had been shouting for sixty years and had finally, finally been heard.

* * *

Ulyana slept that night without taping their mouth, which was either courage or stupidity and was probably both, and dreamed—not in harmony, for the first time, but in unison. One voice. Two singers. A song with no verse missing, sung all the way through to the end, and at the end of it, in the dream, there was no well and no dark and no chair, only two children on a hill in summer with grass stains on their knees, arguing about whose turn it was to be the one who rang the bell.

They woke at dawn with the braid still in, the cold place warm as a held stone, and the house ordinary around them, and they lay there a long time listening to the ordinary sounds—the birds, the wind, the pipes—memorizing them like a language they intended to keep.

Then they got up, and put the kettle on, and their hands set out two cups without being asked.

Ulyana looked at the second cup for a long moment. Then they poured it full, and carried both to the window, and set one on the sill facing the hills, where the light was coming up.

"Good morning," they said.

And from somewhere under the floorboards, or under the hills, or under the skin of the world itself—faint, patient, and unmistakably smiling—came the sound of a bell tolling backward, once, like something clearing its throat to begin again.
