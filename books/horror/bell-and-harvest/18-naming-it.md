## 18. Naming It

The kitchen had become the last warm room in the world, and even it was losing.

Emrys could see their breath now, pluming white in the light of the oil lamp Idris had found in the cellar — the electric lights had died an hour ago, all at once, the way a held breath is released. The windows were white. Not frosted: *filled*, packed to the frames with frost ferns grown so dense they overlapped like feathers on a dead bird's wing, and behind the ferns, in the dark of the yard and the fields beyond, the shapes stood waiting. They did not press at the glass. They didn't need to. They had all the time the night could give them, and the night was giving everything it had.

The bell had stopped tolling in intervals. Now it simply rang — one continuous note, high and thin and patient, like a wire singing in wind, like the sound a dying man's counting would make if you turned it into noise.

"Salt's gone," Wren said. They were kneeling at the door, and their voice had a strange flatness Emrys had learned, over one long terrible night, to read as fear. The line of salt across the threshold had gone grey, then translucent, then had simply ceased, grain by grain, as though the floor were drinking it. "The east line's gone. The kitchen line is — it's going."

"Then we go to the root cellar," Idris said. He was at the table with Maren's ledger open, the parish archives spread around it in loose pages, his collar askew, his hands steady in the way of a man who has decided that steadiness is a duty rather than a feeling. "Consecrated ground held at the property line, but the cellar stone was laid by the Ollerenshaws' grandfather, and he—"

"The cellar stone won't matter," Emrys said.

They hadn't meant to say it aloud. But it was true, and the truth had been building in them all night like pressure behind the eyes, and now that the frost had climbed the table legs and was crawling across the wood toward their hands, small white fingers of it, patient as bookkeeping — now there was no room left for anything untrue.

Idris looked up. "Explain."

"It's not trying to get *in*." Emrys turned their hand over. The frost touched their knuckle and did not burn; it *wrote*, a single curl of crystal blooming across the skin like a pen stroke. "It's been inside the whole time. Inside the house, inside the bargain, inside—" they looked at Wren, and made themselves finish, "—inside Wren. The salt, the mirrors, the thresholds — those were never walls. They were *terms*. Maren's terms. And the terms expired at sundown."

"Then what does it want?" Wren asked, and their voice cracked on *want*, because they knew, they had always known, they had confessed it hours ago in a voice like a door coming off its hinges — *I am not your friend, I am the remainder* — and Emrys had chosen, in that moment, to love them anyway, and had not yet decided whether that choice was courage or the trap working exactly as designed.

"It wants me to sign," Emrys said.

The frost on the window had been forming letters all night. Slowly. Deliberately. The way a solicitor writes. Maren Dunmore's signature had appeared on the pane above the sink at midnight, each letter a lacework of crystal, beautiful the way a spiderweb is beautiful, and beneath it a blank line had been waiting, and beneath the blank line, letter by letter, hour by hour, the frost had been writing *Emrys*.

The *E* was finished. The *m*. It was working on the *r*.

"You could sign," Idris said quietly. "I said it before and I'll say it again, because I promised you my honest counsel and I am apparently a man who keeps promises even when they cost me. One keeper. One corrupted soul, who knows what they are, feeding the thing on terms they control, rather than a hollow that feeds blind every generation. It is not a good choice. It may be the only arithmetic that ends in fewer graves."

"You said arithmetic," Emrys said. "Listen to yourself. That's what it sounds like. That's what it *always* sounds like. That's the whole trick."

The bell note climbed. The frost on the table reached their wrist.

"It came to Maren as mercy," Emrys went on, and their breath was visible, and the cold had reached their chest, and still the words came, because the words were the only thing in the house the cold couldn't take. "Her child was dying. Fever, nine years old, counting backwards under the floorboards — I've read the ledger, Idris, I've read my own name in it, *the boy-child, taken at nine unless* — and something in the dark offered her a trade, and she was a mother, and she said yes. And it let her believe, for fifty years, that she'd made a bargain. That there had been two sides. That there was a debt, and terms, and a ledger, and an inheritance — a whole *grammar* of fairness, so that she could live with it, so that she could die with it, so that she could pass it on like a spoon set."

"Emrys—" Wren started.

"It's not a bargain," Emrys said. "It never was. Bargains have two parties who both risk something. This thing risks *nothing*. It dressed itself up as mercy because mercy is the shape that opens doors. It let Maren sign because a signed paper makes the victim complicit, and complicit people don't ask questions, and villages full of complicit people keep their clocks running fast and their eyes on the road and their dead uncounted for sixty years." They were standing now, though they didn't remember standing, though the frost was at their elbow, writing its patient lace up their sleeve. "It offered me the same deal an hour ago. Name a substitute. Hold the ledger. Live. And do you know what it said, when I asked it what it was? It said, *a kindness, continued*. That's what it calls itself. A kindness."

The window above the sink finished the *r*.

"You cannot fight it with salt," Idris said. "Or prayer. I have prayed all night and I will tell you honestly, Emrys, I felt it the way you feel a letter you've mailed to a wrong address. It doesn't occupy the categories. It *made* the categories — bargain, debt, heirloom, keeper — and everything we do inside them, it eats. So unless you have something outside them—"

"I have its name," Emrys said.

Wren went very still. Not the stillness of hope. The stillness of someone watching a person walk to the edge of something.

"Not its true name — it doesn't have one, I don't think it ever did, I think it's older than names, I think names are one of the doors it uses. But I've spent my whole life listening to the dying, Wren. Twenty years in hospice wards, listening to people count backwards, and everyone told me it was a symptom, and I *documented* it, I wrote it down in little clinical notebooks because I couldn't bear to call it what it was — and do you know what I learned? The dying don't count because they're afraid of the number. They count because something is counting *with* them. And a thing that counts with the dying, that arrives as mercy, that trades one life for another and calls the trade kindness, that builds ledgers and terms and inheritances so that hunger can wear the costume of family forever—"

Emrys turned to face the window. The frost letters. The blank line. The shapes in the fields, motionless, wearing the faces of everyone the ledger had recorded — the Ollerenshaws' lost eldest among them, standing at the front of the crowd with cider still on his coat.

"I know what you are," Emrys said.

The bell note bent. The whole night bent with it, the way a room bends when a held breath finally moves.

"You are not a god and you are not a devil and you are not a debt. You are a hunger. Old and empty and alone in the dark before anything had names, and you learned — you *learned*, the way a crow learns, the way a lure-fish learns — that things come easier to you if you arrive as help. You found a dying child and a desperate mother and you dressed yourself as a bargain because a bargain has a signature, and a signature is a hook, and a hook is how you've fed since before this village had a bell. You didn't save me. You didn't save Maren. You *invested* in us. You are not mercy. You are hunger wearing mercy's face, and you have been wearing it so long you've forgotten there was ever a face under it — or maybe there never was, maybe there's nothing under there at all but appetite, and the ledger, and the counting, and the long patient wait for a family foolish enough to call you kind."

The frost on Emrys's sleeve had stopped growing.

It hung there, mid-letter, a crystal *r* unfinished on their own skin, and Emrys felt it — felt the hesitation, the way you feel a held breath in a dark room — and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was the first time in all its long hunger that anyone had said it out loud. Not banished it. Not prayed at it. Not bargained with it or fled it or inherited it. *Described* it. Truthfully. To its face.

"You keep accounts," Emrys said, softer now, walking toward the window, toward the letters, toward the line where their signature was half-written and waiting. "Because accounts can be inherited. You keep terms, because terms can be enforced. You keep a ledger because a ledger makes hunger look like bookkeeping and bookkeeping looks like order and order looks like *good*. But I have read your ledger, and I have read Maren's heart in it, and I have read mine, and here is what none of your columns ever held: it was never a bargain. It was never a debt. It is not an heirloom. I am not your heir. There is no line of Dunmores for you to feed down, because a line is a family, and a family is a thing you have only ever *imitated*, the way Wren was made to imitate a friend—"

"Don't," Wren whispered. "Emrys. If you finish the thought — if you name it all the way through — I don't know what's left. *I* don't know what's left. I'm made of the same—"

"I know," Emrys said, and did not turn around, because if they turned around they would see Wren's face and the words would die in their throat, and the words were the only weapon, and the night was nearly over, and love — real love, not the imitated kind — sometimes meant saying the thing that costs everything. "I'm sorry, Wren. Forgive me. Forgive me for what I have to do."

The frost letters on the window had begun, very slightly, to *tremble*.

"You are a hunger dressed as mercy," Emrys said to the glass, to the fields, to the bell's endless wire-singing note, to the thing behind all of it that had never once been called by its own true shape. "You are not owed. You are not invited. You are not family, and you never were, and every name you've ever worn — kindness, bargain, debt, heirloom, keeper, *friend* — was a costume, and I see the costume, and I am not afraid of you, and I do not accept your terms, and I never will, and neither will anyone after me, because there is no after you. You are hunger. You are hunger. You are *hunger*, and the night is ending, and I am not signing, and I am not counting, and I am not—"

The bell stopped.

Not faded. *Stopped*, mid-note, like a throat closed by a hand, and in the ringing silence that followed, every frost fern on every window in the house shivered at once — and then, all together, in perfect unison, they began to *unwrite* themselves. Letters dissolving. Signatures lifting off the glass like breath from a mirror. Maren Dunmore's name going first, then the blank line, then the half-formed *Emrys*, each crystal letter collapsing inward, backward, the way a dying man counts — thirty, twenty-nine, twenty-eight — the whole night's terrible bookkeeping reversing itself stroke by stroke.

And in the fields, the shapes in the faces of the counted dead flickered like candle flames in a draft.

And in the doorway behind Emrys, small and terrible and utterly quiet, Wren made a sound Emrys had last heard at age nine, on the morning the fever broke — the sound of something beginning, gently, to be unmade.

* * *

"Emrys." Idris's hand on their arm, gripping hard. "Emrys, look at the ledger."

The ledger lay open on the table where it had lain all night, and its pages were moving. Not turning — *emptying*. The columns of counted-down numbers were erasing themselves from the bottom up, each entry counting backwards toward its own beginning, names thinning to ghosts of ink, dates unwriting, and at the center of the last page Maren's final unfinished entry — the one that had stopped mid-number, mid-thought, sixty years of arithmetic ending in a blank — was filling.

Not with numbers.

With light. Thin, grey, pre-dawn light, the color of the hour that was coming, pouring into the ledger's last line like water into a footprint, and Emrys understood, with the last of the cold still on their skin, that they had not killed it. You couldn't kill hunger. You could only refuse to feed it — and it was unraveling backward through everything it had ever eaten, giving it all back, and it was taking its costumes with it.

The bell. The ledger. The terms. The bargain.

And Wren.

Wren was standing in the doorway with the first grey light of morning coming through the ruined window behind them, and the light was going *through* them — not around, through — and where it passed, Wren was thinner. Not dying. *Thinning*, the way a voice thins down an emptying hallway, and they were smiling, and the smile was the truest thing Emrys had ever seen, and Wren said:

"Emrys. I want to tell you what I am. All the way through. While I still have a mouth to say it with."
