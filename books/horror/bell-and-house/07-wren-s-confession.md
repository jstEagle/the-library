## 7. Wren's Confession

The note appeared in Jude's pigeonhole on a Tuesday, folded into a square so precise it looked machined. No name. Just an address—*The Anchor, back booth, after six*—and beneath it, in pencil worn nearly to the wood of the sentence: *Bring nothing. Say nothing first.*

Jude knew the handwriting. It matched the labels on the janitorial closet: *MOPS*, *KEYS*, *DO NOT MOVE AFTER DARK*, all in that same careful, pressed-down script, as though the writer were afraid the letters might get up and walk if he didn't hold them there.

Wren.

All week Jude had been carrying the negative like a coal in their breast pocket. They'd had it developed at a one-hour photo place two towns over, where a bored teenager slid the prints across the counter and said, "Weird paper," without looking up. The twelve faces came out sharp this time, unsmudged, and Jude had spent a long night with them spread across the kitchen table while the mirrors lagged and rippled and the small figures at the reflected table watched them watch the dead.

They needed Wren to say it out loud. Because if Wren said it—if the man who scrubbed the floors and oiled the hinges and told them not to move anything after dark admitted what was in that photograph—then it wasn't Jude's mind fraying at the edges. It was the world.

The Anchor sat low against the fog like something braced for weather. Inside it was warm and amber-lit and mostly empty, and Wren was already in the back booth, a bottle of whisky on the table between his hands—no glass—and two cups of coffee that steamed like offerings.

"You drink tea," he said, not looking up. "Saw you at the staff room urn. Two sugars, stir it too long." He pushed one cup across. "Whisky's mine."

Jude sat. The booth vinyl exhaled. Somewhere behind the bar a radio murmured a football score to no one.

"Mr. Wren—"

"Custodian. That's the job title now." He finally raised his eyes, and Jude saw what forty years of holding still could do to a face: it hadn't aged so much as settled, like silt. "You've got the picture. I can see the shape of it in your pocket. Take it out."

Jude laid the photographs on the table between them, face down, like cards about to be played.

Wren looked at the backs of them for a long moment. Then he unscrewed the whisky cap, poured a measure into the coffee cup, drank half of it in one motion, and said:

"I was their teacher."

* * *

He didn't cry. That was the first thing Jude noticed, and it unsettled them more than weeping would have. He talked the way men talk about machinery—with the emotion planed off, sanded down, functional.

"Room 4. September 1984 through—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "There isn't a *through*. That's the whole point. There's a before and there's an after and the middle is a Tuesday I have spent forty years trying to remember correctly and failing, because remembering it correctly is the one thing this town will not let me do."

"The fog," Jude said quietly. "The song."

Wren's hand flattened on the table—not slamming it, just pressing, as if to keep it from drifting somewhere. "You've heard it."

"A round. It stops mid-phrase. Mrs. Aldous hums it without knowing she's humming it."

"She'll know by now." Something moved behind his eyes, old and tired and terribly patient. "It gets into people who live alone. It likes a quiet house. A quiet head." He looked at Jude then, really looked, and Jude felt catalogued down to the joists. "And yours has been quiet a long time, hasn't it. I saw it your first week. You stand in doorways waiting for permission that nobody's going to give. You count things when you're nervous—I've watched you count the stair treads outside Room 4 eleven times. You make yourself small and orderly and easy to overlook." He took another pull of whisky. "That's not an insult. It's a diagnosis. And it's why the house picked you."

"It hasn't *picked* me."

"No?" Wren turned one of the photographs over. Twelve children smiled up from 1984, sunlit and smudged and impossibly alive. His thumb hovered over the smallest girl—a gap-toothed thing with her chin tipped up like she was mid-laugh—and didn't land. "Then why do your mirrors run late? Why did Mrs. Aldous stop answering her door? Why does Ilsa Vane draw you surrounded by children you've never met?" He said it flatly, without triumph. He wasn't winning an argument; he was reading a chart. "I'm not here to scare you, Pemberton. I'm here because somebody once sat across from me in this exact booth and didn't tell me enough, soon enough, and I've been paying the interest ever since."

Jude's throat was dry around the tea. "Tell me, then. All of it."

Wren nodded slowly, like a man agreeing to open a door he'd nailed shut decades ago. He poured again. Outside, the fog pressed its grey face flat against the window, listening.

"All right. But you're going to want to call me a liar, and I'd rather you saved it for the end."

* * *

Here is what Wren told Jude, and here is how he told it—because for a few paragraphs the story stops being Jude's to carry. Some stories are heavier than the person they happened to, and this was one of them, and Wren had been holding it alone since before Jude was born.

---

*I taught Year Six. Twenty-three pupils, though the register said eleven—the other twelve were the ones whose parents had already started forgetting, the ones the town kept off the books because keeping them on the books meant admitting they existed, and admitting they existed meant the house would notice them, and the house noticing anyone was the thing Hollowmere had organized itself to prevent.*

*You have to understand what the town is. It isn't a town with a secret. It's a treaty. Sometime in the last century—my grandfather's time, maybe earlier—the people of Hollowmere made an arrangement with the thing under Bell House, and the terms were simple: we don't look at you, you stay small. We don't sing your song, you stay hungry but contained. Forgetting wasn't our failure. Forgetting was the payment.*

*But songs get passed to children the way colds do. Unintentionally. In playgrounds. In rounds sung walking home through fog, four parts overlapping so no single voice carries the whole tune, which is how the town justified letting them sing it at all—each child only ever held a fragment, see, and a fragment is just a folk song.*

*A round needs an ending. Every round ends by coming back to the beginning—that's the shape of it, that's the law of it. Except this one. This one stopped. Mid-phrase, every time, like a clock with the last hour filed off. And the children, being children, found the gap delicious. They spent that whole autumn trying to finish it. Making up endings on the playground. Daring each other to sing past the break.*

*I should have stopped them. I told myself the fragments were harmless. I told myself the fog kept the sound close, that nothing carried. I told myself whatever a man tells himself when stopping the game means becoming the adult who ruins everything, and I was twenty-four years old and wanted, more than anything, to be the teacher they liked.*

*On the Tuesday—it was a Tuesday, I will swear to Tuesdays until I die—they came in from lunch wet with fog and glowing with conspiracy, and they asked me to sing with them. All of us together. The whole round, all four parts, the way they'd practiced.*

*I said yes.*

*God help me. I said yes because it was the happiest they'd been all term.*

*We sang it through once, and it broke off where it always broke off, and the room went strange—the light went thick, the way it does, syrup-light—and Tommy Ashdown, who was nine and fearless, grinned at me and sang the next line anyway. Made it up. Right there. An ending, offered freely, in a bright voice.*

*And the house said yes.*

*That's what nobody understands. The house doesn't take. The house* accepts. *It's a throat, not a trap. It opened, and the room became the inside of it, and my class—the whole twelve, the ones off the books, the ones the town had already paid forward—were simply elsewhere, mid-breath, mid-note, and the song finished itself in a register below hearing, and then it was afternoon, ordinary grey afternoon, and I was standing in front of eleven desks that should have held twenty-three children, and the chalk dust was still settling.*

*I ran to the office. I ran to the constable, the vicar, the publican. And I watched—this is the part I need you to hear, Pemberton, because it's the part I can't forgive—I watched the town choose. Within a day. Within hours. Faces going smooth as eggs. Mothers who'd birthed those twelve looking at me with polite blankness when I said their names. The archivist pulling the photo from the drawer and breathing on it herself, deliberately, smudging her own daughter's face with her own thumb, and saying, "What photo?"*

*Because the treaty said: if the house takes, we forget, and it stays small. If we remember, it grows. Remembering is an invitation. Grief is a doorway and they boarded theirs shut with twelve names they refused to write down.*

*And me? I remembered. I remember because I was the one who sang with them, and the house marked me—left me the memory like a receipt, like a debt instrument. Someone has to hold the account or the whole arrangement collapses, I think. Or maybe it's just cruelty. Maybe it's just the house wanting one witness to starve slowly alongside it.*

*I tried to go in after them. First week, I did everything wrong and everything right and none of it mattered—you can't enter a second that doesn't want you. So I learned instead. I learned the song. All of it. Every verse, note for note, from memory, because I was the only archive left that hadn't agreed to be emptied.*

*Eleven verses. I can sing eleven verses in my sleep, and sometimes I do, and wake my own self humming them.*

*But the twelfth verse—the true ending, the one Tommy improvised and the house accepted—that verse doesn't exist anymore. The house ate it along with him. And the song, the real song underneath, wants an ending. It's a door, and doors want to be shut, and the only thing that shuts this one is a final verse given willingly by someone the house has chosen. Sung aloud. Meant.*

*For forty years I have hummed around that absence. Around it, never into it. I leave the gap exactly the size it is, because filling it wrong is worse than leaving it empty. Fill it with fear and the door opens wider. Fill it with grief and the house feeds. Fill it with anything less than completely meant, and you've just knocked.*

*So I hum. I've hummed for forty years, around a hole shaped like twelve children, and every year the hole stays exactly the same size, and every year I get better at pretending that's mercy instead of cowardice.*

*That's my confession. That's the warning label, if you want to read it that way: I was the adult in the room, and I chose the happy moment over the careful one, and the careful one was the only one that mattered.*

---

The pub had gone quiet around them. Jude realized the radio had stopped, and that the bartender was polishing a glass that was already clean, and that both of them were angled very slightly toward the back booth, listening without appearing to listen.

Wren drained his cup, whisky and coffee dregs together, and set it down with a click that sounded final as a latch.

"So now you know the shape of it," he said, his voice back in the present tense, back in his own throat. "Song's a door. House is a throat. Last verse closes it—but only if the singer means it, and only if the house has chosen the singer, and only—" he paused, and his eyes went to Jude's breast pocket, where the photographs lay, and then up to Jude's face with something awful and gentle in them, "—only it seems the house has been waiting forty years for someone whose emptiness fits the lock."

"Don't," Jude said. Their voice came out smaller than they wanted.

"I'm not pushing you in. I'm telling you what's at the bottom before you lean over." He stood, dropped notes on the table without counting them, and shrugged into a coat that smelled of floor wax and machine oil. At the edge of the booth he stopped, and for the first time all evening his composure cracked—not into tears, but into something rawer, a man showing the wound itself rather than the bandage.

"They were good kids," he said. "Loud ones. Tommy Ashdown laughed like a door falling off its hinges. Merrin Vale could argue the legs off a chair. If you end up in front of them—in the glass, in the second, wherever they are—you tell them their teacher is sorry. Don't tell them he's sorry he sang. Tell them he's sorry he didn't sing the right thing back."

He walked out into the fog, and the fog closed behind him like water.

Jude sat a long while with the twelve faces on the table. Then, without deciding to, they began to hum—carefully, around the gap, the way Wren had shown them without showing them—and in the dark window of The Anchor, their reflection hummed too.

One second early.

* * *

Jude walked home fast, breath ragged, the photographs clutched inside their coat like contraband. The fog was thicker than it had been all autumn, thick enough to chew, and somewhere off to the left—too far left for any house, out where the lane gave up and the moor began—children were singing.

Not fragments tonight. Not a round breaking off mid-phrase.

Tonight they were singing in unison, all voices together, one line repeated over and over with the patience of tide:

*"Come and sit, come and sit, come and sit—"*

And threading beneath it, faint and unmistakable, a boy's laugh like a door falling off its hinges.

Bell House rose out of the fog ahead, every window lit from inside with a warm gold glow that Jude had not left on.

Through the front-room glass, clear as a photograph, Jude could see the kitchen table beyond—reflected deep, doubled, true—and seated around it, thirteen small figures with folded hands, and one empty chair pulled out at the head, waiting, its seat worn smooth as a river stone.

Above the door, hanging where no bell had hung that morning, was a brass schoolbell.

It rang once as Jude reached the gate.

Inside the house, softly, in a voice Jude recognized from a photograph, a child began to sing the first verse.
