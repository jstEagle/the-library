## 9. The Children Know My Name

The drawing appeared on Jude's desk on a Tuesday, which felt significant only because Tuesdays were the days Jude had begun to dread.

It was folded in half, then in half again, the creases sharp as if someone had pressed them flat with a thumbnail, and it sat in the exact center of the teacher's desk, aligned with the edge, the way Jude aligned everything. Jude stood in the doorway of Room 4 with a mug of tea going cold in one hand and looked at it for a long moment before crossing the room to pick it up.

Eleven students. Eleven desks occupied. And yet the paper had been there before the first bell, before Jude had climbed the hill from Bell House through fog that clung like wet wool, before the kettle, before anything. The custodian came at six. Wren did not leave drawings on desks. Jude had begun, without quite admitting it, to keep a mental ledger of everyone who entered the room and when, the way a person might count the exits in a building they didn't trust.

The ledger had no entry for this.

Jude unfolded it.

A face looked back at them. Their own face—there was no mistaking it, though the hand that drew it was small and the lines were confident in the way children's lines are, unbothered by accuracy, arriving at truth by a shorter road. Round glasses. The cowlick at the left temple that Jude fought every morning and lost. The slight downward turn of a mouth that had learned early not to expect good news.

Jude Pemberton, drawn by someone who had looked at them closely enough to know them.

And around the face, in a ring, twelve smaller figures. Twelve small bodies with small hands, holding hands with one another, a chain of them circling Jude's portrait like a wreath. Like a noose. Like a flower crown. Their faces were blank—not unfinished, not smudged, but *blank*, smooth ovals with no features at all, and somehow that was worse than any expression could have been. The only face in the drawing with eyes was Jude's. Twelve featureless children, holding hands, surrounding the one face that could see them.

At the bottom, in pencil, in a child's careful cursive: *your seat is ready.*

Jude set the paper down with the deliberate slowness of someone refusing to let their hands shake, and turned to face the classroom as the students began to filter in, stamping fog off their boots, unwinding scarves, arranging themselves into the ordinary noise of a school morning. Jude scanned their faces one by one. Eleven children. Jude knew them now—knew that Tom Ashby chewed his pencil to splinters, that the Ferrier twins communicated in a language of eyebrow movements, that Petra Osei read under her desk with a book propped inside her maths workbook like a secret drawer.

And Ilsa Vane, who sat in the third row by the window, who was small and watchful and quiet in a way Jude recognized from the inside. Ilsa, whose eyes went to the extra desks the way everyone's did and then, unlike everyone else's, stayed.

Ilsa slid into her seat and did not look at the teacher's desk. She did not look at the drawing. She looked out the window at the fog, and her hands were folded on her desk, and she was humming—barely, a thread of sound, two bars of a round that stopped mid-phrase.

Jude's tea had gone cold in their hand. They set it down on the drawing, covering the words, and began the register in a voice that came out steadier than they had any right to expect.

* * *

They kept the drawing in their satchel all day, folded back along its creases, and took it out four times—in the staff room, in the corridor, in the archive room where they'd begun to haunt the shelves, and once in the toilet, standing at the sink, where the mirror over the basin showed them holding it and did so, as far as Jude could tell, exactly on time. Small mercies. The lag had been holding steady at two seconds for a week now, and Jude had learned not to test it, the way you learn not to press a bruise.

At the final bell, when the room had emptied and the fog had thickened against the windows until the world outside was a gray suggestion, Ilsa Vane came back.

Jude heard her before they saw her—no, that wasn't right. Jude heard *nothing* before they saw her. The room's ordinary settling silence, the creak of the building, the tick of the clock over the door: all of it stopped, the way sound stops underwater, and then Ilsa was standing at the front desk with her coat already on and her bag already over her shoulder, as if she had not walked there but simply arrived, the way things arrived in this town.

"Miss Pemberton." She didn't call them *sir* or *miss* exactly; she said the whole name, both parts, like a word she'd practiced. "I drew you a picture."

"I know," Jude said. "It's lovely. The likeness is very—" They stopped. There was no way to finish that sentence that didn't sound insane. "Did you leave it on my desk this morning?"

Ilsa's brow creased, and for a moment she was just a nine-year-old confused by a teacher's question. "No. I drew it at home last night. They asked me to."

The clock over the door did not tick. Jude noticed that the way you notice a missing stair.

"Who asked you to, Ilsa?"

The girl's expression didn't change. That was the thing that would stay with Jude afterward, that would wake them at three in the morning with their heart going like a trapped bird: Ilsa's expression didn't change at all. No fear, no slyness, no child's delight in a trick. She said it the way she'd have said *it's raining* or *we had chips for tea.*

"The twelve. They said you'd come." She adjusted her bag strap with one small hand. "They've been saving your seat. The one at the front, with the initials on it. They said you'd know which one."

Jude knew which one. The front row, nearest the teacher's desk, there was a desk older than the others, its wood darkened, and carved into its corner in a child's uneven letters: *J.P.* Jude had run a thumb over it in their first week and thought nothing of it. Initials lasted in old schools the way ghosts lasted in old houses—every desk had some history, some long-gone child's mark. It had meant nothing. It had been a coin on a railway track, a cold room, a draft.

It meant everything now.

"Ilsa," Jude said, and crouched to bring their eyes level with hers, the way the training manuals said, the way felt right anyway. "Listen to me. Those twelve children—the ones in the drawing. Have you seen them? In the school, or at Bell House, or anywhere?"

"They're always at the house," Ilsa said. "That's where they practice."

"Practice what?"

"The song." She said it patiently, the way you'd explain something to someone who was being slow, and there was a kindness in her patience that broke something open in Jude's chest. "They practice the song so it'll be right when you learn it. They said you'd be the one to finish it. They've been waiting a really long time." She smiled then, a small, sweet, terrible smile. "They're excited. They don't get new people. You're the first in ages who isn't old."

The fog pressed against the windows. Somewhere in the building a door closed—Jude heard it, or heard the memory of it, or the building performed the sound for their benefit.

"Ilsa, I need you to do something for me." Jude kept their voice level, warm, a teacher's voice, while everything underneath it screamed. "I need you to stay away from Bell House. Can you do that? Don't go near it. Don't listen if you hear singing near it. If you hear singing anywhere, you come find me, or you find Wren—the caretaker—and you tell us. Promise me."

Ilsa considered this with the gravity of a child weighing a rule against its reason. "They said you'd say that," she said. "They said you'd worry. They said to tell you not to worry." She leaned in, and her voice dropped to a confiding whisper, and her breath smelled of peppermint and cold air. "They said you'd understand soon. Because you're quiet like them. Because the house has been listening to you since you were little."

The floor seemed to tilt. Jude gripped the edge of the desk.

"What do you mean, since I was little? What did they say about when I was little?"

But Ilsa was already stepping back, shrugging, a child released from a chore. "That's all they said. Can I go? My mum's waiting."

"Wait—" But she was at the door, and the door was open, and the fog beyond it was the color of nothing at all, and she turned back once, silhouetted, small and neat and utterly unafraid.

"They said to tell you the seat's the one with your letters on it," she said. "In case you forgot. They said you forget things. That's what you're for."

And she was gone into the gray, her footsteps fading on the path, and Jude stood alone in Room 4 with twenty-three desks and a drawing on the desk in front of them and the clock over the door still, still, still not ticking.

* * *

Jude went to the headmistress's office at eight the next morning, before the students arrived, because the thing Ilsa had said had kept them awake all night, turning over and over like a stone in a tumbler, wearing its edges smooth: *you forget things, that's what you're for.* Jude had rehearsed the conversation on the walk—calm, professional, a teacher reporting a concern about a pupil. *Ilsa Vane is hearing voices. Ilsa Vane has been told things she shouldn't know. Ilsa Vane knew about the initials on the desk, which I have never mentioned to anyone, which I did not know were significant until she told me they were.*

The corridor outside the office smelled of dust and old paper, the way it always did, but this morning the smell was stronger, as if something had been opened that had been shut a long time.

The door was ajar. Jude knocked anyway—habit, decency, the small rituals that kept a person feeling like a person—and pushed it open.

"Miss Thackery? I'd like to talk to you about one of my students."

The office was empty.

Not empty the way an office is empty when its occupant is at assembly or in the staff room or off sick. Empty the way a room is empty when it has been empty for a very long time. Dust lay on the desk in a smooth, unbroken gray felt, thick enough to write in, and nothing had written in it. No rings from cups. No fan of papers. No chair pulled out at an angle; the chair sat tucked in neat as a held breath. On the walls, paler rectangles marked where photographs or certificates had once hung, and the hooks that remained held nothing. A filing cabinet stood against the wall, and when Jude—heart tapping faster now, the rational part of them already generating explanations: *she's retired, they've moved her, this is a storage room now*—pulled the drawer, it slid out with a shriek of disuse and contained, in its entirety, a single yellowed exercise book and a dead spider curled in the corner like a comma.

The exercise book was blank. Every page. The cover bore a name half-erased by time and thumbing: *Room 4, 198—*, and then nothing.

Jude stood in the dust-smelling silence and felt the town rearrange itself around them, the way a picture rearranges when you realize it's a mirror. There was no headmistress. There had never been a headmistress. Jude had been *interviewed*—hadn't they? A woman with gray hair and a cardigan and a voice like a closing door. She had said *Room 4 has been between cohorts since 1984* in a tone too casual by half. Jude could see her face. Jude could see her face and could not, now that they tried, remember her name, or her hands, or anything she'd said beyond that one sentence, or how they'd gotten from the interview to the signing of the contract, or—

The contract. Where was the contract?

Their satchel, of course. Everything was in the satchel. Jude dug into it with hands that wanted to shake and found the folder, the papers, the letter of appointment—and the signature at the bottom was their own, and above it, where the headmistress's name should be, the line read only *Headmistress, Hollowmere Primary*, in the same font as everything else, as if the position were a title and no one had ever held it, as if the office had been vacant so long the paperwork had simply grown around the absence.

And there—because the room was not entirely empty—was the bell rope.

It hung in the corner by the window, where a school bell rope has no business hanging in an office, a thick braided cord descending from a small brass ceiling mount, ending in a worn wooden handle polished smooth by decades of hands. Smooth as river stones. Smooth as the extra desks. It hung perfectly still, and the fog outside the window was perfectly still, and Jude's own breath was the only moving thing in the room.

They did not touch it. Some instinct older than rationality said *do not touch it*, and Jude, who had spent a lifetime overriding their instincts in favor of explanations, obeyed this one without argument.

They backed out of the office, closed the door gently, and stood in the corridor listening to their own pulse. Then, because the alternative was to stand there until the fog came in through the walls, they walked—fast, then faster—to find Wren.

* * *

They found him in the boiler room, of course, in the warm dim underworld of the school where the pipes sang their one low note. He was doing something unnecessary to a valve with a wrench, and when Jude came down the stairs he didn't turn around, but his shoulders changed, the way a man's shoulders change when he's been waiting for something.

"They got to the Vane girl," Jude said. No preamble. The preamble had burned off somewhere between the office and the stairs. "Ilsa. She came to me with a drawing—my face and twelve others. She said they'd been saving my seat. She knew about the initials on the desk, Wren. She said the house has been listening to me since I was little. And there's no headmistress. There's no office. There's a bell rope in a room full of dust and a filing cabinet with one blank book in it and I have signed a contract with a ghost." Their voice cracked on the last word and they let it. "Tell me what's happening to her. Tell me what's happening to me."

Wren set down the wrench. He took his time about it, laying it on the workbench with the care of a man arranging his affairs, and when he turned, his face in the boiler light was old and gray and full of something that looked, horribly, like relief.

"Sit down, then," he said, and nodded at an upturned crate. "And I'll tell you what I should have told you the first day. What I should have told anyone, forty years back, if I'd had the guts." He wiped his hands on a rag that did nothing. "You're right that there's no headmistress. There's no governors, no board, no one above you. There hasn't been since '84. The town keeps the school running because the school has to keep running—because the room has to keep having a teacher in it." He looked at Jude with eyes that had been awake too long. "The house needs a teacher, Miss Pemberton. It's been holding the position open. And it's been holding it open for *you*."

The boiler sang. The pipes ticked. Somewhere far above them, faint through the floors, a bell rang the start of the school day—a bell Jude had never heard rung by anyone, in a tower Jude had never found, on a rope hanging in an office no one used.

Jude sat down on the crate, because their legs had made the decision for them.

"Start at the beginning," they said. "And don't hum around anything. Not this time."

Wren nodded slowly, like a man stepping off a ledge he'd been standing on for forty years, and reached for the bottle he kept behind the boiler, and began.

* * *

Jude did not go straight home. They walked the long way, past the church with its dead clock, past the shop that sold postcards of the fog as if the fog were a feature and not a condition, and it was full dark when they finally turned onto their own street and saw Bell House waiting with its windows lit from inside by a light Jude had not left on.

They stopped at the gate. The fog was thick tonight, standing in the lane like a congregation, and the house's windows glowed a soft, warm, wrong yellow, and in the upstairs window—the bathroom window, Mrs. Aldous's flat was the top floor, Mrs. Aldous had the top floor—there was a shape. A woman's shape, standing very still.

Mrs. Aldous. Home late, or up late, or—

The shape began to sway, gently, side to side, the way a person sways when they are singing to themselves. And though the window was closed and the fog was deep and the distance was thirty feet of cold air, Jude heard it—threading down through the glass and the gray, thin and sweet and patient, a round with no ending, verse after verse after verse, and the voice singing it was old, and cracked, and knew every word.

Jude's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the fog.

They were already running for the door.
