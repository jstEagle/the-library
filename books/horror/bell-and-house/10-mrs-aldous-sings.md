## 10. Mrs. Aldous Sings

The knock went unanswered for the third time.

Jude stood on the landing of number six, the shared stairwell smelling of boiled cabbage and old carpet, and listened to the silence behind Mrs. Aldous's door. It wasn't the ordinary silence of an empty flat. Empty flats had a texture to them—a hollowness, a draftiness, the acoustic signature of rooms with no one arranging themselves inside. This was different. This was the silence of someone standing very still.

"Mrs. Aldous? It's Jude. From upstairs."

Nothing. But beneath their feet, through the floorboards of Bell House across the way—no. Not through the floorboards. In their head, faint as a radio in another room, the round was playing again. The one with no ending. *The fog comes down and the light goes out and we—* and then nothing, the phrase amputated, the melody reaching toward a resolution it never arrived at.

Jude had been hearing it all week. On the walk home from school, in the fog that now came before dusk instead of after it. In the kettle's whistle, once, which they had decided was a coincidence because the alternative was not survivable before breakfast. And tonight, standing at their landlady's door, they heard it coming from *inside* the flat—but sung, actually sung, by a human voice, thin and clear and terribly fluent.

Mrs. Aldous did not hum. In three weeks of tenancy she had hummed nothing, whistled nothing, sung nothing. She spoke in the clipped cadences of a woman who had spent fifty years talking mostly to herself and had optimized for efficiency. She kept her curtains drawn against the fog and her opinions close against her chest. She was, Jude had privately catalogued, the least likely person in Hollowmere to sing anything.

The door was unlocked.

Jude told themselves later that they should have turned around then. That a locked door would have been a mercy, a boundary, a sign. But the handle turned without resistance, and the door swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, and the singing pulled at something in Jude's chest like a hook set behind the sternum, and they went in.

* * *

The hallway was dark. The flat's familiar geography asserted itself in shadow—the coat rack with its single macintosh, the umbrella stand, the framed sampler on the wall reading BLESS THIS HOUSE in Mrs. Aldous's own cross-stitch, which Jude had always found quietly funny given the town's relationship to blessings. The singing came from the bathroom, down the hall, where a strip of light lay along the floorboards like a spilled ribbon.

Jude walked toward it. Their feet knew they were walking too slowly, the way feet know when a body is afraid but the body hasn't admitted it yet.

"—and the fog comes down and the light goes out and we keep our places, keep our places—" 

Her voice was wrong. Not weak—Mrs. Aldous was seventy-eight if she was a day, and her voice should have been papery, wavering, an instrument past its prime. It wasn't. It was clear as a struck glass, steady as a metronome, and it carried the melody with a fluency that no one could have learned in an evening. She sang it the way you sing something you've known since childhood. The way you sing something you've been made to memorize.

Jude reached the bathroom doorway and stopped.

Mrs. Aldous stood facing the mirror above the sink, both hands braced on its edge, her spine straight, her head tilted at an angle of perfect attention. The single bulb above the mirror lit her face from directly overhead, carving her features into planes of light and hollow. Her eyes were open. They were bright—not wet, not feverish, bright the way glass is bright, the way a window is bright when there is nothing behind it to cast a shadow—and they were fixed on her reflection with an expression of such serene, total vacancy that Jude's body understood the wrongness before their mind could name it.

She was smiling. Mrs. Aldous did not smile. In three weeks Jude had extracted from her perhaps four expressions, two of them disapproval. Now her mouth curved upward in a smile of simple, childlike pleasure, and she sang, and the song poured out of her complete—all eleven verses, every line that Wren had hummed around and refused to finish, delivered without hesitation, without breathlessness, as though the song were singing itself through her and she was only the room it resonated in.

And behind her—in the mirror—her flat was empty.

That was the detail that broke something loose in Jude's chest. Because the mirror did not show what stood in front of it. The mirror showed the hallway, yes, the correct hallway, the coat rack and the sampler—but empty. No Jude in the doorway. No Mrs. Aldous at the sink. Just the dim corridor receding into darkness, still and vacant, the flat rendered as it might look years from now, or decades, abandoned and waiting. And in the mirror's version of the hallway, the wallpaper was peeling. The carpet was gone. The sampler hung crooked on bare plaster.

The reflection was showing the future. Or showing the truth. Jude couldn't decide which was worse.

"Mrs. Aldous." Their voice came out smaller than intended. "Edith. Can you hear me?"

She sang on. *"—and we keep our places, keep our places, till the bell rings us home—"*

Jude stepped into the bathroom. The tiles were cold through their socks—they'd run over without shoes, they realized, without coat, without thought, because the singing had started and some part of them had understood immediately, the way you understand a scream. The cold climbed their ankles. The air smelled of lavender soap and underneath it something older, mineral, like water that has sat too long in stone.

"Edith." They put a hand on her shoulder.

Her shoulder was warm. That undid Jude more than the eyes, more than the empty reflected hallway—she was warm, alive, a living woman under their palm, and she did not react at all. No flinch. No turn of the head. The hand on her shoulder might have been a coat draped there. She leaned very slightly into the touch, the way a cat leans, absent and trusting, and kept singing.

In the mirror, Jude watched their own hand rest on nothing. The reflected hallway stayed empty. Both of them had already been subtracted from this room, the glass said. This was just the part where the bodies caught up.

Jude gripped her arm above the elbow. "We're leaving. I'm sorry—I'm so sorry—we're leaving now."

They pulled.

She came away from the sink with a sound that was not a gasp and not a cry but something between—a note, actually, a held tone that slid out of the song and up into a register of pure distress, and for one moment her face convulsed and Jude saw *her*, the real Edith Aldous, surfacing from somewhere deep like a drowning woman breaking water, her eyes finding Jude's in the mirror, her mouth shaping a word—

*"—stay,"* she said. Or maybe *"away."* Or maybe the song, swallowing even her last free syllable.

Then they were in the hallway, dragging her, her slippers scuffing the floorboards, her body moving with a terrible pliancy, not resisting exactly but not helping either, the way sleepwalkers don't resist. Behind them the bathroom light burned on. In the dark of the hallway the sampler read BLESS THIS HOUSE, and Jude thought with a hysteria they'd examine later: *it didn't say whose.*

At the front door she stopped. Went rigid. Her hand shot out and caught the doorframe with a grip that startled Jude with its strength, seventy-eight years old and her fingers white-knuckled on the wood, and she turned her face to them and her eyes were still bright and empty and she said, in a voice gone suddenly young:

"It knows my verse now. It's got my verse. I gave it the one about my Thomas, about the summer he courted me under the yew, I gave it willing, I gave it glad—" Her grip tightened. "You have to give it yours, dear. It's been so hungry. Can't you hear how hungry?"

"Edith—"

"You'll want to stay," she whispered. "That's the trick of it. You'll *want* to."

Jude pried her fingers from the frame—one, two, three, each one a small battle—and pulled her over the threshold onto the landing, and the door swung shut behind them on its silent hinges, and the singing cut off mid-phrase.

The silence afterward rang.

* * *

Mrs. Aldous sat in Jude's kitchen at Bell House, wrapped in a quilt, drinking tea she did not drink. She held the cup. She looked at it. Steam rose past her chin and she made no move toward it, and her eyes had come back from wherever they'd been—mostly back, mostly—and they moved around the kitchen with the slow, systematic bewilderment of someone waking in a stranger's house.

"This isn't my house," she said, for the fourth time.

"No," Jude said, for the fourth time. "This is Bell House. You're my landlady. You live at number six, across the lane. You've lived there fifty years. Your husband built the garden wall."

"My Thomas built the garden wall," she agreed, in the tone of someone accepting a fact on faith. Then: "But I don't live there."

"You do."

"I don't think I do anymore, dear." She turned the cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "I think I gave it up. Tonight. Or—" her brow furrowed, a genuine effort of recall, like a woman searching drawers for something she suspects she sold long ago, "—or I gave up the part of me that lived there. There's a difference. I can feel the difference. There's a hole where the remembering goes."

Jude sat across from her and did not trust themselves to speak. The kitchen clock ticked. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like a face. And in the window glass—Jude checked, they couldn't help checking—the kitchen showed only the kitchen, only the two of them, only the ticking clock and the untouched tea. One second late, maybe. Maybe two. But empty otherwise. For now.

"The song," Jude said carefully. "Where did you learn it?"

"I didn't learn it." She said it simply, without drama. "It learned me. It's been learning me for fifty years, a line here, a line there, whenever I was quiet enough. Whenever the house was quiet enough." She looked up, and her eyes were wet now, finally, humanly wet, and Jude could have wept with relief at the sight of ordinary tears. "It takes the lonely ones first. Did Wren tell you that? He'd know. He's been lonely longest."

"He told me some of it."

"He'd leave out the worst." She folded her hands on the quilt. "The worst is that it's kind, while it's taking you. While you're in it. There's a place it shows you—warm, full of people who know your name—and you think, oh, *there* I am, that's where I was always meant to be, why did I ever live anywhere else? And you go willingly. Gladly. I went gladly, dear. Fifty years I kept my curtains shut and my mouth shut and I thought I was safe, and tonight the house across the lane started humming my verse back to me, and I just—" she spread her hands, helpless, "—I wanted to answer. You always want to answer. Nobody wants to be left out of the singing."

Jude's skin crawled. Because they knew. God help them, they knew—the shameful thrill from chapter upon chapter of their own nights, the metronome lagging, the twelve figures at the table with their hands folded, the moment they had spoken to the glass and felt the ripple answer like a greeting. Part of them wanted to sit down. Had wanted it for weeks.

"Your flat," Jude said. "In the mirror. It looked—empty. Old. Like nobody had lived there for years."

"That's what it looks like when it's finished with you." Mrs. Aldous said it without self-pity, which was somehow worse. "It shows you the room after. So you understand what's being offered isn't staying. It's being *kept*."

The clock ticked. Somewhere in the walls—or in the fog, or in the space behind the mirrors, which might be the same place—the round began again, very faint, very patient. *The fog comes down and the light goes out and we—*

Mrs. Aldous's head lifted toward the sound like a flower toward sun. Her hands tightened on the quilt.

"Don't listen," Jude said sharply, and stood, and did the only thing they could think of, the thing that had been building in them all week like pressure behind a dam: they began to talk. Loudly. About nothing. About the boiler, about the leaking gutter, about the appalling state of the staffroom biscuits, about Ilsa Vane's drawing and the way she'd pronounced *Pemberton* with the accent falling wrong, about anything, everything, filling the kitchen with the mundane noise of a life being lived in daylight, and slowly, slowly, Mrs. Aldous's shoulders came down from around her ears, and her eyes found Jude's face and stayed there, anchored, and the faint singing in the walls faded as though embarrassed.

"You'll need somewhere to sleep tonight," Jude said. "Here. Not across the lane. Here, where I can hear you."

"And if I start singing in the night?" she asked. Honest. Terrified. Brave with the particular bravery of the old, who have run out of illusions but not out of mornings.

"Then I'll wake you," Jude said. "However many times it takes."

She slept eventually, in the guest room that had been Jude's study, with the wardrobe mirror covered by a bedsheet Jude pinned up with shaking hands. Jude sat in the kitchen until three in the morning, tea going cold, watching the window where their reflection watched them back, one second adrift, keeping its distance.

And it was there—at three in the morning, in the cold and the fog and the exhaustion—that the thought arrived, fully formed, wearing the voice of common sense:

*Ilsa Vane drew twelve faces around yours. She said they've been saving your seat.*

*The house doesn't take the lonely first because they're easiest.*

*It takes them first because it's practicing. Because it's setting the table.*

*Because the seat it's really saving is mine—and now it's started on my students.*

Jude looked at the dark window, at the fog beyond it, at the shape of the lane where number six stood empty and reflected-empty, and understood with total clarity that whatever came next would not wait politely for the weekend.

In the morning, they were going to find Wren. And this time, Wren was going to tell them everything—including the last line he'd spent forty years refusing to learn.
