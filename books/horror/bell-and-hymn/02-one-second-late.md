## 2. One Second Late

The lag announced itself on the third morning, in the hallway mirror by the stairs.

Mirabel had been photographing the house for two days straight — every room, every drawer, every face of every relative who told her to rest. Her phone held four hundred and eleven images, timestamped, backed up twice. Documentation was how she kept the world from lying to her. If it existed in the record, it existed. If the record contradicted the family, the record won.

So when she raised the phone toward the hallway mirror and the reflection raised its hand a beat after her own, she did not scream. She took the picture.

She stood in the hallway with her arm raised, watching the glass, and counted. *One-Mississippi.* The mirrored arm came up. *Two-Mississippi.* The mirrored phone appeared in the mirrored hand. The hallway behind her reflection — the same wallpaper, the same coat rack, the same door to the parlor — was empty. Then, in the glass only, Miriam walked through it.

Not a trick of grief. Not a smear of light. Miriam, in the gray cardigan she'd worn the last time they'd video-called, crossing the parlor doorway in the reflection, pausing — *looking* — and moving on, the way you move through a room you know is being watched.

The real parlor doorway stood open behind Mirabel. Nothing crossed it.

Her hands shook so badly the second photo blurred. The third didn't. She made herself take eleven more, adjusting angles, and in seven of them Miriam was there, always a half-step behind the present tense, always in rooms Mirabel had just left.

*One second,* she wrote in the notebook she'd started keeping. *The mirror is one second late. She's living in the second we already spent.*

* * *

She told them at dinner, because she was done being the crazy twin.

The house was full in a way it hadn't been in years — the funeral had pulled the scattered cousins out of their cities like a stone dropped in a pond. Dele, who managed a restaurant in Houston and had Miriam's laugh. Auntie Ngozi, who'd driven eleven hours from Atlanta and kept touching Mirabel's shoulder as if checking she was solid. And Ruth, the grandmother's caretaker, a small, steady woman of about sixty who had worked in the Okafor house for thirty years and who alone had not once told Mirabel she was grieving badly.

Mirabel laid her phone on the table and played the video she'd taken that afternoon: the kitchen mirror, the lag, and there — unmistakable, in the glass only — Miriam, filling a glass of water she never got to drink.

The silence afterward had texture. Dele looked at his plate. Auntie Ngozi's hand found her crucifix.

"She's in the lag," Mirabel said. "The mirrors aren't showing us. They're showing *after*. And she's in the after."

"That's not possible," Auntie Ngozi said gently.

"Neither is a stranger wearing my sister's face in her casket," Mirabel said, "but here we all are, planning to bury her tomorrow."

It was Ruth who spoke. Ruth, who never spoke at these dinners, who moved through the house like weather.

"The founders' papers are in the attic," she said. "Mrs. Okafor told me once, when she was clearer. She said if anyone ever started seeing the lag, they should be told: *the papers are in the attic, and the attic is not finished with anyone.*"

"What does that mean?" Dele asked.

"I don't know," Ruth said. "But I've cleaned this house thirty years, and I've never once cleaned the attic. There's always been a reason I couldn't say out loud."

* * *

The attic stairs were behind a door in the upstairs corridor that Mirabel had walked past a hundred times as a child without ever once seeing. That was the thing about the house she was beginning to understand: it didn't hide things. It just declined to be looked at.

She went up with her phone light on and her notebook in her pocket, and Dele came with her because he said if his cousin was going to be haunted he was going to be haunted too, and Ruth came because Ruth had decided, thirty years into silence, that she was done not looking.

The attic was cold and full of the founders. Not the family — the *founders*, the four names on the deed Mirabel had found in the parlor desk: Okafor, Adeyemi, Lindqvist, and a fourth name that appeared everywhere in the papers and nowhere in any family memory. The boxes were labeled in a hand that changed four times across the decades, and the papers inside were not ledgers or letters but *records* — observations, measurements, testimony.

*Mirror lag observed: 0.3 seconds, year two.*

*Mirror lag observed: 0.7 seconds, year nine. Subject A. reports seeing her deceased husband "keeping pace" in the glass. Subject A. made peace. Subject A.'s name no longer appears in parish records.*

*Year eleven. We understand now what it trades. We are drafting the binding.*

And there it was, in a folder tied with rotted ribbon: *THE BINDING OF THE GLASS, being a ritual to close what was opened.* Pages of instructions in three different hands, amended and argued over across years. Salt from the house's own ground. Names spoken into each mirror in reverse order of their taking. A burning. A sealing.

And then the pages simply stopped.

The last page was in a shakier hand — the fourth founder, the one with no family memory. The instructions continued halfway down and then the handwriting deteriorated mid-sentence, the pen dragging a long dark line across the paper as if the hand holding it had failed all at once.

The sentence ended: *and the seventh mirror, being the one that shows the house itself, must be sealed by —*

Nothing. The line ran off the edge of the page. The founder had died — or been taken, or been *kept* — with the binding half-spoken.

"It's unfinished," Dele said. He'd gone pale. "The whole thing. They left it half-done."

"Then we finish it," Mirabel said.

She heard herself say it and was surprised by how little it sounded like hope and how much it sounded like a decision. She photographed every page. She photographed the unfinished line. She made Ruth read the fourth founder's name aloud, and when Ruth said it — *Adaeze* — the attic's single bulb flickered, and somewhere below them a mirror cracked with a sound like a knuckle.

* * *

The house began punishing them that night, and it punished them for looking.

It started small. Dele's reflection in the bathroom mirror turned its head before he did, just once, just enough. Auntie Ngozi found every photograph of Miriam in the family albums face-down by morning, and when she turned them up, they were turned down again by dinner. Ruth woke to find her thirty years of service — her pay stubs, her letters, her photographs — gone from her room, as if the house were erasing her the way it had erased Subject A., the way it had erased everyone who ever *made peace* with the glass.

"They take your name," Mirabel said, standing in the kitchen at 3 a.m. with Ruth, who was holding what remained of her documents in a shoebox like a life raft. "That's the trade. That's what the papers mean. You survive in this house by letting it keep you. Grandmother —"

She stopped. Because she'd been avoiding it for two days, and the attic papers had made it unavoidable.

Her grandmother had lived in this house for sixty years. Her grandmother was still *here* — lucid some hours, fogged others, and Mirabel had assumed the fog was age. But the papers described the fog. *The kept grow quiet. The kept forget in the order they were loved.*

Mirabel climbed the stairs to her grandmother's room and found her awake, sitting up, looking at the mirror on the dressing table with an expression Mirabel had never seen on her: recognition. Not confusion. *Recognition.*

"You found the papers," her grandmother said. It wasn't a question.

"Grandma. What did you sign?"

Her grandmother turned from the mirror, and her eyes were wet and terribly clear.

"In 1979, I signed a paper that let me keep this house and keep my life, and it cost me your grandfather's name, and my sister's face, and the first eight years of your mother's childhood, which I remember as a hole shaped like a hole." Her voice didn't shake. It was past shaking. "I have been paying monthly, Mirabel. For forty-five years. And yesterday a courier came to the door with a new document, because I'm dying, and the house wants the debt made permanent before I go."

"What document?"

Her grandmother reached into her nightstand drawer and laid it on the blanket. A deed. A fresh signature line. A pen placed beside it with a care that looked, in the lamplight, almost tender.

"They call it the transfer," she said. "I call it what it is. If I sign, everything the house has taken stays taken. Forever. Everyone in the glass stays in the glass." She looked at her granddaughter, and in her face Mirabel saw the woman from the photographs, the one who'd had eight years of a daughter and a sister's face and a husband's name. "And if I don't sign, child, I don't know what it does to me. I only know I've been too afraid to find out for six days now, and the pen hasn't left that drawer, and the house has started *waiting*."

Down the hall, in the dark bathroom, the mirror showed the corridor a second behind. And in that lagged corridor, standing where Mirabel had stood an hour before, Miriam raised her hand — not walking past this time, not keeping pace, but pressing her palm flat against the inside of the glass.

Mirabel pressed her hand to the mirror. The glass was warm.

Under Miriam's palm, in the condensation of her breath on the far side of the world, words were being written, one letter at a time, in handwriting Mirabel had known since they were nine years old and sharing a bedroom:

*S E V E N T H*
