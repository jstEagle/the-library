## 1. The Road That Forgets Its Name

The bus to Ashen Bell ran once a day, and it was not so much a bus as a disagreement between two ideas of a vehicle—a mail van that had been given seats the way a man is given a coat two sizes too large, and a schedule that existed mostly as a rumor. Lior Crane had it to himself, which suited him. He had the whole back bench, his field case balanced on the seat beside him like a traveling companion he trusted more than most, and eleven miles of country unfolding past the window that no folklorist of any standing had properly documented since before the war.

He was, he admitted to himself with a private and not entirely charitable pleasure, probably the first person to arrive in Ashen Bell *on purpose* in a decade.

The road climbed out of the river valley in long switchbacks, and with each turn the world behind him grew smaller and more theoretical. By the fourth bend, the last farmstead had folded itself into the haze. By the sixth, the radio in the driver's cab—playing something thin and orchestral, the kind of signal that had traveled a long way to die—crackled once and gave up entirely. The driver reached over, switched it off without comment, and drove on in a silence so complete that Lior could hear the specific note of the tires on gravel changing as the road surface changed.

He took out his notebook. He always took out his notebook; it was less a habit than a reflex, the way other men touched a crucifix or checked a watch. The cover was soft with handling, its spine a white scar of old creases, and he opened it to a fresh page with the small ceremony he allowed himself at the start of every field expedition.

*Ashen Bell, arrival, October the 9th,* he wrote. Then, beneath it, in the compressed shorthand he'd developed over nine years and four monographs: *Approach: 11 mi. single road, no through traffic. Radio loss at mile 6 (terrain? transmitter?). No signage for settlement itself—village name absent from last three markers.*

That last detail had pleased him from the start. Every other hamlet in the county announced itself with a painted board and a population figure, the way a shopkeeper announces his hours. Ashen Bell did not. The road simply stopped being a road to somewhere and became a road that was already there, and the village assembled itself out of the mist the way a photograph develops: first the roofs, then the chimneys, then, last and most reluctantly, the details.

He counted forty-one chimneys. He did not know yet that he would remember the number, or why.

"First time?" the driver asked, catching his eye in the mirror. The man had the flattened vowels of the valley towns and the particular wariness of someone who made this drive daily and had made peace with it the way one makes peace with weather.

"First visit, yes. I'm here to record the songs."

Something happened in the driver's face. It was not large. A stillness moved through it, the way a shadow moves across a field when a bird passes over—nothing changes, and everything changes. "Songs," the driver repeated.

"The harvest singing. The bell traditions. The county archive has fragments—fragments, really, third-hand transcriptions from the 1890s—but nobody's done proper fieldwork. Nobody's recorded the actual voices." Lior said this with the mild enthusiasm he had learned was useful with locals, enthusiasm that signaled *I am harmless, I am a scholar, I am interested in you* and concealed, beneath its warmth, the cooler truth: *I am interested in what you are, the way one is interested in a specimen.* He was not proud of the concealment, exactly, but he was not ashamed of it either. Interest was interest. The songs deserved to outlive the mouths that sang them, and the mouths, he had found, were usually flattered to be asked.

"They sing," the driver said. It was not agreement. It was the way a man might say *it rains*.

"Once a year? At the harvest?"

"Once," said the driver, and pulled the handbrake, and the conversation was over as cleanly as if a door had been shut between them.

* * *

The village had one street, and the street had the quality of a held breath.

Lior noticed it the way he noticed everything, cataloguing as he stepped down from the bus and stood in the road while the mail van turned around with the economy of long practice and ground away the way it had come. The street was wide—wider than a village of this size needed—and the houses stood back from it with their front gardens neat and their gates shut. Forty-one houses. He counted again without meaning to, a habit of mind he thought of as his ledger. Forty-one, and not one of them had a name board. Not one had a gate left open. Curtains hung straight. A bicycle leaned against a wall with its tires flat, as if it had been leaning there a long time and would lean there longer.

And the quiet. That was the thing he wrote about first, sitting that evening in the room above the smithy, because it was the thing that had struck him hardest and the thing he least understood. It was not silence. He had recorded silence in a dozen places—the salt marshes, the disused quarry at Wrenlow, the anechoic chamber at the university, which was not silence at all but the sound of your own blood insisting on itself. This was different. This was a village of perhaps a hundred and fifty people on a midweek morning, and there were no dogs. That was the first absence he logged. Every village he had ever entered had announced him with dogs, a ragged chorus of territorial outrage. Ashen Bell had none, and the absence of their absence was somehow louder than barking would have been.

No dogs. No children in the street, though it was barely past four and the light still had hours left in it. No radio through any window. No one at the well, and there was a well, old and hooded, at the crook of the street, with a bucket that looked recently used. No one at the churchyard gate, though the church itself stood at the head of the village like a punctuation mark, and beside it—of course—the bell tower, low and squat, more a stone drum than a spire, with louvers that were shuttered from the inside.

He wrote: *Bell tower sealed. Shuttered louvers—unusual. Village name absent from signage. No dogs (remarkable). Population visibly present (smoke from 30+ chimneys, washing at two houses, a man repairing a wall who watched me the full length of the street without appearing to move) but audibly absent. The village is inhabited the way a stage is inhabited between acts.*

He was pleased with that last line. He underlined it.

The man repairing the wall was the only person who acknowledged him at all, and the acknowledgment consisted of a nod so brief it might have been a muscle spasm. Lior nodded back with his best fieldworker's smile—the one that had opened a hundred farmhouse doors—and received, in return, nothing. Not hostility. That was what unsettled him, though he declined to be unsettled, and converted the feeling into data instead. Hostility he knew how to work with; hostility was a door with a latch. This was a wall with no door at all. The man simply looked at him, and then looked at the wall, and the trowel resumed its small wet sound, and Lior walked on with the distinct and absurd impression that he had been *measured*.

*Measured for what?* he wrote, and then, annoyed at his own drift toward the melodramatic, crossed it out and wrote instead: *Initial hypothesis: insular community, standard protective response to outsider. Compare: Hollowmere (1936), the Fen settlements (1941). Expect thaw in 2–3 days.*

He was a rationalist the way some men are tall—it was simply his proportions, the frame everything else hung on. He did not believe in the things his subjects believed; he believed *in* them, in their shape and function and beauty, the way an architect believes in a cathedral without believing a single word said under its roof. Nine years of fieldwork had taught him that every haunted village was a village with a history it couldn't afford, every ghost a grievance wearing a sheet, every strange custom a fossil of some practical arrangement long since forgotten. He had never met a mystery he couldn't file. He expected to file this one inside a fortnight and be on the train south with two hours of tape and a monograph in embryo.

The bell tower bothered him, he admitted. Not the shuttered louvers—parishes sealed towers against birds and thieves all the time—but the fact that the tower had no bell rope visible at the ground floor door, and the door itself had no handle on the outside. He stood before it a moment, notebook open, and noticed the stone above the lintel, where parish churches usually carried a date or a dedication. This one carried something else: a long, shallow groove, worn smooth, running the width of the stone, as if generations of hands had drawn one finger along it in passing. A touching-stone. He'd seen them at saints' tombs. He'd never seen one set where no saint, no image, no inscription gave anyone a reason to touch.

He reached up and ran his own finger along it. The stone was cold in a way that October air didn't explain, and smoother than it should have been, and for a half second—no longer, he was sure of the duration, he was a precise man—he had the sensation of having put his hand into a room where something had just stopped speaking.

Then a door opened down the street, and the ordinary sounds of a village resumed around him like water closing over a dropped stone, and he laughed at himself, a short embarrassed breath, and wrote: *Suggestion, auto-suggestion, cold stone. Note for methodology chapter: researcher's first hour in an insular community is a period of heightened suggestibility. Guard against it.*

He did not write down that the groove in the stone had been warm at its center.

* * *

He asked three people for lodging before he understood that he was asking in the wrong grammar.

The first was a woman sweeping a step that did not need sweeping. She looked at him with the flat, patient attention of someone waiting for a train to pass, and said, "You'll want the smithy," and went back to her sweeping, and the sweep of the broom did not change rhythm once while he stood there.

The second was an old man in a chair set out against a wall, positioned with a view of the street, and he said, "You'll want the smithy," in exactly the same tone, with exactly the same words, and Lior, who had transcribed a thousand dialects, noticed that the intonation matched too—not the same sentence, the same *utterance*, replayed. He wrote that down with a small thrill of the collector: a fixed formula of refusal, possibly ritual, possibly apotropaic. *Villagers decline direct engagement with strangers via standardized deflection. Compare the "not from here" formulas of the coastal villages.* He was delighted. This was exactly the sort of thing his monographs were made of.

The smithy stood at the far end of the street, past the well, and it was the only building in Ashen Bell that looked like it was doing anything. Smoke stood up from its chimney in a straight grey column. From within came the sound—the only sound in the whole village—of a hammer on iron, unhurried, evenly spaced, like a clock that had decided to be a blacksmith instead.

The woman who opened the door was perhaps forty, with dark hair going grey at the temples in the way that grief goes grey, and eyes that took his measure in a single pass and did not like the arithmetic. Behind her, half-hidden in the stair shadow, was a girl of eight or so, watching him with an openness that was somehow more unnerving than the village's closed faces.

"You'll be the one asking about songs," the woman said.

"I'm Lior Crane. I'm a folklorist—from the university. I've written to the parish council—"

"I know. Sorrel showed us the letter." She said the name *Sorrel* the way one names a dog one doesn't trust. "Merrin Hale. You'll be wanting a room."

"If it's no trouble—"

"It's trouble." She said it without heat, as a fact, and then stood aside to let him in. "It's trouble, and we'll take it, because every other door in this village will tell you *you'll want the smithy* until you're standing in the road at dark, and I won't have that on my conscience. Two nights. The room above the forge. You'll eat at our table. And you'll not ask Iva questions."

"I'm a very quiet lodger," Lior said, which was a lie so reflexive he barely registered it. He was a lodger like a lit lamp is quiet.

Merrin Hale looked at him for a long moment, and something crossed her face that he would later—much later, when he had learned the village's true grammar—understand to have been a kind of pity. At the time he read it as the natural reserve of a widow toward a stranger, and filed it accordingly, and got it wrong.

"The room's through here," she said. "Mind the salt on the step. It's there for a reason, and the reason isn't you, but you'll not sweep it up all the same."

*Salt on the threshold,* he wrote that night, sitting on the narrow bed with his notebook on his knees and the forge-cool seeping up through the floorboards. *Hale household: salt line at door (renewed, not residual), iron nails driven outward through the window frames—a reversal of the usual inward practice, interesting—dried rowan over the lintel. The widow's protective measures are textbook and total. She has fortified her house against something specific.*

He paused, pen hovering. He was aware of the sentence forming in him, the sentence a lesser scholar would have written—*against evil spirits, against the old fears*—and he was pleased with himself for not writing it, because he was better than that, because he had spent nine years learning that the fears were never the point; the fears were the *symptom*, and somewhere under every salt line was a reason with a human face.

*Against what?* he wrote instead, and underlined it twice, and felt the pleasant quickening in his chest that he had long ago accepted was the true engine of his profession—not scholarship, not preservation, but the hunter's pleasure of a track begun.

Below him the hammer had stopped. The forge had gone dark. And in the silence of his first night in Ashen Bell, Lior Crane lay awake for a while listening to a village that did not make a single sound—not a dog, not a drunk, not a shutter in the wind—and told himself, with the smugness that was his armor and would prove, in the end, to be his one true weakness, that he had it all but filed already.

He did not hear, in the last minute before sleep, the far-off and impossible sound of the shuttered bell tower settling on its stones, the way a throat settles before speech.

But the girl on the floor below did. She lay in her bed with her eyes open and counted, under her breath, the way her mother had taught her: forty-one houses, forty names, and one that walks at counting time.
