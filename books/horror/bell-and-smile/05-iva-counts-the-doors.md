## 5. Iva Counts the Doors

Iva Hale knew how to count before she knew her letters, because in Ashen Bell counting was the first thing you learned and the last thing you were allowed to forget.

Mama had taught her on the stairs, one step for each number, up to forty-one. *Forty-one houses in the hollow,* Mama said. *Count them every morning like brushing your teeth.* So Iva counted them from the bedroom window while the mist was still sitting in the lanes like spilled milk: the smithy where they lived, the Widow Petch's crooked cottage with its leaning chimney, the long low house of Tam Voss with his smell of beer and pipe smoke, the deacon's white house at the top of the hill that everyone pretended wasn't a house because it was the church's shadow. She counted them all, touching each one with her eyes the way Mama touched her hair at night, and she always ended at forty-one, and she always said it twice, because some things you said once and some things you said twice and Iva was eight years old but she knew which was which better than most grown people did.

The stranger had been in their house three nights now. He slept in the room above the forge where Papa used to sleep, back when there was a Papa and not just a coat on a hook that Mama couldn't bring herself to burn. His name was Lior Crane and he had a machine that ate sounds. Iva had watched him feed it a reel of brown tape the way you'd feed a worm to a fish, and he had smiled at her when he caught her looking, and she had smiled back, because Mama said be polite, but politeness was different from trusting and Iva understood that too.

This morning he was sitting at their kitchen table with his notebook open, writing in that fast slanted hand of his, and Mama was out at the well, and Iva decided it was time someone told him about the doors.

She climbed onto the chair across from him. He looked up. His eyes were the color of rainwater left in a bucket — pale, and a little surprised, like everything in this village surprised him even though he'd come here on purpose.

"Do you want to know something?" Iva asked.

"I want to know everything," Mr. Crane said. "That's my whole job."

Iva nodded. That was a good answer. Most people in Ashen Bell wanted to know as little as possible, and they held their knowing like water cupped in two hands, careful not to spill any of it on anyone else.

"There are forty-one houses," she said.

"Yes." He turned to a fresh page. "I counted them myself yesterday. Forty-one dwellings, by my reckoning."

"But there's only forty names."

His pen stopped. "On what?"

"The roll. In the church. Deacon Sorrel keeps it in a box with a lock, but I've seen it. Mama cleans the church on Thursdays and I go with her and I look at things while she scrubs." Iva folded her hands on the table the way the deacon did when he was being serious. "It's a list of everybody. Every family. Every person who belongs. And I counted the names, and there are forty, and there are forty-one houses, so somebody doesn't have a name."

Mr. Crane sat very still. Grown-ups did that sometimes when you told them true things; it was like their insides needed a moment to catch up with their outsides.

"Perhaps you miscounted," he said gently. "Lists can be confusing. There might be a name written small, or two names on one line—"

"I don't miscount," Iva said. "Counting is the one thing I'm good at. Mama says so. Mama says I count like my papa counted, and my papa never got a number wrong in his life." She heard how that came out and looked down at her hands. The coat on the hook creaked a little in the draft from under the door, the way it did sometimes, and neither of them looked at it.

"Who is missing, then?" Mr. Crane asked. His voice had gone quiet, the way voices went in church. "Which household has no name?"

"That's the thing." Iva leaned across the table. Up close he smelled of ink and train smoke and something else underneath, something like cold stone, and she wondered if he noticed it himself or if it only clung to outsiders. "There isn't a gap. That's what's wrong with it. If a house had no name, there'd be a hole in the list, an empty line, and an empty line would make sense. But there isn't a hole. The names all fit together like a fence with no missing rail. It's like the list thinks there are only forty houses." She frowned, working at it. "Like the list forgot one on purpose."

"And which house does the list think isn't there?"

"Ours," Iva said. "Mostly. Some days it's the Petches'. It moves around. But it's ours more than anyone's, since Papa went."

Mr. Crane's pen had made a small blot on the page, a round black star. "Your father's name isn't on the roll?"

"Papa's name was taken off." Iva said it the way you'd say *the milk's gone sour* — a fact, flat and finished. "After the tolling. Deacon Sorrel scraped it off with a knife. I saw the place where it was. The paper's thinner there, like a scar. You can hold it up to the light and see the ghost of the letters if you squint." She had done this. Many times. She liked the ghost letters; they were like Papa waving.

"Why would he scrape it off?"

"So it wouldn't get counted again," Iva said, and then, because he was an outsider and outsiders didn't know anything, she explained it the way Mama had explained it to her, sitting on the edge of the bed with the candle between them. "When the bell tolls, it counts. Not houses. Names. One name gets called and whoever the name belongs to has to walk to the church, and after that they're not on the roll anymore, and their house is the one without a name until the next tolling picks a different one. That's why we have forty-one houses and forty names. There's always one walking."

"Walking where?" Mr. Crane asked. His voice came out strange, thin at the edges.

"At counting time," Iva said. "The missing name walks at counting time. That's what Granny Voss used to say before she stopped saying anything. *Mind the door at counting time, the missing name walks.*" She swung her feet under the chair, heels drumming softly against the wood. "Papa walked. Mama heard him go past the window. She didn't look. You're not supposed to look. Looking makes it worse, Mama says. Looking means you saw, and seeing means you might be seen back."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, a cart rattled somewhere far off, and the forge below ticked as the banked coals settled, and beneath all of it, so faint you could pretend it wasn't there, the bell in the church tower hummed to itself the way a struck glass hums after the sound should have stopped.

Mr. Crane wrote something. Iva watched the pen move. Then he looked up and did the thing grown men did when they thought children were being fanciful — he smiled kindly, and it made his face older and sadder at the same time.

"Iva," he said, "your mother taught you those stories to keep you safe, yes? Sometimes people make rules into stories so children remember them. Don't swim alone becomes a river monster. Don't wander at night becomes a walking ghost. It's a very old trick, and it works very well. But it doesn't mean the ghost is real."

Iva considered this. She liked Mr. Crane. He talked to her like she was a person, which almost nobody did except Mama. But he was wrong in a way that was going to hurt him, and she found she didn't want it to hurt him, which was strange, because she'd only known him three days.

"It's not a story," she said. "Stories end. This doesn't end. It just goes around." She held up her fingers and counted them, one to ten, and started over. "Like that. Stories stop. Counting doesn't."

He laughed — a short breath through his nose, not unkind. "Where did you learn to talk like that?"

"Mama reads me the ledger," Iva said. "Not the church ledger. Our own one. Every family keeps one. Ours goes back further than the church floor. It's just numbers mostly. Dates and tollings and how many. Two hundred years of how many." She slid off the chair and went to the dresser, standing on tiptoe to reach the third drawer, the one Mama thought she didn't know about, and brought back a book wrapped in oilcloth. It was heavy and soft-cornered and it smelled of tallow and dust. She laid it on the table in front of him. "You can look. But don't read it out loud. Never read it out loud."

Mr. Crane unwrapped the oilcloth slowly, the way you'd unwrap something sleeping. Inside was a ledger bound in leather gone dark as chestnuts, its spine stitched with thread that might once have been red. He opened it. Iva watched his eyes move down the columns and saw the moment they stopped moving.

"What is this?" he whispered.

"Tollings," Iva said. "Every fifty years. Look at the dates. They're all the same distance apart, like fence posts." She pointed with one finger, tapping the entries. "That one. And that one. And that one. Always fifty years. Always one name. Except—" she tapped harder, "—here. And here. Twice, there's two names instead of one. Mama says those were the times somebody tried to cheat. Somebody hid, or ran, or gave the wrong name. And the bell took two to balance it out." She looked up at him. "The bell doesn't like being lied to. It counts till it comes out right."

Mr. Crane had gone the color of the paper in the ledger. His hand was resting flat on the page as if to hold the numbers down.

"When is the next date?" he asked.

Iva counted on her fingers, lips moving. She was very good at this part. "Autumn," she said. "Soon. After the harvest verse gets sung. That's how it knows it's time. The song is like a knock on a door, Mama says. Sing it once, and it knocks. Sing it twice—" she stopped herself, pressed her lips together hard, because that was one of the things you didn't say all the way through, even to polite strangers with machines that ate sounds.

"Sing it twice, what?" Mr. Crane asked.

But Iva shook her head and took the ledger back, wrapping it in its oilcloth with quick practiced turns, and put it away in the drawer, and pushed the drawer shut with her hip.

"You'll find out," she said. "Everybody finds out. That's the trouble with this village. Nobody tells you the whole of anything, so everybody finds out the hard way, one piece at a time, and by the time you've got all the pieces it's too late to do anything but stand very still and hope your name is thick enough on the paper."

She climbed back onto her chair. Mr. Crane was staring at the drawer as if it might open on its own.

"Iva," he said at last, "does your mother know you showed me this?"

"She'll know tonight," Iva said. "The ledger always tells her. It's heavier when it's been seen. She can feel it in her arms when she lifts it down." She shrugged. "Don't worry. She won't be angry at you. She'll be angry at herself, for letting you stay long enough to see."

"I should speak with her."

"You will. She'll probably tell you to leave." Iva studied his face — the rainwater eyes, the ink stains on his fingers, the way he kept glancing at his machine sitting in its leather case by the wall like a patient dog. "Will you?"

"Will I what?"

"Leave. When she tells you."

Mr. Crane opened his mouth. Iva waited. She was good at waiting; counting taught you that too, that some numbers take longer than others.

"I haven't finished my work," he said finally.

Iva nodded, unsurprised. Grown-ups always said things like that, work and purpose and important studies, as if the bell cared about anybody's work. She hopped down from the chair and went to the window to count the morning's houses, one through forty-one, touching each roof with her eyes, saving theirs for last the way you saved the biggest bite.

Behind her, the pen scratched. The machine sat in its case. Under the door, the salt line Mama had poured lay neat and unbroken, and outside, far up the hill, the bell hummed its held note to nobody, patient as arithmetic.

"One more thing," Iva said, without turning around. "About the missing name. You asked where it walks."

"I did."

"It walks wherever the counting is loudest," Iva said. "Mama says that. So if you ever hear the bell humming louder than usual — if it gets inside your ears and won't come out — you stand behind a named door and you don't hum along. Whatever tune it gives you, you don't hum along." She turned then, and looked at him straight, the way Mama looked at the doctor, level and unblinking. "Because the second voice always starts as a hum. And it likes best the ones who answer."

Merrin came back up the lane with the yoke creaking across her shoulders and two full pails swinging, and she knew before she reached her own gate that something had been given away.

She felt it in her arms first — no, that was later, that was when she lifted the ledger down that night and it pulled at her palms like a living thing, heavier than it had been since the day they scraped Edmund's name off the church roll. What she felt first was simpler and worse: the quality of the silence around her house had changed. For three days the stranger's presence had sat on the cottage like weather, a pressure she'd learned to bear the way you bear a toothache. Now the pressure had a direction. It pointed inward. Something had been let in.

She found them at the kitchen table, the folklorist bent over his notebook writing fast, and Iva sitting opposite with her ankles crossed and her hands folded, wearing the particular stillness of a child who has delivered a message and is waiting to see whether it lands.

Merrin set down the pails harder than she meant to.

"Iva. Go and see if Widow Petch needs anything fetched."

"Yes, Mama." Iva slid down, and at the door she paused and looked back at Lior Crane with an expression Merrin knew too well — Edmund's expression, the look he'd worn the last morning, calm and sorry and already halfway elsewhere. Then the door closed behind her, and mother and stranger were alone with the ticking forge and the salt line and the ledger in its drawer, heavier now by exactly one pair of eyes.

"Mr. Crane." Merrin kept her voice level. She had practiced level for two years; grief had taught her that, if nothing else. "What did my daughter show you?"

He hesitated. She watched him decide whether to lie, watched the lie assemble itself behind his rainwater eyes, and watched it fail — not because he was honest, but because he was a scholar, and scholars could not bear to hide a finding.

"She showed me your family ledger," he said. "And she told me about the roll. Forty names, forty-one houses. She told me the missing name walks at counting time." He turned the notebook toward her, and there, in his fast slanted hand, was her daughter's knowledge copied out in ink: *the bell counts names, not bodies; the ledger marks the fiftieth year; two names taken in the cheating years; the song is a knock.*

Merrin sat down. Her knees did it without asking her.

"You transcribed it," she said. "You wrote it down."

"That's what I do. I record. I preserve. Oral traditions die when no one writes them down, Mrs. Hale — that's not destruction, that's rescue—"

"Rescue." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "Mr. Crane, do you know what happens to a thing like that when it's written down? Do you know what happens to a spoken thing when you pin it to paper?" She leaned forward. "It stops belonging to the mouths that carried it. It belongs to the page. And pages can be *read*. Read aloud. Read twice. Read by anyone, anywhere, forever." Her hands had closed on the edge of the table. "Everything in this village that survives, survives by being unsayable. By living in one mouth at a time and dying with it. You've taken the pieces my daughter gave you and made them portable."

"I don't understand what harm—"

"No," Merrin said. "You don't. That's the whole of you, Mr. Crane. You don't understand, and you write it down anyway." She stood, and her voice dropped low, which was worse than shouting, and she knew it. "Edmund sang the harvest verse at Tam Voss's table, once, the night before the last tolling, and Tam asked him — begged him — not to ask for the second. And Edmund laughed, because he was like you, he liked knowing things, he liked the shape of a riddle more than the safety of ignorance. And the next autumn the bell rang, and the roll had his name ready, scraped clean off by spring." She was shaking now, and let it show, because maybe showing would reach him where words hadn't. "I buried a husband and I raised a daughter who counts doors like other children count sheep, and I have spent two years keeping this house sealed like a jar, and you have been here four days and my child has already handed you the jar's lid."

The forge ticked below them. Somewhere up the hill, faint as a pulse under skin, the bell went on humming its note that never quite finished.

"Mrs. Hale," Lior Crane said quietly, "the ledger your daughter showed me has dates in it going back two hundred years. Regular as clockwork. Whatever this is — whatever your village believes — there is a *pattern* there. Patterns mean causes. Causes can be found, and found things can be—"

"Don't say fixed." Merrin's voice cracked on it. "Everyone who ever tried to fix it is a number in that book. There's a column for them. Iva can show you. She showed you everything else."

He flinched. Good, she thought. Let it land. Let at least one thing land before the autumn takes whatever it takes.

She crossed to the dresser and drew out the third drawer and lifted the ledger down, and it pulled at her arms like a drowned thing coming up from deep water, heavy with having been seen, and she set it on the table between them with a thump that shook the salt dish.

"Read it, then, since reading is your trade. Read it silently, with your eyes, and understand what you're holding. And then, Mr. Crane—" she leaned close, and the bell's hum seemed to lean with her, filling the pause like water fills a footprint, "—you will pack your machine and your reels and your notebook, and you will take the morning bus, and you will not ask anyone in this village for a single verse ever again. Because I am telling you what I should have told you the first night, over supper, before I let your pretty manners through my door:

"Hearing is how it chooses. My husband heard the second voice one week before the bell took him. And last night, Mr. Crane — last night I passed your room on the stairs, and I heard your recorder turning, and I heard you talking to it in the dark. Asking it questions." Her eyes held his, and she saw the blood leave his face, and she knew. "And I heard something pause, and then answer."
