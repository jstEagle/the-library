## 14. Iva Is Named

Merrin Hale found the roll on the doorstep at first light, lying flat as a communion wafer against the stone, weighted at its corners with four small river pebbles that had no business being there. The nearest water was the mill stream, a quarter mile down the hollow road, and the pebbles were wet.

She stood in the doorway with her shawl clutched at her throat and did not touch it for a long moment. She knew what it was before she knew what it was. Every woman in Ashen Bell learned the shape of the church roll the way they learned the shape of a coffin—by hearsay, mostly, and by the way other people went quiet around it. Vellum gone soft as cloth with age. The deacon's careful hand marching down the margin in columns of names. Forty names. Forty-one houses. And now, at the bottom of the last column, in ink so fresh it still had a faint shine to it, like a snail's track:

*Iva Hale.*

Not *Iva*. Not a child's diminutive. The full name, spelled out whole and formal, the way it would be read aloud. The way it would be called across a churchyard when there was no one left to answer to it.

Merrin looked up and down the lane. Mist sat in the hollows between the houses like milk poured into a bowl. Somewhere a dog was barking—not the frantic bark of alarm but the low, rhythmic complaint of an animal that has smelled something wrong and doesn't know what to do about it. No one else was out. Of course no one else was out. In Ashen Bell you did not come to your neighbor's door at dawn unless you were invited or summoned, and everyone in the village could smell a summons.

She picked up the roll by its edge, the way you'd pick up something dead, and carried it inside.

* * *

The fire was already lit—Iva rose early these days, though she'd never say why, and Merrin had stopped asking. Her daughter sat cross-legged on the hearthstone feeding twigs to the flames with the concentration of a surgeon, and when Merrin came in holding the vellum at arm's length, Iva looked up and her face did something terrible: it didn't change at all.

"I saw them put it there," she said.

Merrin's arms went cold from the shoulder down. "Saw who."

"The mouth man." Iva said it the way she'd say *the postman*. "He walked up the path on his knees. He doesn't have any legs, Mam, just more mouths where his feet should be, and they all talk at once but it sounds like one voice if you don't listen properly." She turned back to the fire. "I drew him. It's under my bed. You said not to show people my drawings anymore."

"I did say that." Merrin's voice came out steady, which surprised her. Inside, something was screaming in a register she hadn't known she owned. "Iva. Look at me."

Iva looked at her. Eight years old, her father's dark eyes, her mother's stubborn chin, and around all of it the particular stillness of a child who has decided that being frightened is a luxury the household cannot afford. Merrin had seen that stillness before, in women standing over fresh graves, in men the morning after a tolling. It should not have been available to a child. It was available to her daughter because her daughter lived here, in this village, under this bell, and children in Ashen Bell grew the way trees grow on a cliff face—crooked, clinging, older than their rings.

"Nothing is going to take you," Merrin said. "Do you hear me? Nothing. Not a roll, not a bell, not a—" she almost said *deacon*, "—not anything. Your name is written nowhere that matters."

"It's written there," Iva said, nodding at the vellum. "Ink's still wet."

The ink was still wet. Merrin held the roll closer to the firelight and watched the letters glisten, and understood, with the flat certainty of a woman who has already buried one husband to arithmetic, that the ink would stay wet until someone answered it or refused it. That was how these things worked. They were patient the way debt is patient.

She fed the roll into the fire.

It caught slowly—vellum always does, it wants to curl and resist—and then all at once, and the flame went a color Merrin had never seen from any ordinary burning. Green, at the edges. A pale marsh-light green, like foxfire, like the glow off standing water in a place where things drowned. The smoke did not rise. It pooled along the ceiling and slid toward the corners of the room, and for one moment—one long, ringing moment—the smoke formed letters. Merrin read them upside down and backwards and understood them anyway:

*RECEIVED.*

Then the smoke was only smoke, and the roll was ash, and Iva was watching her mother with those dark eyes, and outside the dog had stopped barking entirely, which was worse.

"Mam," Iva said quietly. "You're smiling."

Merrin put her hand to her face. She was. Her mouth was curved in a small, pleasant, hospitable smile, the smile you give a caller at the door, and she had not told it to be there.

"Am I," she said, and pressed her lips together until the smile died, and began, with great care, to rake the ashes apart so that nothing of the roll remained even as powder.

* * *

By noon she had been to see three neighbors and gotten three versions of the same silence.

Widow Cetch opened her door two inches and said, through the gap, "Burnt it, did you," in a voice like a woman reporting weather. "That's what Edith did, in 'sixty-one. Burnt hers. Came back by Thursday." Then she closed the door, gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom.

Old Tam Voss wasn't home—or rather, Tam was home, because his cart was in the yard and his coat was on its nail, but he didn't answer, and through the window Merrin saw him sitting in the corner chair facing the wall, very still, a full jug of cider untouched on the table beside him. She had heard, from Lior Crane of all people, what Tam had done—sung the second verse, sung it twice, given the bell a gift it hadn't asked for. The village had not yet decided whether Tam was a dead man walking or a walking dead man, and apparently neither had Tam.

At the smithy she found young Piet shoeing a mare and got the most words out of anyone: "Roll comes to a house, house is counted. Roll burns, count stands anyway. Only thing that's ever moved a name off that vellum is a tolling or a keeper, and we've had no keepers since my gran's gran." He wouldn't look at her while he said it. Horses, it turned out, were easier to look at. "Deacon'll tell you the same, if you can get him to stop polishing the brass long enough."

"And if I go to the deacon?"

Piet finally looked up. He was maybe twenty-five and already had the smith's habit of speaking in short strokes, hammer-blows of words. "Then you'll have asked the man who keeps the ledger whether the ledger's fair. And he'll say yes. And you'll know what yes costs in this village." He bent back to the mare's hoof. "Mrs. Hale. Take the folklorist and the girl and go. Before the frost. Everyone knows the road won't let you after the frost."

"The road let nobody leave," Merrin said. "Ask me how I know."

But Piet had stopped listening, or made himself stop, and the hammer took up its rhythm, and she walked home through streets that watched her with their curtained windows.

* * *

Lior Crane was in his room above theirs when she returned, and she could hear him talking.

This was not unusual anymore—that was the horror of it, how quickly it had become usual—but today the cadence was wrong. He spoke in questions; he always spoke in questions now, even in his sleep, especially in his sleep. But underneath his voice, woven through it like a second thread in cloth, was another voice answering, and the answers had his rhythm too, his pauses, his little scholarly hesitations, as if the thing below the floor of the world had studied him the way he studied ballads.

She stood on the landing with her hand raised to knock and did not knock. Through the door she heard him say, clear and bright with that terrible triumph he mistook for courage: "Say it again. Slower. I want every syllable."

And the other voice said—she couldn't make out the words, only the shape of them, warm and obliging and utterly without mercy—

—and Merrin Hale turned around and went downstairs, because there was nothing she could do about the folklorist, not yet, not tonight, but there was one thing in this world she could do something about, and it was downstairs drawing pictures of a legless man made of mouths, and its name had been written in wet ink on vellum that burned green.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in the kitchen with the salt line renewed at every door and the nails checked at every window—outward-facing, all of them, driven in when the house was built by hands that knew—and she kept the fire fed high and the poker in reach, and at some hour past midnight Iva padded down in her nightgown and climbed into her lap without a word, and they sat together watching the flames like two soldiers watching a border.

"Mam," Iva said, near dawn. "If the roll comes back, will you burn it again?"

"Yes."

"What if it comes back a hundred times?"

"Then I'll burn it a hundred times, and the hundred-and-first too."

Iva considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "The mouth man said you'd get tired."

Merrin's arms tightened around her daughter hard enough that Iva squeaked. "What else did the mouth man say?"

"That tired isn't a feeling here. It's a door." Iva yawned, a huge, boneless, eight-year-old yawn, and Merrin felt the awful ordinary weight of her, the living warmth of her, the skull beneath the hair still soft at the seams. "He talks funny. Like sums. Everything's a sum with him. He said—" Iva's eyes were closing, "—he said forty-one minus one is forty, and forty is a good number, and it doesn't matter which one gets subtracted, only that somebody does the subtracting politely."

"Politely," Merrin repeated.

"With a smile," Iva murmured, and slept.

Merrin sat with her sleeping daughter and her poker and her fire, and outside, far off across the fields, the church bell moved once in the wind—a single note, soft, almost apologetic, the sound a bell makes clearing its throat—and she did not sleep at all.

* * *

Morning came gray and thin. Iva woke and went to feed the cat, and Merrin followed her to the door because she had decided, somewhere in the black hours, that she would be the one to open it, that no door in this house would open ahead of her again.

The roll lay on the step.

Unburnt. Whole. The vellum unblemished, the columns crisp, the deacon's hand immaculate—and at the bottom of the last column, *Iva Hale*, the ink dry now, settled, permanent as a scar. And beneath the name, in a smaller hand, in ink that shimmered faintly green in the dawn light, a line had been added overnight, a clerk's annotation, courteous as a receipt:

*Received in good order. Thank you for your prompt attention. — The Office of the Count.*

Merrin stood on her own doorstep with the roll in her hands and the cold coming up through the stone into her bare feet, and she did not scream, though screaming was right there, available, crowding her throat. Instead she smiled. She felt it happen—the same pleasant, hospitable curve of the mouth, arriving uninvited, wearing her face like a borrowed coat—and this time she understood what it was. It wasn't hers. It was the house's smile, the village's smile, the ledger's smile, offered through her the way a debt collector offers condolences. *Everything in order. Nothing personal. Someone must do the subtracting politely.*

"No," Merrin Hale said aloud, to the roll, to the mist, to the bell hanging silent in its tower across the fields, to whatever knelt on its mouth-feet in the spaces between houses. "No. You do not get her. You took Ed Hale and I signed nothing and I said nothing and I have regretted my silence every day since, so hear this instead: I am done being polite. Burn this, steal this, return it a thousand times—I will stand on this step every morning of my life and refuse it, and I will teach my daughter to refuse it, and when she has a daughter she'll refuse it too, and your tidy sum will never balance, do you understand me? Never."

The mist did not answer. But down the lane, every door of every house opened at once—all forty-one, softly, in unison, like a congregation rising—and behind each threshold stood a villager, pale in the gray light, looking at her, and every one of them was smiling.

Every one except old Tam Voss, at the end of the lane, who was crying.

And behind Merrin, from upstairs, from the folklorist's room, came the sound of Lior Crane's voice asking a question into his recorder—and the second voice answering—and then, unmistakably, both voices together saying, in perfect unison, in tones of dreadful courtesy:

*"Your objection has been noted."*
