## 2. Counting Doors

The house smelled the way grief smells: dust and lavender and something underneath both, older, like the inside of a piano that hasn't been opened in years. Casper stood in the front hall with two duffel bags and a cardboard box of studio equipment, listening to the silence settle around him like snow.

He had not been inside since the funeral. Eleven months, three weeks, four days — he'd stopped counting after the fourth day because counting was supposed to be Rosa's job, not his.

"Okay," he said aloud, to no one, to the hallway, to the dark at the end of it. "Okay."

His voice came back smaller than he'd sent it out.

The drive up from the city had taken four hours through rain that came down in ropes, wipers slapping like a metronome set to panic. He hadn't planned on coming. He'd planned on deadbolting his apartment, sleeping with the TV on, maybe calling in sick for a week until whatever was happening to his show burned itself out or got cancelled. But then Tuesday night, caller number six — a man named Douglas Peretti who had died in a single-car accident on Route 9 and spoke about it with the serene boredom of a man describing a dentist appointment — had said, *You should go home, Casper. She kept a place for you.*

*She* meaning Rosa. And *home* meaning this house, this crooked two-story with its wraparound porch and its windows like closed eyes, forty minutes from the nearest town that had a name anyone outside the county would recognize.

So here he was. Running toward the one place on earth he associated with being small and safe, because everything else had stopped being either.

The lights were the first thing he dealt with. Every bulb in the house — he went room to room with a bag of sixty-watts he'd bought at a gas station two towns back, screwing them in, flipping switches, until the whole place glowed a warm, defiant yellow. Kitchen: lit. Hallway: lit. The stairs, the landing, both bedrooms, the bathroom with its water-stained ceiling, the little sewing room where Rosa used to mend things nobody wore anymore. Even the cellar door got a bulb, though he left that switch off. Some rooms you don't light on the first night. Some rooms you negotiate with.

Rosa had always kept the lights burning. He remembered that now, standing in the kitchen where she'd taught him to make bread when he was seven, flour to his elbows, her hands over his guiding the fold. She never turned anything off. Not the porch light, not the nightlight in the hall, not the little lamp above the stove that hummed like an insect. As a kid he'd thought she was just old-fashioned, afraid of wasting nothing, or maybe just forgetful. Now, with a dead woman's voice still ringing in his ears from three nights ago — *I looked nice, didn't I? In the casket. Everyone said so* — forgetfulness felt like a luxury the living could afford and the dead could not.

He made coffee he didn't drink. He unpacked half of one duffel bag. He called his mother, got her voicemail, said, "Hey, Ma. I'm at Grandma's. Just for a while. Call me back," and hung up before the beep finished, because if she picked up she would ask questions and he did not have answers, only a voice memo on his phone of a broadcast he couldn't bring himself to delete and couldn't bear to play again.

By eleven o'clock the rain had stopped and the house had gone quiet in the particular way old houses do — not silent but *settled*, timbers ticking as they cooled, pipes knocking somewhere in the walls like polite knuckles. Casper sat on the edge of Rosa's bed — his bed now, apparently, because he couldn't face the guest room and couldn't explain why even to himself — and stared at the ceiling.

That was when he remembered the counting.

It came back to him all at once, the way childhood things do: complete, vivid, smelling faintly of lavender. Summer nights, eight years old, thunderstorms rattling the windows, and Rosa sitting on the edge of the bed with her reading glasses pushed up into her gray hair, taking his hand and saying, *Now we count the doors, mijo. Out loud. All of them.*

*Why?* he'd asked, every time, because he was eight and eight-year-olds ask why.

*Because a house likes to be acknowledged,* she'd say. *And because while we're counting, nothing else can come in.* Then she'd lift her chin, close her eyes, and begin, slow and deliberate, like a priest reciting: *"One — the front door. Two — the back door. Three — the cellar door..."*

Eleven doors. There had always been eleven. Front, back, cellar, pantry, the coat closet, the closet in Rosa's room, the closet in the guest room, the bathroom, the linen cupboard under the stairs, the little door to the crawl space off the sewing room, and the narrow painted door at the end of the upstairs hall that led to nothing — a wall behind it, Rosa said, just a wall, but a door is a door and doors get counted.

Eleven. He'd fallen asleep to that number more times than he could remember. It had worked like a lullaby, like a lock turning. *While we're counting, nothing else can come in.*

Casper swung his legs off the bed. His heart was doing something stupid and fast, and he told himself it was just the coffee, the drive, the year he'd had. He told himself he was going to do the ritual because it was comforting, a way of saying goodnight to Rosa, nothing more. He told himself a lot of things standing in the upstairs hallway in his socks with the warm light pooling around him, and none of them helped.

"One," he said, and his voice cracked on it, so he cleared his throat and started over. "One. The front door."

He walked as he counted, because Rosa had walked, touching each doorframe lightly with two fingers like she was greeting an old friend.

"Two. The back door." Down the stairs, through the kitchen that smelled faintly of bread even now, thirty years later, or maybe he was imagining that, wanting it.

"Three. The cellar door." He did not open it. He touched the frame and moved on quickly, the way you pass a dog you don't know.

"Four. The pantry." Its hinges sighed.

"Five. The coat closet." Umbrellas leaned inside like patient mourners.

"Six. My closet." He almost said *Rosa's closet*. His hand hesitated on the frame a half-second too long.

"Seven. The guest room closet."

"Eight. The bathroom."

"Nine. The linen cupboard."

"Ten. The crawl space."

He climbed the stairs for the last one, the painted door at the end of the hall, the one that opened onto solid plaster. His fingers found the frame. "Eleven," he said. "The little door."

Done. Eleven. The same as always. The house acknowledged, the lock turned, nothing come in.

He let out a breath he felt like he'd been holding since Tuesday's broadcast, since the obituary page, since Marjorie Kettle's calm voice describing the flowers on her own casket — white lilies, *they were my favorite, isn't that funny, how everyone remembers* —

Except.

Except that walking back toward Rosa's room, past the linen cupboard, past the bathroom, past the guest room, some animal part of his brain — the part that notices the missing stair in the dark, the stopped clock, the car ahead braking for no visible reason — that part had already done its own count. Silently. Automatically. The way it had done every night of his childhood without being asked.

And that part of him had gotten a different answer.

Casper stopped in the middle of the upstairs hallway. The bulbs he'd screwed in that afternoon buzzed softly overhead. Rain started again against the windows, gentle, apologetic.

"No," he said.

He went back to the top of the stairs and began again.

"One. The front door. Two. The back door. Three. The cellar door. Four. The pantry. Five. The coat closet. Six. My closet. Seven. The guest room closet. Eight. The bathroom. Nine. The linen cupboard. Ten. The crawl space. Eleven. The little door."

Eleven. Fine. Eleven, exactly as before, exactly as always, and he stood there in the hall feeling the relief pour through him like warm water, and then he made the mistake of looking down the hallway toward the far end, past the little painted door, past the sewing room—

There was another door.

It stood between the sewing room and the corner of the hall, flush with the wallpaper, a plain wooden door with a simple brass knob, and it had not been there at eleven o'clock, and it had not been there during either count, and it was there now, in the way that a word is there once you've seen it printed — suddenly impossible to unsee, occupying space with the quiet confidence of something that has always belonged.

The house ticked. A pipe knocked twice, deep in the walls, like a heartbeat heard through a mattress.

Casper did not move toward it. He also, he noticed, did not move away. His body had decided, without consulting him, that the correct response to an impossible door was stillness — the stillness of prey, of a child playing dead, of a man on live radio keeping his voice level while a dead woman tells him about her funeral.

"Okay," he whispered. "Okay. You're tired. You drove four hours. Old houses shift. Old houses have doors you forget."

But he had counted this hallway a thousand times as a boy. He had played in this hallway. There was a scuff mark near the baseboard where nine-year-old Casper had dragged a toy truck, and it was still there, faithful as scripture, three feet from the new door. The door was not old. The wood was pale, unweathered, as if it had grown there overnight, as if the house had simply decided, sometime in the last hour, that it required one more mouth.

He counted a third time. He made himself do it properly, out loud, touching every frame, refusing to hurry, and his hands had begun to shake by the time he reached the pantry — a fine tremor, then worse, so that his fingertips skittered off the coat closet frame and he had to press them flat against the wood to steady them. "Six," he said, and hated how thin his voice sounded, how young. "Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven."

Twelve.

This time he counted the new door. He couldn't help it; it was there, it existed, it had a frame and a knob and it demanded acknowledgment the way all doors do. His two fingers hovered an inch from its surface and he could feel — or imagined he could feel, God, he hoped he imagined it — a faint warmth coming off the wood, like skin, like something recently alive.

"Twelve," he said, and the word fell into the hallway and did not echo, was swallowed, gone.

The fourth count was the worst, because by then he understood what he was actually doing. He wasn't checking. He was pleading. He walked the house like a penitent, touching every door, whispering the numbers, and the numbers came out wrong in his mouth — he stumbled at seven, said ten twice, had to start the upstairs over — and when he finally stood in front of the new door for the last time, his hands were shaking hard enough that he shoved them into his pockets like a teenager caught stealing.

Twelve doors. Four counts. Twelve, twelve, twelve, twelve.

*While we're counting, nothing else can come in.*

"But it's already in," Casper said to the empty hallway. His breath fogged faintly, though the house was warm. "Isn't it. It's already in."

The door said nothing. Doors are good at that. It just stood there being a door, being an invitation shaped like furniture, and Casper thought — absurdly, hysterically — of his show, of the callers, of how they all waited so patiently on the line for him to pick up, and how this door had the same patience, the same courtesy, the same terrible willingness to wait.

He slept — if you could call it that — in the armchair in Rosa's room with every light in the house burning and the bedroom door wedged shut with a chair. Around three in the morning he surfaced from a shallow, colorless sleep and heard, from somewhere below him, distinct and unhurried, the sound of a door opening. Just one. Just once. Hinges that did not creak, because they were new.

He lay in the dark — the relative dark, the lamp-lit dark — with his heart slamming, and waited for footsteps that never came.

In the morning, gray and drizzling, he made himself walk the hallway. The door was still there. Of course it was still there. But now, as he stood regarding it with the numb calm of a man well past the edge of what he can process, he noticed something he'd missed in the night: a faint rectangle of paler wallpaper on the wall beside the sewing room, the ghost-outline of a picture frame long removed. And beneath it, scratched into the paint of the baseboard, small and old and unmistakably in Rosa's handwriting, four words:

*We do not open.*

Casper crouched down and touched the letters. They were worn smooth, repainted over at least once, which meant Rosa had written them years ago. Decades. Which meant Rosa had known about this door — had lived with it, counted around it, kept her lights burning against it — for most of her life.

Which meant the counting had never been a lullaby.

It had been a census. A roll call. A way of knowing, every single night, exactly how many mouths the house had — and whether it had grown any new ones.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. He nearly dropped it getting it out. A text from his mother, replying to last night's voicemail, five hours late the way she was late to everything:

*You're staying THERE? Casper, honey, why would you go to that house?*

And then, before he could type anything, a second message, arriving like a stone dropped down a well:

*Whatever you do, don't look at her photo albums.*

* * *

He should have thrown the phone in the drawer. He should have driven back to the city that morning, checked into a hotel, found a therapist, a priest, a lawyer, anyone whose job involved the word "unrealistic." Instead he stood in the upstairs hallway for a long time, watching the new door with the wary respect you'd give a sleeping animal, and then he went downstairs, put on coffee he still wasn't drinking, and began carrying boxes of Rosa's belongings out of the sewing room — because the albums were in the sewing room, he knew that, bottom shelf of the bookcase, wrapped in a pillowcase the way she wrapped everything fragile — and because his mother had told him not to look at them, and because he had spent thirty-eight years learning that the people who loved him most only forbade the things that mattered.

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked — twice, patiently, like a knock at a door.

Like a knock, Casper thought, from the other side.
