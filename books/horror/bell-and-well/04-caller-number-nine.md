## 4. Caller Number Nine

The ON AIR sign had a hairline crack running through the O, and at 2:14 in the morning Casper Almeida had begun to think of it as an eye that never quite closed.

He sat behind the glass in Studio B of WQRT-AM, a station so old the walls still held their original acoustic tile, yellowed now to the color of old teeth. The board in front of him glowed amber and red. Outside the booth window, the hallway lights were off — they always were after midnight, a cost-saving measure Brandt had instituted two years ago — and the darkness beyond the glass had a quality Casper could only describe as attentive. It didn't press against the window. It waited.

"Caller number eight," he said into the microphone, his voice pitched low and warm, the voice he'd spent eleven years sanding smooth for exactly this hour. "You're live on *The Night Desk*. Go ahead."

The woman who answered was named Dolores Vann, and she had been dead for six days.

Casper knew this because he'd checked. That was the new ritual now, layered over the old ones like sediment: count the doors, keep the lights burning, and between callers, when the cart machine cycled its idents, pull up the county obituary archive on the cracked monitor beside the board and type in the name the caller gave him. Nine nights out of ten lately, the name came back with dates attached. Born 1951. Died last Tuesday. Survived by two daughters and a brother in Mesa.

Dolores Vann's obituary included a photograph. The woman on the line sounded seventy years old and tired in the way only the truly finished can be tired, and the photograph showed her at some church picnic, squinting into sun that no longer touched her.

"I want to talk about the flowers," Dolores was saying. Her voice had a faint wet quality underneath it, like a recording played through a cloth. "Nobody talks about the flowers. Everybody talks about the service, the sermon, the casserole afterward. But I spent forty years planting those beds along the driveway, and I died in April, which means I missed the peonies coming up. Do you understand what that is, Mr. Almeida? To plant something every fall knowing you'll see it bloom, and then not see it?"

"I think I do," Casper said. His hand hovered over the fader. There was a protocol forming in his head, unspoken, unwritten: *never hang up first.* He didn't know where the rule came from. It had simply arrived one night, fully formed, the way all the rules arrived now, and he obeyed it the way he obeyed the door-counting and the lights — with the faith of a man who has seen what happens when the rules break down.

"The peonies came up anyway," Dolores said. "My daughter sent me a picture. Well. She sent it to my phone, and the phone is in a drawer, but I heard it buzz. Isn't that something? Six feet under a drawer and I still heard it buzz."

"That's—" Casper searched for the professional word, the compassionate-host word. "That's love, Dolores. That doesn't stop at the—"

"At the what?" She sounded genuinely curious. Not cruel. Curious the way a child is curious about whether fish sleep.

"Line," he finished quietly.

A pause. Somewhere in the board, a relay clicked, though no light changed.

"You're kind," Dolores said at last. "You're kind to let us talk. Most of them just scream and hang up. Did you know that? The first week, before you started letting us finish, we used to get through to other stations. There's a man in Tucson who does sports talk at midnight, and a woman called him — Marcy, from here, drowned in '88 — and he hung up on her twice and then unplugged his board." A sound that might have been laughter, dry as paper turning. "He moved to Phoenix. We can still reach Phoenix, Mr. Almeida. Distance isn't really our problem."

"What is your problem?" Casper asked, and immediately wished he hadn't, because it sounded like a host's question, a segment question, and this was not a segment.

But Dolores considered it seriously. He could hear her considering — a long, hollow quiet, like wind moving through an empty house.

"Forgetting," she said finally. "We forget fast. Names go first. Then faces. I've been gone six days and I can't remember what my husband's hands looked like, only that they were always warm and always slightly damp, like he'd just washed them. You're doing us a service, you know. Every time you say one of our names out loud, it comes back for a while. Like a lamp turned on in a room that was going dark." Her voice softened. "Say mine again?"

"Dolores Vann," Casper said, and meant it, and hated how much he meant it.

"There," she sighed, satisfied. "There it is. Thank you, sweetheart. I'll let the next one through. He's been holding a long time."

The line went to dead air with a soft click, and Casper sat for a moment in the hum of the board, his heart doing something complicated between grief and terror. Then he pulled up the queue screen. Caller number nine was already waiting. The ID field read **BLOCKED**, which was normal. The hold time read **00:00:00**, which was not. As if the call had been placed the instant number eight hung up. As if it had been waiting longer than that.

He punched it through.

"Caller number nine," he said. "You're live on *The Night Desk*. Go ahead."

Static first — the thick, granular static of a bad connection, or of a connection crossing some distance that wasn't measured in miles. Then a man's voice, middle-aged, hoarse, urgent in a way that made Casper sit forward despite himself.

"Casper. Casper Almeida. Thank God. Thank God you picked up."

"That's the idea," Casper said gently. "What's your name?"

"It doesn't matter, listen to me, there isn't time, she's getting worse—" The man stopped, breathed. The breath had a rattle in it, deep in the chest, the kind of rattle Casper associated with his grandfather's last winter. "I need you to pass along a message. That's why I called. I know how this works now, I've listened, I understand what you are."

"What I *am*," Casper repeated carefully. On the monitor, the waveform of the man's voice jumped and jittered in patterns that looked less like speech and more like something struggling.

"A relay," the man said. "You're the relay. The living side of the line. So pass it along. Tell her—" His voice broke, reassembled itself. "Tell her I'm sorry about the porch light. Tell her I wasn't angry, I was scared, and I didn't know how to say scared, so I said everything else instead. Tell her the last thing I thought about wasn't the argument. It was her making coffee that morning, humming, and how I stood in the doorway like an idiot just watching her, and if I'd known that was the last ordinary morning I would have—"

The line went dead.

Not faded. Not dropped. *Dead* — the abrupt, total silence of a circuit cut, mid-word, the man's mouth open around a word that never arrived. The HOLD light for line nine went dark. The waveform flattened to a single black horizon across the screen.

Casper stared at it.

*Tell her,* the man had said. *Pass it along.* And Casper's mind, which had spent nine nights learning the shape of this new world, did the arithmetic without being asked: the man was calling to reach someone still alive. Someone who listened to the show. Someone whose porch light was off, whose last morning had been watched from a doorway. A widow, probably. A wife.

Except.

Except the man had said *thank God you picked up* in a voice that knew him. Not recognized-the-radio-voice knew him. Knew him. And except the hold time had been zero seconds, and except—

The studio speakers came on.

Not the monitor feed. The main monitors, the big JBLs mounted above the booth window, the ones that carried the program audio back to the host so he could hear himself as the audience heard him. They were switched off. Casper checked the panel with his own eyes even as the voice poured out of them, because the MONITOR toggle sat in its OFF position, gray and inert, and the voice came anyway, filling the little room, and it was the same voice, the same man, hoarse and rattling and close:

"—would have told her. I would have told her every single day. Casper. *Casper.* Are you hearing this? Tell her I'm sorry about the porch light. Tell her I wasn't angry—"

"No," Casper said aloud, to the empty booth, to the speakers, to whoever was using them.

"—I was scared, and I didn't know how to say scared—"

He reached for the master monitor fader and pulled it down. The voice continued at full volume. He killed the amplifier breaker on the wall; the JBLs were on a separate circuit, he remembered suddenly, uselessly, from the station tour they'd given him eleven years ago, and the voice continued, patient now, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world because time had stopped being his problem.

"Tell her the last thing I thought about wasn't the argument."

Casper stood up. His chair rolled backward and bumped the wall. The booth felt smaller than it had five minutes ago, the ceiling lower, the glass darker. Beyond the window, the unlit hallway seemed to have come nearer — he couldn't have said how, couldn't have pointed to anything that had moved, but the darkness past the glass had the density of a crowd standing very politely together.

"It was her making coffee," the speakers said, and now the voice was changing, or Casper was hearing it truly for the first time, the rattle smoothing out, the urgency draining away until what remained was calm and mild and horribly familiar. "Humming. Standing in the doorway like an idiot. If I'd known that was the last ordinary morning—"

"Stop," Casper whispered.

"—I would have told her. I would have told her every single day."

Silence. Real silence, the amplifier dead, the speakers dark, the only sound the tick of the studio clock and Casper's own breathing and, far beneath both, the faint subsonic hum he'd learned to hear in the grandmother's house at night, except he wasn't in the grandmother's house. He was four miles from it, downtown, behind three locked doors, and the hum was here too, rising up through the floor, through the foundation, through whatever old pipes and older ground the station stood on.

His hand found the console phone — the hardline, copper, no wireless anything, the phone Brandt kept "for when the internet dies." He dialed the only number he had memorized besides his own: the house. Four rings. Five. He counted them the way he counted doors, out loud, lips barely moving, because counting was a wall and walls were all he had.

On the sixth ring, the answering machine picked up. His own recorded voice filled the earpiece, cheerful and rehearsed: *"You've reached Casper. Leave a message, and if this is about the show, I read everything—"*

Then the beep. Then a click. Then breathing.

No. Not breathing. Humming.

Three notes, soft and slow, rising and settling, rising and settling. A fragment of melody. He knew it. God help him, he knew it — it lived in his body deeper than memory, in the place where Rosa's kitchen lived, where the smell of cinnamon and dish soap lived, where her hand on his hair lived. Three notes of the lullaby she'd never finished teaching him, hummed into his answering machine from a house that should have been empty, forty minutes before he would drive home to it.

The line clicked off on its own.

Casper lowered the phone. In the glass of the booth window, his reflection sat pale and hollow-eyed in the amber board-light, and behind his reflection — he made himself look, made himself turn — the hallway was just a hallway. Dark tiles, dead fluorescents, the EXIT sign glowing green over the stairwell door. Ordinary. Empty. Attentive.

The queue screen chimed. Caller number ten, waiting. Hold time: 00:00:00.

He looked at the clock. 2:41. Nineteen minutes left in the hour. He could sign off early. He had never once signed off early in eleven years, but he could; there was no rule against it; the rule was only *never hang up first*, and signing off wasn't hanging up, it was—

It was leaving them on hold in the dark, alone, forgetting, names going first and faces after, Dolores Vann losing her husband's warm damp hands forever while he drove home to a house that hummed.

"Oh, you son of a bitch," Casper said softly, to the station, to the well he could feel underneath everything now, underneath the town, underneath the whole clever architecture of rules. Because that was the trap inside the trap, wasn't it? The lights and the doors and the never-hanging-up weren't protections. They were leashes. Every kindness he performed fed the thing that required kindness. Every name he spoke bought a dead stranger another minute of remembering and bought the house another inch of him.

He pressed the button for line ten.

"This is *The Night Desk*," he said, and his voice held, mostly. "Go ahead."

But before the caller could speak, the cart machine fired an ident by itself — the station's overnight automation hiccupping, he'd have told himself once, weeks ago — and a voice that was not the recorded announcer's came out of the broadcast chain, out of every radio within forty miles, out of the dashboard speakers of every trucker running I-80 through the dark, and it was the man from line nine, calm now, gentle now, done begging:

*"She'll hear it, Casper. She always listens. And so will you — you'll hear all of it, soon enough. Ask your grandmother about the fourth verse."*

The ident ended. The automation resumed its normal schedule with a cheerful jingle about a mattress warehouse.

And on the cracked monitor beside the board, the obituary archive page refreshed itself, unprompted, and displayed a single entry, dated tomorrow.

Casper Almeida did not read the name on it. He shut the monitor off, killed the mic, and sat in the humming dark of Studio B with nineteen minutes of airtime owed and a melody rising in his throat like water, three notes, rising and settling, rising and settling, and he pressed both hands over his own mouth and began, silently, desperately, to count doors.
