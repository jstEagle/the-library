## 5. Marta's Kitchen

I told myself I was going to Marta Oduya's for the food.

That was the story I assembled on the walk over, the way I'd been assembling stories in Hollowmere for three years: it was nearly October, the mornings had teeth in them now, and Marta made a stew with smoked pork and black-eyed peas that could make a grown folklorist forget he was supposed to be observing anything. Professional detachment was all very well, but professional detachment didn't come with cornbread.

The truth was less appetizing. The truth was that I hadn't slept properly in four nights, not since I'd listened to that playback — my own breathing, slow and even, and underneath it, threaded through it like a second thread through the same cloth, breathing that was *not* mine. Slower. Deeper. Patient in a way no animal is patient. And then last night, when I'd played the tape again at two in the morning with the lights off because apparently I hated myself, I'd heard something new: at 3:07 a.m., my own voice, thick with sleep, asking a question.

*"Who taught you the song?"*

And another voice answering.

I hadn't been able to make out the answer. That was somehow worse than if I had. A question I didn't remember asking, answered by a voice I couldn't identify, in my own bedroom, while I slept like a man with nothing to fear.

So yes. I went to Marta's for the stew. And for whatever a person can get from sitting in a warm kitchen with someone who has lived in Hollowmere her whole life and never once seemed afraid of it.

Marta opened the door before I knocked. She did that sometimes; I'd stopped finding it eerie and started finding it efficient.

"You look terrible," she said, by way of good morning.

"Thank you. I've been working hard."

"You've been listening to something you shouldn't." She stepped back to let me in. "Come in before the cold does."

Her kitchen was the warmest room in Hollowmere, possibly in the county. Copper pots on the walls, a stove that had been lit since sometime in the previous century as far as I could tell, and always — always — something simmering. Marta Oduya was sixty-something and built like the idea of a grandmother rather than any actual one: small, round, quick-moving, with reading glasses pushed up into gray-threaded hair and hands that were never still. There were four grandchildren in and out of that house at all hours, though this morning the kitchen held only her and the smell of onions doing something wonderful in butter.

"Sit," she said, pointing at the chair by the stove, which was unofficially mine. "You'll have the stew and you'll have bread and you'll tell me what's wrong, and not the version where nothing's wrong."

"I haven't said anything's—"

"You walked here from your cottage with your coat buttoned wrong and your recorder bag on your shoulder like a man carrying a bomb." She turned back to the stove. "Buttoned wrong, Rafael. You've worn that coat three winters. You know how it buttons."

I looked down. She was right. I fixed it, feeling like a child caught out by a substitute teacher.

* * *

She fed me first. This was policy, I think — Marta believed no serious conversation should happen on an empty stomach, and also, I suspected, that a man chewing cannot lie fluently. The stew arrived in a bowl the size of a hubcap, dark and rich, with the smoked pork falling apart and a heat that built slowly at the back of the throat. The bread came after, torn from a loaf still steaming, with butter that she salted herself, which is the kind of detail that makes you understand why people stay in villages they should probably leave.

For a while neither of us spoke. The stove ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked under small feet — Idris, probably, the youngest, who treated mornings as optional.

"Eat," Marta said, when my spoon slowed. "You're thinking instead of eating. Thinking can wait. It's very patient." A pause, and then, so lightly I almost missed it: "Most things here are patient."

I set down the spoon.

"Marta. I need to ask you something, and I want you to tell me the truth, or tell me you won't tell me the truth, but don't do the thing where you feed me until I forget the question."

"That's exactly what I'm doing," she agreed pleasantly. "But go on."

"The harvest song. The one the choir rehearses. On Tuesday they stopped in the middle of the third verse — Mr. Aldous just *stopped*, mid-word, like a clock running down — and nobody would sing it again. Not once. Not to humor me. I've recorded every song in this village twice over and nobody has ever refused me anything, and suddenly everyone's looking at their shoes." I turned the bowl a quarter-turn on the table, an old nervous habit. "And there are other things. Sounds at night. My equipment picking up—" I stopped myself. I hadn't told anyone about the tape. "Things I can't account for. And everyone in this village acts like there's a rulebook everyone else got a copy of. So. What is the count?"

The word was out of my mouth before I'd decided to say it. I hadn't planned to say *the count*. I'm not sure I'd ever consciously thought the phrase. It came up out of some cellar of my mind where I'd evidently been storing things without my permission, and once it was in the air between us, Marta went very still.

Not frightened. Still. The way water goes still.

"Where did you hear that?" she asked.

"I don't know. Did I hear it? Or did I—" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's the problem, Marta. Lately I don't know what I know. I wake up with words in my mouth I didn't put there."

She took off her glasses, cleaned them on her apron, put them back on. All of it slow, deliberate, a woman buying time the way other people buy bread.

"All right," she said. "You'll finish your bowl. Then we'll talk. Not because I'm stalling." A thin smile. "All right, partly because I'm stalling. But mostly because a man makes better decisions fed than hungry, and I need you making very good decisions in the next ten minutes."

I finished the bowl. It felt a little like finishing a dare.

* * *

She sat down across from me then, folding her hands on the table, and for a long moment she just looked at me — the way you look at a fence post you're deciding whether to lean on.

"There are songs," she said finally, "and there are *songs*. You know that already, or you would by now, being the collector you are. Some songs are for keeping. Some are for doing. And one or two are for *counting*."

"Counting."

"Every verse has names in it. Old names, family names, names of houses and fields. When the choir sings the harvest song, they're not celebrating, Rafael. They're taking attendance." She said it flatly, factually, the way you'd explain that the post office closed at noon. "A name sung is a name counted. A name counted is a name kept. Kept safe, kept present, kept—" she searched, "—*held*. Through the winter. Through everything."

I wanted to laugh. Three years of academic discipline rose up in me like a tide: this is folklore function, this is communal memory practice, this is how oral cultures bind identity to place, there are forty papers in this—

"And the ones not sung?" I heard myself ask. My voice sounded far away. "What happens to the uncounted?"

Marta's eyes didn't move from mine.

"They're taken," she said. "When the tally comes due. Which is soon. Which is why Mr. Aldous stopped singing, and why nobody will sing that verse twice — you don't repeat yourself to it, Rafael. You don't give it anything twice. Once is a gift. Twice is an invitation."

The stove ticked. Upstairs, the floorboard creaked again, and this time I heard small bare feet pad to the top of the stairs and stop there, listening. Idris. Seven years old and listening to his grandmother say *taken* in a voice with no drama in it at all, and going back to his room without a sound, because apparently this was normal. Apparently children here learned early which conversations you stood outside of.

"Marta," I said carefully, "that's a beautiful piece of belief-structure, and I mean that as a compliment, but—"

"Don't." She held up one hand, not sharp, but final. "Don't do the thing where you translate me into your language so it stops being true. I've watched you do it for three years. Wind, you wrote. Pipes. Bad wiring in the cottage. Bad wiring." Something moved through her face that was almost tenderness and almost grief. "Rafael. Sweet man. You have written down every rationalization you've ever had about this village. In that little book. In order. Do you think I don't know?"

My mouth went dry. "You've read my—"

"No. I don't need to. Everyone here knows what you write, because everyone here knows what you *don't* write. You write 'wind' on the nights the wind isn't blowing. You write 'pipes' and this village hasn't had pipes since the eighties. You write explanations the way the choir sings verses, love. To keep yourself counted. To keep yourself *safe*." She leaned forward. "It worked, too. For three years it worked. Whatever walks at night heard a man explaining himself and thought: ah, he's busy, he's occupied, he belongs to his own noise. But lately—" she tapped the table twice, softly, "—lately you've stopped explaining. Lately you've started *asking questions*. Out loud. At night. To someone."

The room tilted slightly. I gripped the edge of the table.

"How do you know I ask questions at night?"

"Because everyone in Hollowmere knows, Rafael. Because the house tells us. Because—" she exhaled through her nose, and for the first time she looked tired, genuinely tired, a woman carrying something heavy up a long hill, "—because it wants us to know. It's proud of you. Can you understand that? Whatever answers you at three in the morning, it's *pleased*. You finally spoke to it directly, and now it thinks you and it have an arrangement."

"We don't have an arrangement."

"No," Marta said. "You have an *appointment*. There's a difference, and the difference is autumn."

* * *

I don't remember standing up, but I was standing, and my chair was behind me, and Marta was looking up at me with an expression I'll carry to whatever grave I get.

"Sit down," she said. "You're not leaving. I can see you thinking about leaving, and I want you to sit down and hear the rest, because the rest matters more than the fear."

I sat. My legs made the decision; I just attended.

"Here is what I will tell you, and then I will tell you what I want you to do, and then you'll eat more bread, because you've gone white as flour." She refilled my bowl without asking. "The count must balance. That's the whole of it, the heart of it. Every harvest, the song counts the living, and the number has to come out right — the same as last year, or made right some other way. Names sung keep people. Names missing leave holes. And holes—" her hands folded tight, "—holes get filled. One way or another. By the final verse."

"Filled how?"

"You don't want the list of ways."

"Marta."

"No," she said, gently, immovably. "You don't. Trust me that there are things worse than not knowing, and I am giving you the mercy of one of them."

She let that sit. Outside, wind moved through the beeches along the lane, and I flinched at it — actually flinched, full-body — and Marta saw, and something in her face settled into decision.

"Now. What I want." She reached across and took my hand, hers small and dry and strong as a root. "Leave. Before autumn. Before the rehearsal becomes the singing, before the tally comes due. Pack that ridiculous bag of machines, get in your car, drive to somewhere with streetlights, and don't look back at this village in any mirror, because mirrors are doors and you've been leaving yours uncovered like a fool."

"Marta—"

"I'm begging." She said it simply, without shame, and it landed harder than shouting would have. "Three years I've watched you, and fed you, and liked you — *liked* you, Rafael, don't mistake this for anything colder — and I am begging you to go. Today. This afternoon. Your notes, your tapes, your book — leave them. Burn them if you can manage it. A man who leaves everything can start over. A man who takes his records takes the record *of him*, and it reads records."

"But my work—"

"Is a census," she said quietly. "You've spent three years building a census of Hollowmere, love. Every song transcribed. Every singer named. Every verse numbered." Her grip tightened. "You didn't come here to study our counting, Rafael. You became part of it."

The kitchen was very warm and very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, old pipes ticked and settled, and for the first time in three years I heard them and did not think *pipes*.

"Why are you telling me this now?" I asked. "Why not three years ago? Why not warn me off at the bus stop?"

Marta released my hand and looked at the window, at the light going long and gold across the yard, and when she answered, her voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they step around a sleeping thing.

"Because for three years, you were fine. Explaining. Writing your little reasons. Counted by your own noise, like I said." She turned back to me. "And because there are reasons a house keeps a guest, Rafael, and not all of them are kindness, and I've prayed every night that you'd never need to know which kind ours was."

Before I could ask — and God help me, I was going to ask, the question was already climbing my throat — the stairs creaked, and Idris came down into the kitchen in his socks, hair wild, and climbed straight onto the chair beside me as if he'd been summoned. He looked at me with those grave, unhurried eyes children here all seemed to have, and then at his grandmother, and then he said, conversationally, the way you'd report the weather:

"He's asking the right questions now. It likes that."

Marta closed her eyes. Idris reached over and stole a piece of bread from my plate, entirely at peace.

And I understood, sitting in the warmest kitchen in Hollowmere with my heart going like a trapped bird, that the boy had heard every word through the floorboards — and that he wasn't talking about me.

He was talking about whatever waited, patient as winter, in the dark of my bedroom, for 3 a.m., and its next question.
