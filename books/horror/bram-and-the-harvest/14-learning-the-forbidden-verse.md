## 14. Learning the Forbidden Verse

The wax cylinder arrived in Hollowmere in 1911 and had not been moved since, which was the only reason it still existed.

Rafael found it on a Tuesday, in the church archive behind the vestry, in a cabinet that smelled of cedar and old varnish and something faintly sweeter underneath—wax going soft, he supposed, or the ghost of candles. The box was labeled in a hand that had pressed hard enough to emboss the cardboard: *HOLLOWMERE HARVEST — FULL SONG — DO NOT PLAY AT NIGHT.* Someone had underlined the last three words twice.

"Subtle," Rafael murmured to the empty archive. "Very subtle."

He had been coming to the archive for three years and had somehow never opened this particular cabinet. That bothered him more than the label did. He was a thorough man; thoroughness was his whole personality, according to his doctoral supervisor, who had once told him he alphabetized his anxieties. He should have found this cylinder in his first month. Instead here it sat, exactly where a man named—Rafael squinted at the signature—*E. Aldous Merrivale* had left it, waiting for someone with a phonograph and poor judgment.

He had both now. The phonograph he'd borrowed from the church itself, an Edison Standard with a brass horn like a morning glory, kept in working order by some long-dead curate who had apparently believed in being prepared for emergencies involving sound. And the poor judgment came free.

He carried the box out into the daylight, because the label said *do not play at night* and Rafael Kavanagh had learned, over three years of explaining away nocturnal breathing, that when a village wrote warnings down, they meant them. Sunlight poured through the east windows onto the reading table. Somewhere outside, Marta's youngest was arguing with a chicken about jurisdiction. It was, all things considered, a fine afternoon for transcribing a forbidden verse.

* * *

The cylinder played badly at first. Wax shrinks over a century; the grooves had gone shallow and wandering, and the first thirty seconds were all crackle and sea-sound, as if the recording had been made underwater by a patient octopus. Then a voice surfaced—a man's voice, clipped, educated, the kind of voice that had been trained to project over organ music.

*"Testing. Testing. The fourteenth of September, nineteen hundred and eleven. This is E. Aldous Merrivale, recording the harvest song of Hollowmere in its complete form, against the wishes of—" * a pause, a dry cough * "—against the wishes of everyone, frankly."*

Rafael laughed out loud, alone in the archive, and then felt a chill go through him that had nothing to do with the draft from the door.

Because he knew that cadence. Not the voice—he'd never heard this man speak—but the *cadence*. The little self-deprecating flourish before the confession. The way Merrivale said *frankly* the way Rafael said *honestly*. The professional bravado laid over professional fear. Rafael had recorded himself doing fieldwork notes for fifteen years. He sounded like that. He *sounded like that.*

"He was a folklorist," Rafael said quietly. "Of course he was."

Of course he was. That was how these things worked, wasn't it? The songs needed collectors. The collectors needed the songs. And somewhere between the needing and the collecting, the ledger got hungry.

He set a notebook on the table, sharpened two pencils, and let the cylinder run.

* * *

Merrivale's singers were good. Better than good—their blend was so seamless that Rafael caught himself forgetting there were multiple voices at all, just one warm instrument singing a song he knew intimately and yet did not know. Because this was the full version. All eleven verses of it, including the one the current choir stopped mid-way through every rehearsal, the one old Tam Weller called *the dropped stitch* and refused to discuss, the one that made even the churchwarden find urgent business elsewhere.

Rafael transcribed as he listened, and the verse came out of the wax like something being born slowly and unwillingly:

*One for the name that the reaper keeps,*
*two for the debt that the tally sweeps,*
*three for the mouth that will not say*
*the word that calls the count away—*

He stopped the machine. His hand was not entirely steady, and the pencil line wobbled across *count away* like a seismograph reading.

It was a counting rhyme. Of course it was. Idris sang a counting rhyme on the school steps, a nonsense thing about magpies and missing brothers, and Rafael had filed it under *children's folklore, charming, follow up.* This was the adult version. This was what the children's rhyme grew up into if nobody loved it enough to stop it.

He wound the cylinder forward and listened again. And again. By the fourth pass he could sing along under his breath, and by the sixth he noticed something that turned his stomach over gently, like a boat changing direction.

The verse didn't end where the other verses ended. The others resolved—they landed home on the tonic, satisfied, done. This one *leaned*. The last note hung a half-step high, unresolved, a door held open. Musically speaking, the forbidden verse wasn't a verse at all. It was a question. Eleven verses of census, and then this: a question sung directly to whatever was keeping the books.

And Merrivale's recording answered it.

Not the singers. A different voice, low and layered, arriving through the wax as though it had always been in the room and had merely waited for permission. It spoke only a few words, and the wax distorted them, but Rafael played those five seconds perhaps forty times, until the phonograph's needle wore a visible trench in the groove and he had to stop before he destroyed the only copy in existence.

What he could make out was this: *"Accepted. Entered. One owed."*

Then Merrivale himself, very close to the horn, whispering with the careful brightness of a man narrating his own disaster: *"It accepted the verse. I don't—I don't fully understand. I asked it to take my name instead of the Hollis girl's, and it said accepted, entered, one owed. But it hasn't taken anything. My name is still mine. I feel it still sitting inside me like a coin in a pocket. I think—I think I have given it something and it has given me a receipt, and the receipt is not the same as the transaction."*

A long silence on the cylinder. When Merrivale spoke again, his voice had changed—not frightened anymore, which was worse. Curious. Delighted, almost. The voice of a scholar who has just discovered his life's work.

*"I have begun writing down things I do not remember learning. Whole pages. In my own hand. Yesterday I found a list of every person born in Hollowmere since 1740, and I have no memory of compiling it, and yet the ink is mine, the abbreviations are mine, the spelling errors are mine. I am becoming a very efficient census of myself."*

Rafael put down his pencil.

He thought about his own notebooks. Three years of them, stacked in his cottage, full of observations he'd assumed were his own—and lately, entries that read strangely, entries about "the folklorist" as though written by a friendly colleague describing someone else. He had blamed fatigue. He had blamed the bad wiring.

He thought about the second voice in his sleep recordings, answering his half-asleep questions with facts he had never told anyone, and lately—God help him—answering questions *before he asked them*, in his own cadence, with his own little verbal tics, saying *honestly* where Rafael would say *honestly*.

"One owed," he repeated to the sunlit archive. "You gave it your name and it gave you a receipt."

* * *

The cylinder had more. Merrivale had apparently kept recording right up to whatever happened to him, and the final section was the part Rafael had to listen to standing up, with both hands flat on the table, because sitting down felt too much like settling in.

*"Day nine. The village has stopped using my name. Not angrily—they're kind about it, terribly kind, they bring me pies—but when they speak to me they address the air slightly to my left, as though the man they're talking to is standing beside me. Perhaps he is. I hear him sometimes now. He hums the harvest song while I work. He hums it better than I do."*

*"Day twelve. I tried to leave. I got as far as the crossroads by the mill and the road bent back. I want to be clear in the record that I am not a hysterical man. I measured it. I took bearings. The road bends back. If anyone finds this cylinder: the road bends back, and you cannot walk out of a place that has entered you in a book."*

*"Day fifteen. Last attempt. I understand now what 'one owed' means. The count doesn't want my name—it wants me to *be* the difference. Every ledger needs a balancing figure. I am to be the rounding error. The Hollis girl is safe—she came back yesterday, walked out of the wood with hay in her hair and no memory of being gone, and her mother wept on her, and I stood at the edge of the yard feeling the most extraordinary envy, because she got to come back and I am the one who paid."*

The wax crackled. Underneath the crackle, faintly, unmistakably, someone was humming the harvest song. It was not Merrivale humming. The humming continued straight through Merrivale's next words without pausing for breath, two sounds occupying one throat.

*"If you are hearing this, you are the next fool with a phonograph. Listen to me. The verse works. The substitution works. But you must give the name freely and completely—you must mean to lose it, all of it, every time your mother said it, every time you signed it, every time you whispered it to yourself in the dark to remember who you were. Half a gift is a debt. A whole gift is a payment. I held back. God forgive me, I held back the middle of my name, the piece my wife used when she was angry, because I couldn't bear to lose that, and so the count holds the rest of me hostage against it."*

His voice cracked, and for a moment the folklorist vanished and there was only a tired man in a cold room.

*"Tell whoever finds this that I'm sorry I failed. Tell them the verse is real and the price is real and the door out is real, but you have to walk through it with empty hands. You can't take yourself with you."*

Then the humming voice, close to the horn, clear as a bell, in a cadence that Rafael recognized down to the marrow because it was *his own cadence*, said pleasantly:

*"Testing. Testing."*

And the cylinder ran out.

* * *

Rafael sat in the archive for a long time after the phonograph wound down. Outside, the chicken dispute had been resolved, presumably in the chicken's favor; Idris was now whistling, off-key and triumphant. Ordinary sounds. Living sounds. Rafael held onto them like rungs.

Then he looked down at his notebook, at the transcription he'd made, and saw that beneath the forbidden verse—in his handwriting, in pencil he had no memory of picking up—someone had added a footnote:

*You'll need the whole of it. Don't keep the middle. — A.M.*

Rafael stared at it. The initials were Merrivale's. The handwriting was Merrivale's—or rather, it was Rafael's handwriting doing a flawless impression of Merrivale's, the way his sleep-voice did impressions of him.

"You're helping me," Rafael said aloud, to the empty chair across the table. "Or you're setting the table."

No answer. Just the afternoon light moving slowly across the reading table, and somewhere in the walls of the old church, pipes ticking as they cooled, which was wind, obviously, or plumbing, or—

"Honestly," Rafael said, and heard, very faintly, from a direction with no speaker in it, a second voice echo back, fondly: *"Honestly."*

He packed the cylinder carefully, wrapped the phonograph's horn in cloth, and carried everything out into the light. He had eleven days until the harvest. He had a verse to learn, a name to practice losing, and a family across the lane who fed him stew and never asked why his hands shook.

He also had, he realized as he stepped into the road, absolutely no memory of deciding to borrow the phonograph from the church. He distinctly remembered finding it already sitting by the archive door that morning, wrapped and ready, with a note pinned to the horn in fresh ink:

*For the next fool. — A.M.*
