## 17. What Rafael Forgets

The trick, Marta said, was to practice.

"You don't hand over a name the way you hand over a cup of tea," she told him, setting down a plate of seed cake that smelled like every autumn he'd ever loved. "You've got to loosen your grip first. Otherwise it tears coming out, and a torn name is worse than no name at all. The count doesn't know what to do with scraps."

"So I rehearse forgetting," Rafael said. "Like scales."

"Like scales," Marta agreed. "Except when you get the scale wrong, nobody's ears bleed and the piano doesn't eat you."

He laughed. She didn't. That was the thing about Marta Oduya — she had a laugh like a hearth fire, low and crackling and generous, but she kept it holstered when the subject was the count. He'd learned to read her silences the way he'd once learned to read dialect shifts in field recordings: here a boundary, there a warning, here the place where the map simply stopped.

It was the second week of October. The harvest gathering was nine days away. Somewhere out past the orchards, Idris was still missing, and the village was still rehearsing with an empty chair that no one would look at directly, and Rafael had made his decision the way you make most irreversible decisions — quietly, at three in the morning, with cold feet and no witnesses.

He would give them his name. All of it. The whole ledger entry of himself.

But first, apparently, he had to practice losing it.

* * *

His first exercise, per Marta's instructions, was small. She'd called it "the spoon test."

"Pick something you won't miss," she said. "Something so ordinary it's practically furniture. A memory about a spoon, if you have one. Hold it in your mind and then let it go — not push it away, just open your hand. See what happens."

Rafael sat on the edge of his bed in the Oduyas' spare room — *his* room now, though the word felt provisional lately — and searched his memory for spoons.

There was one. Absurdly vivid, actually: his grandmother's kitchen in Cork, a bent teaspoon she used only for tasting stew, never for eating, because — because because because. He reached for the reason and found a smooth blank place where it should have been, and realized with a lurch that this was the point. He'd already done this once, accidentally, weeks ago, during an earlier practice session he'd half-forgotten having.

"That's cheating," he muttered to the empty room. "I did the homework before class."

But the blankness itself was instructive. It didn't hurt. That was the horrifying part. The memory hadn't been ripped out; it had been *edited*, the way a careful archivist trims a damaged section of tape and splices the healthy parts back together. The stew still existed. The grandmother still existed. Only the little connecting thread — the why of the spoon — was gone, and nothing in him screamed about its absence, because how do you scream about a hole you can't see?

He wrote it all down, of course. That was becoming the central irony of his existence: a man preparing to forget everything, cataloguing the inventory as it left. His journal had become something between a will and a museum.

*October 9th,* he wrote that night. *Lost the spoon today. Grandmother still safe. Stew still safe. Reason gone. Felt nothing, which feels like the worst part.*

He underlined the last sentence twice, then crossed it out, then underlined it again, because he couldn't decide whether it was true or just dramatic, and a folklorist ought to know the difference.

* * *

By the fourth day, the exercises were going deeper, and Rafael had developed a system — because systems were how he coped, always had been. Three years in Hollowmere explaining away night-sounds as wind and bad wiring; that had been a system too, and look how well it had served him.

Morning: small losses. Colors of old classrooms. The middle names of childhood friends. The exact taste of a specific ice cream from a specific shop, retained as a category but dissolving as an event.

Afternoon: verification. He'd read his own older journal entries aloud and check which ones still felt like his. Most did. Some had begun to feel like letters from a friendly stranger.

Evening: the hard ones, the ones Marta called "load-bearing memories." Your mother's voice. The smell of your father's coat. The first time you understood you were going to die, age seven, lying under the apple tree watching the light come through the leaves and thinking, with total clarity, *one day I won't be here to see this.* 

He wasn't supposed to surrender those yet. Those were for the final verse. But he practiced touching them, the way you press a bruise to learn its shape.

And it was in the evenings that the journal started doing the strange thing.

* * *

He noticed it on October 12th. He'd written a long entry about the afternoon's session — he'd given up the memory of learning to ride a bicycle, on purpose, as a test of the full technique, and had spent twenty minutes afterward staring at the bicycle-shaped absence in his history, feeling like a man standing in a room where a wall used to be. Then, tired, he'd written:

*Rafael Kavanagh does not remember learning to ride a bicycle. Rafael Kavanagh is told by his notes that he learned, aged six, on a red bike with a white basket, and finds this plausible but unmoving.*

He stared at it. Read it again. He had written *he* instead of *I*. Not once — throughout the entire final paragraph, four sentences, third person, calm as a police report.

He hadn't decided to do that. He was almost certain he hadn't decided to do that.

He flipped backward through the journal. October 11th: all first person. October 10th: first person, except one line near the end — *the exercises are going well; he suspects they are going too well* — which he'd apparently assumed was a typo and corrected in the margin with a neat *I*. October 9th: first person throughout, though the handwriting in the last paragraph was fractionally larger, rounder, the way his writing went when he was very tired or slightly drunk.

The drift had been happening for days. He'd been too close to see it, the way you don't notice a child growing until a pair of trousers stops fitting.

"Well," he said aloud, to the room, to himself, to whatever portion of himself was taking dictation. "That's either a very good sign or a very bad one."

From the walls, the pipes, the space between the plaster — nothing answered. But he had the distinct sensation of being agreed with.

* * *

Marta found him in her kitchen the next morning, sitting where he always sat, at the chair nearest the stove, holding his journal like it might bite.

"It's writing me in third person," he said, before she'd even finished pouring the tea. Good morning, Marta. Lovely to see you, Marta. My journal has developed a narrator, Marta.

She set the pot down and held out her hand. He gave her the journal. She read the last few pages with the unhurried attention of a woman who had raised five children and could read anything — homework, doctors' notes, omens — without visible alarm.

"This is right," she said finally. "This is how it's meant to go."

"How what's meant to go?"

"The giving. You can't hand over a name while you're still standing inside it. You have to step to one side first. Look at yourself from outside." She tapped the page. "Your own hand is doing it because some part of you understands what's needed. The part that isn't frightened."

"And the part that is frightened?"

"Frightened you keeps saying *I*," Marta said. "Let him. There's room in a man for more than one grammar." She pushed the tea toward him. "My husband, God rest him, gave up his name forty years ago. Not to the count — to the church, which is nearly the same thing but with better singing. For a year before he took his vows, his letters home stopped saying *I*. Wrote about 'Thomas' like Thomas was a cousin he was fond of. His mother wept over it. But Thomas said it was the easiest thing he'd ever done, because by the time the name was asked for, he'd already moved out of it. Like selling a house you've already packed."

"Did he regret it?"

Marta considered. "He missed his mother's cooking," she said. "Everything else, he said, was ballast." Her eyes came up and fixed on him, dark and steady. "You'll want to keep one thing. One memory you refuse to give. Everyone who's done this says so. Pick it now, while you're still you enough to choose."

"What did Thomas keep?"

"A smell," she said. "Rain on hot pavement, the day his brother was born. Forty years a priest and he'd stop mid-sermon sometimes, just breathe, like a man catching a scent through a window." She smiled, and for once the hearth-fire laugh was underneath it, warming the words. "The count takes names. It's greedy, but it's precise. It doesn't take what you don't offer. So offer everything — everything but one."

Rafael looked into his tea. He knew, without having to search, which memory it would be. Not his mother's voice, though he loved it. Not the apple tree. It was smaller than those. It was the first evening at the Oduya table, three years ago, soaked from the rain, a stranger with a suitcase and a fake cover story about studying hedgerows, and Idris — four years old, solemn as a magistrate — pushing his own bowl of soup across the table without being asked, because the stranger looked hungry and that was apparently the whole of the law as the boy understood it.

That one. That one he'd keep. They could have the name, the birthdate, the degrees, the three years of cowardice and denial, the whole sad ledger of Rafael Kavanagh, folklorist, fool, tenant of other people's mercy. But not the soup.

"I've picked it," he said.

Marta nodded slowly, and her eyes went bright, and she turned quickly to the sink and began washing dishes that were already clean.

* * *

The nights were harder.

Because the voice was still there — the second voice, the one the equipment had caught years ago answering his sleep-mumbled questions, the one that had learned his cadences, his pauses, his habit of saying *right, yes, okay* when it was buying time to think. Lately it spoke less. Lately it mostly listened, which was somehow worse, like being observed by a mirror with opinions.

On the thirteenth, practicing the surrender of his university years, he fell asleep mid-exercise with the recorder running, and woke at dawn to find the machine had caught something he needed to hear twice.

His own sleeping voice, asking the questions it always asked — *what do you want, why are you here, are you me* — and then the second voice, patient, familiar, replying:

*"You're doing well. Better than the last one. He fought the forgetting until the end, and so the count took him ragged. You're letting go clean. Right, yes, okay — see? Even that. I have all of you. Every habit. When they sing the verse, there will be nothing left in the shape of you for the count to grab. You'll slip through like water through a net."*

A pause, the length of a breath.

*"One thing, though. Keep the soup."*

Rafael sat on the edge of the bed with the headphones around his neck, cold all over, laughing — actually laughing, a cracked and startled sound — because the thing wearing his voice in the dark had just given him the same advice as Marta, and he genuinely could not tell whether that meant it was on his side, or whether it was simply, faithfully, completely him.

Both, probably. That was the trap and the comfort of it.

* * *

By the fifteenth, the journal was mostly third person, and reading it had become a strange new pleasure, like receiving letters from a pen pal who happened to share his address.

*Rafael Kavanagh gave up the color of his first bicycle today, and the name of his primary school teacher, and the chorus of a song his father sang while shaving. He reports no pain. He reports, in fact, a lightness that alarms him more than grief would. He is beginning to suspect that a self is mostly repetition, and that if you remove the repetitions carefully enough, what remains is not a man but a doorway.*

*He misses things he can no longer name. This is the worst of it — not loss, but the echo of loss, grief with its object amputated. Yesterday he cried for four minutes over nothing, absolutely nothing, and had to ask his notes what he'd been crying about. The notes said: the apple tree. He does not remember any apple tree. He is crying about it anyway.*

*Marta made seed cake. He is keeping the recipe. Some things you write down not because you'll forget them but because you want proof you were the kind of man who bothered.*

He read that last line back and thought: whoever wrote that is someone I'd like. I hope he survives this. I hope somebody gets to be him.

Then he corrected himself — *I hope I get to be him* — and then uncorrected it, because the whole point, the entire terrible elegant point, was that soon there would be no difference between those two sentences. That was the door he was building. That was the trade.

Nine days until the harvest. Eight, now. Somewhere out in the dark fields, a boy was waiting to be counted back, and the only currency the count accepted was a name freely given, and Rafael Kavanagh — *he*, the man in the third person, the friendly stranger in his own handwriting — was spending his last week as himself learning, gently, patiently, scale by scale, how to stop.

That night he wrote one final entry in the first person. Deliberately. As a kind of signature.

*My name is Rafael Tomas Kavanagh. I was born in Cork. I study songs. I love a family that took me in for reasons that weren't kind, and stayed for reasons that were. Tomorrow I start giving the rest of it away. If you're reading this and you don't know who I am — good. That means it worked. Check on Idris for me. And tell Marta the seed cake was the best thing in the whole archive.*

He closed the book. In the walls, soft as a settling house, something exhaled — and for the first time in three years, Rafael heard the night voice say his name.

Not mockingly. Not hungrily.

Farewellingly.

*"Rafael,"* it said, tasting it, folding it, putting it away somewhere safe. *"Right, yes, okay. Ready when you are."*
