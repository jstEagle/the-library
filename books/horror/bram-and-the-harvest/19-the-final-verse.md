## 19. The Final Verse

The morning of the harvest dawned the color of weak tea, and Rafael lay in his narrow bed at the Oduyas' listening to Marta move through the kitchen below, and thought: *this is the last time I will hear her from this bed as a man with a name.*

He had practiced that sentence for three days now, and it still caught in his chest like a fishbone. Not because it frightened him—though God knew it did—but because underneath the fear there was something almost like relief, and he was ashamed of the relief. Three years of pretending not to hear. Three years of explaining away pipes and wind and bad wiring until his rationalizations had worn smooth as river stones. It would be good, some small traitorous part of him whispered, to stop pretending.

He got up before the light fully committed itself and dressed in the clothes Marta had laid out for him two nights ago, when they had all sat around the table and pretended this was an ordinary supper. The shirt was soft with washing. She'd pressed it. He'd noticed the creases down the sleeves and hadn't said anything, because if he said anything she would cry, and if she cried he might not be able to do what needed doing.

Downstairs, the kitchen smelled of cardamom and toast. Idris sat at the table with his chin propped on his fists, watching the field recorder Rafael had given him the night before. The boy had set it in the exact center of the table, equidistant from the marmalade and the salt, as though it were a guest of honor.

"It's loaded," Idris said. "I checked twice."

"Twice is thorough," Rafael said.

"Three times is paranoid."

"Three times is *surviving*, in this village." He ruffled the boy's hair on his way past, and Idris allowed it with the long-suffering dignity of a ten-year-old who has recently become important.

Marta stood at the stove with her back to the room. Rafael could read her shoulders the way he'd learned to read everything in Hollowmere—the tight line of them, the way she stirred what didn't need stirring. He came up beside her and touched her elbow.

"He'll be all right," he said quietly. "The count breaks, or it doesn't. Either way, the boy comes home. That's the shape of the bargain."

"You can't know that."

"No," Rafael agreed. "But I can believe it very loudly, and you can borrow my belief if yours runs low. That's what friends are for."

She laughed—a wet, broken sound—and turned and gripped his arms hard enough to bruise. "You stubborn, foolish, *wonderful* man. Three years I've watched you write your little notes and ask your little questions, and I kept telling myself he'll leave, he's clever, he'll see the edges of this place before it sees him. And instead you go and fall in love with us."

"Somebody had to," Rafael said. "You lot were too busy counting each other to count yourselves lucky."

That earned him a second laugh, half sob, and then Marta pressed her forehead to his chest for one long breath, and stepped back, and became practical, because that was how the Oduyas survived: one breath of grief at a time, rationed like sugar during a war.

"The gathering starts at dusk," she said. "Old Hollow field. You know where."

"I know where."

"Don't eat anything anyone hands you. Don't let them put anything in your hands except the song. And Rafael—" Her voice did something complicated. "When you step into the circle, don't look for me. Look at nothing. The song needs a hollow man, and you have to be hollow for exactly as long as it takes."

"Hollow," Rafael repeated. "Right. I've been practicing. Turns out three years of academic loneliness was excellent training."

* * *

The day passed the way days pass before executions and weddings: too fast and too slow at once, every hour wearing two watches. Rafael walked the village one last time, not because he needed anything but because he wanted to carry it with him, whatever fragment of it survived the forgetting. The crooked sign over the bakery. Mrs. Aldous's cat asleep in the wheelbarrow of the hardware shop, orange and boneless as a dropped scarf. The church with its archive room where he'd found his own name drying in fresh ink, and had run his thumb over it and felt, absurdly, flattered before he felt doomed.

People nodded to him as he passed. That was the horror of it, and the strange grace: they were kind. Since chapter nine of his life here—since the invitations began, the casseroles, the smiling faces at his window—they had been uniformly, unbearably friendly, and he understood now that their warmth was the warmth of people toward a lamb they'd grown fond of. But this morning it seemed to mean more than that. Old Tam Weller, who hadn't spoken to him in two years, stopped him by the well and pressed a boiled sweet into his hand and said, "For after," and walked on without explaining, and Rafael decided not to ask. Some gifts you take on faith.

At noon he went back to the Oduyas' and slept, or lay down and let sleep happen nearby, and dreamed of nothing at all, which he chose to interpret as mercy rather than rehearsal.

* * *

Dusk came down over Old Hollow field like a lid.

Rafael had expected torches—he supposed he'd read too many novels—but the villagers lit no fires. They simply gathered, forty or fifty of them, in a wide ring around the flattened circle of grass where the harvest song had been sung for longer than the church records reached back. The sky overhead was the deep bruised blue of October, and the first stars came out one at a time, cautious, like witnesses deciding whether to testify.

Marta stood at the ring's edge with her husband Emeka and did not look at Rafael, exactly as instructed, and so of course he looked at her, once, quickly, and stored her face somewhere he hoped the forgetting wouldn't find.

The singing began.

It started low, from the older villagers, the way fog starts—from everywhere at once. The harvest song he had recorded a hundred times, the census-song, every verse binding a name to the count. He heard names he knew: Aldous, Weller, Oduya, sung and answered, sung and answered, the call-and-response he'd once transcribed as "traditional antiphonal structure" in a paper that had gotten him politely rejected by two journals. Each name landed in the air and stayed there, visible almost, a thread of sound tying a person to the field, to the count, to the thing beneath the count that had been taking the unnumbered since before anyone wrote anything down.

And under the verses, woven through them, he heard Idris.

Not the boy himself. The boy was gone, taken three nights ago, and the empty chair at the rehearsal had sat unacknowledged while everyone sang around it. But his voice was *in* the song now, high and clear, threaded through the harmony like silver wire through grey cloth, and Rafael's whole body clenched hearing it. *They're using him,* he thought. *They're using my boy as thread.* And the anger that rose in him then was clean and useful, and he held onto it the way you hold a rope over a drop.

The verses went on. The stars multiplied. Somewhere in the dark beyond the ring, something breathed—not wind, not pipes, not bad wiring; it had never been any of those things—and the breathing kept time.

Then the song paused, the way a door pauses before it opens, and every face in the ring turned toward the gap where Rafael stood.

He walked into the circle.

His legs worked, which surprised him. The grass inside the ring was shorter, worn to velvet by centuries of feet, and cold through his shoes, and utterly ordinary, and that ordinariness steadied him more than any courage could have. This was just ground. This was just a song. Songs were his trade; he had spent his life learning other people's songs, and tonight, finally, he would give one back.

He closed his eyes. He made himself hollow, as instructed—which turned out to mean thinking of nothing except the melody, letting the years drain out through his heels into the patient grass.

And he sang the forbidden verse.

It came out of him rough at first, the 1911 wax-cylinder transcription he'd pried off the cracked cylinder with headphones and patience and a steady hand, the verse cut from the church books and remembered only by the dead. His voice cracked on the second line and he let it crack. Perfection wasn't required. Truth was required. The verse was short—that was the terrible elegance of it, four lines, the length of a lullaby—and it did not name the taker, and it did not name the taken. It offered.

*I am the number left outside the wall,*
*I am the verse unsung, the name uncalled,*
*Take mine to balance, take the whole of me,*
*And let the ledger close on what I was.*

The breathing beyond the ring stopped.

The silence that followed was so complete Rafael could hear his own heart, could hear, absurdly, a dog barking two villages over, the world going on its ordinary business while the field held its breath. Then the second voice spoke—not from beyond the ring anymore but from everywhere, from the ground, from his own mouth's memory, in his own cadence, with his own little habit of pausing before proper nouns.

*"You would trade," it said. "Freely?"*

"Freely," Rafael said. His voice sounded small and enormous at once. "Every note. Every year. The name my mother gave me and the ones I gave myself. Kavanagh. Rafael. The folklorist. The fool who took three years to listen. Take the lot, and strike the boy from your book, and let the count break open like a rotten barrel."

*"Names are heavy," the voice said, and it sounded—almost—sad. "Yours has been ripening three years. It will feed us through a hundred winters."*

"Then eat," Rafael said, "and choke."

He sang the verse again, louder, and this time the villagers joined him—all of them, the whole ring, Marta's voice rising over the rest like a gull over waves, and the song changed as it went, he could feel it changing, the threads of names snapping one by one, Aldous, Weller, Oduya, each name coming loose from the count like knots slipping, and the ledger—he couldn't see it but he felt it, felt it the way you feel weather change—*tearing.*

The second voice screamed.

It screamed in his cadence, with his mannerisms, screaming in first person, screaming *no, no, we had an arrangement, we had a* —and then it wasn't words anymore, just a sound like a page burning slowly from the corners inward, and the ring of villagers swayed as one, and the stars overhead blurred and doubled—

And the ledger burned blank mid-song, and the dark beyond the ring rushed inward like water finding a hole, and Rafael Kavanagh stood in the center of the circle with the last syllable dying in his throat and felt his name go with it—felt it leave the way a tooth leaves, sudden and socketed and gone—

The grass was cold. The stars were bright. Someone was crying, softly, joyfully, at the edge of the field.

And out of the dark beyond the ring, small and barefoot and three nights lost, a boy walked toward the fireless circle, blinking, alive, remembered by no one in the village except the woman whose arms opened for him—and by the man standing hollow in the center, who looked at Idris Oduya walking out of the dark and found, reaching for the boy's name, that the word was already gone from him, and reaching for his own, found only a warm and widening quiet—

—and in that quiet, faint as a star going out, one voice remained, asking kindly, in a cadence almost his own:

*"What shall we call you now?"*
