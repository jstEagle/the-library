## 5. Scratching at Three

Hazel Kavanagh believed in three things: salt, steel wool, and the camera.

Everything else—instinct, dread, the prickle at the back of the neck that older men in his trade called *the touch*—was noise. Noise could be filtered. Noise could be documented until it either resolved into something explicable or stopped mattering. He had spent nineteen years turning fear into paperwork, and paperwork had never once lied to him.

So when he decided to stake out St. Brannoc's through the night, he did it the way he did everything: methodically, redundantly, and with more equipment than any reasonable person would call sane.

He arrived at half past nine, while the last light was still bleeding out of the west and the village of Hollowmere had already begun its long exhale into sleep. The church sat on its low rise above the village like a patient animal, hip-deep in yew shadow, its tower black against a sky going the colour of a bruise healing. No bell hung in that tower—he'd confirmed it from three angles—but the belfry louvres stood open to the night like slack mouths, and looking at them too long gave Hazel the peculiar impression of being listened to.

He shook that off. Impressions were noise.

He set up four cameras. The first covered the vestry wall where he'd found the handprint smear, mounted on a tripod inside the churchyard railings, infrared engaged. The second watched the crypt door on the north side, its iron hinges furred with lichen. The third he carried inside and positioned in the nave, aimed down the aisle toward the chancel steps, because whatever moved in the walls of a building eventually crossed open floor if you waited long enough. The fourth—the one he trusted most, an old battered unit he called the Widow because it had outlived three of its replacements—went on the tower stair itself, halfway up, pointed at the door to the ringing chamber.

Then he made his camp in the pew beneath the west gallery, far enough back that the cameras' little red eyes wouldn't bloom in his own footage, close enough to the tower door that he'd hear anything that came down it. He laid out his kit on the pew beside him with the tenderness other men reserved for sleeping children: thermal imager, sound-level meter, two recorders running off separate batteries, a thermos of coffee gone bitter hours ago, and his notebook, already ruled and dated, already headed in his small square handwriting:

*Night observation, St. Brannoc's. Observer: H. Kavanagh. Conditions: clear, wind SW light, temp falling.*

Underneath, as always, he wrote the sentence he wrote at the top of every job, the sentence that was as close to a prayer as he ever came:

*What is observed can be named. What is named can be dealt with.*

He settled in to wait.

* * *

The first hour was nothing but the church breathing.

Old buildings breathe; he knew this. Timber swells and shrinks, stone gives up the day's heat in slow ticks, lead flashing creaks on the roof ridges. He catalogued each sound as it came, giving it its proper name so it couldn't frighten him under some other name later. *Cooling flue, south side. Rook shifting on the tower ledge. Water in a downpipe, moving once and settling.* By eleven o'clock he had filled two pages with the ordinary vocabulary of an old church at night, and the exercise had done what it always did: sanded the edges off his unease.

The village outside was quieter than any village he'd worked. No dogs barking at nothing, no late car, no television glow in a window. Hollowmere didn't go to bed so much as it held still, the way a field holds still when there's a hawk overhead. He'd noticed it over the past days without letting himself name it, and now, sitting alone in the dark with his instruments, he let himself name it at last in the notebook:

*Village exhibits collective avoidance behaviour re: church after dark. Not superstition-as-decoration. Avoidance-as-discipline. Learned.*

He underlined *learned* twice.

At midnight he drank the coffee and regretted it. At one he walked the circuit, torch low, checking the cameras. The vestry wall showed him nothing new in the infrared—just the plaster's pale expanse and the faint ghost of the smear he'd photographed days ago, which the lab people were still failing to explain to his satisfaction. The crypt door was shut. The nave was empty. On the Widow's screen, the tower stair climbed away into darkness, stone steps worn into shallow smiles by centuries of feet.

He went back to his pew. He set his phone alarm for 3:00 a.m.—not because he expected to sleep, but because 3:00 a.m. was the hour the previous tenant of this problem had mentioned, in the way people mention things they've decided not to think about. *It's worst around three,* Reverend Marsh had said, and then had looked immediately ashamed of having said it, as though she'd broken a rule she'd promised someone, long ago, to keep.

Hazel did not believe in witching hours. But he believed in patterns, and patterns needed their boundaries marked.

* * *

At 2:40, the rats stopped.

That was the thing he would fix on afterwards, the detail he returned to again and again in the notebook, because it was the first hard fact of the night and hard facts were his currency. For the past three nights he had heard them—rats or something rat-adjacent moving in the wall cavities, a dry scurrying traffic behind the plaster, constant as rain. Pest controllers learn the sound the way sailors learn weather. It meant vermin, and vermin meant work, and work meant the world was proceeding normally.

At 2:40 the scurrying ceased. All of it. Everywhere. As though a hand had closed over the whole building's small nervous system.

He wrote the time down. His pen made a louder sound than he liked.

For twenty minutes the silence had texture. It pressed against the eardrums the way deep water does. Hazel found himself breathing through his mouth and made himself stop, made himself sit straight, made himself recite the inventory of his equipment aloud in a whisper—*thermal imager, sound meter, recorder one, recorder two, torch primary, torch backup, salt, twelve feet*—because procedure was a rope and he intended to hold it.

At 3:04, the scratching began.

It came from below. That was the first wrong thing, and he noted it with a hand that wanted very much not to be steady and was steady anyway, because his hands had been trained by nineteen years of not being allowed to shake. The scratching came from *below*—from behind the chancel steps, from down where the floor dipped toward the crypt—and it was not rat-scratching. Rat claws are quick, intermittent, businesslike. This was slow. Deliberate. A rhythm of five strokes and a pause, five strokes and a pause, like something counting, or like something dragging itself along by fingers rather than scuttling on toes.

Five strokes. Pause. Five strokes. Pause.

He switched on the thermal imager and swept the chancel. Nothing. Cold stone, cold wood, the ambient blue-grey of a building holding its temperature against the night. Whatever scratched, it gave off no heat, and he wrote that down too, and the writing of it made it real in a way he didn't enjoy.

*3:07. Source has moved. Now behind north wall, adjacent to crypt stairs. Rate of travel approx. one metre per minute. Pattern unchanged.*

Five strokes. Pause. Five strokes. Pause.

*3:09. Source ascending. Crypt stairs.*

Ascending. He stared at the word he'd just written. Things in walls went down, ordinarily. Things in walls fled from light and noise and the pressure of human attention. They did not climb toward the place where a man sat waiting with cameras, unless they didn't know he was there—or unless they didn't care.

Or unless they'd known all along, and had simply been waiting for the hour.

He killed that thought, documented it instead: *Observer experiencing anticipatory anxiety disproportionate to stimulus. Note for review.* The note was a lie and he knew it was a lie, because no stimulus-disproportion existed. The anxiety was exactly proportionate. Something was climbing the inside of his church, slowly, patiently, counting its way up in fives, and every professional instinct he owned was screaming at him in a voice he refused to transcribe.

The scratching reached the top of the crypt stairs at 3:10.

He knew because the sound changed character—it left the muffled world of stone and entered the hollow resonance of the nave's timber, and suddenly it was *in the room with him*, somewhere back beyond the chancel screen, moving along the floor's edge toward the tower. He raised the thermal imager again and held it on the darkness past the screen, and the darkness stayed dark, and the sound kept coming, unhurried, five and pause, five and pause, and the pause was the worst part, because in the pause he could hear something else underneath it—a wet, soft sound, like skin lifting from stone, like knuckles flexing inside a glove of gristle.

His mouth had gone dry around the taste of old coffee. His heart was doing its work loudly and he resented it, resented the body's insistence on fear, resented the way his breath had shortened. He made himself write: *3:11. Source at tower door.*

The tower door was eight feet from his pew.

On the Widow's screen, mounted halfway up the stair, he could see the bottom edge of that door in grainy monochrome. It stood shut. It had stood shut since he'd checked it at one o'clock. And now, watching the little grey rectangle of it, he saw the latch move.

Not violently. Not with any drama. The latch simply lifted, the way a latch lifts when a hand that knows doors takes hold of it, and the door swung inward on hinges that did not creak—they should have creaked; he'd oiled nothing; they should have screamed—and the darkness of the stairwell received whatever came through it, and the Widow recorded forty seconds of static.

He watched it happen on the monitor and heard none of it live. That was the detail that finally cracked something in his composure, though his face never moved: the door opened in total silence, and simultaneously every speaker and earpiece he possessed filled with white roar, a blizzard of signal, forty seconds of it, timestamped 3:11:02 to 3:11:42, and buried in the static—not beneath it, *inside* it, woven through it like a thread through cloth—was a sound he would spend the rest of this case trying not to hear in memory.

Wet knuckles flexing. Close. Very close. Flexing one finger at a time, the way a man cracks his hand before work.

Then the static cut off mid-breath, and the church was silent, and the door stood open onto the stair, and the scratching had stopped because the scratching was done. The thing was no longer in the walls.

It was in the tower. Above him. Directly above him, somewhere in the dark throat of the stair, and it had passed within arm's reach of his pew to get there, and he had neither seen nor heard it except through a machine.

Hazel Kavanagh sat very still for a long time.

* * *

Dawn was still two hours off when he finally moved. He gathered his equipment with hands that obeyed him, packed with the economy of long habit, and then he did the thing he had never once done in nineteen years of pest control, in hundreds of lofts and cellars and wall voids across two counties.

He looked up at the tower stair, into the open dark of it, and he spoke aloud.

"I know you're there," he said. His voice sounded absurd in the empty nave, flat and mortal. "I don't know what you are yet. But I'm going to find out, and I'm going to write it down."

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. But as he turned to go, the thermal imager, slung at his side and still running, chimed softly—the alert he'd set for heat signatures above ambient.

He looked at the screen. In the tower doorway, at head height, exactly where a face would be if the dark had one, was a single point of warmth. Small. Child-sized. Fading even as he watched, like a handprint cooling on glass.

By the time he raised the imager properly, it was gone.

Hazel walked out into the churchyard, where the east was only beginning to consider greying, and stood among the leaning stones with his breath smoking, and understood—with the calm that arrives on the far side of dread, the calm that is dread's final form—that the question had changed. Three nights ago the question had been *what is in the walls*. An hour ago it had been *what opened the door*.

Now, standing in the cold with forty seconds of static on a memory card and a child-sized warmth fading in a doorway, the question was simpler and infinitely worse:

*Why had it shown itself to the one man in Hollowmere who writes everything down?*

He got into his van. He did not start the engine. He sat with the notebook on his knee and, in his small square handwriting, beneath the night's accumulated data, he wrote the entry he would carry with him into everything that followed:

*3:11–3:12. Subject ascended voluntarily. Subject revealed itself selectively—to instrumentation, not to observer. Conclusion: it wanted to be recorded.*

He underlined it. Then, because the truth had a momentum now that he could feel pulling at him like a tide, he added one more line, the kind of line he'd have mocked in another man's report:

*It knows I document. I think that's why I was hired.*

Outside, the first rooks woke in the yews, and somewhere below the church floor, deeper than the crypt, deeper than the foundations, something that had waited a hundred years beneath the stone shifted its weight—and Hazel, who trusted only what he could document, drove back to his hotel to develop photographs of a door that had opened without a sound, knowing that tomorrow the Founders of Hollowmere would come for him, and that no amount of triple fees would buy what they actually wanted.

They didn't want him gone.

They wanted him *invited*.
