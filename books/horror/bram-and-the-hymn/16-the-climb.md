## 16. The Climb

The tower door did not creak. That was the first wrong thing.

A door locked for a hundred years should have screamed on its hinges, should have shed rust in orange flakes, should have resisted the way old things resist — out of habit, out of dignity. Instead it swung inward at the weight of my hand with a smoothness that felt rehearsed, oiled by expectation, as though it had been waiting behind its own wood for exactly this moment and had practiced the opening in the dark.

I stood on the threshold and let the recorder run.

"Six fifty-two p.m.," I said. "Hazel Kavanagh. Entering the tower of St. Brannoc's. Moonrise at eight forty-two. Sunrise at six fifty-eight. That's ten hours and six minutes." I said it the way I say everything that matters: flatly, precisely, so the words would hold their shape later when I needed them to. "I am going up. I am going to read the charter at the top. If anyone finds this recording, the facts are as follows—"

The dark inside the doorway breathed.

Not wind. Wind comes and goes, has texture, has direction. This was a single exhalation from somewhere above me, long and slow, drawn through stone the way breath is drawn through teeth, and it carried a smell I will spend the rest of my life trying not to remember: cold bronze, wet plaster, and underneath it something organic and patient, like the inside of a closed fist.

I stepped through anyway. The door closed behind me without being touched.

* * *

The stair began where stairs begin and then stopped obeying any logic I respected. It spiraled counterclockwise, which is correct for a defensive tower — gives the ascending swordsman the wall at his back — but this was never a defensive tower; it was a parish belfry in a farming village, and there was no reason for it except that whoever built it understood, in their bones, what the tower was for. Who it was built to receive.

The walls were close. My shoulders brushed stone on both sides if I lifted my arms, and the stone was not cold. That was the second wrong thing. Stone in an unheated tower in October should be clammy, grave-cold, the temperature of held breath. This stone was warm. Blood-warm. Warm the way the flank of an animal is warm, and faintly — so faintly I told myself I imagined it — it moved. Not visibly. Not enough to see. Enough to feel against my fingertips when I trailed my hand along it, which I did because trailing my hand along surfaces is documentation too, the oldest kind, the kind you do with your skin.

"Step one," I said aloud.

My voice fell into the spiral and came back changed. Flattened. Swallowed at the edges, as if the tower were tasting it before deciding whether to return it.

"Step two."

I counted because Edith Pryce had told me to count. *It likes a ringer who keeps time,* she'd said, pressing her grandmother's ledger into my hands on the churchyard path an hour ago, her fingers blue with cold and shaking. *Your grandfather kept time. They all kept time. Count your steps, Hazel. Count them out loud. As long as you're keeping the beat, you're ringing. And while you're ringing, you're not being rung.*

I didn't fully understand that. I understood it the way you understand a rope bridge over a gorge: not by analysis but by the absolute refusal to look down.

"Step three. Step four."

The light died fast. By step twenty I was working off the torch on my phone, its beam a small yellow coin of world that moved with me and showed me only what was directly in front of my boots: worn treads, each one dished in the center, hollowed by feet. A century of feet. More than a century. The wear went deep — deeper than foot traffic explains — and when I bent to photograph tread thirty-one, the flash lit up the riser above it and showed me the names.

They were scratched into the stone. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Small letters, uneven, some cut with fingernails rather than tools, rising in columns up the curved wall like ivy grown vertical. I put my light close and read them one by one, and my voice, which had been steady for thirty steps, developed a hairline crack.

"Aldous Vane," I read. "Mercy Vane. Thomas Vane. Edith Pryce. Edith Pryce. Edith Pryce—" the same name repeated down a column nine times, in a child's hand growing gradually into an adult's, a signature aging across decades on the same stretch of wall. "Wilfred Marsh. Wilfred Marsh. Wilfred Marsh."

Marsh. Ilsa's father. Taken in 1961. He had climbed these stairs and he had written his name on the way up, and beneath his name someone else had written, in letters pressed so hard they'd fractured the stone: *NOT FAIR NOT FAIR NOT FAIR*

I photographed all of it. Flash after flash after flash, the little room of stair lighting up white and going black again, white and black, and somewhere in the rhythm of it I realized the flashes had become part of my counting, my metronome, my hymn, and that this was fine, this was good, ritual is ritual whether it's prayer or procedure, and I had never in my life confused the two until tonight.

"Step forty-four," I said. "Documenting inscriptions. Names of prior ringers. Continuing ascent."

And the wall answered me.

*Step forty-four,* said the stone, in no voice at all — in pressure, in vibration, in a tremor that passed through my palm where it lay flat against the masonry and resolved itself, horribly, into syllables. Not mimicry. Emphasis. Agreement. The tower knew what step we were on. The tower had been counting too.

I took my hand off the wall.

* * *

By step sixty the handprints started.

They were small. Child-sized, five-fingered, pressed into the plaster that lined the inner curve of the wall in patches, and every one of them was the same hand — I could tell by the crooked index finger, the one that hooked slightly inward, a detail I'd already seen once before, in dust, on the crypt floor around the sunken bell. Tomas Pryce's hands. Dozens of them. Then hundreds. They climbed the wall beside me the whole way up, overlapping, layered, some faded to ghosts and some sharp as yesterday, a frieze of one boy reaching upward forever, and the terrible thing — the thing I made myself say into the recorder because saying it made it a fact and facts are load-bearing — the terrible thing was that they weren't prints of hands pressed in fear or pressed in struggle.

They were prints of hands braced for climbing.

He went up and up and up, over and over, year after year, decade after decade, a dead boy climbing toward a bell that wasn't there anymore, and every print said the same thing: *almost. almost. almost.*

"Tomas," I said, and the whole tower went still.

Not quiet — still. There is a difference. Quiet is absence of sound. Still is presence of attention. Every stone, every print, every inch of the dark above me and below me gathered itself around that one spoken name the way water gathers around a dropped stone, and for three full seconds — I know because I timed it, because timing is what I do, because measurement is my rosary — nothing in the world moved except my own heart, hammering out a count of its own.

Then, far above, something flexed.

Wet knuckles. That was the phrase from the camera footage, the forty seconds of static, and it was the right phrase and I hated that it was the right phrase: a sound like knuckles flexing inside a wet leather glove, slow, deliberate, joint by joint, the sound of a hand remembering how to be a hand. It came from directly overhead. From the top of the stair. From the belfry.

It had been above me the entire climb.

* * *

Here is what I know about panic, professionally speaking. Panic is a failure of documentation. When the mind can't record what's happening, it stops trying to record anything, and an unrecorded moment is a moment you don't survive — not literally, necessarily, but the self that enters it doesn't come out. So I have trained myself, over twenty years of attics and crawlspaces and wasp-nest eaves, to do the opposite of panic: to narrate. To turn terror into transcript. If I am describing it, I am outside it. If I am outside it, I am alive.

So I described it.

"Step seventy-three," I said, and my voice sounded like someone else's, someone competent, someone calm, and I blessed that stranger and kept him talking. "Something is ascending ahead of me. Or descending. Or stationary at the summit. Sound signature consistent with previous recordings. I am continuing. The charter is in my left jacket pocket. The ledger pages are in my right. The lab report is folded with them. Sunrise at six fifty-eight. Ten hours and two minutes remain. Step seventy-four."

The breathing started at step eighty.

It came through the walls themselves — not from the stairwell air but from inside the stone, a slow tidal respiration, inhale-exhale, inhale-exhale, and it matched my pace. When I stopped to photograph a cluster of names, it stopped. When I resumed, it resumed. One breath per flight. One breath per flight. I tested it twice, stopping abruptly mid-stride, and both times the tower held its breath with me, patiently, the way a parent holds still during a child's game of statues, and there was something in that patience worse than menace. Menace wants to hurt you. Patience wants to keep you.

"Step ninety-six," I said. "It is breathing with me. It is matching my pace. Note for the record: I am not afraid."

That was a lie and the tower knew it was a lie, and the exhale that followed was long and soft and almost tender, the way you'd soothe a frightened animal you intended to keep.

* * *

At step one hundred and eleven I found the older rings of wear.

The flashlight caught them low on the outer wall, beneath the newer scratches: concentric grooves worn into the stone at knee height, hip height, shoulder height — bands, polished smooth, circling the entire shaft of the stair. I ran my fingers over them. They were old. Older than the names. Older than 1911. Some of them, I understood with a lurch, predated the tower's plaster, predated its paint, maybe predated its roof — wear laid down in rings like the growth rings of a tree, each band a season, each season a harvest, each harvest a name.

This stair had been climbed before there was a bell to ring. Before there was a Tomas. The covenant was older than its paperwork. The paperwork was just the latest handwriting on a very old wall.

"How many," I whispered, and then caught myself and said it properly, for the record: "How many ringers have climbed this stair?"

The breathing paused. And then, from everywhere at once — from the stones, from the prints, from the dark coiled at the top of the spiral — came a sound I had not heard it make before. A counting sound. A tapping. Fingertips on the inside of the masonry, one-two-three, one-two-three, unhurried, going on and on past any number I could track, past a hundred, past two hundred, patient as sediment, and I put my hand over my mouth and made myself stop listening before the number finished, because I understood suddenly that the number would not finish. That it had never finished. That the counting was the tower, and the tower was the counting, and every name in Edith's ledger was one tap among thousands.

I climbed faster. The breathing matched me, faster. I slowed. It slowed. There was no outrunning a thing that wore the walls, and besides — I reminded myself, step by step, word by word — I was not running from it. I was walking toward it. That was the whole plan. Walk toward it, stand under it, open my mouth, and read.

Facts are load-bearing. Tonight I would find out how much weight they hold.

* * *

Step one hundred and forty. The air changed.

It thickened. It acquired weight and grain, a texture like standing inside a struck note, and I could feel it against my face the way you feel the approach of weather. Somewhere above me, in the belfry where no bell hung, the air was already tolling — a pressure wave too low to hear, rolling out in slow cycles, and each cycle pressed against my chest and my eardrums and the fluid of my spine until my whole body was a resonating chamber, tuned, claimed, played.

The scratching started then. Directly overhead. Following me. For the last hundred steps it had breathed with me; now it moved with me, claws or knuckles or fingers dragging through the fabric of the tower itself, one revolution of the spiral for every revolution of mine, always exactly one turn above, always exactly out of sight, and I understood finally that it wasn't hunting me and it wasn't herding me.

It was escorting me.

The way you'd escort a guest. The way you'd walk someone to the door. The way, on a night a hundred years ago, the founding families of Hollowmere must have walked a small boy with a crooked index finger to the foot of these stairs, and patted his head, and told him to go up and ring the bell, and watched him climb, and listened to him climb, and heard him reach the top.

And then heard the bell.

I stopped. One turn below the summit, the last tread before the belfry arch glowing faintly in my torchlight, moonlit air visible beyond it. Above me the movement ceased. The tower held its breath. Everything waited — the walls, the prints, the thousand names, the patient breathing thing wearing the stone — everything waited for me to take the final step, and I stood there with my heart slamming and my recorder running and I said, clearly, in the flattest voice I owned:

"Hazel Kavanagh. Step one hundred and forty-one. Final step. Time: seven fifty-one p.m. Fifty-one minutes until moonrise. For the record—" and here my voice did break, just once, a clean fracture down the middle, "—for the record, whatever is up there, it has been climbing beside me since the door. It knows my name. It wrote my name in a ledger before I ever came to Hollowmere. And I want it known that I am not its ringer. I am its witness. Those are not the same thing."

The silence that followed had a quality I can only call consideration.

Then, from the top of the stair, soft as a child asking to be found: it knocked.

Three times. On the inside of the world.

And the belfry arch ahead of me filled, edge to edge, with pale moonlight that had no business existing yet — the moon still forty minutes below the ridge — and in that impossible light I saw the last thing I saw before I climbed into it: small handprints on the archway stone, fresh, glistening, still wet, arranged in a neat ascending row like an invitation.

Like a hand offered downward, to help me up.

I took the step.
