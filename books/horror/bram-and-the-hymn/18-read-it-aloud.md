## 18. Read It Aloud

The tolling had a texture. That was the thing no one had ever told him, because no one had ever lived to describe it. It wasn't sound so much as pressure—a slow, wet percussion that came not from the empty belfry above him but from the air itself, from the stones, from the marrow of the tower, as though the whole structure were a throat and something inside it was learning to speak.

Hazel Kavanagh pressed his back against the parapet wall and did what he always did when the world came apart at the seams.

He checked his equipment.

Recorder: running. Battery: ninety-one percent. Documents: in the waterproof sleeve against his chest, where his heartbeat could feel them like a second pulse. Salt line at his feet, broken now—broken by the reaching—but he didn't look down at it. Looking down meant looking at the place where a child should have been standing and wasn't.

The presence hung in the belfry's open heart, framed by the louvres and the rising harvest moon, and it was shaped like an absence. That was the only way his mind would permit him to see it: a Tomas-shaped hole in the moonlight, roughly four feet of nothing, edged with a faint shimmer like heat off summer tarmac. Where its hands should have been, there were hands—or the idea of hands, small ones, mapped entirely in whorls and ridges, hundreds of overlapping fingerprints pressed into the dark like leaves laminated one atop another until they became a surface.

It reached for him.

Not fast. Not violent. The way a child reaches for a parent across a crowded room—certain, patient, terribly sure of being wanted.

"Stay," said the tower.

Or rather: the tolling bent itself around the shape of the word, the way water bends around a stone, and Hazel understood it anyway. *Stay. Ring. Be the hand. Be the name in the book.*

"No," Hazel said aloud, because saying things aloud was about to become the whole of his religion.

The reaching paused. The air tightened.

He had done the arithmetic on the stair—six hundred and eleven steps, each one counted and named, each one narrated into the recorder while something climbed beside him inside the walls, matching his pace, breathing through the mortar with a sound like wet knuckles flexing. He had held himself together with procedure all the way up. Procedure was his prayer. But procedure had run out of steps, and now there was nothing left between him and the reaching except the one weapon he'd carried since the beginning, the one thing he'd been building toward without fully knowing it:

Documentation.

His hands were shaking. He noted that too, out loud, because noting things out loud was how he kept them real. "Hands exhibiting tremor consistent with acute stress response. Subject remains functional." His voice sounded thin and absurd in the vast stone throat of the belfry. Good. Let it be absurd. Absurd was human. Absurd was alive.

He drew out the first document.

* * *

The charter was a single sheet of vellum, soft as cloth from a century of hiding, and Reverend Marsh's hands had trembled when she gave it to him just after sunset, in the vestry that smelled of candle wax and old fear.

"Read it," she had said. "Where it can hear you. I never could. Fifty years I've stood in this church and I couldn't say it out loud. My father's name is in that ledger, Mr. Kavanagh. Struck through. And I have spent my whole life with it lodged in my throat like a stone." Her eyes had been dry in the terrible way of eyes that cried themselves empty decades ago. "You're the ringer now. Not to ring the bell. To ring the truth."

So Hazel rang it.

He unfolded the vellum and held it up—not to the absence, exactly, but to the space it occupied, the way you hold a document to the light—and he read.

"'Charter of the Bell of Saint Brannoc, recast in the year of our Lord nineteen hundred and eleven.'" His voice cracked on the first sentence and he let it crack; cracked notes were still notes. "'Be it recorded that the bell known as Old Brannoc, cast in seventeen forty-three, having been condemned following the death of the child Tomas Pryce, is hereby melted and recast into a new bell, which shall not be hung, but given—'"

The word landed in the belfry like a dropped stone.

"*Given.* Not sold. Not buried. Given." Hazel's voice found its footing and leaned into it, the way he'd learned to lean into wind on exposed rooftops. "'Given back to that which received him, in perpetual satisfaction, by the undersigned founders of this parish: Aldous Vane. Margaret Pryce. Cornelius Marsh. Edith's grandmother's own hand, here, third signature down. Six names. Six families. One child.'"

The tolling faltered.

It was small, that falter—the way a heartbeat stutters when a name is spoken in a sleeping house—but Hazel felt it in his teeth, in the fillings, in the floor of his skull. The reaching hand stopped mid-air. The fingerprints along its edge rippled, rearranging, like a crowd turning to look.

"It wasn't a drowning," Hazel said, and now his voice was steadier, because this part was the lab report, and the lab report was bedrock. He pulled the printed pages from the sleeve. "I'm going to read the findings now. Forensic analysis, samples taken from the grease-smear on the vestry wall, north side, three feet above the floor. You left it there. A hundred and thirteen years ago, you put your hand on that wall, and nobody ever cleaned it, and last month I photographed it, and last week a laboratory told me what it was."

The air had gone very still. Even the moonlight seemed to lean in.

"'Prints are human. Adult-sized palm, juvenile finger length, consistent with a child of approximately nine years. Ridge detail intact. Oxidation of the lipid residue dates deposition to circa 1911, plus or minus five years.'" He looked up from the page. "Do you understand what that means? It means you've been standing in that vestry the whole time. All these years. With your hand on the wall. Waiting for someone to come and measure it. Waiting for someone to *write it down.*"

The absence made a sound.

It was not the tolling. It was smaller than the tolling, thinner, a note caught halfway between a bell and a boy—a sound like a breath drawn in 1911 and held ever since, finally beginning to shake.

Hazel's chest hurt. He catalogued the pain—stress-induced, non-cardiac, keep going—and kept going.

* * *

"The ledger next."

Edith Pryce's ledger, her grandmother's ledger, its pages soft as moth wings, every entry in faded iron-gall ink. He'd photographed every page. Now he read them, and his voice carried down through the louvres and out over Hollowmere, over the churchyard where the Founders stood assembled in their covenant-locked procession, whether they willed it or no. He didn't know if they could hear him. He read as if they could. He read as if the whole village were one ear pressed to the tower wall.

"Harvest, nineteen twelve. 'Invited up: Samuel Vane, wheelwright.' Struck through." His voice flattened into the cadence of record-keeping, and the flatness was mercy—it let him say the unsayable. "Harvest, nineteen fifteen. 'Invited up: Peter Marsh, shepherd.' Struck through. Nineteen twenty-one. 'Invited up: Thomas Vane, aged sixteen.' Struck through. Nineteen thirty-four. 'Invited up: Robert Pryce.'"

Each struck-through name fell into the dark like a coin into a well, and each one took longer to hit bottom.

"Nineteen sixty-one." His throat closed. He forced it open. This one mattered more than the others, though he couldn't have said why until he said it. "'Invited up: Gerald Marsh, verger, aged thirty-four. Father of one daughter, Ilsa, aged six.'" He lowered the page. "She remembers you," he said to the darkness, to the absence, to whatever was listening behind the listening. "She's spent fifty years unable to say your name or his. She sent me up here with the charter in my coat. Whatever happens tonight—that's not nothing. That's a woman unclenching her jaw after half a century. You did that. Being heard does that."

The reaching hand had lowered. Not withdrawn—lowered, the way a dog lowers its head when a voice turns gentle.

And Hazel felt the horror in the room change key.

Because it had never been hunger, not exactly. Or rather—it was hunger, but hunger of a specific and terrible kind. The Founders had thought they were feeding a monster. They had brought it ringers the way you bring offerings to something in the dark, and the thing in the dark had accepted them, because a child alone in the bronze for a hundred years will accept anything that comes up the stairs. It wanted a ringer because a ringer stays. Because a ringer holds the rope. Because a ringer, in the end, is someone who *stays with you.*

"You were nine," Hazel said quietly. "Tomas Pryce. They told the county you drowned in the mill race. They told each other the bell tolled on its own and called you to the water. But the charter says otherwise, doesn't it? 'Given, not lost.' Four words. Your mother signed it. Can you imagine—" His voice broke clean through and he let it. "Can you imagine signing that? Putting your own child into the fire and calling it satisfaction?"

The tolling had stopped entirely now. The silence was worse and better at once—an opened wound, air on raw nerve.

"So here's what happened. Here's the truth, and I'm going to say every fact of it out loud, because facts are the only sacrament I know."

* * *

He read the coroner's fragment. He read the parish burial register—the entry for Tomas Pryce, dated October 1911, cause of death left blank, the ink of the blank heavier than the ink of the name, as if the vicar had pressed hard enough to write something and then chosen emptiness instead. He read Edith's testimony, transcribed in his own careful hand: *"Grandmother said the blight took everything two summers running, and old Aldous Vane said the bell knew how to stop it, and she never forgave herself for believing him, and she wrote the ledger till her hands gave out so that someone, someday, would count the cost."*

He read the metallurgical analysis of the bronze ring fragments the Founders wore: composition consistent with the 1743 casting, trace isotopes matching the crypt bell, proof that the metal had never left, that the melting had been no burial at all but a feeding, a distribution, six families carrying pieces of their sin on their fingers for a century like wedding bands.

And with each document, each fact laid out in the open air of the belfry, the absence changed. It was hard to describe even to himself, and he was recording himself describing it, so he tried: it was as if the fingerprints were being read. As if each documented fact ran over those ridges like a stylus, and the dark grooves of a century began, slowly, to make music instead of menace.

The shimmer at its edges softened. The Tomas-shaped hole in the moonlight grew less like a wound and more like a boy standing very still, listening to someone finally tell the story right.

"They melted you into a bell and then they were too afraid to hang it," Hazel said. "So they buried you under the floor and walked circles around you for a hundred years, wearing you on their fingers, feeding you strangers, and calling it an inheritance." He folded the charter closed, gently, the way you close a hymnal. "That's not a covenant. That's a crime scene. And I'm the pest controller, Tomas. Do you know what I do? I find what's infesting a building, and I identify it, and I name it, and I write the report."

He raised his recorder higher, as if presenting evidence.

"And the report says: the infestation at St. Brannoc's is guilt. One hundred and thirteen years old. Six families. Fully documented."

The silence that followed was the deepest he had ever stood inside. It had weight. It had temperature. Somewhere far below, through six hundred and eleven steps of stone, he thought he heard the churchyard shift—the creak of old knees kneeling, or running, or both—and a single voice, thin with age and fury, screaming something about renewal, about the covenant, about what could still be done before dawn.

Aldous Vane. Still fighting the light.

But up here, in the belfry, the absence did not renew anything. It stood in the moonlight with its thousand-fingered hands hanging quiet at its sides, and slowly—so slowly Hazel might have imagined it, and he noted the uncertainty aloud for the record, time-stamped, honest to the last—it turned its faceless face toward the east, where the sky over the ridge had begun, almost imperceptibly, to think about grey.

"Listen," Hazel whispered, and it wasn't procedure anymore, and it wasn't documentation, and it wasn't prayer either, or maybe it was all three fused into one thing with no name yet. "Sunrise is at 6:58. You don't have to take anyone up. You never had to. You just had to be *counted.*"

The eastern sky paled by a degree.

And far below, in the dark of the churchyard, the covenant began—link by link, name by name—to snap.
