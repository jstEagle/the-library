## 22. The Hymn

Three weeks after the tower fell silent, Hazel Kavanagh drove out of Hollowmere for the last time with his windows down and his recorder in the passenger seat, switched off.

He had never been a man who left things switched off. For thirty-one years he had documented everything—the droppings and the smear-marks, the claw-grooves with their impossible fingerprints, the forty seconds of static, the wet flex of knuckles inside the plaster—and the documentation had been his liturgy, his rosary of receipts and timestamps, the small stubborn prayer he offered against a world that preferred to rot quietly behind its walls. But some things, he was learning, were not meant to be filed. Some things you carried instead, the way you carried a scar: not as evidence, but as testimony that you had been somewhere and come back.

The van was new. The old one had burned to its axles in the churchyard on the night before the harvest moon, and the insurance assessor had written *electrical fault* in the box marked cause, because that was the story everyone had agreed to tell. Hazel had let them tell it. There were enough true stories in Hollowmere now; they did not need another one circulating like a coin too hot to hold.

The village shrank in his mirrors. He watched it go the way you watch a fever break—the church tower last of all, grey against a sky rinsed clean by three weeks of rain, its belfry empty and open-arched and finally, finally just a hole in the air where bells are supposed to hang. Scaffolding hugged one flank of it. Marsh had told him the masons were coming from Exeter, proper men with proper drawings, to hang a new bell—a bell cast honestly, from honest bronze, with no child folded into it, no covenant annealed into the metal. She had asked him if he wanted his name read at the casting. He had said no. His name had been read into enough ledgers already.

*Given, not lost.* The phrase still surfaced sometimes, unbidden, the way a tune surfaces when your hands are busy. It had been the hinge of everything—the four words in the 1911 charter that turned a drowning into a donation, a murder into a maintenance schedule. He had read it aloud in the belfry until his voice cracked, and the dark had listened, and the listening had been the beginning of the letting-go.

He took the coast road rather than the moor road. Not because it was faster—it wasn't—but because he wanted the sea on his left, wide and indifferent and incapable of keeping secrets, and because the moor road passed the mill race, and he was not ready to look at the mill race again. Maybe he never would be. Tomas Pryce had gone into that water—or had seemed to go into it, according to the story the village told itself for a hundred years—and the truth was that the water had only ever been the stage dressing. The real going had been upward, into the tower, into the bronze, into the long dark patience of a thing that only wanted what any child wants: someone to stay, someone to say his name, someone to remember him out loud.

Hazel had stayed until sunrise. That was the whole of his heroism, if it could be called that—he had done his job, which was to document, and the documenting had happened to be lethal to the covenant. Six-fifty-eight in the morning, the first ringer in a hundred and thirteen years to walk down those stairs with the light coming up gold through the louvres, and Edith Pryce waiting at the bottom with her grandmother's ledger pressed to her chest like a shield she no longer needed, weeping without any sound at all.

He thought about her often now. More than he thought about the thing in the tower, actually. The thing had been terrible, but it had also been simple—a hunger, an ache, a small hand-shaped absence reaching out of the wall of the world. Edith was complicated. Edith had fed it names for eighty years by silence alone, inheriting the ledger at nineteen when her mother's hands shook too badly to write, entering each harvest's sacrifice in a copperplate hand and striking each name through when the moon came up full over the ridge. She had burned the ledger page by page in her grate the week after dawn, and she had told Hazel afterward that the smoke smelled like nothing at all, like paper, like just paper, and that this had made her cry harder than anything in her life.

Some inheritances must be sung out rather than buried. He didn't know yet, driving, that he was on his way to learn that. He only knew that the road was wet and shining, that the hedges ran with the small business of sparrows, that the sea kept flashing between stone walls like a card being dealt and withdrawn, dealt and withdrawn.

* * *

It was the schoolyard that stopped him.

He came around the shoulder of the hill above Porthollow—a village he'd never heard of, a scatter of whitewashed cottages and one chapel and a council school with a slate roof and a yard painted with hopscotch squares faded to ghosts—and the sound reached the car before the sight of it did. Children's voices. A dozen of them, maybe more, out for morning break, standing in a loose ring the way children do when a game has rules nobody remembers inventing.

They were singing.

Hazel braked without deciding to brake. His hands did it, and then his foot, and then the handbrake, and then he simply sat there in the ticking quiet of the cooling engine with his mouth slightly open, listening.

It was not a hymn he knew. He knew hymns the way most lapsed men know them—in fragments, in funerals, in the low drone of school assemblies decades gone—and this was none of the ones he knew. It had an old shape to it, modal and narrow, the kind of melody that gets passed hand to hand below the level of notation, and the children sang it unaccompanied, in the flat confident unison of children who have never once wondered where a song came from.

And the words—

The words went through him like cold water finding a crack.

*Given, not lost,* sang the children. *Given, not lost. The bell was melted but the metal kept. Toll for the little one, toll for the given, wake him gently, he has slept.*

His skin tightened from scalp to sole. For one vertiginous moment he was back in the belfry, back in the dark with the air tolling around him like the inside of a struck bell, back with the child-shaped absence reaching for him with fingers made entirely of fingerprints—and then the moment passed, because the morning held, because the sun was out, because the children were only children, swinging their arms, one of them turning a slow circle in the middle as though she were the needle of a compass finding something.

He got out of the car.

He didn't decide that either. Later he would understand that his body had understood first, the way it had understood at 3:11 a.m. in the vestry, the way it had understood on the stair when something climbed beside him matching his pace breath for breath. His body knew when a thing required witnessing. He crossed the road and stood at the low stone wall of the schoolyard, and he took out his phone—not to record, he realized; there was nothing in him that wanted to record this—but simply to have something in his hands, the way you carry a torch in a dark house even when the lights work.

The song went on. It had verses. Of course it had verses; it had had a hundred years to grow them, or longer—who knew how far back the melody went, whether it predated the bell, whether it had been waiting in the district all along for a bell to be cast so it could attach itself, the way ivy waits for a wall.

*Toll for the little one, given not lost,*
*the founders fed him and the founders paid,*
*say his name at the breaking of the day*
*and the bell need never be re-cast.*

There it was. *Say his name at the breaking of the day.* The charter's own grammar, the covenant's own arithmetic, worn smooth into playground rhythm the way the crypt floor had been worn smooth by a century of small circling feet. And the last verse—

*Say his name at the breaking of the day,*
*and the bell need never be re-cast.*

—that was new. That was *this month's* new. Because the bell *had* been recast, was being recast even now under proper masons' drawings in a foundry in Exeter, and the song had updated itself, the way songs do, the way living things do.

A woman in a waxed coat came out of the school door with a whistle on a lanyard and clapped her hands twice, and the ring broke up into running clusters, and the singing stopped mid-word, and the ordinary noise of a schoolyard rushed back in—shouting, laughter, a ball hitting brick, the small politics of the climbing frame. The girl who had been turning in the centre stopped turning. She looked directly at Hazel across the wall. Seven or eight years old, gap-toothed, hair cut in a blunt fringe, and for a moment—not a frightening moment, he would insist on that later, not frightening, only *attentive*—she looked at him the way you look at someone whose name you've just learned.

Then she ran off after the others, and the whistle blew again, and the morning closed over the whole thing like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

He found the headteacher inside, a tired man named Polkinghorne with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, who was happy enough to talk once Hazel mentioned he'd worked in Hollowmere—everyone, it turned out, had a cousin or a memory or a ghost story about Hollowmere; the village's reputation had spread like ink in water for a century, always diluted, never quite gone.

"The hymn?" Polkinghorne said, when Hazel asked. "Oh, that's been here forever. Well—" he corrected himself, "—a long time. The infants learn it in the autumn term. We don't teach it, exactly. They bring it up from the nursery, year after year. One cohort passes it to the next like a cold." He smiled, a bit helplessly. "Nobody knows who wrote it. I looked, when I first came. Asked the oldest families. The Vane family used to be here—moved away after the war, or after something—and the Pryces had a branch up this way once, I think. Farming folk." He shrugged. "It's a good tune. Keeps them calm in the winter term. Better than half the stuff in the hymn book, if I'm honest."

"Does anyone know what it means?" Hazel asked. "The words."

Polkinghorne considered. Outside the window, the children swarmed and shrieked in the sun. "I suppose I always assumed it meant what it says," he said. "That somebody was lost, and somebody was sorry, and saying their name was the fix." He rubbed his eyes under the glasses. "Which is more sense than most hymns make, when you come down to it."

Hazel thanked him and went out and stood by his car for a long time.

Because that was it. That was the whole of it, laid out plain by a tired man in a staff room that smelled of coffee and whiteboard markers. *Somebody was lost, and somebody was sorry, and saying their name was the fix.* A hundred years of horror in Hollowmere had been that sentence run backwards—run backwards through bronze and blood and ledger-ink, the fix withheld, the name unsaid, the sorrow compounded annually like debt. The Founders had known the fix the entire time. It was in their charter. It was in their children's mouths every autumn. All they ever had to do was sing it straight instead of crooked, say the name instead of feeding the silence, and the covenant would have snapped a century before Hazel Kavanagh ever got a callout about scratching in a vestry wall.

They hadn't sung it out. They had buried it. And what you bury does not die; it only learns to climb.

But the children—the children had kept the true version alive underneath, the way a seed keeps under snow. *Say his name at the breaking of the day.* They didn't know what they were carrying. They carried it anyway, faithfully, generation to generation, a pilot light burning under a hundred years of deliberate dark. And now the dark was lifted, and the name had been said—at the breaking of the day, at six-fifty-eight precisely, by a pest controller with a recorder and a charter and no other weapon—and the song was free to mean what it said.

Marsh had proposed melting the buried bell properly, with Tomas's name read into the record first, witnesses present, the whole truth spoken aloud over the crucible. Edith had burned the ledger. The Founders faced exposure—Vane screaming to the end that the covenant could be renewed, the rest of them grey-faced and hollowed, some kneeling in the churchyard grass while the truth rang down from their own tower. Justice outside the law, imperfect, unfinished. Hazel's final report sat archived in three places, addressed to no authority, because there was no authority for what had happened in Hollowmere except the human ear.

And now a schoolyard twenty miles away was singing the correction.

* * *

He drove to the sea and stopped at a lay-by above a stony beach and let the engine tick and the gulls argue, and he thought about inheritance.

Everyone inherited something. That was the discovery of his fifty-third year, the one the tower had taught him and the schoolyard had confirmed: that guilt compounds, that silence is a solvent that preserves whatever you pour into it, that a secret fed for a century grows knuckles. His own father had left him a house full of locked drawers and a distrust of anything undocumented, and Hazel had spent his life converting fear into files, and it had served him, and it had saved him, and it was not enough—not by itself. Documentation was the skeleton of witness. But witness needed a voice. The truth in a drawer protected no one. The truth aloud, in daylight, in front of the people it concerned—that was the exorcism. That was the hymn.

He thought about the girl in the yard, turning like a compass needle. He thought about the masons in Exeter pouring honest bronze while Marsh read a dead child's name into the record. He thought about Edith, who had told him on his last visit that she was learning to sing—actually sing, joined a choir in Tavistock, couldn't carry a tune, didn't care. "A hundred years of quiet," she'd said, "deserves a hundred years of noise. I've started late. I'll make do."

The tide came in below him, unhurried, erasing and erasing and never losing anything, only moving it.

He got back on the road as the afternoon leaned toward gold. Somewhere behind him the new bell was taking shape in its mould, and ahead of him the coast unspooled, and in a schoolyard he would never see again the children would learn the hymn again next autumn from the children who knew it, and they would sing it wrong and right and wrong again, and it would keep changing, because it was alive now instead of buried, and living things change.

At the top of the next hill the road dipped toward the water, and from somewhere below—a farm, a chapel, a house with its windows open to the warm end of the year—someone was singing. One voice, unhurried, wordless or nearly so, rising and falling in a line that felt older than the road.

Hazel slowed. Listened. Let the last note fade before he crested the hill and lost it.

Then he drove on, into the evening, with the windows down and the recorder switched off, carrying what he carried, singing what he could not yet name, home.
