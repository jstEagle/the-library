## 17. The Price Named

Father Anselmo had not slept. Wren could see it in the way he moved through the sacristy—careful, deliberate, a man rationing his own body like the last of the water. The church of Salcedo had no mirrors, had never had mirrors since 1974, and so when Wren sat down across from him at the little table where he prepared his sermons, there was nothing between them but a candle, a pot of coffee gone bitter with sitting, and forty years of silence finally running out.

"You've read her notes," Anselmo said. It was not a question. Wren's grandmother's field notebooks lay open on the table between them, the pages soft as cloth from handling, the ink faded to the color of dried blood.

"Twice," Wren said. "The burial song cuts off mid-verse. Page after page, and then—" They turned to the last entry, dated the third of November, 1974. *They are singing it wrong because no one remembers how it ends. I have it now. I have all of them.* "Then nothing."

"She stopped writing." Anselmo poured coffee neither of them would drink. His hand shook, and he let it. "That was the week she took the portrait."

"I know what she did, Father. Marta told me. She photographed the mirror instead of singing to it. She took the dead instead of sending them home."

"Yes." He said it without flinching, which surprised Wren. He was an old man, and Wren had expected an old man's evasions—the same polite fog the village had wrapped around everything since Wren arrived. But Anselmo looked at them the way doctors look at X-rays. Directly. Without comfort. "You know what she did. You do not yet know what she *cost*. And you cannot decide anything until you understand that."

Wren leaned back. The sacristy chair creaked. Outside, someone was nailing black cloth over a window frame, the hammer blows slow and tired. Three days since the fire in the square. Three days since the canvas refused to burn and every mirror in Salcedo began lagging at once, and three more villagers woke to find their reflections gone like teeth pulled in the night. Señora Ibáñez had been the first. She would not be the last. Everyone knew it the way you know weather is coming—in the bones, before the sky says anything.

"The rite," Wren said. "You said there might be a rite. That the song can be finished. Tell me."

Anselmo folded his hands. For a long moment he only looked at the candle flame, and Wren understood he was arranging words he had kept sealed for half a century, words that had never once been spoken aloud because speaking them made them possible.

"When the flood came," he began, "I was twenty-six years old. Newly ordained. I remember it because I buried eleven people in four days and did not sing a single one of them home properly. Do you understand what that means, in a village like this? The burial song—it is not decoration. It is a road. You sing the dead along it, verse by verse, and each verse is a landmark: the door of the house, the gate of the yard, the bend in the river, the hill. Eleven verses for the full journey. We got them to the river—the seventh verse—and then the water took the rest of the road away. There were no more verses sung in Salcedo that year. There was no voice left that knew them, and no strength to learn."

"And my grandmother knew them."

"She was a collector. That was her gift and her sin—she collected things the way other people collect debts. She had recorded the full song years before, in another village, from a woman who died the following winter. When she came here after the flood, she carried the ending in her machine, in her notebooks, in her throat. The dead knew it. That is why they came to the mirrors. Not to haunt us. To ask. Every night for weeks they stood behind the glass and sang the seven verses we had managed and then stopped, and waited, and looked at us with those faces—" His jaw tightened. "Faces asking a question. *Finish it.*"

"And she photographed them instead."

"She stood where you will stand," Anselmo said quietly, "in the flooded chapel ruins, with the water still in it, and she raised her camera at the glass, and she took them. All eleven. She bound their question into an image and she carried it away, and she told herself—I found this in a letter she wrote to the bishop, begging forgiveness, a letter he filed and forgot—that she was *preserving* them. That a photograph was safer than a song. That the dead would keep better in silver than in memory." He laughed, a dry terrible sound. "Keep. As if grief were jam."

Wren's mouth was dry. "And the rite. To undo it."

"To undo it, the song must be sung whole, in the place it broke, by a voice the dead will recognize. The collector's line. Your blood, your voice—that part you have already guessed, and Tomás has already tried to argue me out of it, and I have already told him the dead do not negotiate." He looked up. "But that is only half of it, child. Listen to me now, because this is the part that has kept me silent for fifty years, and it is the part that matters."

He turned the candle toward himself, and the light carved deep shadows under his eyes.

"A song is a road, I told you. But a road needs weight on it or it is only air. When the dead cross—from the glass into the world, from the world into whatever waits past the singing—they must pass *through* something living. Something anchored. A reflection freely offered holds the door open the way a hand holds a door. One face, given willingly, stays behind in the glass to be the hinge. The dead walk through it and come out the far side, home, finished. And the face that held the door—" He stopped. Started again, gentler. "The face does not come back. It remains in the mirror. Not as a prisoner. As a keeper. It smiles, sometimes. It watches. But it is no longer yours. You will walk out of that chapel with your body and your breath and your memories, and you will never again see yourself anywhere—not in glass, not in water, not in the dark screen of a phone, not in the eyes of the people who love you. They will see you. You will see only absence."

The candle flickered. Somewhere in the church above them, a shutter banged in wind that may or may not have been wind.

"That is the price," Anselmo said. "Not death. Death would be simpler, and cheaper, and God knows I have prayed for something cheaper. This is worse than death. This is being *unwitnessed*—by yourself—for the rest of your life. Every morning, a stranger's bed. Every window, a ghost where you should be. Survival has always been someone else's price in this village. In 1974 it was eleven people who paid for a flood with their faces. Now it is one person who pays for a theft with theirs. The arithmetic never changes. Only the number gets smaller, and the choosing gets harder."

Wren sat very still. They thought of the portrait in the crate upstairs at Marta's house, wrapped in its funeral shroud, the eleven faces pressed against the inside of painted glass. They thought of their own reflection, ten seconds late now, sometimes twelve, finishing movements they hadn't started, smiling without cause. Smiling *knowingly*, Wren had begun to think lately. Smiling like something that had already been told the plan.

"If I refuse?"

"Then the lag keeps growing. A minute now. Tomorrow, five minutes. The day after, the village lives a quarter hour behind itself, and the dead step out whenever the delay is wide enough to hold them, and they take whoever stands closest to the glass. It will not stop, Wren. It has been patient for fifty years. Patience is the one thing the dead have more of than the living."

"And if someone else offers? Tomás wants to. He's told me. He stood in the square yesterday and told the whole village he'd do it."

"He cannot." Anselmo's voice was iron wrapped in kindness. "The door answers the thief's blood. Your grandmother opened it; your line closes it. If Tomás stood in that water and offered his face, the glass would not even ripple. He would stand there offering himself to a locked house." He paused, and something older and sadder moved through his expression. "I asked the bishop, in 1975, whether the Church had a rite for this. He sent me a book on exorcism. The Church has rites for demons, for doubt, for dying. It has nothing for a debt. Debts are older than the Church. Debts are the oldest prayer there is."

Wren looked down at their grandmother's notebook, at the last line of ink. *I have all of them.* Written like triumph. Written like love. Both at once, which was the whole horror of it—love and theft sharing one hand, one pen, one heartbeat.

"There's something else," Anselmo said. He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the cabinet against the wall—a plain oak thing, unlocked, unguarded for fifty years because no one had ever wanted what was inside it. He brought back a single sheet of paper, brittle and yellowed, and laid it beside the notebook. A letter, in a woman's handwriting Wren recognized from the field notes. Their grandmother's hand, younger, faster.

"Read the postscript."

Wren read aloud, their voice strange in the mirrorless room: "*If anyone of mine ever comes to finish what I could not—tell them the glass will ask for more than the portrait. Tell them I know, because it asked me, and I said no, and that is why I could never come back. Twice I packed the car. Twice I turned around at the pass. Not fear of the dead. Fear of the price. Tell them I am sorry. Tell them the dead sang to me every night until I died, and I never once sang back.*"

The silence afterward was total. Even the hammering outside had stopped.

"She knew," Wren said finally. "Fifty years ago, she knew exactly what you just told me."

"She knew, and she chose her face over their rest, and she lived forty more years hearing them sing." Anselmo sat back down heavily. "Do not judge her too hard, child. You have not yet stood in the water. None of us knows what we will choose until the glass is open in front of us and our face is the coin on the table."

He reached across and closed the notebook gently, the way you close the eyes of the dead.

"The procession is tomorrow night," he said. "The whole village will walk to the ruins with candles, and they will wait at the tree line, and whatever happens in that water, they will witness it—because witnessing is the one thing this village owes everyone, and the one thing it failed to give in 1974. Tomás will want to carry the portrait for you. Let him carry it as far as the tree line. No farther." He met Wren's eyes, and for the first time since Wren had known him, the old priest looked afraid—not of the dead, but of the living, and what the living do to the people they love. "Because I have watched that boy watch you, and I have watched you watch him back, and I am an old man who has buried too many people to pretend I don't know what comes next. He will try to take your place at the water. And you will have to stop him. And stopping him, Wren, may cost you more than the glass will."

Outside, the sun was going down. In three days' time, dusk had become the hour the whole village dreaded, the hour when windows filled with faces pressed patiently against the inside of the light. Wren gathered the notebook and the letter with hands that felt borrowed, and at the sacristy door they paused.

"Father. When my grandmother turned back at the pass—twice—did she ever say what the reflection sounded like? The one that would stay behind?"

Anselmo was quiet a moment.

"No," he said. "But the dead did. On the last night of her life, in Oviedo, the nurses heard her talking to someone in the dark hospital room. Asking it questions. And once—only once—they heard it answer." He folded his hands. "It used her voice, child. It said: *Soon.*"

Wren walked home through streets already going dark, past windows draped in black cloth, past doors where families had nailed small mirrors shut from the inside, and everywhere, in every pane of glass that remained uncovered, their reflection walked with them—on time now, perfectly on time, matching them step for step.

Smiling.

Waiting to find out which of them would be keeping the door.
