## 1. The Call Comes From Inside the Listing

The email arrived at 6:47 a.m. on a Tuesday, which Sable Amberdale would later recognize as the first clue, because nothing honest happens before seven and nothing honest happens on a Tuesday.

*Vacant estate property. Activity reported. Previous contractors declined the job. Asking for a full inspection and documentation.*

Sable read it twice over her coffee, which had gone the color of regret, and thought: *declined by whom, exactly?*

Pest control was a trade built on people not asking follow-up questions. The client wanted the mice gone, the wasps gone, the raccoons in the soffit gone, and they wanted it done quietly and without moral commentary. Sable had built her small reputation on the documentation part — photographs, timestamps, written findings, a paper trail so thorough that insurance adjusters wept with gratitude — but even she had limits, and one of them had always been the phrase *previous contractors declined.* In her experience, contractors declined for two reasons: the client was crazy, or the house was.

She typed back: *What kind of activity?*

The reply came in four minutes. *Rodent, we believe. Droppings in the parlor. But the parlor is a problem.*

*The parlor is a problem* was not a sentence a property manager wrote by accident. Sable set down her mug, pulled up the listing, and spent a long moment looking at the photographs of a house she had never seen before and had known her whole life.

It was her grandmother's house.

Not metaphorically. Not *reminded her of.* It was the actual house on Corben Hollow Road, the one with the wraparound porch and the slate roof and the weathervane shaped like a heron that had pointed east since before Sable was born and, judging by the listing photos, pointed east still. The listing called it *the Hollis Estate, probate sale, sold as-is.* Hollis was her grandmother's maiden name. The probate had been dragging through the courts since last spring, when Grandma Ruth had died in her sleep with the decency and good timing that had characterized everything else about her.

Sable sat with that for a while. Then she did what she always did when the world behaved strangely: she got out her notebook, wrote the date and time, and documented her own confusion for the record.

*6:58 a.m. — Received inspection request for 14 Corben Hollow Rd from Meridian Probate Services. Property is Amberdale family home. Client unaware of connection, or is lying. Both options bad.*

She took the job anyway. She told herself it was because the fee was good and October was thin on work, and that was true, but it was also true that Sable had never once in her life let a strange thing go unexamined. Her sister Priya called it a compulsion. Sable called it due diligence. The difference was mostly a matter of who was doing the naming.

* * *

She arrived at eleven with her kit: two cameras, a headlamp, a tape measure, sample bags, a clipboard with pre-printed inspection forms, and a thermos of coffee she had made strong enough to qualify as a structural material. The house looked the way it had always looked, which was to say it looked like it was waiting for someone to come home. That was the thing about her grandmother's house — it had never managed to look vacant, even in the years after Grandma Ruth moved into the assisted living place and the rooms sat empty. It looked *held.* Kept. Like a coat hung up for someone who'd be back by supper.

The weathervane pointed east. Sable photographed it anyway. Timestamp: 11:04 a.m.

The front door was unlocked, which she noted, because a vacant probate property with an unlocked door was either negligence or an invitation, and she disliked both. Inside, the air was cool and still and smelled the way it had always smelled — cedar and old paper and, faintly, the ghost of the lavender sachets Grandma Ruth had tucked into every drawer in the house like a woman defending a perimeter.

"Hello?" she said, out of habit, and then felt foolish, because the house had been empty for over a year and the house knew it and she knew that the house knew it. Still, the word went down the hall and came back to her a half-second late, and she wrote that down too, because a half-second late was data.

*11:09 a.m. — Acoustics abnormal in front hall. Or imagination. Will re-test.*

She started where she always started: the perimeter. Foundation, sill, weep holes, the gap under the back door where the mice of the world had conducted their commerce with this house for a century. She photographed everything, logged everything, narrated quietly to her recorder in the flat professional voice she used for reports. *Exterior, southeast corner. No visible entry points. Vegetation clearance adequate. 11:16 a.m.*

The house listened. She couldn't have said how she knew that, only that the silence had a quality of attention to it, the way a room feels different when someone is in the next room not moving. She had worked a hundred empty houses and never once felt *observed* by one. She wrote it down, because feelings were data too, just badly labeled.

The droppings were in the parlor.

Of course they were. *But the parlor is a problem.* She stood in the doorway for a moment before going in, and the moment stretched longer than it should have, because the parlor was the room she had been told never to photograph.

That had been one of Grandma Ruth's rules, delivered without explanation, in the tone she used for rules that were not negotiable: *We don't take pictures in the parlor, dear. We take pictures everywhere else we like.* Young Sable had assumed it was about the good furniture, or the light, or some old-fashioned idea about privacy for a room where people received callers. She had been eleven and had filed it under *grandmother things,* a category that included not whistling indoors and never putting hats on the bed.

Now she stood in the doorway of that room with a camera in her hand and a professional obligation to document, and the rule sat up in her chest like a cat that had been sleeping.

She went in. She was forty-one years old, she owned a ladder, and she did not follow rules that came with no reasoning attached.

The parlor was exactly as she remembered it: the faded rose wallpaper, the marble-topped table with the doily, the horsehair settee, the tall window with its view of the back garden. Dust lay over everything in an even gray blanket, undisturbed, and that was the first wrong thing, because dust undisturbed did not explain the droppings scattered across the floorboards in a neat little constellation near the hearth.

She crouched. Rodent droppings were honest work — you could read a whole infestation in them like tea leaves, species and diet and how long since the animal passed through. She'd read thousands. These were the wrong size, the wrong color, the wrong *texture.* She put on a glove, collected one in a sample bag, and held it up to the window light.

It was paper. A tiny scrap of paper, curled at the edges, dense with a brownish stain that might have been age or might have been something else. There was writing on it. She got her loupe out of the kit — she carried a loupe because half of pest control was knowing what you were actually looking at — and read:

*—entered in the ledger this day, the sum of—*

She collected the rest. Eleven scraps in all, each one a fragment of what was unmistakably an old accounting record, the handwriting copperplate and confident, the ink the color of dried blood though she was nearly certain it wasn't. Ledger entries. Someone had written ledger entries and the pages had been torn into pieces the size of mouse droppings and scattered across a floor in a house that had been sealed for a year.

*11:52 a.m. — Debris in parlor is not biological. Paper fragments, handwritten, ledger format. Origin unknown. Method of deposit unknown.*

Her hand was steady when she wrote it. Her hand was less steady when she bagged the samples, and her hand was not steady at all when she photographed the hearth and, reviewing the shot on the camera's little screen, noticed that the dust on the horsehair settee was disturbed. Not footprints. Not animal tracks. Just a smooth, deliberate sweep across the cushion, as though someone had recently run a hand over it. Checking it. Making it ready.

Sable stood in the middle of her grandmother's parlor and did the thing she always did when the professional calm began to crack, which was to narrate to the recorder in her flattest voice, because flatness was a wall you could stand behind.

"Inspection of interior parlor complete at 12:04 p.m. No evidence of pest activity. Evidence of — " and here she paused, because the next word mattered, words always mattered, and she chose it with care — "evidence of *arrangement.*"

The house was quiet in a way that had weight to it, like the pause after someone asks a question.

She got out of there. She told herself it was to review the samples under better light, which was even true, but she walked fast down the front hall and did not look back at the parlor door, and she noted that too, in the notebook, in handwriting slightly less even than her usual: *Did not look back. Why not.*

In the kitchen — a normal room, a safe room, a room with a kettle and a table where she'd eaten a thousand breakfasts — she spread the paper fragments out on a dish towel and pieced them together like a jigsaw, and got, out of eleven scraps, three legible lines.

The first was the ledger fragment she'd already read. The second said, in the same copperplate: *—paid in full, the account of E. M—* and then a tear.

The third fragment was different. The handwriting was different — smaller, cramped, pressed hard into the paper — and the ink was a cheap modern blue ballpoint, and Sable knew that handwriting the way you know your own face in a mirror, because she had seen it on a thousand grocery lists and birthday cards and the margins of her childhood homework.

Grandma Ruth's hand. Written sometime in the last forty years, pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through the paper, and it said:

*It is not finished. One chair. Always one chair more.*

Sable sat in her grandmother's kitchen with the scrap in her gloved hand and the afternoon light going long and amber through the window, and she understood, with the particular cold clarity of a professional recognizing that a job has stopped being a job, that the activity in this house had never been rodents, that the previous contractors had been right to decline, and that whatever the parlor was a problem *of,* it had been a problem for a very long time — long enough for her grandmother to know, and to say nothing, and to leave her granddaughter to walk into it with a clipboard and a headlamp like a woman checking a crawl space.

She photographed the scrap. Timestamp: 3:31 p.m. Then, because she was who she was, she opened her notebook to a fresh page and wrote at the top, in letters that only shook a little:

*Question one: who is E.M.?*

And beneath it, because the house had gone very quiet in a listening sort of way, and because the answer arrived in her head whole and unwelcome, like a bill:

*Question two: what chair?*

That night, at her kitchen table, she would open her grandmother's old photograph albums — the ones from the house, the ones she'd inherited along with everything else — and she would find out.
