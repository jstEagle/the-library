## 2. One Chair Too Many

The trouble with evidence is that it never lies. It just waits for you to ask the right question.

I got home a little after nine, cold to the bone in that way October gets when it's practicing for November. I made tea I didn't drink and sat down at my kitchen table with my laptop and my notebook and did what I always do after a job: the debrief. Timestamps, room-by-room notes, photos sorted into folders named like a boring person's tax returns. *Amberdale Estate — Interior — 4:12 PM.* *Amberdale Estate — Basement — 4:38 PM.* My grandmother used to say I documented my life like I was building a case against it. She wasn't wrong. It's how I keep the world from sliding around on me. If it's written down, it happened once, and then it stays happened.

The Amberdale house had been an easy shoot, all things considered. Empty rooms photograph well — they hold still. I'd gone through the ground floor methodically: entry hall, front parlor, dining room, kitchen, back parlor, study. Forty-one frames of nothing more alarming than dust and good bones.

I was on frame thirty-one when I stopped drinking my tea.

The front parlor. Shot at 4:19 PM, wide angle, natural light through the east window doing its best with the grime on the glass. A handsome room, even abandoned. Fireplace, mantel, faded wallpaper with a pattern of small blue flowers that had almost given up being flowers. And furniture — not much, because most of it had been sold off or hauled away years ago, according to the listing agent, who'd sounded relieved about it. A settee under the window. A side table. A rocking chair by the hearth with a broken runner.

And a straight-backed wooden chair, centered in the middle of the floor, facing the camera.

I stared at it for a long moment, then did the thing you do when your brain offers you something impossible: I looked away and looked back, as if the chair might have the decency to be embarrassed.

It didn't.

Here's the problem, and I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only religion I've ever kept. In my field notes, written at 4:21 PM, standing in that exact room, I recorded: *"Front parlor. Settee (window), side table, rocker (hearth, damaged). Floor bare otherwise. Wallpaper failing NE corner."* Floor bare otherwise. I remember the bare floor. I remember thinking the room looked like a stage set between acts, everything pushed to the edges, the middle of the floor swept clean and waiting.

There is no chair in my memory of that room. There is very definitely a chair in my photograph of it.

I zoomed in. The chair was old-fashioned, dark wood, high back, the kind of thing you'd see in a portrait of somebody stern. It sat dead center, square to the lens, positioned with the kind of deliberate neatness that furniture doesn't manage on its own. No dust film visible on the seat — though at that resolution I couldn't swear to it. The rest of the room wore its abandonment like a coat. The chair wore nothing at all.

I checked the metadata twice. Same file, same timestamp, unedited. Then I pulled up the shots I'd taken of the same room from the doorway, three frames earlier, at a different angle.

No chair.

Three frames, ninety seconds apart. One empty floor. One chair, sitting where no one had left it, facing me like it had been told I was coming.

I sat back. The tea had gone cold, which was fine, because I didn't want it anymore anyway.

Now — a professional considers the mundane explanations first. That's the whole discipline. So: could someone have moved a chair into the room in the ninety seconds between my shots? The house was locked when I arrived; the agent met me, unlocked, left. I worked alone. I heard no footsteps, no scraping of wood on hardwood — and a chair that heavy would scrape. Could it be a photographic artifact, some trick of exposure? I've been shooting infestation documentation for eleven years. I know what artifacts look like. They smear. They ghost. They don't arrive with joinery and a finish.

Could I have simply forgotten seeing it? This was the one I turned over longest, lying in bed that night with the ceiling doing its usual impression of nothing. Memory is a bad witness; I say that to clients all the time. But forgetting isn't the same as inventing. I hadn't just failed to notice a chair. I had written down, in my own handwriting, in my own shorthand, *floor bare otherwise* — a note whose entire purpose was to record the absence of exactly such a thing. You don't document an absence unless the absence struck you.

Something had put a chair in that room, and had done it quietly, and had pointed it at my camera.

I should have stopped there. Any sensible person stops there. You log the anomaly, you call the client, you decline the follow-up, you take the loss on the invoice. Instead, around midnight, I went out to the garage and brought in the boxes.

My grandmother's albums. Eleven of them, cloth-bound, going back to 1958, inherited last spring along with the house she'd lived in for fifty years and the paperwork nobody wants. I'd been meaning to sort them properly. I hadn't. Grief keeps excellent books but pays its bills late.

I want to be clear about why I opened them. It wasn't sentiment. It was methodology. The listing agent had mentioned, in passing, that the estate house had been "in the family" generations back — some distant branch, the Amberdales, before it passed to a trust and then to vacancy. And my grandmother had grown up twenty miles from that property. Small towns, old families. If there were photographs of that parlor anywhere within fifty miles, they'd be in her albums, because my grandmother photographed everything: funerals, christenings, picnics, houses, other people's houses, the interiors of other people's houses, which she considered fair game if the curtains were open.

I started with the oldest album and worked forward, and I found the parlor four times before I found anything strange, and then I found it strange every single time.

1959. A wedding reception, people crammed into the front parlor of what was unmistakably the same room — same fireplace, same wallpaper, younger and bluer flowers. Settee under the window. Rocker by the hearth. And there, center floor, square to the camera: the chair. Occupied, in this one, by a woman in a dark dress whose face the print had washed to a smudge. Everyone else in the photo was mid-laugh or mid-sip, caught loose. The woman in the chair sat perfectly still, hands folded, facing the lens the way you face a mirror.

1963. Christmas. Same room, different angle, shot from the doorway — my grandmother's habit, always from the doorway, I noticed, never inside. Settee, table, rocker. Chair. Centered. Facing the camera. Empty this time, and somehow worse for it, the way an empty seat at a full table is worse than an empty table.

1971. A funeral gathering, black clothes, grim mouths. Chair. Closer to the camera now — noticeably closer, maybe two feet nearer the lens than in '63, though the camera position looked identical. As if between photographs it had been walking toward whoever held the camera, and only moved when observed.

1980. Picnic on the lawn outside, the parlor's tall window visible behind the potato salad. Through the glass, dim behind the reflection: the chair. Inside a locked-looking room, in a house the album's caption said had been closed up since '78. Facing the window. Facing out.

I laid the prints out on my kitchen table in chronological order and stood over them like a coroner. Same chair. I'd stake my license on it — same high back, same turned legs, same slight lean to the left that a hundred years of use gives a piece of furniture, a limp in wood form. Decades apart, different cameras, different film stocks, different decades of my grandmother's increasingly shaky hands.

Always the same room. Always the same position, dead center, square to the lens.

Always closer.

I measured. Not with instruments — with a pencil and the width of my thumbnail, which is a real technique, thank you, and better than nothing. From 1959 to 1980, across five appearances, the chair advanced toward the camera in every frame. Small steps. Patient ones. Whatever pace it was keeping, it wasn't hurrying, and it wasn't stopping.

And here is the part I have said out loud exactly once since, to Priya, who told me to stop talking:

In the 1980 photo, the chair was close enough to the window that if it took one more step, it would be outside the room. Outside the photograph. On the lawn, among the picnic blankets, among the living.

That was forty-five years ago.

I stood in my kitchen at two in the morning with my grandmother's dead friends smiling up at me out of glossy paper, and I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. Today, in my own photograph, taken this afternoon, the chair had been centered in the middle of the front parlor floor. Facing my camera. Which meant that sometime between 1980 and now, it had either stepped back —

— or the camera had come to it.

Because that was the other reading, wasn't it. The one I kept turning over like a stone with something damp underneath. Maybe the chair hadn't been approaching my grandmother all those years. Maybe it had been approaching the *house*. Its house. Holding its place, center floor, patient as a reservation, while the family scattered and the trust dithered and the listing agents came and went. And now the house was for sale again, and a pest controller with a camera had walked in and stood exactly where my grandmother used to stand — in the doorway, never quite inside — and raised a lens.

And the chair had turned to face her.

I thought about my field note. *Floor bare otherwise.* Written at 4:21 PM. The photograph says 4:19 PM. Two minutes. Somewhere in those two minutes, between the shutter click and my pen, the room had decided to show me what it had been holding in reserve.

I put the albums away. I stacked the prints face-down, which accomplished nothing and made me feel marginally less watched. I went to bed and did not sleep so much as lie adjacent to sleep, listening to my own house tick and settle, every sound renegotiating itself into a footstep, a scrape, wood finding its place on a floor.

At six I gave up, made coffee, and opened my laptop to write the report I owed the agent. Professional language. Documented anomalies. Recommend further assessment.

Instead I found myself pulling up the county records, because there was one more question, and questions are like that — you can't un-ask them, you can only fail to answer them. The listing had called it the Harrow House, after the road. But the agent had said *the family*. Families have names.

The deed history loaded slowly, the way old things do.

The Amberdale house. Built 1897. Held by the Amberdale family continuously until 1931 — and then a gap, a transfer I had to squint at, the property passing abruptly out of the family name in the winter of 1931 and into a trust. Something had ended, that year. Someone had signed it away.

And my grandmother's albums — I went back to the first one, the 1959 wedding, and looked again at the smudged woman in the chair, hands folded, face washed out, sitting in a house that hadn't belonged to her family for twenty-eight years by then, in a chair that faced the camera like it knew her.

On the page opposite the photograph, in my grandmother's young, slanted handwriting, the caption read:

*Aunt Vera's wedding — the Marlow place.*

Not the Amberdale place. The Marlow place.

I sat very still in my cold kitchen while the coffee went cold too, and understood that whatever was counting chairs in that house had a name, and that my grandmother had known it, and had written it down once, casually, beside a wedding photo, and never — in eleven albums, in fifty years of photographing everything — taken a single picture of that parlor again.

Except she had. Four times. Always from the doorway. Always with the chair in it.

She hadn't stopped looking. She'd just stopped getting closer.

I picked up my phone and scrolled to Priya's number, thumb hovering, and then didn't call, because what was I going to say? *Hi, sorry to wake you, the chair in Grandma's old photos has been walking toward us since Eisenhower?*

Instead I opened the 1959 album one more time, and this time I didn't look at the chair at all. I looked at the doorway in the background of the shot — the far doorway, the one into the hall — where, half-cut by the frame's edge, someone stood watching the party. A thin figure in an apron, dark-haired, face turned toward the camera with an expression the print was too small and too old to carry.

Under the figure, faint as breath, someone had written a name in pencil and half erased it.

All I could make out was the beginning. *Edi—*

* * *

The frost was due Thursday.
