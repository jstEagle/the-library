## 13. A Face for a Face

The tunnel did not let her leave.

August had turned toward the arch three times now, and three times the passage behind her had rearranged itself — not dramatically, not with any theatrical groan of stone, but the way a dream rearranges itself when you try to look directly at its exits. The wallpapered corridor simply became a longer version of itself. The bricked arch at her back became another stretch of wall, furred with mold, sweating cold. The tin sat in her hands, heavier than tin had any right to be, and the photograph inside it sat heavier still.

She stopped walking. There was no point walking.

"Okay," she said, and her voice came out flattened by the close air, swallowed almost before it left her. "Okay. I know you're here."

The dark ahead of her answered by becoming less dark. Not light, exactly — the tunnel had no light source she could find, and yet she could see, the way you can see in dreams, the way you can see underwater when something below you turns its face up. Shapes resolved. The corridor stretched ahead of her into a depth the house's footprint couldn't possibly contain, and standing in that depth, small and patient, was the girl.

Denny Marsh, or the thing that had worn Denny Marsh for fifty years, stood with its hands folded in front of its corduroy dress. Its head was tilted at the angle of a child waiting to be told a story. The lake smell came off it in slow waves, cold and mineral and green, and beneath the lake smell there was something else now, something August had been trying not to name since the tin came out of the wall: a smell like the inside of a mirror. Like silver and glass and the breath you fog onto your own reflection.

"You have it," the thing said.

It used Denny's voice, a little girl's voice, bright and slightly hoarse the way children get when they've been shouting all afternoon at a lake. But it said the words in August's cadence. It had been listening to her for days, and it had learned.

"I have it," August said. Her arms ached from holding the tin against her chest. She made herself lower it to her side, made her voice level. "And I know what it is. Ruth's tape said — the anchor. The thing that keeps you here. Keeps *her* here."

"Denny," the thing said, tasting the name. "Yes. She is the hook in the meat of this place. She has been the hook a long, long time." It smiled. The smile was the problem, had always been the problem — it arrived a half-second before the rest of the face agreed to it, so that the smile seemed to come from somewhere behind the girl, some operator standing further back in the dark. "You want to take the hook out."

"I want Denny to rest. I want my grandmother's ghost to rest. I want to go home."

"Three wants." The thing unfolded one small hand and held up fingers, an absurd pantomime of a counting game. "And the house will grant you the first two. The house is generous. The house has always been generous." The third finger stayed folded. "The third want is the expensive one."

"Then tell me the price." August's heart was slamming, but she'd spent ten years in condemned buildings with failing headlamps and unstable floors, and she knew the trick of fear: you didn't feed it, you used it. You made it do your breathing for you. "No riddles. No trades dressed up as gifts. Plain terms. That's the only deal I'll make."

Something flickered across the child's face — not anger. Interest. The smile softened into something almost respectful, which was worse.

"Plain terms," the thing repeated. "Very well. August Crane. Skeptic. Mapper of unmapped rooms. Here are the plain terms."

It began to walk toward her, and it did not walk the way a child walks. It walked the way water walks when it finds a new channel — without effort, without hurry, simply arriving along the path of least resistance. August held her ground. The tin in her hand had gone cold enough to burn.

"Return the photograph to the water at the drowned hour, and the hook comes free. Denny Marsh rests. The dead girl goes wherever drowned girls go, and the smile that borrowed her has nothing left to hold it to this soil — except, of course, the debt."

"The debt."

"Every door out of a trap has a toll." The thing stopped an arm's length away. This close, August could see that its eyes were not eyes. They were the color of the tunnel's dark, given just enough shine to pass. "Your grandmother understood this. Edith Crane sat where you are standing, in this same corridor, with this same tin, and she heard these same plain terms. Someone returns the photograph. Someone stays as keeper. The keeper holds the door. The keeper keeps the count. The keeper is the hinge that lets the house forget and lets the living leave."

"Stay," August said. "Stay where. For how long?"

The thing tilted its head again, and for a moment it looked genuinely puzzled by the question, the way a shark might be puzzled by the concept of mercy.

"Until there is another keeper."

* * *

The corridor changed around them while the thing spoke, or perhaps August's mind simply stopped insisting on the tunnel's original dimensions. The walls drew back. The wallpaper — that sad parade of faded roses — receded into a gloom that smelled of lake water and old silver, and the floor under her feet became something else, something softer, and when August looked down she was standing on grass.

Not real grass. Grass the way memory renders grass: every blade correct, none of them individual. A summer evening lay across everything, gold and endless, the light of roughly seven o'clock in July, and August knew — knew with the sick certainty of a person recognizing a place they've never been — that this was the Crane backyard in 1974.

The thing stood beside her. It had stopped pretending to be small. It was still shaped like Denny Marsh, still in the corduroy dress, but it stood now the way a landlord stands in a property, and the evening lay around it like furniture it owned.

"You want proof the terms are honest," it said. "Skeptics want proof. So. Look."

It made a small gesture, a child's gesture, fingers flicking off a thumb like flicking water — and the air in front of August filled with photographs.

Not photographs. Postcards. That was the only word her mind would give her. They hung in the golden air at the height of her eyes, each one the size of her palm, each one bordered in that creamy white of old Kodak stock, and each one was *moving*, the way the light in an old home movie moves, a little too fast, a little too bright, everything slightly overexposed with joy.

The first one showed a woman in her thirties standing at the edge of a lake, laughing at someone behind the camera, one hand raised to shield her eyes. August's breath went out of her. She knew the laugh. She had heard it once, on a cassette, in a shoebox of tapes her grandmother had sent her mother and that her mother had never played. The laugh was Edith Crane, thirty years old, and she was beautiful in the way that people are beautiful when they don't know yet what the summer will cost.

"She paid with these," the thing said, and its voice had gone gentle, almost tender, which was the cruelest register it owned. "When she left. She could not carry the summer out with her — the keeper's terms do not allow it — and so she left it here. With me. I keep it for her. I keep everything for everyone. That is what I am, August. I am not the monster in this house. I am the *storage*."

The second postcard: a picnic table. A birthday cake with a crooked candle. The same woman — Edith — laughing again, and beside her a man August didn't know, and beside *him* a small girl with a gap in her teeth and wet hair, holding up a paper plate as if toasting the camera.

"Denny," August said.

"Denny. The last summer she had. The only summer she had." The thing's voice did something complicated — it was grief performed by something that had studied grief from the outside, through glass. "They took her photograph at four-forty in the afternoon on the eleventh of July. She went into the water at five-ten. I have the whole afternoon. Would you like to see the whole afternoon? I have it at every frame. I can play it forward. I can play it backward. I can play it so slowly that the last hour of her life lasts as long as yours has."

"I don't want—"

"Everyone wants, at the end." The postcards drifted, rearranged. "Here. This one is yours."

August looked, and wished with her whole body that she hadn't.

The postcard showed a hospital corridor. Not 1974. The colors were the colors of now — the flat institutional green, the humming fluorescent white. And in the corridor, sitting in a plastic chair with her elbows on her knees and her face in her hands, was a woman August recognized from mirrors: herself, eleven years younger, waiting outside an ICU room while a machine breathed for her mother. The postcard moved. The figure in it rocked slightly, forward and back, the way people rock when they are holding themselves together with rhythm because nothing else will hold.

"You keep this one too?" August's voice came out strange. Flat. Far away.

"You left it behind you years ago, the way people leave things. You walked out of that corridor and you built a self that did not include it, and the self you built is the one that maps tunnels and doesn't cry and doesn't call her grandmother back when the letters come." The thing turned its head. The smile was back, holding steady while the rest of its face went slack as a dropped marionette. "I keep what people set down. Your grandmother set down a summer. You set down a corridor. I am very full, August Crane. This house has been *feeding* me for a hundred years, one small abandoned thing at a time."

The postcards turned slowly in the golden light. August made herself look at them — at Edith at the lake, Edith at the cake, Edith in the tunnel, because there it was now, a new one drifting into place: Edith Crane in the wallpapered dark, sitting against the brick with a candle stub, writing tally marks with something sharp, her face lit with a stubbornness that August recognized from her own mirror, her own stubborn jaw, her own refusal to be afraid in the ordinary ways people were allowed to be afraid.

"She survived," August said quietly. "Down here. A year. The marks say a year."

"She survived," the thing agreed. "She returned nothing. She paid nothing. She did the one thing no one had ever done in this house." It leaned close, and its breath smelled of the bottom of a lake, cold and vegetal and old. "She *forgot*. She let me take the whole summer out of her head, every hour of it, and she walked up those stairs into the rest of her life as a woman who had never lost anything at all. And I have kept her summer for fifty years, waiting for the debt to come due. Debts always come due. They came due when she died. They came due when the house passed to you."

The golden evening flickered. For one frame — one dropped frame, like a film catching in a projector — August saw the truth underneath the summer: the tunnel, the dark, the mold, the cold, the tallies, the small dry room with the cot. Then the light came back, warm and lying.

"So here are the plain terms, one last time, plainly." The thing held up its small hand and folded the fingers down one by one. "Return the photograph, and Denny rests. Return the photograph, and Edith's ghost goes free — she is still here, did you know? Fifty years, standing in her own kitchen, waiting for a debt she can't remember owing. Return the photograph, and the door opens, and you may walk out of 1974 and up into your own time and sell this house and never think of it again."

The last finger stayed up.

"And the keeper," August said.

"And the keeper." The thing smiled its arriving-half-second-early smile. "Someone must stay. That is the oldest term, older than me, older than the house. A door this heavy needs a hinge. Your grandmother would not be the hinge. The girl's father came down looking for her, and he—" it paused, and something almost like regret crossed it, or a memory of regret filed away for later use, "—he is still here, in the deep room, being the hinge, being *so tired*, August, fifty years of tired. He would like to stop. You could let him stop. You could be the hinge instead. You would be good at it. You like counting things."

The postcards drifted around them like snow. Edith laughing at the lake. August rocking in the plastic chair. The keeper's cot, somewhere below, waiting.

"Or," the thing said, "you do what Edith did. You pay the other price. You hand me the summer you're standing in — this one, the one where you found the tin, the one where you were brave, the whole of it, down to why you came — and you walk out empty, and the debt stays, and the house waits for the next Crane. There is always a next Crane. Houses like this make sure of it."

August stood in the false golden light with the tin in her hand and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that every person who had ever survived this house had survived it by leaving someone else holding the door. Edith had left the keeper's father. The keeper's father had left the debt. The debt had left her.

Survival here was never free. It was only ever *forwarded*.

"I need to see something first," she heard herself say. Her voice was steady, which surprised her. "The memories. All of them. Hers — the summer you took. Before I choose."

The thing regarded her for a long moment. The lake smell deepened. Somewhere far above them, faint as a memory of a sound, a clock in a kitchen that no longer existed began to strike the hour — and August's skin went cold, because she knew without knowing how she knew that the hour it was striking was five-ten, the drowned hour, and it was closer than she'd thought.

"Look," the thing said, and opened its hands, and all the postcards in the world came down around her like rain. "But choose quickly, August. The water is already in the walls."

The first postcard to touch her fingers showed the tunnel, and Edith Crane on her last night in it, and she was crying — and she was smiling — and she was choosing.
