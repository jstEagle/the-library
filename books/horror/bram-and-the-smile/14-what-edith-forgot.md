## 14. What Edith Forgot

The Smile showed her the memories the way a child shows a collection of dead things — proudly, one at a time, held up to the light.

They were not images exactly. They were *rooms*. August understood that within moments of the first one opening. Each memory was a small, warm chamber with walls made of the moment itself, and she stood inside them the way she had once stood inside abandoned buildings, careful not to touch anything, knowing the floors were rotten.

The first room was a kitchen. Her grandmother's kitchen — but younger, brighter, the linoleum a yellow that had faded to bone by the time August was born. A woman stood at the counter with her back turned, and August knew her before she turned around, knew her from the set of the shoulders, from the particular way she pressed her knuckles into dough as if the dough had personally wronged her.

Edith Crane. Thirty-some years old. Alive in a way August had never once seen her — and August had seen her at Christmas, at funerals, at the hospital, and had never seen her *lit from inside* like this.

"Where are we?" August asked, and her voice came out wrong, too loud for the little room, and the woman at the counter did not hear it. Of course she didn't. This was not a place. This was a thing being remembered, and August was only the newest ghost in it.

*These are hers,* the Smile said, and it did not speak so much as the idea of speaking occurred in her skull, fully formed, wearing her own voice like a borrowed coat. *She left them behind when she went up the stairs. I keep what is owed.*

August stood in her grandmother's memory of a June morning and felt her hands shake.

The kitchen memory ended the way a match does — a small bloom of dark, and then another room. A porch. Rain coming down in silver ropes off the eaves, and Edith on the swing with a paperback she wasn't reading, watching the driveway as if a car were due. The smell of the rain came through whole: wet asphalt, cut grass, the mineral tang of the lake a quarter mile off. August had grown up two hundred miles from this house and the smell still reached into her chest and squeezed.

*She was waiting for someone,* the Smile said, and there was something in it that was almost tenderness, which was the worst thing August had encountered yet, worse than the lag, worse than the shards of mirror each holding a tiny intact her. *She waited a long time. That is why I kept this one.*

"Whose memories are the others?" August asked. "The ones in the walls. The tallies."

The Smile did not answer. It only opened the next room, and the next, and August understood that it was not going to answer, that it had been asked this question by someone else once, a long time ago, by a man with a lantern and a father's stubbornness, and it had answered him the same way.

* * *

The rooms changed. The summer deepened. August learned to read them the way she read buildings — by their damage.

There was Edith laughing in a backyard, wine in a jelly jar, a woman beside her with a sunburned nose and a laugh like a screen door — Ruth, unmistakably, fifty years younger, and August's throat closed because Ruth Voss was a voice on a tape and a face at a fence and here she was *alive*, here she was *happy*, and the happiness had a date on it like a carton of milk. July 1974.

There was Edith kneeling on a dock, hand on the back of a small girl in a yellow swimsuit, and the girl was turned toward the camera and smiling, and August had seen that photograph already, had pulled it from a tin in a wall cavity with her own two hands, and she knew what the lake did to that girl eleven minutes after the shutter clicked.

And there was the last room.

She knew it was the last one before anything in it resolved, because the others had all been warm. This one was cold the way a cellar is cold, the way the tunnel was cold, and the light in it was the light of a flashlight dying by degrees, and August wanted to step back and could not, because there was nothing to step back *onto*. She was inside a memory the way a drowning woman is inside a lake.

Edith was in the tunnel.

Not the tunnel as August had found it — dry, dusted, wallpapered with decades like tree rings. This was the tunnel *young*, the walls raw-dug, the smell of wet clay and candle smoke, and Edith was sitting against the bricked arch with a candle guttering in a saucer and the photograph of the drowned girl in her lap, and she had been crying for a long time. August could tell because crying leaves a topography. Edith's face was a map of a country that had been at war with itself.

The candlelight did something terrible and beautiful to her. It made her look like every photograph of every woman who had ever survived something.

"You have to understand," Edith said, and August flinched, because the memory was not supposed to hear her — but then she saw that Edith was not talking to her. Edith was talking to the dark. To the thing in the dark. To the Smile, which in 1974 had not yet learned to wear a child's face, or perhaps had only just learned, and stood at the edge of the candlelight in a shape that August's mind refused to render, a shape like an argument the eye kept losing.

"It's not mine to give," Edith said. "That's what you keep not hearing. It's not *mine*. The face. The summer. The *name*." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, wet and cracked. "You want Edith Crane. There is no Edith Crane without that summer. Take the summer and you take her, and then what are you wearing? What's the point of a face if there's nobody behind it?"

The shape at the edge of the light did not move. It did not need to. August could feel it thinking, the way you can feel weather coming through a bad tooth.

"I know what you're offering," Edith said. "The keeper's room. The cot. The tallies. A year down here, five years, fifty — however long it takes for someone else to be stupid enough to buy this house." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, a gesture so familiar, so *grandmotherly*, that August made a sound she didn't know she was making. "You showed me the room. You showed me the man who's in there now. He's still in there, isn't he? He's been in there since July."

The shape said nothing, and the nothing was an answer.

"He went looking for his little girl," Edith whispered. "And you put him in a room and gave him tallies to scratch and you've been *keeping* him like — like a jar of something." Her voice came apart. She pressed both hands over her mouth and breathed through her fingers, and when she spoke again it was small and level and it was the voice August knew, the voice that had said *eat your vegetables* and *call when you land* and, once, on a phone call August had cut short because she was busy, *I love you, August, whatever happens, I love you.*

"So here's my counteroffer," Edith said. "You can't have the summer. But I'll give you the *remembering*. Every night I spent down here. Every tally. The whole year of it — I'll hand it over, willingly, all at once, and I'll walk up those stairs and not know why my hands are dirty. You'll have it. All of it. Forever. And the debt —" she lifted the photograph, and the drowned girl smiled up out of it, and August's stomach turned because she knew, now, that the smile in the photograph was the Smile's first draft — "the debt stays. Unpaid. For whoever comes next."

The candle guttered.

The shape at the edge of the light considered, and August, standing inside her grandmother's worst hour, felt the consideration pass over her like the shadow of a plane.

*She chose forgetting,* the Smile said, in August's own voice, in the present tense, in the cold of the real tunnel where August's body still stood with the tin in her hands. *It was the first trade anyone ever offered me. I did not know what I had until it was gone. A memory freely given tastes different than one taken. Richer. She seasoned it with her grief and I have been savoring it for fifty years.*

The memory-room collapsed around August like a held breath released.

* * *

She came back to herself on her knees in the tunnel, the tin open in her lap, Denny Marsh's last photograph face-up between her fingers. The candle was long gone but the dark was not total; the mortar of the arch glowed faintly, the way it had at 3 a.m., the way it always would, a light with no source, a light like the memory of light.

She had thought she understood. Coming down here, tape in her pocket, photograph in hand, she had built herself a clean little story: grandmother fought the monster, grandmother won, grandmother paid a price. It was a good story. It had a shape. It was the kind of story August told herself about abandoned places — someone left, something happened, the building kept the shape of it.

But buildings didn't choose. Buildings didn't sit in the dark and negotiate.

Edith had chosen.

Edith had sat against this exact arch, in this exact cold, with the drowned girl's photograph in her lap, and she had looked at the keeper's room — the cot, the tallies, the desiccated man who had only wanted his daughter back — and she had chosen the stairs. She had chosen to walk up into her kitchen and not know why her hands were dirty, and she had stayed up there for fifty years, and she had let the debt sit in the walls of her house like a pipe waiting to burst.

And she had left it to August.

The will. The house, the furniture, the boxes in the attic — *to my granddaughter, August Crane, whom I love.* August had read it four times in the lawyer's office and thought only that it was strange, that they had been estranged, that the old woman had reached out so rarely and so awkwardly and now, in death, had handed her an entire house she didn't want.

Not strange. Deliberate. The way a fuse is deliberate.

"She knew," August said aloud. Her voice bounced down the tunnel and came back wrong, flattened, as if the walls were eating the edges of it. "She knew it would come due. She knew the lag, the mirrors, the — she knew someone would have to come down here eventually, and she made sure it would be *me*." The anger came up hot and fast and she let it, welcomed it, because anger was easier than the thing underneath it. "Fifty years. She had fifty years to tell me. One phone call. One letter. *August, if anything strange happens with the mirrors —* anything. She could have —"

She could have what? Warned her? August had spent her whole life not answering. Two Christmases missed. Calls let go to voicemail. The last time she'd seen Edith Crane alive, at the funeral home, in a casket, she had stood at a polite distance and felt mostly nothing, and had told herself that nothing was grief's honest shape for a relationship that starved.

The anger hit the thing underneath and cracked it open, and the thing underneath was this: Edith Crane had sat in the dark at thirty-four years old and chosen to forget her own worst year rather than become a jar of something in a room underground, and she had carried the debt up the stairs alone, and she had lived fifty years in a house where every mirror ran a half-second late and she *could not remember why it frightened her*, and every time she brushed her teeth, every time she passed the hallway glass, some animal part of her had flinched at a thing she could not name, and she had flinched alone, for fifty years, because the one person she might have told had never once picked up the phone.

August put her forehead against the cold clay of the tunnel wall and cried the way she had not cried at the funeral, ugly and graceless, her breath hitching in the dark.

The Smile waited. It was very good at waiting. It had been waiting, in one form or another, since July 1974, and it knew, the way predators know, that grief is just hunger with the serial numbers filed off, and that a person crying in a tunnel is a person whose edges have gone soft.

*You understand now,* it said gently, in her voice. *The house. The lag. The pull you felt the first night, the restlessness that walked you down here with your little light and your little machine. That was not curiosity. That was inheritance. She left you the debt the way one leaves a clock still running. And the clock has nearly finished its hour.*

August lifted her head. Her eyes were hot and her throat was raw and the photograph lay in the tin like a held breath.

"What happens if I just leave," she said. "Right now. Walk out. Take the picture, take the tape, burn the house down from the outside."

*Then I keep what I keep,* the Smile said, and for the first time it did not sound patient. It sounded like something very old and very hungry doing an impression of patience, and the impression was slipping. *And you will spend the rest of your life in a world of glass and water, waiting for your own face to catch up to you. And when it does — and it will, August, it is already doing it — you will come back down these stairs on your knees, and I will still be here. I am always here. Ask the man in the keeper's room how leaving went for him.*

The mortar of the arch pulsed, slow, like something breathing behind a wall.

And August — wrecked, grieving, furious, alone — looked down at the photograph of Denny Marsh, and at the tin, and at the faint glow in the bricks, and understood that there was no version of this where she walked away clean. There was only the question her grandmother had answered wrong, or answered *small*, fifty years ago in this same dark, and whether August Crane was going to answer it the same way.

Behind her, far up the tunnel, faint as a pulse under skin, she heard the house.

She heard the mirrors.

All of them, at once, ticking over — and the lag between the tick and the room had stretched, she could feel it in her teeth, and somewhere above her head her own reflection was falling further and further behind, mouthing something at her with her mouth, in the dark of an empty bathroom, and the gap was widening, second by second by second.
