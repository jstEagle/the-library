## 5. Knocking at 3 A.M.

The knock came at three o'clock in the morning, and it came from below.

Theo knew the time precisely because she had been awake to see it happen—had watched the digits on her phone flip from 2:59 to 3:00 with the flat, incurious attention of someone who has not slept properly in eleven days and no longer expects to. The screen's glow was the only light in Bram's old bedroom, laying a pale rectangle across the ceiling, and she was lying on top of the covers in her clothes because undressing felt like agreeing to stay.

Three knocks. Slow ones. Spaced far enough apart that each one had time to finish before the next began, the way a polite visitor knocks on a door they are not entirely sure they should be knocking on. Not wood-on-wood, exactly. Deeper than that. A knuckle against stone, or stone against stone, muffled by the earth itself, rising up through the joists and the subfloor and the soles of her bare feet where they hung off the edge of the mattress.

*Knock.*

She did not move. Her heart performed a single hard contraction, like a fist closing, and then went carefully quiet, the way it does when the body understands something before the mind consents to.

*Knock.*

The house settled around her. That was the phrase she reached for, and she held onto it the way a drowning woman holds a plank—*the house is settling*—because houses do settle, everyone knows this, old houses especially. The Iversen farmhouse had been standing since 1911. It had weathered ninety winters of freeze and thaw, its foundation stones shifting by fractions of inches, its timbers shrinking and swelling with the seasons, groaning at night like an old man turning over in bed. She had grown up hearing those sounds. Every child raised in an old house learns to translate its language: the tick of cooling pipes, the sigh of wind finding the eaves, the long creak of a floorboard contracting as the temperature drops. None of it meant anything. All of it meant nothing.

*Knock.*

But settling didn't have rhythm. Settling didn't come in threes. Settling didn't sound, if she was honest—and honesty arrived now, uninvited, in the dark, with the terrible clarity of 3 A.M.—settling didn't sound like *someone down there, knocking patiently, waiting to be answered.*

Her grandmother's voice surfaced from thirty years down, unbidden, in the exact cadence Edith Iversen had used when Theo was seven and had asked why they never went into the cellar after supper: *Nothing down there wants anything from you, Theo-girl, as long as you don't go looking for it.* And then, softer, almost to herself, the sentence that had lived in Theo ever since like a splinter too deep to work out: *And don't you ever answer it, whatever it sounds like.*

Whatever *it*. Seven-year-old Theo hadn't thought to ask what *it* was. Thirty-four-year-old Theo lay in her dead brother's bed and understood, with a slow cold spreading through her chest, that she had just spent three knocks deciding whether to answer.

Because that was the thing about a knock. A knock was a question. It demanded a response the way a name demands a face. And some animal part of her brain—the part that predated language, that predated houses, that had kept her ancestors alive on savannas full of things that knocked—had already composed the answer. *I'm here. I heard you. Come up.*

She pressed her palm flat against the mattress and made herself breathe. Four counts in. Six counts out. The technique her therapist had taught her after Bram died, though Dr. Amara had been thinking of panic attacks and grief spirals, not of whatever this was.

It was the pipes. It had to be the pipes. The farmhouse still had its original galvanized plumbing, and when the well pump cycled—or when it didn't cycle, when air got into the lines—water hammer could produce the most extraordinary sounds. Banging. Knocking. Rhythmic thuds that traveled impossibly far through the system and seemed to come from places no pipe ran. She'd read about it once, years ago, in one of those home-repair articles Bram used to send her with captions like *see, our house isn't haunted, it's just poor maintenance.* Water hammer. Trapped air. Thermal expansion in the hot water heater.

Except the water had been shut off since Tuesday. The county had come out and capped the line at the road when the estate transferred into her name—she'd signed the paperwork herself, watched the technician close the curb stop with a long-handled key, listened to him say *you'll want to winterize, pipes'll burst otherwise.* There was no water in the pipes. There were no pipes running under the cellar stairs anyway; Bram had shown her the layout once, laughing at how the original builder had routed everything along the east wall as if afraid of the west corner.

So not the pipes.

The furnace, then. The oil furnace in the cellar was ancient, and metal expanded and contracted—

Except the furnace tank was empty too. She'd checked the gauge herself the first night, standing in the cellar doorway with her phone flashlight, refusing to step past the threshold, noting the float resting at zero. No fuel, no fire, no cycling. Nothing down there but cold concrete, Bram's abandoned boxes, and the stairs going down into a darkness that her flashlight had seemed reluctant to enter, the beam thinning out at the edges like light through deep water.

Not the furnace. Not the pipes. Not the house settling, because the house had been silent all evening—she had lain awake cataloguing its sounds the way she'd catalogued its rooms, and it had creaked and sighed and ticked exactly as an old house should, and then at 3:00 A.M. exactly, it had knocked. Three times. From the bottom of the cellar stairs.

From the bottom of the stairs. Not the cellar itself. Not somewhere deep beneath the floor. The knocks had come from right at the foot of the staircase, which meant—if they had come from anything at all—that whatever made them had climbed the steps partway and stopped. Had stood, or waited, at the boundary between below and above, and knocked on the underside of the door at the top of the stairs. The door she had closed before bed. The door she had closed firmly, with both hands, and told herself the firmness was just habit.

She had not locked it. There was no lock. There had never been a lock on that door, and now she understood why, and the understanding sat in her stomach like swallowed ice.

*Don't answer it,* Edith had said. But Edith had also taped a list inside the pantry, and rule number two had been more specific than any grandmother's superstition had a right to be: **No answering the well.** Not *no answering noises.* Not *don't go downstairs.* Answering *the well.* As if the well asked things. As if the well had questions, and the house was simply the shape its voice took on its way up.

Theo pulled the covers over herself even though she wasn't cold, the way children do, as if cotton could negotiate with the dark. She stared at the ceiling until the pale rectangle of her phone dimmed and died, and she did not sleep, and she did not go down, and she did not answer.

That, at least, she could tell herself later. That much, she had gotten right.

* * *

Morning came gray and unhurried, the way mornings came to Hollow Creek in October, fog sitting in the low fields like something poured there overnight. Theo descended the main staircase at 7:40 with her hair unwashed and her resolve assembled piece by piece on the way down: coffee first, then the estate inventory, then a call to the lawyer about whether the county's excavation crew had started yet, then—maybe—a call to Dr. Amara, who would say *grief does strange things to sleep architecture, Theo,* and who would be right, probably, and whose being right would feel like a door closing gently in her face.

The kitchen smelled of dust and cold ash. She filled the kettle because the ritual of it steadied her hands, set it on the burner, and turned, and saw the pantry door standing open.

She stopped with her hand still on the kettle's handle.

She had closed it. She knew she had closed it, because closing it had become part of her nightly circuit—mudroom bolt, kitchen window latch, pantry door shut, cellar door shut, stairs, bed. Two nights running she had performed this small liturgy, and this morning the pantry door stood open a hand's width, swinging very slightly on its hinges, as if recently touched. Beyond it the pantry shelves receded into shadow, rows of empty jars catching the gray window light, and the wall where the list had been taped was

bare.

The kettle began its low gathering whistle and she ignored it. She crossed the kitchen and pushed the pantry door wide and stood in the doorway looking at the rectangle of faded paint where the paper had been. Four decades of sunlight had aged the wallpaper around it unevenly, so the outline remained—a ghost of a rectangle, slightly less yellowed than the wall surrounding it, the way a picture leaves a shadow of itself behind. Faint adhesive residue in the corners. Nothing else.

She checked the shelf below. She checked the floor. She got down on her knees, absurdly, and looked under the bottom shelf into the dark gap between it and the baseboard, and found nothing but a dead moth and a button.

The list was gone. Edith's rules—*nothing down the stairs after dark, no answering the well, keep the pantry stocked even in summer, if the water rises don't look at what rises with it*—written in her grandmother's upright schoolteacher hand on the back of a 1970s calendar page, the ink browned with age, the tape yellowed to amber. The list that had survived fifty years in that spot, that had outlasted Edith herself, that Bram had photographed on his last visit and sent to her with the message *should we frame this lol*—gone. Taken in the night. While she lay upstairs listening to three knocks and telling herself stories about water hammer.

The kettle screamed. She let it scream for ten full seconds before her body moved without consulting her, took it off the burner, and stood holding it while the whistle wound down into silence and the silence filled back up with the house's ordinary sounds—the refrigerator's hum, a truck passing on the county road, wind in the dead garden.

Rational explanations presented themselves in orderly procession, and she examined each one like a customs officer waving through travelers she suspected of smuggling something.

Draft. A draft had blown the pantry door open. Possible. The house leaked air everywhere; she'd felt it on her ankles the first night. But the door opened inward, toward the pantry, away from the prevailing draft, and it had been shut tight enough that she'd had to press the latch with her thumb.

Mice. Raccoons. Some animal had gotten in and nosed the door. Possible, except the door was heavy oak, and the list had been taped above head height, and no raccoon peeled tape off walls in clean sheets.

She'd forgotten to close it. This was the most comfortable explanation and therefore the most suspect. Grief scrambled memory; she'd read that too. Eleven days of bad sleep, the funeral, the reception with its chorus of identical condolences—*he's where he was always going*—it was entirely plausible that last night, exhausted, she had skipped the pantry, skipped the cellar door, gone straight up. Entirely plausible. Except that she remembered doing it. She remembered the specific resistance of the pantry latch, remembered the cellar door's swollen hinge needing an extra push, remembered standing at the bottom of the stairs listening to the house tick and thinking, with a satisfaction she'd been embarrassed by even then, *all sealed.*

She remembered. And memory, when it contradicted your need for safety, was supposed to be the thing you trusted anyway. Wasn't that the whole point of memory?

She made the coffee. She drank it standing at the sink, watching the fog burn off the back field, and she noticed that she was keeping the window in view, and the reflection in it, and that some part of her attention had stayed fixed on the pantry doorway behind her the entire time, reading it peripherally, the way you watch a stranger on an empty train.

Then she did the thing she would think about afterward with a complicated shame: she went to the junk drawer, took out the roll of duct tape, and taped the pantry door shut. Three strips. Then she went down the hall and did the same to the cellar door, working quickly, not letting herself look down the stairwell into the dark below, taping across the gap with shaking competent hands like a woman sealing a letter she never wanted read.

It's the house settling, she told herself, smoothing the last strip flat. It's grief. It's an old house and a tired mind and a brother's ashes in an urn on the mantel, and none of it means anything.

The words worked the way a bandage works on a wound that needs stitches: covering, not closing. Because underneath the rationalizations, in the place where she actually lived, another thought had taken root overnight, quiet and patient as the knocking had been, and it was this:

The list hadn't fallen. Lists don't fall. Something in this house had read her grandmother's rules, and had decided, after fifty years, that the rules no longer applied.

Or—worse, and she made herself hold this version too, staring at her own pale face in the darkened kitchen window—something in this house had decided that the rules no longer applied *because the person they were written to protect was finally home.*

Bram's ashes sat on the living room mantel in their temporary urn, a plain walnut box the funeral home had provided, and as she passed through the room she caught herself speaking to it, quietly, the way you speak to a sleeping person you don't want to wake.

"Did you hear it too?" she asked. "At the end? Is that what happened to you?"

The box said nothing, obviously. But the house said something—not in words, not in knocks, just in the quality of its silence, which had changed since morning, which felt less like emptiness now and more like attention. Like a held breath. Like something below the floorboards leaning in to listen to what she would do next.

Outside, far down the county road, she heard the diesel rattle of heavy equipment gearing up—the excavation crew arriving early for their first day of digging. Dana Okafor's crew, the lawyer had said. Breaking ground on the new foundation, forty yards from the barn.

Theo looked at the taped pantry door, and then at the window, and made herself a promise she already suspected she wouldn't keep: that tonight, whatever knocked, she would not be alone in the house to hear it.

She picked up her phone and scrolled to the number the foreman had left with the estate paperwork, and her thumb hovered over it, and the house waited, silent, all around her.
