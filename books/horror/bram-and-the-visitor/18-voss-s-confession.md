## 18. Voss's Confession

The ledger lived in a green metal box under the floor of the church basement, beneath a loose board his father could find in the dark, and Reeve Voss was nine years old the first time his father lifted him down the ladder and set the lantern between them like a third member of the family.

"Sit," his father said, and Reeve sat on the cold concrete, knees drawn up, while Harold Voss knelt with the box open and the book laid flat across both palms the way other men held their Bibles.

It was not a Bible. It was an accounting book, the old-fashioned kind with a marbled cover and columns ruled in faded red, and the handwriting inside changed every few pages—different hands, different decades, some of it slanted and elegant, some of it cramped as if written by someone afraid of being interrupted. Names in one column. Dates in another. In a third, amounts.

"What is it?" Reeve asked, because his father had woken him at midnight for this, had carried him through the rain behind the church with a hand clamped over his mouth until they were underground, and a boy wants to know what all that secrecy bought.

"This is what Hollow Creek owes," his father said. "And this is how we pay it."

He turned the pages slowly, letting the lantern light pool in each column, and he did not explain the names. He didn't have to. Reeve knew half of them from the cemetery stones on the hill—the Aldermans, the Pruitts, a Croft, two generations of Merricks—and the other half he knew from the empty lots around town where houses had been torn down and never rebuilt, or from the gaps in class photographs where a child had simply stopped appearing, the way a tooth stops being in your mouth and becomes only the place where it used to be.

"Why do we owe it?"

His father closed the book. For a moment he looked, in the lantern light, less like the man who ran the hardware store and chaired the council and shook every hand at every funeral than like a boy himself, caught somewhere he shouldn't be. Then the look folded away, the way it always did, and Harold Voss became Harold Voss again: solid, certain, a man who believed that a thing done steadily for fifty years was the same as a thing done rightly.

"Because our grandfathers owed it," he said. "And their fathers before them. And because the alternative—" he tapped the box lid once, softly, like knocking on a door he hoped would not answer— "is worse. That's all you need to understand tonight. We keep the accounts current. That's the whole job. Everything else in this town—the church, the council, the harvest supper, all of it—is just the shape we build around the keeping."

Reeve remembered asking one more question, the question any child would ask, the question that had no good answer then and has no good answer now: *What happens to them? The names?*

His father blew out the lantern.

* * *

The year the well refused their offering was 1981, and Reeve was fourteen, and he understood afterward that his father had begun preparing him for the work too early precisely because of what happened that autumn—but understanding came late, the way all forgiveness does, and at fourteen he knew only that something had gone wrong with the arrangement everyone pretended did not exist.

He knew the rhythm of it without ever being told, the way farm children know the rhythm of weather. Once a year, sometimes twice if the winter was long, the men of the council would meet at the Iversen place after dark, and there would be a bucket, and there would be a name spoken low over the water, and the well would take what it was given and go quiet again. Reeve had never seen the taking itself. What he had seen were the mornings after: his father coming home gray-faced at dawn, washing his hands at the kitchen sink for a long time, longer than dirt required, and his mother setting out coffee without asking anything, and the whole house moving gently around the two of them like people walking past a sleeping dog.

In October of 1981 it was the Pruitt girl's turn—no. That's the lie the town told itself for fifty years, that there were turns, that there was fairness in it, rotation like a chore chart. There was no fairness. There was only who could be spared and who couldn't, and the council decided, and the families were paid or persuaded or simply never asked, and the missing person reports went into a drawer in the sheriff's office where Reeve's own father's signature would one day close them. In October of 1981 the council chose a drifter, a man nobody could account for, a man whose absence would raise no flags because he had arrived raising none.

The well refused him.

Reeve learned this the way he learned most things about the arrangement: sideways, in fragments, from the quality of silence in rooms. His father came home on the second night instead of the first, and he did not wash his hands, and he sat in the dark kitchen without turning on a light, and when Reeve's mother asked, her voice very small, *Did it take him*, Harold Voss said, "It wouldn't drink." And then, after a long time, in a voice Reeve had never heard his father use—not in front of him, not ever—"It said it wanted someone sweeter."

His sister Ruthie was eleven.

Reeve will carry the arithmetic of those three days to whatever grave receives him. The council met again. His father argued—he learned this much later, from a dying man's confession, that Harold Voss stood in the church basement and said *not my children, take it from me, take me*—and the others, the Alderman brothers and old Croft and the rest, listened with the terrible patience of men who had done the math already. The well had refused. A refusal meant hunger. Hunger meant it would come up the property line on its own, the way water comes up through a floor, and it would not stop at one. It never stopped at one when it fed itself. The Hollis boy in '74—that had been Edith Iversen acting alone, grief-mad, and even that single unauthorized offering had nearly emptied the town of its nerve. If the well rose uninvited, there was no telling where it would quit.

Ruthie was eleven, and she liked to catch frogs at the creek, and she had a gap between her front teeth she was forever showing off, and on the third night of October she went out after supper to fetch eggs from the cold shed and did not come back, and the town searched for six days with lanterns and dogs and dragging lines in the pond, and found nothing, and closed nothing, because there was nothing to close. No body. No report. Just a girl-shaped hole in a family photograph and a mother who stopped speaking above a whisper for the rest of her life.

Reeve found out the truth at nineteen, home from a semester at State, when his father got drunk for the only time in Reeve's memory—two fingers of rye becoming the bottle—and sat him down at this same kitchen table and told him everything in a flat recitation, dates and names, the ledger recited aloud, and ended with Ruthie. *We tried to buy it a stranger. It wanted a child of the congregation. It wanted us to know the price was real.* And then Harold Voss put his face in his hands, this man who had taught his son that the keeping of accounts was the whole job, and wept the way stone weeps, without sound, without movement, just wetness tracking down through the seams.

Reeve had wanted, in that moment, to hate him. He had waited for the hate to come the way you wait for a train. It never arrived. What arrived instead was worse: comprehension. Because his father had lost a daughter to the debt and had gone on paying it anyway, had gone on chairing the council, had gone on teaching his son the columns and the dates, and Reeve understood—even at nineteen, even furious, even grieving—that his father believed, with his entire being, that the alternative was worse. That if the Vosses ever broke the chain, the well would rise, and it would not take one. It would take until it was full.

That was the creed. Not evil. Arithmetic.

* * *

Harold Voss died in 2009 with the ledger's location written nowhere and known everywhere, the way the important things in Hollow Creek are known, and Reeve took up the green box and the loose board and the midnight trips behind the church, and he has kept the accounts current for sixteen years.

People outside would call it murder. Reeve has had a long time to sit with that word, alone in the church basement with the lantern, and he has made his peace with what it can't account for. Murder requires a victim chosen out of malice. What the council does is triage. Every name in the ledger was weighed against every name not in it. Every payment was the smallest one available. When the well asked for someone sweeter in 1981, the council gave it a stranger first—they tried—and when the stranger wasn't enough, the town grieved, and buried its grief, and paid the next year's entry on time, because the only thing more unbearable than what they'd done was the thought of doing nothing and learning what the well took when left to serve itself.

He thinks of it as flood control. That's the honest word for it. You cannot argue with the river. You can only build the levee, year after year, generation after generation, and sandbag it in the wet seasons, and accept that the levee is made of the town itself—of its names, its children, its silences—and that the day you stop building is the day the water comes for everything.

This is why the Iversen girl frightens him.

Not the noises in her house—houses settle, wells echo, grief invents company. Not the excavation, though Dana Okafor's crew has dug closer to the door than anyone has in fifty years, and that must be handled. What frightens him is Theo Iversen herself: the way she looks at people here, the way she listens. She has the listening face. Her grandmother had it too, near the end—Edith, in her last years, saying things at the harvest supper that made the room go quiet, asking after the Hollis family as if they still existed anywhere but in the ledger. The listening ones are dangerous. The listening ones want to know what the accounts are for, and knowing is the one thing the arrangement cannot survive. Everyone else in Hollow Creek keeps the ledger the way you keep a septic system: necessary, unspeakable, maintained. But a listener starts pulling threads, and Reeve has read enough history to know that towns don't survive their own exhumations. The truth doesn't free anyone here. The truth drowns everyone.

So he has offered her money. Generous money, signed that afternoon, no questions—a kindness shaped exactly like a bribe because it is one, and he doesn't flinch from the word. Better she takes it. Better she leaves with her brother's ashes and her inheritance of nightmares and builds a life in some city where the ground is only ground. He means it as mercy. He needs her to believe it's mercy, and mostly he does believe it himself, in the daylight hours, and the hours when he doesn't are spent kneeling at the loose board with the lantern, checking the columns, confirming that everything is current.

Except.

Except that last week he opened the box and found the newest page damp. Not stained—damp, as though the paper had been held over steam, and the ink of the final entry, Bram Iversen's name, dated this spring, had feathered outward like a wound in rain. And beneath the ledger, where no one had put it, lay a single dried reed from the edge of the Iversen well.

The well has been dry since Reeve was a boy. Dry as a ring of stones, dry as a scar. There are no reeds at the Iversen well. There have been no reeds for forty years.

He stands now at his kitchen window, past midnight, watching the dark line of the creek bottoms, and he tells himself the old catechism: *we keep the accounts current, that's the whole job.* But the accounts are current. Bram Iversen was paid. The door is covered. The Iversen girl has been offered her exit. Everything the ledger requires has been done, done properly, done in the order his father taught him at age nine with a lantern between them—

—and the well is filling anyway.

Tomorrow he will call the council together. Tomorrow they will assemble at the farm at dusk, ledgers in hand, the whole congregation, and perform the rite as it has been performed since 1921, and give the water what it asks for, and go home, and sleep. It has worked every year but one. The odds are in their favor. He repeats this to himself the way his father repeated it, the way his grandfather must have, the litany of men standing on levees built out of their neighbors.

But as he turns off the kitchen light, Reeve Voss hears—from the direction of the creek bottoms, faint and patient, unmistakable—the sound of water rising in a dry place, and for the first time in his life he wonders whether he has spent fifty years holding back the flood, or merely teaching it the way to the door.
