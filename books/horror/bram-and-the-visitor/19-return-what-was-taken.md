## 19. Return What Was Taken

The pipes began to speak on the third night after Theo found Bram's notebook.

She had not slept properly since. The notebook sat on the kitchen table where she'd left it, its pages fanned open to the last entry, and every time she passed it she felt the way she imagined a person feels standing at the edge of a hole they cannot see the bottom of. *It asked for me by name.* Five words in her brother's cramped handwriting, and beneath them nothing—no date, no signature, just the faint impression of the pen pressed hard enough to tear through to the next page, as if he had been gripping it too tightly, as if his hand had been shaking.

She had told herself she would sleep in the truck. She had told herself a great many things.

At 2:47 in the morning—the hour she was beginning to think of as the house's waking hour—a sound came up through the floorboards that was not water moving through old copper. It was too deliberate for that. Too rhythmic. A knocking, but not the three slow knocks from the cellar stairs; this was different, a shuddering pulse that traveled the length of the plumbing like something being pushed through a throat, and then stopped, and then came again, and Theo lay in her childhood bed with the quilt pulled to her chin and understood, with a clarity that arrived whole and terrible, that the house was trying to form words.

*Return.*

The first word came out of the bathroom faucet when she finally made herself get up and stand in the doorway of the little hall bathroom, the one with the clawfoot tub and the mirror her mother had covered with a shelf of perfume bottles because she didn't like seeing herself first thing in the morning. The faucet was off. The pipes behind the wall groaned, swelled, and from the spout—not dripping, not running, but speaking, each syllable a wet percussive burst of air forced through standing water—came:

*Re-turn.*

Theo did not scream. She would remember later, with a strange detached pride, that she did not scream. What she did was stand there in her flannel pajamas with her arms wrapped around herself while the cold of the unheated room crawled up through the floorboards into her bare feet, and she listened, because some animal part of her brain had already done the arithmetic: the well had refilled overnight. Bram's notebook pages about it had been damp. And now the water that had risen fifty feet down a stone shaft was rising through the bones of the house itself, filling the pipes, filling the walls, and whatever filled it was using the plumbing the way a child uses two tin cans and a string.

*What,* she said. Her voice sounded very small in the tiled room. *What was taken.*

The answer took so long to come that she almost convinced herself she'd imagined the first word. Then the bathtub drain exhaled—a long, wet, rattling breath—and the voice came up out of it, and it was not one voice. It was several, layered, the way a chord is several notes: something old underneath, something patient, and threaded through them, thin and high and unmistakable, a child's voice saying words it could not possibly have known how to say.

*Nineteen seventy-four.*

*Return what was taken.*

*Before the well refills.*

*Or it takes again.*

The last sentence landed in the room like a stone dropped down a shaft, and Theo counted the seconds waiting for it to hit bottom, and no bottom came.

---

She made coffee at four in the morning because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. The kitchen was dark except for the window over the sink, where the yard light her father had installed in 1989 threw its orange circle across the frost-killed garden, and beyond the circle the property line ran invisible toward the old well—the dry well, the dead well, the well that had been a ring of stones and a hazard sign since Theo was eight years old and which now, according to Bram's measurements and the damp pages and the smell that had crept into the mudroom, stood half-full of black water.

She thought about calling Dana Okafor. She had the foreman's number written on the back of a receipt from the Gas-N-Go, and she had picked up her phone twice since the funeral reception, twice since *He's where he was always going*, and both times she had put it down again without dialing, because saying any of this out loud—to anyone, even the one person in Hollow Creek who seemed to want the truth—would make it real in a way that the notebook and the knocking and the damp pages had somehow not managed.

But the pipes had spoken. There was a threshold past which realness stopped being optional.

Instead of calling, she opened Bram's notebook again and read it cover to cover for the fourth time, and this time she read it the way you read a document instead of the way you read a letter—as evidence, as record, as the work product of a man who had spent months doing what Theo should have been doing since the day she arrived with his ashes in a cardboard box on her passenger seat.

Bram had measured everything. That was the thing that kept catching in her chest: not the horror of it but the diligence. Page after page of careful figures—the well's diameter at the rim (four feet, two inches), the depth to the first ledge (nineteen feet), the temperature of the air rising out of it on successive mornings in October (a degree colder each time, until the readings went below freezing while the mornings themselves did not). He had drawn the cellar door beneath the barn from memory, hinged inside, smooth-faced, handleless, and annotated the sketch: *Dad said Grandpa sealed it in '61. Dad was wrong. You can't seal a door that only opens one way. It doesn't keep things out. It keeps the appointment.*

And then, in an entry dated eleven days before his death, the handwriting changing—looser, faster, the letters leaning like grass in wind:

*It knows I know. Last night it used Mom's voice. Tonight it used mine. It asked for me by name.*

Eleven days. Whatever bargain the town had been keeping for fifty years, whatever ledger Voss's family kept in whatever basement, Bram had stumbled onto the machinery of it, and eleven days later he was dead at the bottom of the quarry road with his truck wrapped around a guardrail, and everyone in Hollow Creek had come to the reception with casseroles and the same rehearsed sentence, and Theo had stood in her black dress accepting their condolences like a woman collecting a debt she didn't know she owed.

*Down payment.* The phrase arrived in her head fully formed, and she set down her coffee because her hands were no longer steady enough to hold it. That was what Bram's death had been. Not an accident. Not even, exactly, a murder—or not only that. A payment. An installment against a larger sum. The house had told her as much in the language of pipes: *return what was taken or it takes again.* Which meant the taking had already resumed. Which meant Bram was the first withdrawal against Theo's refusal—a refusal she hadn't even known she was making, hadn't known there was anything to refuse, because nobody had handed her the terms until she'd found them taped inside a pantry door and buried in a cold cellar wall.

They had killed her brother because they assumed she would do what the town had always done. Pay. Forget. Sign whatever they put in front of her—Voss's relocation package was still sitting on the hall table, that thick cream envelope with its generous figure, its *no questions*, its kindness shaped exactly like a bribe. They had assumed grief would make her pliable. They had assumed wrong, and Bram had paid the difference.

Theo stood at the kitchen window with her arms crossed tight over her sternum, holding herself together the way you hold a cracked bowl, and watched the orange circle of the yard light, and past it the darker shape of the well stones, and she let herself feel all of it at once—the guilt, because she had been the twin who left; the relief, that shameful survivor's arithmetic she'd been carrying since the state trooper called, *it could have been me, it should have been me, why wasn't it me*; and underneath both, rising like cold water in a shaft, the fury. Fury at Edith, who had taken a child to feed her own grief. Fury at the town, which had turned atrocity into a budget line. Fury at Bram, unreasonable and enormous, for digging, for measuring, for writing *it asked for me by name* and then getting in his truck anyway.

And under the fury, quietest of all, the thing she would need most: certainty. They had wanted her pliable. She was going to be the opposite of pliable.

---

Dawn came gray and windless. Theo dressed in work clothes, pulled on her father's old canvas coat from the hook by the door, and went outside to look at the well.

Up close, in daylight, it looked almost ordinary—which was somehow worse. The ring of fieldstones still stood where it had stood her whole childhood, mossy on the north side, and the wooden cover her grandfather had built was still nailed down over the mouth, though two of the nails had worked loose and the board gapped at one corner like a mouth about to speak. The air coming out of the gap was cold and mineral and wet, and it moved—gently, steadily, outward, a slow exhalation, as if the ground beneath Hollow Creek were breathing around whatever it held.

She knelt and put her eye to the gap.

Fifty years ago she would have seen darkness and the far glint of a dropped penny. Now she saw water. Black, flat, utterly still, sitting perhaps fifteen feet down—higher than Bram's last measurement, higher than it had been two nights ago. Rising. And on its surface, distorted by the stone throat above it but unmistakable, was a reflection that did not match the gray morning sky. The sky above the water, down there in the dark, was the color of lamplight.

Theo sat back on her heels. Her breath came out in white plumes. Somewhere behind her a crow called once from the oak and then thought better of it.

"Daniel," she said aloud, to the well, to the morning, testing the weight of it. The name she'd found in the county archive, in a file closed by a deputy named Voss. Daniel Hollis, age eight, missing since June 1974, no body ever recovered, case closed, file dusty, family scattered, one brother left alive in a care facility an hour east who whispered *she lied to both of us.*

The well did not answer. But the exhalation from the gap paused—one full second, maybe two, the way a person pauses when they hear their own name—and then resumed, and Theo got to her feet with her heart slamming and walked backward away from the stones until the barn blocked her view of them.

*It wants to be remembered doing it.* Edith's last unsent letter. Theo had read those drafts six times now, forty years of apologies composed and abandoned in her grandmother's careful hand, and the final line had rearranged everything Theo thought she understood. The well didn't hunger for children. It hungered for witness. For acknowledgment. For someone, someday, to say *yes, this happened, we did this, his name was Daniel Hollis and we fed him to a hole in the ground and then we had potluck.*

But the pipes hadn't asked to be remembered. The pipes had asked for something back.

She turned that over all the way to the smokehouse and back, pacing the frost-stiff yard while the sun climbed and failed to warm anything. Return what was taken in 1974. The Hollis boy had never been returned—his bones were somewhere on this property, wrapped, if the letters were honest, in Edith's shawl, and Theo had spent two evenings walking the grounds with a folding shovel and a dowser's instinct she didn't believe in, narrowing the search to the flat ground behind the smokehouse where the lilacs grew in a line too straight to be accidental. That was the obvious reading: give the boy back. Bury him with his family, say his name over a proper grave, let him go home.

But Bram's death had already been given. The town had already paid, in the currency it knew best. So what remained outstanding? What was still, after fifty-six years, *taken*?

Theo stopped at the edge of the yard, at the boundary between the orange circle of the yard light's reach and the wilder grass beyond, and looked back at the farmhouse—the house she'd been born in, the house her grandmother had ruled with a list of rules taped inside the pantry, the house that legally could never be sold, that covenanted its owners *in perpetuity, undisturbed*. The trap of belonging, the lawyer had called it, apologetically, as if he were delivering weather.

Undisturbed. In perpetuity. As if the property were a body and the covenant a promise not to perform an autopsy.

Her stomach turned over slowly, the way water turns over in a deep lake.

"No," she said out loud, to the house, to the fields, to the listening morning. "No. You don't get me."

But even as she said it she heard the flaw in it, heard the small desperate grammatical error, because the house had never asked for her. The pipes had asked for what was taken. And if the taking in 1974 had never really been about the boy—if Edith's letters were right, if the hunger was for witness, for memory, for the crime itself to be held and known—then what had been taken that June wasn't only Daniel Hollis. It was the truth of him. It was his name, spoken freely, mourned openly, carried home. The town had buried that along with the bones, deeper, and had tended that burial for fifty years with ledgers and lies and rehearsed condolences, and the well had drunk the silence and grown, and grown, and grown.

Return what was taken. All of it. The boy, and the truth, and the names, and the confession, dragged up into daylight where the whole town would have to watch.

Behind her, inside the house, a pipe knocked once—soft, almost courteous, a knock at a door rather than a fist on a wall—and Theo turned and looked at the kitchen window and understood that the house had heard her thinking, and that it agreed, and that agreement from a thing like this was more frightening than refusal.

She went inside and picked up the phone and dialed the number on the back of the Gas-N-Go receipt, and Dana Okafor answered on the second ring, her voice rough with a sleeplessness Theo recognized, and Theo said, "I know where the bones are. Bring your camera and bring a shovel, and Dana—" she paused, looking at the notebook on the table, at the entry dated eleven days before her brother died, "—bring them today. Before the well finishes filling. Because when it does, it's going to start taking again, and I don't intend to be here when it does unless I'm here to end it."

There was a silence on the line, and in it Theo could hear the motel's ice machine, and traffic, and the ordinary world going on an hour away as if Hollow Creek were a place where ordinary things happened.

"Tonight," Dana said finally. "After dark?"

"After dark," Theo said, and hung up, and stood alone in the kitchen of the house that owned her, watching the light strengthen over the lilacs behind the smokehouse, over the ground that had kept a child's secret for fifty years, and she thought: *they took my brother as the down payment on my silence. So my silence is the one thing I will never pay.*

Out past the barn, faintly, the well exhaled.
