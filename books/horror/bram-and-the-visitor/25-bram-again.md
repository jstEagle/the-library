## 25. Bram, Again

The morning light came through the kitchen window the way it always had, slanting and honeyed through the east glass, catching the dust motes above the table where their mother used to set out two bowls of oatmeal and let the twins fight over which one had more brown sugar. Theo stood in the doorway with a cup of coffee going cold in her hands, and Bram was sitting at the table.

Not standing at the foot of her bed. Not dripping in the dark. Not a shape she could dismiss as sleep still clinging to her.

He was sitting at the kitchen table in the daylight, in the gray sweater he'd worn to Thanksgiving two years ago, his elbows on the wood, his hands wrapped around a mug of coffee that steamed gently as if someone had poured it moments ago. His hair was damp but drying, curling at the ends the way it did when he let it grow too long. He looked up at her with an expression of such ordinary, unbearable fondness that Theo's knees nearly went.

"Morning," he said. "You look terrible."

The mug in her hands trembled. Coffee lapped over the rim and scalded her thumb, and the small bright pain of it was the only thing that kept her upright.

"You're not real," she said.

Bram—whatever wore Bram—tilted its head, and the gesture was so precisely him, so exactly the way he'd responded to every accusation of his childhood, that grief rose in her like floodwater. "That's a hell of a thing to say to your brother."

"You're dead." Her voice cracked on the word. "I carried you out of the well house myself. I held the urn. I know what you weigh now, Bram. You weigh nothing."

Something flickered across its face—not hurt, not quite. A recalculation. The way a salesman's smile adjusts when the customer hesitates at the price.

"Sit down," it said gently. "Your coffee's getting cold. I made it how you like it. Cardamom. Nobody else ever remembered the cardamom."

And that was the cruelest part, Theo thought distantly, as her legs carried her toward the chair across from it without her quite deciding to move. Not the face. Not the voice. But the cardamom. Because no one else *had* remembered. No one in thirty-one years had known that she took cardamom in her coffee except Bram, who'd learned it from their grandmother's kitchen, from the tin Edith kept above the stove, and who had teased her about it every single morning of their shared childhood until the teasing became ritual and the ritual became love.

She sat down. She told herself it was strategy. She told herself a great many things.

* * *

The kitchen looked different with him in it. That was the thing she couldn't stop noticing. For nine days the farmhouse had been a mouth—dark hallways, breathing pipes, doors that opened like lips—and now, with Bram sitting at the table, the room seemed almost warm. Almost safe. The light lay across the floorboards in long gold bars. Outside the window, October was doing its best work: the maples burning orange along the fence line, crows walking the stubble field like little undertakers. It looked like a morning from before. From when they were eight and ten and twelve, and the worst thing in the world was that Mom made them share a bedroom.

"I've been thinking," the thing said, turning its mug slowly on the table, "about what you said. At the door. About leaving." It smiled. "You can't, you know. The deed won't let you. Voss showed you the covenant. In perpetuity, undisturbed. You're stuck here, Theo. So I've been thinking—maybe stuck isn't the right word. Maybe the word is *home*."

"The lawyer said I could sign it over to the county."

"And watch them bulldoze this place? Put up condos where we built the tree fort?" Its voice sharpened, just slightly, just enough. "You'd let them?"

"There is no tree fort anymore. There's a foundation crew and a tarped hole in the ground and a town full of people who fed children to a well for fifty years."

The thing was quiet for a moment. When it spoke again, its voice had gone soft, wounded—the voice Bram had used the night their father left, the night he'd sat on the bottom bunk and asked Theo if it was because of something they'd done. She hated herself for how much it landed.

"I died, Theo," it said. "I know that. I remember the water. I remember how cold it was, and how quiet, and then—" it spread its hands, "—then I was here. And I've been waiting for you. Every night, knocking. Do you know how long three o'clock takes to pass when you're waiting for your sister to answer?"

"Stop it."

"I'm not angry. I want you to understand. All those nights, all that knocking—I wasn't trying to scare you. I was lonely." It leaned forward. Its eyes were Bram's eyes, gray-green, flecked, the left one with the tiny burst vessel from the time he'd cried so hard at nine years old about a dead sparrow. "You came home to bury me and instead you're packing boxes to run away. Stay. Stay and we'll fix the place up. We'll do what we should have done years ago. You and me, in the house where we grew up. Forever's not so bad when you're not alone in it."

Theo's hands were shaking around her mug. She drank anyway, because her throat was dry, and the coffee tasted perfect—cardamom and cream and a sweetness that was exactly, exactly right.

* * *

She caught herself, an hour later, measuring the kitchen window for curtains.

She didn't remember deciding to do it. She'd been in the pantry looking for tape—she'd been cataloguing the estate sale items, she had a spreadsheet, she had a *plan*—and then she was standing at the window with a cloth tape measure pulled along the frame, murmuring numbers under her breath, and somewhere in the last twenty minutes there had been a conversation, hadn't there, about whether the south-facing windows would take heavier drapes for winter. Had she said that? Had it said that? The words felt like they belonged to both of them, like a duet she'd been singing in her sleep.

The tape measure hung limp in her hand. Through the window she could see the well on the property line, brimming black and mirror-still, and beyond it the barn, and beneath the barn the tarp that covered the door, and she thought: *it's working on me while I'm not looking.*

When she turned around, Bram was leaning in the pantry doorway watching her with an expression of gentle satisfaction.

"See?" it said. "It's already better. You're already home."

"What are you?" she asked. Quietly. Almost kindly. Because shouting had done nothing, and running had done nothing, and there was a part of her—she admitted it now, in the privacy of her own horror—that wanted to know it properly before she fought it.

"I'm your brother."

"No." She set the tape measure on the counter with deliberate care, the way their mother had taught them to put down tools, so they'd be there next time. "My brother is in an urn in the front hall. My brother weighed sixty-one kilograms and laughed like a donkey and was terrified of the cellar his whole life. My brother—" her voice broke, and she let it, she was tired of holding it, "—my brother wrote in his notebook that it asked for him by name. He was *scared*, whatever you are. He died scared. And you have never once looked scared."

The thing wearing Bram's face went very still.

It was a small thing, that stillness, but it was the first true thing she'd seen in it. Everything else moved too smoothly, breathed too easily, emoted with the practiced warmth of something that had studied love from the outside. But now it stopped, entirely, the way water stops when it freezes, and for half a second the kitchen light seemed to bend wrong around its edges, and Theo saw—or thought she saw—something behind Bram's face looking out through it. Something patient. Something old and wet and hungry for company, standing at the bottom of fifty feet of dark water, listening to the footsteps of the living overhead, wanting not blood, not sacrifice—

Wanting to be *kept*. Wanting someone to stay. Wanting, with the whole of its drowned and nameless heart, for the people above never, ever to leave.

"That's better," it said quietly, and its voice had changed, or she had finally heard it truly—a faint echo under the words, a sound like speech heard through a stone wall, like a voice coming up a well shaft. "You're starting to see me."

"I see what you are," Theo said. "You're not my brother. You're my grief. You're the well wearing my grief like a coat, and you found the shape that fits best because I handed it to you. I walked into this house carrying him, and you picked my pocket."

"Bram," it said, "is wherever Bram is. I'm what's left of him here. Isn't that the same thing?"

"No." And she found, saying it, that she believed it all the way down. "Bram is the urn. Bram is the notebook in my room with his handwriting in it, measurements and dates and fear. Bram is the boy who called me every Sunday even when he had nothing to say. You're a lure. You're the anglerfish light. You're what the well uses when it wants something to stay, and it has wanted something to stay since 1974, and it has never once gotten it, and that—" she stepped closer, close enough to see that the burst vessel in the left eye was gone, that detail lost in the copying, "—that is why you keep knocking. Not because you're lonely. Because you're *empty*."

For a moment the kitchen was silent. The crows outside had stopped calling. Even the house—the house that breathed, the house that knocked, the house whose pipes had spoken ultimatums through the walls—was holding its breath.

Then the thing smiled, and it was Bram's smile, and it was not Bram's smile at all, and it said:

"Then why are you still here, Theo? You figured me out days ago. Dana offered you her truck. The road goes all the way to the county line and past it. You could be eating gas-station coffee by sundown." It turned its mug slowly, slowly. "But you're measuring curtains. So maybe ask yourself who's wearing whom."

* * *

She called Dana from the landline in the hall, because her cell phone had shown no bars since the night the well refilled, and she kept her voice low even though the thing at the kitchen table could surely hear her through any wall it pleased.

"It's in daylight now," she said. "It's sitting at my kitchen table drinking coffee."

A pause on the line. Then Dana's voice, flat and controlled, the voice of a woman who had spent a week refusing to be afraid and was losing. "Doing what?"

"Being him. Being *better* than him. Saying things only Bram would know. It knew about the cardamom, Dana. It knew about the sparrow. It sat there and grieved at me, and I sat down, and an hour later I was measuring windows." Theo pressed her forehead against the cool wallpaper. "I think that's how it works. I think it doesn't drag anyone anywhere. I think it just makes staying feel like love until you forget there was ever another option."

"Can you leave? Right now, today?"

Theo looked down the hallway toward the kitchen. Golden light. The smell of coffee. The sound of a mug being set down softly on wood, once, twice, like a patient knock.

"Yes," she said. "I could. That's the horrible part. Nothing's stopping me. The door's right there." She closed her eyes. "But it's not asking me to stay anymore, Dana. Listen to what I'm telling you. This morning it asked. Tonight it won't need to. And I need to finish this before I stop being someone who wants to."

"Finish what? Theo. *Finish what?*"

Theo watched the light in the kitchen doorway waver, just slightly, like the surface of still water disturbed by a single drop falling from very far above.

"Edith's letters," she said. "There's a line in the last one I haven't been able to get out of my head. *It doesn't want the boy. It wants to be remembered doing it.* I thought she meant the town. I thought she meant guilt." Her grip tightened on the receiver. "But it's been trying to get me to stay for nine days, and it hasn't touched me, hasn't hurt me, hasn't done anything but *love* me in the exact shape I needed. And I keep wondering what a thing like that does when love doesn't work."

From the kitchen, pleasantly, in Bram's voice, pitched to carry down the hall:

"Theo? Your lunch is ready."

Dana heard it. Theo heard Dana hear it—the sharp intake of breath, the rustle of a woman coming up off a motel bed. "Get out of there. Get out now and come to me and we'll figure out the rest from—"

"Dana." Theo's voice was steadier than she felt. Steadier than it had any right to be. "It made me lunch."

She hung up the phone, and stood a moment in the dim hall with her hand flat against the wallpaper, feeling the old plaster breathe against her palm, and understood that she had just made a choice, though she could not yet have said what it was.

In the kitchen, the thing wearing her brother set two plates on the table—soup, from the recipe their mother used to make when they were sick, dill and lemon and far too much pepper—and pulled out her chair for her, and waited, smiling, in the golden afternoon light, for her to come and sit down and stay.

And beneath the floor, beneath the cellar stairs, beneath the sealed stone door hinged on the inside, the well waited too—fuller than it had been that morning, blacker, closer to the lip of its stones—as if something below had begun, at last, to rise.
