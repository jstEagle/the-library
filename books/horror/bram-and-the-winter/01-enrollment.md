## 1. Enrollment

The taxi driver refused to take me all the way up the drive.

"Roads aren't gritted past the gate," he said, which was a lie, because I could see the road, and I could see that it had been gritted — the salt lay in neat gray stripes like something a careful person had done at dawn. What he meant was: *I don't like the look of that place.* People who drive other people for a living develop an instinct about buildings the way sailors do about weather, and I have learned since to trust it. That morning I paid him, tipped him too much out of some nervous generosity, and dragged my suitcase through snow that squeaked like new leather.

Blackfen School sat at the bottom of its own valley the way a stone sits at the bottom of a cupped hand. Gray stone, slate roof, ivy gone black with frost — every boarding school you've ever seen in a film, assembled from a kit and left out in the cold too long. The windows were lit amber against the early dusk, and I remember thinking, with the optimism of a woman who has just resigned from a job she hated and moved two hundred miles on the strength of a letter and a telephone call: *warm.*

It was not warm. I want to be clear about that from the beginning. Blackfen was never warm. It was *lit*, which is a different thing entirely, the way a photograph of a fire is different from a fire.

Mr. Vane met me in the entrance hall himself. He was smaller than his voice had suggested on the telephone — a compact man, silver-templed, with the kind of handshake that is calibrated to communicate sincerity. He wore tweed the way other men wear armor, and he smiled before I'd finished saying my name, as though my arrival were the best thing that had happened to him all year.

"Miss Brightwater. At last." He took my suitcase without asking, which I noted and forgave. "You must be frozen. Come through — Mrs. Pell has left soup. There is always soup on the first day of term; it's practically liturgical."

The entrance hall was vast and panelled and hung with photographs. I did what I always do in new buildings, which is read them. Photographs are a school's confession, if you know how to listen: they tell you what it values, whom it keeps, whom it quietly stops keeping. Row upon row of them marched up the staircase wall — class after class, year after year, going back further than seemed reasonable. Boys in stiff collars giving way to boys in blazers giving way, eventually, to children of both sexes in modern uniforms, the whole century arranged in ascending rows like a choir.

"Founded 1871," Vane said, following my gaze with evident pride. "We've never closed. Not for war, not for fever, not once. There are schools older, but none more *continuous*."

Continuous. I wrote the word down later. At the time I only smiled and said something polite about tradition, because I was tired and cold and wanted very badly for this place to be the fresh start the advertisement in my head had promised.

* * *

My room was in the east wing, fourth floor, above the science labs. It had a bed, a desk, a radiator that ticked like a patient clock, and a window looking out over the quad, where snow lay undisturbed except for one line of footprints that crossed it diagonally, stopped in the middle, and did not resume. I stood at that window longer than I should have, inventing explanations. Someone dropped something. Someone changed their mind. Someone was called back by a voice.

Then I unpacked, and then — this is the important part, the part everything else grows from — I opened a fresh notebook and began to write.

I have kept documentation journals since I was nine years old, when my grandmother died and I discovered that grief makes liars of memory. She had been dead four days before I realized I couldn't reliably recall the sound of her voice, and the panic of that — the sheer *theft* of it — turned me into what I am. I write things down. Conversations, dates, the names of shopkeepers, the exact phrasing of anything anyone says twice. People find it charming until they find it unnerving, and then they find it something else entirely. But it is not a quirk. It is a fortification. A thing written down cannot be taken from you. A thing written down happened, whatever anyone says afterward.

So, first entry, Blackfen School, January the third:

*Arrived 2:40 p.m. Taxi driver would not pass the gate. Headmaster: Mr. A. Vane, approx. 60, warm manner, handshake rehearsed. School founded 1871, "never closed." My room: E4/12. Radiator functional. Soup: leek and potato, excellent.*

And then, because the notebook was open and honesty is cheaper at the end of a page:

*I am frightened of how much I want this to work out.*

That is the thing about fresh starts nobody warns you: desperation looks exactly like hope from the inside. I had spent six years at Halloway Comprehensive being slowly ground into chalk dust — a department head who took credit, a timetable designed by someone who hated me specifically, a corridor incident in November I still can't write about. When Blackfen's letter came — *we have heard excellent things, Miss Brightwater; we believe you will flourish here* — I read "flourish" and thought: yes. Yes, I would like to flourish somewhere the flourishes are possible. I was so busy wanting it that when the cold spots started, I filed them under *old building* and moved on.

They started that first evening. Corridors in old schools are drafty; I know this; I have taught in three of them. But there is a difference between a draft and a place where the temperature simply *ends*, a vertical seam in the air you walk through like walking through a held breath. There was one outside the library. One at the top of the west stairs. One, most memorably, directly beneath the portrait of the founders in the main hall — two severe Victorian faces, a Mr. Aldous Blackfen and a Dr. Edmund Fenwick (the school's name was a marriage of theirs, Vane told me later, "a partnership in every sense"), painted staring slightly past each other as though neither could quite bear to look at the other.

I stood in that cold spot for a full minute on my first night, hands in my armpits, telling myself: *radiator pipes, badly lagged, 1871 plumbing.* And then I went back to my room and wrote it down anyway, because even lies deserve footnotes.

*Cold spot, main hall, beneath founders' portrait. Approx. 3 paces wide. No visible source. Note: check boiler room location tomorrow.*

I fell asleep that night to the ticking radiator and the particular silence of deep countryside, and if I heard anything in that silence — a long exhalation, as though the building itself had been waiting all day to relax — I have decided not to record it.

* * *

Term began properly the next morning, and I met my class.

Form 4B, fourteen children aged thirteen and fourteen, arranged in the basement classroom Vane apologetically assigned me ("the light is poor, but it's quiet — teachers either love it or hate it"). They were good children. Quiet, watchful, well-mannered in that way rural children are when their school is also their whole world. A girl named Anouk with ink-stained fingers who corrected my pronunciation of her name gently, twice, until I got it right. A boy in the second row who laughed at exactly the right moments, as though he'd studied laughter from a manual. And Tomas.

Tomas Reyes sat by the window. He had a chipped front tooth and a habit of writing his essays in pencil first and copying them over in pen so carefully that the pages looked printed. When I collected their first exercise — a short piece on a winter memory — his was the last one handed in and the best one by a distance. Three hundred words about ice on a horse trough at his grandfather's farm, written with such specific love that I read it twice at my desk while the classroom emptied, and then a third time standing up, because some writing makes you restless.

"You write like someone who's listening," I told him at the door.

He looked pleased and embarrassed and said, "Nobody's ever said that," and I wrote the exchange down that evening, word for word, including the tooth, including the light through the window, including the fact that his exercise book had a coffee ring on the cover that he'd tried to scrub out.

Why do I tell you this now? Because in three weeks' time I would be the only person alive holding proof that conversation occurred, and I want it on the record — *my* record — that Tomas Reyes existed, that he was funny and careful and thirteen, that he listened better than any child I have taught before or since.

But that is ahead of us. On that first Thursday, he was simply a boy handing in homework, and I was simply a teacher who had finally, after six bad years, found a place where the flourishes seemed possible.

* * *

It was Vane himself who showed me the photograph. Though "showed" is generous; I found it, and he explained it, and the explanation was the first time I felt the floor of the place shift half an inch beneath me.

I'd been invited to his study for sherry — sherry! at 4 p.m.! — and while he wrestled with the bottle's ancient cork I did what I do, which is read the room. His desk, immaculate. His bookshelves, weighted toward history and administration. And on the wall behind the desk, given pride of place, a large framed class photograph, sepia-toned, dated 1911 in a copperplate caption: *Blackfen School, Winter Term, Form III.*

There were perhaps twenty children in it, arranged on the steps of the hall I now knew so well. And I counted them twice, because something was wrong with the arithmetic of the image — gaps. Not missing persons, exactly. *Blurred* ones. Places where the composition continued but the subject didn't: a smudge where a face should be, a smear of coat-sleeve connected to nothing, one entire region between two boys that looked like the photographer had breathed on the plate at the wrong moment. Four or five such absences, scattered through the rows, and the surviving children all angled subtly away from them, the way people lean from a smell.

"Ah," said Vane behind me, and his voice had not changed temperature at all, which I later understood was itself remarkable. "The famous photograph."

"Some of the exposures have degraded," I said. "The emulsion's eaten through."

"That's the accepted explanation," he agreed pleasantly, and handed me a glass. "Photography was a temperamental art in those days. Long exposures. Children move. The results can be... imperfect."

"But the rest of the image is perfectly sharp," I said. "Every button, every blade of grass. If the emulsion were failing, it wouldn't fail selectively. It doesn't know which faces to eat."

I said it lightly, the way you offer a small correction to a colleague, and I watched — really watched, the way I watch everything — as something passed across his face. Not guilt. Not fear. Something more like a man watching weather approach that he has already decided not to mention to guests.

"The school has always preferred the accepted explanation," he said, and smiled, and refilled my glass, and asked me about my journey, and whether the soup had been satisfactory, and whether I was settling in, and by the time I left the study I had agreed to run the Friday literature society and forgotten, temporarily, the shape of those blurred gaps.

Not permanently. Never permanently. That night I drew the photograph from memory in my journal — I'm a fair sketcher when it matters — and marked each gap with a small x, and beneath it wrote:

*Five absentees, 1911. Vane calls it degradation. Emulsions don't discriminate. Ask about school records, enrollment lists, 1910–1912. Ask casually.*

I underlined *casually* twice, because I already knew, in the animal way one knows things before one knows them, that casual was going to be difficult. That asking questions in this place might be like knocking on walls in a house where something sleeps behind the plaster.

The radiator ticked. Outside, the line of footprints in the quad had been covered by fresh snow, so that no one had ever stopped there at all, so that no one had ever stood in the middle of the white and turned back.

I blew out my lamp. And in the dark, from somewhere below the floorboards — below the basement, below the foundations, from whatever depth a valley keeps its secrets — I heard, or almost heard, or dreamed I heard, a sound I would come to know intimately: a reel turning. Tape moving slowly past a head. As though somewhere beneath my bed, something had begun, patiently, to record.
