## 2. Notice of Inheritance

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Oscar would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days the hospice lost people. Not Fridays, when the weekend's small hope seemed to gather them up and carry them off, not Sundays with their church-bell solemnity — Tuesdays. Mr. Abernathy had gone on a Tuesday. So had Mrs. Okonkwo, and the quiet man in Room 4 whose name nobody could ever quite remember. Death at Rosewood Hospice kept business hours, apparently, and it preferred the middle of the week.

Oscar found the envelope wedged between a flyer for gutter cleaning and a postcard from a dentist she'd stopped seeing three years ago. It was heavy cream stock, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than her coffee maker had cost new. Her name was typed on the front — *Ms. Oscar Villalobos* — no first-name familiarity, no *care of*, just her name in a serif font that looked like it had been ironed.

She stood in her kitchen holding it for a long moment before she opened it, because she already knew what it was. There was only one person left in the world who would generate heavy cream-colored correspondence, and that person had been dead for eleven days.

Her mother had died on a Sunday, alone, in the house in Hollow Creek, and the county had needed nine days to find her because the one person who might have called — the one person who should have called — hadn't spoken to Elena Villalobos in six years. Oscar had learned about it from a voicemail from a stranger, a neighbor woman with a voice like dry leaves, saying *I'm so sorry to tell you this, honey* in the tone people use for house fires and car accidents. She had listened to it twice, standing in the hospice break room with her lunch half-eaten, and then she had finished her sandwich, because grief was not an excuse to neglect her blood sugar, and blood sugar was something you could chart.

The letter was from a firm in Ashford County: *Whitlock & Graves, Attorneys at Law*. It ran two pages, and Oscar read it the way she read lab results — top to bottom, twice, hunting for anomalies.

*Dear Ms. Villalobos,* it began, and then a great deal of legal language about the estate of Elena Marie Villalobos, deceased, and how the entirety of said estate — the property at 114 Corvair Lane, Hollow Creek, together with its contents — passed unconditionally to her sole surviving heir.

Except it wasn't unconditional. The condition sat in the middle of page two like a stone in a shoe:

*Be advised that the decedent's will specifies as a term of bequest that the beneficiary take possession of the property in person, and remain upon the premises for a period of no less than one (1) night, said night being the occasion of the season's first frost. Should the beneficiary decline this condition, the property and all contents shall pass to the Hollow Creek Historical Society, which has expressed willingness to demolish the structure.*

Oscar set the letter down on the counter and looked at it for a while.

There was a P.S., handwritten, in a spidery lawyer's cursive: *Ms. Villalobos — I've handled a great many estates in thirty-one years of practice. I have never seen a condition worded quite like this one. I'd advise you to call me before you decide anything. — R. Whitlock*

She didn't call him. Calling meant talking, and talking meant explaining, and explaining meant admitting that she didn't know why the words *first frost* made her hands go cold, only that they did — the way certain smells did, or the sound of a screen door, or counting backwards out loud.

Because that was the other thing. The letter had arrived the morning after Mr. Abernathy finally reached one.

* * *

Marisol cornered her in the med room two days later, which was where all conversations at Rosewood happened, because the med room was the only place without cameras and without dying people. Marisol had been there twelve years, longer than Oscar, and she had the particular wisdom of a woman who had watched four hundred people die and drawn from it exactly one conclusion: *you say the thing now, because later is a lie we tell ourselves.*

"You're going." Marisol said it flatly, arms crossed, leaning against the locked cabinet. "You already decided. I can see it on your face."

"I'm considering it," Oscar said, restocking the morphine drawer with the focused attention of someone defusing a bomb. "It's a house. Houses are assets. Assets are liquidated."

"Uh-huh. And the condition?"

"It's a weird condition. Lawyers put weird conditions in wills all the time. My mother was—" Oscar paused over the drawer. "My mother was a strange woman. This tracks."

"Oscar." Marisol never used her name unless things were serious; otherwise it was *honey*, *mija*, *girl*. "Your mama died alone in that house and nobody knew for nine days. Nine days. You know what happens to a body in nine days in an unheated house?" She held up a hand before Oscar could answer. "Don't tell me. You know, I know, we both know. And now the house wants you to come sleep in it. One night. The coldest night of the year so far. That's not a will, that's a trap with a bow on it."

"It's a legal instrument."

"It's your mama asking you for something from beyond the grave, and I don't care how many pages of legalese are wrapped around it, that's what it is." Marisol's voice softened, which was worse than the sharpness. "Mija. Six years you didn't call that woman. Six years. Whatever's in that house, whatever she wanted from you — you don't owe it to her. Dead is dead. You don't have to go collect."

And that was the thing Marisol didn't understand, couldn't understand, because Oscar had never told anyone, not Marisol, not a therapist, not the notebook she kept locked in her nightstand: she wasn't going for her mother. Her mother could rot in whatever ground they'd put her in; Oscar had made her peace with that particular debt years ago, or thought she had. She was going because of the frost. Because when she read the words *first frost* she had felt something turn over in her chest like a key in a lock she'd forgotten existed, and because for twenty years — since she was seven years old, since the winter everything went quiet inside her — she had flinched at cold mornings without knowing why, and she was forty-one years old and tired of flinching at weather.

"I'll go, I'll inventory the place, I'll stay the night if the forecast cooperates, and I'll sell it by spring," she said. "It's fine."

"Nothing about this is fine."

"Everything is fine until it isn't, and then you document it and move on." Oscar snapped the drawer shut. "That's the job, Mari. That's life. You chart it and you move on."

Marisol looked at her for a long moment with an expression Oscar refused to interpret. Then she hugged her, hard, quick, the way you hug someone getting on a plane into a storm, and said into her ear: "Call me every hour. I mean it. Every hour, or I'm driving out there myself, and I don't even know where Hollow Creek is, but I'll find it, mija, I swear to God I will."

"Every hour," Oscar agreed, because it was easier than arguing, and because some part of her — the part she kept smallest and darkest — wanted the lifeline.

* * *

She packed that Friday night like she was prepping for a code.

This was not a decision she made consciously; it was simply how her hands moved. Duffel bag, the black one, the go-bag. Two changes of clothes rolled tight. Toiletries. Her stethoscope, because old houses and old women had taught her that a heart could stop anywhere, anytime, and she would rather have it and not need it. A trauma shears. A penlight. Nitrile gloves, a full box, because you never knew. A bottle of ibuprofen, a bottle of diphenhydramine, a bottle of lorazepam prescribed to her three years ago after a bad shift and never once taken, tucked into the side pocket like a talisman she didn't believe in but carried anyway.

A flashlight. Two flashlights. Batteries for both.

She stood over the duffel on her bed and realized she was packing for a medical emergency, and that somewhere in the last ten minutes she had also, without noticing, added a box of latex gloves and a CPR pocket mask, and that the bag weighed eleven pounds and contained nothing — not one single item — that a person brings home to the house where she grew up. No photographs to look at. No comfort object. No book for a sleepless night.

What kind of person packs nitrile gloves to claim an inheritance?

The kind, Oscar thought, zipping the bag, who doesn't intend to inherit anything. Who intends to process a scene.

She checked the weather on her phone before bed. Hollow Creek sat up in the folds of the northern hills, higher and colder than the city, and the forecast for the coming week showed a cold front sliding down out of Canada like something being lowered carefully into place. Wednesday night: low of 29. Thursday: 26. The forecaster used the phrase *first widespread frost* with the bright, empty cheerfulness of a man reading words he didn't feel.

Oscar stared at the little blue icon of the cold front — a cartoon cloud trailing cartoon snow — and felt the key turn again in that lock behind her sternum.

*First frost,* the letter said. *Said night being the occasion of the season's first frost.*

Her mother had been dead eleven days. The will had been drafted — Whitlock's letter said so — fourteen months ago. Which meant that fourteen months ago, dying or knowing she was dying, Elena Villalobos had sat in a lawyer's office and specified, precisely, the exact night her daughter must sleep under her roof. Not *within thirty days of death*. Not *on the anniversary*. The first frost.

As if she knew. As if she'd been waiting for it too.

Oscar turned off the phone and lay in the dark and did not sleep, and around three in the morning she got up and added one more thing to the duffel: a photograph of herself at seven years old, the only one she owned, dug out from the bottom of a file box she'd sworn she'd thrown away years ago. In it she stood on the porch of the house on Corvair Lane, squinting into summer sun, and beside her — cut off at the edge of the frame, as if someone else had been standing there and stepped away at the last second — was a smear of empty porch swing.

She didn't remember putting the photo in the box. She didn't remember cutting whoever it was out of the frame.

But her hands, folding the picture into her jacket pocket, remembered being seven, and shook.
