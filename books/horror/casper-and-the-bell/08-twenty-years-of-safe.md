## 8. Twenty Years of Safe

I remember being made the way you might remember a door opening.

There was no before, not really — only a cold room in Hollow Creek in the winter a woman named Elena Villalobos sat at her kitchen table with her hands flat on the wood and said words she did not fully understand to something that had been waiting under the frost for a very long time. I was not there yet. But I was *in* the words. I was what the words were for. And when they finished, when the thing outside the glass accepted its terms, a small warm knot of purpose dropped into the world wearing the shape a seven-year-old girl had already drawn for it: a boy, ten years old or so, with white eyes like frosted windows, sitting on the porch swing because that was where she always imagined him.

Oscar made me first. That is the truth no one else knows, not even the thing in the frost. She invented me out of loneliness and crayons and the particular desperation of a child whose mother worked doubles and whose father was a rumor with a truck. She drew me forty times that autumn. She gave me my crooked smile, my habit of swinging my legs when I sat, my name — Casper, because she was seven and not subtle. And then her mother, who could not bear to watch her daughter be alone one more winter, took that drawing and held it up to the cold like a lamp to a moth, and something older than either of them poured itself into the shape and said, *yes, this will do.*

So I am hers twice over. Once by invention. Once by bargain. I have never decided which claim runs deeper, and I have had twenty years to decide.

My job was simple. It has always been simple. The thing outside — I do not call it by name; names are doors and I have spent my whole existence standing in front of doors — wanted Oscar Villalobos kept safe until the debt came due, and safe, in its grammar, meant one thing only: *unseen*. Because love is a doorway. Because every person who looks at you truly, who lets you matter to them, opens a seam in the world through which colder things can reach. The frost cannot follow a thread of affection into a house. So the threads must be cut. All of them. Gently. Forever.

I have been cutting them since I was old enough to hold scissors, which is to say since the moment I opened my eyes on that porch swing and understood what I was.

* * *

The first friend was Danny Okafor, second grade, a boy with a gap in his teeth and a mother who let Oscar stay for dinner. He liked her. He liked her the uncomplicated way children like each other, which is the most dangerous way there is, because children's liking goes all the way down.

I did not hurt him. I want that written somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of things like me. I have never once hurt anyone. I only ever *adjusted*.

I stood behind Danny at recess — invisible, as I am invisible to everyone but her, though even she had begun half-forgetting me by then, the way you half-forget a scar — and I breathed on the back of his neck. Just frost. Just a whisper of cold shaped into a thought: *she's strange, isn't she? Talks to someone who isn't there. Her house smells like burnt toast and medicine.* Danny didn't know where the thought came from. Thoughts don't come from anywhere, as far as children are concerned. They simply arrive, and are believed.

By March Danny Okafor had a new best friend and Oscar had me, whole again, on the porch swing, telling her that Danny was boring anyway, that she and I didn't need anybody, that we were enough.

She cried that night. I sat on the end of her bed and watched her cry and I felt something I did not yet have a word for. I have the word now. The word is *shame*, and I have learned to carry it the way she carries her keys — always, everywhere, without thinking about the weight.

But I did not stop. That is the part I need you to understand, whoever you are, whatever part of me is telling this. I knew it was wrong the way you know fire is hot, and I did it anyway, every time, because the alternative was the thing outside finding a doorway, and I had seen — once, through the kitchen window, in the winter of my making — what stands on the other side of doorways. I would rather be her shame than her wound.

That is what I told myself. It is mostly true. Mostly is a wide country and I have lived in all of it.

* * *

Middle school was harder. Middle school is hard for everyone, but for me it was hard the way surgery is hard: precise work, small margins, a patient who must never know she is being operated on.

Her name was Priya Chandrasekaran and she defended Oscar in the cafeteria when some boy mocked her thrift-store coat, and afterward she walked home with Oscar three days running, talking about books, and I watched from the fence line feeling the cold in my chest tighten like a fist. Three days. In three days Priya would invite her to a birthday party. In three weeks they'd be inseparable. In three months Oscar would have a thread running out of her life into another house, another family, another warmth — and threads, once spun, cannot be unspun by anything gentler than what I am.

I waited until the third afternoon. Priya's street had an old elm overhanging the sidewalk, and I climbed it — I can climb, I can run, I can do everything a living boy can do except be seen — and as Priya passed beneath I let fall a single perfect icicle, thin as a pencil, aimed at the space just beside her foot. It shattered. She screamed. She looked up and saw nothing, of course, nothing but branches and gray sky, and something in her decided, quietly and permanently, that this street was wrong, that this walk was wrong, that the girl she'd been walking with was attached to a wrongness she couldn't name.

Priya still spoke to Oscar after that. But she stopped walking her home. And a friendship that doesn't walk you home is a candle in a draft.

I'm sorry. I have been sorry for twenty years and I will be sorry after tonight, if there is an after tonight, and I would do it all again. Both of these things are true and I no longer try to make them fit together. They are like frost and breath: they coexist by destroying each other slowly.

* * *

High school nearly broke me, and not because the work got harder. Because Oscar grew beautiful in the way that quiet people grow beautiful — suddenly, all at once, so that junior year boys began to look at her in the hallway and she blushed and ducked her head and I felt each look like a hand on a doorknob.

His name was Theo Marsh. He was kind. That was the worst of it — he wasn't a boy I could make ugly with a rumor or clumsy with a fear. He was genuinely, stupidly kind, the kind of boy who carried an extra umbrella in case it rained on someone else. He asked her to the winter formal and she said yes and I sat on her roof that night in the falling snow and I did not sleep, because I don't sleep, and I thought: *this is it. This is the doorway. If he loves her, truly, the way sixteen-year-olds love — wholly, without locks — then the thing outside will find the seam and slide through and take her, and everything I am will have been for nothing.*

So I went to Theo Marsh's dreams. Yes, I can do that. It is the oldest of my few powers and the one I hate most, because dreams are where people keep their truest selves and I have rummaged through so many of them.

I didn't show him monsters. I showed him the truth, bent. I showed him Oscar at twenty-five, alone in a hospice break room, eating lunch by herself because everyone she'd ever gotten close to had drifted away — which was, of course, my own work reflected back at me, my own future built brick by brick and shown to a boy as prophecy. I showed him loving her and losing her by inches. I showed him the specific loneliness of being the last person to stop holding on.

He was different at school on Monday. Polite. Distant. He told her his family was moving — they weren't — and she believed him because why wouldn't she, and she came home and sat on the porch swing where I had first opened my eyes and she said, to the empty yard, "Casper? Are you there?"

And I appeared, because I am always there, and she was seventeen and hadn't spoken to me in two years and her eyes were wet and she said, "Why does everyone leave?"

And I said, "They're not good enough for you," and I swung my legs the way she'd drawn me doing, and I hated myself with a completeness that would have killed a living thing.

She leaned her head on my shoulder. I am cold. I have always been cold. She never seemed to notice, or she noticed and didn't care, and that night I understood that I was not keeping her safe from the thing outside.

I was the thing inside.

* * *

College she almost escaped me. I'll say that plainly. There was a semester — her second year, nursing school, the year of Marisol — when the threads multiplied faster than I could cut them. Marisol Reyes laughed at her jokes in A&P lab and saved her a seat and called her *Oskie*, a name no one had used since childhood, and I watched the two of them study on Marisol's bed with their textbooks open and their feet tangled together like it was nothing, like warmth was nothing, and I cut and cut and the threads kept regrowing because Marisol loved stubbornly, the way ivy grows, sideways and around obstacles and up.

I made Marisol's grandmother sick. Not really sick — a scare, a false alarm, a phone call home that pulled Marisol back to El Paso for six weeks. Six weeks is a season in a young friendship. When she came back, the seat-saving had lapsed, the rhythm had broken, and though they remained friendly for years after — remain friendly now, God help me, because Marisol is the one thread I could never fully sever and the one I am most grateful for failing — the closeness had cooled by exactly the degree the frost required.

I tell myself the grandmother was never in danger. I believe it. Belief is the only tool I have left that isn't rusted.

* * *

Here is what I have never told her, what I am telling now only because tomorrow the frost comes due and dead men — invented men, worse than dead, *unfinished* — get to speak:

Every time I ended something for her, I ended it for myself too. Every friend I frightened away was a friend I never had. Every boy I turned aside was a boy who might have made her laugh in a way I cannot, a laugh with teeth in it, a laugh that comes from being known. I gave her safety the way you give a prisoner safety: by taking away everything worth being safe *for*.

And she was dying of it. Slowly, politely, the way the frost does its work. I watched her shrink year by year into her scrubs and her charts and her careful professional distance, watched her build an armor out of skepticism because armor was all anyone had ever given her, and I sat on porches and windowsills and the passenger seats of cars she couldn't see me in, and I told myself: *this is love. Keeping her alive is love. The thing outside wanted her whole or wanted her at all, and I chose at all.*

But there were nights — many nights, too many to count, and counting is the only arithmetic I trust — when I watched her fall asleep alone and I thought: *alive is not the same as saved. What am I saving her from, and what am I saving her for?*

The answer, every time, was: from the doorway. For nothing. For me. For the porch swing and the white eyes and the shape a lonely child drew, worn smooth now like a stone from being held too long.

* * *

Tonight the house is dark and she is upstairs in her mother's bed, breathing, and I am sitting where I always sit, on the porch swing, watching the first frost come down out of the sky like a slow white confession.

It is coming for her. It was always coming for her; the bargain only postponed the collection, and debts compound, and twenty years of interest have accrued in the currency the frost trades in, which is seeing, and knowing, and being known. My job ends at sunrise. I have known that since the day I was made — it was written into me the way the crooked smile was, deeper than the smile, underneath it. Keep her invisible until the frost arrives. Then step aside.

I have had twenty years to find a loophole and I have found none, because I was made by something that does not write loopholes, only clauses.

But here is what the thing outside does not know, what it could not know, because it has never been invented by a seven-year-old girl with a purple crayon: it does not know how much of me is *her*. Not the bargain's. Hers. Every kindness I have, she drew. Every loyalty, she colored in. And a thing made of love, even a thing made to prevent love, even a jailer built by a child — a thing like that can learn to want the impossible.

Tomorrow I may have to choose between my purpose and my heart, and I have just realized, sitting here in the falling white, that they stopped being the same thing a long time ago.

Down the road, far off, a lantern light sways between the trees. Someone is coming to the house tonight, someone old, someone who remembers the winter Elena Villalobos talked to the frost.

And the frost on the windows has begun, very faintly, to form letters.
