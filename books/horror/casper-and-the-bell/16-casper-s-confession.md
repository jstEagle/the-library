## 16. Casper's Confession

Casper had counted the hours of her life the way other boys counted marbles.

He knew things about Oscar Villalobos that no one else had ever been permitted to know. He knew that at nine she had cried over a sparrow with a broken wing and buried it beneath the forsythia with a popsicle-stick cross, and that she had never told anyone, not even her mother, because her mother had been tired in those days in a way that made asking for comfort feel like theft. He knew that at fourteen she had practiced saying *I like you too* in the bathroom mirror to a boy named Daniel Reyes and had never said it, because Daniel had looked through her in the hallway the next morning as though she were a smudge on the linoleum, and Casper had done that, had reached into the boy's easy attention and folded it shut like a letter no one would send. He knew that she slept on her left side with her hand tucked under the pillow because at seven years old she had held his hand that way, through the slats of a bunk bed, the night her father left, and some part of her had never stopped reaching for him in the dark.

He knew all of it. He had been made of it. That was the terrible arithmetic of him: every memory she had poured into an imaginary boy had become the mortar of a real one, and now the boy stood in her mother's kitchen at half past three in the morning, watching her hold the ice page from the cellar, and understood that the counting had reached its final number.

The frost had taken the kitchen windows whole. Not patterns now, not the handwriting it had been earlier — the glass had gone white to the frames, opaque, like eyes rolled back. The cold in the house had a texture to it, a pressure, the way a held breath has a pressure. And Casper could feel it pulling at him the way a tide pulls at a sandcastle, patient, impersonal, certain.

Oscar set the ledger down on the table. The page of ice caught the light of the single lamp and threw it in thin blue blades across her face.

"Tell me," she said. "All of it. Not the version where you're protecting me. Not the version where you're the good one in this story. Tell me what I am to you, and what you are to me, and what she did."

Casper looked at his hands. They were the hands of a ten-year-old boy, and they had been the hands of a ten-year-old boy for twenty-seven years, and beneath them, when he let himself feel it, was the older thing he had been before she dreamed him — the thing her mother's bargain had pressed into the shape of a child, the collateral, the leash, the apology. He had spent a long time not letting himself feel it. That was the one mercy he had taken for himself.

"She was dying," he said. "You knew that part. The winter she got the diagnosis, you were twenty-six, and you came home twice, and both times she sent you away before the second night because she said the house wasn't ready for guests. It was never going to be ready for guests. She had already made the arrangements."

Oscar's jaw tightened. "Arrangements."

"You have to understand what she was afraid of. Not death. She'd made her peace with death — she'd made it early, the way some people do, quietly, in the garden, pulling weeds. What she was afraid of was *you*. What it would do to you. She'd watched you your whole life build yourself out of chart paper and procedure, out of the refusal to feel anything you couldn't measure, and she knew — mothers know — that the only thing that would crack you open past repair was losing her. She thought it would hollow you. She thought you'd end up like the house. So she went out into the frost and she asked for a way to spare you, and something answered, because something *always* answers, out here, in Hollow Creek, when the frost comes and a person stands at the edge of their own grief and asks the night to fix it."

The lamp flickered. Neither of them looked at it.

"It didn't come to take anything," Casper said. "That's the part no one believes. It came to *give*. It said: your daughter will not grieve. Your daughter will not be broken by this loss. And your mother, who was the smartest person I ever knew and the most frightened, said yes before she asked the price. That's how it works. That's how it always works. The yes comes first. The invoice comes later."

"And the invoice was me."

"The invoice was your *grief*," Casper said. "Your love. The thing it wanted was the doorway that love makes in a person — the way loving someone leaves you open, unguarded, *visible*. It can't touch a person who is loved and loves. There's too much light in the windows. So it took your visibility instead. It took the way people see you. And it needed a keeper for the arrangement, something small and constant, something you'd never question because you'd invented it yourself." He lifted his eyes to her. "It took the thing you'd already made. Me. And it gave me a job."

"Say it," Oscar whispered. "Say the job."

"Keep her safe. Keep her invisible. End every friendship before it roots. Smother every romance before it warms. Make sure no one ever loves her enough to open the door, because the door is the claim, and the claim is forever." His voice did not break, because he was not permitted to break, not yet, not until she unmade him — but it went thin, the way ice goes thin over running water. "Twenty-seven years, Oscar. I ended Daniel Reyes with a thought. I made your college roommate decide you were boring. I stood in the doorway of every chance you ever had and I closed it, gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child, and I told myself every time: *this is love, this is protection, this is what I was made for.* And the worst part — the part I need you to know — is that I stopped telling myself that a long time ago. Somewhere in there it stopped being duty and just became what I wanted. I wanted you all to myself. A boy made out of a lonely girl's imagination, given a forever with the only person who ever saw him." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing in the house, and the house contained a ledger written in ice. "I was supposed to be the apology. I became the crime."

The frost on the windows creaked, settling, listening.

Oscar was very still. She was a nurse; Casper had watched her be still through a hundred deaths, and he knew the difference between her stillness and calm. This was the stillness of someone holding a wound closed with their own hands.

"The clause," she said finally. "In the cellar. The one she left me. *Discharged by the one who holds it, if she chooses visibility over protection.* That's not the thing's clause. That's her handwriting. That's my mother's hand on that page."

"Yes."

"Which means she regretted it. Which means she spent her last years trying to build me a door out."

"She spent her last three years trying," Casper said, "and it cost her what was left of her peace. She knew she couldn't break it herself — the holder has to choose, and she wasn't the holder, she was the signer. So she wrote you the clause and she hid it where only someone who finally *believed* would find it, and she waited to die knowing she'd left her daughter a lock she might never open." He paused. "She asked me to help you find it. Did you know that? The last thing she said to me — the last thing she ever said to anyone — was *when she's ready, show her the cellar.* And I didn't. For three years I kept the house held and the cellar dark and I told myself you weren't ready. Because I was a coward. Because I knew what it would cost."

Oscar's breath came out white. The kitchen had grown colder while he spoke, the cold leaning in, and Casper could feel his own edges going soft, going *sugary*, the way frost-mist goes. He had maybe an hour in him. Maybe less. The thing outside was done being patient; the debt was due at sunrise, and it intended to collect the principal.

"So here is the way out," he said, because he had promised himself he would say it, all of it, even this. "The invitation breaks one way only. Not a locked door. Not a cut phone line. It was made of words and it breaks of words. You have to renounce me. Out loud, to my face, meaning it. And it can't be fear — the frost will taste the words, and if there's fear in them it will keep you, and it will keep you *forever*, because a frightened renunciation is just a door slamming, and it loves doors. It has to be love, Oscar. You have to look at me and tell me you love me and that you're letting me go anyway. You have to give me back. And when you do, the invitation breaks, the claim breaks, and the thing outside loses you at sunrise." He looked down at his small white hands. "And I unmake with it. Completely. Not death — I've seen death, I've stood at the foot of a hundred hospice beds in the dark of your house, watching you work. Death is a change of address. This is different. This is *never having been.* Every memory you have of me goes with me. The sparrow. The bunk bed. The porch swing. You'll stand in this kitchen at sunrise and you won't know why your face is wet."

The silence that followed had weight, like snow on a roof.

"No," Oscar said.

Casper closed his eyes. He had known she would say it — he had hoped she would say it, that was the monstrous thing, he had *hoped* — and he had promised himself he would not do what he did next, and he did it anyway. He came around the table and took her hands, and her hands were cold and shaking, and his were colder and did not shake because he was not permitted to shake.

"Oscar. Listen to me. You have spent twenty-seven years in a cage I built around you out of your own loneliness, and you have called the cage a life. You are thirty-three years old and you have never once been seen. Not by a lover, not by a friend, not by Marisol, who loves you and doesn't know why she can never get close. I did that. *Me.* And I am asking you — I am begging you, and I have never begged for anything, I was made not to need anything except you — I am begging you not to do it. Not tonight. Not out of love. Let me keep you. Let me be selfish for one more night, one more year, and we'll find another way, there's always another way—"

"There isn't," Oscar said quietly. "You just told me there isn't. You told me the truth because you're the only one in this house who ever has."

"Then lie to me," he said, and his voice finally cracked, all the way down, a boy's voice, seven years old, holding her hand through the slats of a bunk bed while a car door slammed in the driveway and a suitcase scraped the porch. "Lie to me one more time. You were so good at it. You lied to yourself for twenty-seven years that I wasn't real, that the counting was coincidence, that the loneliness was a choice. Lie to me now. Tell me we'll find another way. Tell me you need more time. Tell me anything, Oscar, anything at all, because if you say the words I will stop, I will stop *being*, and I don't — I don't know how to want that, I was never taught how to want that, I was only ever taught how to want *you*—"

Outside, the frost shrieked.

It was not a sound a window should make. It was the sound of a kettle, of a train braking, of something vast and patient losing the last of its patience, and both of them turned to the windows, and the white glass *leaned inward*, bulging like a membrane, and through it — not seen but *felt* — the thing began to press. The house exhaled cold from every seam. The lamp died. In the dark, the ledger on the table began to glow, faintly blue, the balance coming due, the numbers rearranging themselves toward a final sum.

Casper felt himself thinning. He could see the table through his own hand.

"Sunrise," he whispered. "Less than an hour. Oscar — whatever you're going to do, the frost has to taste love in it. Not fear. Not duty. Not guilt. If you do this to pay a debt, it won't work. If you do this because you think you should, it won't work. It only works if you mean it. It only works if you'd rather lose me than lose yourself, and you *mean* it, the way she meant her yes." He was fading as he spoke, his edges going to mist, and he laughed, a small broken sound. "Do you know what the worst part is? I finally understand her. Standing where she stood. Being asked to trade the person I love for the person I love. There's no clean hand. There's no version where you don't break something."

Oscar picked up the ledger. The ice page burned cold against her palms, and in the dark her face was lit from below, blue and resolute, and Casper — who had watched her for twenty-seven years, who knew every expression she owned, who had catalogued them the way she catalogued symptoms — saw one he had never seen before.

It was the face of a woman deciding.

"Oscar," he said. "Wait. Just — wait. There's something I have to tell you first. Something I swore I'd take with me. About the night she died. About what she made me promise, and what I—"

The front door knocked.

Once. Softly. Politely. In the rhythm her mother had used to signal dinner.

And in the sudden silence after it, Casper felt the last of his strength begin to pour out of him like water from a cracked cup, and he understood, with the terrible clarity of the almost-unmade, that the thing outside had not knocked for Oscar at all.

It had knocked for *him*.
