## 2. Letters from a House That Hates You

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were the days Bram Delacroix could almost pretend the job was ordinary—medication passes at eight, wound care at ten, the long golden drift of the afternoon when the dying slept and the hallway smelled of lemon disinfectant instead of the sweet, cellarish smell that arrived with the end. Tuesdays were for pretending. The letter should have known better.

It sat in the mailbox between a coupon mailer and an electric bill, cream-colored, heavy stock, the kind of envelope that expected to be opened with a letter opener by someone who owned one. Bram stood in their apartment doorway in scrubs still smelling faintly of another person's last hours and turned it over twice before going inside.

*Delacroix Estate — Final Notice of Inheritance*, said the return address. A law office in Harrow Bend, Louisiana, a town Bram had spent nineteen years not thinking about with the discipline of a monk.

They made coffee they didn't drink and opened it at the kitchen table under the buzzing fluorescent tube, because some part of them understood that this was not a letter you read in soft light.

*Dear Mr. Delacroix,* it began, and Bram's mouth went dry at the *Mr.*, because the lawyer had clearly never met them and had guessed, and guessed wrong, and somehow even the paper of their inheritance didn't know what they were. *We regret to inform you of the passing of your aunt, Verity Delacroix, on the evening of September 14th, at her residence on Bend Road. As the sole surviving member of the Delacroix line, you are named as heir to the property and its contents.*

Bram read the date three times. September 14th. They had been on shift. They had been down the hall, in fact, holding Mrs. Abernathy's hand while she counted backward from ten in a voice like gravel dragged through honey—a voice that had never belonged to Mrs. Abernathy at all—and somewhere in Louisiana, at the same hour, their aunt had been doing the same thing, or its mirror, or its source.

The letter went on about probate, about appraisals, about the necessity of Bram appearing *in person* to take possession. There was a phrase near the bottom that Bram read four times without it becoming any clearer: *Please note that the property cannot be transferred until the annual obligation is addressed.* No explanation. No footnote. Just that, sitting there in legalese like a bone in a loaf of bread.

*The annual obligation.*

Bram set the letter down. Their hands were steady—they were always steady; a decade of hospice work had burned the tremor out of them—and that steadiness frightened them more than shaking would have.

* * *

That night they dreamed of the house, which was not unusual. What was unusual was that in the dream, the house was waiting up for them.

They woke at 3 a.m. with the taste of dirt in their mouth and lay listening to the refrigerator hum, and because sleep was finished with them, they let themselves remember. Nineteen years old, the last summer. The kitchen with its oilcloth tablecloth printed with lemons gone gray from washing. Aunt Verity at the stove, stirring something that wasn't food, humming a tune that bent in the middle like a road washed out by rain.

"You'll want to sign the book before you go," Verity had said, not turning around. "Everyone signs the book."

"What book?"

"The family book." She had said it the way other people said *the family Bible*, and Bram, nineteen and desperate to be anywhere else, had laughed—the wrong laugh, too sharp—and said, "This family doesn't have anything. Not even photographs. Why do you think I'm leaving?"

Verity had stopped stirring. The spoon had gone still against the pot's rim, and the silence that followed had been so complete that Bram heard the other sound underneath it, the one they'd been pretending not to hear for two weeks: the slow, tidal breathing rising through the floorboards from the cellar door. In. Out. Patient as a sleeper. Patient as something that had been sleeping a very long time and did not mind waiting longer.

"It breathes," Bram had said. "Aunt Verity. The cellar breathes."

And Verity had turned then, and her face had been kind in a way that was worse than anger, and she'd said, "Of course it does, cher. It's been holding its breath since your grandfather signed. It's only polite to let it start again."

Bram had left that night. Left the duffel half-packed, left the truck Verity offered, walked the six miles into Harrow Bend with the breathing fading behind them mile by mile and never quite fading, caught the Greyhound east, and built a life out of other people's deaths where the counting was just a symptom, just terminal lucidity, just the brain's circuits firing down like a chandelier losing bulbs—one, then another, then another, until the dark.

Ten years of rationalizations. Ten years of telling students and colleagues and themselves: *it's hypoxia, it's a release mechanism, the dying count down because the body knows how to leave the way it knows how to breathe.* And every time a patient's lips began to move with numbers that weren't theirs, Bram held the hand and nodded and wrote it up in clinical language, and never once—not once—had any of them reached zero.

One. Always one. Then the smile, then the silence, like a door closing gently from the inside.

* * *

The bus dropped them at the edge of Harrow Bend on a Friday afternoon, two weeks after the letter, because Bram had told themselves they'd sell the place sight unseen and had known, even as they said it, that they were lying. Some inheritances you can't handle remotely. Some wounds you have to stand inside of.

The town hadn't decayed so much as settled, the way silt settles. The gas station had new pumps and the same 1970s signage. The church steeple leaned a degree further west than memory insisted. An old man on a bench outside the hardware store watched Bram walk past with the flat, unhurried attention of someone checking a face against a much older one, and when Bram met his eyes he looked down at his hands and began, softly, to count his fingers. One to ten. Then back again.

The house sat at the end of Bend Road where the road gave up and became gravel and then became suggestion. It was worse than Bram remembered and exactly as bad, both at once—the way a face you've dreamed for years is worse than the photograph and yet the photograph was always true. Two stories of cypress siding gone silver-gray, porch posts furred with lichen, shutters hanging at angles that suggested exhaustion rather than damage. The yard was a meadow now, waist-high with goldenrod and broomsedge, except for a strip of raw earth running along the eastern side where, Bram would learn later, a survey crew had recently been at work. The scar of it showed pale against the grass, like skin.

Bram stood at the property line for a long time. This was the ritual of homecomings nobody warned you about: the standing at the threshold, the body refusing what the mind has already decided. Somewhere behind the house, water moved in the bayou with a sound like slow swallowing.

"You the Delacroix one?"

Bram turned. A woman stood at the fence of the neighboring property, sixty-ish, sunburnt, holding a trowel like a weapon she'd decided against using. Her eyes went over Bram the way people read weather.

"Bram," they said. "Verity was my aunt."

"Ruthie Boudreaux." She didn't offer a hand. She was looking past Bram at the house, at the windows, and her jaw worked once before she spoke again. "Your aunt was good people. Strange, but good." A pause with weight in it. "You'll be staying out here, then."

"I'm selling it, actually. As soon as the paperwork—"

"Mm." Ruthie Boudreaux looked at Bram directly then, and there was something in her face Bram recognized from work—from the families who knew what was coming and had decided to say it anyway, once, so that later they could remember having said it. "My mama cleaned for the Delacroixes, forty years. She wouldn't go in after sundown. Wouldn't say why, and my daddy beat her over it twice, and she still wouldn't say why." She gathered herself, trowel and all. "Whatever Verity left you, cher—you ought to look at it in daylight. All of it. Daylight only."

She turned back to her garden, and the conversation was over, and Bram carried the strangeness of it up the walk like a stone in each shoe.

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of dust and beneath the dust of something older and sweeter, the way a crypt smells if a crypt kept a pantry. Bram moved room to room with the late light coming gold through the windows, cataloguing: furniture under sheets, a clock stopped at 7:14, Verity's reading glasses folded on the arm of a chair as though she'd be right back. On the kitchen wall hung the oilcloth tablecloth's ghost—a rectangle of less-faded paint where it had hung for decades. In the pantry, shelves of preserved jars, all labeled in Verity's small backward-slanting hand. Bram lifted one to the light. The label read, simply, *September*. The contents were dark and moved slightly when tilted, and Bram set it back with exaggerated gentleness, the way you set down a sleeping animal.

Upstairs, Verity's bedroom was neat as a hospital corner. On the nightstand, a Bible, a glass of water gone to dust-skinned stillness, and a spiral notebook. Bram shouldn't have opened it. They knew that the way you know not to scratch. They opened it.

It was a record of counts. Dates down the left margin, years of them, and beside each date a number. Bram flipped backward through the pages—*9/12: 4. 9/11: 6. 8/30: 10*—and understood slowly, with a cold that started in the teeth, that Verity had been tracking how far down the counting went. That she had counted her own countdowns like a woman measuring floodwater creeping up her stairs. The numbers grew larger toward the front of the notebook, closer to her death. The final entry, dated the day she died, was written in a hand that shook so badly it barely resembled hers:

*10. It starts at ten now. It used to start at three. It is getting ready to ask.*

Below that, pressed hard enough to tear the paper, one more line:

*DO NOT ANSWER THE DOOR AT NIGHT EVEN IF IT SOUNDS LIKE ME*

Bram closed the notebook. Stood in the dimming room with their heart going like a trapped bird. Outside, the light had turned the color of the inside of a jar, and the goldenrod in the yard had gone to black silhouettes, and from below—from beneath the floor, beneath the foundation, from the direction of the eastern yard where the earth lay scarred and open—came a sound Bram had spent nineteen years pretending was a memory.

Breathing. Slow. Tidal. In.

Out.

And underneath it, faint as a radio through a wall, something else: a voice, patient and pleased, beginning to count.

"—three," it said. "Two. One."

Then, warmly, in a voice like Verity's, like Mrs. Abernathy's, like every dying voice Bram had ever held hands with:

"We're so glad you came home. We've been keeping your place."

Downstairs, in the kitchen, in the empty house, a drawer slid open with a wooden knock, and paper whispered against paper, as if a ledger were being turned to a fresh page.
