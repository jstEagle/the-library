## 8. The Invitation

The door did not open so much as exhale.

Bram stood at the top of the cellar stairs with the ledger pressed to their chest like a shield that had never once worked, and watched the stone swing inward on hinges that should have been rusted shut for two hundred years. No groan of old iron. No grinding of grit. The door moved the way a mouth moves when it has been waiting politely through a long story and finally gets its turn to speak.

Warm air rose out of the dark beneath the house. It smelled—Bram's stomach turned before their mind caught up—it smelled like Sunday dinner. Like browned butter and sage and the particular sweetness of apples left too long in a bowl on the counter. It smelled like every kitchen Bram had ever been loved in, distilled into one rising breath.

Behind them, upstairs, Denny was shouting something about the trucks, about the road, about the way the driveway kept folding back on itself like a tongue returning to the roof of the mouth. His voice came down the stairwell thinned and distant, as if the house were swallowing it in sips.

Bram went down anyway.

They would spend the rest of their life unable to explain why. Not courage. Not surrender. Something more humiliating than either: the pull of a place that knew your name before you did, the way a wound knows the shape of the knife.

* * *

The room below was not a cellar.

Cellars are cold, damp, indifferent. This room was warm as a held hand. Lamplight—actual lamplight, oil lamps with brass fittings gone green with age—burned along the walls without flickering, and the light fell on a long table set for supper. A cloth of white linen. Candles burned down to pools. Chairs arranged with the careful asymmetry of a family that has done this ten thousand times and no longer needs to think about it.

And at the head of the table sat Aunt Verity.

She looked exactly as she had at the funeral home—except there she had been waxy and wrong, a costume of herself, and here she was *more* than herself, more Verity than Verity had ever managed to be in life. Her silver hair was pinned the way she'd worn it when Bram was nine and the world still had edges. Her hands lay folded on the linen, and her knuckles were the knuckles Bram saw now, nightly, in their own hands, arriving uninvited like relatives.

"Sit down, sweetheart," Verity said. "You've had such a long day."

Her voice was kind. That was the unforgivable part. Ten years of hospice work had taught Bram every register of human kindness—the performed, the desperate, the genuine—and this was genuine. Whatever wore his aunt's face was not performing love. It was love, the way a spider's web is architecture.

"I'm not hungry," Bram said. Their voice sounded young. Nineteen again, standing at the top of these stairs with a duffel bag, listening to the breathing.

"You're always hungry," Verity said gently. "You just learned to call it something else. Loneliness. Duty. Professional detachment." She unfolded her hands and turned one palm upward, an offering, an invitation, a mirror. "You count other people out of the world, Bram. Do you know how many? Four hundred and six. You keep the number. You tell yourself you don't, but you do. Someone has to."

The chair at the table's near end pulled back by itself. Not violently. Courteously.

Bram did not sit. But they did not leave, either, and they understood, watching themselves stand there in the lamplight, that not-leaving was already a kind of sitting.

"What are you?" they asked.

Verity smiled, and the smile was Aunt Verity's exactly, the one that preceded fudge or bad news. "I'm the reason there are Delacroixes," she said. "Not the other way around. Your people didn't build a house on my land, sweetheart. They built a house around me. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. A house can be sold. Left. Burned." Her eyes—Verity's gray-green eyes, the family eyes, Bram's eyes—held steady. "A family cannot."

"The ledgers," Bram said. "Every harvest. Every generation."

"A renewal," Verity said, with the patience of someone explaining grace to a child. "An invitation, renewed. I invite, and a Delacroix accepts, and for another year I am fed and you are spared and the counting stays where it belongs—in the dying, at the edges, in the ones already leaving. Ten years you listened to it, Bram. Ten years you told yourself it was oxygen starvation, hypoxia, the brain's last fireworks. And wasn't it easier, those years? Wasn't the world quieter, knowing someone was keeping the books?"

Bram's throat closed. Because it had been easier. Because the counting had never frightened them the way it frightened the other nurses; it had felt like recognition, like hearing a language they'd been forbidden as a child but never forgot.

"This year no one signed," Bram said.

"No one signed." Verity's kindness did not waver, and that made it worse. "Your aunt meant to. She was coming home to sign—she died on the road, four miles out, counting down from ten with a smile on her face because she thought she'd made it in time. She almost did." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "So now the invitation is open. Unclaimed. And an open invitation, Bram, is a hungry thing. It will feed itself if no one feeds it. It started with your crew. It will work outward. Harrow Bend first. Then past the bend. It doesn't know how to stop. It only knows how to count."

"How many?"

Verity looked at them with something that was almost tenderness, almost pity, and entirely patient.

"All of them," she said. "Everyone whose name is written anywhere near yours. Everyone you've ever counted. Four hundred and six, sweetheart, plus everyone who ever loved you, which—" the smile deepened, terribly, "—is a shorter list than you pretend."

* * *

Bram found the ledger on the table then, though it hadn't been there before. Open. Turned to tomorrow's date. The blank line waited under two centuries of Delacroix names, each written backwards—XIRBALCÉD MARB—and Bram understood suddenly why the family wrote them that way. Not superstition. Courtesy. You don't give a thing your true name forward-facing. You give it the reflection. You give it what it sees when it looks at you.

"You want me to sign," Bram said.

"I want you to *come home*," Verity said, and her voice cracked—not with menace, with feeling, real feeling, and Bram hated how much they wanted to believe it. "You ran away at nineteen because you heard me breathing and nobody would tell you the truth. You spent ten years holding strangers' hands while they counted down, and you called it a career, and every night you drove home to an apartment with nothing in the refrigerator and nothing on the walls, and you told yourself that was freedom." She rose from her chair. She crossed the room. She stopped close enough that Bram could smell the lavender water, Verity's lavender water, the exact scent of being seven years old and sick and cared for. "You were never free, Bram. You were unclaimed. There is a difference, and the difference is everything."

Her hand rose toward Bram's cheek.

"Don't," Bram whispered.

Verity paused—and withdrew, gracefully, respectfully, and somehow that respect was the most frightening thing yet. It could wait. It had waited two hundred years. It could afford courtesy.

"I won't touch you," she said. "I have never once touched a Delacroix against their will. Read your own history. We are gentle. We are *family*. Every horror you've imagined about us, every nightmare you fled from—we have never done any of it. We ask. We wait. We set the table." She gestured at the supper laid out for no one. "The thing downstairs in your imagination, the thing with the teeth—that's what happens when we're refused. When we're starved. I am showing you my manners, Bram, because I am hoping you'll show me yours."

"And if I refuse?"

Verity's face did something terrible then: it softened into grief. Genuine grief. The face of a mother at a graveside.

"Then I stop being polite," she said quietly. "And you will hear what I sound like when I'm hungry. So will Denny Okafor, who has three daughters and a bad heart. So will the boy who runs the excavator, who is twenty-three and calls his mother every Sunday. So will the town, and the county, and outward, in rings, like a stone dropped in water. I will count all of it down, and I will not stop at one, because one is a courtesy, and courtesies are for families."

The lamps burned steady. The candles pooled. Somewhere above them, faintly, muffled through two hundred years of floorboards, Bram heard a man's voice counting backwards in his sleep.

"But if you sign," Verity said, and warmth flooded back into her voice like sun through parted cloud, "then it's you. Only you. One vessel, one year, one harvest. They walk out at dawn and live whole lives and die ordinary deaths, counting down from ten like everyone else, and never know they were saved. And you—" she reached out again, slowly, giving Bram every chance to step back, and this time Bram did not, and cool fingers tucked a strand of hair behind their ear the way Verity used to, the way no one had done since, "—you come inside. You sit at this table. You learn the recipes. You learn the names on the foundation stones. You will never eat dinner alone again, Bram. Never spend another night in a white apartment listening to a refrigerator hum. Is that so terrible? Is belonging so terrible a price?"

And God help them, Bram stood there in the lamplight with their aunt's hand at their temple, and every cold year of their adult life rose up and testified. The empty chairs. The holidays worked by choice because working them hurt less than spending them alone. The patients' families who said *you're an angel* and never once asked Bram anything true about themselves.

It wanted to say yes. It wanted it with a force that had nothing to do with the thing wearing Verity's face. That was the trap inside the trap: the monster had merely made an offer, and Bram's own loneliness was doing the selling.

"Yes," Bram heard themselves say, and watched relief move across the dead woman's features like weather. "I'll sign."

Verity closed her eyes. "Thank you," she breathed. "Oh, sweetheart. Welcome home."

The ledger slid across the linen into Bram's hands. The pen was already uncapped. On the blank line, under two hundred years of backward names, the ink waited.

Bram took it. And as they bent over the page—as the lamp flame leaned in like a reader, as the whole house held its breath around them—they remembered, with a clarity that felt less like thought than like inheritance, something Grandpa Odell had said once, drunk on the porch, laughing at nothing: *the stones remember their own names better than we do.*

Bram dipped the pen.

And began to write.
