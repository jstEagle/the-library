## 10. Doors, Uncounted

Dawn came the way it always does at Hollowmere—slowly, apologetically, like a substitute teacher who'd rather be anywhere else. I woke on the floor of Hall B with my back against the piano, my throat raw as a scraped knee, and for one merciful moment I didn't remember anything at all.

Then it all came back. All of it. Every year of it.

That's the thing nobody tells you about getting your memory back: it doesn't arrive like a letter. It arrives like a flood in a house you'd furnished so carefully without it. The man called Lior Galloway had a mother who died when he was nine, a degree from a university whose name he could never quite say with confidence, a habit of counting doors because it made him feel like the world had edges. All of that was still there, still load-bearing. But underneath it now, holding up everything, was a boy named Casper Vane who had run down a corridor in 1974 while his classmates sang behind him, and who had been caught by the dawn and made new.

I lay there and let both lives sit on top of me like sleeping dogs.

The piano was warm. I noticed that before I noticed anything else—the varnish under my palm held heat like something recently alive. On the music stand, someone had left a single sheet of paper, yellowed to the color of weak tea. Miss Vesper's handwriting, small and upright and patient:

*You sang it better than I ever did. He would have been proud. I'm sorry it took fifty years.*

No signature. She didn't need one. And she wasn't there—I felt the absence the way you feel an empty chair at a table. Whatever was left of Edith Vesper had gone wherever teachers go when the last bell finally rings, and I found, to my surprise, that I hoped it was somewhere with chalk dust in the light and no benefactors in it at all.

I got up. My knees cracked like kindling. Fifty years old in one life, twelve in another, and my body had apparently decided to split the difference.

* * *

The school was empty.

I don't mean quiet. Hollowmere had been quiet since the night I arrived; quiet is just a sound this place makes. I mean empty—the way a shell is empty after the creature has gone. Walking the corridors, I kept expecting resistance, that pressure behind my sternum the building had pressed against me all these weeks, the hum under the floorboards, the sense of being watched by something patient and wrong. It was gone. The walls were just walls. Plaster and lathe and fifty years of repainting over whatever happened here.

In Hall B, the lights I'd kept burning every night per the caretaker's rules were all dead, and I didn't turn them back on. Some rules are scaffolding. You take them down when the building is finished.

I passed the classroom where I'd first seen chalk dust falling through empty air. The board was clean. Someone—or something—had written nothing on it at all, and the nothing looked deliberate, like a period at the end of a very long sentence.

Then I went to the wall.

* * *

It took me most of the morning to open the cavity properly, because I refused to do it carelessly. I found a pry bar in the maintenance room, second door past the boiler—and yes, I counted, out of habit, and then made myself stop, because Casper Vane had counted doors too, in the days before, when counting was how a frightened child measured how far away safety was. Some inheritances you keep. Some you set down gently, like shoes too small to wear anymore.

The section of wall behind the piano storage came away easier than it should have. As if it had only ever been waiting for permission.

Inside: chalk dust. A drift of it, fine and white, settled over everything like the first snow of a winter that lasted half a century. And beneath the dust, arranged in a careful row, eleven pairs of shoes.

Small ones. School shoes, mostly, scuffed at the toes the way children's shoes always are, laces knotted by fingers that hadn't yet learned efficiency. One pair of red galoshes, buckled. One pair of church shoes polished so lovingly they must have belonged to a child whose family had very little and loved him very much. Eleven pairs. I crouched there in the cold draft of the opened wall and touched each one, and said each name aloud, because names are the least we owe the dead and sometimes the most:

"Thomas. Ruthie. Edmund. Sarah. Peter. Annabel. Joseph. Margaret. Daniel. Lucy. Wren."

Eleven voices, sung into the walls by a woman who loved them enough to do an unforgivable thing, waiting fifty years for the twelfth child to come back and finish the song. I had spent weeks thinking of them as a haunting. Standing there, I understood them differently. They hadn't been a haunting. They'd been a classroom, holding roll call, waiting for one absent student.

And now the roll was closed.

I don't know what I expected to feel. Triumph, maybe. Relief. What I felt instead was the specific grief of a man standing in an emptied nursery—joy that the children were free, and underneath the joy, the cold knowledge of what freedom had cost and who had paid it. Justice outside the law is still justice, Miss Vesper's letters had argued. But it isn't free. Somebody always covers the tuition.

She had. The children had. And somewhere in the middle of the night, so had I—not my body, thank God, but the man I'd been pretending to be. Lior Galloway, night caretaker, keeper of lights and counters of doors, had dissolved somewhere around the third verse, and what stood up off the floor of Hall B at dawn was somebody else entirely. Not Casper either. Casper was a boy, and boys stay twelve forever or they don't stay at all.

I was something new. A man with two pasts and no permission slip for either.

* * *

I packed that afternoon. It didn't take long—Lior Galloway had traveled light, because a fabricated man knows instinctively not to accumulate evidence. One bag. The sleep-study tapes I took, all of them, wrapped in a sweater. The world should know what happened at Hollowmere, even if the world would never believe it. There's a journalist in the county seat who owes me—owed Galloway—a favor, and favors, unlike identities, survive their owners.

The letters from the piano bench went into an envelope with Miss Vesper's name on it. If any family of hers remained, they deserved to know what she'd done and why, and to make of it whatever truth they could carry. I wouldn't presume to decide for them whether she was a monster or a saint. I'd spent enough time inside that particular question to know the answer was *yes*.

Last, I took the photograph. The class photo from 1974, faces scratched out, Miss Vesper circled. Except when I looked at it that afternoon, the scratches were gone. Twelve faces looked back at me, clear as day, and I knew every one of them. In the back row, third from the left, a skinny boy with a cowlick and a gap in his smile was looking slightly off-camera, the way you do when you're already planning to run.

"That was the plan, huh," I said to him. "Even then."

He didn't answer. But I swear the gap-toothed grin got a little wider.

* * *

The retired janitor was waiting by the gate when I came out. I don't know how long he'd been there; he had the quality of certain old men of having possibly always been there, part of the landscape, like a fence post.

"You got 'em out, then," he said. Not a question.

"I got them out."

He nodded slowly, looking past me at the school. From outside, in the flat gray light, Hollowmere looked smaller than it ever had. Just a building. Brick and slate and too many windows, going softly to seed.

"And you?" he asked. "Who are you today?"

It was, I realized, the first honest question anyone had asked me in fifty years, and I intended to answer it honestly.

"I don't know yet," I said. "But I'm going to be the one who decides. That's more than I've ever been."

The old man smiled with most of his teeth. "Good answer. Your mother—" he stopped himself, and I watched him decide, deliberately, to finish it. "Your mother waited tables in town, you know. After. Used to leave a light on in her kitchen window every night till the day she died. Never told anyone why."

My throat closed. I managed, "Now I know why."

"Aye." He tipped his cap. "Go on, then. Road's clear."

* * *

I walked out through the front doors without counting them.

Understand what that cost me. Counting was the last bone of the scaffold, the final habit of both my lives—Casper counting corridors, Galloway counting rooms, two frightened people separated by fifty years and united by arithmetic. To walk out uncounted was to walk out undefended. Every door I passed was a door I didn't verify, a threshold I didn't measure, an edge of the world I declined to confirm.

I passed nine of them anyway. Or eight. Or eleven. I genuinely don't know, and God help me, it felt like flying.

Behind me, Hollowmere sat in its valley with its windows full of morning, and if you'd told anyone it had once swallowed a classroom whole, they'd have called you a liar, and they'd have been right, and it wouldn't have mattered. The school was done. Whatever it had been—a prison, a sanctuary, a throat—it was just a building now, waiting for the county to decide between demolition and asbestos abatement, which are often the same decision wearing different coats.

At the gate I stopped once and looked back. Not at the school. At the wall. Somewhere in that brick and plaster, eleven children had waited half a century, and I wanted—needed—to mark the spot somehow. But there was nothing to mark. No plaque, no scar, not even a cold spot. Just a wall, keeping nothing but itself.

"Goodbye, Wren," I said quietly, to the smallest voice on the tapes, the one who'd first said my name. "Tell them the twelfth made it. Tell them he remembers."

And then I turned and walked down the road toward the bus stop in town, carrying one bag, twelve lifetimes' worth of memory, and a melody I will hum until the day I die—not the binding verses, never those, but the last one. The verse of release. Mine now. The only verse that was ever truly mine.

The bus came at noon. The driver asked where I was headed, and I opened my mouth to give the answer I'd prepared—the safe one, the Galloway answer, some neutral town where a man with no verifiable past could start again invisible.

What came out instead was my real name. Both of them.

"Casper," I said. "And then wherever the road goes after that."

The driver looked at me a moment too long, then shrugged and took my fare. As the school shrank in the window behind me, I caught myself doing it—one last time, involuntarily, my lips moving with the old arithmetic: *one door, two doors, three—*

I stopped. I laughed until my eyes stung. And I let the count go, unnumbered, unfinished, free—

—which is when I noticed, folded into my jacket pocket, a sheet of paper in handwriting I knew better than my own, dated tomorrow.
