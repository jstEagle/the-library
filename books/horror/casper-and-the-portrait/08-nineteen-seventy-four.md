## 8. Nineteen Seventy-Four

Odile did not decide to sleep in the house. The house decided it for them, the way it had decided so much already — by degrees, by inches, by the slow narrowing of every alternative until only one doorway remained open. The bank's deadline sat in their inbox like a stone. The appraisal had to be finalized. And Odile had learned, over eleven nights in the Harrow house, that the numbers would not come while the house was still holding its breath.

So they set up the cot in the front parlor, the one room whose dimensions had stayed honest, and they laid out the ritual the way they always did: lights on, all of them, the lamp in the hall, the lamp in the kitchen, the flashlight on the floor beside the cot pointed at the door. Doors counted. Exits logged. The recorder running on the side table, its red eye open.

"You can talk to me," Odile said aloud, to the room, to the frost, to whoever was listening. "I know where you are. I found the well. I'm going to bring you up. I promise. But I need to know who did this. I need to know what happened."

The house said nothing. The furnace ticked. Outside, the temperature was falling toward the first hard frost of the season, and the windows were already fogging at the corners, patient as paper waiting for ink.

Odile slept.

The transition was not a dream. That was the first thing they understood, later, when they tried to reconstruct it: dreams have the texture of dreams, the soft illogic, the melting of one scene into the next. This had the texture of a hallway. Cold floorboards under bare feet. The particular smell of a house in deep winter, woodsmoke and wool and the mineral breath of frozen earth coming up through the cellar.

Odile was small.

They looked down and saw hands — a child's hands, chapped at the knuckles, the left thumbnail bitten to the quick. A flannel pajama top, faded plaid, buttons mismatched by one. When they breathed, the breath was high and thin and came from a chest barely wider than a loaf of bread.

*Etienne.*

The name arrived not as a thought but as a fact, the way a surveyor knows a boundary line — not because anyone told them, but because the ground itself declares it. Odile was walking the upstairs hall of the Harrow house in the winter of 1974, and they were walking it from three feet off the floor, and the doorways were the right size now, generous, and the house was warm and full of the sounds of a family packing.

That was the wrong part. That was the part Odile's professional mind seized on and would not release, even inside the body of a nine-year-old boy: *packing*. The sound of suitcases being dragged from under beds. The wet slap of tape being pulled off a roll. Their mother's voice downstairs, low and fast, saying *not now, Etienne, go play, go play* — and the particular brightness in her voice that Odile recognized from a bad year of their own childhood, the brightness that means *fear*.

The boy's memory did not understand it. The boy's memory was a child's memory, and so it kept reaching for explanations that would make the world kind: *We're going on a trip. We're going to Grandma's. Papa got a new job somewhere warm.* The memory reached and reached, and Odile, riding inside it like a passenger in their own skull, felt each explanation fail and fall away, one by one, the way rungs fail under a climber.

Odile tried to speak. To the mother, to the house, to the recorder fifty years away on a side table. What came out was a child's voice, and what it said was:

"Mama, am I coming too?"

And the mother — Odile saw her only in fragments, a jaw, a hand gripping a suitcase handle too hard, a wedding ring turned so the stone faced inward — the mother knelt down. She smelled of cold air and cigarettes and something under it, something sour, the smell of a person who has been crying in a car. She took the boy's face in her two cold hands and she said:

"Etienne. Listen to me. You have to be quiet now. Can you be quiet? Can you be my brave boy and be quiet?"

And Odile, inside him, felt the answer rise — *yes, yes, anything, Mama* — because that is what children say, because a child will agree to anything if the fear in the adult's voice is large enough, and the agreement was the worst thing Odile had ever felt, worse than the frost, worse than the breathing on the tape, because it was the moment a boy signed a contract he could not read.

* * *

The house showed the rest in order, and it did not look away, and it did not let Odile look away.

The father. Odile had pieced him together from records — the deed, the tax filings, the signature that pressed so hard it tore through three sheets of paper. In the memory he was a shape in the doorway of the kitchen, big the way weather is big, and the boy's body knew him the way a dog's body knows a storm: before the thunder, in the joints. The father did not look at Etienne once that night. That was the detail that would come back to Odile for the rest of their life. He packed the truck. He loaded the suitcases. He spoke to the mother in a voice too low to carry words, and the words that did carry were not words but weights: *the boy, the boy, what about the boy* — and the father's silence in reply was not hesitation. It was arithmetic. Odile felt it happen in the boy's chest, the arithmetic being done, the weighing of one small life against whatever the father had done — Odile still did not know, even now, even inside the memory; the house kept that room dark, even for itself — and the sum came out, and the sum was that Etienne would not fit in the truck.

The mother came up the stairs one last time. She was carrying a blanket and a glass of water, and her hands were shaking so badly the water rang against the glass like a small bell. She took the boy down the back stairs, through the kitchen that smelled of bleach — *bleach, why bleach, Odile's mind clawed at it, why bleach in a kitchen at midnight* — and out the back door into the blue-black cold, across the dead garden, past the greenhouse, its glass panes catching the moon like a hundred small eyes.

The well cap was off.

The boy did not understand. That was the thing, that was the unbearable thing, the thing Odile would carry up out of 1974 like a stone in the chest: *he did not understand.* He thought it was a game. He thought they were playing hide, the way Papa made him hide sometimes, the way you had to be quiet and still and the quietest boy won. He climbed down the iron rungs because his mother told him to, because she was crying and he wanted to make the crying stop, because he was her brave boy.

"Count to a hundred," she said from above. Her voice came down the stone throat of the well and broke into pieces on the way. "Count to a hundred and don't come out until I come get you. I'll come get you. I promise. I'll come get you, my love."

"One," said Etienne, in Odile's mouth, in Odile's memory, in a voice that had been counting ever since.

The lid came down. The bolts — Odile heard them, felt them through the stone, three heavy sounds like doors in a bank, like a vault closing, like a decision being filed. The dark was total. The cold came up through the boy's knees where he knelt on the wet stone. Above him, muffled, the truck started. He counted. He counted past a hundred because no one came, and children know the rule: if you lose count, you start over, because maybe the number was wrong, maybe that was the game, maybe if he counted *perfectly* she would come.

He counted for a long time.

The memory did not follow him all the way. The house was cruel in its fidelity but it was not cruel without limit; at some point the counting thinned into cold, and the cold thinned into a kind of waiting that had no hours in it, and the memory let go of Odile's hand the way you release a child's hand at a graveside — gently, and not all at once.

* * *

Odile woke on the cot at 4:17 in the morning with the recorder still running and their own voice on the tape, thin and cracked and counting.

"—ninety-eight, ninety-nine, one hundred. One hundred and one. One hundred and two—"

They stopped the tape. They sat up in the dark with all the lights burning and they did the thing they always did, the ritual, the procedure: they counted the doors. Front door. Back door. Kitchen door. Two upstairs. They counted them out loud, and their voice was an adult's voice, and it shook, and the counting did not help, because now Odile knew what counting was *for*. It was what you did when no one was coming. It was the sound a boy made in the dark to keep from being nothing at all.

They knew, now. All of it. The bleach. The truck. The father's arithmetic and the mother's promise she could not keep, the promise that was worse than a lie because she had meant it, Odile was sure she had meant it, and meaning it had changed nothing. The town that had looked away. The deed signed in January with a signature that tore the paper, as if the hand signing it wanted to leave a mark deeper than ink.

And Odile knew something else, something that had come up out of the well with them: the house had not been haunting them. Not once. Not the frost, not the voice, not the narrowing doorways. A haunting is a complaint. This had been a *file*, opened and maintained for fifty years, waiting for someone with the right instruments — someone who counted, someone who measured, someone who could read a deed and a date and a lie — to come and close it properly.

The house had appraised them, and they had passed.

Odile got up. They did not turn on more lights; there were no more lights. They walked through the parlor, through the hall, past the frost already blooming on the windows — and the frost tonight was not writing. It was waiting. Every pane held the same faint outline, not a word but a shape: a small rectangle, the size of a well cap, the size of a headstone, the size of a name.

They picked up the phone and dialed the number Marguerite had scrawled on the back of the newspaper clipping, the clerk who had gone pale at a name, and while it rang Odile looked out at the first hard frost of the season silvering the dead garden, the collapsed greenhouse, the ground above the well.

"Marguerite," Odile said, when she answered. "It's Odile Delacroix. I know who did it. I know what they did. And I need a shovel, and a truck, and I need you to bring your mother."

On the other end of the line, Marguerite said nothing for a long moment. Then, very quietly, in the voice of a woman setting down something she had carried her whole life:

"I'll bring the shovels. My mother can't walk that far anymore." A breath. "But she can talk. God help us all — she can finally talk."

Outside, on every window of the Harrow house, the frost began to write.
