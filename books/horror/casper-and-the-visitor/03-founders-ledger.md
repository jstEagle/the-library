## 3. Founders' Ledger

The Hollowmere Historical Society kept its archives in the basement of the old grange hall, three rooms of shelving sunk into the hillside like a grave that had been furnished for company. Lior had been down there twice before in their life — once in fourth grade for a local history unit, once to look up a deed dispute for a client whose barn was legally two barns — and both times the air had tasted the same: cold stone, old paper, and beneath it all, faintly, water. Not the smell of water. The smell of a place that remembered water the way an amputee remembers a hand.

Lior arrived at nine on a Tuesday with a canvas satchel: camera, spare batteries, cotton gloves, a notebook with a fresh page ruled for the day's log. The habit of documentation had carried them through the Harrow farm and the porch and the recording that played back as rain on a roof that wasn't there. If the world was going to behave impossibly, then Lior would behave impeccably. Timestamps. Cross-references. A chain of custody for the truth.

The society's volunteer archivist was a woman named Dorothea Pell, eighty if she was a day, who looked at Lior's gloves with open approval.

"Nobody wears the gloves," she said. "Nobody even asks for them. You want the founding families?"

"Harrow. And whoever else signed the original town charter."

"Four names." Dorothea counted them off on fingers gone soft and yellowed at the knuckles. "Harrow, Vesper, Quill, and Marsh. The Charter Families, we called them in school. There's a portrait of the four of them in the upstairs hall. Harrow's always struck me as the one who blinked."

She led Lior to the second room, where the ledgers lived flat in acid-free boxes, and left them alone with a desk lamp and a promise to come back before lunch.

The first box held the town survey of 1841. Lior photographed the cover, the spine, the first page, working methodically, and it was on page eleven that the day's log got its first entry.

*9:41 a.m. — Survey of 1841, p. 11. Watercourse notation ends mid-sentence.*

The sentence read: *The principal water source serving the settlement is the spring-fed well situated at the* — and then nothing. Not torn. Not water-damaged. The page had been cut, a clean rectangular excision, the blade passing so close beneath the line of text that the descenders of the letters hung over the void like men leaning off a cliff. Lior turned the page. The next entry picked up as though the well had never existed: *The settlement is further served by two streams of reliable flow in the western hills.*

Lior photographed the gap from four angles, including raking light to show the knife's path. The cut was confident. Someone who razors a page in a panic leaves a jagged edge. This was a person who had sat down, opened the survey flat, and taken their time.

* * *

The charter itself was in the third box, under glassine, and it was beautiful — a broadsheet in brown iron-gall ink, four signatures at the bottom in four different hands. Harrow's signature was the largest, the H looped like a gallows. Above the signatures, the charter's preamble named the signatories as *proprietors of the Hollowmere lands and of the waters thereunder.*

Waters thereunder. Lior wrote the phrase out twice in the notebook and underlined it both times. Under. Not upon. Not alongside. Underneath. As if the water were a tenant with its own floor.

The second ledger in the box was the founders' account book, 1841 through 1859, and it was here that the gaps stopped being surgical and became a wound. Pages 40 through 47: gone. Pages 88 through 91: gone. And in the surviving pages, a pattern in the entries that Lior might have missed if the fingerprints hadn't already taught them how to look at small things.

The account book recorded ordinary commerce — seed, salt, nails, a mule — but every few weeks, always in Harrow's hand, always entered last, there was a disbursement marked only with a word and a sum.

*To the well, 2/6.*

*To the well, 4/-.*

*To the well, being the agreed portion, 3/6.*

No goods listed. No purpose. Money paid to a hole in the ground, in regular installments, for eighteen years. Lior photographed every entry and sat back in the chair and listened to the building. The grange hall settled and ticked above them the way old buildings do, and the furnace in the corner made its low water-jacketed hum, and none of it was sinister, and all of it was. Lior had the distinct and unprofessional sensation of being read while reading — of the town's attention passing through the walls like damp through plaster, finding them, and considering.

They wrote it down in the log anyway: *11:15 a.m. — Subject experiences sensation of observation. No evidence. Noting for completeness.*

If you couldn't trust the record, you could at least make the record honest.

* * *

The 1841 census fragment was the next box over, and it was the fragment that broke the case open, or seemed to. The census had been transcribed by the society in the 1930s from a deteriorating original, and the transcription survived complete even where the original hadn't. Lior found the Harrow household: Elias Harrow, farmer, 44. His wife, Constance, 39. Three children listed, ages 14, 11, and 8.

The original census, when Lior located it in the same box, had a fourth child's entry. The transcription had skipped it — or the transcriber's eye had slid off it, which Lior suspected was the more accurate description, because the fourth entry was still there in the faded ink, faint as breath on glass: a child listed with no name in the name column, age recorded as *7*, and in the occupation column, in the census-taker's careful hand, a single word.

*Kept.*

Lior photographed it eleven times. The word did not change. *Kept.* Not a farmhand, not a scholar, not *at home* like the other children. Kept, the way one keeps a fire, or a secret, or a vessel sealed against spillage.

And the age — seven. Lior's own age, once, when a mud-stained figure had first appeared at the fence line and introduced himself as Wren, as if the name had been waiting in Lior's mouth all along.

The notebook page got the full treatment: date, source, transcription, and a note in capital letters that Lior underlined until the paper nearly tore: *FOUR CHILDREN. LEDGER PAYS "THE WELL." SURVEY WELL REMOVED. WHO WAS KEPT, AND KEPT FOR WHAT?*

* * *

Lior worked through the afternoon. Dorothea brought tea at one and asked no questions, which Lior chose to read as professional courtesy rather than the watchfulness it probably was. By four, the pattern had assembled itself out of tax rolls, church registers, and the minutes of the Hollowmere town meeting, 1846–1861, and the pattern was this:

The Charter Families had four lines of descent, and three of them had died out, thinned, or scattered — a Vesper line ending in an unmarried schoolteacher in 1912, the Quills gone west after a fire nobody would describe in the society's own history pamphlet, the Marshes reduced to a single grave plot and a street name. Only the Harrows had stayed. Only the Harrows still held land at the town's edge, still held the farmhouse with the sealed plaster, still held — Lior now understood — the office that came down through the generations like a deed: the office of paying the well.

And the payments had never stopped. The account book ended in 1859, but the town meeting minutes took the practice up where it left off, laundered into line items: *maintenance of the common spring, $12.* *Wellhead upkeep, $18.* In 1974 the line item vanished from the public record entirely, and Lior would have bet the contents of their satchel that it had gone private — that it lived now in a checkbook or a cash envelope in a farmhouse kitchen, paid quarterly by a woman named Ada Harrow who knew exactly what lived in her walls.

The last thing Lior pulled, because Dorothea was locking the outer door and the light had gone the color of weak tea, was the society's pamphlet history of the town, written in 1953 by a proud descendant. It was a cheerful little document, all grist mills and church socials, and it contained a single paragraph about the town's water that Lior read three times:

*Hollowmere has never wanted for water, though our founders wisely chose to draw from the high spring rather than the old shaft on the Harrow parcel, which the first Elias Harrow ordered filled and closed "for reasons of sanitation." The shaft has remained closed these hundred years, and long may it.*

*For reasons of sanitation.* Lior stood in the darkening archive with the pamphlet in their gloved hands and thought about the phrase the way a pest controller thinks about a client's euphemisms. Nobody orders a shaft filled for reasons of sanitation. Nobody keeps paying into a closed hole for a hundred years out of tidiness. And nobody — this was the thought that made Lior's hands go cold inside the cotton gloves — nobody seals a room's plaster from the inside unless the thing being sealed in is something the family intends to keep, the way the census-taker's word had said. Kept.

The well wasn't closed. The well had been *converted.* A mouth, ordered shut, and then fed on a schedule for a century and a half, and when the descendants' payments and prayers and half-remembered ritual weren't enough — when the vessel they'd made to carry the confessions down had run off or worn out or simply stopped — then the well had begun to speak for itself, in five small fingerprints, pressed from within.

Lior packed the satchel, logged the final entries, and climbed the stairs into the evening.

Dorothea was waiting at the top with Lior's coat over her arm, and her face in the lamplight was kind and old and carefully blank. "Find what you were looking for?" she asked.

"I found where it isn't," Lior said. "The pages that are missing. The well that isn't in the survey."

Dorothea nodded slowly, and for a moment something moved behind her eyes — not fear exactly, but the tired recognition of it, the look of a woman who had lived her whole life in a town built over a mouth. "My grandmother used to say," she offered, handing over the coat, "that in Hollowmere you don't ask what the water remembers. You just mind what you say near the drains." She smiled. "You'll want to talk to Ada Harrow, I expect. Everyone who finds the gaps eventually wants to talk to Ada."

"You know about the gaps?"

"I dust the boxes, dear. I know which pages I'm not allowed to dust." She held the door open onto the cold. "Tell her Dorothea sent you. She won't like it. But she'll talk."

Outside, the streetlights had come on down the empty main street, and the town lay quiet under a sky the color of deep water, and Lior stood on the grange steps a moment longer, listening. Beneath the silence — beneath the wind in the wires and the far-off complaint of a dog — there was a sound they had been hearing all day without naming it, the way you don't name a drip until the bucket is full.

Somewhere under the town, faint and patient, water was rising.

Lior turned toward the Harrow farm, and began the walk.
