## 10. Inventory

The water came back on a Tuesday, and it ran clear.

Not the gray-brown of Hollowmere's old pipes, not the faint iron tang that everyone in town had stopped tasting because they'd never known anything else. Clear. Cold enough to ache in the teeth. Lior stood at their kitchen sink for eleven minutes — they timed it, because timing was what they had instead of faith — letting the tap run, watching the light bend through it, waiting for something to surface.

Nothing did. That was the point. Nothing ever would again.

They drank a glass of it standing up, like medicine. It tasted the way snowmelt tastes before anyone has touched it, before it has been asked to carry anything down into the dark. Lior thought: *this is what water is when no one has confessed to it.* Then they rinsed the glass, dried it, set it back in the cupboard with the others, and went to work.

Work was almost nothing now. The Harrow farm stood empty; Ada had signed it over to the county and left without selling so much as a chair, though rumor said she'd taken the ledger pages she still had and burned them in a coffee can on her sister's lawn in Veldt County, one by one, until the fire got hungry and she had to stop. The other descendants had scattered or gone quiet in the way Hollowmere people had always been quiet, which was different from silence only in that you could hear them breathing behind it. No more call-outs about scratching in walls. No more sealed rooms. The town's vermin, whatever they had been, were gone — or fed, or witnessed, depending on which word you could live with.

Lior kept working anyway. Kept the truck stocked, kept the log current, kept photographing the ordinary evidence of an ordinary town: mouse droppings under the feed store, a wasp nest going papery gold under the eaves of the church. Documentation was still prayer. It was just that now the prayers were small ones, and nothing answered them, and that was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

On the last Thursday of the month, Lior sat down to write the final report.

They had written reports all their life — for the county, for the pest control board, for insurance adjusters who wanted times and dates and product names. This one would go nowhere. There was no office for it. No form existed that had a field for *imaginary friend, age seven, unchanged after twenty years.* No line item for *children, fed to well, 1891–1911, names attached.* No checkbox marked *witnessed.*

So Lior made their own form. They started a fresh notebook, the good kind with the sewn spine, and titled the first page in block capitals, the way they titled everything:

**INVENTORY OF HOLLOWMERE. FINAL ACCOUNT. FILED WITH: NO ONE.**

And then they wrote everything.

It took four days. They wrote the way they photographed — methodically, completely, refusing to look away. They wrote the call-out to the Harrow farm, the scratching inside the plaster, the five small fingerprints pressed into fresh paint from within the wall, and how they had known those fingerprints the way you know your own handwriting, because once, at seven years old, they had held a hand that left them. They wrote the porch, and Wren sitting on it mud-stained and patient, and the sentence Wren had carried across twenty years like a stone carried in the mouth: *You were supposed to help me finish it.* They wrote that the recording played back as water sounds, and that they had listened to it forty-one times before deleting it, and that deleting it had felt like drowning something small.

They transcribed the founders' ledger, every fragment they'd recovered, gaps and all, and where pages had been razored out they wrote *PAGE REMOVED BY HAND* and left the space open, because an honest inventory records its own holes. They wrote what the well had been fed. They wrote the names — all nine of them, the children, 1891 through 1911, copied out in full, first names and family names, because the founders had recorded them only as *stock* and *provision* and *the usual arrangement,* and someone had to write them as children. Lior wrote each name three times. Once in the account. Once in a list by itself. Once at the very end, alone on a page, the way you'd leave a porch light on.

They wrote Ada's offer and their own refusal, and they were careful there, careful as a surgeon, because the truth was that they had looked at the check for longer than zero seconds, and an inventory that flattered its author was just another kind of razored page. They wrote the sealed room, the nails, the lath scratched with the founders' rite, and the sentence that had rearranged everything: *the well was never hungry; it was starved of acknowledgment.* They wrote the dawn at the wellhead. They wrote their own voice cracking on the third name. They wrote the descendants screaming, and the water rising, and the water stopping — rising no further, going still, going quiet, going *attentive,* if water can be said to attend, and Lior believed now that it could, because they had read testimony to it and watched it listen.

Last, they wrote Wren.

They tried three times. The first attempt was clinical and they burned it in the sink. The second was weeping and they burned that too. The third attempt took most of a night and came out like this:

*Wren was invented by a lonely child in the summer of the drought, out of mud and wanting, and was taken by men in good coats and repurposed as a vessel, a courier of confessions, a thing that carried guilt downhill so the guilty could sleep. Wren held the last line of the ritual alone for twenty years inside a wall, pressing fingerprints into paint so that someone — anyone — would come and see. Someone finally came. Wren was my friend. Wren was never allowed to be only my friend. Wren is dissolved now, into clean water, which is the closest thing to a grave this town knows how to dig, and I am the sole inheritor of everything Wren knew, and this report is the estate's settlement, and I sign it alone.*

Lior signed it. Dated it. Closed the notebook and sat in the dark of the kitchen until the tap, running somewhere in the house's bones, sounded less like plumbing and more like a long slow breath being let out.

* * *

In the morning they took the notebook to the porch.

Not their porch. The Harrow porch — the farmhouse stood empty now, door unlocked, county signage curling off the front window, and nobody in Hollowmere would trouble themselves to stop a pest controller walking onto a property they'd been paid to walk onto for years. Besides, it wasn't trespassing. It was delivery.

Wren had waited on that porch. Twenty years ago and twenty days ago both. It was the only address Wren had ever had.

Lior set the notebook on the top step, weighted it with a flat stone from the dry wellhead — the wellhead itself was fenced now, county tape and a padlock, but the stones around it were loose and ordinary and one of them fit Lior's pocket perfectly. They adjusted the notebook twice, squared its corners, stepped back, and looked at it the way they looked at any finished job: checking the seals, checking the closure, making sure nothing could get back in.

Then they drove home and slept ten hours without dreaming, which frightened them more than any dream would have.

* * *

They went back on Sunday morning, because of course they went back. An inventory isn't finished until you verify.

Dew lay heavy on the Harrow yard, silvering the dead grass, beading the fence wire. The porch boards were dark with it. Lior came up the walk slowly, boots loud in the wet hush, and saw the notebook exactly where they'd left it, stone on top, corners square.

The pages were damp.

That was expected — dew gets into everything, into paper especially, into the sewn spine of a good notebook left out overnight. Lior crouched, lifted the stone, and turned to the last page to check for warping, for bleed, for the small ruin water leaves in ink.

The last page was legible. Every word of it.

But beneath Lior's signature, beneath the date, in the space they had deliberately left blank — because a final account should end with room, should always end with room — there was a new line.

The handwriting was small and round and slightly uneven, the letters leaning left the way a child leans toward the thing they're drawing. It was pressed deep into the damp paper, deeper than pen pressure, as if written with a fingertip. As if written from within.

*Received in full. Thank you for seeing me. — W*

Lior knelt on the wet boards for a long time. The dew soaked through both knees and they didn't move. Down in the yard, water stood in the wheel ruts of the old drive, clear and cold and holding the morning sky perfectly, and in the water's held sky a single bird crossed, going somewhere, unbothered, unhurried, fed on nothing but the day.

Lior took out their phone. Photographed the page — timestamp, geotag, the whole liturgy. Then, because some documents are not meant for filing, they closed the notebook, tucked it inside their coat, against the chest, where it would stay warm and dry, and stood up.

The town was waking. Somewhere a pump kicked on, drawing clean water. Somewhere a child was sleeping late in a bed they had walked away from once and never would again. Somewhere Ada Harrow was burning the last page, or keeping it, or both.

Lior walked back down the porch steps, past the spot where a friend had waited twenty years to be seen, and paused at the bottom, one hand on the rail.

"You're welcome," they said, to the empty yard, to the clear water, to whoever was listening now that listening was the town's whole business.

The dew on the rail ran cold under their palm, and for half a second — no longer, no less — it felt like a small hand closing over theirs, mud-stained and patient, letting go.

Lior logged the time, got in the truck, and drove home through a town that was, at last, only a town.
