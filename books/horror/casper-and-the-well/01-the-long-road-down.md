## 1. The Long Road Down

The road to Casper's Well did not so much descend as surrender. Silas Joubert noticed this somewhere past the third mile marker, when the asphalt gave up its center line and the hedges began leaning in like gossips at a funeral.

They had been driving for two hours from the nearest city with a name on a map, and for the last forty minutes the radio had been doing something strange—not static exactly, but a kind of thickening, as though the signal were wading through honey. Silas turned it off rather than listen to the weather report dissolve syllable by syllable into a hum that sounded, uncomfortably, almost melodic.

"Charming," they said to the car, which was a ten-year-old hatchback named Bessie and had heard worse.

The guidebook—there had been a guidebook, dog-eared, bought secondhand, its section on Casper's Well consisting of one paragraph and a photograph of a duck—described the village as "a hidden gem nestled in the folds of the Vellum Hills." What the guidebook did not mention was that the road down into those folds was single-track, unmarked, and shaded by trees that seemed to have opinions about visitors. Their branches arched overhead in a way that was either picturesque or conspiratorial, depending on one's mood, and Silas's mood had been deteriorating since the cell signal died twenty minutes ago with a small apologetic chirp.

No bars. Not *one* bar. The phone displayed the little empty battery of connectivity with what Silas chose to interpret as sympathy.

They checked it again anyway. This is what people do when lost: they consult the device that has already told them everything it knows, hoping it has secretly learned more out of embarrassment.

The trees thinned. The road leveled. And then the hollow opened below them all at once, the way a secret opens when someone finally stops pretending to keep it—a bowl of green land sunk between hills, holding a village the way a cupped hand holds water. Slate roofs. A church spire. Chimney smoke standing perfectly straight in air that didn't seem to move. A river—or perhaps the same well the place was named for, though surely not—glinting at the bottom of it all like a coin at the bottom of a pocket.

It was, Silas admitted, beautiful. The kind of beautiful that makes you want to take a picture and also check your locks.

They drove down into it.

* * *

Casper's Well performed normalcy the way an amateur theatrical society performs Shakespeare: earnestly, with visible effort, and with certain details slightly wrong. The bakery on the square had bread in the window—good bread, by the smell—but the baker himself stood behind the counter watching Silas's car pass with the fixed attention of a man watching weather he'd been promised wouldn't come. Two children playing hopscotch froze mid-hop, considered the stranger, and resumed their game only after the car had fully passed, their chalk scuffing out a rhythm that sounded, faintly, like counting.

Silas parked outside the schoolhouse, which stood at the edge of the square with the confident posture of the largest building in any small village: stone, two stories, a bell tower that was conspicuously, aggressively empty. Where a bell should have hung there was nothing but a crossbeam and a length of frayed rope dangling in the stillness, turning very slowly, though Silas could feel no wind on their face.

"Huh," said Silas, who was a novelist before they were a teacher and had learned to treat *huh* as the first draft of every important observation.

A brass plaque beside the door read CASPER'S WELL PRIMARY SCHOOL — EST. 1874, and beneath it, in smaller letters someone had added later and clearly regretted: MIND THE STEP.

Silas minded the step.

Inside smelled of floor wax, chalk dust, and stewed tea—the universal perfume of institutional England, comforting in its way, like the smell of a grandmother's house even if you never had a grandmother with a house. The corridor walls bore the usual gallery: crayon harvests, paper snowmen, a watercolor of a horse that had clearly been through some things. And between these, at intervals, framed photographs of classes going back decades. Rows of children in rows of collars, squinting into suns that no longer shone.

Silas stopped at the most recent one. Nineteen children. They counted twice, because something about the number felt provisional, like a word you write and immediately doubt the spelling of.

"Mr. Joubert?"

The voice belonged to a small round man descending the stairs with the care of someone carrying something fragile inside his own chest. He wore a cardigan the color of weak tea and an expression of profound relief, as though Silas were not a new teacher but a rescue party.

"Headmaster Prosser," Silas said, offering a hand. "Though I confess I expected someone taller. Your letters had such authority."

"I type standing up," said Prosser gravely, and then, catching the joke a full three seconds late, produced a laugh like a kettle being persuaded. "You found us, then. Wonderful. Wonderful. We were beginning to—" He stopped himself, recalibrated. "The agency said Thursday."

"It is Thursday."

"Yes! Yes, of course." He beamed, and the beam had cracks in it, hairline fractures of something else underneath. "Come through. Tea? There's tea. Mrs. Aldous made it special."

*Special* turned out to mean strong enough to strip varnish, served in a staff room where four chairs faced each other in a circle with the wary geometry of people who had once argued about something and agreed never to again. Prosser talked. He talked about the boiler (temperamental), the curriculum (flexible), the parents (devoted), the village (lovely), and the previous teacher, Miss Ellery, who had "moved on suddenly" for reasons he gestured at vaguely, as if the reasons were a shelf too high to reach.

"And before her?" Silas asked pleasantly.

"Mr. Okafor. Also moved on."

"And before him?"

Prosser looked at his tea. "We've had a bit of turnover," he admitted. "Rural postings are hard to fill. People come, they find us charming, and then—" he spread his hands, "—life calls them elsewhere. Cities. Careers. The sea, in one case."

"The sea."

"He was a keen swimmer."

Silas wrote none of this down but filed all of it, because that was what Silas did: they collected details the way other people collected spoons, without always knowing why until years later, when the whole drawer turned out to hold something.

"Shall I see my classroom?"

* * *

The classroom was on the ground floor, north-facing, with tall windows that let in a light the color of weak sunlight through water. Twenty-six desks. Silas counted them automatically, the way one counts stairs in the dark. Twenty-six desks arranged in neat rows, and against the far wall, stacked with their legs folded together like sleeping animals, seven more.

"Storage," said Prosser, following their gaze. "Spare desks. Every school has spare desks."

"Mm," said Silas. "And how many children this term?"

"Nineteen." Prosser said it quickly, the way one says a number that has been said too many times. "Nineteen lovely children. You'll adore them. Bright as buttons, the lot. Well. Most of them. Maren Tull is bright as something considerably sharper than a button."

"And last term?"

"Twenty."

"The term before?"

Prosser's teacup, halfway to his mouth, paused in the air. It hung there for a moment, suspended, and Silas watched the headmaster decide—visibly, almost mechanically decide—not to answer honestly, and then decide to answer anyway, because lying outright to a person standing in front of you requires energy that rural headmasters hoard like coal.

"Twenty-one," he said. "But that's just—it's the times, isn't it? Families moving away. The young ones go off to cities, no work here for them, no—" He ran out of nouns and drank his tea instead.

"No work," Silas agreed. "Of course."

There was a silence with a shape to it. Silas had spent enough years around grief and guilt to know that silences have shapes; this one was long and narrow, like a grave dug for something small.

"I'll need the enrollment records," Silas said lightly. "For planning. Seating charts, resources, all that."

"Of course! Of course. The ledger's in my office. I'll have it sent round." And then, briskly, before Silas could ask anything else: "Let me show you the cottage!"

* * *

The cottage came with the post: two rooms and a garden at the end of Latchet Row, furnished in the style known as Inherited From Someone Dead. There was a bed with a quilt stitched in patterns that might have been flowers or might have been eyes. There was a kitchen with a stove that lit on the third try and a pantry stocked, thoughtfully, with tins whose labels had faded to suggestions. On the windowsill above the sink sat a jar of wildflower honey with a handwritten label: *FROM THE HOLLOW — EAT WELL.*

"Eat well," Silas repeated aloud. It sounded less like a wish than an instruction.

Through the kitchen window, the village lay below in its bowl, roofs and chimneys and the church spire, all of it steeped in the amber light of early evening. It should have been cozy. It was trying very hard to be cozy. But Silas noticed—and once noticed, could not stop noticing—that nearly every house had its shutters closed. Not curtains drawn; shutters, actual wooden shutters, latched over windows on a clear evening with hours of light left. Here and there a window stood open and shutterless, and those houses looked strangely naked, like faces caught mid-blink.

Perhaps it was a local custom. Perhaps the light was harsher than it looked. Perhaps shutters were simply what one did in Casper's Well, the way one minded steps and ate well.

Silas unpacked exactly one bag—the one containing notebooks, pens, and a paperback novel for emergencies—left the rest in defiant piles, and walked back up to the school as the light went long and gold.

* * *

Prosser had left the ledger on his desk with a sticky note reading *ALL YOURS!* in handwriting that pressed so hard into the paper it had torn through in places. The office was narrow and smelled of pipe smoke from some departed predecessor, and the ledger itself was a magnificent object: leather-bound, corner-brassed, its spine cracked in the way of books that have been opened at the same pages for a hundred years.

Silas carried it to the classroom, set it under the lamp, and began at the beginning.

Enrollment figures, autumn intake, year by year. The handwriting changed hands across the decades—spidery, then blocky, then a slanted modern cursive—but the numbers marched backward with a regularity that Silas's eye caught before their mind did, the way a musician hears a wrong note before identifying it.

This year: 19. Last year: 20. Before that: 21. Then 22. Then 23.

Silas turned back another decade. The pattern held, patient as erosion. Twenty-six children in the oldest entry still legible, and then twenty-five, and twenty-four, each autumn subtracting one, each year's ink drying over the loss without comment, without asterisk, without the small marginal notes a conscientious headmaster might make—*family relocated*, *transferred to parish school*, *sad circumstances*. Nothing. Just the number, dropping by one, like a clock ticking in a language only the ledger understood.

One child a year. One desk folded against the wall.

Silas sat back. Outside the tall windows, the last light was draining out of the hollow, and the village below was lighting itself lamp by lamp—and closing itself shutter by shutter, one by one, in a sequence so orderly it looked rehearsed. Somewhere down among the roofs, a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

The empty bell tower creaked above the classroom, its frayed rope swaying in air that still refused to move.

"Well," said Silas Joubert to the ledger, to the stacked desks, to the dark coming down the hill. "That's not ominous at all."

The ledger, naturally, said nothing. But when Silas closed it, the cover fell open again on its own—cracked spine, worn hinge—to the same page, the same column of shrinking numbers, as if the book had a favorite subject and intended to keep returning to it.

Silas weighted it shut with the honey jar and went home in the dark, walking carefully, minding every step.
