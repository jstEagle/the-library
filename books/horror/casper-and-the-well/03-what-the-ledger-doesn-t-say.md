## 3. What the Ledger Doesn't Say

The school records room lived at the end of a corridor that smelled of chalk dust and old radiator paint, behind a door with a frosted glass panel that read, in flaking gold letters, *ARCHIVE — KNOCK FIRST*. Nobody had knocked first in decades. Silas was fairly sure nobody had gone in at all since the last headmaster retired, because when they turned the handle on Thursday evening the door opened with the reluctant groan of something that had made peace with being forgotten.

"Hello, history," Silas said to the dark. "I'm the new problem."

The light switch worked on the second try, which felt about right for Casper's Well. A single bulb swung gently overhead, though there was no draft. Silas chose not to think about that.

The room was narrow and deep as a coffin stood on end, shelved floor to ceiling with ledgers, exam booklets, attendance registers going back to what looked like the reign of some very bored Victorian clerk. Boxes of exercise books were stacked in towers that leaned toward each other like conspirators. There was a desk under the window with a green banker's lamp, and — this was the detail that made Silas smile despite themselves — someone had left a tin of shortbread on it, decades ago, now fossilized into a single geological stratum of biscuit.

They'd been meaning to do this all week. Honestly, they had. But teaching took it out of you in ways the job listing hadn't mentioned: nineteen children, twenty-six desks, and one small girl named Maren who drew a bell with no clapper on every single worksheet, including the maths ones, including the one where she'd been asked to show her working and had instead shown a bell.

Silas pulled up the creaky chair, clicked the banker's lamp, and got to work.

* * *

It started, as these things always do, with something boring.

Enrollment registers. Silas wanted them for the most mundane reason imaginable: the county office had sent a form asking for historical attendance figures, and Silas intended to fill it out correctly, because filling out forms correctly was one of the few pleasures left to a person of their temperament.

The registers were beautiful objects, actually — cloth-bound, hand-ruled, the names written in a procession of hands across the century. A spidery pre-war script. A confident post-war loop. The increasingly defeated ballpoint of the eighties. And then, in the most recent volumes, the tidy blue ink of Headmaster Prosser, who had hired Silas over a video call in which his camera had pointed exclusively at his forehead.

Silas counted backward through the autumn terms, pencil in hand, making little tally marks on a scrap of paper.

This year: nineteen children enrolled.

Last year: twenty.

The year before: twenty-one.

"Huh," said Silas, and reached for another volume.

Twenty-two. Twenty-three. Twenty-four. The line went back, and back, and back, unbroken as a heartbeat, each November term exactly one child fewer than the last. Not two. Never three. Never a year where the number held steady or, God forbid, grew. One. Every year. Like a subscription. Like rent.

Silas sat back in the chair, which complained.

There were perfectly reasonable explanations. Families moved. Villages hollowed out — that was practically the national pastime these days; half the country's small schools were closing for exactly this reason. Rural depopulation. The pull of cities. Nothing sinister about arithmetic.

Except that depopulation didn't usually happen one family at a time, once a year, in the same week, for eighty years, without ever missing a beat. Depopulation was messy. It came in clusters and droughts. This was metronomic. This was *punctual*.

"You're reading too many novels," Silas told themselves, which was ironic, given that they wrote them, and given that the novels they wrote were precisely the kind where a person found an arithmetic pattern in an old register and should have run immediately.

They did not run immediately. They got up, fetched the attendance files from the second shelf — the individual pupil records, manila folders soft with age — and began pulling the ones whose surnames matched the gaps.

* * *

The first file belonged to a boy called Tomas Ellery, 1974.

His record was ordinary right up until it wasn't. Birth date, address (a farm up on the ridge), vaccination card, a teacher's note praising his reading but lamenting his tendency to "narrate his own actions aloud, constantly, even in silence-adjacent activities." Silas liked him already. Then, near the back, a final entry in the headmaster's hand:

*Tomas has been absent since the 14th. His mother says he went out to see the frost and —*

And nothing. The sentence simply stopped. No full stop, no signature, no continuation on the next page. The next page was a different pupil entirely, as if whoever was writing had been interrupted mid-word and never come back to it.

Silas turned the page back. Turned it forward again. The sentence ended at the dash like a cliff edge.

"All right," Silas murmured. "That's a bit much."

They pulled the next file. 1961, a girl named Anwen Prydderch. Same shape: ordinary life rendered in careful bureaucratic prose, then a final note — *Anwen was heard singing after supper on the night of the —*

Mid-sentence. No period. The handwriting didn't trail off, either; it stopped clean, mid-flow, the way a recording stops when someone lifts the needle. As if the writer hadn't run out of words but out of *permission*.

Silas pulled another. And another. They worked faster now, the thrill of investigation humming in their chest — that lovely, treacherous hum, the same one that kept them up nights finishing manuscripts, the one that always felt like discovery and was so often just the sound of a trap closing.

1949. *On the morning of the 12th of November the child was not in her bed and her boots were found by the —*

1936. *The family has asked that we say he transferred, which is kinder than saying —*

1922. *She was a sweet girl who loved the water and on the night the bell —*

Every file. Every single one, ending mid-sentence, mid-thought, mid-name. Eighty years of clerks reaching for the truth and having the pen taken out of their hands by something. Silas laid the folders out on the desk in a long pale row, like a paper spine, like the vertebrae of something enormous buried under the village.

And the dates. Silas checked them twice, then a third time with a growing coldness in the stomach that no amount of rational self-talk could warm. The fourteenth of November. The twelfth. The fifteenth. The thirteenth. All within the same week. The same *week*, every time, decade after decade — a week in mid-November when, apparently, the children of Casper's Well had a standing appointment.

Silas realized they were holding their breath and let it out slowly.

"Okay," they said to the empty room. "Okay. It's a coincidence. It's seasonal. People get restless in November. It's dark early, roads flood, families—"

Their eye caught the oldest folder on the shelf, the one at the very end, its manila gone the color of weak tea, tied shut with a ribbon that had once been red. The label read **1898** in faded iron-gall ink, and beneath it, smaller: *E. CASPER.*

Casper. As in Casper's Well. As in the name of the village itself.

Silas untied the ribbon. The knot crumbled.

* * *

The handwriting inside belonged to someone educated and frightened, which is a combination that shows in the letterforms — elegant loops executed slightly too hard, the pen pressing through the paper in places. Edith Casper, presumably, or E. Casper at any rate, a teacher by the look of it, the school's own founding instructor. The entries began conventionally enough: roll numbers, supply orders, a complaint about the stove. Then, near the end, the entries shortened. The dates clustered. And on the final page, dated the seventeenth of November, 1898, a single paragraph that Silas read three times before their hands would hold still enough to copy it into their notebook:

*The bell was given to the water, as was agreed, and the water has kept its side. I write this knowing it will be the last thing I am permitted to write, for I have seen how the pages end for others and I will not pretend ignorance. If any soul reads this: the well does not hate us. That is the worst of it. It keeps its bargains to the letter. We gave it our dead when we had them, and when we had none we gave it our children, and it accepted both with the same terrible courtesy. Do not sing along. Whatever you hear rising from the hollow, however sweet, however familiar — do not sing along, do not answer, do not give it your —*

The paragraph ended there. Mid-word. *Your.* Your what? Your name? Your voice? Your attention, your love, your self?

Silas sat in the green lamplight with the ghost of a dead teacher's terror in their lap and noticed, distantly, that the bulb overhead had begun to sway again. Still no draft. The corridor outside was silent in the particular way corridors are silent when they are listening.

"Given to the water," Silas repeated quietly. "The bell."

Because that was the other thing — the thing they'd been circling all week without naming. Maren's drawings. A bell with no clapper, on every worksheet, drawn with the patient insistence of a child trying to describe a dream she couldn't wake from. And yesterday, when Silas had asked her, casually, *why a bell, Maren?*, she had looked up with those clear grey eyes and said, "So it can't ring unless it wants to," and gone back to her sums as if she'd said something perfectly reasonable, which was somehow worse than anything unreasonable she could have said.

Silas flipped through the rest of the 1898 file hoping for more — a continuation, a second notebook, anything — but the folder held only a supply invoice and a pressed flower that might once have been a violet. The teacher had known. Had *written it down*, deliberately, for whoever came after. *If any soul reads this.* A message in a bottle, floated forward through a hundred and twenty-seven years, and it had washed up on the desk of exactly the sort of person who could not leave a mystery alone, which Silas suspected was not a coincidence either.

They looked at the row of folders. Nineteen desks, nineteen children, and a ledger whose every wound closed itself mid-sentence, as if the village's whole recorded history had been edited by something with a very specific sense of mercy — or a very specific sense of appetite.

The lamp flickered once.

Silas decided, with the calm of a person who has made several bad decisions in a row and intends to make them efficiently, that they would take the 1898 file home. Just that one. For research. Professional interest. Absolutely normal behavior for a new teacher in their second week.

They tucked it inside their coat, turned off the lamp, and paused at the door with their hand on the switch.

From somewhere down the corridor — faint, muffled, possibly the boiler, almost certainly the boiler — came a single low tone. Not a ring. Not yet a ring. More like a throat clearing. Like something warming up.

Silas locked the archive door, walked out into the blue November evening, and did not whistle on the way home, though they very much wanted to, and only later would they understand why they'd resisted — why some animal part of them had already learned the rule that Edith Casper had died trying to write down:

Do not sing along.

At the cottage, they set the file on the kitchen table beside the cold teapot, poured themselves a generous whisky, and opened their notebook to a fresh page. At the top, in their neatest hand, they wrote:

*Questions for Constable Vane.*

Then, after a moment, they crossed out *questions* and wrote, more honestly:

*Excuses to knock on Constable Vane's door.*

The whisky helped. A little. Outside, the wind moved through the hollow, and beneath the wind — very far beneath, down where the water was — Silas thought they could hear something practicing.
