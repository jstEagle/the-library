## 5. The Bell That Was Melted

The parish office of Casper's Well occupied what had once been the front room of a house, and it smelled the way all such offices smell: of paper gone soft at the edges, of dust that had been disturbed and then allowed to settle again for years at a stretch, of the particular sweetness of a pot of tea that had been brewed, abandoned, and reheated so many times it had achieved a kind of immortality. The man who kept the records was named Mr. Tallow, which struck Silas as the sort of name a village invents for itself when it wants to seem quaint to outsiders, and he was somewhere north of eighty, with hands that trembled not from age but from what Silas would later learn was a lifetime of being careful.

"Parish records," Silas said. "I'm looking for anything about the church bell. The old one."

Mr. Tallow's teacup paused halfway to his mouth. It was a small pause, a nothing pause, and Silas might have missed it entirely if they hadn't spent the past week learning to read this village the way one reads a difficult student — not for what it said, but for what it declined to say.

"The bell," Mr. Tallow said. "There's no bell."

"That's what I've been told. Melted down in 1911. I'd like to see the record of it."

Another pause, longer this one. Through the window behind him, Silas could see the hollow's far wall rising steep and green, and the shutters of the houses there, all of them closed against a morning that was, by any reasonable measure, perfectly pleasant.

"You're the new teacher," Mr. Tallow said. It wasn't a question. Everyone in Casper's Well knew who Silas was by now, the way a body knows where a splinter has lodged.

"I am."

"Teacher Joubert." He set the cup down. It clicked against the saucer, and Silas noticed that his hand had steadied itself, deliberately, the way a man steadies himself before lifting something heavy. "Why would a schoolteacher want to know about a bell that doesn't exist?"

"Because it used to exist," Silas said, "and because the children draw it. Every one of them. A bell with no clapper, on their worksheets, on their slate margins, on the fogged windows. I teach nineteen children, Mr. Tallow, and I'd guess eighteen of them have drawn that bell at least once. The nineteenth is Maren, and she's drawn it forty times." He paused. "I'm not accusing anyone of anything. I'm a teacher. When a whole class is obsessed with the same image and won't tell me why, I go looking. It's the same instinct that makes me check the lost-and-found box. Things go in. Things ought to come out."

Mr. Tallow looked at Silas for a long moment, and something in his face shifted — not warmth, exactly, but a kind of weary recognition, the look of a man who has been waiting a long time for someone to ask the right question and dreading it all the while.

"The 1911 volumes are in the back," he said. "Third shelf. The parish was consolidated that year, so the records are... incomplete. Read what you like. I'll be in the garden." And he rose, with the unhurried dignity of a man who had decided that whatever Silas found, he did not wish to be in the room for the finding of it.

The back room was narrow, lit by a single window whose glass had gone wavy with age, so that the light came through it already distorted, already lying a little. The shelves were pine, homemade, sagging in the middle the way shelves do when they've been asked to hold more than anyone intended. Silas found the 1911 volume on the third shelf, exactly where promised, and carried it to the table by the window.

It was a ledger bound in green cloth that had faded to the color of pond water. The spine cracked when Silas opened it — that soft, fibrous crack of old glue surrendering — and the smell rose up: dust and vinegar and something faintly mineral, like wet stone. Silas had spent enough years among old books to know that smell. Old paper, old ink, old decisions.

The entries were in a copperplate hand, meticulous, the hand of a parish clerk who took pride in his work. Silas read through the ordinary business of a year: baptisms, marriages, the repair of a fence, the purchase of lamp oil. And then, on a page dated the fourteenth of March, 1911:

*It is resolved by this parish, in council assembled, that the bell of Casper's Well church be taken down and melted, and its metal sold to the foundry at Harrow's Bend, the sum realized to be applied to the roof fund. The bell having been found to be the cause of much distress and division among the parishioners, and its voice being no longer tolerable to God-fearing folk.*

Silas read it twice. *The cause of much distress and division.* That was all. No explanation of what distress, what division, what a bell could possibly have done to a parish to make God-fearing folk find its voice intolerable. Bells were rung for weddings and harvests and funerals; bells were the voice of a village's gladness and grief. To melt one was to cut out a tongue. And the clerk had written it all down in his beautiful copperplate as though he were recording the purchase of lamp oil, which, Silas supposed, in a way he was.

There was a marginal note, smaller, squeezed into the left margin as though the clerk had written it and then thought better of it but couldn't bring himself to cross it out:

*The foundry man asked why the clapper was wrapped in wire. We did not tell him.*

Silas sat back. The light through the wavy glass lay in pale bars across the page. *We did not tell him.* Tell him what? That the clapper had been bound — bound by whom, when, and why? A bell with a bound clapper was a silenced bell, and a silenced bell that still caused distress was a bell that had been making noise anyway.

Silas copied the entries into a notebook, in their own less beautiful hand, and kept turning pages. The next relevant entry came in April, a receipt: *Received of the Harrow's Bend Foundry, for the metal of the Casper's Well bell, being one hundred and forty pounds, the sum of nine pounds ten.* One hundred and forty pounds of bronze. Silas did the arithmetic idly, the way a teacher always does — that was a bell of considerable size, a proper bell, the kind that takes a team of oxen to raise into a tower.

And then, near the back of the volume, in the section where the clerk recorded sundry expenses, an entry dated the third of May, 1911:

*Paid to the widow Kettering, for her silence in the matter of the bell, the sum of one pound.*

Silas stared at it. Kettering. The name turned over in their mind and would not settle. There was an Ilsa Kettering, ninety-one years old, who lived at the far end of the hollow in a house with a green door, and whom the children spoke of only in whispers, if at all. A widow in 1911 would be — Silas worked it out — too old to be Ilsa herself, unless Ilsa had been a child then. A widow's daughter, perhaps. A girl who had been paid a pound to keep quiet about something, and who had, by every indication, kept quiet about it for over a century.

Silas closed the ledger and sat for a moment in the wavy light, listening. The village outside was quiet the way a held breath is quiet. And underneath the quiet, faint as a memory of a memory, Silas could almost — almost — hear something. A resonance. The way a room remembers a sound after the sound has stopped, if you're foolish enough to listen for it.

"You found it, then." Mr. Tallow stood in the doorway, garden gloves still on, soil on his knuckles. His face was unreadable, but his eyes were not; his eyes looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with his age.

"I found that the parish paid a widow a pound for her silence," Silas said. "And that the bell's clapper was wrapped in wire before it was ever melted. And that nobody told the foundry man why."

Mr. Tallow came into the room and stood looking down at the open ledger, at the copperplate entries, as though greeting old acquaintances he'd hoped never to see again.

"My grandfather was clerk," he said. "That's his hand. He wrote that marginal note the week before he stopped writing anything at all. He never told my father why. My father asked him once, when he was dying, and my grandfather said—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "He said, *the bell was the least of what we silenced, and the only part we could melt.*"

Silas felt the words go through them like cold water. "What did he mean?"

"I don't know," Mr. Tallow said. "And I've spent eighty years being grateful that I don't." He looked at Silas then, and his expression was the expression Silas had seen on the faces of the villagers all week, the performing normalcy of it, the shutters, the careful nothing. "Teacher Joubert. You seem like a person who checks the lost-and-found box. So let me tell you what I tell myself every night: some things that go in are not meant to come out. The bell is melted. There is no bell. Go home before dark, lock your shutters, and if you ever hear one ring — and you won't, because there is no bell — do not listen to it."

He turned and went out to his garden, and Silas was left alone with the ledger and the wavy light and the growing, unwelcome certainty that everyone in Casper's Well had been having the same conversation in their heads for a hundred years, and that Silas had just been handed their lines.

* * *

The evening came down into the hollow like water filling a bowl. Silas walked home from the schoolhouse with the notebook in their coat pocket, and the walk felt different now — the same lane, the same low stone walls, the same sheep cropping the same indifferent grass, but all of it transposed into a new key. Every shuttered window looked like a closed eye. Every chimney smoke rose straight and thin, like a held breath let out slowly.

Edith was waiting at the corner where the lane forked, leaning against the wall with her constable's hat pushed back, and the sight of her did what it had done all week — loosened something in Silas's chest that they hadn't known was clenched.

"You look," she said, "like a man who's been reading old paper."

"Old paper and older silences. I went to see Tallow. About the bell."

Edith's face did something complicated and quick, there and gone, like a fish turning under water. "And?"

"And it was melted in 1911. Sold to a foundry. One hundred and forty pounds of bronze, gone, sold for nine pounds ten. There's a receipt. There's a marginal note about the clapper being wired. There's a payment to a widow Kettering for her silence." Silas watched her face as they said it. "So you'll understand my confusion, Constable Vane, when I say that I have now confirmed, in writing, in your own parish records, that the bell of Casper's Well does not exist. Has not existed for over a hundred years. There is no tower. There is no bell. The metal was sold and made into, for all I know, saucepans."

"That's right," Edith said. Her voice was even. Her hands, folded on her belt, were not.

"So when I tell you that on my first night here I woke at three in the morning certain I'd heard a single bell note — a note with no church, no tower, no source — you'll tell me I dreamed it."

"You dreamed it," Edith said.

"And the shutters that closed all down the street the next morning—"

"Casualty of the wind," Edith said. "We get wind in the hollow."

"You are lying to me," Silas said, "with tremendous skill, and I want you to know that I admire it. I'm a teacher. I can tell when a student hasn't done the reading, and I can tell when one has done the reading and is pretending they haven't. You've done the reading, Constable. You've done all the reading. You're the best-read person in this village and you're standing there telling me the book is blank."

Edith looked at them for a long moment. The light was going gold and then grey behind her, and her face was caught between the two, and Silas thought — not for the first time, and with a growing unease that had nothing to do with bells — that they could look at this woman for a very long time and not exhaust the looking.

"Go home, Silas," she said quietly. It was the first time she'd used their name. "Lock your shutters. And if you hear anything tonight — anything at all — put your head under the blanket and count to a hundred, and do not, whatever you do, count the rings."

"Count the rings," Silas repeated. "That implies there will be more than one."

"Goodnight, Silas." She touched the brim of her hat, turned, and walked away up the lane, and Silas stood watching her go and understood, with the particular clarity of the very tired, that she had just told them something true while appearing to tell them nothing at all.

* * *

Silas did not sleep. They told themselves it was the tea, and then the records, and then simply the village itself, which pressed against the cottage windows like a question waiting to be asked. They read. They made notes. They drew, without quite meaning to, a bell with no clapper, and looked at their own handiwork with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, and turned the page.

The cottage the school provided stood at the lane's end, last before the fields began and the ground dipped toward the low place where, Silas had noticed but declined to investigate, the trees grew in a ring. The village lay below and around it, a scatter of roofs in the dark, every window shuttered, every house holding its breath.

At three o'clock in the morning, the bell rang.

It was one note. One only. And it did not come from any direction, which was the first impossibility; sound comes from somewhere, always, even in dreams. This came from everywhere and nowhere, from the ground and the air and, Silas would have sworn, from inside their own chest, as though the note had been struck against their ribs as against a bell of their own. It was low and sweet and terribly clear, and it went on and on, not fading the way sounds fade but *withdrawing*, the way a hand withdraws, slowly, reluctantly, from a shoulder.

Silas's teeth ached. That was the strangest part, the detail they would return to later, turning it over: the note had been so deep and so pure that their teeth had ached with it, their fillings, the bones of their jaw, as though the sound had found in them a hollow place that resonated.

And then — and this was the part that knocked the last prop from under Silas's careful, rational, teacher's world — the village answered.

Not with voices. With shutters. All down the hollow, in the dark, there came a sound like a long sigh, a rustle, a clatter of wood on wood — and Silas was at the window in time to see it: shutter after shutter closing, house after house, down the lane and up the lane and along the far wall of the hollow, the whole village sealing itself in one motion, one breath, one practiced, century-deep reflex. It was like watching a flock of birds turn all at once, or a school of fish, some shared nervous system firing through forty houses at the sound of one note. Nobody screamed. Nobody ran. Nobody lit a lamp. The village simply closed, the way a hand closes, around the sound, and the sound withdrew, and the silence that came after was the deepest Silas had ever heard — a silence with weight, a silence that pressed on the eardrums like deep water.

Silas stood at the window with the cold glass against their forehead, and their breath fogged it, and their heart was going like a hammer, and they thought: *I heard it. I heard the bell that does not exist. I heard it with my own ears and my own aching teeth, and forty households heard it too, and every one of them will tell me tomorrow that they slept soundly.*

And beneath the fear, in a small, quiet room at the back of their mind where Silas kept the things they were not yet ready to think, a thought formed itself, unbidden, in the copperplate hand of a dead parish clerk:

*The bell was the least of what we silenced, and the only part we could melt.*

They had melted the bell. They had sold the metal and spent the money on a roof. And the bell had rung anyway, for a hundred years, at three in the morning, in a village with no tower, and the village had closed its shutters around the sound like a hand around a flame it dared not look at but could not put out.

Silas did not count the rings. There had been only one.

But as they finally lay down, blanket to their chin, eyes open in the dark, they found themselves thinking of Edith's parting words, and of the way she'd said *count to a hundred* — and of what she had not said, which was *and it will stop.* She had said nothing about it stopping. She had given Silas instructions for enduring the night, not for ending it, and the distinction sat in Silas's chest like a swallowed stone.

Outside, down in the dark of the hollow, in the low place where the trees grew in a ring, the water was very still. And somewhere — in the ground, in the air, in the aching hollow of Silas's own chest — the note went on withdrawing, slowly, reluctantly, like a hand that had every intention of coming back.
