## 6. Nineteen, Eighteen

The morning Silas Galloway learned what Casper's Well did with its missing children began, as so many terrible mornings do, with coffee that tasted faintly of iron and a walk to school under a sky the color of old dishwater.

They had been in the village eleven days now. Eleven days of teaching arithmetic to nineteen children who were polite in a way that made the skin crawl if you thought about it too long—polite the way hotel staff are polite, or witnesses. Eleven days of Maren Ostergaard drawing her bell on every worksheet, every margin, every corner of every page, always the same bell: round-bellied, rope-hung, and utterly without a clapper. A bell that could not ring. Silas had begun to think of it as Maren's signature, the way other children drew hearts or horses.

Eleven days, and then eighteen desks occupied and one standing empty at the third row, second from the window.

Silas noticed it before they'd fully crossed the threshold, the way you notice a tooth missing from a comb. The desk itself was still there—small, wooden, its surface carved with decades of initials—but the chair was gone. Not pushed in. Not knocked over. Gone, and in its place, against the wall by the coat hooks, sat a stack of chairs that had not been there yesterday, Maren's among them, tucked neatly beneath the others like a card returned to the bottom of the deck.

"Good morning, Mr. Galloway," said the class, nineteen voices minus one, in unison so perfect it sounded rehearsed, because it was rehearsed; Silas had learned that much in week one. Bram had told them, chuckling into his flour-dusted apron, that the children practiced their greeting the way choir children practice hymns. *So it sounds nice for the new teacher.* At the time it had seemed charming.

"Pip," Silas said, setting down their satchel, aiming for lightness and mostly hitting it. "We're doing long division today, which I know is everyone's favorite. Who can tell me where Maren is?"

The silence lasted four seconds. Silas counted. It was a habit they were developing in this village, counting silences, because the silences here had weight and duration and texture, like weather.

Then Tomas Hedlund, a freckled boy in the front row with the earnest face of a child who has never once been in trouble, raised his hand.

"Yes, Tomas?"

"Maren's not coming back."

"I see. Is she ill? Has she moved away?" Silas kept their voice pleasant, teacherly, the voice they used for explaining fractions. Inside, something small and cold had begun to turn over, like a key in a lock that didn't want to catch.

Tomas looked at Silas with genuine puzzlement, the puzzlement of a child asked why the sky was up. "She's not coming back," he repeated, kindly, as though Silas were the one being slow.

And that was that. The class opened their books. Long division proceeded. Hands went up; mistakes were made about remainders; little Anneliese Brandt chewed her pencil down to splinters. Nobody looked at the empty space where Maren's chair had been, and Silas realized, with a sensation like stepping off a stair that wasn't there, that this was the most disturbing thing of all—not grief, not fear, not even curiosity, but the smooth, practiced absence of all three. The class had moved her chair away the way you'd move a chair away after a dinner guest leaves. Except guests leave through doors.

At recess Silas stood at the window and watched the yard. The children played tag. They argued over a jump rope. They did all the things children do, competently, loudly—and Silas found themselves scanning for Maren's red mittens, the ones she'd worn every day since Silas arrived, and not finding them, and understanding that they would not find them, ever, anywhere, and that the village understood this too, and had arranged itself around the absence the way water arranges itself around a stone.

*One child each autumn,* the ledger had shown. *Each autumn, one.*

It was November.

* * *

Edith Vane came by at lunch, which Silas suspected was not a coincidence, because Edith Vane's visits had a way of arriving precisely when Silas was on the verge of asking questions in public.

She stood in the doorway of the classroom with her constable's cap in her hands—a habit of hers, Silas had noticed; she removed the cap indoors like a man of an older generation, a courtesy that sat oddly on her, softened her, made her look briefly like someone who might laugh easily somewhere else, in some other life. She was perhaps thirty-five, tall, with a sunburned steadiness about her eyes that Silas had decided, against all better judgment, was the most trustworthy thing in the village.

"Constable Vane," Silas said. "Come to welcome me again? You've welcomed me twice already. At this rate I'll be the most welcomed teacher in provincial history."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—the almost-smile that Silas had begun, shamefully, to work for. "Heard you had a question this morning."

"Heard correctly." Silas set down the chalk. "Maren Ostergaard. Where is she?"

"That's not a matter for the school."

"It's very much a matter for the school. She's my student. There's no absence note. No parent has come. No one has filed anything, no one has searched, no one—" Silas stopped, reined themselves in. "Her chair is stacked against the wall, Constable. Stacked. Like she's furniture we're putting into storage."

Edith put her cap on the nearest desk—Anneliese's, though Anneliese was outside—and looked at it for a moment as if it were a difficult document. "Families handle things their own way here."

"What things? What happened to her?"

"You've been here eleven days, Mr. Galloway."

"Twelve tomorrow. And in twelve days I've learned to teach long division, identify every child by their chewing habits, and determine that the baker waters down his jam. What I haven't learned is what happens to the children who stop coming to school. So enlighten me. Slowly. I'm a quick study but I'll make allowances."

The almost-smile surfaced properly this time, brief and unwilling, and Silas felt it land somewhere under their ribs and hated themselves a little for how pleased they were. Then it was gone, and Edith's face did the thing it did when she was being the constable instead of the person: everything behind her eyes stepped backward and closed a door.

"Maren's family has left the district," she said. "That's all there is to say."

"They left in the night? Without packing? Her mittens are still on the hook by her door—I walked past this morning. Red wool, one thumb mended with blue thread. People don't leave mended mittens behind, Constable. Mended things are loved things."

Edith picked up her cap. For a moment Silas thought she would say something else—her jaw worked once, twice, the way it did when words were queuing up and being dismissed. "Walk home before dark," she said instead. "Please."

"You give that warning like it's a prescription."

"Because it works better than an explanation." She paused at the door, half-turned. "You're a good teacher. The children like you. Maren liked you best of any of them." A beat. "Liked."

And she was gone, leaving Silas alone with twenty-six desks, nineteen chairs, and the growing, sickening arithmetic of a village that used past tense about living girls.

* * *

That evening Silas did the thing they had promised themselves they wouldn't do, which was go looking.

Not far. Not dramatically. Just a walk—or so they told themselves, lantern in hand, boots crunching the first hard frost of the season, past the shuttered bakery and the shuttered chandlery and the house whose shutters were never open anyway, down the lane that the map called Well Road and the villagers called nothing at all, because Silas had noticed they never named it. You could ask for directions to the church, the mill, the Kettering farm. Ask the way to the well and people developed sudden deafness, or sudden errands.

The hollow deepened as the lane descended. That was the geography of the place, the thing Silas had felt since the first day driving in: Casper's Well wasn't built around the water so much as sunk beside it, the whole village tilted slightly toward the low ground like furniture sliding across a floor that wasn't level. The trees changed near the bottom—older, blacker, willows leaning in over a stone wall that appeared out of the frost like the spine of something buried.

There was a gate. Unlocked, unremarkable, its paint gone gray. Beyond it, a path of flat stones, and beyond that—

Silas stopped.

The well was not what they had expected, though they couldn't have said what they had expected. Something picturesque, maybe; villages like this usually dressed their horrors in cobblestones and geraniums. But there were no geraniums. There was a wide rim of old granite, wider than any well had a right to be—ten feet across, perhaps more, a mouth rather than a mouthful—and the stones of it were worn smooth and dark, and the water below was perfectly, absolutely still. Not a ripple. Not a reflection of the moon either, Silas realized after a moment, and their skin went cold in a way the frost hadn't managed. The water gave back nothing. It was less a surface than an absence shaped like water.

But it was the rim that made Silas's breath fail.

Shoes.

Small shoes, arranged around the granite lip with a neatness that was worse than disorder would have been. Boots and buckled shoes and one pair of red rubber galoshes, sized for a six-year-old, placed side by side, toes pointing outward, as if their owners had stepped out of them and walked away into the air. Dozens of pairs. Generations of pairs. Some green with moss, some recent enough that the frost lay bright on the leather. One pair, near the path, was red wool—no. Not wool. Silas leaned closer, hating themselves, needing to know. Knitted boot-linings, pulled out and draped over the stones. Blue thread at the mended thumb.

Maren's mittens were not at her door anymore.

Silas stood in the freezing dark for a long time, lantern trembling just enough to make the shadows of the shoes crawl slowly around the rim, a slow procession going nowhere. They thought about running. They thought, absurdly, about picking up the galoshes and carrying them back to town, some instinct of rescue with nowhere to go. They thought about Edith's face, and Bram's weeping hum, and Tomas saying *she's not coming back* with the gentle patience of a child comforting a slow adult, and they understood, finally, at the bottom of the hollow with their breath smoking in the lantern light, that the village had not lost these children.

The village had *paid* them.

"One each autumn," Silas said aloud, and their voice fell into the well and did not echo, and that was somehow the worst of it—that the well swallowed sound the way it swallowed reflections, taking everything and giving back nothing, not even the courtesy of an answer.

Behind them, a twig snapped.

Silas spun, lantern up. The path was empty. The willows hung motionless. But far above, at the top of the hollow, a shutter closed—one, then another, then a soft cascade of them, street by street, the whole village pulling its covers up to its chin, and Silas stood alone among the shoes with the certain knowledge that every pair of eyes in Casper's Well knew exactly where they were standing, and had let them come.

On the walk back, climbing out of the hollow, Silas passed the schoolhouse and saw, without meaning to, without wanting to, that the classroom window was lit—though they had blown out the stove themselves at four o'clock—and that in the lamplight, faint as breath on glass, the shape of a small figure sat at the third-row desk by the window, head bent over a worksheet, drawing.

Silas did not go in. They walked faster. And all the way home, in the teeth-chattering dark, they caught themselves humming—something tuneless, something they didn't know they knew—until they reached their own door and clamped their mouth shut and stood in the cold porch listening to the silence where the melody had been, trying desperately to remember whether they had heard it somewhere in the village, or whether it had simply risen in them, unbidden, from somewhere below.

Three bars. It was only three bars.

It stopped, always, at the same place—as if waiting for someone to teach it the rest.
