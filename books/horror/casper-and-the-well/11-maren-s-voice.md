## 11. Maren's Voice

The nights in Casper's Well had a way of arranging themselves around Silas, the way water arranges itself around a stone—patiently, and with an eventual intention.

They had taken to sleeping badly. This was not unusual for a novelist; Silas had spent thirty years treating insomnia as a professional asset, a quiet back room where the day's loose threads could be untangled. But the sleeplessness here had a different texture. It wasn't the busy, generative kind. It was the kind where you lay still and listened to a village hold its breath.

On the fourth night after Maren's desk was moved away, Silas gave up on sleep entirely, dressed, and sat by the window of their rented room above the old chandlery with a cup of tea going cold beside them. The hollow was dark in the particular way that drowned places are dark—not merely unlit but *unwilling*, as if the darkness had been poured in like ballast. Below, the street was empty. Every shutter was closed. Even the constable's office showed only the pilot light of its stove through the gap under the door.

And then, faintly, from down past the schoolhouse, from the low ground where the well sat in its ring of stones:

Singing.

Silas went very still.

It was a child's voice. A girl's voice, clear and unhurried, carrying up through the cold air with the strange fidelity that sound achieves over water. And Silas knew it. Of course they knew it. They had spent three weeks listening to that voice answer questions about long division and read aloud about the migration of geese, had heard it stumble over *rhythm* and triumph over *necessary*, had told it once, gently, that its essay about her grandmother's garden was the best thing in the class's folder.

Maren.

*Maren is gone,* said the part of Silas's mind that had read the ledger, that had seen the empty desk carried to the corner without comment, that had watched nineteen become eighteen while the whole village behaved as though arithmetic were a matter of opinion.

But the voice did not care what the ledger said. The voice was singing the song—the bell-song, the one the children hummed and never finished—and it was coming up out of the ground.

Silas stood at the window for a long time, telling themselves all the reasonable things. Wind in a chimney flue. Water over stones. A night bird, some local species with an uncanny range. They were a rational person. They were, in fact, professionally committed to the idea that every haunting has a human explanation if you dig deep enough, and they had dug deep enough in this village to find something far worse than ghosts: bookkeeping. Ledgers. Files ending mid-sentence. A covenant, Edith had called it, in the flat voice she used when she wanted to be believed.

The singing stopped between verses, the way it always stopped, at the same bar—a little lift, a held breath, a door left open—and then began again.

Silas put on their coat.

Later—they would think about this later, in the years after, whenever they allowed themselves to think about it at all—they would try to reconstruct the decision and would fail. There was no decision. That was the horror of it, they'd say. There was no moment where I chose to go. There was a window, and there was a voice, and the distance between them simply stopped existing. It was like being a word in a sentence that had already been written.

* * *

The lane down to the well was wet with dew, and Silas's shoes soaked through in the first fifty yards. They barely noticed. The singing grew clearer with each step—not louder, exactly, but more *present*, the way a face becomes more present as it turns toward you. It came from the ring of stones at the bottom of the hollow, where the grass grew too green and too short, cropped close as a churchyard.

Silas crested the last rise and saw the well.

In daylight it was a modest thing: a circle of old stones, a wooden frame, a rope running down into blackness. The small shoes were arranged along the rim with a neatness that made Silas's stomach turn—dozens of them, decades of them, leather rotted soft and canvas gone to lace, each pair set toe-outward as if their owners had stepped out of the world and left them behind like coats at a party.

Now, at night, the well was not modest at all. Now it was the center of everything, the still point the whole valley tilted toward, and the singing rose out of it the way heat rises off a road in summer.

The voice sang the first verse. Then the second. Silas knew them now; Bram's drunken weeping had taught them that much, and the recording—that wrong, impossible recording with its fourth verse Silas had never captured—had taught them to be afraid of the space after the second. The children's version always stopped at the same bar. Maren's voice stopped there too.

And then it started again. First verse. Second verse.

Stop.

Silas realized, with a lurch, that they were humming.

They clamped their mouth shut so hard their teeth clicked. Their own breath hung in front of them, silver in the cold. The melody kept going inside them anyway, patient as tide, filling the space behind their ribs. It was a simple tune. That was the trap of it, Silas thought—it was built like a folk song, built to be carried, built so that any fool who heard it twice owned it forever. Three bars and a rest. Three bars and a rest. And the rest was shaped exactly like a question.

*I know how this works,* Silas told themselves. *Edith told me. Never sing along. Never answer.*

But knowing a rule and standing ten feet from a well at three in the morning while a missing child sings your name out of the earth are two different arrangements of the same knowledge, and only one of them holds.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing that undid them. In the third repetition, the voice changed—not the tune, but the words, the nonsense syllables resolving into something with weight, and the weight was:

*Mr. Galloway. Mr. Galloway. Come and hear the ending.*

Maren's voice. Unchanged. Not older, not waterlogged, not warped by whatever the well was or did. She sounded exactly as she had on the Tuesday she'd asked to stay in from recess because she wanted to finish her story about the fox. *Unchanged since she vanished,* said the cold narrator in Silas's head, and then, worse: *which means she hasn't changed. Which means wherever she is, she is still exactly herself. Which means she is waiting.*

Silas took a step forward. The grass under their feet was wet and warm, which was wrong, and they noticed it was wrong, and they took another step anyway.

The rope swayed against the well frame. No wind. It swayed anyway, slow, like something below was testing it.

"Mr. Galloway," said the well, in a child's voice, kindly. "We saved you the good part."

"I can't," Silas whispered. Their lips were moving. They hadn't decided to move them. "I'm sorry, sweetheart, I can't—"

"You can," said Maren, reasonable, gentle, the tone she used to argue for extra reading time. "You just start where everyone stops. Everybody stops because nobody knows. But you could learn. You're a teacher. Teachers know endings."

Three bars. The rest shaped like a question.

Silas opened their mouth—

—and the world slammed shut.

Not silence; force. An arm across their chest, hard as a fence rail, hauling them backward off the grass, and a voice in their ear saying, low and shaking, "No. *No.* Look at me. Look at me, Silas."

Edith Vane. Out of uniform, hair loose, breathing hard, her arms locked around them from behind, dragging them back step by step while Silas's body fought her without their permission—feet scrabbling on the wet grass, lungs full of a tune that wanted out. The well's voice kept singing, sweet and unhurried, and Silas heard their own throat working around the shape of the next note, felt the hum building in their chest like a swallowed shout.

"Sing anything else," Edith gasped. "Anything. Recite—recite tax forms, recite your own books, I don't care—"

"*The well was sunk in eighteen eleven,*" Silas said wildly, the first sentence their panicked mind produced, "*by the parish of Saint Aldhelm's, at a cost of forty pounds—*"

"Yes. Good. Keep going."

"*—the mason being one T. Hedgerow, whose initials appear on the third course—*" They were crying without having decided to do that either. The tune drained out of them slowly, grudgingly, like water from a punctured boat, and by the time they reached the mason's disputed payment schedule their knees had stopped holding the melody and begun, instead, simply failing. Edith caught most of their weight and walked them backward up the rise, away from the ring of stones, away from the small shoes, away from the voice that called, once more, without anger, without urgency, terribly:

"Goodnight, Mr. Galloway. I'll keep your seat dry."

Then the ordinary dark closed over the lane, and there was nothing but two people breathing hard in the cold.

* * *

Edith got them back to the room above the chandlery and lit the lamp with hands that were not steady. Only then did Silas see her right hand.

The palm and fingers were scorched red, blistered along the lines of the grip, the skin split in two places where she had seized the well's rope—because that was what she had done, Silas understood with a fresh wave of nausea. When her arm around their chest hadn't been enough, when Silas had been pulling toward the stones with the whole dumb strength of the summoned, she had grabbed the rope itself, the rope that ran down into the black, and used it to anchor them both, and the rope had burned her like a stove lid.

"It doesn't like to be touched," Edith said, following their gaze. She said it lightly, the way people describe weather that has ruined their harvest. "Nobody touches the rope. I knew that. I grabbed it anyway." She flexed the hand, winced, and smiled at Silas with a crooked attempt at humor. "Occupational hazard. Constable's oath. Protect and serve, even the idiots."

"Don't," Silas said. Their voice came out cracked. "Don't joke. Please. I nearly—" They couldn't finish it. There was no finishing it. There was only the memory of their own feet moving, of their mouth opening, of a child's voice saying *we saved you the good part*, and the understanding settling into them like cold silt: *it didn't summon me with fear. It summoned me with her. With kindness. With homework.*

Edith knelt at the hearth and began dressing her hand from a tin of salve that lived, apparently, in every house in Casper's Well, the way plasters live in other houses. Her movements were practiced. That was somehow the worst detail yet. People who were new to burning did not have a system.

"How many times have you done this?" Silas asked quietly. "Pulled someone back."

She was silent long enough that the fire popped twice.

"My uncle, when I was fourteen. He'd lost his daughter the year before. He used to walk down there on ringing nights and stand at the edge and listen to her. For hours. Everyone knew. Everyone let him, because what else do you give a man like that?" She wound the bandage with her teeth and her left hand. "One night he leaned too far. I was the one sent to fetch him home. He fought me the same way you fought me. Same strength. Same nothing-behind-the-eyes except the song." She tied off the bandage. "He thanked me the next morning. That was the worst part. He cried and thanked me and asked me to never tell his wife how close it had been, and I've kept that promise for twenty-six years, and I hate every single day of it."

"Edith—"

"She called you by name." Edith looked up, and the lamplight made her eyes look bruised. "Maren did. The well doesn't use names unless—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and Silas watched her decide, visibly, whether to finish the sentence. She finished it. "Unless it means to keep them. It's never used mine. It's never used anyone's who wasn't already written down somewhere."

"Written down where?"

"That's the wrong question tonight." She stood, tucked the salve tin away, and did the small domestic things of someone delaying a departure—banking the fire, straightening the cold teacup, setting Silas's abandoned novel square on the table. At the door she paused, hand on the latch, her bandaged hand cradled against her ribs.

"You'll want to go back tomorrow," she said. "In daylight. To prove to yourself it was a dream, or to understand it, or because you're a writer and you can't help digging at the wound. I know. I've watched people want to. I'm asking you—begging you—to wait. Give me a few days. There are things you need to understand before you stand at that rim again, and I'd rather you learned them from me than from it."

"Things like what?"

Edith looked at her burned hand, and then at Silas, and something moved across her face that was older than affection and younger than despair.

"Things like why my family has kept this well for six generations," she said, "and what exactly it is we've been keeping it *for*. And Silas—" her voice dropped, and the gentleness in it frightened him more than the singing had, "—when I tell you, I need you to remember that I pulled you back. Whatever else is true. Remember whose hands were on the rope."

She let herself out into the dark. Silas stood a long time at the window, watching until her lamp crossed the square and vanished behind her door.

Down in the hollow, faint as a memory of a memory, a child's voice began the first verse again.

It stopped, as always, at the bar where the ending should be.

And in the pause—in the question-shaped silence—Silas could have sworn the well was waiting for an answer, patient as arithmetic, certain as a seat kept dry.

Somewhere in the village, a shutter closed. Somewhere, a ledger waited with blank pages and room for one more name. And Silas, who had come to Casper's Well to teach children about endings, stood in the dark and understood, at last, that the well had not been calling them down.

It had been calling them *home*.
