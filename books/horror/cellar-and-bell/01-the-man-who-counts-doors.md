## 1. The Man Who Counts Doors

The farmhouse had eleven ways in, and Cyrus Nakamura had counted every one of them before he touched a single tool.

He counted the way other men breathed — without deciding to, without effort, and with a small satisfaction when the number came out even. Eleven was not even. He stood in the yard of the Petch farm at first light, breath steaming, and made himself count again because eleven bothered him the way a crooked picture frame bothers a stranger: not his problem, but impossible to ignore.

Eleven again. The kitchen door, the back door, the cellar hatch, the coal chute, four ground-floor windows with rotted sills, two upstairs windows whose frames had shrunk away from the brick like old teeth from gums, and the gap beneath the eaves where the soffit had been gnawed into lace. Eleven openings into a house that held a family of five, forty sacks of seed grain, and — according to Mrs. Petch, who had paid him half up front in coins wrapped in a dishcloth — something that moved in the walls at night.

Rats, she'd said. Big ones. She'd said it the way people said *rats*, which was to say quickly, and looking slightly to the left of his face, as though the word itself might turn its head if observed directly.

Cyrus did not ask what else she thought it might be. Asking was how you got invited to dinner. Dinner was how you got remembered. Being remembered was how you ended up on someone's Christmas card list, and then on their visiting roster, and then — worst of all — expected. He had spent thirty-one years constructing a life with the structural integrity of smoke, and he maintained it with the devotion other men gave to church or drink.

No small talk. No receipts with addresses. No roots.

He worked through the morning in silence, and the silence was not rudeness; it was architecture. When Mr. Petch ambled out to offer tea, Cyrus lifted a hand — not quite a wave, more a gesture meaning *I am occupied* — and kept kneeling at the cellar hatch, pressing hardware cloth over the gap and pinning it with washers driven deep into sound wood. Rats could chew through wood, through lead, through soft mortar. They could not chew through patience applied correctly. That was the whole trade, if he was honest about it, though no one had ever asked him to be honest about it. Most people wanted a man with poison and a confident manner. What they got was a man who understood that a house was an animal too, that it breathed through its gaps, and that anything breathing could be taught to hold its breath.

By noon he had sealed seven of the eleven. He saved the hardest for last, as ritual demanded order: the coal chute first, then the windows, working low to high, because rats were creatures of the ground and you closed the world from the bottom up. His hands moved with the economy of long practice — measure, cut, fit, fix — and his mind moved elsewhere, the way it always did, drifting along the grooves worn by years of solitary work. He thought about nothing much. Nothing was his favorite subject. Nothing was safe.

It was while he was flat on his belly in the loft, wire in his teeth, sealing the eighth opening, that he heard the scratching.

Not in the wall. Under it. Under the floor, under the foundations, down where the earth began — a slow, deliberate drag, like something drawing a fingernail along the underside of the world. It stopped when he stopped. It started when he started. It matched him, stroke for stroke, the way a shadow matches a hand.

Cyrus lay very still among the dust and the smell of old hay. He told himself it was a pipe. Old houses had pipes; pipes ticked and dragged and settled. He told himself this with the fluency of a man who had told himself many things over many years, and he finished the seal, and the scratching did not come back, and he climbed down and did not mention it, because mentioning things was how conversations started.

* * *

Mrs. Petch found him at three o'clock packing his van — a van with no signage, no name painted on the side, just grey paint going greyer — and pressed the second half of his fee into his palm along with a loaf of bread still warm enough to fog the paper.

"You'll stay for supper," she said. It was not quite a question. Country hospitality operated on the principle that refusal required evidence of prior engagement, and Cyrus had none, ever, anywhere.

"Work calls," he said. "Thank you for the bread."

"There's rats in the Hollowmere cellars too," Mr. Petch offered from the doorway, chewing a straw that had long since surrendered any flavor. "Big house up past the church. Aldery woman's been after someone all season. Nobody'll take it."

"Why won't anyone take it?" Cyrus asked, and immediately regretted it, because questions were doors and he'd just opened one.

Mr. Petch shrugged with elaborate vagueness. "Long way to drive. Funny old place." A pause, and then, with the air of a man setting down something heavy: "Funny old bell they used to have, an' all."

His wife's elbow found his ribs with the precision of long marriage, and the conversation died there, strangled in the crib. They watched him load his tools. Mrs. Petch looked at him with an expression he couldn't file — pity, maybe, or the particular sorrow of country people for anyone young who didn't yet know what they didn't know.

"Mind how you go," she said, which was a thing people said, and also, somehow, not.

* * *

He drove twelve miles before he stopped for the night, parking in the lee of a hedgerow off a lane that led nowhere anyone needed urgently to be. This was another ritual: never sleep within sight of yesterday's job. Distance was hygiene. He ate two slices of the Petch bread, drank tea from a thermos gone brown with tannin, and read by lamplight — a battered paperback of bridge engineering, of all things, bought for sixpence years ago because bridges interested him. Bridges went places on purpose. Houses just sat where they were and let things in.

The letter was under the passenger seat.

He saw it when he reached for the thermos lid, and his hand stopped halfway, the way a hand stops when the body knows something the mind hasn't caught up to. White envelope. No stamp. No postmark. Which meant it had been placed — in the door pocket of a locked van, in an empty hedgerow lane, sometime between the farmhouse and here — or it had always been there, waiting to be noticed, which was worse.

His name was written across the front in copperplate handwriting.

*Cyrus.*

Not Mr. Nakamura. Not Pest Control Services, c/o. Just *Cyrus*, in a hand he knew better than his own, because he had spent the first nine years of his life watching it move across shopping lists and birthday cards and the margins of his schoolbooks where she corrected his arithmetic in soft grey pencil.

His mother's handwriting.

Mara Nakamura had been dead for twenty-two years. He had stood at the graveside aged nine, holding an umbrella over a woman in black who wasn't family and wouldn't say what she was, while rain hit the coffin lid like fingers testing a drum. There had been no letters afterward. There had been almost nothing afterward — a box of her things delivered by courier, unasked-for, containing winter clothes in his size for the boy he would become, and a knitting needle, single, wrapped in tissue, and no note at all. He had carried that box through fourteen rented rooms and never once opened the clothes. You didn't wear a dead woman's guesses about your future. It felt like letting her keep planning.

Now she had written to him.

Cyrus sat in the cold van for a long time. Outside, the hedge moved in the wind with a dry, whispering restlessness, and somewhere far off — so far he could have imagined it, and later he would decide he had imagined it — a bell rang once. Not a church bell's rounded voice. Something thinner. Something recast and cracked and trying anyway.

He opened the envelope with a knife, because tearing it felt too intimate, and even intimacy-by-proxy deserved caution.

Inside was a single sheet, folded twice, in the same copperplate:

*Cyrus. The cellar at Hollowmere Manor wants clearing. Triple your rate, and I know what your rate is, because I set it. Come before the equinox. Wear your gloves. Don't count the doors.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. The last line was pure Mara — she had teased him about the counting since he was four, since the afternoon he'd informed her, gravely, that the cottage had nine doors and one of them was lying. *Don't count the doors* was a joke. It was also, he understood with a cold settling in his stomach, exactly the sort of thing a person says when they mean it most.

Triple his rate. She knew his rate. His rate was a number he had never written down, never spoken aloud, quoted only face to face in cash terms that left no trail. Nobody knew it. Nobody living.

He read the letter four times. On the fourth reading he noticed what he should have noticed first: the ink. It hadn't faded. Twenty-two years in an envelope, and the copperplate sat on the page wet-black and fresh, as though the words had been written that morning, as though the hand that wrote them was — no. He put the thought down carefully, the way you put down something with a hot base, and refused to pick it back up.

Professionalism, he told himself. This is a job. A strange job, oddly sourced, triple pay. Strange jobs paid best precisely because normal people wouldn't touch them, and Cyrus Nakamura had built a whole quiet life out of being the man who would. Infestations didn't care about provenance. A cellar was a cellar. Rats were rats.

And if the letter was something else — if it was what some cold, practical corner of him whispered it might be — then it was an invitation, and invitations were the one thing his rituals existed to refuse.

He should burn it. He knew that the way he knew weather coming. Burn it, drive north, take a contract in a town where nobody's dead mother knew his rates.

Instead he smoothed the letter flat on his knee and read it a fifth time, and the fifth time he saw it: pressed faintly into the paper below the writing, visible only when the lamplight raked across at an angle — a handprint. Small. Grease-dark. Child-sized, five fingers splayed, as though someone had taken hold of the page from the other side while it was being written.

Cyrus turned the letter over. The back was blank. The print showed through the paper like a bruise through skin.

He sat until the lamp burned low, listening to the hedge whisper and the far-off memory of a bell, and then he did the thing he would spend the rest of this story not forgiving himself for: he folded the letter along its original creases, tucked it inside his ledger of accounts — the one book he owned that recorded everything, addresses excluded — and told himself he was accepting a job.

Not answering a letter. Not keeping an appointment twenty-two years late. A job. Triple rate. Cellar work. He'd cleared harder cellars than any manor's, probably, though he couldn't name one, which should have told him something.

In the morning he would drive to Hollowmere. He fell asleep with the van locked, the knife open on the seat beside him, and the bread uneaten, because Mrs. Petch's kindness had begun to feel like bait in a trap he couldn't yet see the shape of.

Just before sleep took him, the scratching came again — under the floor of the world, patient, unhurried — and this time it did not stop when he stopped.

It counted along with him instead. Eleven. Twelve.

Twelve was one more than the farmhouse had.

Twelve was one more than anything should have.
