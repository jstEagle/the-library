## 8. What the Mirror Shows Instead

The metronome sat on the dresser for three days before Juniper could bring herself to stop winding it.

She had bought it at a secondhand shop in Corrick's Crossing on her way through town—a brass pyramid with a chipped base, the kind of object that looked like it had survived several owners and outlived them all. She told herself it was for grading, for pacing herself through stacks of essays, but that was a lie she'd stopped bothering to make convincing even to herself. The metronome was a witness. It was the only thing in the house besides her that she trusted to keep time.

Because the house did not.

She had spent the first week telling herself the lag was fatigue. New teachers were tired; tired people misperceived things; the eye was a sloppy instrument and the brain a sloppier editor. She had written this in her notebook in her neatest hand, under the heading *Hypothesis: Perceptual Error*, and then she had sat looking at the words until they curdled. Because she was a scientist of small things by temperament—she counted roadkill on highways, she memorized the exact phrasing of evasions—and she knew what she had seen. Her reflection blinked after she did. Not with her. After.

One second. She was almost sure it was one second, exactly, every time, like a clock that had been wound slightly wrong and never corrected.

So: the metronome. She set it ticking at sixty beats per minute—*one, two, one, two*, the sound filling the bedroom like a small patient heart—and she stood before the oval mirror above the dresser and raised her right hand on a beat.

Her reflection raised its left hand on the next beat.

Every time. She did it twenty times, thirty, until her arm ached and the metronome's tick had worn a groove in her attention. One second. Always one second. Not drifting, not random, not the flicker of a tired optic nerve. A delay. As if the light itself had to travel farther than light should have to travel—as if the image coming back to her from the glass had been sent from somewhere just slightly behind the present, a place where her hand went up a heartbeat later than here.

She wrote it down. *Confirmed. 1.0 sec. Consistent.* Her handwriting shook, and she hated that it shook, and she wrote that down too, because documentation was a kind of prayer and prayers did not require steady hands, only honest ones.

That was three days ago. Tonight the metronome ticked again, but she wasn't watching it. She was watching the mirror show her something else entirely.

* * *

It began, as these things apparently did in the Voss farmhouse, with the light.

Dusk came early now—the last week of September, the sun surrendering the valley a little more each evening, sliding behind the western ridge while the sky over Harrow's Bend still held a bruised blue hour. Juniper had come upstairs to change out of her school clothes, and she had flipped the lamp on, and the bulb had buzzed and dimmed and steadied, the way old bulbs did, and she had glanced at the mirror without thinking, the way anyone glances at a mirror, to check the ordinary fact of their own face.

The face looking back at her was hers. But the room around it was not.

The dresser was wrong first. In the mirror it stood against a different wall, and it was smaller, scuffed white paint instead of dark walnut, its top crowded with objects she didn't own: a hairbrush with a cracked handle, a tin cup holding pencils, a folded dish towel gone gray with age. Behind the reflection of her shoulder there was a bed—not her bed, not the iron frame with the quilt she'd brought from her mother's house, but a narrow child's bed pushed up under the window, its headboard painted with fading yellow birds. A rag rug lay on the floorboards in a pattern of concentric diamonds. On the wall beside the window hung a hook, and on the hook hung a coat.

A small coat. Wool, brown, buttoned to the collar, sized for a child of eight or nine. Its sleeves hung straight down, empty, and in the lamplight of the reflected room they seemed to sway very slightly, though the reflected room had no draft in it that Juniper could see.

Juniper did not scream. Later she would be proud of this, in the grim way one is proud of surviving a thing rather than handling it well. What she did was stand absolutely still, both hands flat on the dresser's edge, and look, and count—one, two, one, two, the metronome going in the real room, silent in the reflected one—and notice that her reflection was standing exactly as she was standing, hands flat, shoulders rigid, except that behind her reflected shoulder the child's room went on being there, complete and quiet and lit by a lamp that cast its glow in a direction the actual lamp was not pointing.

She turned her head slowly to look at the actual room. Iron bed. Quilt. No hook on the wall, no coat, no rug, no yellow birds. The wallpaper was the same faded rose in both rooms—that much matched—but everything else had been subtracted or replaced, as if the mirror were remembering a different tenant.

When she turned back, the reflection showed her own bedroom again. Her dresser, her quilt, her lamp. Her face, blinking one second late.

She got the notebook. She wrote for forty minutes.

* * *

The next morning she tested it the way she tested everything now: methodically, with the metronome, with notes taken in real time, with the particular discipline of a woman who has decided that if she is going to be haunted she will at least be haunted *accurately*.

The lag was still there. One second, faithful as ever. But the other room—the remembered room—did not come back at will. It came back at dusk. That was the first pattern. She noticed it on the second evening, when the light through the west window went thin and blue-gray and the mirror's surface seemed to deepen, the way water deepens when clouds pass over it, and then the child's bed swam up through the present like a shape rising through pond water. At full dark it faded again. Under electric light alone it never appeared at all. Whatever the mirror was doing, it needed the day's last light to do it—the hour when, her grandmother would have said, the veil wears thin. Juniper didn't write that in the notebook. She thought it, and disliked herself for thinking it, and wrote instead: *Manifestation window: approx. 15–20 min., dusk, natural light dependent.*

The second pattern took longer, and it was the one that changed everything.

On the fourth evening the room in the mirror was different.

Still a child's room—still the same proportions, the same window, the same rose wallpaper—but the bed had been moved to the opposite wall, and the coat on the hook was a different coat, navy blue with brass buttons, and smaller. And there was chalk dust. That was the detail that stopped her breath: a fine pale dust on the floorboards near the door, tracked in little footprints, the unmistakable residue of a child who had been playing with chalk—or writing with it—somewhere they shouldn't have been. In the mirror-room, someone had tried to sweep it up and given up halfway, the broom marks visible, the dust pushed into a gray half-moon against the baseboard.

Two rooms. Two children. Two eras, she was nearly certain—the furniture styles differed by decades, the coats, the toys (a tin truck in the first room, a rag doll in the second). The mirror was not showing her one memory. It was showing her a sequence. A history. The room as it had been, and been, and been again, layered like sediment, and the mirror was an archaeologist's trench cut straight down through all of it.

She stood in the blue dusk with her arms wrapped around herself and felt the awe arrive before the terror did, which surprised her. Because it *was* awesome, in the old sense—the sense that made people build churches. Here was a thing that remembered. Not recorded, not photographed—remembered, the way a body remembers a wound, the way a valley remembers a flood. Every child who had ever slept in this room had left an impression in the glass, and the glass was giving them back, one dusk at a time, in order or out of order, she couldn't yet tell. Memory made architectural. The house was not haunted in the way stories meant it—no rattling chains, no grieving specter. The house was haunted the way a ledger is haunted. It kept accounts.

And then the terror arrived, right on schedule, because if the mirror remembered everyone who had lived here—

Then it remembered Ellery Voss.

Then it might remember what happened to Ellery Voss.

Then it might remember the eleven photographs missing from the yearbook wall at school, and the sealed cellar door, and Marisol Quintero's whisper—*the school eats slow*—and everything else this town had swept into its gray half-moon against the baseboard and pretended was gone.

* * *

She began keeping vigil. Dusk to dark, every evening, notebook open, metronome ticking, a thermos of coffee going cold beside her. She told herself it was research. She was aware, in some honest basement of herself, that it was closer to devotion—that she had found the one place in the world where the past consented to be seen, and she could not stay away from it, the way you cannot stay away from the grave of someone you loved badly and too briefly.

The rooms came nightly now, and they accumulated. She catalogued them the way she catalogued everything: by distinguishing features, by era, by the small heartbreaking specifics that no one else would ever think to record.

Room three had a boy's bicycle wheel leaning against the wall and a poster of a baseball player whose name she couldn't read in the dim glass. Room four was stripped nearly bare—a mattress on the floor, a single candle stub on the windowsill—and the coat on the hook was a girl's, patched at both elbows, and Juniper sat with that one a long time, thinking about poverty arriving in the valley sometime in the middle of the century, thinking about which years those might have been.

Room five broke her heart in a new way, because in room five the bed was made with hospital corners and there was a school satchel hanging beside the coat, and propped on the pillow was a book, spine out, and Juniper leaned so close to the glass her breath fogged it, trying to read the title, and the fog of her breath appeared in the mirror a full second after she exhaled—over the ghost-image of the pillow, over the book, obscuring it—and she understood suddenly that she was the intruder here, that these rooms were not being shown to her but merely persisted through her, that she was a smudge on a window looking in at lives that had ended decades before she was born.

On the seventh evening, the mirror showed her the cellar door.

Not the whole room—just a corner of it, the way a photograph catches what the photographer wasn't aiming at. The child's room was there, whatever era it belonged to, but in the far edge of the glass, beyond the doorway of the reflected room, there was a stairwell going down, and at the bottom of the stairwell, a door. Dark wood. Iron hinges. And painted on it, in letters that had once been careful and had deteriorated into something frantic:

**KEEP IT COUNTED**

Juniper's pen stopped moving.

She knew that door. She had photographed that door—through the chained and padlocked hatch at the east end of Harrow's Bend Consolidated School, the door Principal Grieves called "the old boiler access" and would not discuss, the door that had no key in the district's key log, no entry in any maintenance record she could find. She had crouched on the school's basement steps with her phone camera and shot it through the gap in the hatch, and the photo showed the same dark wood, the same iron hinges, and paint too flaked to read.

But the mirror remembered what the paint had said.

*Keep it counted.*

Counted. Not locked. Not forgotten. Counted—as in tallied, as in numbered, as in a ledger. The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well, and she sat listening for it to hit bottom, and the bottom was a long way down.

The metronome ticked. One, two. One, two. In the mirror, her reflection sat with its pen stopped too, one second behind her, in a room where a child's coat hung on a hook and a stairwell descended toward a door that somebody had once believed counting was the same as keeping.

* * *

She dreamed about the coat that night. In the dream she was small, and the coat was hers, and she was reaching up for the hook to take it down because someone was calling her name from downstairs in a voice like warm milk, and just before her fingers closed on the wool she woke with her heart slamming and the clock reading 3:14 a.m. and the bedroom mirror across from her catching the streetlight and holding it, patiently, one second longer than the night outside.

She got up. She stood in front of the glass in the dark, in her sleep shirt, hair loose, and she said aloud, to the room or the glass or whatever listened from inside the walls:

"Who lived here?"

The mirror said nothing, of course. Mirrors answer questions the way wells do—by returning your own face, altered slightly by depth. But as she watched, in the faint borrowed light, the surface seemed to shift the way dusk shifted it, and for a moment—not the child's room, not any of the catalogued rooms—she saw a man. Tall, stooped, standing where she stood, holding something flat and heavy against his chest with both arms, the way you hold a book you are protecting, or a lid you are holding shut. His face was in shadow. His hands were enormous and gentle. He was looking down at the thing he carried, and his mouth was moving, and though no sound crossed the glass, she caught the shape of the words, repeated over and over, patient as prayer:

*Not yours. Not yours. None of it is yours to give.*

Then he was gone, and the mirror held only her, one second late, and the streetlight, and the long silence of the house settling around her like a body pulling a blanket higher.

She wrote the words down before she let herself feel anything about them. *Not yours. None of it is yours to give.* Then she sat on the edge of the bed with the notebook open on her knees and let the feeling come, and it was grief, which made no sense, because she had never known him. But grief has never required acquaintance. She had read enough of this town's silences to know that Ellery Voss had been the last person in Harrow's Bend who tried to say no—who had stood in this room, maybe, in this exact spot, holding his ledger against his chest like a shield, telling the dark beneath the school that the children were not currency, that none of it was theirs to give.

And the town had erased his photographs and lost his keys and let his house rot around the memory of him, and in 1974 he had walked into the ground and not walked out.

At dawn she dressed, packed her bag, and wrote one line at the top of a fresh page, underlining it twice:

*County records office opens at nine. Find everything they have on Ellery Voss.*

Behind her, in the mirror, her reflection was already writing it—one second ago, or one second from now, she could no longer say which direction the delay ran.
