## 18. She Rewrites It From Memory

The notebook lay open on the kitchen table like a mouth that had forgotten how to speak.

Juniper had turned every page twice before she let herself believe it. The paper wasn't torn, wasn't burned, wasn't water-stained. It was simply *blank*—that soft, unmarked cream of a fresh legal pad, as if no pen had ever touched it, as if the three weeks of her life spent bent over these pages had been a dream she'd had at her desk. She held a sheet up to the window. No indentations. No ghost of graphite pressed through from the page before. Whatever had taken the words had taken the *weight* of them, the physical history of her hand moving across the paper, and left nothing behind but fiber and pulp.

The aunt's voice came back to her across the folding table in the empty classroom, across the smell of coffee and floor wax and the particular silence of parents who have agreed in advance on every answer: *Stop writing.*

She hadn't stopped. She had driven home with the notebook on the passenger seat, had carried it inside past the hallway mirror—*one second late, always one second late*—had set it on the table and made tea and opened it with the small ceremony she'd developed for it, a kind of communion, and found it hollowed out.

The tea went cold in her hands.

Outside, the last of the October light drained from the valley, and the fields went the color of old bone. Somewhere down the road a dog began to bark and then thought better of it. The house did what the house always did at dusk: it settled, the way the landlord had said the cellar had settled, and Juniper had learned in three weeks that *settled* was a word people in Harrow's Bend used the way other people used *forgiven*—to mean something had stopped moving, not that it had stopped wanting.

She looked at the notebook. The notebook looked blankly back.

"Fine," she said aloud, to the room, to the walls, to whatever kept its patient small shape in the glass. "You can have the paper. You can't have the memory."

Her hand was shaking when she pulled a fresh legal pad from her school bag—the good one, the one she'd been saving—and uncapped a pen. She flattened the blank notebook open beside it as a reference, which was a joke, because it had nothing to give her, and began at the beginning, the way you begin anything that matters: with a date.

*September 3. Arrived Harrow's Bend at dusk. Counted nine dead animals on the road between the county line and town. Raccoon, raccoon, something I couldn't name. The landlord, Mr. Pell, signed the lease on the porch and would not come inside. When I asked about the cellar he said it had "settled." He said it the way you'd say a dog had stopped barking.*

The pen moved. The pen kept moving.

* * *

She worked until her eyes burned, then worked past that, into the country of headache where words stop looking like words and start looking like insects arranged on the page. The kitchen filled with the small sounds of her labor—the scratch of the pen, the creak of her chair, the tick of the wall clock that she had never once caught in the act of ticking. She wrote the overheard remarks. She wrote the exact wording of Grieves's evasions, which she had memorized the way other people memorized song lyrics, because she had understood early that in Harrow's Bend the truth lived not in what anyone said but in the precise architecture of what they refused to say.

*Grieves, Sept 19, re: sealed wing: "We keep that closed for HVAC reasons." There is no HVAC in the east wing. There is no ventilation at all. The air in that hallway does not move. I have stood in it. It moves the way water moves in a well.*

*Grieves, Sept 26, re: yearbook wall: "Some classes didn't have good photo years." Eleven classes in a row? I asked. He smiled with his whole face and none of his eyes and said, "Harvest was hard on attendance."*

*Grieves, Oct 2, re: janitor's closet: "Lost the keys before my time." His grandfather was principal in 1962. His grandfather was principal in 1974. The keys were never lost. The keys were kept.*

She wrote Marisol's name-rhyme out in full, the sing-song list of the eleven, the way the girl had chanted it with her chin up and her eyes somewhere else, somewhere she'd been taken without her consent: *Wren and Odile and Thomas Bright, Hattie and the two Cole boys, Doraleen's brother Amos, Ruthie, Silas, Pearl—* and the rest, all of them, all eleven, and the years beside them like epitaphs. She wrote the dates of the harvest moons. She wrote the ledger entries she'd memorized, the child's careful hand, the column headed *PAYMENTS RENDERED* that had made her sit down on the cellar stairs with the ledger in her lap and her heart going like a trapped bird.

*Ellery Voss. Hired 1961. Custodian. He knew. He knew for thirteen years and he wrote it all down in a child's hand because—why? I keep coming back to this. Maybe because the thing under the school thinks in a child's hand. Maybe because he wanted the record to be readable by the ones it was about. Maybe because his own hand had started shaking and never stopped.*

Her hand was shaking now too. She noticed it the way you notice weather.

* * *

Around eleven the cramping started, the fist of her right hand locking around the pen like a claw, and she stopped and pressed it flat against the table and flexed her fingers and thought about stopping, seriously thought about it, the way you think about anything that would be so easy.

The house was quiet in the particular way it got quiet, which was not the absence of sound but the presence of attention. She could feel the walls listening. She could feel the hallway listening. She could feel, with a certainty that had stopped frightening her and become simply a fact of her life like the weather or the lag, the mirror at the end of the hall paying attention.

She got up. She got a glass of water. She stood in the kitchen in the dark with the water and looked at the black window, where her reflection stood in a kitchen in the dark holding a glass of water one second behind her, and she said to it:

"You can't have this either."

Her reflection, one second later, said it too. She was almost sure of the timing. She had stopped being able to tell whether she was testing the lag or whether the lag had become the rhythm her whole nervous system kept time to, like a heart that beat on someone else's count.

She went back to the table. She wrote on.

* * *

The pages came back in the wrong order, memory being honest about its own architecture. She wrote the archive first because it was freshest—Voss's file, the commendations, then the complaints, the slow darkening of the language as the years went on. *1963: Voss requests the east wing be "left open." Request denied. 1966: Voss found in the sealed stairwell at 3 a.m., claims he was "counting." 1969: Voss refuses to prepare the school for Harvest Festival, states in writing that "we should not decorate a mouth."* She wrote that last one twice, pressing hard, because it was the sentence that had changed everything for her, the sentence that had turned a mystery into a duty. *We should not decorate a mouth.*

She wrote about the shoes. She had only read about the shoes so far, in Voss's marginalia, in the ledger's terrible accounting, but she wrote down what the ledger described—pairs, arranged, decades of them—and her pen hesitated over the page because some part of her understood that she was not documenting the past. She was transcribing a schedule.

Then she wrote the thing she had not written anywhere, the thing that had been sitting in her chest since the aunt had looked at her notebook with eyes like a well:

*They are not hiding it from me. They are hiding it from each other. The whole town is a conspiracy of one person each, everyone keeping their own small piece of the silence so no one ever has to hold the whole weight. Grieves keeps the keys. Pell keeps the porch. The farmers keep the festival. The parents keep the attendance records. And Marisol keeps the names, in a nursery rhyme, without knowing what she's carrying. It isn't a cover-up. It's a load-bearing wall. And I have been pulling bricks out for three weeks, and tonight they pulled back.*

The clock said 1:14 when she flexed her hand and found it would not fully open.

She switched the pen to her left hand. Her left-handed writing looked like a hostage's confession, letters leaning and cramped, but it was legible, and legible was enough, and she kept going because the alternative was to stop and stopping was what they wanted and she had decided, somewhere in the last hour, in the cold kitchen, with the glass of water, that she would rather lose the hand than lose the record. It was not bravery. She wanted to be honest about that, even in her own head, even on the page. It was spite, and grief, and the specific stubbornness of a woman who had grown up watching her mother's stories get corrected out of her by relatives who preferred a kinder version, and who had sworn, at fourteen, at a kitchen table very much like this one, that she would rather die than let a true thing be unwritten.

Her mother had died not knowing that the vow had taken.

*Oct 12,* she wrote, left-handed, the letters wobbling. *They took the notebook. I am writing it again. Whoever finds this: the first version was better. It had my doubts in it. This version only has what I'm sure of, and I'm sorry, because the doubts were true too, and you deserve the whole weight, not the clean version. But this is what survived. Write it down. Whatever they tell you is settled—write it down.*

* * *

It was past two when the pen tore through the paper.

Not because she pressed too hard—though she was pressing hard, her left hand cramping now in sympathy with her right, both fists aching, both hands ruined for grading tomorrow, let tomorrow take care of itself—but because the page beneath had gone soft, had gone the consistency of wet leaves, and the nib went through it into the table, and when she pulled the pen back the hole in the paper had edges that curled inward, slowly, like something breathing through a wound.

She looked at the hole for a long time.

Then she turned the page, found dry paper further in, and kept writing.

That was when she heard it: a sound from the hallway, small and rhythmic, like a palm striking a palm. Once. A pause. Twice. A longer pause. Three times.

She did not turn around immediately. That was a discipline she had learned in the last weeks, the way you learn not to look down when you're on a high place—not because looking down does anything, but because the looking is a kind of invitation, and she had stopped extending invitations. She finished the sentence she was writing. She dotted the period. Then she stood, slowly, and walked the twelve feet to the end of the hall, and stood in front of the mirror.

The mirror showed the hallway. The hallway was empty. The hallway was empty for one second, and then the mirror caught up, and in the caught-up version—the delayed version, the version that belonged to the house and not to her—there was a figure standing behind her reflected self.

Small. Child-sized. Patient. Its hands were raised to about the height a child's hands would be, and they were coming together, and coming apart, in the slowest applause Juniper had ever seen. There was nothing mocking in it. That was the worst part. It applauded the way an audience applauds at the end of a recital—earnest, a little solemn, the applause of someone who has been made to sit through a long performance and is genuinely, terribly glad it is good.

She turned around. The hall was empty. She turned back. The mirror held its one second of empty hall, and then the small figure was there again, hands meeting, hands parting, and she understood that it was not applauding her. It was applauding the writing. The record. The *keeping* of it. The thing under the school had spent ninety years being documented by custodians and bookkeepers and principals, and here at last was someone doing it properly, in a child's own spirit, page after page after page, and whatever it was, it approved.

"Stop it," she said to the glass. Her voice came out flat and steady, which surprised her. "I'm not doing this for you."

The figure's hands kept moving. Behind her, in the real hallway, the dark stayed dark. But she noticed—she made herself notice, because noticing was the whole job now—that the applause had a rhythm, and the rhythm was not random. She counted. Hands together, one. Apart, two. Together, three. It was keeping time with something, and it took her a long moment, standing in her own hallway at two in the morning with her ruined hands, to place it.

It was keeping time with the pen. With the scratch and lift of the pen. It had been applauding in her rhythm all along. Every page she wrote, it clapped. Every word. It was not an audience. It was a *metronome*, and she was the music.

Juniper went back to the table. She sat down. She picked up the pen with her screaming right hand because the right hand had written the original and the original deserved the original hand, and she wrote, at the bottom of the page, in letters that dug grooves into the paper:

*Oct 13, 2:20 a.m. The mirror applauds. Let it. Applause is what you give when the show is over and you want the performer to leave the stage. I'm not leaving the stage. I'm building one.*

The figure in the mirror, one second behind her, went on clapping, soft and small and glad, all the rest of the night, and Juniper wrote through all of it, page after page, until the sky over the fields turned the gray of dishwater and the first light came into the kitchen and showed her what she'd done: forty-one pages, reconstructed, cramp-scarred, left-handed in the middle, true.

She capped the pen. She flexed her hands, and both of them shook like leaves, and she laughed once, a short ugly sound with no humor in it, because she was so tired that her body had begun making decisions without her.

Then she looked at the last page, and stopped laughing.

At the bottom, beneath her final line, in a hand that was not hers—a child's hand, round and careful, the same hand as the ledger, the same hand as *PAYMENTS RENDERED*—someone had written a single word:

*Tomorrow.*

And beneath it, smaller, almost shy, an arrow pointing down through the paper, toward the floor, toward the cellar, toward the settled dark under the house where the floorboard still stood a half-inch proud of its neighbors.

Juniper sat very still while the sun came up over Harrow's Bend and the mirror at the end of the hall, in the growing light, showed her an empty hallway in perfect, punctual, one-second-late silence. The word sat on the page. The arrow pointed down. And she understood, with the clarity of the completely exhausted, that the thing under the school had not been threatening her with *tomorrow*.

It had been telling her where to go.
