## 21. Grieves Breaks

The coffee in the faculty lounge had gone cold an hour ago, and Aldous Grieves drank it anyway, because drinking it was a thing he did at four o'clock on a Thursday, and the doing of things at their appointed times was the only architecture left holding him upright.

He had been principal of Harrow's Bend Consolidated School for nineteen years. His father had held the post for thirty-one before him, and his grandfather for twenty-two before that, and somewhere back along that line—1931, to be exact, though the family never said the year aloud, only *since the beginning*—the Grieveses had become what they were.

Bookkeepers.

His mother had used the word once, when he was nine, standing at the kitchen window watching the harvest moon come up over the water tower, her hands wet with dishwater and trembling in a way he hadn't understood then. *We're bookkeepers, Aldy,* she'd said. *We don't decide anything. We just keep the accounts.* And she had smiled at him, that terrible smile adults make when they are handing you something heavy and calling it a gift, and she had dried her hands, and they had eaten pork chops, and nothing was ever said again.

He thought about her now, watching the parking lot empty through the lounge's smudged window. The last bus pulled away trailing diesel breath. The flag hung slack against its pole like something drowned.

Sterling's car was still there.

He had watched her all week the way you watch a crack spread across a dam—not wanting to look, unable to look anywhere else. He'd seen her photographing the sealed cellar door with her phone held steady as a surgeon's hand. He'd seen her in the archive room on Tuesday, pulling the Voss file, her lips moving as she read. He'd seen her come out of the county building with photocopies fanned against her chest like a hand of cards she intended to play.

And last night, someone had told him—the way such things were always told him, arriving in his awareness without a messenger, the way the ledger's knowledge arrived—what she had found under the floorboards of the Voss house.

She had the book.

Aldous set down the cold coffee. His hands were not shaking. That was the thing no one understood about men like him; people expected the guilty to tremble, but guilt, after enough years, stopped being an earthquake and became a climate. You lived inside it. You dressed for it every morning. It did not shake you anymore. It simply was the weather of your life.

He walked the long hallway toward her classroom, his shoes making the sound shoes always made on these floors—a sound he had heard ten thousand times and which tonight seemed to say something, some syllable repeated, *hush, hush, hush.*

Her door was open. She sat at her desk grading papers by lamplight, and outside the windows the October dusk was coming down purple and cold, and the first star over the water tower was not a star, though only he knew to look for it.

Miss Sterling looked up. She had the kind of face that would have been pretty if it ever relaxed, but it didn't relax; it worked, even at rest, even now, turning over whatever it turned over.

"Principal Grieves." She didn't stand. "Office hours ended at three."

"I know." He stepped inside and closed the door behind him, and heard how that sounded, and opened it again six inches, because he wanted her to know he wasn't that kind of man, and then realized there was no kind of man he could claim to be that would survive the next five minutes, and closed it anyway.

"You've been to the county archive," he said.

"Yes."

"And the Voss house came furnished."

Something moved behind her eyes—not fear, not yet. Calculation. She was deciding how much she knew, how much to spend, where the leverage lay. He almost admired her. In another life, in a town without a cellar under it, she would have been a fine teacher and a nuisance and nothing more.

"Aldous," she said, using his first name deliberately, the way lawyers do, "I'm going to ask you one question, and I want you to consider very carefully whether you can answer it honestly."

He waited.

"Is Marisol Quintero going to be taken?"

There it was. No preamble, no circumlocution. She had learned already that this town answered everything except the direct question, and so she asked nothing else.

And Aldous Grieves, who had rehearsed denials in the shower for two weeks—who had prepared the whole liturgy, *I don't know what you mean, Miss Sterling, children move away, families are private, Harrow's Bend has had its tragedies same as anyone*—heard himself say:

"Sit down, please."

* * *

They sat facing each other across the small desks, which was absurd, two adults folded into furniture built for thirteen-year-olds, and perhaps that was fitting, because everything in this school had been built for children and everything in this town had been paid for with them.

"How much do you know," he asked, "exactly?"

"The founders. 1931. The flood they bought off. A child every few harvests since." Her voice was level, but he could hear the effort holding it level, the way you can hear a rope bearing weight. "Ellery Voss tried to stop it. He died trying. There's a skeleton in my cellar, Principal Grieves. There is a dead man in my house who spent thirteen years trying to end what your family administrates."

"My grandfather's brother-in-law, actually," Aldous said, and was horrified to hear the pedantry in it, the little correction rising out of him like a reflex, like a cough. Nineteen years of keeping the accounts. You became the ledger, eventually. You began to think in its columns.

"That's what you have to say about it? A genealogy?"

"No." He pressed his palms flat against the child-sized desk, feeling its smallness, its cheap laminate warmth. "That's what I say when I'm frightened. Forgive me. I haven't told anyone any of this in—" He calculated. "No one living has heard me say any of this."

Outside, the wind came up from the river valley, carrying the smell of cut corn and cold water. Somewhere far below the floor—farther than any floor went—he imagined—or remembered, which was worse—that he could hear the humming.

"Tell me how it works," she said. "The choosing. Tell me you don't choose."

"We don't." And here his voice did break, finally, the crack in the dam, and he was grateful and ashamed in equal measure. "God help me, we don't. Everyone in this town thinks—we let them think—the Grieveses pick. That we sit in some back room and select a child like selecting a lamb. Do you know what that does to a family? What it did to my mother, being hated for a power she didn't have?" He laughed, a dry awful sound. "We're not priests, Miss Sterling. We're clerks. The ledger chooses. It always has. A name darkens, and we read it, and we make the arrangements, and we tell ourselves we're only following the terms. My father used to say the terms were written before any of us were born, and that made it clean. Clean!" He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "It's not clean. Nothing about it is clean. But it isn't choice. Choice would at least be a sin I owned."

Juniper Sterling was quiet for a long moment. The lamp hummed. In the corner of the ceiling, a moth ticked against the fluorescent tube it would never reach.

"Then refuse," she said. "Refuse to read the name. Refuse to make the arrangements. Let it come collect its own debts."

"You don't understand what refusal costs."

"Voss refused."

"Voss died alone in a cellar with his bones in his lap," Aldous said, sharper than he meant to, and saw her flinch, and pressed on because the wound was open now and there was no bandaging it halfway. "You think the flood of '31 was weather? You think the valley just happened to stay dry for ninety years while every river around us drowned its towns? There's a reason Harrow's Bend has never flooded, Miss Sterling. There's a reason our crops come in when the county's fail. There's a reason nobody here starved in the Dust Bowl years while Kansas blew away around us. We eat. We have always eaten. That was the bargain, and the bargain has a price column, and the price column has names in it, and this year—" His voice failed. He started again, smaller. "This year the ink is already drying."

"Then we return what was stolen," she said. "Voss figured it out. The terms allow it. Stolen things must be returned if named correctly at the hour of the moon. The children weren't given—they were taken. Your founders committed theft and called it contract. We can break it."

For one moment—one bright, stupid, mortal moment—hope moved through Aldous Grieves like blood returning to a numb limb, painful and sweet, and he nearly wept right there in the eighth-grade classroom among the paper pumpkins and the cursive alphabet strung above the board.

Then the other thing rose in him. The older thing. The thing his mother had dried her hands and smiled through.

"And if you're wrong?" he whispered. "If the naming fails—if the hour is wrong, if the wording is wrong, if the thing beneath us decides the loophole doesn't hold—then the contract stands, defaulted and insulted, and it collects what it's owed plus penalty. You've read the old entries. You know what penalty meant in '43. Two names instead of one. And if it takes the whole valley—" He stood, because sitting was impossible now, and paced between the desks, his hands opening and closing. "Do you know what's under this town besides the school? Four hundred people. A grain elevator. A hospital with eleven beds. If the valley floods, those people drown in their beds in the dark, and every one of them will be dead because a schoolteacher from out of state decided ninety years of survival was a crime she needed to prosecute."

"It *is* a crime."

"Yes," he said simply. "It is. I've known it was a crime since I was nine years old. I've kept the books on a crime my entire life. Do you imagine I sleep well? Do you imagine my father slept well, or his father? We are not monsters, Miss Sterling. We are something worse. We are reasonable men. Every year we sit down with the arithmetic and we choose four hundred lives over one, and every year the arithmetic wins, and every year we tell ourselves that next time we'll find another way, and there has never been another way, and now you arrive with a photocopied ledger and a dead custodian's theory and you ask me to bet the whole valley on a loophole."

"I'm asking you to bet it on the truth."

"The truth doesn't have a good record here!"

The shout rang off the tile and died. Both of them stood breathing. Down the hall, the building ticked and settled, cooling into evening, full of the small sounds of an empty school—and beneath those sounds, faint as a pulse through a wall, the other sound, the patient one, the one that never quite stopped.

Aldous sank back into the small desk. When he spoke again, all the fire was gone out of him, and what remained was quieter and more terrible.

"She's twelve years old," he said. "Marisol. I've read her file. I know her reading level. I know her aunt's phone number. I know she draws horses in the margins of her math homework. Last spring she stayed after class to ask me if I was lonely, because I ate lunch in my office every day." His eyes were wet and he did not wipe them. "You want to know the worst part of being a bookkeeper? It isn't the fear. It's that you learn every child's name before the ledger does. You learn them as students, as people, as kids who need lunch money and glasses and someone to notice them. And then one October the ink comes, and you already love them a little, and you walk down to their classroom anyway, and you smile, and you say everything is fine."

"So don't," Juniper said. "Don't walk down. Help me. Doraleen Hask is with me. We have the rite. We have the name Voss died holding. Give me the school keys, give me the sealed wing, give me whatever your family kept—records, terms, the original contract. Three days, Aldous. The moon rises Saturday. Help me end it, and no Grieves ever keeps a book again."

He looked at her for a long time. And she must have seen something in his face—some flicker of the man he might have been born as, in some other town, under some other sky—because she leaned forward, and her voice gentled.

"You could be free," she said.

And Aldous Grieves, principal, bookkeeper, third of his line, shook his head slowly, and wept without drama, the way old pipes leak, and said the thing he had been sent into this room to say, the thing the ledger had put in his mouth before he ever stood up from his cold coffee:

"If you interfere, it won't take her quietly. It'll take her in front of witnesses. It'll take her however it needs to, and it will take whoever stands beside her, and when it's finished the valley will still owe, because interference voids the restitution clause—you must know that, you must have read the margins—it voids everything, and we start again in worse terms, and your name will be in the book next time, Miss Sterling, yours, I've seen the space for it, there's a blank line after Quintero and the ink is waiting to see whose hand fills it."

He stood. He straightened his cardigan with both hands, a small fastidious gesture, the gesture of a man putting his uniform back on.

"Let it happen the old way," he begged her, and his voice cracked clean through on *old*. "Quietly. At the festival, like always, so the town can keep its story, so no one drowns, so no one else dies in a cellar alone. Please. Please. I am begging you as a man who has done this eleven times and hated it eleven times—let her go through with it quietly, and grieve her, and teach somewhere else, and live."

The silence afterward lasted a long time.

When Juniper Sterling finally spoke, her voice was not angry. That was the worst of it. It was sad, and certain, and it sounded like a door closing on the last warm room in the world.

"No," she said.

Aldous nodded, as if she'd confirmed an appointment. He gathered his coat. At the door he paused, his back to her, and said, "I'm sorry. I truly am. You seem like a good teacher."

Then he walked out through the dark halls of the school he had kept for nineteen years, past the trophy case and the yearbook wall with its eleven gaps, down to his office, where he unlocked the bottom drawer and took out the ring of keys his grandfather had carried and his father had carried, brass worn smooth as river stones.

One key was different from the others. Older. Black at the teeth.

He had never used it. He had only ever handed it over, once per generation, to whoever came to collect.

Saturday, the moon would rise. But when Aldous got into his car and looked back at the school—at the sealed wing, at the dark windows stacked like closed eyes—he understood, with the calm of a man reading a figure already entered in a column, that the moon would not wait for Saturday.

Nothing in this town waited anymore. The schedule was moving. The account was closing early.

And somewhere in the fields south of town, a girl who drew horses in her margins was already hearing the humming, and beginning, without knowing why, to pack a bag.
