## 32. Down the Long Stairs

The door at the bottom of the janitor's closet was already open when they came back down.

Juniper had left it closed—she remembered closing it, remembered the weight of the iron latch and the way it had resisted her hand like a jaw refusing to unclench—but now it stood ajar, breathing out cold air that smelled of river mud and chalk dust and something older underneath both, something like the inside of a stone.

"Marisol first," Doraleen said. Her voice was steady in the way of old women who have outlived every steadier voice around them. She kept one hand on the girl's shoulder, and Marisol, who had been crying in the car and then not crying and then crying again, wiped her face with her sleeve and nodded like a soldier.

The flashlight beam went into the dark past the door and did not come back. Juniper noticed that before she noticed anything else: the light simply stopped, as if the darkness beyond the threshold were a substance, dense enough to drink.

"Stairs," she said. "There were stairs."

"There are always stairs," Doraleen said. "That's how it keeps you walking. Nobody ever arrives standing still."

They went down.

* * *

The stairs were wrong in a way Juniper's mind kept trying to correct and kept failing to. There were too many of them. The school above them was two stories tall on a concrete slab; no staircase beneath it could run this deep. But the steps kept coming, worn limestone instead of poured concrete, each one dished in the center by feet that had climbed and descended for longer than Harrow's Bend had existed. Her hand found the wall for balance and the wall was warm. Not body-warm. Ground-warm. The warmth of soil in late summer when you dig down past the cool layer and touch the part of the earth that never quite lets go of the sun.

Or never quite lets go of fire. She chose not to finish the thought.

Marisol's small hand slipped into hers. Juniper squeezed it and felt the girl squeeze back, once, deliberately—a signal, not a comfort. *I'm here. I'm choosing this.* The ledger was folded into Marisol's jacket pocket, wrapped in a pillowcase, because Doraleen had insisted it be carried by the one it wanted. "You don't hand a debt to a stranger," she'd said. "It has to feel the weight of who owes it."

Behind them, far above, a door boomed shut. Grieves. He had locked the school behind them—not to trap them, he'd wept, not to trap anyone, only so nothing else could get in, so no one else could follow, so the town would have its night of ignorance undisturbed. Juniper hadn't believed him then. Standing now in the throat of the earth, listening to the boom echo downward instead of up, she believed him less.

The humming came up to meet them.

It was the tune Marisol sang—the skipping rhyme, the one about the lady and the river—and it rose through the stone like water rising through a sponge, filling the stairwell from below. Marisol's grip tightened until her nails bit Juniper's palm.

"That's my song," she whispered. "That's been my song my whole life. My mamá used to sing it."

"Your mamá learned it here," Doraleen said quietly. "Everybody in this valley learned it here. It isn't a song, child. It's a summons wearing a melody."

* * *

The shoes began at step two hundred—or where Juniper guessed step two hundred might be; counting had become meaningless somewhere in the dark—and they did not stop.

They were arranged in pairs along the outer edge of the spiral, toes pointing down, as if their owners had stepped out of them mid-descent and continued on barefoot. Small shoes. Oxfords with cracked leather. Canvas sneakers gone stiff as shrouds. A pair of buckle shoes black with age, and beside them, incongruously bright, a pair of rubber boots printed with cartoon frogs, the kind you could buy at any store in any decade, the kind that meant some mother had knelt at a door and tugged them onto small feet and said *don't track mud.*

Juniper made herself look at each pair. That was the discipline she'd built all autumn, the whole architecture of her survival: *look at it, name it, write it down.* But there was no notebook here, no pen, no page to pin the horror to. There was only looking.

"1962," Doraleen murmured, touching a pair of saddle shoes without quite touching them. "Thomas Ellery Pruitt. He'd just gotten those. His mother said he wore them to bed." Her finger moved on. "1965. The Alvarez girls. They went together; the thing took them together, held hands going down, folks said, though nobody living saw it." Another pair, tiny, soft-soled. "1968."

She stopped.

"Doraleen," Juniper said gently.

"My brother's." Doraleen's voice didn't waver but her hand did, hovering over a pair of scuffed brown loafers, laces still tied in the double knot she herself had taught him because he couldn't manage the loops. "Wendell Hask. Eleven years old. He was carrying a jar of lightning bugs." She withdrew her hand slowly, folding it into the other at her waist, and stood very straight. "Keep walking. We're losing the hour."

But Juniper saw what the old woman's face did in the flashlight glow—how it aged ten years between one breath and the next, and then set itself, deliberately, like a jaw, like a door, like a town deciding not to remember.

Marisol was staring at the frog boots. "Whose are those?"

"I don't know," Juniper said.

"They're wet," Marisol said.

They weren't. But when Juniper looked again, the toes of the little boots glistened, and a bead of water gathered at the heel and fell, silently, onto stone that swallowed it without sound. Water from 1931, still dripping off a child's boots. Still falling. Still arriving.

"Walk," Juniper said, and her own voice sounded far away, delayed by a second, like everything else in this valley. "Don't stop walking."

* * *

The stairs ended without ending. One moment there was stone underfoot descending; the next there was stone underfoot level, and the walls fell away, and the flashlight beam, which had been a narrow obedient thing all the way down, suddenly opened like an eye.

Juniper raised the light and understood that she had brought a candle into a cathedral, or a match into a storm, or a single word into a language with no other words.

The chamber was vast and it was three places at once, and her mind, which had spent months training itself on impossibility, finally lay down and let her see.

It was the cellar of the Voss farmhouse—she knew the fieldstone, the low joists overhead, the particular stain on the floorboards where the coal chute had leaked. It was the school—rows of desks stretched away into the dark, child-sized, arranged in concentric rings like ripples, and on the nearest desk sat a spelling book open to a page she could almost read. And it was the valley—the floor of the chamber sloped away into water, black and slow, a river running through a room, and the waterline climbed the fieldstone walls in a tide mark exactly as high as the flood of '31 must have reached, and above that line, hung in the air like flies in amber, hung the drowned things: a fence post, a church pew, a photograph curling in its frame, all suspended, all preserved, all *waiting.*

"It's all one place," Marisol whispered. "It was always all one place."

"The flood never ended," Doraleen said. "That's what nobody understood. They think the water went down in the spring of '31. It didn't go anywhere. It just moved indoors." She lifted her chin toward the far side of the chamber. "And there they are. Look. Look at what signed your valley away."

* * *

They stood in a ring at the chamber's heart, where the desks gave way to bare stone: eleven figures, twelve with the empty space they'd left for the one who'd died before the signing finished, and they were ghosts the way heat shimmer is a ghost—present, insubstantial, utterly real. Men in wool suits and women in shirtwaists, farmers and bankers and one preacher whose collar glowed faintly, and they stood in their circle with pens in their hands, and they were signing.

Still signing. After ninety-three years.

The document floated between them at chest height, a scroll of paper long enough to wrap a coffin, and as Juniper watched, one ghost after another leaned in and wrote, the pens scratching with a sound like teeth, like frost forming, like the first crack in a dam. Their faces were serene. That was the worst of it. Not agony, not damnation—serenity. The calm of people doing arithmetic. One woman paused to tuck a strand of hair behind her ear, an ordinary gesture, a kitchen gesture, and then bent back to her signature as if the fate of children were a line on a harvest ledger, which, Juniper understood, it was.

A man near the ring's edge looked up.

His eyes found Juniper across the chamber with terrible precision, and his mouth moved, and though no sound crossed the distance, she read the shape of it easily, because she had read it in Grieves's office, in the landlord's porch, in every deflection this town had ever offered her:

*It was necessary.*

Then he returned to his signature.

"They don't know they're dead," Doraleen said. "Or they don't know they're done. Either way, they're still agreeing. Every second of every year since 1931, they're down here saying yes." She spat, an old woman's contempt, precise as punctuation. "My father stood in that ring. I've known it since I was nine. I used to pray he'd stop."

"Has anyone ever asked him to?" Marisol said.

Doraleen turned and looked at the girl for a long moment. "No," she said. "Nobody ever asked. Everybody just watched." She took a breath that shook only a little. "Well. Tonight somebody asks."

* * *

The water spoke first.

Not in words—in recognition. The black river running through the chamber's floor swelled against its banks, a slow tidal lean, the way a dog leans toward a familiar hand, and the temperature dropped so fast that Juniper's breath bloomed white and the moisture on the fieldstone froze with a sound like whispering. The humming swelled to fill everything, the walls, the water, the space behind her ears, and it resolved, horribly, into harmony—children's voices, dozens of them, singing the skipping rhyme in rounds, overlapping, patient, endless.

And then the presence arrived, and it did not arrive from anywhere. It condensed. The dark between the desks grew denser, the shadows of the founders' ring deepened, and the air itself seemed to develop attention—the specific, unbearable feeling of being looked at by something that has been looking for a very long time.

Marisol stepped forward.

"No—" Juniper grabbed for her, but the girl slipped free with the boneless ease of children, and stood at the ring's broken edge with the pillowcase bundle clutched to her chest, chin up, tears freezing on her cheeks, and said in a clear voice that the chamber carried to every corner:

"You can't have me. We read the terms. You keep what's *given*, and I wasn't given. I was *written*. And written things can be unwritten."

The attention of the place swung to her, vast and delighted, the way a cat is delighted.

And the humming changed key.

It became sweet. It became the most beautiful sound Juniper had ever heard—lullabies and radio songs and her mother's voice reading aloud, all braided together, all promising rest, safety, warmth, an ending to fear. The water glowed faintly gold. The founders' ghosts smiled in their sleepwalk circle. Somewhere in the sweetness, Juniper heard her own name spoken kindly, heard offers taking shape like hands opening—

*Doraleen's hand closed on her wrist, hard, nails in flesh.* "It lies beautifully," the old woman breathed. "Hold the ledger. Hold the names. Don't listen with anything but your skin."

Juniper gripped the pillowcase bundle through Marisol's jacket, through the girl's own arms, and fixed her eyes on the floating scroll, on the signatures still being scratched into existence, and she said the thing she had come all this way to say, the first word of the sentence Voss had died trying to finish:

"These children were stolen."

The chamber went silent.

Every ghost stopped writing. Every voice in the hum cut off at once. The water stilled to glass. And in that perfect, ringing silence, the presence drew itself up out of the dark to consider her—vast, old, hungry, and for the first time in ninety-three years,

*listening.*

The scroll between the founders' hands trembled. The nearest ghost's pen hovered, ink gathering at its nib, waiting to see whether the next word it wrote would be a signature

or a retraction.

Juniper opened the pillowcase.
