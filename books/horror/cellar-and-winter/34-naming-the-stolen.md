## 34. Naming the Stolen

The chamber did not have walls so much as it had memories of walls.

Juniper stood at the edge of the flooded valley that was also a cellar and was also, impossibly, the underbelly of Harrow's Bend School, and she understood that the space around her had been built the way sediment is built—layer upon layer of the same year, the same bargain, pressed down until time itself forgot which layer came first. Water stood ankle-deep and perfectly still, black as ink, warm as breath. Above her, where the ceiling should have been, there was only darkness going up and up, and somewhere in that darkness the school's foundations hung like ribs.

The founders stood in their ring.

Twelve of them, or thirteen—she could not make the number hold still—dressed in the wool and broadcloth of 1931, their faces smooth as wax left too near a flame. Each held a pen. Each held a page. And they were signing, always signing, the scratch of nib on paper the only sound beneath the humming, which came from everywhere now, from the water and the dark above and from Marisol herself, who stood pressed against Juniper's side with her small hand fisted in Juniper's coat.

Grieves had stopped at the top of the stairs. He stood silhouetted against the last gray light of the stairwell, one hand still on Marisol's shoulder where he'd delivered her like a parcel, and his face when he turned to look at Juniper was not triumphant. It was the face of a man watching a surgery he had scheduled himself, praying for complications.

"Miss Sterling," he said, and his voice echoed wrong, arriving before his lips moved, "you can still put the book down."

"No," said Doraleen Hask.

She stepped past them both, ancient and straight-backed, her coat hem dragging through the black water, and she took her place at Juniper's left the way they had rehearsed it in her kitchen over two nights of cold coffee: Doraleen at the left, witness; Marisol behind, protected; Juniper at the center, holding the ledger open against her forearm like a hymnal.

"The terms," Doralene said to the ring of founders, and her voice cracked across the chamber like ice giving way. "You wrote them. We read them back to you tonight."

* * *

Juniper opened the ledger.

The child's handwriting looked up at her from the first page—Ellery Voss had copied it all in that careful, round-eyed script, or perhaps the ledger had always been written by a child, in every generation, because whatever kept the accounts below wanted them kept innocently—and the names were there, each with its date, each with its terrible little notation. *Payment rendered. Payment rendered. Payment rendered.* Eleven entries across forty-three years, and beside each one, in the margin, in ink that had faded to the color of weak tea, the same three words in the founders' formal hand:

*Freely given. Freely given. Freely given.*

That was the lie. That had always been the lie. The children had been lured with coins in gutters and notes folded into lunch pails and promises about kittens; they had been taken from fields at dusk and from porches at dawn; and the men who signed the bargain in 1931 had written *freely given* into the contract because the thing beneath the school required it, because even a hunger as old as floodwater needed the paperwork to be clean.

But the terms cut both ways. Doraleen had found it, squinting at the margins with a magnifying glass and seventy years of grievance: *What is freely given may be kept. What is stolen must be returned, if named correctly, at the hour of the moon.*

The moon was up. Juniper could feel it through however many feet of earth lay above them—a pressure, a tide, a great white eye pressing down through the soil—and the humming had changed key to match it.

She lifted her voice.

It surprised her, how steady it came out. She had expected to shake. She had spent weeks shaking—at mirrors, at notebooks emptied overnight, at small shoes arranged in pairs along a corridor deeper than the building above them was tall—but standing here, in the place where the town's whole rotten architecture began, she felt something she had not felt since the day she'd driven in counting roadkill: clarity. Cold and complete, like the first hard frost.

"This is a reading of the record," she said. "Entered at the hour of the moon, per the terms."

The signing stopped.

Twelve pens, or thirteen, hung above twelve pages, or thirteen, and the silence that fell was so total Juniper could hear the water around her ankles ticking as it cooled. The founders' faces turned toward her—not their heads, just their faces, rotating slightly like portraits adjusting themselves on a wall.

She read the first name.

"Walter Hask," she said. "Taken October the ninth, 1962. Age nine. Taken from the Hask farm on Corncrake Road while his sister watched the road for him. He did not go freely. He did not know what waited. He was *stolen*—"

The word landed in the chamber like a stone dropped into a well, and the well answered. The black water shivered outward from Juniper's feet in a perfect ring. Somewhere far above, or very near, something exhaled.

"—and what is stolen must be returned."

Doraleen made a sound beside her. Not a word. Something older than words, the sound a body makes when a wound it has carried for fifty-six years is finally touched. Her brother's name, spoken aloud in this place for the first time since the harvest moon of 1962.

Juniper turned the page.

"Edith Carrow. Taken September the twenty-eighth, 1963. Age ten. Lured from the schoolyard with a promise of kittens that did not exist. She cried for her mother for—" Juniper's throat closed, because the ledger knew this, the ledger recorded it in the child's matter-of-fact hand, *she cried until the third hour, then stopped,* "—and she did not go freely. She was stolen. Return her."

The humming rose half a tone.

"Raymond Pell. Taken October the fourth, 1965. Age eight." Page turn. "Susan Vann. Taken September the nineteenth, 1966. Age eleven. She fought. It is written here that she fought, and the ledger calls that *consent*, and the ledger lies." Her voice did not rise; it sharpened, the way winter sharpens. "Thomas Hask. Taken October the eleventh, 1968. Age seven. His sister has come here tonight, an old woman now, to stand witness that he was stolen. Return him."

Doraleen's chin was up. Tears ran freely down the seams of her face and she let them, unashamed, her eyes fixed on the ring of founders as if she could burn them down by looking.

Page after page. Juniper gave each name its full weight, its date, its age, its particular theft—the boy taken from the flood plain, the girl taken from the church steps during evening service while the congregation sang, the twins taken together in 1971 because the ledger noted, with a child's terrible economy, *two at once, a discount*. Every name she spoke struck the water and rang outward, and every ring of it made the founders' pages flutter though there was no wind, made their pens tremble, made the darkness overhead groan like a hull in deep water.

The chamber was getting colder. Her breath had begun to show.

"Abel Grieves," she read, and heard the man at the top of the stairs flinch. "Taken October the second, 1972. Age ten. Your own blood, Mr. Grieves. Your family kept the books and paid the books with their own sons when the ledger demanded it. He was stolen. Return him."

"Clara Moss. Taken September the thirtieth, 1973. Age nine."

And then the last entry. The unfinished one. The page where Ellery Voss's careful handwriting simply stopped mid-stroke, the pen dragged sideways off the paper as if the hand writing it had been pulled away.

"And Ellery Voss," Juniper said. "Custodian of Harrow's Bend School. Hired in 1961. He discovered what you were. He spent thirteen years trying to end it, and in October of 1974 he came down here alone, at the wrong hour, without the correct names, and you took him too. He is not a payment. He was never a payment. He was a man who tried to do right in a town that had forgotten how, and you stole him, and I am naming him, and I demand his return."

She closed the ledger.

The sound of it—soft, final, a child's book of debts shutting—went through the chamber like a verdict.

* * *

For three heartbeats, nothing.

Then the founders moved.

Not walking—they had never walked, Juniper realized; they had stood in their ring for ninety years the way stains stand in wood—and yet suddenly they were all facing her, all twelve, all thirteen, their wax faces split by expressions that were trying very hard to be reasonable. That was the horror of them, she thought. Not their age or their stillness but their *reasonableness*, the look of men about to explain why the terrible thing was actually the sensible thing.

"You misunderstand the instrument," said the tallest one, and his voice was every landlord who wouldn't cross a porch, every principal pouring coffee no one drank, every cheerful deflection she had endured since dusk on her first day in Harrow's Bend. "The account is settled. Settled accounts cannot be reopened. The valley did not flood. Ninety years of harvests came in. Everyone ate."

"Everyone ate," Juniper repeated.

"The arithmetic was cruel but the arithmetic was *necessary*—"

"Then say it plainly." She held the ledger against her chest. "Say: we fed children to the dark so our wheat would grow. Say: we wrote *freely given* on the graves of stolen kids because the contract needed it and we were glad to sign. Say it, and I'll believe your arithmetic matters more than theirs."

The founder's mouth worked. No words came out. Because the words were true, and the chamber—the whole flooded, layered, sedimented memory of the place—was listening for truth the way the moon listened for names.

The entity tried something else.

The water around them went silver. And out of that silver, rising like reflections rising out of a lagging mirror, came the children—all of them at once, all eleven, plus Voss in his coveralls with his kind tired face—standing in the black water, smiling, whole, unaged, exactly as they'd been on the days they vanished. Walter Hask waved. Clara Moss giggled behind her hand.

*Stay,* said the chamber, in a voice made of humming and warm ground and every beautiful lie ever told beautifully. *They are happy here. Nothing hurts here. Take the book home and the girl stays safe and everyone forgets, and forgetting is a kind of mercy, isn't it? You wanted mercy. You've wanted it since the day you drove in.*

Beside her, she felt Doraleen sway.

"Doraleen." Juniper did not look away from the shining children. "Look at Walter's shoes."

A pause. Then, low and iron-hard: "Those aren't his shoes. His mother bought him new ones for picture day. Those are the shoes from the *photograph they printed*. It's showing us the photograph." A wet, furious laugh. "It doesn't even remember them. It remembers our *grief* and it's wearing it."

The silver flickered.

"It lies beautifully," Doraleen had warned her, two nights ago, in a kitchen that smelled of coffee and old paper. "That's its whole talent. Everything else it does with hunger. But the lying—it does like art."

Juniper raised the ledger again, and this time she did not open it. She held it shut, against her heart, and she spoke the terms from memory, because her notebook had been emptied once already and she had learned that the only archive that couldn't be burned was the one she carried in her body.

"What is freely given may be kept," she recited. "What is stolen must be returned, if named correctly, at the hour of the moon. Eleven names I have named. A twelfth I add: Ellery Voss, named correctly, at the hour of the moon. The record shows *taken*, not given. The record shows *lured*, not willing. The record shows children who fought, who cried, who ran, who were carried. There is no consent in these pages. There is only your signature, and a signature is not permission—it is *confession*."

The founders' pens ignited.

Not with fire—with light, cold and white, the color of the moon that pressed down through the earth, and it ran along the lines of their signatures the way flame runs along a fuse. The founders looked down at their own burning hands and did not scream. They went on trying to look reasonable, even as the ink lifted off the air in long curling ribbons of white fire, even as the pages in their grip went translucent and then transparent and then were simply gone, ninety years of false accounting unwriting itself in front of her.

*You will drown us,* said the voice, and for the first time it did not sound beautiful. It sounded old, and afraid, and vast. *Without the payments, the valley returns to what it was. Without me, the river remembers its course. You will trade twelve lives for a drowned town.*

"The river runs high and harmless," Juniper said, "or it doesn't. Either way, we stop paying you in children. That was never yours to price."

The white light broke upward.

It came through the chamber like water through a broken dam—inverted, flooding the ceiling-darkness, flooding the hanging ribs of the school's foundation, flooding the stairs where Grieves stood with his arm thrown across his eyes—and everywhere it touched, the humming shattered into a thousand separate voices, and the voices were crying out, and the voices were *names*, all the names at once, called by themselves, in their own young voices, for the first time in decades.

Marisol buried her face in Juniper's coat. Juniper put her free hand on the girl's head and held the ledger up into the light with the other, and she shouted the last line of the rite into the roar, the line Doraleen had made her rehearse until her throat was raw:

"*Returned! All of them—returned!*"

The signatures finished burning.

The founders came apart with them, quietly, like ash deciding to be ash, and where the ring had stood there was only the black water, and in the black water, floating gently, face-down, were eleven small shapes and one large one, drifting toward the shore of the cellar floor as if set adrift by a current that had finally, finally reversed.

The light died to a single pale glow, moon-colored, sourceless.

The humming stopped.

In the ringing silence, Juniper became aware of three things at once: that the water around her ankles had gone cold—truly cold, river-cold; that Marisol was trembling against her side but alive, alive, alive; and that the nearest of the floating shapes had begun, slowly, to turn over.

Doraleen was already wading toward it, arms out, whispering her brother's name like a prayer and a curse braided together.

The shape turned. It reached the shallows. It lifted its head.

And it opened its mouth and began to scream.
