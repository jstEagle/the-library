## 6. Wax and Lead

The core drill bit bit into the wax at 6:47 in the morning, and Elias Brandt felt the sound of it in his teeth.

It wasn't the sound a drill should make. He'd been running crews for eleven years, through granite and clay and the fossilized garbage of older cities, and he knew the vocabulary of the bit—the shriek against rebar, the wet grind of saturated soil, the clean chatter of good stone. This was none of those. This was soft. The drill sank into the seal the way a spoon sinks into tallow left too long near a stove, and the resistance it offered was not mechanical at all. It was, he thought before he could stop himself, the resistance of something deciding to let him.

"Foreman." Dziga stood at his elbow, arms crossed, hard hat pulled low. "City said no sampling. Your words, Monday morning. *Nobody touches the door.* Your words."

"The city said no demolition. No breach. No removal of structural material." Elias kept his eyes on the drill housing, on the slow accumulation of pale material curling up around the bit's flutes like the rind of some enormous fruit. "A two-centimeter core is a sample. I wrote the distinction myself."

"You wrote it so you could do this."

"Yes."

Dziga exhaled through his nose, a long gray plume in the cold of the shaft. The Meridian site had gone down four levels now, past the parking strata, past the Victorian sewers, into a band of bedrock the geologists kept calling *anomalous* in their reports, as if the word were a hand pressed flat against something that wanted to stand up. The stone door sat at the bottom of the access cut like a held breath. Lead sheeting, green with age, nailed over a frame of black oak gone hard as iron. And across the whole face of it, layered thick as bark, the wax.

Elias had photographed it three times now. The photos never came out right. In every frame the wax looked flat, featureless, a smear of candlelight color—but standing in front of it, you could see the surface had texture. Ridges. Rills. A pattern that repeated in a way his eye kept trying to resolve into letters and his eye kept failing, the way it fails at the pattern of rain on a window.

"Run it slow," he said.

"It's already slow. It's slower than slow. It's practically apologetic." Dziga spat into the sump. "I want it on record I think this is a bad idea."

"It's on record. I'll initial it."

The core came free at 7:03. Sixteen minutes for two centimeters, which was absurd, which was the kind of number that would get the whole operation audited if he ever reported it honestly. He lifted the sample tube with both hands, careful as a man lifting a sleeping child, and the cylinder of wax inside was pale gold, translucent at the edges, and threaded through its length—fine as veins in marble—were dark lines.

He didn't look at them closely. Not yet. Not with Dziga watching, and Okafor, and the two laborers from the north crew who had already started calling the door *the tomb* in voices pitched just loud enough to carry. He capped the tube, labeled it in his own handwriting—*MERIDIAN SUB-BASEMENT, SEAL MATERIAL, E.B.*—and carried it up four ladders and out into the gray morning light of the site office, where he set it on his desk under the fluorescent tubes and finally, alone, allowed himself to look.

* * *

The dark lines were hair.

Not one hair. Dozens. Hundreds. Running through the wax the way straw runs through old bricks, oriented—this was the detail that made him sit down—oriented. Not tangled, not random. All running in the same direction, following the curve of the core, following, he realized slowly, with his hand rising unconsciously to his own scalp, the contour of a head. As if the wax had been poured over someone. As if the seal were a casting taken from a face, and the hair had come away with it, and there had been so much wax, so many pourings, so many faces, that the hair of all of them had become a stratum, a geology, a record.

He turned the tube under the light. The hair was brown. Then black. Then a coarse iron gray. Different lengths, different thicknesses. One strand, fine as spider silk, was unmistakably a child's.

Elias had a steady stomach. It was a professional requirement and a family trait; his father could eat lunch over an open grave survey and never lose his appetite. But his stomach moved now, a slow cold roll, and he set the tube down and pressed his palms flat against the desk until the wood's ordinary grain—coffee rings, a scratch from a staple gun, the whole dumb honest debris of office life—brought him back.

There was more in the wax. He could see it now that he knew to look. Small pale inclusions near the core's outer edge, where the wax met the lead. He worked the sample free with a palette knife from his survey kit, slicing thin sections onto a sheet of white paper, and the sections came away like the pages of some obscene book.

Fingernails. He counted nine of them in the first three slices. Human, small, some with the pale lunula still visible at the base, cut or torn free and pressed into the wax like tokens, like signatures. And then, in the fourth slice, at the very center of the core, at the deepest point, at the place that had touched the door itself—

A tooth.

A milk tooth. Lower incisor, by the shape of it, root fully resorbed, crown worn smooth. The kind of tooth that comes out in a child's hand, or in a mouthful of apple, or—his mind supplied the image unasked, and he let it come, because he was a Brandt and Brandts did not look away from what they built—because someone had pried it loose and set it in the wax deliberately, at the center, at the heart, the way you'd set a keystone.

He sat for a long time. The site noise went on around him without pause—the generators, the hoist, Okafor's laugh from somewhere above—and the tooth lay on its square of white paper, small and white and impossibly ordinary, and Elias Brandt understood that he was looking at the oldest thing he would ever touch. Older than the city. Older than the street names, older than the parish registers, older, he suspected, than the language the founding documents were written in. Someone had stood where the door now stood, on the inside, and sealed it behind them, and they had taken their child's tooth with them, or their child's tooth had been taken, and either way it had gone into the wax, into the seal, into the work.

*We build things to last,* his great-grandfather's generation had believed. He'd grown up on the family stories: the Brandts who laid the Meridian footings, the Brandts who rebuilt the east seawall after the flood of '09, the Brandts whose name was on three plaques in the city archive and one, famously, on a cornerstone downtown. *We build things to last.* He had always heard it as pride.

Sitting in the site office with a child's tooth on a piece of paper, he heard it differently. We build things to last. Not a boast. A sentence. A term of service.

He photographed the sections. He photographed the tooth. Then he wrapped everything back into the sample tube, sealed it in an evidence bag, and locked it in the steel cabinet behind his desk, and he sat with the key in his fist for a while, feeling its teeth press into his palm, and thought: *someone should have told us. Someone should have told us what we were maintaining.*

And then, because he was honest with himself in the way that made him difficult to work for, he thought: *someone did. And we stopped listening on purpose.*

* * *

He didn't sleep that night. He went home to his apartment above the dry cleaner on Vasseur Street and he ate standing up and he read, for the third time, the site geology report, and then he read the historical survey appendix, and then he pulled the one box of family papers he'd inherited when his mother died and went through it page by page under the kitchen light, looking for wax, looking for teeth, looking for anything. There was nothing. There were deeds and photographs and his great-grandfather's ceremonial shovel from the seawall groundbreaking, and there was a single letter, water-stained, that he'd read a dozen times and always found bland: his great-grandfather Augustin writing to his brother in 1911 about the Meridian contract, the good terms, the land payment, and then one line that Elias had always skimmed past and now read ten times in a row:

*The work below is not like the work above, and the terms reflect it. We are paid once for the building and forever for the keeping. Do not envy us the land.*

He fell asleep at the kitchen table around three with the letter under his hand, and dreamed of a door, and in the dream he was on the wrong side of it, and the wax was being poured over him in warm layers, and someone was counting. He woke with the taste of tallow in his mouth and his own name half-spoken in the dark.

* * *

Dziga quit at seven the next morning.

Elias knew before he said a word. He came up the access ladder into the gray drizzle and found his crew chief standing by the hoist cage with his bag already packed, his hard hat clean and clipped to it, his high-visibility vest folded with a neatness that was itself a kind of message. Dziga was not a neat man. Dziga's locker was a landslide. The folded vest said *this is final* more clearly than anything he could have said.

"You opened it," Dziga said.

"I cored it. Two centimeters."

"You opened it and you looked." Dziga's face in the drizzle was the color of wet ash. Twenty-two years underground. A man who'd once eaten his lunch sitting on a coffin lid during a cemetery relocation and told jokes about it. "I saw the bag in your cabinet. I saw what you brought out of it yesterday. I've been in this trade my whole life, Elias. I've pulled bodies out of old wells. I've cut through plague pits. I found a mummified dog in a chimney once and kept it on my shelf for a year." He stopped. His jaw worked. "You don't seal a door with hair."

"Dziga—"

"You don't seal a door with *teeth*." His voice cracked on the word and he hated it, Elias could see him hating it, a lifetime of underground composure failing him at the last. "Wax and lead, fine. Bad old masonry, fine. People were ignorant, people were frightened, people buried their dead under their floors and nailed horseshoes over their doors. But that—" he pointed down, at the access cut, at the four levels of ladder and light between them and the stone door, "—that isn't fear of the dead. That's fear of something that isn't dead. And somebody knew it, and somebody made sure we'd never know it, and you went and looked anyway, and now it knows we looked."

"That's not—"

"Okafor heard it last night. In the shaft. After we all went up." Dziga stepped into the hoist cage and gripped the rail. "A hum. Low. Like a room full of people breathing in time. He thought it was the pumps. It wasn't the pumps, Elias. The pumps were off."

Elias said nothing. There was nothing true he could say, because Okafor's hum was the same hum he'd heard himself, two nights ago, standing alone at the bottom of the cut with his palm flat against the lead sheeting, feeling through the metal and the wax and the stone a vibration that was not machinery and not water and not any settling the earth had ever done, a vibration with *patience* in it, and he had taken his hand away and told no one.

"Sign my time sheet," Dziga said. "And Elias—" he paused, and for a moment the crew chief looked out of his face, the man who'd taught him how to read soil, how to hear a void before the probe found it, "—whatever your family did down there, whatever they were paid, don't pay the rest of it. Walk away. Some contracts you inherit, you burn."

The cage rose. Elias stood in the drizzle watching it go, and then he turned and walked back down the four ladders to the bottom of the cut, alone, because he was a Brandt and Brandts did not look away.

The door waited. The wax glistened faintly in the work lights, and in its surface, in the rills and ridges his camera could never capture, the pattern had shifted—or he had never seen it rightly, or it was tired and it was morning and his eyes were wrong. But standing there with the taste of tallow still in his mouth, Elias Brandt could have sworn that the ridges of the wax had arranged themselves, overnight, into something almost like a face.

And the hum was there. Faint. Steady. Breathing in time.

He put his hand on the lead, and this time he did not take it away, and he stood there until the crew came down and found him, and no one said anything, because they had all, by then, heard it too.

That evening, the city's planning office called. A subsidence concern at the Meridian site, they said. They were sending a surveyor.

Her name, the clerk read off the work order, was Iversen.

Elias stood in the site office with the phone against his ear and the sample cabinet at his back, and he thought of his great-grandfather's letter, and of the word *keeping*, and he heard himself say, "Send her down."
