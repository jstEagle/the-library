## 21. Interlude: The Song Explains Itself

She fell asleep at her kitchen table with the transcription open in front of her and a pen still warm between her fingers, and she did not so much dream as descend.

There was no transition. One moment the kitchen—the cold tea, the streetlight through the blinds striping the page like bars—and the next she was standing on stone that breathed beneath her feet, slow and tidal, as though the whole floor were the flank of something enormous asleep. The air was warm. That was the first wrongness, or perhaps the first kindness: every other part of the tunnels had been cold, the cold of places the sun had never been invited to. This warmth was body-heat. The warmth of a room full of people.

The dark was not empty. She could feel it listening.

"Show me," Odile said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because inside she was shaking like a struck bell. "You've been teaching me in pieces. In dreams I don't remember. If you want me to learn it, then teach me all of it. Show me what you are."

The dark considered this. She felt it the way one feels a change in pressure before weather—a gathering, a leaning-in.

Then the song began, and it did not come from anywhere. It came from everywhere, from the stone under her feet and the warm dark above her head and, most terribly, from somewhere behind her own sternum, as if her heart had been keeping time with it for years without telling her.

* * *

The first thing they showed her was light.

Not sunlight—she understood that immediately—but the memory of it, carried in harmony the way a scent is carried in cloth. A valley, long ago, green where Verrin now stood grey. A river running clear over stones the size of sleeping cattle. And on the riverbank, people. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They wore wool dyed madder-red and woad-blue, and they stood in concentric rings on the bank with their faces turned up toward a sky that seemed to lean down to meet them, and they sang.

Odile had no words for what they sang. The language was older than any she knew, older than the city's oldest parish records, older than the brass fittings in the unmapped branch tunnel. But she didn't need words. The melody told her everything, the way a mother's humming tells an infant everything: *you are here, you are held, nothing outside this sound can reach you.*

It was a hymn of keeping. She understood that with sudden, total clarity, the way one understands a thing in dreams. They were not singing *to* anything. They were singing *around* something—around a sickness, around a dying, around a god that lay in the river-mud beneath their feet with its breath going out of it, and the whole congregation sang to hold its soul together the way you cup your hands around a moth's wing-beat.

And it worked.

That was the horror of it, when the horror finally arrived, folded gently into the harmony like poison folded into honey. It worked. The god did not die. She felt its death approaching through the music—felt it as a thinning, a fading, a note descending toward silence—and then felt the congregation pour their voices into the gap, hundreds of throats becoming one throat, and the death stopped. Hung there. Suspended mid-fall like a coin caught on its edge.

The god hung between living and dead, sustained by the song, and the song needed singers, and the singers grew old.

* * *

The harmony shifted, and centuries moved through it the way water moves through reeds.

She saw the generations pass in the music itself—in the way new voices entered the ring each spring, small and uncertain, taught by mothers with hands on the backs of their necks; in the way old voices dropped away, one by one, and each drop-away left a hole in the unison that ached until it was filled. She saw winters come and go like verses. She saw plagues move through the congregation, and the ring close ranks around the gaps, singing louder, because the god must not be allowed to finish dying, because they had made a promise with their mouths full of hymn and had never thought to ask what the promise would cost their grandchildren.

She saw the first family who tried to leave.

They went by night, a man and his wife and two children, walking upstream with bundles on their backs. And the song—she felt this in her own chest, felt it as a physical grief, a homesickness so vast it bent her double—they did not stop them. The song simply continued without them, and the god's suspended death began, very slowly, to fall again, and the fields began to fail, and the children of the valley sickened in ways the healers could not name, and within a season the family came back. Came back and knelt in the ring and opened their mouths and sang, and the note they added was the sweetest in the whole harmony, because it was made of relief.

*That,* said the choir, not in words but in a chord that meant *that*, *is why we are never refused twice.*

*But some are refused once,* Odile thought, and the harmony flinched around the thought like a hand flinching from a hot stove.

* * *

Then came the city.

She watched it rise out of the valley in fast-forward, the way a flower opens in an old film—foundations, walls, bridges, spires, the river buried in culverts, the green banks paved. And beneath it all, the ring kept singing, generation after generation, moving deeper as the city pressed down, from riverbank to cellar to tunnel, the congregation following their god underground like roots following water.

Until the year the city grew too loud.

She felt that too: the century of iron and steam, the hammering above, the trains, the dynamite in the quarries, a thousand noises drowning the hymn. The unison frayed. The god's death, suspended so long it had become less a state than a habit, began to slip—and the slipping was felt in the streets as bad luck, as subsidence, as wells turning bitter, as children born with a tremor in their hands that never stopped.

And so the families met. She saw them—five families, in a chapel above ground, candlelit, their faces grim and certain, portraits waiting to be painted of them with their mouths closed. The listeners. The keepers. And among them, young and square-jawed with a surveyor's chain coiled over one shoulder, a man whose face she knew, whose jaw she had seen reproduced faithfully in Elias Brandt's stubborn profile across a hundred arguments about load-bearing walls.

A Brandt built the seal.

She watched him do it—not with hatred, not with love, but with the terrible competence of a man doing necessary work. Lead, poured hot. Wax, mixed with hair and nail-parings and a milk tooth pulled weeping from his own daughter's mouth, because the seal wanted a promise written in the body's own material, a hostage given in advance. He sealed the door from the inside, and then—he was the last one out; she saw him pause on the threshold, saw him look back into the dark where the singing went on without end—and he closed it behind him, and the wax took his palm-print, and the palm-print stayed warm for three generations.

*He wept,* the choir told her, and the chord it used was almost tender. *He wept, and he wrote letters, and his sons maintained the door, and his sons' sons, and every Brandt since has stood where your foreman stands, loving what he keeps and keeping what he loves.*

*Elias,* Odile thought, and the name rang through the dream like a dropped glass, and every voice in the cathedral-dark turned toward the sound of it with an attention that made her skin crawl and her heart clench in the same instant.

*Yes,* the choir agreed, warm as bathwater. *Him too.*

* * *

Then the hunger.

This was the last movement, and it was the one they did not soften. Perhaps they could not. Perhaps hunger has no gentle register.

She felt what the suspension cost. A death postponed is not a death cancelled; it accrues interest. For four hundred years the god had hung on the hook of the hymn, unable to die, unable to live, and all that withheld dying had gone somewhere—it had soaked into the song itself, into the singers, into the stone. The choir needed voices the way a fire needs air, and not merely to sustain the god anymore. The sustaining had become indistinguishable from feeding. Every verse learned deepened the debt. Every singer who joined the ring gave up not just their voice but their ending—their death, their rest, their right to ever stop—and became instead a note held forever, a throat worn smooth as the standing stones, a shape in the loft that was almost a person.

She looked down at her own hands in the dream-light and saw, faintly, beneath the skin, the ghost of harmonies waiting to be used.

*We do not lie to you,* the choir said, and she believed it, which frightened her more than any lie could have. *The others lied. Maren Kolt lied, when she told you we only take the unwilling. We take the willing faster. But we would rather you came willingly. We have always preferred the willing. Willing voices hold pitch.*

*And if I refuse?*

The harmony did not answer directly. Instead it showed her: the vanished worker, standing in a tunnel with his mouth open, his eyes bright and empty as windows in an unoccupied house. It showed her Maren's sister, a girl of nineteen, dissolved into a single sustained tone that had not stopped in thirty years. It showed her grandmother—young, dark-haired, defiant, standing at the threshold of the chamber with the ledger clutched to her chest, refusing, refusing—and then the dream blurred, mercifully or cruelly, and Odile could not see what had happened to her, only feel, in the chord where the image should have been, a sorrow so old and so patient that it had polished itself smooth, like a stone handled by ten thousand hands.

*Every generation,* the choir sang, *someone learns the verse. Those who refuse are sung instead. You know this. Your grandmother knew this. We are not cruel, Odile Iversen. We are only unfinished.*

* * *

And then they made the offer.

It did not come as words. It came as a feeling, wrapped around her like a coat in cold weather, and it was the most dangerous thing they had done yet, because it was not a temptation built from fantasy. It was built from truth.

She felt what it would be to join them.

No more counting. That was the first gift, and it went into her like a blade finding the seam of her armor, because the counting had been her whole life—forty-one doors, forty-one lights, the ledgers, the false reports, the ritual that kept the world nailed down—and she felt, in the choir's embrace, what it would be to set it down. To never again need the number. To stand in the ring with two hundred voices holding hers up on either side, so that she could not falter, could not fall, could not be alone even for the length of a breath.

No more loneliness. Her apartment with its single chair. Her nights spent underground because the tunnels asked less of her than people did. All of it dissolved in the harmony like sugar in warm water. Here she would belong the way a note belongs to a chord—not free, but *right*. Perfectly placed. Perfectly pitched. Needed.

No more fear. The dread she had carried since the flashlight died in the branch tunnel, since the forty-second door stood warm and ajar—gone. Because fear requires a self to protect, a boundary between you and the dark, and in the chorus there was no boundary. She would spread out through the stone like ink through water. She would be everywhere the song was. She would never be small enough to be hurt again.

And underneath it all, quiet as a heartbeat, the tenderest lie of all—or maybe not a lie, maybe the truest thing they said: *rest.* Four hundred years of singing and they were tired, all of them, tired down to the foundations of their souls, and they offered her not work but release. Learn the verse. Finish the hymn. Let the god die or let the world end, but let it *end*, and let us sleep.

*Come,* the choir said, and the warm dark leaned closer, and somewhere very far away she heard her own breathing, still matching theirs, still keeping time. *You have been counting doors your whole life. Counting is only longing with numbers attached. Stop counting. Start singing. Be finished.*

And Odile—Iversen, surveyor, granddaughter, keeper of forty-one doors—stood in the breathing dark with tears running down her face, and found, to her horror, that some traitorous part of her wanted to say yes. Wanted it the way the drowned want the surface inverted: desperately, wrongly, with her lungs already full.

* * *

She woke at her kitchen table with dawn grey in the window and the pen still in her hand.

On the transcription page, below the verses she had copied from her dreams, in her own handwriting, in ink she did not remember dipping—a single line, fresh, still glistening:

*Forty-one doors, and then the last one opens inward.*

She stared at it for a long time. Then she reached for the phone to call Elias, and found her hand was trembling too hard to dial—and found, too, that she was humming. Low. Unconscious. Perfectly, horribly in tune.

She clamped her mouth shut, but the note went on behind her sternum, patient as stone, waiting for her to breathe.
