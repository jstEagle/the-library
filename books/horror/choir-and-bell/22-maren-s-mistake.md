## 22. Maren's Mistake

Maren Kolt had been counting the days since she first saw Odile Iversen's name, and the number was one hundred and eleven.

She counted the way her mother had taught her, the way all the listener families counted—not for comfort, not for control, but as a kind of prayer. Each number was a small offering. *One hundred and eleven days since the ledger turned its page. One hundred and eleven days since the song chose its next voice.* She had watched Odile through the long autumn: at the municipal office where she filed her false reports, at the corner bakery where she bought the same rye loaf every Thursday, at the mouth of the maintenance tunnel on Delacroix Street where she descended with her flashlight and her meticulous, doomed arithmetic. Maren had stood in doorways and behind the frosted glass of tram stops and thought, *she has her grandmother's jaw, her grandmother's stubbornness, her grandmother's terrible gift for being exactly where the song wants her.*

And now, in the chapel with its portraits of closed-mouthed singers, Maren was going to tell her the truth. Or the shape of a truth. She had not yet decided which.

The chapel smelled of cold stone and extinguished candles, of the particular dust that gathers only in rooms where no one prays anymore but everyone remembers how. Odile sat in the third pew with her hands folded, and beside her Elias Brandt sat with his hands balled into fists, the way he had done everything since Maren had led them through the deconsecrated door. He did not trust her. Good. Distrust was a kind of armor, and the Brandt man would need every plate of it before winter.

Above them, the portraits watched. Five generations of listeners rendered in oils that had darkened to the color of old tea, every face painted with lips pressed shut, eyes lowered or averted, as if each sitter had been caught mid-flinch. Maren had grown up beneath these faces. As a child she had believed they were ashamed. It had taken her thirty years to understand they were not ashamed at all.

They were listening.

"You said five families kept vigil here," Odile said. Her voice was steady, but Maren could hear the hum underneath it now—could hear it in anyone the song had touched, a faint resonance in the vowels, like a wineglass remembering a note. "Vigil against what, exactly? You've told us what the seal keeps in. You haven't told us what the listeners *do*."

"We keep the names," Maren said. "We keep the ledger current. We make sure the inheritance passes cleanly, mother to daughter, father to son, generation to generation, so that no one is ever taken by surprise." She paused. "Surprise is cruel. The song is cruel enough without it."

"That's not an answer," Elias said.

"No," Maren agreed. "It isn't."

She walked to the front of the chapel, to the small alcove behind the pulpit where the oldest portrait hung—the one no visitor was ever shown. A woman in a high-collared dress, painted in 1841, her mouth a thin line, her hands holding a hymnal closed against her chest. *Anneliese Kolt.* Maren's great-great-great-grandmother, though the family had shortened and shifted the name over the centuries the way names do when they are trying to hide.

"Her sister's name was Grete," Maren said. "There's no portrait of Grete. There are no portraits of anyone the song took, because the song doesn't need portraits. It keeps its own records." She touched the frame. The gilt was worn to bare wood where generations of Kolts had touched it in the same place, the way a doorframe wears where hands push it open. "I'm going to tell you about my sister, Odile. And when I'm finished, you're going to understand why every warning I've given you was true, and why none of it was kind."

* * *

My sister's name was Ilse.

She was four years younger than me, and she laughed before she understood jokes, which is the purest form of laughter there is. She had our mother's crooked smile and our father's terrible singing voice—he was famously, gloriously tone-deaf, and at family gatherings he would bellow hymns off-key with such conviction that the whole room would dissolve. We grew up in the chapel house, the two of us, in the rooms behind this sanctuary, and our mother taught us the ledger the way other mothers teach table manners: early, gently, as a fact of the world. *There is a song under the city. Our family listens for it. We do not sing.*

Ilse was the one who asked why.

You have to understand—Odile, Elias—that the listeners' children are raised on a diet of almost. Almost the truth. Almost the whole story. We learn that the choir exists, that it hungers, that a verse is missing, that every generation someone is chosen to learn the ending. What we do not learn—what our mothers and fathers cannot bring themselves to say, because saying it makes it real—is what happens to the ones who are chosen and refuse. The official language is *they are sung instead.* Four words. A euphemism with the weight of a tombstone. It was only when I was nineteen, going through Anneliese's papers, that I found what the phrase actually meant. The ledgers are precise, if you know how to read them. *Sung* is a verb with an object. The choir does not kill you and add your voice to itself. That would be mercy. The choir takes you the way a river takes a dropped coin—you are still you, sinking, aware, for a very long time.

Ilse found the same papers two years later. She was seventeen, and she had our father's stubbornness without his humor, and she decided that if the song wanted a singer, it could damn well have one on her own terms. She would learn the final verse, she told me. She would sing it *perfectly*, better than anyone, and she would sing it alone, and the choir would be finished—ended, freed, whatever the right word was—and no one would ever be taken again. She had it all worked out. She had copied the backward verses from the hymnal into a notebook and turned them forward, and she practiced in the cellar where our mother couldn't hear, and I let her. God forgive me. I was twenty-one and I thought she was playing at heroism the way children play at pirates, and I thought our mother would catch her in time, and I said nothing.

Our mother did catch her. Three weeks before the winter solstice, when the seal is thinnest. And here is the thing I need you to understand, Odile, because everything else follows from it: our mother was not angry. She was *terrified*, and her terror had a very specific shape. She sat Ilse down at the kitchen table and she said: *You cannot sing the verse alone. Not because you would fail. Because you would succeed.*

The final verse is not a song. It is an instruction. The choir has been singing the same unison for three hundred years, holding a dead god at the edge of death, and what the verse carries—the words in the language older than the city—is the command that tells the harmony what to become. Sung in unison, by the whole choir, it would be an ending: the god dies, the song stops, the tunnels are only tunnels. But sung *alone*—sung by one voice standing in for the whole—the command has nowhere to go except inward. One voice cannot unmake the choir. One voice can only unmake itself, completely, and offer the pieces up as a gift. A soloist doesn't end the song. A soloist becomes the song's new center. Its heart. The note everything else tunes itself to, forever.

Our mother knew this because our mother had read the ledger entries from 1841. Anneliese's sister Grete had tried exactly what Ilse wanted to try. Grete had gone down into the tunnels on the winter solstice of 1841 with the verse copied forward in her own hand, and she had sung it alone, and she had been *magnificent*—the ledgers say so, in Anneliese's handwriting, the letters pressed so hard into the paper they tore. *She sang beautifully. It was the worst thing she could have done.* The choir took her not as a voice but as a tuning fork. For eleven months afterward, every member of the Kolt family heard her in their sleep, perfect, patient, calling the rest of us down to join her. Our mother's grandmother had gone deaf in one ear trying not to listen. In the end it was Anneliese who went down—not to sing, but to stand at the door of the choir loft and speak Grete's name backward until the call went quiet. That is the listeners' real work, Odile. Not vigil. *Retrieval*. We are the ones who go down and call our taken family members home, or fail, and come back up with our ears bleeding, and add a line to the ledger, and raise our children on almost.

Ilse heard all of this at the kitchen table, and I watched her face, and I saw the moment she decided our mother was lying. Seventeen-year-olds are very good at that. She said, "You just don't want to lose me," and our mother said, "Yes, and that is the *least* of the reasons," and Ilse went to her room, and three weeks later, on the solstice, she went down through the Delacroix tunnel with her notebook and her father's flashlight, and I did not stop her, because I was twenty-one and in love with a boy in Antwerp and I had left the chapel house two days before and I *knew*, some animal part of me knew, and I let the train take me away because the song had already begun to use my sister's voice in my dreams and I could not bear it.

I have had thirty years to decide whether I could have stopped her. The answer is yes. The answer is always yes. That is what the listeners are for, in the end—not to guard the seal, not to keep the names, but to be the last hand on the shoulder of the one who is chosen. And I took my hand off my sister's shoulder and got on a train.

* * *

Maren stopped. The chapel was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe ticked as the building cooled, and the sound was so ordinary it was almost obscene.

"She reached the choir loft," Odile said quietly. It was not a question.

"She reached it. The ledgers record what the listeners on watch that night heard through the stone. She sang the verse alone, and she sang it *flawlessly*—every interval true, every word in its place, the grammar of it perfect. And the choir did not end." Maren's hand was still on the portrait frame. She noticed she had been gripping it hard enough to hurt and made herself let go. "The choir *arranged itself around her*. Do you understand? Three hundred voices that had held one note for three centuries suddenly had a melody to move toward. My sister became the thing the song was reaching for. And the song, which had been patient for three hundred years, became impatient. The seal cracked in nine places that winter. Two listeners from the Vasseur family went down to mend it and did not come back. It took the combined vigil of all five families, four years of nightly calling, to quiet her voice enough that the city above stopped dreaming in unison."

"And Ilse?" Odile asked. "Is she still—"

"She is still singing. They are all still singing. That is the part no one tells you: the choir does not consume its taken ones the way fire consumes wood. It keeps them. My sister is down there right now, in the loft, in the shape that is almost a person, holding the note I should have stopped her from reaching. And every night for thirty years I have heard her in the hour before dawn, singing the verse back to me, *perfectly*, the way only she could, asking me in her wordless way why I wasn't there."

Elias had gone gray. "You said your warnings were true but not kind. What did you mean? What aren't you telling us?"

Maren turned to face them both. This was the part she had rehearsed for a hundred and eleven days, and it still sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"I mean that the listeners do not only listen," she said. "Sit down, Brandt. Both of you. Because the next part is the reason I found you, Odile Iversen, and followed you, and warned you, and it is not the reason you think."

* * *

The listeners feed the choir.

Not deliberately—not in the way a farmer feeds stock, with buckets and schedule. But deliberately in the way that matters, in the way that keeps a family safe for generation after generation while other families' children are taken. Think about it, Odile, and you will see it, because you are a surveyor and you know how to read a pattern in a landscape. Five families. Three hundred years. The song chooses a singer in every generation—someone with the inheritance in their blood, someone whose name appears in the ledger. And in every generation, the one chosen is *found*. Guided. Warned. Taught the rules: learn the verse, never join the harmony, never sing alone. And the warnings work, don't they? Mostly? The chosen one masters the verse, sings it at the appointed hour in the appointed way, and the choir is fed its ending-for-now, and the seal holds, and the listeners' families sleep soundly.

But who does the song take when the chosen one succeeds?

Maren had watched Odile's face as she said it, and she watched it now in memory, in the chapel's cold light, and it was the face she had expected: the slow horror of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want to do. The vanished worker from the Meridian dig. The names in the old ledgers that were not listener names. The city above, which had given the choir three hundred years of voices and never once been told.

"The warnings are a leash," Maren said. "We teach the chosen one to sing the verse correctly because a correct verse is a *controlled* verse. The choir gets its completion, its taste of ending, its annual meal of resolution—and it stays sealed. And the ones it takes in between, the drifters and the diggers and the lost, the ones who wander too close to the doors—we do not warn them. We do not even see them. They are not in the ledger. They are what the ledger is *for*, Odile. It is a list of who is protected. Everyone not on it is a candidate."

"You let it take them." Elias's voice was very low. "Your families have been running a farm."

"We have been running a *fence*," Maren said, and heard how it sounded, and did not flinch, because she had decided long ago that she would not flinch, that flinching was the luxury of people who had not heard their sister singing in the pre-dawn hour for thirty years. "And I will tell you the worst of it, since we have come this far. When Ilse went down, my mother did not only grieve. She sat in this chapel that same night, in this pew, and she opened the ledger, and she wrote the date, and beside the date she wrote *Ilse Kolt, taken, verse attempted alone*—and then she wrote the entry that has been written after every taking in our family's history. *Balance restored. The seal holds.* Because that is the truth, Brandt. The choir's hunger is not infinite. It is *calibrated*. It takes what the verse does not satisfy, and no more, so long as the verse is sung right. My sister's failure bought the city four quiet years. Her voice, down there, holding that perfect note, is the reason the seal survived the tremors of '61 and the subsidence of '78. She is not only a victim. She is a *pillar*. And I have spent thirty years telling myself that this makes her suffering meaningful, because the alternative is to admit that we—five families, three hundred years, all our vigil and all our ledgers and all our closed-mouthed portraits—we are not the choir's wardens. We are its *kitchen*. We keep the recipe. We plate the meal. We teach the chosen one to cook it properly, and we let it eat whoever wanders in from the cold, and we call the arrangement an inheritance."

The pipe in the wall ticked. Odile had not moved. Elias had risen from the pew and stood with his back to the portraits, as if he could not bear to have them behind him.

"Then why tell us now?" Odile said at last. "Why confess this? What do you want from me?"

And here, Maren thought, is the stone I have been carrying up the hill for a hundred and eleven days.

"I want you to sing the verse *wrong*," she said. "Not imperfectly—wrong. Deliberately, catastrophically, in a way no Kolt or Vasseur or Brandt has ever dared, because every listener who understood what the verse truly was also understood that a wrong verse might unmake everything, including the pillars, including the seal, including the thing itself—and we were too much cowards to risk the thing itself coming loose. I told you to master it because that is what I was raised to say. I am un-saying it now. I am thirty years too late for Ilse, and I will not be too late for you. Learn the verse, Odile. Learn it better than my sister did. And then, when the hour comes—break it. Sing it like my father sang hymns. Sing it like a human being who never learned to carry a tune. Give the choir a command it cannot obey, an ending it cannot reach, a note it cannot tune itself to. Shatter the unison. Kill the song. Let my sister finally stop holding her note."

The silence that followed had weight. Odile was looking at her with an expression Maren had seen once before, on her mother's face, the night she finally admitted what the listeners were: the look of someone receiving a truth and immediately beginning to distrust the hands that passed it.

"You're lying about something," Odile said. "You've told us three truths and you're lying about one thing. I count, Maren. It's what I do."

Maren smiled, and was surprised to find that the smile hurt her face, because she realized she had not truly smiled since Antwerp.

"You're right," she said. "There is one more thing. And you will not believe it until you see it, so I will only say this much: the verse cannot be broken from above. It can only be broken from inside the choir loft, in front of the assembled voices, with the seal open behind you and no way back up. Which means that when you go down—and you *will* go down, Odile, the song has already chosen you and the choice is not negotiable—you will not go alone. You will go with the one person in Verrin whose family name is written on the door itself."

She looked at Elias Brandt, and saw him understand, and saw him hate understanding.

"The Brandts didn't just build the seal," she said. "Ask him what else his great-grandfather's letters say. Ask him what a doorwarden does when the door is open. And then ask yourselves both why the song waited three hundred years to choose a singer and a warden in the same winter—and why it chose two people who would fall in love."

She picked up her coat. At the chapel door she paused, her hand on wood that had been touched by five generations of hands, and said the last of it without turning around.

"I lost my sister because I looked away at the wrong moment. I am not looking away now. Whatever you decide—sing it right, sing it wrong, sing it not at all—I will be standing at the Delacroix door on the solstice, and this time I will not get on the train."

The door closed behind her. In the cold chapel, beneath five generations of closed mouths, Odile and Elias looked at each other, and neither of them said the thing they were both thinking: that Maren Kolt had told the truth about everything except her own heart, and that the song had been listening to every word, and that somewhere beneath their feet, patient as stone, something had just heard exactly the plan it had been waiting three hundred years to hear.
