## 3. What the Crew Unearthed

The machines arrived on a Tuesday, which Isandro thought was fitting—Tuesdays were the day she did her invoicing, the day most given over to arithmetic and small, survivable truths. She stood at the edge of the Verrone property with her clipboard against her chest like a shield and watched the excavator swing its arm in slow, patient arcs, peeling the earth back from the east lawn the way a hand peels a bandage from a wound it isn't sure it wants to see.

The crew was four men and a woman named Brannigan who did the talking. They were contracted through the county, not through Isandro's firm, but the appraisal required documentation of all site work affecting the structure's value, so here she was, documenting. That was the word she kept returning to in her head. *Documenting.* It sounded clean. It sounded like something that happened to paper.

"Ground's wrong," Brannigan said, spitting into the fresh trench. "Been saying it since we broke ground. Too soft out here, then too hard, like somebody packed it by hand."

"Somebody probably did," Isandro said, and meant it as a joke, but Brannigan looked at her with an expression that had no joke anywhere in its neighborhood.

The house watched them work. Isandro had stopped pretending otherwise around the fourth morning of her stay. Windows that should have reflected clouds instead held a kind of attention, and when the excavator's bucket bit down and shrieked against something that wasn't soil, every pane in the east face seemed to lean forward at once.

The shriek was wrong for stone. Stone screamed high and flinty. This sound came up through the ground low and resonant, more like a struck bell than a broken rock, and it went on far longer than impact could account for. The operator killed the engine. For a moment the only noise was the tick of cooling metal and, underneath it—or perhaps only underneath Isandro's own pulse—a faint vibration in the earth, rhythmic, patient, like something below rolling over in its sleep and settling again.

"Call it," Brannigan said to no one in particular, and climbed down into the trench.

Isandro followed because following was her job, and because standing at the rim felt, suddenly, like being the last person awake in a house where everyone else has heard the noise too and is waiting to see who goes first.

They dug by hand after that. The machine had cracked whatever it was, but the crew worked around the fracture with brushes and hand trowels now, the way archaeologists do, though nobody had called anyone and nobody had any business doing archaeology. By late afternoon they had cleared a rectangle roughly the size of a door—which was exactly what it was—and Isandro stood above it in the trench with dirt sifting down onto her boots, looking at the topmost carved line of letters, and felt the ground tilt under her in a way that had nothing to do with footing.

**MARCHETTI**

Her name. Not Verrone. Not the name of any family the county records connected to this property across two hundred years of deeds and deaths. *Marchetti*, cut deep into granite, the serifs sharp enough that the mason might have finished the work yesterday, though the lichen in the grooves had been growing since before her grandmother was born.

"That your people?" Brannigan asked. She'd seen the way Isandro had gone still.

"It's my name," Isandro said. Her voice came out level, which cost her something. "I don't know why it's here."

It was a lie, or half of one, or maybe just the truth arriving ahead of her understanding, the way weather arrives before the storm you can feel building behind it. Because some part of her—the part that had spent thirty-four years making herself unremarkable, forgettable, a woman whose name slid off people the way rain slides off glass—some part of her had known the moment the machines started digging that they would find this. Had known it the way you know a hymn's next note before you've heard it sung.

The door itself was sealed. That was the detail the crew kept circling back to, the one they'd tell their families about, the one that would make the story worth repeating: there were no hinges on the outside, no ring to pull, no seam wide enough for a crowbar. The stone fit the frame the way a lid fits a box, flush and total, and every mark of tooling—the chisel scores, the smoothing strokes—ran on the *inside* face, visible only where the excavator's blow had split a corner away.

"Sealed from the inside," Brannigan said, running her thumb along the fracture. "Somebody shut this door and then somebody else—no." She stopped. Frowned. Started over. "Somebody shut themselves in."

"Or was shut in," said the youngest crew member, a kid named Pell whose humor until then had been relentless and terrible. Nobody laughed. The trench was cold even though the afternoon wasn't, and the light coming down into it fell wrong, pooling around the door without touching it, as if the stone drank what landed on it.

Isandro photographed everything. Her hands were steady. She noted dimensions, depth, orientation—the door faced north, toward the house, toward the eleven doors inside it—and her handwriting in the margin of the survey sheet came out cramped and slanting, and she did not look closely at it, because some instinct told her that if she looked closely she would recognize it, and she was not ready to be recognized.

By dusk the county had been called and a tarp had been thrown over the find, weighted with sandbags, and the crew had gone home to their dinners and their ordinary lives, and Isandro had walked back across a lawn that seemed to spring slightly under her feet, greener here than anywhere else on the property, fed by something.

She did not go into the basement that night. She told herself it was because the appraisal didn't require it. She told herself a great many things, and lit every lamp she'd brought, and sat at the kitchen table with her paperwork spread out like a barricade, and waited for sleep the way you wait for a tide you can't argue with.

* * *

Sleep took her at the table, chin on her folded arms, pen still in her hand. And the house, which had been measuring her for days—she understood this later, understood it the way you understand the shape of a keyhole once the key is already turning—took the opportunity to open something in her that she had kept shut.

She dreamed of her grandmother's kitchen, except it wasn't her grandmother's kitchen; it was the Verrone kitchen, transposed, the same worn table and the same blue enamel kettle sitting in a room with higher ceilings and windows that looked out on a lawn being torn open by machinery. Her grandmother stood at the stove with her back turned, small and stooped, wearing the cardigan with the burnt sleeve Isandro remembered from childhood, from before, from the years when remembering had still been allowed to be gentle.

Her grandmother was humming.

That was the impossible part. Not that the dead appeared in dreams—dreams did that, dreams were cheap that way—but the tune itself. Isandro knew every song her grandmother had ever hummed. She had catalogued them without meaning to, the way she catalogued everything: the Neapolitan folk melody she used while kneading bread, the half-remembered radio standard from the forties, the lullaby she'd sung to Isandro's brother when his lungs were failing him, the same lullaby she'd sung to Isandro after, when the house was too quiet and silence had become a thing with teeth. Isandro knew them all the way you know the floor of your own home in the dark.

This was none of them.

It moved in a minor key, unhurried, each phrase descending a step further than comfort suggested, and yet it was warm—it was the warmest thing Isandro had ever heard, warmth like a hand on the back of the neck, like a coat laid over sleeping shoulders. It sounded the way being loved felt, if love could be given a melody. And beneath the warmth ran something older and colder, a second voice woven through the first, so low it was less heard than felt, humming the same notes a half-beat behind, the way an echo comes from somewhere deeper than the room.

Her grandmother turned from the stove. In the dream her face was clear—young, almost, unlined in a way Isandro had never seen it—and she looked at Isandro with an expression of such specific sorrow that Isandro woke with it still printed on the backs of her eyes.

"You always hummed that when you thought I couldn't hear," dream-her-grandmother said. "You'll need all the verses where you're going."

Then the kettle screamed, or the house did, or Isandro did, and she came upright at the kitchen table with the lamps burning and her heart going like a trapped bird.

Dawn was a gray suggestion at the windows. Her pen had moved in the night. Across the survey sheet, over her careful dimension lines, in handwriting that was hers and was not hers—slanted, cramped, pressed hard enough to emboss the pages beneath—someone had written the melody out. Staff lines ruled freehand, steady as a print shop's. Notes filled them, verse after verse, the ink brown-black like old blood, and beside the final line, where the melody descended past the range of anything human lungs would want to attempt, a single word:

*unfinished.*

Isandro sat very still. Outside, the tarp over the unearthed door had come loose at one corner despite the sandbags, and the morning wind lifted it and let it fall, lifted it and let it fall, in a rhythm almost like breathing. Almost like keeping time.

She looked down at the score again, at her grandmother's sorrow, at the word *unfinished*, and understood—with the calm, distant clarity of a woman reading someone else's appraisal—that inheritance does not wait to be invited. It arrives the way water arrives, finding every crack you didn't know you had, filling every room you thought was empty.

And somewhere below the lawn, below the sealed door, something that had been fed verses for two hundred years had noticed that the singing had stopped—

—and had begun, softly, patiently, to count the days.
