## 1. The Second Singing

The bus let Ada off where the road gave up and became a lane, and the lane gave up and became water.

Hollowmere sat at the bottom of what had once been a valley. The drowned fields lay on either side of the village like sheets of dark glass, and in them stood the upper halves of trees — oaks and ash that had been tall when the reservoir came, now reduced to bare gray fingers pointing out of the flood, marking graves of orchards and hedgerows and one farm gate that still swung, very slowly, with no wind to move it. The water was so still it doubled everything: church tower, chimney pots, the low sun. Two villages, Ada thought. One above, one below, holding hands.

They had been warned about the light here — how it went amber early and stayed amber, as if the whole valley were lit from beneath rather than above — but no one had mentioned the quiet. Not silence; Hollowmere wasn't silent. There were rooks in the tower, a dog somewhere behind the stone cottages, the tick and knock of cooling engines from a tractor parked by the green. But under all of it ran a hush like held breath, the acoustic signature of a place listening to itself.

Ada shifted the field recorder bag higher on their shoulder and walked down into the village.

*Choir and Room.* That was the working title of the project, three years old now, funded by a grant that required "documentation of endangered vernacular song traditions." Ada had collected ballads in the Hebrides, lullabies in the Carpathians, work songs from men who'd dredged harbors their grandfathers had built. They knew how this went. You arrived, you bought a round, you complimented someone's garden, and eventually — usually on the third evening, after trust had been built brick by patient brick — someone sang. The songs always came with stories attached, and the stories were the real harvest.

Hollowmere had been recommended by an archivist in the county records office, a woman with ink-stained fingers who had pulled up short when Ada asked why the village interested her particularly.

"They sing differently there," she'd said. And then, when Ada waited for more: "Or they used to."

The Green Man was the only pub, squat and slate-roofed at the head of the lane, its sign showing a man made of leaves with an expression Ada could only call apologetic. Inside: low beams, a fire already lit against the autumn damp, six locals who turned as one to look at the door and then, just as one, returned to their drinks.

"Evening," said the landlord, a broad man with forearms like ham hocks. "You'll be the folklorist, then. Room's ready."

Word traveled fast in a village of two hundred. Ada ordered a pint of the local bitter and asked, lightly, whether anyone might know the old harvest hymns — the ones sung before the valley flooded, when Hollowmere had been two parishes and the wheat grew down to the river.

The landlord's rag stopped moving across the bar for exactly one second. Then it resumed.

"You'd want Maren Pryce," he said. "Oldest voice in the village. She remembers what the rest of us only heard about." He wrote nothing down, but Ada understood the audience was over. "She's the cottage with the blue door, past the chapel. Mind, she doesn't get many visitors."

There was something in the way he said *visitors* — not unfriendly, but careful, the way you'd say *matches* near a gas leak.

* * *

Maren Pryce opened her blue door before Ada finished knocking, which meant she'd seen them coming down the lane. She was small and straight-backed, mid-eighties perhaps, with white hair braided into a crown and eyes the pale gray of the flooded fields. She looked at Ada for a long moment — at the recorder bag, specifically — and something moved through her face like weather.

"You're the one collecting songs," she said. Not a question.

"If you're willing," Ada said. "Only if you're willing."

Maren stepped back from the door. "Come in, then. It's rude to make a guest stand in the cold, even a stranger." The word *stranger* carried no warmth, but no hostility either. Just classification.

The cottage smelled of woodsmoke and dried lavender and, underneath, faintly, of pond water — that green, mineral smell Ada would come to think of as the smell of Hollowmere itself. A fire burned in the front room. On every wall hung framed photographs, dozens of them, and Ada's eye snagged on them immediately: christenings, weddings, harvest suppers, school groups. Village photographs. And in nearly every one, at the edge or the background or reflected in a window, stood the church tower.

"The bell tower," Ada said, nodding at a 1950s fete photo. "It's beautiful. Is the bell original?"

Something happened to the room. It was nothing Ada could have sworn to in a court of law — Maren didn't move, the fire didn't gutter — but the air changed pressure, the way it does before thunder.

"Sit down," Maren said. "I'll make tea, and I'll sing you the harvest hymn, because my mother sang it and her mother before her, and it ought to be written down somewhere before I go. And then you'll be on your way."

It was not the most gracious invitation Ada had ever received. It was also, they suspected, the only one on offer.

The tea came in cups older than Ada's parents. Maren sat in the chair opposite, folded her small hands, and looked into the fire for a moment as if consulting someone there. Then she sang.

Her voice was thin and dry as winter grass, but true — pitch-perfect in the way of people who learned music before electricity, when the only reference was the human throat. The melody was modal, minor-leaning, with a rising phrase on the third line that caught in the chest like a stair missed in the dark. The words were English but old-flavored:

*"Down in the valley the wheat stands gold,*
*the river runs silver, the bell rings bold,*
*and all that we gather, and all that we grow,*
*we carry to churchyard, we carry below—"*

Below. The word dropped through the verse like a stone through still water, and the melody bent around it, minor where you expected major, and rose again. Ada felt the hair lift on their forearms. Three years of fieldwork had taught them to hear the difference between a song that had been sung ten thousand times and worn smooth as a pebble, and a song that had been sung carefully, guarded, each performance a controlled release. This was the latter. This was a song kept in a box.

Ada let the recorder run and did not move until the last note faded. Then, softly: "That's extraordinary. The descending figure on 'below' — is that traditional, or—"

"It's the hymn," Maren said flatly.

"Could you sing it once more? Just the first verse? For safety — the recording sometimes—"

Maren Pryce went white.

Not flushed, not embarrassed — *white*, the blood leaving her face in visible stages, lips first, then cheeks, until she looked like the photographs on her own walls. Her hands, which had been folded, gripped the arms of her chair, and Ada saw that her knuckles had gone the color of candle wax.

"No," she said.

"I'm sorry, I only meant—"

"I said *no*." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it — it stayed level, conversational, while her eyes fixed on some point slightly to the left of Ada's head, as though something had entered the room and stood between them. "Once is giving. Twice is asking. You don't ask twice. Not here."

"Maren, I don't understand—"

"You don't need to understand. You need to leave." She unfolded herself from the chair with surprising speed, crossed to the door, and opened it onto the amber dusk. The flooded fields beyond her garden wall had gone the color of brass. "Thank you for the visit. Mind the lane, it floods after dark."

"Maren—"

"*Mind the lane.*"

The door closed. Not slammed — closed, with a click so precise and final it was worse than slamming, the sound of a lock engaging somewhere deeper than the latch. Ada stood on the path a moment, recorder still running in their bag, capturing forty seconds of nothing but wind off the water and, distantly, a dog barking twice and stopping as if corrected.

Then Ada noticed the curtains.

Along the lane, in the windows of the neighboring cottages, curtains were moving. Not opening — settling. The particular slow sway of fabric recently released. Every house Ada could see had been watching Maren's door, and every house had decided, in the same instant, to stop.

* * *

Back at the Green Man, the fire had been built up and the same six locals occupied the same seats. Ada bought a second pint and tried a different angle, casual, sideways.

"Lovely old lady, Mrs. Pryce. She sang me a harvest hymn — beautiful thing. I wondered if any of the men remembered the verses? Sometimes the parts differ—"

The nearest local, a weathered man mending a fishing net that Ada suspected was purely decorative, set down his twine.

"We don't sing twice," he said.

"I'm sorry?"

"Whatever she told you. Whatever anyone tells you." He didn't look up from his net. His hands were steady; his voice was steady; everything except his eyes was steady, and his eyes had gone to the window, to the darkening water. "We don't sing anything twice. Once for keeping, once for God, and never the third time. That's just how it is here."

"That's not how anywhere else is," Ada said, gently. "I've collected songs in forty villages. Repetition is how they survive. Teaching, learning — it's all singing things again."

The man wound twine around his fingers, once, twice, three times, and cut it.

"Then you've been lucky," he said, "in your villages."

The landlord called last orders at nine, which was absurdly early, and Ada climbed the narrow stairs to their room feeling the whole building lean in behind them. The bed was clean and cold. Through the window, the church tower stood black against the blacker hills, and below it the flooded valley held a smear of reflected light that Ada told themselves was the moon, though the moon hadn't risen yet.

They played back Maren's hymn on headphones, twice — listening, not singing, telling themselves firmly that listening was different — and transcribed the melody by lamplight. The descending phrase on *below*. The stair-missed-in-the-dark rise. In the margin of the notebook, without quite deciding to, Ada wrote: *Why does a village rehearse a refusal?*

Because that was what they'd seen tonight. Not superstition, not eccentricity. *Rehearsal.* Six locals with the same answer shaped in their mouths. A landlord with a pause measured to the half-second. An old woman whose terror had the smoothness of long practice — fear worn like a stone worn by water, by years, by decades. You did not flinch that precisely at a question unless you had flinched at it a thousand times before, in unison, together.

Hollowmere was courteous. Hollowmere was watchful. Hollowmere was a choir that had agreed, note by note, on the one song it would never sing.

Ada switched off the lamp. In the dark, the water smell crept through the window frame, green and mineral and patient, and far out across the flooded fields — so faint it might have been a rope creaking against a mooring, might have been a branch, might have been nothing — something rang.

One clear bronze note.

Ada lay still, heart tapping at their ribs, waiting for a second strike. Wind chimes ring in threes. Bells ring in changes. Everything that rings, rings again.

The note did not come again. But the silence afterward was not empty. It was the silence of a room after someone has spoken and is waiting to hear whether you will answer.

Ada did not sleep for a long time, and when they finally did, they dreamed of a melody rising out of deep water, and of six small voices singing a verse they had never heard, and somehow, impossibly, already knew.
