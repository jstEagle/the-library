## 2. What the Water Keeps

The parish archive of Hollowmere lived, as everything in Hollowmere seemed to live, below the waterline.

Ada found it on their second morning, after a night spent listening to the house settle and the lake press its flat grey shoulder against the valley walls. The village had no library to speak of — only a reading room attached to the church hall, and beneath that, down a flight of steps that ended in a smell of cold stone and standing water, the archive itself. The caretaker had left the door unlocked. Ada would come to understand that this was not carelessness. In Hollowmere, doors were unlocked the way mouths were kept shut: as a matter of policy.

Inside, the air was thick enough to chew. Dehumidifiers hummed in two corners like patient insects, fighting a war they had clearly been losing for decades. Metal shelving rose in ranks through the gloom, and along the floor, where the flood of — when was it? 1968? 1952? — had reached its high tide, every box wore a tidemark of pale silt, like the archive had been dipped in milk and left to dry. The water had taken the bottoms of things. The bottoms of the boxes, the bottoms of the pages, the bottoms of names. Whatever the water kept, it kept it by taking.

Ada set up their recorder out of habit, then switched it off again. This was not fieldwork yet. This was something older and stranger: research.

They were looking for music, but they had learned — from Maren's slammed door, from the way the greengrocer had smiled and changed the subject, from the postmistress who said *we don't really sing much here, love* while her eyes flicked to the church tower — that in Hollowmere you did not ask about songs directly. You asked about other things and let the songs surface on their own, like drowned bodies.

So Ada asked about the bell.

* * *

It took them most of the morning to find the first document, and it was almost disappointingly ordinary. A parish council minute book, 1911, the entries in a copperplate hand gone brown as tea:

*August 14th. Resolved that the church bell, being cracked past mending, be sold to Messrs. Whitcombe & Sons of Birmingham, there to be broken up for scrap. The sum of £9 received. Same to be applied toward the purchase of a new bell, funds permitting.*

*September 2nd. Funds do not permit. Matter deferred.*

And then, three years later, in a different, wearier hand:

*Matter closed.*

That was all. The bell of Hollowmere had been melted down before the Great War. Nine pounds. Broken up for scrap in Birmingham, a city Ada had passed through twice without stopping. There was no bell. There had been no bell for over a hundred years.

Ada sat back on the little wooden chair and let that settle. It explained things, didn't it? The villagers' oddness about singing — some old superstition, some folk-memory tangled around a bell that no longer existed. Grief without an object. That happened in drowned places; Ada had seen it in the valleys of Wales, in the coastal towns eaten by the sea. People kept mourning shapes long after the thing itself was gone.

Except.

Except that yesterday evening, walking back from the shop, Ada had heard the church tower creak. Not unusual — old towers creaked, settling against the wind. But this creak had been rhythmic, pendular, a slow heavy swing, and Ada had stood in the lane with their shopping going warm in their bag and listened to the sound of something enormous moving inside a tower that contained, according to the parish council of 1911, nothing at all.

Ada went back to the shelves.

* * *

The photographs began in the 1890s and ran, with gaps, to the present decade. Ada laid them out on the reading table in chronological order, weighted at the corners with brass paperweights green with age, and worked through them the way they'd work through any evidence — patiently, without deciding in advance what they wanted to find.

The earliest ones showed the tower bare-crowned. A Victorian photograph, all sepia and stiff sky: the church of St. Brannoc-at-the-Water, squat and dark, its tower ending in a plain parapet. No bell. A second image from 1908, same. Then a gap — the war, presumably, or simply no one with a camera — and then:

1934. A wedding party on the church steps, faces blurred with movement, the bride mid-laugh. And above them, framed in the tower's louvred opening, unmistakable even at this size, even after ninety years of silvering: a bell. Dark-mouthed, heavy-shouldered, hanging exactly where a bell should hang.

Ada checked the date. Checked the minute book. Checked the photograph again.

Melted in 1911. Photographed in 1934.

They told themselves it was a replacement. But the minute book said funds never permitted. And when Ada found the next photograph — 1951, a confirmation class lined on the grass, forty solemn children — the bell hung above them at precisely the same angle, the same dark mouth, the same impossible weight of shadow. And the next. 1968, the year of the flood: the churchyard half underwater, coffins — Ada looked away from that one, then made themselves look back — coffins bobbing gently among the headstones like moored boats, and the bell above it all, dry, serene, untouched by any waterline.

Every photograph since 1911 showed the bell. Every document insisted there was no bell.

The evidence contradicted itself politely, the way the villagers did. Nothing raised its voice. Nothing admitted anything. The archive simply offered both truths side by side and waited, with the courtesy of a locked door, for Ada to choose which lie they could live inside.

Ada's hands were steady. They noticed that their hands were steady, and noted it the way you note a symptom.

* * *

"You'll want to put those back in order."

The voice came from the stairwell, and Ada turned too fast, knocking a paperweight to the floor. The man coming down the steps was perhaps sixty, tall and stooped, dressed in the uniform of rural caretakers everywhere — waxed jacket, corduroy, boots that had known every path in the parish. He moved carefully, as if the stairs might object to him. His face was long and weathered and arranged into an expression of mild apology, but his eyes did not participate. They were pale grey and entirely still, like the surface of the lake on a windless day.

"Idris," he said. "I keep the church. And the archive, such as it is."

"Ada Okonkwo. I'm—"

"I know who you are." He came to the table and looked down at the spread of photographs without touching them. "You're the one collecting songs."

"That's right. Folk hymns, harvest songs, anything—"

"There's nothing here worth collecting." He said it gently, the way you tell someone the road is flooded. "I'd have saved you the trip if I could."

"The harvest hymn I recorded from Maren Voss yesterday—"

Idris's still eyes moved to Ada's face for the first time, and Ada felt the look like a change in air pressure.

"She sang for you." Not a question.

"Once. She wouldn't sing it again."

"No," Idris said softly. "She wouldn't." He began gathering the photographs, squaring their edges with small precise movements, and Ada watched the evidence disappear back into its box. "You've been reading about the bell."

"I've been reading that there isn't one."

"Mm." He slid the box onto the shelf, aligned it with its neighbours. For a moment he stood with his back to Ada, and the dehumidifiers hummed, and somewhere above them a pipe knocked once, twice, like a polite request. "There's a version of this conversation where I tell you the photographs are mislabelled," he said. "Where I say the 1911 record is wrong, or incomplete, or that the scrap dealer reneged. People like tidy explanations. You seem like someone who likes tidy explanations."

"And the other version?"

He turned. Up close, Ada could see how tired he was — not the tiredness of a day, but of decades, worn into him like the silt-marks on the boxes. Tired the way a lighthouse keeper is tired.

"The other version is that you should pack your recorder, catch the morning bus, and write your book about somewhere else." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, and it frightened Ada more than anger would have. "Because whatever you think you're doing here — preserving, recording, keeping — the water keeps things too. It keeps them very well. And it has been waiting a long time for someone curious enough to come looking."

"That sounds like a threat."

"It's a warning. Threats are what we use on each other." He climbed two steps, paused, and without turning back said: "One thing, since you'll ignore everything else. If you hear the bell — and you will, everyone does eventually — don't go to it. Don't acknowledge it. The village won't, and you'll think us cowards or liars, and maybe we are. But acknowledging it is answering it. And nothing good has ever answered that bell."

"What happens if you answer it?"

Idris stood a moment longer in the stairwell's dim light, and Ada saw him weigh the truth and find it too heavy to carry up the stairs.

"It's not what happens to you," he said. "It's what happens to the night."

Then he was gone, his footsteps fading up into the ordinary world, leaving Ada alone with the humming machines and the tidemarks and the box of photographs that showed, year after patient year, a bell that had been melted down before the Great War.

Ada sat for a long time in the cold. Outside, the light was beginning to fail, the lake going the colour of pewter, the tower rising black against it — and in the louvred opening, just visible, just barely, a shape that might have been shadow and might have been weight and might have been waiting.

Ada packed up their notes slowly. They did not believe. They told themselves they did not believe, twice, three times, the way you repeat a hymn until it takes hold.

At dusk, across the whole length of the drowned valley, the bell rang.
