## 5. The Hymn Without a Name

The summer of 1974 came into Hollowmere the way honey comes off a spoon — slow, golden, and impossible to hold. Maren was nineteen that year, and she remembered it afterward the way you remember a dream you woke from too late: in pieces, in light, in the sound of her sister's voice climbing the church steps with six children strung out behind her like beads on a thread.

Her sister's name was Eirlys.

That was the first thing the water took. Not her body — the valley had drowned long before either of them was born, and Hollowmere had learned to bury its dead above the floodline, in the thin churchyard where the grass grew pale and stubborn. What the water took was the name itself. Say *Eirlys* now and it came back to you sounding like someone else's word, worn smooth as a pebble, belonging to no one. Maren had spent fifty years saying it to herself in the dark, testing it, trying to keep its edges sharp, and every year it dulled a little more. By the time the folk-song woman came asking questions, Maren could no longer be sure she hadn't invented her sister entirely — some imaginary companion built out of grief and guilt, given a face because a face was needed.

But inside the loop, inside this night that never ended, Eirlys was solid as bread and twice as warm.

Maren stood at the vestry window with a stack of hymnals in her arms she had no reason to be carrying, watching through the warped glass as her sister arranged the children in the front pews. It was late afternoon, the light coming down the valley long and amber, laying itself across the water so the whole drowned village below seemed lit from underneath. The six of them sat in two rows of three, smallest in front: the Prosser twins with their matching cowlicks, solemn little Gwendolen Price who always wore her coat indoors, the Hollis boy whose real name nobody used because he'd been christened after his grandfather and hated it, and at the end, feet not reaching the floor, Idris's father — no, not yet, not then; just a boy called Owen, swinging his legs, already the sexton's son, already learning which keys opened which doors.

"Again from the top," Eirlys said, and her voice carried through the stone the way voices did in Hollowmere, softened by damp, made round by echo. "And this time, don't push it. Let it come up out of you like breath on a cold morning."

They sang.

Maren's arms went slack. The hymnals slid against each other with a papery whisper, and she didn't notice, because the song was rising through the church the way heat rises off a road in August — visible almost, if you tilted your head, a shimmer in the air between the pews.

It had no name. That was the whole point of it. Eirlys had written it herself, over one whole winter, sitting at the kitchen table with the lamp turned low and her pencil moving like something possessed — gently possessed, Maren had thought then, thinking of possession only as the church meant it, as enthusiasm, as the spirit moving through a willing hand. She'd asked her sister once what they should call it, and Eirlys had laughed and said, *You don't name a thing like this. A name is a handle. You don't give anything a handle unless you want it picked up.*

Maren hadn't understood. Nineteen-year-olds understand very little, though they believe the opposite.

She understood now, across fifty years and one unending night: the song was a door, and doors don't need names, but they do need hinges, and the hinge of this one was kindness. That was what Eirlys had built into it, note by note, verse by verse — an invitation written in the only language the old drowned things beneath the water still understood. Not Latin. Not scripture. Kindness. The melody bent downward where hymns bent upward; it asked instead of commanded; it left space in every line, gaps the way you leave a gate unlatched, for something to come through if it wanted. And what it wanted to call was not a devil. Maren knew that with the certainty of someone who has watched a thing work and seen what it gathered. The children were not afraid. That was the detail the village had gotten wrong for half a century, the lie that had calcified into truth: the children had not been lured, or tricked, or taken. They had sung a welcome, and something kind had begun, very slowly, very carefully, to answer.

She could hear it even now, under their small voices — not a second voice, nothing so crude, but a richness, a depth, as though the church itself had decided to hum along. The bell rope hung motionless in its corner. The melted bell — scrap iron since 1911, sold and carted away before anyone living was born — was silent in the tower, and yet the tower resonated, faintly, the way a struck tuning fork makes its neighbor sing.

"Beautiful," Eirlys said softly, when the last note settled. "You see? It isn't frightening at all. It's only lonely. Everything old is only lonely." She smiled at them, that smile of hers that made you feel chosen. "Next Sunday we'll try it all together with the congregation. Just once. A song should only ever be sung once, dears. After that it stops being yours."

Maren's arms tightened around the hymnals. *A song should only ever be sung once.* Her sister had said it lightly, a teacher's rule, a way to keep the children careful. She could not have known how literally the dark would take her. Or perhaps she had. Perhaps that winter at the kitchen table, Eirlys had understood exactly what she was building and built the fence into it — the rule, the single singing, the mercy of never repeating — and trusted the village to keep it.

The village had kept it for fifty years out of terror. Eirlys had meant it out of love. Between those two things lay everything that had gone wrong.

* * *

It happened three days later, on a Thursday, with rain coming down the valley in grey ropes and the water high against the sea wall, licking at the lowest steps of the churchyard as though counting them.

Maren had walked up through the village to bring her sister her forgotten shawl, and she was standing under the lych-gate arch wringing rain from her hair when she saw Mr. Prydderch come out of the church carrying the manuscript.

He moved quickly for an old man. He was the sexton — gravedigger, bell-ringer, keeper of keys — and he had opposed the song from the beginning, Maren knew that much; there had been words at the parish council, words behind the vestry door, his voice like gravel sliding: *We don't know what's under this valley. We don't knock.* But she had thought him merely stubborn, a man defending the silence of the old ways, and she was not prepared for what she saw now, which was not stubbornness but theft — quick, furtive, guilty, the manuscript folded wrong against its creases and shoved inside his coat as he glanced up and down the empty street with the eyes of a man doing something he would spend the rest of his life explaining to himself.

She stepped back into the shadow of the arch. Rain drummed on the slate above her head. She watched him go — not toward the vicarage, not toward his own cottage, but around the side of the church, toward the tower door, the small iron-studded door that only the sexton's ring would open.

She should have called out. She should have run after him, seized his arm, shouted for her sister, for the vicar, for anyone. Fifty years later, lying awake in her narrow bed while the bell rang its one clear note across the water, Maren replayed that moment ten thousand times and in ten thousand versions she stopped him. In this one — the true one, the one the loop preserved like a fly in amber — she told herself he was only putting it somewhere safe. Old men locked things away. It was probably fine. Eirlys would want it kept carefully anyway.

So she stood under the lych-gate and let him pass, and the rain came down, and the water rose a finger's width up the churchyard steps, and somewhere beneath the tower, in the dark, a man knelt on flagstones with a pry bar, lifting them one by one, and buried a song alive.

Because that was the thing Maren understood only afterward, only too late, only completely: you cannot bury a song. You can bury paper. The song goes on singing wherever it is, in the dark, muffled, alone — and a song written as an invitation, left unanswered, does not simply stop. It waits. It leans against whatever is nearest. And what was nearest, what the sexton in his fear and piety had chosen as the weight to pin it down, was the bell. The melted bell, the hollow place, the one object in Hollowmere shaped like a throat.

He had not silenced the calling. He had taught it hunger.

* * *

Sunday came bright and windless, and the children arrived early, scrubbed and excited, because today the whole congregation would hear the song, today it would be sung by everyone at once, one great single offering, and then it would be finished, given, done — a door opened once, generously, and closed again with thanks.

Eirlys reached for the music stand.

The stand was empty.

Maren watched her sister's face across the width of the church, watched understanding arrive in stages — confusion, then a search of the vestry, then the terrible stillness of a woman realizing that the only copy of the only thing she had ever made had gone somewhere she could not follow. Eirlys did not accuse anyone. That was the worst part, looking back. She simply stood at the front of the church with her hands folded, and said, quietly, to the room, to the children, to the listening dark beneath the floor: *We'll wait. It will come back. Songs always find their way home.*

The children waited too. They were good children. They sat in their pews with their hands in their laps, and the service happened around them, and the congregation sang the ordinary hymns, and outside the sun moved slowly across the water, and under the tower, under the flagstones, under the weight of a dead bell, the stolen song sang to itself in the dark and got no answer, and leaned harder against the walls of its prison, and began, very patiently, to pull.

At dusk the six children were still there. Their parents came for them and found the pews empty.

That was the version the village told. But Maren had been in the churchyard when they searched, and she had heard the sexton praying — not the prayers of a guilty man, though he was one, but the prayers of a terrified one, the flat rapid muttering of a man who has nailed a door shut and can hear something on the other side turning the handle, gently, without anger, over and over. He never confessed. He grew strange and quiet and died in 1961, and his grandson Idris took the keys, and the keys stayed in the family, and the family kept the secret the way you keep a wound clean — by never letting anyone look at it.

And the night did not end. That was the part no one outside ever grasped. For the children, for the song, for the thing the song had begun to call — it was still that Sunday. Still the empty music stand. Still the waiting. Every dusk the bell rang once, because a bell is a throat and even a melted one remembers how to speak, and every night the church filled with amber light and six small voices rose from the front pews singing a hymn that had no name, singing it perfectly, endlessly, waiting for the verse that would let them finish — and no one came, and no one answered, and the door stayed shut, and the water rose a finger's width up the churchyard steps, and fell, and rose.

Maren stood at the vestry window in the loop's eternal late afternoon, holding hymnals no one would ever need, and watched her sister smile at the children, and felt the guilt move through her the way the tide moves through the drowned streets below — total, patient, without malice, filling every room.

She had let him pass. She had said nothing. One sentence under the lych-gate, one shout in the rain, and none of it — none of it — would have happened.

Now, at last, after fifty years, footsteps were coming up the path. Someone new. Someone with a recorder and soft city shoes and questions, someone the loop had been waiting for the way the song had been waiting — patiently, hungrily, kindly.

Maren turned from the window, and her heart, young again in this endless evening, began to pound.

They were here. Whoever they were — they had finally come to finish the song.
