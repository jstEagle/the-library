## 10. Keeper of the Note

Morning came to Hollowmere the way it had not come in fifty years: plainly.

Maren Okonkwo stood at her kitchen window with a cup of tea going cold in her hands and watched the light lie down across the water like something tame. The valley was still drowned — that had never been the trouble, the water itself — but the light this morning did not pool strangely in the shallows, did not gather in the reeds as though listening. It simply fell. It touched the church tower on the rise above the floodline and slid down its flanks and found the grass, and nothing about it asked anything of anyone.

The bell hung silent in the tower.

She could see it from here, a dark shape in the arched opening, and for the first time since she was nine years old it looked like what the photographs had always claimed it was: a bell. Ordinary bronze. Ordinary shadow. It did not hang *waiting*. It hung the way tools hang in a barn between seasons, patient without intention.

Maren had slept, she realized. Actually slept, deep and dreamless, her body heavy as a filled sack, and woken to birdsong that was only birdsong. Her hands were steady around the cup. She kept looking down at them as if they belonged to someone more fortunate.

*Ada,* she thought, and the name caught in her chest like a fishbone, because it was not a name anymore. Ada had said so, there in the churchyard mud with the dawn coming up behind them and six children walking hand in hand through doors that opened onto morning — Ada had smiled with a face already going smooth and unmemoried, and had said, *Don't keep it. Give it away. I don't need mine where I'm going.*

And then the doors had closed, gently, the way a mother closes a door on a sleeping child, and when Maren turned around the churchyard held no one but herself and six small graves that had finally, after fifty years, grown names.

* * *

She walked up to the churchyard before the village was fully awake, because she wanted to see the stones alone first, before anyone else's grief got mixed into them.

They were new-cut, pale as peeled fruit. Six of them, in a row along the east wall where the light came first, each one bearing a name and a single date, because they had all gone into the ground on the same morning even if their bodies had never been found. Even now, Maren noticed, the earth beneath each stone lay undisturbed. Hollowmere had given the children markers, not coffins. It was the best the village could do, and it was late by half a century, and Maren knelt down in the wet grass anyway and put her palm flat against the first stone because some debts you pay in the only currency left to you, which is presence.

*BRIDGET FARROW.* Her sister's handwriting, or near enough. The mason must have worked from the manuscript page, from the signature at the bottom of the hymn Bridget had written to call something kind. Maren traced the letters and felt the morning wind move through the yew trees overhead, and under the wind, faint as breath on a window, she heard humming.

She knew the tune. Of course she knew the tune. She had heard it sung once, complete, in a looping church that existed nowhere on any map of any year, and she would know it if it were hummed underwater, through stone, from the far side of death.

It was coming from the tower.

* * *

The door at the base of the bell tower had always been stiff, and it was stiff now, and Maren let herself feel grateful for that — for one ordinary difficulty in a world that had spent two days teaching her that difficulties were the least of what doors could hold.

Inside, the stair wound upward through cold air that smelled of stone and pigeon and, underneath, very faintly, of pond water and old paper. The manuscript. They had buried it again — she and Idris together, an hour after sunrise, kneeling in the belfry floor's loose flags while the village watched from a respectful distance and pretended not to watch at all. But they had buried it *openly*, this time, with words spoken over it, with Bridget's name said aloud by everyone at once, once, together. A song sung twice by a whole village, freely given. If the thing below the floor wanted a second singing, it could have that one, honest and communal, and be satisfied.

Or so they hoped. So they prayed, those few who still prayed, which after last night was rather more than before.

Maren climbed. Her knees complained. She let them; complaint was a living thing's privilege.

The belfry opened around her, four arches full of grey-gold light, the great bell hanging in its yoke above the open trapdoor through which the morning came up cool from the valley. And standing at the rail, looking out over the water, was a figure made of the light itself — not solid, not quite, but present the way heat shimmer is present, the way a note hangs in a room after the string stops.

It turned toward her. It had Ada's outline. It had almost Ada's face, the way a reflection has your face — accurate and yet belonging elsewhere.

"Hallo, Maren," it said, and the voice was Ada's voice rung softly, struck gently, a voice with a bell inside it now. "You came up early."

"You're keeping it," Maren said. Not a question. She had known since the churchyard. "The second singing."

"Someone has to." The figure smiled, and the smile was kind, and it was also not entirely Ada's, the way a house you've moved into is not entirely yours no matter how you arrange the furniture. "That was always the shape of it. The song wants completing. Every time someone sings the first line and stops — every harvest hymn, every half-remembered verse — it reaches for the rest. For fifty years it reached, and there was nothing there, so it took. The children. Idris's grandfather's voice. Little pieces of everyone who ever hummed it and walked away." The figure looked out at the water. "But if the rest of the song is already being sung — continuously, willingly, here — then there's nothing for it to take. It can lean on me instead. I'm quite strong. I was a stubborn person. You remember."

"I remember," Maren said, and her throat closed. "I met you three days ago."

"Three days," the keeper agreed, amused. "And I'll remember you forever, which is more than most people get from anyone. Fair trade, I'd call it."

"It isn't fair. Nothing about it is fair."

"No," the keeper said softly. "But it's *finished*, Maren. Do you understand what that means? Fifty years of an unfinished sentence, and now it ends. Your sister wrote a song to call something kind, and it was kind, it only ever needed the whole of itself, and now it has the whole of itself, and the children are out." The figure gestured downward, toward the east wall, toward the six pale stones. "Go and look at their faces on the stones. The mason said he couldn't get them right. He got them right. He doesn't know how. *I* know how."

Maren went to the rail beside the figure. Below, the drowned valley lay silvered and calm, and somewhere under that water was the parish the bell had once called to worship, and somewhere beyond the water, in whatever morning the children had walked into, six small people were — she refused to think *gone*. They were *elsewhere*. Elsewhere was a place. Elsewhere had doors, and the doors had opened outward.

"I brought you something," Maren said.

She took it from her coat pocket: a small tin, rusted at the seams, that had sat in her pantry for fifty years holding buttons. Inside, wrapped in waxed paper gone translucent with age, was a lock of hair, fine and brown, tied with ribbon faded to the color of weak tea. Bridget's. Cut the summer before she died, before the sexton stole the manuscript and buried the kindness under the bell and taught the song to starve.

"I couldn't put it on the grave," Maren said. "There's no grave. There's just the stone. And I thought—" Her voice failed and restarted. "I thought you might keep it. Where you are. Since you're keeping things now."

The keeper held out both hands, and they were nearly solid in the morning light, nearly warm-looking, and Maren set the little tin in them and watched the fingers close around it carefully, the way you hold a bird.

"Oh," the keeper said, and the bell-voice cracked, and for one moment it was entirely Ada Okonkwo again, young and far from home and holding a dead woman's hair. "Oh, Maren. Thank you. Thank you."

"She'd have liked you," Maren managed. "My sister. Stubborn. Wrote songs for things that didn't exist yet."

"So do you," said the keeper. "You sang with me. Last night. In the loop. Did you think I didn't notice? You harmonized on a hymn you'd only heard once, fifty years ago, as a child, and you were note-perfect." The smile returned, gentler. "It runs in the family. The calling-things-kind. You're the last of it, now. That's why I need to ask you something, and you're going to want to say no."

"Then don't ask."

"I'm asking anyway." The keeper turned fully toward her, and the light of it steadied, gathered, became almost unbearably clear, the way the bell's single note had once been clear across the whole valley. "Someone will come. Someday. A collector, a folklorist, a curious child with a phone. Someone will hear the story — the true story, the one the village finally tells now — and someone will come to the tower and want to sing the hymn. Just the first line. Just to try it. And when they do, I will reach for them, because that is what I am now, that reaching is my whole job, and I will need someone on the other side of the door to tell them *no*. To tell them the second singing is kept. To stand where you stood last night and give permission instead of fear." The keeper's eyes — Ada's eyes, brown and tired and resolute — held hers. "Be my Maren. Be the one who visits. I can't come down anymore. But you can come up."

Maren stood a long time in the cold ringing quiet of the belfry, and she thought of fifty years of slammed doors, of a village rehearsing its silence, of a sister's song starving under a floor, of six names cut late into stone. She thought of how mourning, done alone, curdles. Done in company, it ferments into something you can live on.

"I'll come Sundays," she said at last. "After service. I'll bring tea. I expect you can't drink it, but I'll bring it, and we'll pretend, and it will be good for both of us to pretend."

"That," said the keeper of the note, "is the kindest hymn anyone ever wrote for me."

Maren laughed — a wet, broken, real laugh, the first in fifty years that cost her nothing — and turned to go down the stair, back into the plain morning, back into the long work of teaching a frightened village to sing whole songs again, once, together, freely.

At the bottom of the tower she paused, hand on the stiff door, and listened once more to the humming overhead: gentle, endless, complete.

And beneath it, so faint she would spend the rest of her life unsure whether she had truly heard it, a new sound — a seventh voice, small and clear, joining the line.

The keeper, it seemed, was not keeping the song alone anymore.

Something else had come to learn it.
