## 10. Duty Calls Collect

The email arrived at 6:47 on a Thursday morning, which meant it had been composed the night before by someone who wanted her to feel the weight of a full night's deliberation behind it.

*RE: Pemberton Estate — Final Valuation Overdue.*

Coral read it standing in the kitchen of the house that was not yet hers to value and not hers to leave, holding her phone in one hand and a mug of tea she would not drink in the other. The message was three paragraphs of corporate courtesy wrapped around an iron core: the firm's client — a development consortium with a name like a law firm married a bank — required the completed appraisal within seventy-two hours. Her provisional figures had already been circulated. All that remained was her signature on the structural assessment, the certification of condition, the formal declaration that 14 Marrow Lane was what the paperwork said it was: a property. An asset. A thing with walls and a roof and nothing else.

*We appreciate that family estates can present... complications,* the email said, in the tone of a man patting a dog he doesn't trust. *However, you were engaged precisely because of your reputation for thoroughness without sentimentality. The partners have every confidence in you.*

She set the phone face-down on the counter. Above her head, through the ceiling, the hallway waited — the one room the boiler could not warm, where her breath still fogged in July, where the cold lay along the floorboards like something sleeping on its side.

*Thoroughness without sentimentality.* She almost laughed. She had built a career on that phrase, or rather the phrase had built her, printed on conference badges and quoted back at her in trade journals: *Pemberton debunks haunted listings*, they wrote, as if debunking were a service like damp-proofing. Ten years of walking into houses that made people nervous and walking out with numbers. Ten years of being the person who measured the dark and found it exactly as long as it looked.

The kettle clicked off. She hadn't remembered switching it on.

* * *

She worked at the dining table because there was nowhere else, and because some stubborn professional instinct told her that if she stopped using the room as a room, it would stop being one. Twelve chairs. She counted them again, the way she counted them every morning now, a small liturgy of sanity. Twelve ladder-back chairs around a long oak table, their seats worn pale in the middle by generations of sitting. In the photographs — all of them, the estate agent's shots from 2011, Edith's own yellowed snapshots from decades before, the pictures Coral herself had taken on day one — thirteen.

She had stopped photographing the dining room. That was the compromise she'd reached with herself, the little treaty signed in the border country between skeptic and believer: she would not feed it evidence, and in exchange she would not look directly at what the evidence said. The chair in the images had been moving closer to the camera, shot by shot, a sequence with the grammar of approach. She knew what came next in that grammar. Everything approaches until it arrives.

Her laptop opened onto the appraisal template. Section 4: Structural Integrity. The cursor blinked in the comment field with the patience of a held breath.

She began, because beginning is what professionals do when they cannot proceed. *Load-bearing walls appear sound. Roofline true. No evidence of subsidence.* All of it true, insultingly true — the house was built better than anything constructed in the last century, its bones laid down by men who expected their work to outlive them, and perhaps it had been expected to outlive more than them too. She typed measurements. She typed dates. She described the cellar access and then deleted the description of the cellar access, because the cellar access led down to a false floor, and beneath the false floor was a mouth, and *no evidence of subsidence* was the funniest sentence she had ever written in her life.

Her phone buzzed. Dev, from the regional office, a junior surveyor she'd mentored two years ago, earnest as a spaniel. *Heard you've got the Pemberton job. My nan used to say never take work past Alder Bridge. Why's that?*

She stared at the message for a long time. Then she typed: *Local superstition. Nothing in it.* And felt the lie go into the world wearing her name.

* * *

At eleven she called Marcus, her supervisor, because avoidance had a half-life and hers was nearly spent.

"Coral." His voice did the thing it always did, the warmth laid over calculation like icing over a brick. "Good. Listen, I'll be straight with you, because I know you hate the dance. The consortium wants this closed by Friday. There's a clause in their financing — the whole acquisition package collapses if the valuation slips past the quarter. You understand what that means."

"It means they need a number."

"They need *your* number. Which is why we sent you. Look—" papers shuffled somewhere in London, a sound like dry leaves, like small bones. "You've been on-site what, three weeks? That's generous for a residential. Nobody will question your figures. And frankly, after the Halloway case, the partners are keen to see the Pemberton name attached to something clean."

"The Halloway case," Coral repeated. The house she'd certified sound that later ate a young couple's savings when the subsidence revealed itself — the one blemish, the one time her measurements had been wrong, or rather right about everything except the thing that mattered. "That wasn't my error."

"No," Marcus agreed smoothly. "It was the soil report's error. But you see how these things attach. A clean sign-off here, and the narrative resets. *Pemberton restores confidence in Marrow Lane property.* That's a good headline. That's a career headline."

She walked as he talked, phone against her ear, pacing the length of the hall because her body needed to move even while her mind stood very still. Her breath fogged. Of course it did. July outside; January in the corridor; the house keeping its own seasons the way it kept its own count.

"Marcus," she said. "What does the firm do if I file the report with reservations? Full disclosure. Condition noted as anomalous, pending further investigation."

The silence on the line lasted four seconds, which in Marcus-time was a fiscal quarter.

"Then the deal dies," he said, "and the client sues us for the financing costs, and the partners ask why their most reliable surveyor went strange on a routine job, and someone has to answer for that. Coral. It's a house. Old houses creak. You've said so yourself, a hundred times, at a hundred dinners. *Houses are just physics with dust on.*"

*Houses are just physics with dust on.* Her own words, returned to her like a cheque bouncing. She looked up the stairwell, into the dimness above, where the attic door stood shut over the reels of tape, over Edith's voice singing alone in 1974 until a second voice joined uninvited.

"I'll have it done by Friday," she heard herself say.

"There she is," Marcus said, satisfied, and hung up, and Coral stood in the cold hall holding a dead phone and understanding, with terrible clarity, that she had just lied to both of them — to him, about her intentions, and to herself, about everything else.

* * *

She drafted the report that afternoon. That was the horror of it, the part she would turn over in the small hours afterward: how easily it came. Twenty years of training funnelled through her fingers, section after section assembling itself in the approved register, the language of assurance, the grammar of *no cause for concern*. She knew exactly how a clean report sounded because she had written dozens, and the house gave her no trouble at all — cooperated, even, sitting quiet and measurable under her instruments, its walls plumb, its floors level, behaving like a building with the diligence of something that understood what was being decided upstairs.

*Section 7: Environmental Observations. Damp: none significant. Ventilation: adequate. Thermal anomalies:* —

Her cursor stopped. Thermal anomalies. The hallway. The rooms that gained six inches between the morning measurement and the afternoon one, so that her tape measure seemed to disagree with itself, so that the house was larger or smaller depending on when you asked it. She had the figures in her field book, columns of them, honest numbers that refused to reconcile. Any competent reviewer would spot the discrepancy. Any competent reviewer would ask questions. Unless the field notes simply didn't make it into the appendix.

She sat with her hands flat on the table, on either side of the laptop, twelve chairs around her, and examined the shape of the thing she was building. Not a lie, exactly. Lies were active; this was curatorial. A selection. An omission of the impossible in favour of the sellable. Every surveyor alive had done it — smoothed out the anomaly, rounded off the number that would frighten the buyer, filed the weirdness under *inconclusive* and moved on. The profession ran on the collective agreement that houses should be houses. The institution didn't need her truth. The institution needed her signature, and her signature was worth more than her soul on the open market, and everyone involved — Marcus, the consortium, the whole grey apparatus of valuation and certification — would sleep perfectly well tonight inside the fiction she produced for them.

That was the corruption, she thought. Not a bribe offered in a car park. Just this: the gentle, structural certainty that the lie was the professional act and the truth was the malfunction.

She saved the draft. *PEMBERTON_ESTATE_FINAL_v3.docx.* The filename sat there on her screen like a loaded thing.

* * *

Evening came down over the garden, gold going to grey, and she took her tea out to the back step because the dining room had begun to feel like a waiting room. From here she could see the slight depression in the lawn, the place the old maps said the well had been — filled in 1893, according to the parish record, though *filled* was doing charitable work in that sentence. The grass grew differently there. Thicker. Greener. Fed by something.

Aldous hadn't answered his door in two days. She'd walked to the cottage Wednesday evening and again this morning, and stood in his narrow front garden among the stonecrop, and known — the way you know things now, in this village, in this new register of knowing — that he was home. Curtains moved. A shadow crossed the parlour window with the deliberate stillness of a man deciding not to exist. He'd pressed the hymnal into her hands and given her his one warning — *eleven verses, never ask for twelve* — and whatever he'd seen in her face since, whatever he'd heard in her voice when she'd told him about the tapes, about verse ten rising up note-perfect from below, had been enough to send him behind his own door to wait out whatever was coming. Cowardice, she'd thought at first. Now, sitting on the cold step of a house that breathed, she suspected it was something sadder: he had spent fifty years unable to walk through that gate, and he was spending the distance he had left carefully.

Her phone buzzed. Dev again. *Any chance I could come shadow you next week? Boss says I need site hours on heritage properties. Also my nan's thing is bugging me. What's past Alder Bridge?*

She typed *I'll let you know* and put the phone away, and hated how much that sounded like a promise, and hated more the small part of her that was relieved — relief at the thought of another body on site, a witness, a colleague, someone from the world of tape measures and timesheets who might look at the thirteenth chair and see nothing at all, and thereby prove it was all in her head. She caught herself praying, almost, for the ordinary blindness of other people. That was what the house did to you. It made you want company the way drowning makes you want company.

* * *

Night. She opened the draft one last time, meaning only to reread it, and found her cursor in Section 9, *Summary of Findings*, where the template asked for a concluding statement in plain language suitable for the client.

*In summary, 14 Marrow Lane presents as a structurally sound late-Victorian residence in good repair, offering significant development potential. No conditions were observed which would materially affect value.*

No conditions were observed.

She thought about the word *observed*. About observation as a professional act — the calibrated eye, the instrument, the trained refusal to see what isn't there and, crucially, the trained willingness not to see what is. She had observed this house for three weeks. She had observed a chair that existed only in photographs and approached the camera like a patient animal. She had observed a hymn answered from underground, a hallway with winter in it, her grandmother's posture taking shape in blurred images day by day, less blurred, closer, *arriving*. None of that was unobserved. All of it was unreportable. The gap between those two words was where her career lived, and the house knew it, and perhaps that was the real transaction happening here — not Coral lying to the consortium, but the house letting her lie, permitting the paperwork, allowing the fiction of a sound structure, because a house certified empty and safe would soon belong to someone, and ownership was a form of invitation.

Or — and this thought arrived with the temperature of the hallway — the house didn't care about the report at all. The report was for the living. The house had other paperwork.

She looked at the summary statement. Her finger hovered over the trackpad. Submit. Seventy-two hours compressed to one click. Her flat deposit, her licence, ten years of being the woman who measures the dark and finds it exactly as long as it looks — all of it balanced on a cursor blinking in a text field, asking her to certify that no conditions were observed, in a house where the primary condition was that it observed *her*.

She closed the laptop without submitting.

The click of the lid sounded, in the silent dining room, exactly like a chair being pushed back from a table.

And from the hallway — the cold hallway, the July-winter hallway — came a sound she had not heard before, faint and rhythmic and unmistakable once identified: water. Rising. Somewhere below the floors, below the cellar, below the false floor, something filling like a tide coming in, and threaded through it, thin as wire, a hum. Eleven verses' worth of melody, cycling patiently, missing only one voice.

Her phone lit up on the table beside her. Dev again, sent forty seconds ago:

*Sorry to message late — weird question. Do you ever get a tune stuck in your head on site? Because I've had one all evening and I can't place it, and it's driving me mad.*
