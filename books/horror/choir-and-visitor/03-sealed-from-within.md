## 3. Sealed From Within

The wall was warm.

Dana Okafor had been running excavation crews for eleven years, and in that time she had learned to read brick the way sailors read sky. Old brick told you its age in the mortar. Fill material told you it had nothing to say. This wall—this impossible, unsurveyed wall sitting forty feet below Delacroix Street where every drawing in the city archive swore there should be nothing but glacial till and old storm clay—was telling her something she didn't want to hear.

She pressed her palm flat against it. The bricks held the heat of a body. Not sun-warm, not pipe-warm. Body-warm, the way a pillow stays warm after someone gets up.

"Forewoman." Marcus, her youngest, stood at the edge of the work light with his hard hat crooked. "You want us to keep going?"

The morning's order had come down through three layers of management: breach the anomaly, log it as fill, pour the footings by Friday. The city had a schedule. The city always had a schedule. Schedules were how cities pretended the ground beneath them was inert.

"Yeah," Dana said, and hated how easily the word came out. "Keep going."

The jackhammer bit into the mortar at 9:40 in the morning. It took two hours to open a gap wide enough for a man to fit through, and the whole time Dana stood with her hand on the wall, feeling the vibration travel up through her wrist bones like the wall itself was clearing its throat.

When the gap opened, the air that came out was wrong. Not sewer-gas wrong, not methane wrong—she'd smelled both, knew both. This air was *used*. Warm and close and faintly sweet, like the inside of a piano, like felt hammers and old varnish and something underneath all of it that made the back of her teeth ache.

Marcus went through first because he was twenty-three and hadn't yet learned that curiosity was a bill that always came due. His headlamp swept the space beyond.

"Dana," he said, and his voice had gone strange and flat. "You need to see this."

It wasn't fill material. It wasn't anything. Beyond the breached wall lay a short corridor of dressed stone, and at the end of the corridor stood a door.

A door. Down here, in the dark, behind a wall that existed on no survey ever drawn—a door, oak, iron-banded, intact, as if it had been waiting for them with its coat already on.

But it was the hinges that did it. Dana saw them before she saw anything else, because her eye went to hardware first, always had. The hinges were on the *inside*.

Doors are built for the side they defend. A door with hinges on the inside is a door meant to be barred from within. Meant to be held shut by someone standing on this side—the dark side, the buried side—and she stood there in the work light spilling through the broken wall and understood, in the wordless way you understand a dropped glass, that somebody had once stood exactly where she was standing and pushed against this door while something on the other side pushed back.

The oak was scarred. Long vertical gouges ran down from the height of a man's shoulders to the floor, dozens of them, hundreds, overlapping, the wood beneath splintered pale as bone. Fingernail scratches. Some of the grooves still held what looked—she made herself look, made herself be sure—like the dark crescents of nails themselves, embedded in the grain.

"They were locked in," Marcus said. "Whoever sealed that wall—they locked these people in on purpose."

"No." Dana heard her own voice come out steady, which surprised her. "Look again. The wall came later. The door was sealed first. They shut it from this side." She crouched at the base of the door, where the gouges ended in a churned mess of scrapes, like hands had gone on searching even after their owners could no longer stand. "They weren't locking people in. They were locking something out. Or—" she stopped.

Or keeping it company.

There was a brass plate screwed into the oak at eye level, green with age except where a century of absent hands had polished a small oval bright. She wiped it with her glove. The letters came up clean:

**CHOIR HALL — 1911**

Behind her, someone laughed. One short, nervous bark of a laugh, the kind men make when the alternative is screaming. She didn't turn around to see who. She was looking at the door handle, which was turned all the way to the left, and then all the way to the right, and then back—slowly, gently, patiently—as though whatever stood on the other side had been trying the lock for a hundred and fourteen years and had all the time in the world to keep trying.

"Nobody touches it," Dana said. "Nobody opens it. Get the camera."

She photographed everything. The hinges. The scratches. The plate. The handle mid-turn, caught frozen between positions like a man caught mid-sentence. Forty-one photographs, though she wouldn't notice the number until later, and when she noticed it she would wish she hadn't.

Her report went up the chain at 4:15 that afternoon. At 5:02 her supervisor called.

"Okafor. I got your file on the Delacroix anomaly."

"It's not fill material, Sal. There's a structure down there. A door with—"

"I read it. Here's what's going to happen. You're going to amend the report. Unidentified masonry, pre-1900, no structural significance, cleared for demolition pending footing installation."

"Sal—"

"The city has a schedule, Dana. That pillar goes in Friday or the whole Delacroix project slips a quarter and everybody under me starts asking why my crew found a *door* instead of dirt." A pause, and then, softer, the tone he used when he thought he was being kind: "Old cities have old junk in the ground. You know that. Log it and move on."

She logged it. And then she printed the photographs—all forty-one—and put them in her truck's glove box, because some instinct older than her career told her that reports get amended and photographs get deleted, and paper in a glove box answers to nobody.

That night she dreamed of a door turning its handle, slowly, patiently, and woke at 3 a.m. with the taste of old varnish in her mouth and the absolute certainty that somewhere below Delacroix Street, something had finally gotten tired of trying the lock.

* * *

Edmund Hartwell knew the tunnels the way other men knew their own handwriting, and so it took him only four seconds to know that the handwriting had changed.

He was in his apartment above the shuttered laundromat on Vane Street, running the week's seismograph logs through his laptop—the cheap geophone he'd buried in the utility tunnel off Corben Row fed into a salvaged data logger, and the logger fed into him, nightly, the way scripture feeds the devout. He'd kept the ritual for six years. Six years of lines. Traffic rumble, blue on the printout, regular as breathing. Water in the flood mains, a slower blue. The 2:14 freight train, a spike he could set his watch by.

And now, starting at 11:37 the previous night, something else.

He zoomed in and felt the skin of his arms tighten.

The new signal was rhythmic. Not mechanical rhythm—machines were either metronomes or chaos, and this was neither. It swelled and fell. It paused where a breath would pause. It resumed. Four beats, a rest, four beats, a rest, over and over, sustained for eleven minutes and then gone, as if whatever made it had grown bored, or satisfied, or full.

He printed the trace and laid it beside six years of archives. Nothing like it. Not once. In six years the ground beneath Grayfall had hummed with pipes and trains and the slow tides of the water table, and never—not one night—had it *sung*.

Unless it had, and he'd never had the ear to hear it.

Edmund sat very still in the blue light of his monitor and made himself think like a surveyor instead of a frightened man. Data first. Fear second. He pulled the coordinates: the signal's strongest amplitude pointed south-southwest, toward the old industrial grid, toward—

Delacroix Street.

He knew Delacroix. Of course he knew Delacroix; he knew every street and what slept under it. Branch line nine ran beneath it, the one he'd surveyed in March, the one with forty-one doors—thirty-seven of them false, four of them sealed, all of them counted, all of them logged in his master map in his own small, precise hand. He had walked that branch eleven times. He had never found a corridor of dressed stone. He had never found an oak door with a brass plate.

Which meant the crew had found something his maps said could not exist, and Edmund Hartwell had learned long ago that when the tunnels contradicted the map, the map lost.

He opened his logbook to the current page and wrote, in the handwriting he kept deliberately boring, legible to any stranger who might someday find it:

*March 14. New seismic signature, Corben Row geophone. Rhythmic. Organic cadence. Duration 11 min., onset 2337 hrs. Bearing consistent with Delacroix St. excavation. Hypothesis: machinery. Confidence in hypothesis: none.*

He stopped. Looked at the trace again. Four beats, a rest, four beats.

It wasn't machinery. Machinery didn't take rests. Machinery didn't swell on the third beat like it was leaning into a note.

His grandmother had sung in a church choir in another city, in another life, and as a boy he'd fallen asleep downstairs listening to rehearsal drifting through the floorboards—that same swelling on the third beat, voices leaning together into the harmony like people leaning into wind. He had spent thirty-eight years keeping rules underground: count the doors, count the lights, never answer a sound with a sound, stay invisible. Rules for staying unnoticed by whatever else lived down there. And the first rule, the rule under all the others, the one he'd never written down because writing it would make it real:

*Never hum in the tunnels.*

Because humming was an invitation. Because a voice in the dark was a hook with your name on it.

He looked at the seismograph trace, at those patient, swelling beats rising out of the ground beneath Delacroix Street, and understood that the rule had never been about him. It had never mattered whether *he* hummed. Something down there had been humming all along, for years maybe, decades, centuries—too deep, too faint, too patient for his little geophone to catch. And now a city crew with a jackhammer and a schedule had cracked open the silence around it, and the thing was stretching, and testing, and singing to itself in the dark like a man whistling on his way to work.

Edmund closed the logbook. Then, because dread arriving on schedule is still dread, and schedules are a kind of comfort, he did what he always did. He checked the counts.

Forty-one doors on branch nine. He'd verify tomorrow night. Count the doors, count the lights, go home, sleep. The system held. The system always held.

He was almost asleep when the freight train passed at 2:14, right on time, and beneath its familiar spike he heard—or felt, or imagined, the distinction had begun to matter less—in the floor of his apartment, faint as a pulse through a wall:

Four beats. A rest. Four beats.

Learning the rhythm of the city above it. Learning, note by note, the shape of everything it intended to sing.

He got up and turned on every light in the apartment and did not go back to bed, and at dawn he packed his survey bag, because there was one thing worse than a wrong count, and that was not knowing why the count was wrong.

Branch nine waited. So did door number forty-two—he just didn't know its name yet.
