## 5. The Archivist

The city's records office smelled like dust and burnt coffee and the particular despair of public institutions—Dana had been in enough of them to know it was universal. She'd taken a half day, which her crew could ill afford, and told herself it was due diligence. Nothing more. A forewoman covering her ass before the Delacroix anomaly became someone else's problem to ignore.

The clerk at the reference desk, a young man with wire-rimmed glasses and the posture of someone who'd already given up on being asked interesting questions, looked up when she said she wanted everything on the Grayfall Choir Hall fire.

"The what?"

"Choir Hall. 1911. Fire."

He typed with two fingers. "I'm not finding anything under Choir Hall."

"Try Grayfall Music Hall. Or the Delacroix Street fire." She'd spent two nights digging through microfiche at the public library, and the name kept shifting the way names do in old papers—the *Grayfall Choral Society Hall*, the *Delacroix Street Auditorium*, once, memorably, just *the place where the singers went*. Forty-one dead. That number never changed.

"Ah," the clerk said. "There's a restricted collection flag on that. You'd need—" he squinted at the screen, "—a records request form, and honestly, most of that material was deaccessioned in the nineties. Budget cuts."

"Deaccessioned where?"

He shrugged with his whole body. "Somebody took it home, probably. Happens all the time. There was an archivist who got let go around then—he was always fighting about that stuff. Renner? Something like that. He made such a stink they had to escort him out."

"Do you have an address?"

"I really shouldn't—"

Dana set her hard hat on the counter. It still had Delacroix Street clay dried in the vents of it, orange-red and stubborn. "I've got forty-one people buried under a street my crew is currently excavating," she said, which was true in spirit if not in letter, "and the city's answer so far has been *log it as fill*. I don't have time for shouldn'ts."

He gave her the address. He didn't even make her sign anything.

* * *

Ivo Renner lived above a shuttered laundromat on Corvus Avenue, in a building whose ground-floor windows were papered over with decades of sun-bleached flyers for wash-and-fold specials. The stairwell smelled like mildew and old paper, and by the third flight Dana understood why: the smell was coming from under the door at the top, seeping out like something alive.

She knocked. No answer. She knocked again, harder, and heard something inside—a chair scraping, a shuffle, a long pause that felt like assessment.

"Who is it?" The voice was dry, suspicious, and pitched like a man who hadn't used it loudly in years.

"Dana Okafor. Crew Nineteen, Department of Public Works. I need to ask you about the 1911 choir hall fire."

Silence. Then, closer to the door: "There was no fire."

"I know there was a fire. Forty-one people died."

"No." The lock turned—one, two, three separate bolts—and the door opened six inches on a chain. One eye appeared in the gap, pale gray, ringed with skin gone soft from indoor living. "There was a fire. But nobody died in it. That's the part everyone gets wrong, and it's the only part that matters." The eye studied her hard hat, her boots, the clay on her sleeves. "You're from the excavation. Delacroix Street."

"How did you—"

"Because I've been waiting thirty years for someone from Delacroix Street to knock on this door." The chain rattled off. "Come in. Don't touch anything. And don't hum."

The last instruction landed so casually, so precisely, that Dana stopped halfway across the threshold. "What did you say?"

"Don't hum. Some people hum without noticing. Nervous habit. If you feel one coming on, swallow it." He was already shuffling away into the apartment, a narrow man in a cardigan the color of weak tea, gesturing vaguely at a kitchen chair as if the chair were a guest he tolerated. "Sit there. It's the only one without paper stacked on it."

The apartment was not an apartment. It was an archive that had eaten a man and worn his shape. Every wall was shelved floor to ceiling, and every shelf was double-stacked with binders, boxes, ledger books, and file folders tied with string. The kitchen table held a spread of photographs weighted down with coffee mugs. The bathtub—visible through a half-open door—was full of document tubes. In the corner, a cot barely wider than Ivo himself sat wedged between two filing cabinets, sheets rumpled, a reading lamp clamped to the headboard. The man slept inside his obsession like a shipwreck survivor sleeping inside a whale.

"You were the city archivist," Dana said, taking the chair.

"Assistant archivist. Twenty-two years. They fired me in 1994 for, quote, *unauthorized retention of municipal property*, end quote." He said it the way other men recite battle wounds. "Which means I refused to let them throw away the only complete record of the choir hall incident because it didn't fit in their new compactus system. So yes. Guilty. Everything you see here, I saved. Nobody else was going to."

"Incident," Dana repeated. "Not fire."

Ivo paused mid-shuffle. For the first time he turned and looked at her fully, and she saw that behind the gray eye and the cardigan and the mildew smell was a mind running at a temperature most people never reached. "You're not here because your supervisor sent you," he said. "Supervisors send people to tell me to stop calling. You're here because something happened that the paperwork can't absorb."

Dana thought about lying. She was good at her job partly because she knew when not to. "We hit a wall under Delacroix Street last week. Brick. Not on any survey, any utility map, nothing. Warm. We broke through it Thursday and found a door behind it—hinges mounted on the *inside*, like whoever built it wanted to keep something in or keep themselves in, we couldn't tell. Brass plate says CHOIR HALL — 1911. My supervisor told me to log it as fill material and pour concrete." She pulled out her phone, found the photos, and held it up. "So I photographed it instead. And now three of my crew are sleepwalking toward that site in their sleep, and the city wants us back to work Monday, and I have run out of people to ask."

Ivo took the phone from her hands with the reverence of a priest receiving a relic. He zoomed in on the brass plate. His thumb trembled slightly.

"That's the service entrance," he said quietly. "That's the door the choristers used. Stage left, down the stairs, past the robe room." He looked up. "Hinges on the inside."

"Yeah."

"They sealed it from within." He set the phone down on the table with exaggerated care, as though it might detonate. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? In 1911, forty-one people walked into that building's lower level, and they shut the door behind them, and they fastened it from the inside, and then the building burned above them. The official record—which I will show you, I have the coroner's report, I have the insurance investigation, I have the *newspaper* accounts, all of them contradicting each other in instructive ways—says they died of smoke inhalation trying to escape. But smoke doesn't seal doors. Smoke doesn't mount hinges." He began pulling boxes, moving down the shelves with the economy of long practice. "Smoke doesn't leave fingernail scratches on the *outside* face of the wood."

Dana's mouth had gone dry. "The scratches were on our door too. Inside face. Wait—" she replayed the memory, the flashlight beam crawling over gouged wood, "no. Outside. The side facing the tunnel."

Ivo froze. Slowly, he turned around. "Facing the tunnel," he repeated. "Facing *outward*."

"Yes."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere below, a bus sighed at its stop, and the windows buzzed faintly.

"That's new information," Ivo said at last. His voice had changed—flatter, careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice and testing each footfall. "In ninety-one years, that detail has never been different. Every account, every fragment, every secondhand retelling: scratches on the inside. People clawing to get *in* after the door closed, or clawing at a door that wouldn't open. I built theories on it. Whole frameworks." He wiped his palms on his cardigan. "If the scratches are on the outside now, then either every historian and witness for a century misremembered the same detail, or—"

"Or the door's been used since," Dana finished.

"Or," Ivo said, "the thing on the other side has been practicing."

* * *

He fed her tea she didn't want, in a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST ARCHIVIST, and then he showed her the files, and the afternoon unspooled like a slow-motion disaster.

The coroner's report listed forty-one deaths, cause: asphyxiation. But the recovery timeline was wrong—bodies recovered over four days, some from chambers the fire never reached, some, according to a marginal note in a firefighter's personal log that Ivo had tracked down at auction in 1987, "found seated, orderly, like a congregation." The insurance investigator's report noted that the choir hall's lower level showed no fire damage whatsoever. The newspapers ran forty-one dead in the headline and then, in paragraph nine of the follow-up story, mentioned almost in passing that the hall's famous organ had been found playing when firefighters breached the stage door—though the gas lines had been cut.

"Playing," Dana said. "By what?"

"That," Ivo said, "is the question I have spent thirty years and two marriages failing to answer." He said it without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather. "And then there's this."

From a flat wooden case lined with green felt, he lifted a book.

It was a hymnal—octavo size, cloth-bound, its spine cracked white with age. THE GRAYFALL CHORAL SOCIETY was stamped in faded gilt across the cover. Ivo opened it on the cleared half of the kitchen table with both hands, the way you'd handle a newborn or a bomb.

"This is the society's master copy. The choirmaster's own annotated edition. I acquired it in 1979 from an estate sale—the family of the hall's caretaker. Look at the use marks. Every hymn in the standard repertoire shows wear. Except one." He turned pages—Luther, Wesley, Latin settings with penciled tempo markings in a precise Victorian hand—until he reached a section near the end where the pages went pristine. Unused. And then, at the very back:

A torn stub. Ragged-edged, torn with force, not carelessness. Whatever page had followed the last surviving hymn was gone.

"What was on it?"

"I don't know. Nobody knows. That's the point." Ivo's finger hovered over the stub without touching it. "Every copy of this hymnal I've ever traced—there were two hundred printed for the society in 1909—I've found eleven. All eleven have that final page torn out. Not lost. Torn. Same tear, same violence, like the pages were removed deliberately, systematically, before distribution or immediately after. Someone wanted that hymn to exist in no complete copy anywhere." He finally looked up at her. "And the choirmaster, Elias Wren, mailed a letter to his sister in Harwick the week before the fire. The postal record survives—it's in the parish archive, I checked it against the delivery ledgers twice. A letter, thick enough to be rated as a packet. To his sister. Three days before he and forty of his singers sealed themselves into a basement and burned a building down over their own heads."

Dana sat with that. Outside, the light had begun to go amber; the papered windows glowed like old bruises.

"You think the missing page was in that letter."

"I think Elias Wren was a careful man who annotated every tempo and corrected every printer's error, and who tore the ending out of two hundred books, and who then sent the only surviving copy of that ending to the one person he trusted absolutely, with instructions I would give a great deal to read." Ivo's voice dropped. "And I think whatever he sent is the reason the door stayed shut for a hundred and twelve years. Until your crew put a machine against it."

"We didn't know."

"Nobody ever knows. That's how these things work. The city paves over its own warnings and calls it progress, and then some forewoman with a camera does the only honest thing anyone's done since 1911." He almost smiled. It was a rusty motion, like a gate unused for years. "Why did you photograph it? Your supervisor said fill. You'd have kept your job easier by saying nothing."

Dana turned her phone over in her hands, the photo of the brass plate glowing on the screen. CHOIR HALL — 1911. She thought about Marcus Osei standing too long in front of the breach with his head tilted, listening. About the warmth in the brick, like something breathing behind it. About the way the whole site had felt since Thursday—not dangerous exactly, but *attended*.

"My father worked the docks," she said. "Forty years. He used to say the water doesn't care what the shipping schedule says. You can file all the paperwork you want—the tide comes in anyway." She shrugged, embarrassed by the poetry and unwilling to take it back. "Something under Delacroix Street isn't fill. I don't know what it is. But I know the difference between a wall and a lie, and the city is asking me to build one on top of the other. I figured somebody, somewhere, might have written down what's actually down there. And every road I followed led to a man who got fired for refusing to throw those writings away."

Ivo studied her for a long moment. Whatever he saw seemed to satisfy some test he'd been administering since the chain came off the door.

"There's a condition," he said. "If we do this—if I help you—we do it properly. Documentation, cross-referencing, verification. No ghost stories, no folklore, none of the internet's carnival nonsense. This thing has survived a century on people refusing to look at it straight. I refuse to be one more person who squints." He was already clearing space on the table, sweeping mugs aside, laying out the hymnal, the coroner's report, a map of 1911 Delacroix Street so detailed it showed the choir hall's foundations in dashed lines—directly beneath her excavation site. "And there's a rule. Non-negotiable."

"The humming thing."

"The humming thing." He fixed her with the gray eyes. "You'll understand eventually. When you do, you'll wish you'd taken it more seriously on day one. Everyone does."

Dana nodded slowly. Two skeptics, she thought—one fired for keeping the truth, one paid to bury it—shaking hands over a table covered in a hundred years of evidence that the truth had teeth.

"So," she said. "Elias Wren's sister. Harwick. Where do we start?"

Ivo was already pulling a parish register toward him, opening it to a ribbon marker that had clearly lived there for years.

"We start," he said, "with the question that's been keeping me up at night since 1994." His finger came down on the register, on a line of faded ink. "Wren tore the ending out of every book. He mailed the last copy away. Which means somewhere out there is a piece of paper that a hundred and twelve years of silence depends on—and I want to know, before we go anywhere near it, whether the family that kept it all this time ever understood what they were holding." He looked up. "Because the postal record says the packet arrived. But the parish death register says the sister died eight months later. Of no recorded cause. At thirty-four years old. And the entry right beneath hers—" he turned the register so Dana could see, "—is her daughter. Age six. Same month."

The radiator ticked. The amber light crawled another inch across the table, across the torn stub of the hymnal's final page, ragged as a wound.

"Somebody in that family line is still alive," Ivo said quietly. "The great-niece. And Dana—I think she's the last person on earth who knows what happens when you read that page aloud."
