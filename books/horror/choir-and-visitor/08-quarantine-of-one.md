## 8. Quarantine of One

The first one was Marcus Webb, and Dana found him at 4:17 in the morning because she'd stopped sleeping at the site and started driving past it instead.

She told herself it was due diligence. She told herself a lot of things that week. The truth was simpler: since the door came down—since the hinges on the wrong side, the fingernail grooves fanned across the inside like pressed flowers, the brass plate reading CHOIR HALL — 1911—she had not been able to make her body believe the site was safe at night. So she drove. Loop after loop through the empty grid streets around Delacroix, thermos of coffee going cold in the cupholder, radio off because the radio felt wrong lately, all that music arriving uninvited into the cab.

Marcus was walking down the center line of Delacroix Street in his socks.

No coat. No boots. His hard hat still on, absurdly, perched on his head like he'd never taken it off, like some part of him believed he was still on shift. He walked with his arms loose at his sides and his chin lifted slightly, the way people walk when they're listening to something beautiful from very far away, and his eyes were open, and they were aimed at nothing.

Dana pulled alongside him and hit the hazards. "Marcus."

He didn't stop. She got out and fell into step beside him, and up close she could hear it—the sound coming out of him, low and steady under his breath. Not words. Not yet words. A hummed line that rose and fell in a pattern her ear kept trying to complete, kept reaching for the next note the way a tongue reaches for a missing tooth.

"Marcus. Hey. Big man." She put a hand on his shoulder.

He turned his head toward her without breaking stride, and for one full second his eyes focused on hers, and what was in them wasn't sleep. It was longing. Pure, naked, unbearable longing, the look of a man standing outside a lit window at Christmas, and then it slid away again and he was walking, humming, and she had to plant herself in front of him and shove, both hands flat on his chest, to stop his forward motion.

He weighed too much. Sleepwalking bodies always did, some animal stubbornness in them, but she braced and pushed and finally he rocked to a halt, swaying, humming.

"Marcus. It's Dana. You're on Delacroix Street. It's four in the morning and you're in your socks."

His mouth kept moving. The hum went on. Then, slowly, like a machine powering down through its stages, he shuddered, blinked, and looked around at the street, the sodium lights, her, himself—and Dana watched the exact moment he understood where he was, because his face did something she'd only seen once before, on a man who'd woken up mid-collapse in a trench that should have killed him.

"Oh God," Marcus said. His voice was wrecked. Raw, scraped, like he'd been using it for hours. "Oh God, Ms. Okafor, I was—I was almost there. I was almost *there*." And he started to cry, standing in the middle of the road in his socks, and Dana held onto his shoulders and looked up the length of Delacroix Street toward the site fence, toward the breach, and felt the cold come up out of the ground and into her teeth.

*Almost there.* Not *what happened to me*. Not *how did I get here*.

*Almost there.*

She got him into the truck. On the drive back to his place he asked her twice what day it was, and both times, before she could answer, his lips started moving again, silently, shaping notes, and he caught himself doing it and pressed his hand over his own mouth like he was holding a wound shut.

*

By noon she had four names.

Not just Marcus. Reyes, who lived two miles east and whose wife found the back door open and his boots on the porch, facing the street. The Okafor—no relation—kid, twenty-three, first excavation job, found by a neighbor sitting on the curb outside his building at three a.m., rocking slightly, humming. And Tomás, who hadn't made it out of his apartment at all but whose girlfriend reported he'd spent the night standing at the foot of the bed, facing south, toward Delacroix, humming until she shook him awake, and even then he wouldn't stop for another minute, and when he did stop he said the same thing Marcus said: *I was almost there.*

Four men. All of Crew Nineteen. All of them men who'd been inside the breach, who'd stood in that warm brick corridor, who'd touched or leaned against or simply breathed the air near the door with the hinges on the inside.

Dana sat in her truck outside the site office and called the city's emergency line, and then the contractor's safety officer, and then, when neither produced anything but hold music and forms, she called the number for the Department of Public Works duty desk and got a woman named Brandt who sounded like she was eating lunch while she listened.

"So four workers exhibited somnambulant behavior," Brandt said. Dana could hear chewing. "Following exposure to an undocumented subsurface structure."

"They're being *pulled*, Ms. Brandt. They're walking toward it in their sleep. One of them was halfway there in his socks on an open road."

"Mm. Any injuries?"

"No. That's not—that's not the point—"

"Then I'll log it as a fatigue incident. Your crew's been pushing long shifts on a compressed schedule; sleep disturbances are consistent with overwork. Has anyone actually been hurt?"

Dana closed her eyes. "Not yet."

"Then here's what happens next. You file an incident report, you document the fatigue mitigation measures, and the schedule holds. We're already nine days behind on Delacroix, Ms. Okafor, and the councilman whose district this is has a press conference Friday about infrastructure investment. If work stops on that site, the story becomes delays and cost overruns, and I promise you nobody in this conversation wants that story." A pause; the chewing stopped. "You seem like a conscientious forewoman. Conscientious forewomen document things. Document this."

The line went dead in the particular way lines go dead when someone has hung up rather than finished.

Dana sat with the phone in her lap. Through the windshield she could see the site fence, the breach shoring, the orange plastic mesh stirring in the wind like something breathing under a sheet. Nine days behind schedule. She thought about the brass plate. CHOIR HALL — 1911. She thought about hinges on the inside, and fingernails, dozens of sets of fingernails, worn into the wood at shoulder height and lower, at the height of someone kneeling.

They'd sealed themselves in. Somebody had looked at that door and read it right, or half right, and the city's answer was *log it as fill material*.

Her supervisor, when she escalated to him directly, was gentler about it, which somehow made it worse. "Dana. I know how it looks. But you've got no mechanism here. No injury, no hazard classification, nothing a safety order can attach to. You want to lock down a site because four guys had bad dreams? Take the win—they're fine. Post someone on nights if it'll help you sleep. Keep the crew rotating away from the breach face. Do what you can inside the lines." He sighed, and she heard in the sigh a man who had once been her, decades ago, before the lines wore grooves in him. "Inside the lines is where we live, Dana."

So she locked down what she could. She split the crew into shifts that never overlapped, so no more than six of them were ever underground. She moved the break trailers two blocks north and posted a rotation on night watch at the fence—paid overtime out of her own contingency line and dared anyone upstairs to question it. She wrote FATIGUE PROTOCOL in block letters on the whiteboard and underneath it, smaller, where only the crew would bother reading: *If you catch yourself humming, tell me. Nobody gets laughed at. Nobody hums.*

And every night she drove the loop past Delacroix Street, and every night she found fewer of them walking and more of them *standing*—which was worse, somehow. Standing at their windows. Standing at bus stops that had no buses at that hour. Once, the new kid, the Okafor kid, standing perfectly still in the middle of the pedestrian bridge over the canal, facing south, and when she climbed up to him he said, without turning, in a voice gone soft and happy, "It's better in the low parts, Ms. Okafor. Down where the water remembers the singing. Did you know water keeps songs? It keeps everything."

She walked him home. She did not sleep.

*

The man appeared at the fence on the sixth night.

She saw him from the truck first—a shape standing at the corner where the orange mesh met the chain-link, motionless, hands in the pockets of a canvas jacket, watching the site with the patience of someone who had been watching it for a long time. Her first thought was press. Her second was city. Her third, as she got out and approached with her flashlight up, was neither: he was too thin for either category, too careful, dressed in layers that had been chosen for function a long time ago and were now mostly memory. Gray stubble. Eyes that did the thing she'd started noticing in mirrors—kept flicking to the breach shoring, counting it, even while looking at her.

"That's far enough," she said. "Site's closed to public."

"I know. I need ten minutes and I need you to not call anyone." He said it without drama, the way you'd say *I need you to hold this level*. "My name's Edmund Hartwell. I map the tunnels under this part of the city. All of them. Including the ones your survey doesn't have."

Dana kept the flashlight where it was. "There's no tunnels on the city survey under Delacroix except the storm main."

"There's forty-one doors under Delacroix," he said. "There were forty-one. As of eleven days ago there are forty-two, and the forty-second is the one your crew opened, and I would very much like to talk to the person in charge before whatever's behind it finishes learning everyone's voice."

The flashlight beam held steady on his chest because her hands were steadier than the rest of her. *Learning everyone's voice.* Marcus, humming. Tomás, humming. The kid on the bridge: *water keeps songs.*

"You're the one who filed the comment on the geotech survey," she said slowly. "Three years ago. Anonymous attachment, hand-drawn cross-sections, flagged structural voids under four parcels. The city rejected it as unsourced."

"They rejected it because I wouldn't say how I got the measurements." Something that wasn't quite a smile crossed his face. "I don't file paperwork from places I'm not supposed to be. It creates a trail."

"And now you're standing at my fence asking me to create one."

"Now I'm standing at your fence because the thing below us has started recruiting, and the city's response is a fatigue protocol." He said it flatly, no accusation in it, and that was somehow the most alarming part—he spoke about the city the way you speak about weather. "I watched the watch rotation. Two-hour posts, single person, facing outward. Whoever set that up understands the problem better than the city does. I assumed that was you."

"It was me."

"Then you're who I came to see." He crouched, unhurried, keeping his movements large and legible the way you do with a dog you don't know, and lifted the flap of a satchel at his hip. "I brought copies. Not the originals—I don't let the originals leave my apartment—but enough. Branches, depths, door counts, dates. Everything I've mapped in nine years, and everything I've logged since your crew broke the seal. Including the seismograph traces." He straightened, holding out a fat envelope wrapped in a freezer bag against the damp. "Rhythmic vibration starting the night of the breach. Regular as a pulse. It's not machinery. I've mapped under every pump station and subway line in this grid. It's not machinery."

Dana didn't take the envelope yet. "Why bring it to me? Why not the city? Press? Anyone with actual authority?"

"Because the city will seal the records and keep digging, the press will run it for a news cycle and make it harder to fix quietly, and anyone with authority has proven—" he nodded back toward the floodlights of the site, toward the shored breach glowing in the dark "—exactly what they'll do with it. You posted night watch on your own money. You split your crew so fewer people are exposed at once. You're the only person in this equation acting like the situation is real." He paused. "Also, four of your people are already half-singing. I've been listening to the recordings. Their pitch is improving. Whatever's teaching them is a patient teacher, and I'd like to be talking to someone who's noticed that before it graduates the class."

The wind moved the orange mesh along the fence line, that slow breathing ripple, and Dana became aware of the sound underneath the site noise—the pumps, the generators, the distant traffic—and how none of it quite covered the low tone rising from the breach shoring, so faint you could convince yourself it was a truck idling three blocks away, except it had been there every night for a week, and trucks moved.

"How do I know any of this is real?" she said. "How do I know you're not the problem? Some guy who's been living down there so long he's—" she gestured, inadequate, "—part of it."

"You don't," Edmund said simply. "That's the honest answer, and I'd think less of you if you accepted anything else. What I can offer is this: check my maps against your own surveys. Every line I've drawn that overlaps city data will match. Every line that doesn't overlap will turn out to exist—you can verify one branch tomorrow by dropping a sonar line down the old utility shaft on Kessler, forty feet north of where the survey says the main runs. I'm not asking for trust. I'm asking for verification, and then for alliance, because I've spent nine years staying invisible to whatever lives down there by following rules, and those rules stopped working eleven days ago, and I don't think either of us fixes this alone."

He held out the envelope again. Rain was starting, fine and cold, spotting the freezer bag.

Dana looked at it. She thought about Brandt chewing. About *inside the lines is where we live*. About Marcus crying in the middle of Delacroix Street saying *I was almost there*, and about the fingernail marks on the inside of a door sealed in 1911 by people who had understood, completely, what the city refused to understand now.

She took the envelope.

"One condition," she said. "You don't go down there. Not alone, not at all, not until I say otherwise. You're data, Mr. Hartwell, not a second front."

Something moved through his face then—too fast to name, gone before she could read it, though later, much later, she would learn its name was relief mixed with guilt mixed with a plan already forming. "Agreed," Edmund Hartwell said. "For now."

He turned up his collar against the rain and nodded once toward the breach, and as he did, the low tone beneath the site shifted—lifted, adjusted, settled—and Dana heard, unmistakably, in the space between one generator throb and the next, a new voice join the hum from deep below the ground.

A voice she knew. Steady, careful, patient.

Edmund heard it too. He went very still, head tilted, listening the way she imagined he listened to everything—with a count running underneath.

"That's Priya Chandrasekaran," Dana said. "She's on my crew. She's never been within fifty feet of the breach."

"I know," Edmund said quietly. "It's stopped waiting for people to come to it."
