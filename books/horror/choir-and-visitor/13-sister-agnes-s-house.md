## 13. Sister Agnes's House

The bus dropped Dana at the end of the coast road with six hours of salt wind behind it and nothing ahead but gray water and a building the color of old bone.

She'd taken two days off—burned nearly all her remaining leave to do it—and told the city she was visiting family. In a way that was true now. The circle had become a family faster than any crew she'd ever run: Edmund with his maps spread across Ivo's kitchen table like scripture, Priya sleeping in the armchair because she was afraid of what walked in her sleep, Ivo surrounded by paper like a man building a raft out of documents. Nine days on the excavation schedule. Eight now. The city's engineers were already staging equipment at the Delacroix site, and every phone call Dana made to stop them went into the same polite void.

So she'd come here herself, because institutions had failed her and an old woman by the sea might not.

Sister Agnes's Rest Home sat where the road gave up, a long low building facing the Atlantic as if it had been set there to watch for something coming off the water. Gulls wheeled over the roof. A garden gone slightly wild ran down toward the dunes, rosemary and gorse and a stone birdbath filled with rain. It should have been peaceful. Dana felt her shoulders climb toward her ears anyway. She hadn't been able to shake the feeling since Priya's night walk—the feeling of being late for something, of a rehearsal already underway somewhere beneath her feet.

Inside smelled of lavender and floor wax and, underneath it, faintly, of tide. A nurse with reading glasses on a chain looked up from the front desk.

"I'm here to see Agnes Delacroix-Marchand," Dana said. "I called ahead."

"Miss Okafor." The nurse smiled. "She's been asking for you since breakfast. She doesn't ask for people, usually. You must be someone."

Dana didn't know how to answer that. "We've never met."

"Well." The nurse came around the desk, lowering her voice as if delivering a weather report. "Fair warning. She's sharp as a tack but she does things her own way. She'll want tea. She'll want you to sit through the whole pot. And she may test you. She was a music teacher for forty years. She can tell when someone's lying by how they breathe."

*By how they breathe.* Dana kept her face still. "I'll keep my breathing honest."

* * *

Agnes Delacroix-Marchand received her in a room at the end of the east wing, a corner room with windows on two sides so the ocean got in everywhere—light, sound, smell. She was small and upright in a wingback chair, silver hair pinned with military precision, hands folded on a blanket over her knees. On the side table beside her: a teapot under a knitted cozy, two cups, and a photograph in a tarnished silver frame showing a young man in a high-collared choir robe, standing very straight, his mouth set like a man who has already decided something.

"You have his jaw," Agnes said, before Dana had even finished sitting down. "Not the face. The jaw. Set like that." She nodded at the photograph. "My great-uncle Émile. Choirmaster of Grayfall Choir Hall. Sit. Pour. Milk's in the little jug, sugar's in the blue bowl, and don't tell me you take it black, everyone says they take it black and nobody means it."

Dana poured. Her hands were steady. She'd poured coffee for crews at five a.m. after collapses; she could pour tea for one small fierce woman.

"You're the one digging up Delacroix Street," Agnes said. It wasn't a question.

"The city is digging," Dana said. "I'm trying to stop them."

"Mm." Agnes accepted her cup, held it without drinking, and studied Dana over the rim with eyes that were pale gray and absolutely unclouded. "Ivo Renner wrote to me. Did he tell you? Three letters over four years, each one politer than the last, each one asking about family papers. I never answered. I don't answer questions about Émile. So you'll understand my confusion when I find myself expecting a stranger at eleven o'clock on a Tuesday." She sipped. "Something's changed. Something's made you desperate enough that I could feel it through the telephone. Tell me what."

And Dana did. She'd planned to be careful—to build trust first, to ease in—but the words came out of her in a rush the moment she opened her mouth, as if they'd been waiting behind her teeth for weeks. The wall that wasn't on any survey. The door hinged from the inside. The fingernail scratches. The humming in the seismograph logs. The night worker found singing at dawn with his throat bleeding. Priya standing on Delacroix Street at three in the morning, harmonizing with something under the asphalt, describing it as *a rehearsal I'm late for*. The demolition order. Nine lines. Eight days.

Agnes listened without interrupting once. When Dana finished, the old woman set down her cup with a small precise click, and for a long moment the only sound in the room was the ocean.

"Forty-one singers," Agnes said at last. "That's what your records say died in the fire. Forty-one voices." She turned the photograph slightly, so the young man faced Dana directly. "Émile was forty-two."

Dana's breath caught. "He wasn't counted?"

"He wasn't *in* the building when it burned. That's the official story, and it's true, in the way official stories are true—he walked out the front door at nine-forty that evening, and the fire started at ten, and he was never seen again. They searched for him for months. My grandmother, his sister Céleste, waited for a body that never came." Agnes's voice was level, unhurried, a teacher's voice laying out a lesson she'd rehearsed in her head for eighty years. "Céleste received a package in the spring of 1912. No return address. Inside was a hymnal page, torn out, folded in thirds, and a letter in Émile's hand. My grandmother burned the letter. She read it once, by lamplight, and then she held it over the stove while my mother watched, and she made my mother swear on the family name never to speak of what it said."

"But she kept the page."

"She kept the page because burning it felt like a second death, and Céleste Marchand did not kill her brother twice." Agnes's chin lifted a fraction. "It's been in this family for a hundred and thirteen years. It will not be surrendered to a stranger because a stranger asks nicely."

"I'm not asking nicely," Dana said. "I'm asking scared."

For the first time, something moved behind the pale eyes—not softening exactly, but recognition. One skeptic to another, across a hundred years.

"Good," Agnes said quietly. "Honest fear. That's rarer than you'd think. Most people who come to me about Émile come hungry. Historians, ghost-chasers, one dreadful man with a podcast. They want a story. You want to survive." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the blanket. "Tell me what you know of the hymn itself. Not the fire. The hymn."

"It's Latin," Dana said. "Ivo's been translating. The recovered verses are a lullaby—literally, a song for putting something to sleep. The final verse is the lock. We think the singers sealed themselves in with it. With *it*. Whatever's under Grayfall."

"And you know it's learning voices."

"Yes."

"How many so far?"

Dana counted against her will. "One worker. Eleven seconds of recording—forty-one voices layered over something older. Half of Crew Nineteen walks in their sleep or worse. Priya. And—" She stopped.

"And the man with the maps," Agnes said. Not unkindly. "Ivo mentioned him in his last letter. Edmund Hartwell. He said the man counts doors." She watched Dana's face do whatever it did. "Ah. There it is. You care for him."

"He's going to do something stupid," Dana said. "Noble-stupid. The worst kind. And I need that page to give him another option, or at least to know what the cost actually is before he pays it blind."

Agnes was silent for a long time. Outside, a gull cried and cried and gave up. Then the old woman reached beneath the blanket, beneath the cushion of the wingback chair—Dana realized the chair had been reupholstered around a hiding place decades ago, seams let out and restitched by careful hands—and drew out a flat parcel wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed string, gone amber with age.

She did not hand it over. She held it in both hands, on her knees, like something warm she was afraid of setting down.

"My mother warned me about this before she warned me about strangers or traffic or hot stoves," Agnes said. "I was six years old. She sat me on her knee at this same ocean, pointed at the water, and said: *the red hymn is a door, and doors only open one way unless you know the ninth line, and no one alive knows the ninth line, so you will never sing past the eighth.* Those were her words. Never sing past the eighth." Her thumb moved along the string. "I asked her why. Children ask why. And she went white as that foam out there and said, *because the ninth line isn't sung, it's answered, and God help whoever answers.*"

"The ninth line is cut off," Dana said. "Mid-word. We know. Ivo thinks—it's not sung. It's given. Something has to be offered."

"Then Ivo is smarter than the archivists who fired him, which is a low bar, but still." Agnes almost smiled. It faded fast. "You want to know what happened to the last person who read it aloud. Everyone wants to know that eventually. It's the question under all the other questions." She looked out at the water. "No."

"No?"

"No. I won't tell you." She said it gently, the way you decline a child's request to touch a stove. "Not because I'm cruel. Because telling is how it spreads. My grandmother learned that. Every person who hears the story carries a piece of it home like a seed on a boot. You've noticed, haven't you? How it recruits. Through longing, your girl said. Through wanting to belong to something. Well—a story is a belonging too. A terrible story most of all. People *want* to carry it." She turned back to Dana, and her voice dropped, and for a moment she sounded less like a teacher and more like the six-year-old who'd been warned. "I will give you the page. I decided that before you arrived—I felt it coming, the way you feel weather in old bones. But the price is that you take it whole and you don't ask me for the ending. Some knowledge is a load-bearing wall, Miss Okafor. You pull it out and the whole house comes down on whoever's standing inside."

Dana wanted to argue. Every instinct trained by a decade of incident reports and safety briefings screamed that incomplete information kills people—that the last person who read it aloud was exactly the data point she needed. She thought of Edmund's neat handwriting, his note already half-formed in some locked room of his mind. *Door forty-two opens both ways.*

But she also thought of Priya's face at three a.m., tear-streaked and serene, humming harmonies no one had taught her. Longing. Belonging. A seed on a boot.

"All right," Dana said. "Whole, and no questions about the ending. But one thing that isn't the ending. Yes or no, and you can refuse." She leaned forward. "Is the last person who read it aloud still alive?"

Agnes held her gaze for a long, long moment. The ocean filled the silence. Then she said, "Define alive," and picked up the teapot, and refilled both cups with steady hands, and that was the only answer Dana would ever get.

* * *

They sat through the second pot, as promised. Agnes talked about other things—her forty years teaching music in a schoolroom that smelled of chalk and radiator heat, the year she'd had to retire because she caught herself transposing a melody she'd never been taught, a melody that arrived fully formed in her head one February morning and would not leave until she'd written it down and burned the paper. "Eight bars," she said. "Of the lullaby. Just the lullaby, just the first eight, and even that much was like holding a lit match near a curtain. I burned it in the sink and I have not hummed since 1987. You'd be amazed how much of a life you can live without humming. You'd be amazed what you notice, listening instead."

She made Dana promise three things before she'd release the parcel. First: no one sings past the eighth line except by deliberate choice, alone, knowing the cost. Second: the page goes to the man who counts doors, because "he's the one being asked, and a debt should go to the debtor." Third—and here Agnes gripped Dana's wrist with a strength that surprised them both—"when it's done, whatever happens, you come back and sit through a pot of tea and tell me nothing at all. Just sit. Can you promise that?"

"I promise," Dana said, and meant it, and understood suddenly that this was the inheritance: not the page, but the vigil. Someone always left keeping watch. Someone always came back to report the silence.

At the door, coat on, parcel heavy in her bag like a paving stone, Dana paused. "Why 'the red hymn'? Ivo's copy isn't printed in red."

Agnes stood in her doorway with the ocean light behind her, small and straight and ancient.

"Because that's what Céleste called it after she read the letter," she said. "Not what it's printed in. What it costs. Red is what's given." She touched Dana's sleeve, once, lightly. "Go home, Miss Okafor. Count your days. And when the man with the maps makes his choice—make sure he makes it with the ninth line in his hand, not just the eight. A man should know the shape of the door before he walks through it."

The bus back inland didn't come for two hours. Dana spent them on the seawall with the parcel on her knees, watching the water, not opening the oilcloth. Twice she caught herself breathing in a rhythm she hadn't chosen—in for four, hold for two, out for four, hold for two—and twice she broke it deliberately, angrily, like shaking off a hand on her shoulder.

Her phone buzzed. Ivo. Then Edmund. Then Ivo again, three messages stacked:

**Alarms at the site. All of them.**
**City moved the demolition up. Pillar work starts tomorrow morning.**
**Where are you. How far out. Bring it fast.**

Dana stood up on the seawall with the wind pulling at her coat and the parcel pressed to her chest like a second heartbeat, and looked back once at the bone-colored house on the shore, where a small figure stood in the east window, watching her go, keeping the vigil already.

Seven days, she thought. Maybe six.

She started running for the bus stop.
