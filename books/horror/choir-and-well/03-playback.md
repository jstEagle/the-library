## 3. Playback

The recorder was a cheap thing, forty pounds of plastic and circuitry Yara had bought years ago for site inspections he'd never done. It had sat in his kit bag through three jobs and one marriage, and now it sat on the nightstand of the blue room at Hollowmere House, its red standby light pulsing like something breathing.

He told himself it was for the report. Subsidence documentation. Acoustic anomalies in older structures were a legitimate surveying concern — settling timbers, thermal expansion, water in the walls. He'd write it up in dry language. *Nocturnal percussive sounds consistent with pipe contraction.* He'd attach the file as evidence of diligence, not fear.

At 11:40 p.m., he set the device to record, checked the battery twice, and lay down in a bed that smelled of dust and lavender gone to rot.

The tapping started at some point past midnight. He knew because he surfaced from sleep just enough to hear it — three knocks, a pause, three knocks again — coming from inside the wall behind his headboard, patient as a clerk waiting for a signature. He did not open his eyes. He told himself: *pipes*. The word was a small warm stone he held against his chest until sleep took him back under.

* * *

Morning came grey through the shutters. Yara made coffee in the kitchen that still held six chairs around an empty table, though the census records listed only four residents the year the family vanished. He'd noted that discrepancy yesterday and filed it under *clerical error*, another stone in the wall he was building between himself and this house.

Then he carried the recorder to the table, connected it to his laptop, and pressed play.

His own voice first, muffled by the pillow: shifting, a cough, the creak of old springs. Then silence, long enough that he almost skipped forward — and then the tapping began, and he stopped skipping.

Three knocks. Pause. Three knocks.

Not pipes. Pipes didn't pause. Pipes didn't have *rhythm*.

Yara's hand hovered over the trackpad. He told himself: *the house settling, wood contracting in sequence, coincidence of pattern.* The stone was getting smaller. He kept listening.

At 1:17 a.m., his sleeping self mumbled something — he leaned in, turned the volume up, replayed it. It sounded like *"…don't know."*

And then a second voice answered him.

It came from everywhere in the recording at once, which should have been impossible — no microphone picked up sound like that, without direction, without distance, as if the voice were inside the room's own air. It was neither male nor female, or perhaps both, a voice like a well might sound if a well could speak: deep, resonant, with a wet echo at the bottom of every word.

*"What is your name?"*

His sleeping self stirred. "Yara," he said, thick with sleep. "Yara Blackwood."

"Yara," the voice repeated, tasting it. "Blackwood. Born?"

"March. March ninth."

"Mother's name?"

A long pause. Then his sleeping self, quieter: "Why are you asking me that?"

"What is your mother's name?"

"I don't—" A breath. "Elena."

Yara stopped the playback so hard the laptop skidded on the table.

The kitchen was very quiet. Six empty chairs. Grey light. His own heartbeat, loud and stupid in his ears.

He hadn't said their mother's name aloud in fifteen years. Not at the funeral — there hadn't been a funeral, not really, just a service Theo hadn't come to. Not since. The name lived in a sealed room in him, and he had just heard himself hand it over like a key to a stranger in the dark.

*You were asleep,* he told himself. *Sleep-talking. Confabulation. People answer questions in their sleep all the time; it doesn't mean anyone asked.*

But someone had asked. The voice was right there on the file, timestamped, waveform visible on his screen — a flat, dense band of sound beneath his own thin spikes, like bedrock under topsoil.

He made himself play the rest.

The interview went on for two hours. The voice asked about his childhood home — and his sleeping self described it, room by room, the green kitchen tiles, the crack in the hallway ceiling shaped like a river delta, the smell of his mother's cardigan, camphor and wool. Things Yara hadn't let himself think about in years, dredged up whole and offered freely, a man emptying his pockets to a mugger in a dream.

The voice asked dates. When his father left. When Elena stopped speaking above a whisper. When the boys stopped speaking to each other. And his sleeping self answered everything, honest as a deposition, while the red light burned on the nightstand like a small patient eye.

At 3:03 a.m., the voice changed its questions.

"You dream of her."

"Yes."

"What does she say?"

"She stands at a gate," his sleeping self murmured, and Yara felt the hair rise on his arms, because he did dream that, had dreamed it for weeks before he ever took this job, had assumed it was stress, guilt, the anniversary season. "An iron gate. She says—"

"Yes."

"She says the count isn't finished."

Silence on the recording. Long silence. Then the second voice, softer, almost tender, and somehow that was worse:

"Elena," it said. "Elena Blackwood. We have her here."

Yara ripped the headphones off.

The kitchen tilted. He gripped the table's edge and breathed, counted, did all the small mechanical things a body does when the mind refuses to keep driving. Outside, a crow argued with the wind in the dead garden. Inside, nothing moved, but he had the sudden animal certainty that the house was listening to him listen — that somewhere below, down the cold stairwell to the cellar he had not yet surveyed, something had just heard its own voice played back and was adjusting its approach.

Because that was what this was. He saw it now with terrible professional clarity, the way he could see load-bearing walls behind plaster. This was an *interview*. Two nights running — he checked the previous night's ambient noise, found the same triple knock, the same cadence — the house had been taking his statement while he slept. Names, dates, griefs. It was building a file on him the way he built files on houses. Appraisal. Assessment. *What is this property worth?*

Except he was the property.

"No," he said aloud, to the kitchen, to the house. "Whatever you think you're doing — no."

The lights flickered once. Or the cloud passed over the sun. He chose the sun. He was still choosing things; that was something.

He saved the file, then deleted it, then recovered it from the trash within thirty seconds, hands shaking, because destroying evidence was not in him, had never been in him. Evidence was how you survived. Evidence was how you'd survived his father, survived the silence after, survived fifteen years of explaining away every wrong thing until explaining was all he was.

*Fifteen years of explanations.* That was what Theo had called it, the last time they spoke, shouting through a phone line: *You can explain anything, Yara. One day you'll explain yourself out of your own life.*

He looked at the laptop. At the waveform lying there like a confession.

There was one more thing on the recording. He'd stopped playback at 3:04. The file ran to 6:12 a.m. He should stop. Every rational instinct said stop, seal it, send it to no one, leave this house today and invoice for travel only.

He pressed play.

For nearly three hours, nothing. His own breathing. The house ticking. Then, at 5:51 a.m., just before dawn, the second voice returned one last time — and it wasn't asking anymore.

"Five," it said, in the dark of the blue room, in the dark of the recording, in the dark of Yara's waking skull. "We are five short. But the moon is full in nineteen days, and the ledger is patient, and Elena's boy sleeps so *honestly*."

Then, faintly, from far below the floorboards — from the cellar, from the well he had measured yesterday at eleven feet deep with water at the bottom that reflected no light — came a sound he would spend the rest of this story trying to forget:

Knocking. Three knocks. A pause.

And then, muffled, wet, rising up through the earth like a voice through water:

*"Yara."*

Not a question. An answer. As if somewhere down there, something had finally gotten the name right.

The front gate clanged.

Yara froze, pen halfway to the intake ledger where he'd been compulsively logging timestamps. Through the grimy window he could see a figure standing at the iron gate — tall, dark-haired, wearing a rucksack and an expression Yara knew from mirrors and from fifteen years of deliberately forgotten photographs.

Theo.

Who should not be here. Who had never seen this house. Who was, even now, staring up at the windows with the look of a man arriving at a place he'd been dreaming of for months.

Yara's heart was doing something complicated — dread and hope fighting for the same cramped space — and beneath both, colder than either, the thought the house had planted in his sleep, sprouting now in daylight:

*Five short. And now there are two more bodies under this roof.*

He went to the door.
