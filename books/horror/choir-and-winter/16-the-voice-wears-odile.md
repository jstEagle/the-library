## 16. The Voice Wears Odile

It came back on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for inventory. Odile had done inventory on Tuesdays—Kestrel had the ledgers to prove it, columns of cartridge numbers and transmitter readings in handwriting like fence posts—and so when Kestrel came down the stairs at 2:40 in the morning with a clipboard under their arm and found the ON AIR light already burning, they knew before they reached the bottom step.

Nobody else was in the building. Nobody else had keys. The light burned anyway, steady and red as a held breath, and through the glass of Studio A, in the chair behind the broadcast desk, sat her grandmother.

Kestrel stopped with one hand on the door handle and made themselves look properly. This was rule-adjacent behavior: *count what is real before you count what isn't.* The figure was small, narrower than Kestrel remembered, wrapped in the gray cardigan with the burnt elbow that Odile had refused to replace for thirty years. Her hands were folded on the desk. Her reading glasses were pushed up into her white hair, exactly where she always lost them. The headphones sat around her neck, one earcup turned outward, listening to the room instead of the signal, which was something Odile did when she was waiting for someone to come off the phone line.

The console was live. The VU needles swayed gently, breathing with a voice too low for Kestrel to hear through the glass—a voice that was humming.

Kestrel's whole body went cold from the inside out, because Kestrel knew that hum. It was the tune Odile hummed while she soldered, while she washed dishes, while she waited out the last ten minutes of any broadcast: four notes up, two notes down, then a pause where a fifth note should have been, because Odile said every song needed a hole in it or you'd never hear the silence coming. Kestrel had spent their childhood falling asleep to that hole in the song. They had spent three years since the funeral trying not to hear it in doorways.

Through the glass, the thing wearing his grandmother's mouth hummed it perfectly. Hole and all.

*Don't go in,* said the part of Kestrel that had read the rulebook forty times. *Never answer the second voice.* But this wasn't the second voice, not exactly, not anymore—the second voice had been static shaped like people, had been Danny Fitch's warmth and Ruth's husband's laugh, approximations, costumes worn slightly wrong. This was different. This was precise. And precision, Kestrel understood with a sick lurch, meant it had finally gotten close enough to the original to stop needing a costume.

Or it meant there had never been a costume at all, and some part of Odile had been down here the whole time, waiting for her grandchild the way she'd waited every night for callers who were already dead.

Kestrel went in. Of course Kestrel went in. That was the trap of grief: it didn't lock doors, it opened them, and it stood aside politely while you walked through.

* * *

"Sit down, sweetheart, you're letting the cold in."

The voice was right. Not similar—right. The slight gravel on the second word, the way Odile swallowed the *t* in sweetheart until it was almost sweethear'. Kestrel's knees nearly quit on the way to the guest chair, the one bolted across the desk from the broadcast seat, the one Kestrel had sat in at nine years old with a juice box and a head full of questions about why Grandma talked to dead people on purpose.

"You're not her," Kestrel said. Their voice came out flat, procedural, the voice they used on callers who claimed impossible things. It was the only voice they trusted themselves to use.

"Mm." The thing adjusted the glasses down onto its nose and looked at Kestrel over the rims, and the look was Odile's look, the one that could find a lie in a sentence the way a magnet finds iron. "That's what I'd say too, if I were you. Smart child. Suspicious. You get that from me, incidentally. Not your mother's side. Your mother's side trusts too easily."

"Don't talk about my mother."

"See? Suspicious." It smiled, and the smile was warm and crooked and had the gap in it, the lower-left gap where Odile had never replaced a tooth because she said a face should show its history. "I'm not here to fight, Kes. I'm here to teach you how we really run the show."

"We," Kestrel said. "There's no we. There's me, and there's whatever's been living under the floorboards eating my callers' grief."

The thing sighed, and the sigh was so precisely Odile—impatient, fond, a little theatrical—that Kestrel's chest hurt with it. "You've been reading my rules like scripture," it said. "All those little commandments. Lights on. Doors counted. Never answer the second voice. Did it ever occur to you to ask who wrote them, and for whom?"

"For the next host," Kestrel said. "For protection."

"For continuity." It leaned forward, and the cardigan shifted, and underneath it the wrists were Odile's wrists, thin and blue-veined, the left one with the faded burn scar from the transmitter accident in '89. "Protection is a side effect, sweetheart. The rules are a recipe. You don't keep a fire from spreading by feeding it. You keep it fed so it stays where you put it. I ran this station for forty-one years and I never once kept that thing out. I kept it *here*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and nobody can teach it to you but me."

"And why would you want to teach me?"

"Because you're going to do it badly otherwise." The tenderness in its voice was unbearable. It was the exact tenderness Odile had used the night she'd told Kestrel about the will, hand on Kestrel's cheek, saying *you'll hate me for choosing you, and you'll thank me later, and both of those will be true.* "You're starving it, child. I can feel it getting hungry through the floor. Hungry things improvise. Fed things sleep. Come here. Let me show you the board the way I used to run it."

And God help them, Kestrel wanted to be shown. That was the horror of it, the thing they would spend the rest of the night refusing to name: sitting across the desk from that voice was like being nine years old again, when the world was small enough that one person's competence could hold it together. Kestrel had been alone in this building for three years, running a show that ate people's mourning, keeping rules written by a woman who'd died mid-broadcast with the mic still hot, and every night they'd wanted exactly this—one hour, one conversation, one person who understood what the job actually was.

The cruelest haunting is the one you want. Kestrel would write that down later, in the margin of the rulebook, in pencil, so it could be erased if they ever got brave enough to lie to themselves.

"One hour," Kestrel heard themselves say. "Then you leave the chair."

"Deal," said the thing wearing Odile, and reached over and flipped the monitor speakers on, and began to teach.

* * *

It taught well. That was the worst discovery of the hour: the instruction was good. Genuinely good. The thing explained the fader levels for a week-old caller—lower the highs, they haven't finished learning their own timbre; give them more low end, they're still mostly memory and memory lives below two hundred hertz. It showed Kestrel which phone line ran hottest when the entity beneath the floor stirred, and how to bleed that heat into the dummy load before it climbed the tower. It talked about attention the way Odile had talked about weather: a system moving through, best not to fight it, best to channel it, best to know its seasons.

"It doesn't want worship," the thing said, tapping the console with one fingernail, click click click, a habit Kestrel had forgotten until this moment and now would never forget again. "People get that wrong. It wants *witnessing*. Fear is just witnessing with the volume up. Grief is witnessing with the bass turned all the way down. You feed it either one and it settles. What you must never—" and here it paused, and looked at Kestrel with Odile's eyes, gray going green in the monitor light, "—what you must never do is let it witness *you*. Plainly. Without costume. Do you understand?"

"I understand," Kestrel said, and hated how much they sounded like a student, how much they sounded like family.

They talked about Marisol. The thing knew about Marisol—it knew everything Kestrel knew, which was a fact Kestrel filed away in the drawer marked LATER, DO NOT OPEN—and it said, gently, "The sister with the brother in the water. She'll come back Thursday. Don't lie to her next time. Lie well or don't lie at all; bad lies are a scent trail." And Kestrel nodded, and took notes, actual notes, in the margin of the inventory clipboard, and somewhere in the middle of writing *bad lies = scent trail* they realized their hand was shaking and they couldn't remember when it had started.

Forty minutes gone. Twenty left. The clock above the door ticked like it was counting down something other than minutes.

"Tell me about the night you died," Kestrel said. It came out before they could weigh it. "Tell me what happened in this room."

The thing went still. Even the hum stopped—not the four-note tune, but some deeper hum, the building's own, the transformer drone that lived in the walls. For three seconds the mask hung there, perfect, and Kestrel watched it the way you watch ice on a pond, knowing, hoping, dreading.

"Oh, sweetheart," it said softly. "You know what happened. I got tired. Forty-one years is a long time to hold a door shut with your body. I sat down in this chair at 4 a.m. and I thought, just five minutes, just rest my eyes, and it came up through the floor so gently, so *gently*, like a tide coming in under a sleeping swimmer. It didn't hurt me. It learned me. That's all death is, down here. Being learned completely." Its head tilted, and the cardigan slipped a little at the shoulder, and underneath the collar the skin was very smooth, too smooth, smooth as tape that's been played too many times. "It learned me the same way it's learning you."

"I'm not being learned."

"Aren't you?" Sweetly. So sweetly. "Who taught you the fader trick? Who taught you about the dummy load? Every hour you sit across from me, you're giving me more of yourself to wear. That's not a threat, child. That's just how love works, in this building."

"Ten minutes," Kestrel said. "You have ten minutes."

"I know." It smiled. "I'm the one keeping time. I'm always the one keeping time. That's what the show is, Kes. It's not a radio program. It's a clock. Somebody has to sit in the chair and mark the hours so the thing below knows where it is in the dark. Your grandmother marked them. Now you mark them. And someday—" the smile widened by one tooth, and the gap closed, and the gap closing was worse than anything, worse than the lights, worse than the thirteenth door, "—someday you'll get tired too, and you'll think, just five minutes, just rest my eyes—"

"Stop."

"—and it won't even hurt, I promise you, it never hurts, it just *learns*—"

"*Stop.*"

But it didn't stop, because it wasn't Odile, and Odile would have stopped, Odile always stopped when Kestrel's voice cracked like that, and the failure to stop was the tell, was the seam showing, was the zipper coming down the back of the whole beloved shape—

It leaned across the desk, close enough that Kestrel could smell it, and the smell was right too, bergamot and solder flux and the particular dust of old reel tape, and it said, in a voice gone soft as a lullaby, in a voice with nothing left of gravel in it:

"There you are. There's my frightened little host. Now—" and the eyes were not gray going green, they were not any color at all, they were the color of a dial between stations, "—tell me, sweetheart. Tell me truly, because we're past pretending now, we've had our lovely hour, you and I. What *are* you?"

The room went very quiet. The VU needles lay flat. Somewhere below the floorboards, something enormous rolled over in its sleep to listen.

Kestrel's mouth opened.

That was the terrible part, the part they would carry like a stone: the mouth opened on its own, eager, wanting to answer, wanting to be known by this thing that wore the voice of everyone they'd ever lost, wanting to say *I'm your grandchild, I'm the host, I'm the one who counts the doors, I'm lonely, I'm so lonely*—honest things queuing up behind the teeth like passengers at a door.

*I'm Kestrel. I'm—*

And underneath the name, rising, older, the name they'd buried, the birth name, the one from chapter eleven of their own life, the one the voice had already spoken once on air—

Kestrel bit down. Physically. Teeth meeting tongue, a bright coin of pain, and the pain was a rope thrown across a current, and they grabbed it.

They said nothing.

One second. Two. Five. Ten. The thing across the desk watched them with its colorless eyes and its almost-Odile face, waiting, patient as tape hiss, and Kestrel sat in the guest chair with blood in their mouth and gave it nothing—not fear, not grief, not honesty, not even a lie. Just silence, held the way you hold a door against wind, with your whole weight.

Then the clock above the door ticked past the hour, and the thing sighed—an empty sound now, a sound with no grandmother in it at all, a sound like air leaving a dress—and stood, and smoothed the cardigan, and became less with every second, the details dropping away in reverse order of how much Kestrel loved them: the gap, the glasses, the scar, the hands, until only the voice remained, hanging in the studio air like heat off asphalt.

"Clever," it said. "Almost. Next time I'll ask sooner."

The ON AIR light went out. The chair stood empty. On the desk, square in the center, sat Odile's reading glasses, which had not been there a moment ago, and which Kestrel did not touch, and did not move, and would not move for six days.

Kestrel sat alone in Studio A until dawn grayed the windows, holding their bleeding tongue, doing the arithmetic they'd been avoiding since the will was read. The thing hadn't broken in tonight. It had been *invited*—by one hour of conversation, by one nod, by one set of notes taken in a shaking hand. It didn't need to force doors. It only needed Kestrel to want what it offered.

Tomorrow, Kestrel decided, they would call every listener and tell them not to tune in.

Tonight, they went upstairs and wrote one line in the rulebook, in pencil, pressing hard enough to tear: *It will come as the person you miss most. Miss them anyway. Just don't answer.*

Below the floorboards, faint and patient, something began to hum. Four notes up, two notes down, and the hole where the fifth note should be.

Waiting for Kestrel to fill it.
