## 22. Someone Else's Quiet Hours

The station came with the property, the way a well comes with land—something old and useful that you didn't dig yourself.

Priya Chandrasekaran stood in the gravel lot at 9:14 on a Tuesday morning in late October, holding the realtor's keys in one hand and a paper cup of gas-station coffee in the other, and looked up at the building the way other people looked at cathedrms. It was squat and cinderblock and painted the color of oatmeal, but it had bones. A transmitter tower rose behind it, guy-wires singing faintly in the wind like someone plucking a very long instrument. The sign out front still read WKQH — THE QUIET HOURS, though the letters had faded to the shade of things remembered rather than seen.

"Most buyers want it demolished," the realtor said. She was a brisk woman named Halloran who wore her blazer like armor. "Tear it down, put up storage units. But you said—"

"I said I wanted it exactly as it is." Priya pushed through the front door before Halloran could produce the right key. The lock was stiff but surrendered. "Every wall. Every wire."

Inside smelled the way old buildings smell when they've been empty but not abandoned: dust and paper and the ghost of coffee, and underneath it something mineral and cool, like the air in a cellar. Priya breathed it in and felt her chest go loose. She'd spent eleven years in broadcast in three cities, climbing toward markets that never quite wanted her, and she'd spent the last two of those years quietly dying behind a morning-show desk in a city that talked over everything. When the inheritance from an aunt she'd barely known had come through—a modest sum, earmarked, unbelievably, for "the purchase of a small station"—she'd understood it as permission. Her aunt, whoever she had been, had apparently believed in second acts conducted in small rooms.

The main studio was intact. That was the first miracle. The console sat where consoles sit, all dials and faders and little amber lights, a beautiful fossil of analog engineering. Two broadcast chairs, one worn smooth at the arms, one nearly new. Foam panels on the walls gone slightly amber with age. And shelves—shelves everywhere—of reel-to-reel tapes, each labeled in handwriting that started confident and grew cramped.

"Halloran," Priya called, already drifting toward the shelves. "Who was the last host?"

The realtor consulted a folder. "An Oyelaran. Kestrel Oyelaran. Ran it for—hmm, looks like about four years? Before that, decades, some Odile Oyelaran. Family operation." She flipped a page. "Station's been dark almost two months. The owners—the corporate entity, Verity Broadcast Holdings—they were oddly eager to sell. Practically gave it away, once you made an offer. I assumed water damage or mold."

"No mold," Priya said, running a finger along a tape labeled *OCT 14 — MARISOL C.?* and finding nothing wrong with it at all. The cardboard was dry, the label crisp. Everything here was preserved the way things are preserved inside glass cases: deliberately, carefully, by someone who had cared.

She found the rulebook on the third day.

She'd been clearing out the back office—dust sheets off the furniture, windows cracked to let October move through—and pulled open a shallow drawer beneath the window expecting spare cables. Instead: a notebook, cloth-bound, its cover soft as suede from handling. On the first page, in the same confident hand as the tape labels:

*THE QUIET HOURS — HOUSE RULES.*

*1. Keep the lights on. All of them. Always.*

*2. Count the doors. There are twelve. If you count thirteen, do not open the thirteenth.*

*3. Never answer the second voice.*

Priya read the whole thing standing up, twice, smiling the way you smile at a dead relative's eccentricities. There were rules about mic discipline, about signing off before 5:59, about callers whose voices sounded "a week too smooth." There was a recipe for coffee that would keep a person awake without wrecking their hands. There was a page near the middle with a torn stub, and on the stub, in smaller writing, as if added later:

*...and if it asks you what you are—*

And then nothing. The sentence just stopped, amputated, the rest of it living somewhere Priya would never find it.

She photographed every page with her phone, more out of archivist instinct than anything else, and set the book on the desk where it belonged. Whoever this Oyelaran had been, they'd left in a hurry—or left meaning to come back—and Priya knew what it was to leave a room intending to return and never doing it. She decided she'd keep the book safe until the owner came for it, if they ever did. Meanwhile, she had work to do.

The FCC paperwork would take weeks. The license transfer, the tower inspection, the transmitter certification. But Priya Chandrasekaran had waited eleven years for a room of her own, and she was not a woman who wasted waiting time. By Friday she had the console powered up on generator current, cleaning contacts with isopropyl alcohol and cotton swabs, testing each channel, each pot, each fader slide. The board woke up under her hands section by section, lights blooming amber across its face like a town coming back on the grid.

It was, she thought, the happiest week of her adult life.

On Saturday night she stayed late. Past midnight, past one. The building around her settled into its nighttime register—cooling metal ticks, wind worrying the guy-wires, the refrigerator-sized hum of equipment kept warm against the cold. She'd brought a sleeping bag and a thermos and the honest intention of falling asleep in the studio chair, because she'd read somewhere that you should sleep in a house before you buy it, and this house was going to be hers in every way that mattered.

Around 2 a.m., idly, she patched herself into the monitor chain—not broadcasting, not transmitting, just listening to the room through the board's own ears—and began testing the phone hybrid channels. Seven lines. She dialed the test number for each one from her cell, heard the ring, heard the pickup, heard her own voice say *line four test* come back to her slightly delayed and slightly strange, the way your voice always sounds strange played back to you.

All seven lines tested clean.

She was reaching for the thermos when the first light came on.

Line two. The little amber lamp above the fader, glowing steady, the board's plain statement: *someone is calling.*

Priya looked at it. Looked at her cell phone—no call. Looked at the patch bay—all channels closed, nothing routed. The light burned on, patient and unbothered.

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty studio, "you're a haunted old girl, aren't you?" She said it fondly. Old equipment did strange things; phantom lights were practically a genre of folklore among engineers. She reached out and cleared the line the way you clear any stuck call, a flick of a switch, and the light went out.

Then line five lit up. Then line one. Then six, three, seven, four—each in turn, unhurried, like fingers walking across a piano.

Priya sat back down slowly. The studio was very quiet in the specific way rooms get quiet when they're listening back. She could hear the wind. She could hear her own pulse. She could hear, very faintly, from somewhere below the floor—beneath the studio, beneath the concrete slab, down where there should have been only earth and pipe and darkness—a sound like a room full of people breathing in unison.

She told herself it was the HVAC.

The lights cycled twice more, all seven lines, on and off, on and off, and then—this was the part she would remember later, the part she would describe to no one, because there was no one to describe it to—they all came on together and stayed on.

Seven calls. No callers. The board holding its breath.

Priya counted. She was good at counting; it was a broadcaster's tic, counting seconds of dead air, counting beats of intro music. She counted the way the rules in the drawer had evidently taught someone else to count, without knowing why.

At eleven seconds, every light went out at once.

The silence afterward had texture. It had weight. It pressed gently against the windows and lay down along the floorboards, and somewhere below, the breathing sound—breathing, or humming, or a choir warming up very far underground—slowed, and softened, and settled into something that was almost, but not quite, a snore.

Priya Chandrasekaran sat in the worn-smooth chair in the dark studio of the station she loved already, loved with the helpless love of a woman who had finally been given her room, and she did what broadcasters do with dead air.

She filled it.

"Okay," she said softly, to the console, to the tapes, to the drawer with the rules in it, to whatever was breathing under the floor. "Okay. I don't know what you are yet. But I'm going to find out."

Her voice sounded good in that room. It sounded, honestly, better than it had ever sounded anywhere. Warm. Close. Like a voice meant for this exact distance between mouth and microphone.

She worked until dawn. She inventoried the archive, alphabetized the reels, taped a fresh label on the rulebook drawer so she wouldn't lose it. At 5:40 she watched the sky through the studio's small east window go from black to iron to the pale bruised pink of first light, and she felt wonderful—wired, alive, certain. This was the place. This was the work. Whatever the Oyelarans had been doing here, they'd built something worth continuing, and Priya Chandrasekaran had never in her life walked away from a working console.

At 5:52, making her final checks, she noticed the ON AIR switch.

It was a heavy toggle, older than everything else in the building, its red lens dulled by forty thousand hours of fingertips. Someone had written on the masking tape beside it, in the cramped late-period handwriting from the tapes, two words:

*Don't. Please.*

Priya looked at it for a long moment. Then she smiled, peeled the tape off, rolled it into a little ball, and dropped it in the wastebasket, because she was not a woman who let other people's fears run her station.

Below the floor, very quietly, something turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to dream of her voice.

Outside, the sun came up on WKQH, and the tower hummed, and the building waited the way it had always waited—for the next hand, the next name, the next quiet hour to fill.

Priya reached for the switch.
