## 2. Nine

The man in the Late Room died at 5:47 in the morning, and Coral knew it before the monitors told her, because the counting stopped.

She had been sitting with him since four, charting his respirations by the light of a single lamp turned low, and all night he had murmured that word into the dark the way other men murmured prayers. *Nine.* Not distressed. Not delirious, even. He said it the way you'd say the name of a place you were homesick for, or a woman you'd loved badly and long. His lips barely moved. His eyes stayed shut. And at 5:47, between one breath and the breath that should have followed it, the word simply wasn't there anymore, and the room was so quiet Coral could hear the house settling around her like an animal turning over in its sleep.

She checked him the way she'd been trained to check anyone. Pupils, pulse, the stillness of the chest under her flattened palm. She noted the time. She called the number Verity Ashcombe had left on the whiteboard in the staff corridor — a landline, not a mobile, which should have told her something even then — and reported the death in the flat professional voice she kept for exactly this purpose, the voice that had carried her through eleven years of other people's last hours in other people's houses.

"Was he counting?" Verity asked.

Coral paused. "I'm sorry?"

"The word. Nine. Was he saying it when he went?"

The question was so precisely wrong that Coral's spine prickled. "He was sleeping, Mrs. Ashcombe. He passed in his sleep."

A silence on the line, long enough that Coral checked whether the call had dropped. Then: "Of course he was sleeping. They always are, at the end of it. Thank you, nurse. Leave the door closed, would you. He'll want the quiet."

*He'll want.* Not *he wanted.* Coral hung up and stood in the corridor with the phone still warm against her ear and told herself, firmly, that grief made people speak strangely, that old families had their own liturgies, that there was nothing in the world so ordinary as a woman who had said the same words over too many deathbeds and worn the grammar smooth.

The Late Room was at the back of Hollowmere House, past the orangery that hadn't held an orange tree in living memory, and its windows faced east. Coral drew the curtains against the dawn out of some instinct she couldn't name — it felt wrong, somehow, to let the light reach him now that the word had gone out of him — and in the dimness she caught her own shape in the tall mirror beside the door, a grey smudge of a woman with her hand still raised to the curtain ring.

She looked away before the reflection could do anything so presumptuous as look back.

* * *

By nine that morning, two other residents had begun to count.

Coral learned it the way she learned most things at Hollowmere: sideways, in fragments, from the small betrayals of routine. The medication round brought her first to Mr. Ellery in the Blue Room, a retired schoolmaster whose lungs were riddled and whose chart said he had weeks, perhaps, and who greeted her with a smile and then, as she was capping the syringe, said — conversationally, almost pleasantly — "Eight."

"Sorry?"

"Eight," he repeated, and tapped his temple. "It moved down. It was nine all yesterday evening, sitting up there like a wasp against the glass, and now it's eight. Lower. Closer in." He said it the way a man reports a change in the weather. "You'll want to note that, I expect. You're the note-taking sort. I can always tell."

Coral smiled the smile she kept for patients who'd drifted — warm, noncommittal, the smile that agreed with everything and committed to nothing — and finished her round, and wrote in the margin of his chart, in her own tidy hand: *Pt reports auditory phenomenon. Persistent. No distress.*

The second was Mrs. Okafor in the Rose Room, who had been at Hollowmere eleven days and whom Coral had not yet heard speak above a whisper. Coral found her sitting up in bed with her hands folded, staring at the wall opposite — at the mirror that hung there, oval, gilt-framed, one of the house's endless population of mirrors, in every room, every corridor, even the lavatories, even the linen cupboard, which Coral had thought was simply the eccentricity of a family with more money than sense.

"Seven," Mrs. Okafor said, without turning around.

Coral's pen stopped.

"It was eight at breakfast," the old woman said to the mirror, or to the room, or to no one. "It's going faster for some of us than for others. Did you know that? The schoolmaster's is dawdling. Mine has somewhere to be."

Coral made herself cross the room and take the old woman's wrist, professional, gentle. The pulse was steady. The skin was cool but not cold. Mrs. Okafor turned at last and looked up at her, and her eyes were clear — clearer than any dying woman's eyes had a right to be, lucid and dark and faintly amused, the eyes of someone watching a card trick and already bored of it.

"You're new," she said. "You still flinch. Give it a week."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," Mrs. Okafor agreed pleasantly. "You don't. Yet." She turned back to her mirror. "Six," she said, to no one, and Coral fled down the corridor with her pulse loud in her ears and her chart clutched to her chest like a shield, and in the washroom off the service stairs she stood over the sink and breathed until her hands stopped shaking, and did not look at the mirror above the basin, and was furious with herself for not looking, and looked anyway.

Only her own face. Grey-eyed, tired, thirty-four years old, a woman nobody had ever once mistaken for memorable. Nothing lagged. Nothing whispered. She gripped the porcelain and said, out loud, to the room: "It's morphine. It's end-of-life phenomena. It's terminal lucidity and auditory hallucination and it is *documented*," and the word *documented* came out with a desperation that embarrassed her, and she turned the tap on hard so she couldn't hear herself anymore.

* * *

She documented. That was the thing she could do, the thing she was for. Coral had learned long ago that the world made a kind of sense if you wrote it down fast enough, that a fact set down in ink in a neat hand was a fact that couldn't turn on you in the dark. So she documented.

By afternoon she had filled four pages of her own notebook — the private one, the one she kept in her uniform pocket, not the official charts — with times and names and word-for-word quotations. *05:47 — Mr. H—, Late Room. Counting ceased at death (last observed: "nine," 04:12). 09:20 — Mr. Ellery, Blue Room: "eight." Lucid, oriented ×3. 11:05 — Mrs. Okafor, Rose Room: "seven," then "six" within the hour. Rate of descent: accelerating, individual variation noted.*

She wrote it like a chart because charts didn't believe in anything. Charts recorded. Charts didn't notice that the numbers were going *down*, that the dead man had started at nine and the others had started at nine and were now falling past him, that the count was not a symptom but a — but a what? She made herself stop at the edge of the sentence. A symptom had a cause. A symptom could be treated. What Mrs. Okafor had was not a symptom of anything; it was a *schedule*.

She refused the word *countdown*. She wrote *descending numerical vocalization* instead, and the clinical phrase looked so absurd sitting there on the page, so small and pale against what it was trying to hold, that she almost laughed, alone in the nurses' station with the rain starting against the windows.

Because here was the thing she would not write down, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: the man in the Late Room had said *nine* at dusk, and died at dawn. Twelve hours. And Mrs. Okafor was at six by mid-afternoon of her first day counting, and if the interval kept halving —

No. She pressed the pen hard enough to dent the page. *If* was a pattern-seeking word. *If* was the word that had gotten her laughed out of two jobs already, the nurse who noticed things, the nurse who "read too much into" the bruises that were really falls, the staffing rosters that were really something else. She had learned what happened to women who noticed things. They got called difficult. They got called imaginative. They got their shifts cut until they quit, and the world went on not seeing them, which — she'd told herself for years — was a kind of safety.

Be invisible, she'd learned. Be useful. Chart the pulse, not the pattern.

She was still telling herself that at 4 p.m., when she looked up from the station desk and found Verity Ashcombe standing in the corridor watching her.

* * *

The matriarch of Hollowmere did not walk so much as arrive; Coral never heard her footsteps, only the fact of her, suddenly present, the way weather arrives. She was seventy-something and straight-backed, dressed in dark green wool, with her white hair pinned in a knot so severe it looked architectural, and her eyes were the pale grey of the sky over the house, and they were fixed on Coral with an attention that had weight to it, like a hand on the back of the neck.

"Nurse Oyelaran." She pronounced it correctly, which unsettled Coral more than mispronunciation would have. Few people got it right on a first attempt, and the ones who bothered to learn it usually wanted something. "How is Mrs. Okafor this afternoon?"

"Stable," Coral said. "Comfortable."

"Is she counting?"

The question, again. The same question, asked in the same tone — not morbid, not curious. *Procedural.* As if the count were a vital sign the family tracked, a number on a chart, and Verity Ashcombe its physician.

"She's saying numbers," Coral said carefully. "Some of our residents do, near the end. It's well documented in the literature."

"Is it." Verity's gaze didn't move. It was not hostile; that was the worst of it. It was *appraising* — the look of a woman checking a seam, a measurement, a fit. "And what does your literature say about the numbers going down?"

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere in the house a door closed, softly, the way doors closed at Hollowmere — without slamming, without echo, as though the house swallowed the sound whole.

"I chart what I observe," Coral said. "I don't interpret."

"No," Verity said, and something moved at the corner of her mouth that was not quite a smile. "I don't imagine you do. You're the invisible sort. The house likes that in its nurses." She turned, then, and paused, and said over her shoulder, without looking back: "Mrs. Okafor will want her window open tonight. See to it, would you. She'll want to hear the water."

And she was gone down the corridor, straight-backed, soundless, and Coral stood at the station desk with her heart going hard and her notebook under her hand, and understood two things with terrible clarity.

The first was that Verity Ashcombe knew exactly what the counting was, and had known for a long time, and asked about it the way you ask about a harvest.

The second was that *the house likes that in its nurses* was not a compliment. It was a job description. It was the reason Coral Oyelaran — unmemorable, unseen, a woman who had spent her whole life perfecting the art of not being noticed — was standing in this corridor at all.

She went back to her charting. Her pen moved; her hand was steady; the letters came out neat and clinical. *Descending numerical vocalization, three residents to date. No common diagnosis. No common medication. No common—*

She stopped. Because there *was* a common factor, and it wasn't the residents, and it wasn't the morphine, and it wasn't the literature.

It was the house. Three rooms, three deaths-in-progress, and the same murmur rising in each one like water finding its level — and every room in Hollowmere House had a mirror in it, and every mirror had been polished that morning by hands she'd never seen, and the man who had died at dawn had said *nine* at dusk with his eyes closed, and Coral had drawn the curtains against the dawn because it had felt wrong to let the light reach him.

She had felt it. She had *known* something was over, before the monitors knew, and she had written *passed in his sleep* to Verity Ashcombe in her flat professional voice, and the flat professional voice had lied, because she had known, and knowing was not charting, and charting was the armor, and the armor —

The armor had a crack in it. She could feel the draft coming through.

In the Rose Room, faint but distinct, carried down the corridor on the smell of rain and old roses, an old woman's voice said, clearly, conversationally, to no one and to the mirror and possibly to Coral:

"Five."

Coral looked down at her notebook. Under her last neat line of charting, without her having decided to write it, her pen had added four words in her own tidy hand:

*It knows we're counting.*
