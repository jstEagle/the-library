## 3. House Rules

The laminated sheet arrived on the second morning, slid beneath Coral's door like a summons she hadn't been charged with.

She found it at six-forty, when she stepped out into the corridor in her creased uniform and nearly trod it underfoot. It lay there face-up, glossy and pale as a fish belly, and for a moment she only looked at it — the way you look at something that has appeared where nothing was before, letting your mind catch up to your eyes. The corridor behind her was empty. The corridor ahead was empty. Somewhere below, the house's old pipes ticked and settled like a cooling animal.

She picked it up.

**HOLLOWMERE HOUSE — RESIDENT AND STAFF CONDUCT**

The heading was printed in a serif font, formal and faintly funereal, the kind of typeface used on memorial cards. Beneath it, seven rules, each numbered, each phrased with that particular gentleness institutions use when they mean *or else*:

1. Residents are to be treated with dignity and warmth at all times.
2. Staff are not to congregate in resident rooms.
3. **Mirrors are not to be uncovered or consulted after dark.** All hallway mirrors will be draped by housekeeping at 6 p.m. Staff must not remove drapes for any reason.
4. No visitors without prior written approval from Mrs. Ashcombe.
5. Staff are not to discuss residents' end-of-life vocalizations — including but not limited to counting behaviors — with other staff, residents, or family members.
6. Meals are taken in the kitchen. The dining room is reserved for family use.
7. Any concerns regarding the above should be raised directly with Mrs. Ashcombe, who is always available.

*Always available.* Coral read that twice. In three days she had seen Verity Ashcombe exactly four times, and each time the woman had seemed less like a person encountered than a condition of the building, like damp or draughts — present in a doorway, at the foot of the stairs, at the far end of the garden path, always dressed in that same deep green wool, always watching with an expression that wasn't quite welcome and wasn't quite suspicion but lived somewhere in the marrow between them.

Rule five was the one that made her skin prickle. *Counting behaviors.* So they knew. They had known long enough to laminate it, to find the softest possible word for it — *vocalizations*, as if the dying were birdsong — and print it on card stock and seal it in plastic so it could be wiped clean.

She turned the sheet over. The back was blank, but the lamination had trapped a small air bubble near the bottom corner, a silver blister no bigger than a thumbnail. She pressed it with her finger. It shifted, stubbornly, and would not pop.

"Housekeeping does the drapes," said a voice behind her.

Coral didn't jump, though it was close. She'd spent nine years in care work learning not to startle — patients hated it, families resented it, and once, in a dementia ward in Leeds, a man with a belt had taught her the hard way that a flinching nurse invites things. She turned instead, slowly, and found a man leaning against the wall by the linen cupboard.

He was maybe forty, tall and narrow, with the kind of face that seemed designed to be forgotten: brown hair going grey at the temples, brown eyes, a jaw shadowed with two days of stubble he couldn't quite be bothered with. His scrubs were navy and slightly too big. A lanyard hung around his neck with a photo ID so faded the face on it might have been anyone's, might have been his, might have been a smudge of weather on glass.

"You're the new one," he said. Not unkindly. "Coral."

"And you're—"

"Dev. Dev Anand." He pushed off the wall and offered a hand. His grip was dry and brief. "Night shift, mostly. Eleven years." He said the number the way people say a prison sentence they've stopped appealing.

"Eleven years." She let a little professional warmth into her voice, the kind she used with difficult relatives. "You must know this place better than the Ashcombes do."

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, submerged, gone. Like a fish turning under ice. "Nobody knows this place better than the Ashcombes," he said. "That's rather the point of them." He nodded at the laminated sheet in her hand. "Read rule three properly. It's the one people trip over."

"I've read it."

"Mm." He glanced down the corridor, both directions, a habit so worn it looked involuntary. "They'll tell you the drapes are for the residents' peace. Dying people don't like mirrors, they say. Bad feng shui. Old superstition." He smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes; it stayed around his mouth like frost on a window, decorative and thin. "It's not for the residents."

"For us, then."

"For whoever's looking." He said it lightly, tossed off, and immediately began walking away, down toward the stairwell, his trainers silent on the runner carpet. Over his shoulder: "Kitchen's through the green door if you want tea. I make it strong enough to strip varnish."

Coral stood a moment longer in the corridor. The morning light came through the landing window in a long amber bar and lay across the floorboards, and at the far end of the hall, the first of the day's mirror-drapes hung already over its frame — grey cloth, floor-length, shaped beneath like something standing very still.

She went to make tea.

* * *

The kitchen at Hollowmere House was the only room that felt like it belonged to the living. It was big and slightly shabby, with a Aga that ran too hot and windows fogged at the corners, and someone — Dev, presumably — had pinned a calendar of canal boats to the wall beside the fridge, years out of date, never turned. There were two mismatched armchairs by the radiator and a table scarred with rings from a hundred mugs. Someone had tried, over the years, to make this room a refuge, and the trying itself was visible everywhere, like fingerprints in dust.

Dev poured her tea from a battered brown pot without asking how she took it. It was, as promised, strong enough to strip paint. She drank it anyway, cradling the mug, feeling the heat soak through ceramic into her palms.

"So," she said. "Eleven years."

"Eleven years in March." He sat across from her, elbows on the table. Up close she could see the tiredness in him properly now — not the ordinary tiredness of night shifts but something structural, load-bearing tiredness, the kind that gets built into a person like rebar into concrete. "You're trial week, yes? Verity does that. Trial week, then the contract, then—" he waved a hand vaguely, "—settling in."

"That's what she keeps saying. Settle in permanently."

"She says it to everyone." He turned his mug a quarter-turn on the table, then another quarter, a slow rotational fidget. "The residents. The staff. The electrician who came about the boiler in — God, 2019? She told him to settle in permanently and he laughed, and she didn't."

Coral watched him over the rim of her mug. "And did you settle in?"

That fish-under-ice movement again, behind his eyes. "I stopped asking questions somewhere around year four," he said. "That's the closest thing to settling in this house offers."

There it was. The opening, wide as a barn door. Every instinct she had — the good ones, the ones about trust and timing, and the worse ones, the ones about loneliness reaching for company — leaned toward it. Three days she had been here. Three days of Mr. Ellery murmuring *nine* at dusk with his hands making small paddling motions above the blanket, of the dawn death that had come quiet as snowfall, of the two new voices that had started up yesterday afternoon — Mrs. Okonkwo in the blue room and sweet, confused Mr. Petch in the garden wing — each of them, independently, unmistakably, saying *eight.*

Three days of telling herself: morphine. Terminal restlessness. Hypoxia. The brain's filing system collapsing and spilling numbers instead of names. She had written it all in her own notebook in her own tidy clinical hand, and the writing had helped, the way a dam helps, which is to say temporarily and while the water keeps rising.

Rule five said she couldn't discuss it.

Rule five was sitting between them right now like a third person at the table.

"The man who died Monday," Coral said. "Ellery."

"Ronald." Dev's voice went flat and careful. "Yes."

"He was counting."

The mug stopped turning. Dev looked at her, and for a moment — just a moment — the quiet-eyed mask slipped, and underneath it she saw something that frightened her more than any ghost could have: recognition. He wasn't surprised. He was *relieved*, in some terrible exhausted way, the way a person is relieved when the pain finally gets a name.

"He was on four milligrams of morphine sulphate per hour," Dev said, reciting it like scripture, like a spell against listening to himself. "Terminal restlessness presents as repetitive vocalization. It's well documented. You'll see it in any hospice."

"We both know we won't."

"Coral." He said her name gently, the way you'd speak to someone standing too near a ledge. He glanced at the kitchen door — closed, but not locked; nothing in this house locked except, she was beginning to suspect, the things that mattered — and lowered his voice anyway, until it was barely above the hum of the Aga. "Rule five isn't a suggestion. It's not even really a rule. It's more like... a load-bearing wall. You don't knock on it to see if it's real."

"But it is real. The count. You've heard it."

"I've worked eleven years in end-of-life care." He stood, abruptly, and carried his mug to the sink, and stood there with his back to her, shoulders hunched, running the tap over nothing. "People say strange things at the end. That's all I'm prepared to say in this kitchen, which may have ears, in this house, which definitely does."

"Does it?" Coral kept her voice level. "Have ears?"

Dev turned the tap off. In the sudden quiet she could hear the house itself — that low, almost subliminal settling it did at all hours, timbers shifting, pipes ticking, as if the building were breathing shallowly in its sleep. Or pretending to sleep.

"There's a woman in the blue room," Dev said, still facing the sink. "Okonkwo. Adaeze. Lovely woman. Was a midwife forty years, delivered half the town. She started counting yesterday afternoon." His knuckles whitened on the sink edge. "When I went in at midnight she caught my hand — she's got a grip like a dockworker still — and she said, *'Nurse, tell me honestly, am I dying today or tomorrow?'* And I gave her the standard answer, the honest answer, the answer I've given a hundred times: *no one can say, Adaeze, we take it as it comes.*"

He turned around. His face in the kitchen's yellow light was grey as porridge.

"And she laughed at me. She laughed, and she said, *'You know perfectly well, because I know perfectly well, and we both know why we know.'*" He swallowed. "Then she asked me to open the curtains so she could see the garden, and I did, and she watched the garden for twenty minutes, and then she said *seven* — sorry, *said a number* — and went to sleep smiling."

The silence after that had texture. Coral sat in it and felt her armor — the skepticism she wore like a second uniform, starched and buttoned to the throat — feel, for the first time, less like protection than like costume.

"They altered Ellery's chart," she said quietly. "Did you know that?"

Dev went very still.

"Time of first vocalization. Someone's written it back a full day, in handwriting that looks like mine." She laughed, short and humourless. "My handwriting. I've been here seventy-two hours. My handwriting is barely established."

"Don't." The word came out cracked. Dev crossed back to the table but didn't sit; he stood over it, gripping the chair-back, and she saw that his hands were shaking, finely, continuously, like wire in wind. "Don't start keeping records, Coral. Don't start noticing. I'm begging you, and I don't beg."

"Why not? What happens to people who notice?"

He looked at her for a long time. Outside the fogged window, the morning was brightening, cold and clear, the kind of early-spring light that promises warmth it doesn't intend to deliver. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, through floors and plaster and a hundred years of settled dust, a voice began to murmur — rhythmic, patient, unhurried as a metronome — and Coral's whole body refused to assign it a number, refused, refused, and assigned it anyway.

*Eight.*

Mrs. Okonkwo, keeping her appointment.

Dev heard it too. She watched him hear it — watched the sound go through him like current through earth. And then she watched him do the most frightening thing she had yet witnessed in Hollowmere House, more frightening than any whisper or drape or watching woman in green wool:

He ignored it. Deliberately, practisedly, with the smooth expertise of long habit, he picked up the teapot, refilled her cup, and said, "Milk's in the blue jug, if you want it," as if the world contained nothing else worth mentioning.

"That's what happens," he said softly. "To people who notice. Eventually they learn to pour tea instead." He set the pot down. "Year four, for me. You're on day three. You're ahead of schedule."

* * *

She found Verity Ashcombe in the front hall at noon, adjusting one of the mirror-drapes.

The hall was the house's throat — wide, panelled in dark oak gone almost black, rising two storeys to a lantern skylight that dropped pale light onto the checkerboard floor. Mirrors lined it: eight of them, tall gilt-framed things, every one shrouded since six o'clock the previous evening in grey holland cloth. Verity stood before the nearest, on a small stepladder, tugging the drape's hem straight with both hands, and she did not turn around when Coral's footsteps crossed the tiles, though Coral would have bet anything the woman had heard her from the moment she left the kitchen.

"Mrs. Ashcombe. I wanted to ask about the conduct sheet."

"Rule three." Verity smoothed a wrinkle from the cloth. Her hands were elegant, ringed, unhurried. "Everyone asks about rule three."

"Actually it was rule five."

That got her. Verity descended the ladder — slowly, joints audibly protesting — and turned, and regarded Coral with those pale, patient eyes that seemed to hold a great deal of weather behind them. Up close she smelled of lavender and something older underneath, beeswax maybe, or stone.

"Rule five," Verity repeated. "The counting."

"The residents' vocalizations. I'm a nurse, Mrs. Ashcombe. Clinical documentation is half my job. If residents are exhibiting a shared symptom, I need to record onset times, frequency, associated—"

"You need to do your job," Verity agreed pleasantly. "And your job, Miss Oyelaran, is comfort. Not diagnosis. Not pattern." She came closer, and her voice softened into something almost maternal, which was somehow worse. "These are dying people. Whatever they say at the end belongs to them — their last words, their last privacy. We don't write it down. We don't compare notes over it like crows on a fence. We let them keep it." A small smile. "Can you understand that? Can you give them that?"

Every word was reasonable. Every word was kind. Coral stood in the beautiful dark hall with its eight shrouded mirrors and felt, with total clarity, that she was being handled — that kindness here was a tool with a specific shape, fitted precisely to the hand that used it.

"I can give them that," Coral said.

"Wonderful." Verity touched her briefly on the forearm, two fingers, light as a bird landing. "Settle in, Miss Oyelaran. Permanently, I hope."

She swept away toward the study, wool skirt whispering against the tiles, and Coral stood alone in the hall. The skylight had gone overcast while they talked; the light had thinned to a grey wash, and the shrouded mirrors stood along the walls like a row of covered furniture in a dead relative's house — or like witnesses with sheets over their heads, waiting.

On impulse she crossed to the nearest one and lifted the hem of the drape, just an inch, just enough to slide two fingers beneath the cloth and touch the cold gilt curve of the frame. Rule three. After dark, the sheet said. It was noon.

Beneath the cloth, the mirror showed her the hall: the tiles, the staircase, the grey light. Empty. Ordinary. Herself, holding up a corner of fabric, looking tired and unconvinced.

She let the drape fall.

But as she turned away — as she took the first step toward the stairs and the blue room and Mrs. Okonkwo's patient, smiling countdown — she could have sworn the mirror needed a moment longer than she did to finish letting go of her.
