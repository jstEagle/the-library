## 4. One Second Behind

The mirror in the staff bathroom had a crack in the corner that Coral had been avoiding with her eyes for three days.

It was a small thing, a hairline fracture running from the upper left bezel down toward the sink like a vein in a wrist, and she had decided—without ever admitting to herself that she'd decided it—that she simply did not look at that corner. She looked at the middle of the mirror, at her own face, at the tired brown eyes and the flat line of her mouth, and she did her job: checked her pupils, tucked a stray braid behind her ear, made sure there was no blood on her collar after turning Mr. Ellery in the night. Nurse's business. Nothing more.

Hollowmere House had too many mirrors. She'd noticed it on her first evening, standing in the entrance hall while Verity Ashcombe gave her the tour in that voice like poured cream, and she'd counted without meaning to—eleven mirrors on the ground floor alone, gilt-framed and silvered and dark as pond water, hung in places where no mirror needed to be. At the end of corridors. Facing stairwells. One in the linen closet, which was the kind of detail that should have been funny and instead sat in Coral's chest like a swallowed stone. Who needed to check their reflection while reaching for a folded sheet?

*Residents like to see themselves,* Verity had said, catching her look. *It's reassuring, near the end. To know you're still there.*

Coral had smiled the smile she used with difficult families and written nothing down, because there was nothing clinical to write. But the sentence had stayed with her the way a splinter stays, working deeper every time you forgot about it. *To know you're still there.* As if the alternative were common enough to guard against.

On the fourth morning, in the staff bathroom, washing her hands under water that ran cold no matter how long the tap heated, Coral raised her hand to push back her sleeve.

Her reflection did not.

She stood with her hand up, sleeve half-pushed, watching the woman in the glass stand with her hand down. It lasted less than a second—the length of a blink, the length of a misfiring nerve—and then the reflection caught up, sleeve sliding back, hand rising in its belated echo, and everything was ordinary again. Soap. Water. The cracked corner she didn't look at.

Coral dried her hands on the paper towel very slowly, one finger at a time, because if she moved fast she would run, and nurses do not run. Nurses assess. Nurses take vitals. Nurses write things down.

*You're overtired,* she told herself, meeting her own eyes in the glass. They looked back promptly, obediently, right on time. *You've been sleeping four hours a night since Mr. Ellery died saying numbers. You're seeing what grief and exhaustion build out of nothing.*

But Mr. Ellery hadn't been hers to grieve. That was the thing. She'd known him six days. And two other residents had started counting now—Mrs. Adeyemi at eight, old Mr. Pryce at seven—and they said their numbers at dusk, always dusk, in voices so calm it was worse than screaming, and Coral had documented all of it in her neat clinical hand because documenting was the only prayer she knew.

She looked at the mirror one more time. Her reflection looked back. On time. Innocent.

"Right," she said aloud, and her mouth in the glass said it with her, syllable for syllable, and she almost believed it.

Almost.

* * *

She didn't smash anything. That was the first decision she made, and she made it deliberately, sitting on the edge of her narrow bed in the staff wing with her hands pressed between her knees. Part of her wanted to—the animal part, the part that wanted to put her fist through every silvered surface in this house and hear them all come down like ice on a lake. Smash the hall mirror. Smash the one in the linen closet. Smash the tall oval one at the end of the east corridor that seemed to watch her walk past it twice a day.

But smashing was a confession. Smashing said *I believe*, and belief, in Coral Oyelaran's economy, was a currency she'd never been able to afford. Belief got you sent home from jobs. Belief got you pitied. Belief meant the world had won another round against the girl nobody looked at twice, and she had spent thirty-one years making herself proof against that.

So instead she did what she always did with a problem that frightened her. She got out her watch.

It was a cheap digital thing, a nurse's watch, the kind with a stopwatch function and a band you could wipe clean of everything. She'd bought it her first year of training and it had outlasted three phones and one relationship. She turned it over in her hands now, thumb finding the lap button by muscle memory, and thought: *fine. If my reflection is late, I'll time it. If it's not late, I'll have data proving I'm exhausted, and I'll ask Dev to swap me onto days for a week, and this will be over.*

Procedure. The terror went into the procedure like blood into a gauze pad, absorbed, contained, measurable.

That night, after lights-out, she stood in front of the long mirror in the east corridor with her watch held low against her thigh, and she waited until the house was quiet—not just quiet but *held*, the way Hollowmere got after midnight, as though the whole building were listening through its walls—and she began.

She raised her hand. Watched. Pressed the lap button when her hand rose; pressed it again when the reflection's hand rose.

0.4 seconds.

She frowned. Too fast. Could be her own reaction time muddying both presses. She tried again, slower, exaggerating the movement, lifting her arm like a conductor beginning a symphony.

0.6.

Again. 0.7. Again. 0.5. Again, again, again, twenty repetitions, her arm aching, the corridor cold around her, moonlight coming through the window at the far end and lying across the floorboards like something spilled. The lag varied—half a second, sometimes less, once nearly a full second—but it never vanished. Never once did the reflection move *with* her. Best case, it was only a little behind. Like a resident who couldn't quite keep up. Like someone following her down a corridor, always a few steps back.

Coral lowered her arm and stood breathing in the dark, and the woman in the mirror lowered hers a moment later, and they regarded each other across the gap like two people separated by glass and time.

"You're real," Coral whispered, testing, and watched her own mouth shape the words a beat after she felt them leave her lips. "I'm real," she said, and the reflection agreed too late to be believed.

She wrote the times down. Of course she wrote them down. Date, location, method, results, in her pocket notebook with the elastic band, beneath the entries for Mrs. Adeyemi's count and Mr. Pryce's count and the exact wording of rule three on Verity Ashcombe's laminated card. *No discussing the count.* She wasn't discussing it. She was *charting* it. There was a difference, and the difference was going to keep her sane or kill her, one of the two.

* * *

The next day she tested the other mirrors, because a good nurse doesn't chart one vital sign and call it an assessment.

The bathroom mirror: lag present, 0.3 to 0.8 seconds, worst in the morning. The small square one above the residents' sitting room fireplace: lag present, and here something else—when she timed it, she had the distinct sense that the reflection was looking slightly off to her left, at something beside her, though the room behind her held only a dying rubber plant and a doily-covered table. The oval mirror at the corridor's end: she saved it for last, and she saved it for last because she was afraid of it, and she noted that in the book too, honestly, because fear was data.

At the oval mirror the lag stretched to a full second and a half, and when she lifted her watch-hand, the reflected watch-face showed her a number before her own did. Not a different number. Just *first*. As if the glass were reading ahead in the book of her.

She stopped testing after that. She walked back to the nurses' station with her heart going hard and steady, the way it did during codes, and she found Dev there doing a medication count, his big careful hands sorting blister packs into rows.

Dev had worked at Hollowmere eleven years. He was forty-something and soft-spoken and he moved through the house like a man moving through a room where someone is sleeping—careful, apologetic, eyes down. On her second day he'd shown her which floorboard creaked outside Mr. Pryce's door and which kettle stuck, and he'd said, almost fondly, "You'll settle in," and Coral had thought: everyone in this house says some version of that sentence, and none of them mean the week.

"Dev." She kept her voice level, professional, pitched under the hum of the medicine fridge. "Question. Hypothetical."

He didn't look up from the blister packs. "Mm."

"If a patient reported that their reflection moved late—lagged behind them—how would you chart that?"

The sorting stopped. Just for a moment—a pill caught between his fingers, unmoving—and then resumed. "Perceptual disturbance," he said. "Note onset, frequency, any meds changes. Rule out neurological causes."

"And if the patient was me?"

Now he looked up. His eyes were the thing people didn't notice about Dev at first—they were quiet eyes, settled eyes, the eyes of a man who had seen something years ago and made his peace with it, or made his surrender, and Coral could never tell which. Eleven years. He'd handed her the laminated rules himself, day one, with a little shrug that said *not mine, just passing them on.*

"Coral." He said her name gently, the way you speak to someone standing too close to a ledge. "Some houses are old. Old glass isn't flat. Old silvering warps. Things look off in old mirrors. Everyone knows that."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the answer there is." He finished the count, closed the cabinet, locked it with a small definitive click. Then, softer, not looking at her: "Chart your patients. Chart your patients and go home at the end of your trial week, and don't chart yourself. This house—" A pause, long enough that the fridge hum filled it. "This house gives people back exactly what they bring to it. Bring it doubt, it leaves you alone. Bring it attention—" He shook his head, picked up his clipboard. "Bring it attention and it gets interested."

He walked away down the corridor, past the oval mirror, and Coral watched him pass his own reflection and thought—she would swear afterward she thought it, not imagined it—that his reflection kept walking a half-step after he'd stopped, and then remembered itself, and stopped too.

She wrote it down. *11:40 a.m. Subject D., reflection latency approx. 0.5 sec. Subject appears unaware or unconcerned.* And beneath it, because honesty was the whole point of the exercise: *If he's unaware after eleven years, he has chosen not to know. Why? What does knowing cost here?*

* * *

That night she dreamed of the entrance hall, all eleven mirrors lit by candlelight that came from no candles, and in each mirror stood a different version of her—at sixteen, at nine, at twenty-five in her graduation whites, at seven in her mother's church dress—and none of them were moving when she moved. They were all a beat behind, all of them, a whole gallery of Coral Oyelarans arriving late to their own life, and in the dream she understood that this was not a malfunction. This was the house's oldest courtesy. *To know you're still there,* Verity had said. The mirrors didn't show you yourself. They showed the house checking whether you were still there, and reporting back a half-second after it mattered.

She woke at 2:14 a.m. with her heart slamming and lay in the dark listening to Hollowmere breathe—or whatever the sound was, the deep structural sigh the house made at night, timbers settling or something settling that had never been timber—and she made herself get up and make tea, because lying there was worse.

The kitchen was empty. Dev's rota said nights, but his chair was vacant, his mug gone, and the house was so still she could hear the tick of the hallway clock three rooms away. She filled the kettle. Above the sink—of course there was a mirror above the sink, small, rectangular, framed in copper gone green with age—her reflection appeared as she approached, and she stopped, and she watched it arrive.

Late. Half a second, maybe less. Tired eyes. Braid coming loose. A woman holding nothing, wanting nothing, visible to no one anywhere except, apparently, to this house, which had hired her within a week of her applying, which had called her back *the same afternoon*, which had told her at the interview, in Verity's cream-poured voice, *we find that quiet people do well here, Miss Oyelaran. People who don't need to be seen.*

She had taken it as kindness. She had taken it as the first place that understood her.

The kettle began to rise toward its whistle, and in the mirror, Coral watched her own reflection open its mouth—just slightly, just barely, a fraction before she felt any impulse to open hers—as if it were about to say something. A warning. A number. Her name spoken backward.

Then her own breath fogged the glass, and the moment passed, and the reflection was only her again, late and tired and alone.

She made her tea. She carried it back down the corridor, and at the oval mirror she did not look, and she wrote one final line in the notebook by the light under the door:

*Day 4. Latency confirmed across all surfaces tested. Varies 0.3–1.5 sec. Increasing near large mirrors. Reflections occasionally anticipate. Note: anticipation is worse than lag. A lagging mirror is broken. An anticipating one is paying attention.*

She clicked the pen closed. Somewhere above her, faint and unhurried, in a voice she recognized as Mrs. Adeyemi's, a number drifted down through the floorboards:

"Six."

And Coral sat very still in the dark with her cooling tea, and understood that she had been timing the wrong thing all along. It wasn't her reflection that was falling behind.

It was the count, catching up.
