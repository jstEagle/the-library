## 7. Dev's Eleven Years

Dev Okafor had learned, over eleven years, to count the things that didn't count.

The seconds between the kettle clicking off and the water actually pouring. The breaths he took in the linen closet when the corridors went quiet and the house began to hum its low, patient note through the floorboards. The names of residents who had left Hollowmere — not died, *left* — which was a list so short it fit inside a single held breath: none. He counted these because counting was what you did instead of screaming, and screaming was against no rule Verity Ashcombe had ever laminated, but it was against every rule the house had written into his bones.

He was restocking the med cart at half past eleven when Coral found him. She came down the east corridor with her shoes making almost no sound — she moved like someone who'd spent a lifetime practicing not being noticed, and Dev recognized the technique because he'd invented it — and she set a folded piece of paper on the cart's steel shelf without a word.

He knew what it was before he looked. He'd known for three days, since the man in Room 4 said *nine* at dusk and was dead by dawn with his mouth still shaped around a number. Coral had been writing everything down. Dates, times, names, the countdown each resident carried like a fever. Dev had watched her do it with the particular horror of a man watching someone else pick up a live wire he'd dropped years ago.

"You've seen this," she said quietly. Not a question. Her eyes were steady and dark and very tired. "Eleven years, Dev. You've seen all of it."

The corridor light above them buzzed. Down the hall, one of the mirrors — they were everywhere in Hollowmere, hung in corners where mirrors had no business being, angled so that you could never quite see yourself whole — caught the two of them standing together and held them there a beat too long, the way a hand holds a moth.

"Not here," Dev said.

* * *

They ended up in the kitchen, because the kitchen was the only room in Hollowmere House that felt like it belonged to the living. Something about the steam, the clatter, the smell of over-steeped tea and yesterday's soup — the house's attention slid off kitchens the way it slid off nothing else. Dev had tested this theory for a decade. It was the closest thing he had to religion.

He made two cups of tea he didn't want, because making tea was also a thing you did instead of screaming, and sat across from Coral at the scarred oak table while the night pressed its face against the windows.

"Rule three," she said. "No discussing the count."

"I know what rule three is."

"You're breaking it anyway."

Dev turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table. Outside, wind moved through the cypress trees along the drive, and the sound they made was not quite the sound trees make; it was closer to breathing, slow and satisfied, the respiration of something enormous asleep but not deeply.

"There was a man," he said. "Edmund Hale. 2019. He was — " Dev searched for the word and hated every candidate. "He was *clear*. That's the word we used, back then, before I stopped using words like that. Clear-headed. He had emphysema and a bad heart and maybe eight months on any honest chart, and he was sharp as broken glass right up until the numbers started."

"Seven?" Coral asked. "Was he a seven?"

"He was a nine." Dev's mouth had gone dry around the memory. "Same as Mr. Okonkwo last week. Nine at dusk. And Edmund — he wasn't confused like the others get. He knew what the number was. He told me, plain as anything, *Dev, I am not ready to be finished here, and I don't mean dead.* And I believed him. God help me, I believed him, and I was younger then, and I still thought eleven years hadn't already happened to me."

Coral leaned forward. In the overhead light her face had the fine-drawn look of someone who had been invisible for so long that being seen now felt like sunburn. "What did you do?"

"What you're going to do." He met her eyes and made himself hold there. "I walked him out."

* * *

It had been a Tuesday in October, cold enough that the grass on the front lawn had gone silver and stiff. Dev had waited until the night staff rotation put him alone on the east wing, had signed out Edmund Hale for a "palliative drive" that existed on no form, and had gotten the old man into his own battered Citroën with a blanket over his knees and an oxygen tank wedged behind the passenger seat.

"Fifty yards," Dev said now, staring into his tea. "That's all it was. Fifty yards past the gate. You could still see the house behind you in the rearview mirror — I remember that, I remember thinking *don't look, Edmund, keep your eyes on the road ahead* — and he was laughing. Actually laughing. He said the air tasted different outside the walls. He said it tasted like 1971."

"And then?"

Dev set the cup down because his hands had begun to shake, and he was a nurse, and nurses do not shake, or rather they shake only in private, in kitchens, at midnight, across from women who are about to make his complicity their problem too.

"The car died. Not stalled — *died*. Engine, lights, radio, all of it, at once, like something pulled a plug. And the number started up. Not in Edmund's voice. In both our ears. Loud. Like it was coming up through the seats." He swallowed. "Four. It said four. Edmund had been down to six by then, and it said four, and it said it the way you'd say a name you were pleased to remember."

Coral had gone very still. "What did you do?"

"What could I do? I tried to carry him. He weighed nothing, he was all bird-bones by then, and I got him maybe ten more yards toward the road before my legs simply — stopped answering. Not weak. *Stopped*, like they'd been reassigned. And Edmund stopped struggling in my arms and went calm, horribly calm, and he patted my cheek the way you'd comfort a child having a nightmare, and he said, *It's all right, son. I remember now. I've always lived here.*"

The refrigerator cycled off. In the silence that followed, Dev could hear the house breathing through the walls, that low tidal sound, and he saw Coral hear it too — saw her head tilt a degree, cataloguing, filing, refusing.

"He walked back," Dev said. "I didn't carry him. I couldn't even stand. He walked back up the drive on his own feet, past me, through the gate, and the doors opened for him before he touched them. And I sat on the wet grass until sunrise because my body wouldn't take instructions anymore, and when I finally got back inside, Edmund was in his room, in bed, sleeping like a baby. And when he woke up—"

Dev stopped. There was a version of this story he told himself some nights, a version where the ending was merely sad. He made himself tell the true one.

"When he woke up, he didn't know me. Didn't know his own daughter's name — she'd visited every Sunday for six years. Didn't know why he was in a hospice at all. He'd lost the whole last year of himself, wiped clean, and—" Dev's voice snagged, and he pushed through it the way you push through a door you know is locked. "And his chart said he was sixty-nine. His *chart*, Coral. I checked his birthday against his ID against his NHS record. He was seventy. The house took a year off him like a fee. Like interest."

* * *

Coral sat with that for a long moment. Dev watched her do what she did — the clinical thing, the armor thing, the turning-terror-into-procedure thing that fooled nobody, least of all her. He'd done it himself for a decade and he knew exactly how heavy the armor got, and how little it stopped.

"So it let him go," she said finally, "and then it took him back and billed him."

"It doesn't let anyone go. That's the thing you won't hear yet." Dev leaned forward, and the words came out of him now in a rush, eleven years of them, pressurized. "You think you've found a pattern. Countdown, death, next resident starts counting. Very tidy. Very documentable. You've got your little pages, your times and dates, and you think if you can just *chart it correctly* you'll have power over it. I know, because I had the same notebook. Mine was blue. I burned it in 2016 and the smoke smelled like my mother's kitchen and I have never told anyone that until right now."

"Dev—"

"*Stop seeing patterns.*" It came out louder than he meant, and somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled, attentive. He lowered his voice to almost nothing. "Please. I'm begging you, and I have never begged anyone for anything in my life. The pattern isn't a pattern. It's a *hook*. The house shows you the pattern the way a fisherman shows a fish the fly. Every person who ever tried to understand it got kept. Edmund understood it and lost a year. Before him there was a doctor, Ruth Adeyemi, brilliant woman, kept a log for four months — she's not anywhere. Not dead. *Not anywhere.* I checked. There's no grave, no record, no family who remembers her face. The house doesn't kill you for looking. It *edits* you."

Coral's jaw tightened. He could see her doing the arithmetic she couldn't help doing: Ruth Adeyemi, Edmund Hale, the man in Room 4, the two new counters on the west wing. Filing. Charting. Refusing.

"You stayed," she said softly. "Eleven years. Why?"

Because he was a coward. Because the pay was good and the questions were few. Because somewhere in year three he'd realized the house had stopped noticing him — that mirrors held him loosely, that Verity Ashcombe looked through him, that he could walk the corridors at 3 a.m. and feel nothing but his own footsteps — and that invisibility, which had wounded him his whole life in the world outside, was here the only shelter on offer. Because staying unseen was a kind of survival, and he had gotten very good at it, and very tired, and the tiredness had calcified into a life.

"Because I stopped being worth taking," he said. "That's the trade they don't print on the rules sheet. You stay long enough, keep your head down, stop asking, stop counting, stop *seeing* — and the house loses interest in you. Eleven years, Coral. Do you know what I am here? Furniture. Friendly furniture. And furniture gets to grow old."

"That's not living."

"No." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "It's shelving. But it's *alive*, which is more than Ruth Adeyemi is, wherever she is, and more than Edmund would've been if I'd tried again. I could have run with him a second time. A third. I didn't. I signed his chart, I attended his funeral — oh yes, the house gave him a lovely service, Verity cried beautifully — and I told myself the lie you're currently telling yourself, which is that understanding the thing will save the next one."

Coral reached across the table and, before he could withdraw, laid her hand flat on top of his. Her palm was warm and dry and steady, and the touch went through Dev like current, because he could not remember the last time anyone at Hollowmere had touched him on purpose.

"I'm not going to stop seeing," she said. "I'm sorry. I know what it cost you to tell me this. But there's a woman on the west wing — Ada Voss — and she's counting too, and she's *lucid*, Dev, she knows exactly what the number means, and she asked me today, joking, except it wasn't a joke, to help her die somewhere else. And I said I'd think about it. And I've thought about it. And I'm not furniture."

Dev closed his eyes. Behind them he saw the October grass, silver and stiff, and the rearview mirror full of a house that watched its own gate, and Edmund Hale's hand patting his cheek, gentle as forgiveness, terrible as surrender.

"Then you'll need to know the rest," he heard himself say, and was astonished at his own voice, at the sound of a man stepping off a shelf after eleven years. "If you're going to try Ada Voss, you'll need to know what the house does with the ones it catches. And you'll need to know why it hired *you* — because it did hire you, Coral, it chose you out of every nurse in England, and it wasn't for your references."

Her hand stilled on his. "What do you mean?"

"The mirrors," Dev said. "Have you looked at yourself in them lately? Really looked? Timed it?"

The kitchen light flickered once, apologetic, and down the east corridor, in a mirror angled to catch the doorway, two people sat at a table holding hands — though only one of them was moving yet.
