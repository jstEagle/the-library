## 8. What the Mirrors Keep

Coral had learned to sleep in the shallow way nurses do, the sleep that keeps one ear open for a call bell or a change in breathing, and so it was no surprise when she woke at 2:47 a.m. with her heart already going. What surprised her was that nothing had woken her at all. No bell. No cry down the corridor. Just the house itself, pressing against her door like a patient standing too close.

She lay in the narrow staff bed and listened. The radiators ticked. Somewhere below, a clock she'd never been able to find counted its slow hours. And beneath all of it, faint as breath on glass, came the sound of the count — not spoken now but *hummed*, almost, by the walls themselves, as if the house had stopped needing mouths.

Three residents were left counting. Mrs. Ellery at four. Mr. Sable at three. Old Mr. Petch, who hadn't opened his eyes in nine days, at five — out of order, out of time, the house apparently willing to wait for him.

Coral got up because getting up was what she did instead of believing. She dressed in the dark, pinned her hair back, and told herself she was doing rounds. Rounds were procedure. Procedure was a wall you could stand behind while the world did its terrible arithmetic.

The corridor outside her room was wrong before she even reached the stairs.

* * *

She noticed it first in the tall mirror at the landing's turn — the one Verity Ashcombe dusted every morning with a cloth kept only for that purpose, the one Coral had privately named the Widow. Her reflection arrived late. Not the half-second lag she'd measured with her watch two nights ago, stopwatch-precise, charted like a vital sign. This was worse. This was a full second, maybe more, and the lag had changed character: it wasn't her reflection moving behind her anymore. It was moving *without* her.

She stood still and watched herself keep walking.

Only a step. Only a single stride forward before the image caught up, snapped into alignment like a bone going back into joint, and then it was just Coral again, tired woman in cheap scrubs, hand on the banister, telling herself about fatigue and low light and the tricks a mind plays at 3 a.m. But her pulse was in her throat, and her hands, when she looked at them, were steady in the way of hands that have decided to be steady later.

"Rounds," she said aloud, and her voice sounded small in the wide dark of the house.

She went down.

* * *

The ground floor at night was a different country. By day Hollowmere was all chintz and polish, Verity's flowers on every table, the smell of lemon wax laid over older smells like a bandage over a wound. At night the wax gave up. Underneath it the house smelled of cold stone and something vegetal, pond-water deep, the smell of the spring that had given the place its name and that Coral had never once heard running.

The mirrors were everywhere at this hour. She'd counted them her first day — forty-one, an absurd number for a private hospice — and at night they stopped being furniture and became windows, or eyes, or doors. She walked the long gallery with her penlight held low, and she did not look at them, and looking away from forty-one mirrors is its own kind of looking.

It was in the gallery that she first saw the past.

She didn't understand it at first. The mirror at the gallery's end — a great gilt-framed thing, taller than any guest the house would ever admit — showed the corridor behind her, as mirrors do. Except the corridor it showed was furnished differently. A sideboard stood where there was now a plant stand. The wallpaper climbed in a pattern of small blue birds that existed nowhere in the present house. And in the reflection, a woman in a grey dress stood at the sideboard, signing a document laid flat before her, her hand moving steadily, her face lit by lamplight that had no source in Coral's world.

Coral turned around. Empty corridor. Plant stand. Her own penlight trembling slightly in her grip.

She turned back. The woman was still signing. Now Coral could see the paper clearly, could see the lawyer's inkwell, the second man standing witness with his hat held to his chest. The woman signed, and set down the pen, and smiled — a small, finished smile, the smile of someone who has just done a thing that cannot be undone and has decided to be glad about it.

Then she looked up. Directly out of the mirror. Directly, Coral would have sworn, at her.

And Coral understood, with the calm that descends when terror exceeds some internal threshold and simply floods the machinery, that she was not looking at a reflection of anything. She was looking at a recording. The mirror was keeping what it saw, the way charts keep vitals, the way ledgers keep debts.

The woman in 1950-something — the dress said fifties, the haircut said fifties — faded like breath off glass, and another scene rose in her place.

* * *

Coral should have gone back to bed. She knew that the way you know a hot stove. Instead she pulled the little notebook from her pocket — the same notebook where she charted the counts, the lags, the deaths, all in her flattest clinical hand, because flatness was armor — and she began to walk the gallery mirror by mirror, watching the house confess.

In the second frame: a young man refusing. He stood in what was unmistakably this very gallery, decades ago, and he was backing away from a desk where papers waited, shaking his head, saying words Coral couldn't hear but whose shape she recognized from last week, from the lawyer's velvet voice: *just a formality, just residence, perpetual, it means nothing, sign.* The young man backed away until his shoulders met the wall, and then hands came into the frame — kind hands, unhurried hands, the hands of people who had done this many times — and they took him under the arms, gently, the way you move a resident who has become confused, and carried him out of the frame toward the front of the house.

Toward the door.

In the third frame, the front hall itself, seen in a mirror Coral passed every morning without a glance. A different decade — longer dresses, a gasolier. An old man being carried back through the front door, limp as laundry, his shoes still on his feet. Behind him came a woman in black who closed the door softly, as if not to wake him. As if he were sleeping. As if he'd only been out for a walk and had tired himself.

Coral wrote: *They come back. They always come back.* Her handwriting was not as flat as she wanted it to be.

In the fourth frame: a signing. In the fifth: a refusal, and the refusal ended the same way the other refusal had ended. In the sixth: a funeral in the garden, a small knot of mourners beside a hole, and — Coral leaned closer, breath fogging the glass — the mourners were all facing the wrong way. They faced the house. Every one of them. Their backs to the grave, their faces lifted to the windows, as if the burial were the formality and the house the deceased, or the opposite of deceased, or whatever a thing like Hollowmere was instead.

She moved down the gallery and the decades shuffled. Seventies now — she recognized the fashion, the wide collars, and her stomach tightened because Ada Voss had said 1974, had said it lightly, over cards, the way you mention an old address. In the seventies-mirror a young woman with Ada's exact stubborn jaw sat at the desk and did not sign, and the lawyer's smile stayed fixed while his eyes went cold as well-water, and the young woman stood, and walked out of the frame with her chin up, and Coral's heart went with her, and then —

Then the front-hall mirror again, and the young woman coming back through the door, supported between two nurses, weeping without sound, and the date — Coral could read it on a newspaper on the hall table, could read it plainly — was three days after she'd walked out.

*They always come back.*

Coral stood in the dark gallery with her notebook pressed to her chest and made herself look at the last thing, the thing she had been circling without circling, the question that had been waiting for her since the landing.

She went back along the frames. Signing, refusing, carrying, returning. Decades of residents, dozens of faces, the whole long congregation of the counted. She examined each scene the way she'd examine a wound, thoroughly, professionally, not flinching.

She was not in any of them.

That was not strange in itself — she was new; none of these years contained her. But that wasn't what she meant, and she knew it wasn't, and the knowing came up through her like cold water through floorboards.

She held her penlight up to the great gilt mirror, the Widow, and looked at herself in the present tense. There she was: tired face, pinned hair, penlight, notebook. Present. Accounted for.

Except.

She raised her left hand. The reflection raised its right, a beat late — a full second now, maybe more, the lag grown fat in the night hours. She lowered it. It followed. And in the space of that second, in the gap between the hand and its echo, Coral looked closely at her own face and saw what she had been refusing to see since the trial week began.

The reflection was not quite her. The proportions were hers. The expression was hers. But it was like a nurse's chart of a person rather than the person — accurate, complete, and somehow *administered*. Rendered. The eyes reflected light a fraction wrong, the way eyes in old photographs go wrong. And behind the rendered face, faint as a watermark, the gallery kept flickering — the blue-bird wallpaper, the sideboard, the woman signing — showing through her as though she were printed on thinner paper than everything else.

As though she were newer. As though she were *added*.

Coral thought about the interview. She thought about how Verity Ashcombe had barely asked her a single question about nursing — no scenario questions, no references chased, none of the usual gauntlet — and had instead watched her across the desk for a long, silent minute and then said, *You'll do. You settle in permanently, of course.* She thought about how the residents' eyes slid off her in the first days, how she'd had to introduce herself twice to people she'd already introduced herself to. She thought about Dev, eleven years here, quiet-eyed, who never appeared in the staff photographs in the front hall — she'd checked, she'd checked them all, and there were gaps in the groupings, spaces where a person might have stood, and she'd told herself it was rotation, turnover, ordinary churn.

She thought about rule three. *No discussing the count.* And why a house would need such a rule, unless the count worked better on people who didn't know they were inside it.

"No," she said to the mirror, quietly, the way you say no to a diagnosis.

The reflection said it too — a full second later, lips arriving at the word after the word had died in the air, and in that second of gap Coral saw the gallery behind her reflected-self empty, and then occupied, and then empty again, decades cycling through the space where she stood, and in none of those decades, in none of those rooms, in none of those gathered, grieved, carried, returned congregations, was there ever anyone who looked like her.

The house remembered everyone it had ever taken.

It had no memory of her at all.

Because she wasn't in the past. Because she had never been in the past. Because whatever she was, whatever Verity had seen across that desk and recognized and hired, had not been part of these rooms until the paperwork brought her in — and the mirrors, honest to a fault, kept only what truly belonged.

*I don't reflect right,* she thought, and the thought did not feel like horror. That was the worst of it. It felt like recognition. Like reading an old chart and finding your own name in it at last. All her life she had been easy to overlook — teachers losing her name, landlords losing her letters, lovers losing her entirely within a month — and she had built a whole self out of that invisibility, called it professionalism, called it independence, worn it like a coat that fit.

It wasn't a coat. It was a property. And Verity Ashcombe had hired the property.

Her watch said 3:14. Above her, faintly, muffled by floors and decency, Mr. Sable's voice said "three," and then, after a silence too short, said it again, softer, like a man practicing.

Coral clicked off her penlight. In the sudden dark the mirrors kept glowing anyway, faintly, holding their decades, and she stood among all that kept light with her notebook against her chest and understood the final thing, the thing that made her knees want to fold:

The house collected the dying. That was the bargain, that was the count, that was the ledger. But the house also needed hands. Someone to carry the trays, chart the pulses, hold the dying steady while the numbers ran down. Someone who wouldn't show up in the record. Someone who didn't reflect right, who could move through forty-one mirrors without disturbing a single image, who could be forgotten by every room she entered.

Someone invisible.

They hadn't hired a nurse. They'd hired a ghost who still breathed, to tend the ghosts who didn't.

And upstairs, in the east wing, Ada Voss — lucid, sharp-tongued, counting — was the last resident who had ever escaped this house, and the only one who might remember what Coral was, because Ada had seen 1974, and Ada, Coral suddenly knew with total certainty, had seen the nurses too.

Coral turned for the stairs. In the Widow's glass, her reflection stayed behind a moment longer than she did — standing in the empty gallery, head tilted, watching her go with an expression Coral had never worn in her life.

An expression of ownership.
