## 9. Verity's Grief

Verity Ashcombe woke before the house did.

This was not a figure of speech, though she had learned long ago that most people would take it as one. The house slept in its own way — a settling of beams, a hush in the pipes, the mirrors going dim and thoughtful behind their dust sheets of morning light — and she had spent sixty-one years learning its rhythms the way other women learned the moods of husbands. She rose at half past five, dressed in the dark without switching on a lamp, because lamps were for people who needed to see and Verity knew this house by touch, by breath, by the particular temperature of each corridor at each hour. The east passage ran cold until seven. The landing above the Late Room held its warmth like a cupped palm. And the nursery — though no child had slept there since 1974 — was always, always warm.

She stood in its doorway now, as she did every morning, with her hand flat against the doorframe the way you'd check a fevered brow.

"Good morning, my love," she said.

The room said nothing back. It never did, not in words. But the warmth pressed against her palm like a small animal leaning into a hand, and that was enough. That had to be enough. It had been enough for fifty years, and she was too old now, too tired, too far along the road to begin asking whether enough was actually the word for it.

On the mantel below the little window sat the photographs. Forty-one of them, arranged in strict chronological order, though only someone who knew the house would understand the ordering. They were not photographs of Lissie. There were hardly any of those left; grief had eaten them the way damp eats plaster, from the inside out, leaving the surface intact. No — these were photographs of residents. Mr. Callum with his spaniel, 1988. Mrs. Adeyemi laughing at something off-camera, 1996. The three sisters from Leeds who had arrived together and, in defiance of all probability, departed together within a single week, 2003. Each photograph had been fed to the house in its turn — carried up to the nursery, held against the warm wall, whispered over with a name and a date — and then returned to its place on the mantel, where it aged strangely, colors deepening rather than fading, faces growing more vivid as the people in them grew less so.

Verity straightened the frame at the end of the row. It was new. A man named Pell, admitted in February, dead nine days ago at dawn with the word *nine* still wet on his lips.

"Thank you," she told him quietly. "You were very helpful."

She meant it. That was the thing no one would ever understand about her, the thing Coral Oyelaran with her careful nurse's eyes and her invisible-woman walk would never grasp no matter how many ledgers she copied out in her small slanted hand: Verity meant it. Every kindness she performed in this house was real. The good china at teatime. The fresh flowers — cold spring flowers, snowdrops and hellebores, because the house liked what the season gave reluctantly. The way she sat with the dying and held their hands and did not look away when the counting started, not once in fifty years, not even the first time, when she had been twenty-two years old and her mother had pressed her fingers against the wall of the nursery and said, *now you'll understand what we keep here.*

Love and duty were not two things in Hollowmere House. They had never been two things anywhere, if you were honest. Every cruel act in history had been performed by someone who believed they were keeping something safe.

* * *

At six-thirty she made her rounds.

Not the nurses' rounds — those belonged to Dev, quiet-eyed Dev, eleven years faithful, and now to the new girl, the Oyelaran woman, who moved through the corridors like a draft through a keyhole. Verity had hired her precisely for that quality, though she'd have been hard put to explain it aloud. The house required witnesses sometimes. It required them the way a fire requires air: not constantly, but absolutely, or everything went out. A witness who reflected properly, who took up space in memory and mirror alike, would eventually be collected like the rest. But a woman who slid through rooms unnoticed, whose name people forgot mid-sentence, whose own mother had once introduced her at a wedding as *the other one* — such a woman could watch without being watched, could record without being recorded. The house tolerated her the way it tolerated mice.

Verity's rounds were different. She walked the house the way you'd walk a church before a service, touching things, righting things, feeding it.

In the drawing room she paused before the tall mirror between the windows — the great Ashcombe glass, brought over from the townhouse in 1899, its silvering gone the color of weak tea at the edges. She did not look at herself. That was rule one of her private liturgy: never your own face first. Instead she held up the small offering she'd brought from the study — a photograph of the garden taken in 1961, Lissie not yet born, her father alive and squinting into a sun that had seemed then inexhaustible — and she pressed it flat against the glass.

"For the keeping," she said. "Ashcombe blood, freely given."

The mirror took it. There was no other way to describe what happened, though she had spent decades trying to find a clinical vocabulary for it, if only to have something to say when the doctors came — and they always came, eventually, the psychiatrists with their gentle questions about bereavement and fixation. The glass warmed under her palm. The surface shivered, once, like the skin of milk. And when she drew the photograph away, the image on it had changed: the garden was emptier, the squinting man gone, the sun somehow less certain of itself. The picture had been *read*. Something on the far side of the silver had looked at 1961 and taken what it wanted.

She hung the altered photograph back in the corridor among the others, where the staff had long ago stopped noticing that some of the pictures didn't quite match their frames' histories.

The names were the other food. This was older than the photographs, older than her tenure, older than her grandmother's grandmother. The founding count of 1899 had established the terms — perpetual occupancy paid in residents, a household counted down and sealed — but a bargain, her mother had taught her, is only as strong as its maintenance. Names kept the ledger warm. So Verity spoke them daily, walking the corridors: the dead ones first, in order of departure, then the living, then — last, softest, in the doorway of the warm room — *Lissie. Lissie. Lissie.* Three times, because three was the house's number, because the count itself moved in threes when it was hungry.

People would call it madness. Let them. Madness was just love with nowhere left to go, pooling in the lowest room of the body.

* * *

At breakfast she watched the new girl across the table and thought about mercy.

Coral Oyelaran ate the way she did everything — efficiently, invisibly, angling her body so that she took up the least possible space at a table built for twelve. Verity had seen the type before. The house attracted them: the unregarded, the overlooked, people who had practiced being furniture so long they'd achieved it. Her own mother had called them *grey stock*, and had used them shamelessly, and had died unwept in the east bedroom while the count ticked down around her and not one grey soul in the house lifted a finger, because you cannot be mourned by people you trained not to see you.

Verity had sworn she would be different. She wondered, sometimes, washing the good china alone at night, whether different was a direction or just a slower route to the same place.

The girl was copying things. Of course she was. Verity had found the first traces days ago — a page torn from a nursing journal, folded wrong, left in the linen cupboard where the rules said nothing went. Ink impressions on a blotter. The particular silence of someone listening at doors, which is not the absence of sound but a texture of it, a held-breath quality in the air of a corridor. Verity could have stopped it. Could have confronted her, dismissed her, done any of the hundred decisive things her mother would have done by now, with the lawyer on speed-dial and the margin clause already drafted.

Instead she had let it run. Because there was a question Verity needed answered, and only a watcher could answer it, and the question was this:

*Does the count still work?*

Fifty years ago it had sung through the house like water through pipes. Nine down to zero, resident by resident, and at the bottom of every count, the nursery door had stood open and the warmth had risen and Verity — young, ruined, freshly bereaved — had stood in it and felt her daughter *near*. Not present. Near. Like heat felt through a wall. Like a voice heard through floorboards, words indistinguishable but cadence unmistakable. The count was the summons and the dying were the bellows and at the bottom of every descent, for one breathless moment, the wall between here and wherever-Lissie-was grew thin as paper.

That was why the papers mattered. That was why the family closed ranks around them like a wound. Every signature refused was a year the count faltered. Every resident who slipped away unsigned — as Ada Voss had slipped away in '74, clever, wicked Ada, faking her death and robbing the house of its final voice — thinned the summons. Verity had spent five decades patching the ritual with photographs and names and spoken counts of her own, whispering numbers into the warm room at night like a woman feeding a fire that wanted proper coal.

And lately — she would not say it aloud, not even to the mirrors — lately the warmth came later. The nearness came thinner. Last month, at the bottom of Mr. Pell's count, she had stood in the nursery with her arms open and felt — almost nothing. A draught. A suggestion of heat, like a memory of a memory.

Either the house was dying, or her daughter was moving further away, and both possibilities were unbearable, and only one of them could be fixed by paper and pressure and a lawyer's velvet voice.

So she watched the grey girl copy her notes, and she let her, and she told herself it was strategy.

It was mostly hope. Hope that somewhere in those stolen pages was an answer Verity hadn't tried. Fifty years of devotion and she was pinning the last of it on a woman she'd hired precisely because no one would ever notice her.

Her mother would have laughed. Verity buttered her toast and did not think about her mother.

* * *

In the afternoon she visited Ada Voss.

She brought shortbread and the newspaper and performed the whole liturgy of kindness, because Ada was due her kindness — Ada above all of them. The old woman sat by the window with her hands folded on the blanket like a retired judge, sharp-eyed, lucid, insufferable, and Verity loved her and hated her in exactly equal measure, which was the truest feeling in the house.

"You're doing your rounds face," Ada said, before Verity had fully settled into the chair. "I know the rounds face. Fifty years I've watched you lot come in with the rounds face."

"And how does my face differ from my other faces?"

"It's the one where you're deciding whether I'm coal or kindling." Ada's mouth twitched. "Sit properly, Verity. You hover like weather."

Verity sat. They talked about nothing for a while — the hellebores, the state of the lane, the obituaries, which Ada read aloud in a voice of savage delight, pronouncing judgment on the recently dead like a magistrate reviewing old cases. And underneath the talk, as always, ran the other conversation, the one conducted entirely in subtext, the one that had been running since 1974:

*I know what you are,* said Ada's eyes.
*I know what you cost us,* said Verity's.
*And yet here we both are.*

"Your new nurse," Ada said at last, casually, in the tone of a woman remarking on the weather. "The quiet one. She has kind hands."

"She has adequate hands."

"Mm. Kind hands and a way of standing in doorways like she's already halfway through them." Ada turned her head, and for a moment the retired-judge mask slipped and something older showed underneath — something frightened, and fierce about being frightened. "You should sign me out yourself, Verity. Save everyone the trouble. I'm eighty-nine. My affairs are in order. I've been in order since before you were born."

"The papers protect you, Ada."

"The papers *keep* me." The old woman's voice cracked like ice on a pond. "There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone in this county, because you've spent fifty years pretending the difference doesn't exist so you can go on sleeping at night in a house full of—"

"I sleep fine," Verity said, and stood, and gathered the tea things, and her hands were perfectly steady, because she had practiced steadiness the way other women practiced piano. At the door she paused. "The count will come for you soon, Ada. When it does, I'll sit with you. I'll hold your hand. I promise you that much, whatever else you believe about me."

"I believe you'll hold my hand," Ada said quietly. "That's the horror of you, girl. You'll mean it, too."

* * *

Night. The house at its most awake.

Verity climbed to the nursery with the day's harvest: two photographs, three names spoken aloud into the corridor, and a lock of hair from Mr. Pell's brush, saved from the undertaker's bag by a donation to the hospice fund that no one would ever audit. She laid them out on the warm sill in the dark, in the pattern her mother had taught her, and she began the private count — not the residents' count, which belonged to the dying, but her own, the maintenance count, the nightly winding of the clock.

"Lissie," she whispered. "Lissie. Lissie."

The warmth rose to meet her, and it was thinner tonight. Thinner than last month. Thinner than Christmas. She pressed both palms flat against the wall and felt it the way you feel a pulse going faint under your fingers — there, but retreating, like a swimmer deciding on the far shore.

"No," she said aloud, and her voice broke the room's hush like a stone through a window. "No, you stay *near*. Do you hear me? I have kept every term. I have fed it fifty years of names. I have given it—" her voice failed, recovered, went flat and terrible, "—I have given it everything it asked, and you do not get to move further away. Do you hear me? You do *not*."

The wall was silent. The wall was always silent. Somewhere below, faint as a held breath, she thought she heard a number — not from a resident's room, not yet, but from the house itself, from the joists and the silvered glass, a low syllable like the first drop of rain before a storm that intends to flood.

She went downstairs to check the Late Room, and found the night nurse's chart open on the desk, and beside it a page in unfamiliar handwriting — small, slanted, precise — recording times and whispers and a column of descending numbers.

The grey girl's notes. Left out. Either carelessly, or deliberately, the way you leave a door open for a cat.

Verity read the page twice by the light of the hall lamp. Then she carried it upstairs to her study, set it on the blotter beside the estate documents, and sat looking at the two of them together — the evidence and the instrument, the watching and the keeping — while outside, in the dark, the house began, very softly, to count.
