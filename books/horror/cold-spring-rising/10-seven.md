## 10. Seven

The house had a way of telling you things before it told you them. Coral had learned to read the signs the way she'd once read weather over the moors as a girl—the pressure dropping, the light going strange and green, the birds going quiet all at once. Hollowmere's version was subtler: the smell of lilies in rooms where no lilies grew, the clocks ticking fractionally out of sync with one another until the whole corridor hummed like a struck tuning fork, and now, on the morning after Dev disappeared from every roster and every memory but hers, seven place settings in the residents' dining room where there had been nine.

She counted them twice. Then a third time, standing in the doorway with her hands still cold from the outside air, watching the porcelain gleam under the chandelier like small pale faces turned up toward her.

Seven.

* * *

The two who died were Mr. Aldous Prynne and Mrs. Eileen Sutcliffe, and they died within four hours of each other, both of them mid-sentence.

Coral was with Aldous when it happened. He'd been drifting for days—seventy-eight years old, pancreatic cancer that had spread with the quiet efficiency of water finding its level—and he'd taken to talking in his sleep, murmuring fragments about his mother's kitchen, about a dog named Biscuit, about a wife Coral had never heard him mention while awake. At 2:14 in the morning he opened his eyes, looked directly at her with an expression of such sudden clarity that she leaned forward, pen already moving on her chart, and said:

"The thing about swans is—" and stopped. Not trailed off. Stopped, the way a clock stops, the way a held breath stops. His eyes stayed open. His chest kept rising and falling for another eleven seconds by Coral's watch, and then it didn't, and the room went so quiet she could hear the house itself settling around her, timbers shifting like something rolling over in its sleep.

She sat with him for a while. She closed his eyes. She wrote the time in the chart in her careful, upright hand—*2:14 a.m., resident expired peacefully, no distress observed*—and then she went down the corridor to check on Mrs. Sutcliffe, because that was procedure, because procedure was the raft she clung to, because if she stopped doing things in order she would start screaming and never stop.

Eileen Sutcliffe was sitting up in bed.

This was wrong in itself; Eileen hadn't sat up unassisted in nine days. She was ninety-one, hips gone to lace, heart failing by increments the cardiologist had described as "a matter of weeks, perhaps days." But there she was, propped against her pillows with her hands folded, looking at the window where the curtains moved though Coral had latched it shut at bedtime.

"Nurse," Eileen said. Her voice was strong. That was wrong too. "Did I ever tell you about my Thomas? He proposed to me on the pier at Blackpool, in the rain, and I said yes before he'd finished asking, because I'd known since—"

And she stopped.

Coral stood in the doorway holding Aldous's chart against her chest like a shield and watched Eileen Sutcliffe die with her sentence hanging in the air unfinished, and the count—that whisper that lived in the walls now, that had been getting louder for days, *nine, eight, eight, seven*—went silent for exactly three seconds, and then resumed, softer, satisfied, like something swallowing.

* * *

By dawn she had done everything procedure required. She had called the doctor on file—a Dr. Fenwick whose voice on the phone had a lagged quality, arriving half a beat after his words should have, though that might have been the line. She had laid the bodies out according to the house protocol, which involved drawing the curtains and placing a sprig of dried lavender on each pillow, a practice Coral had never seen written down anywhere but which Dev had shown her in her first week without ever explaining it. Dev. Whose name was not on any roster anymore. Whose room, when she'd passed it that morning, smelled of fresh paint.

She washed her hands. She made tea she didn't drink. And then, because she was Coral Oyelaran, who had survived twenty-nine years of being overlooked by turning herself into a documentarian of her own life—receipts kept, dates logged, photographs of parking spaces so no one could tell her she'd imagined where she left the car—she sat down in the nurses' station and began to audit.

The charts were in the cabinet behind the desk, alphabetical, bound in grey covers with the Ashcombe crest stamped in gold: a heron standing in shallow water, its neck curved into a shape that might have been a question mark or a hook.

Aldous Prynne's chart was third from the top.

She opened it to the night's entries and felt the floor tilt gently beneath her chair.

Her own handwriting looked back at her. Not similar handwriting—identical, the particular way her loops closed too tightly, the way her sevens were crossed with a flat European bar she'd picked up from a Belgian preceptor years ago, the slight rightward slant that got worse when she was tired. The entry read:

*2:09 a.m. — Resident agitated, speaking of swans. Reassured. Morphine 2.5mg SC administered per PRN order.*

*2:31 a.m. — Resident expired. Family notified.*

But Aldous had died at 2:14. She had written it herself. She had not given morphine—his pump was running continuous, she'd checked the rate twice. And she had certainly not notified any family, because Aldous Prynne had outlived everyone he'd ever loved and told her so, bitterly, cheerfully, only last Tuesday.

She turned the pages with fingers that did not feel like her own. Every entry for the past week had been rewritten. Dates shifted. Times adjusted. A note in her hand from three days ago described a fall she had never witnessed, a bruise on Aldous's hip that had never existed. The chart told a story of a man declining in an orderly, expected, medically unremarkable fashion, and every word of it was in her handwriting, and none of it had happened.

Eileen Sutcliffe's chart was worse. The final entry there read:

*3:47 a.m. — Resident found unresponsive. Resuscitation not attempted per advance directive. Time of death pronounced 3:52 a.m.*

Eileen had died at 2:19. Coral knew this the way she knew her own name. She had been standing in the doorway. She had heard the sentence stop.

Unless she hadn't. Unless she had dreamed the whole of it, the sitting up, the strong voice, the pier at Blackpool—unless her sleep-starved brain had composed a death scene out of whole cloth and inserted it into her memory between one round and the next. People did that. Nurses did that. She had read studies about it, the confabulations of the exhausted mind stitching plausible narratives over gaps in attention.

That was the thing about gaslighting done properly, she thought, staring at the grey cover with its golden heron. It didn't need to argue with you. It only needed to offer you a version of events more boring than the truth, and let your own doubt do the rest.

* * *

She did what she always did. She measured.

From the station drawer she took a fresh chart page, loose paper, nothing official, nothing the house had touched—or so she told herself, knowing even as she thought it how thin the comfort was. She wrote down everything: times, sentences, the three seconds of silence in the walls. She wrote *ALDOUS DIED MID-SENTENCE* and underlined it twice. She wrote *I DID NOT WRITE THOSE CHART ENTRIES* and underlined that twice as well, and then, because honesty was the one discipline she had never allowed herself to abandon, she added a third line in smaller letters:

*I think I did not write those chart entries.*

The pen hovered. She looked at the words and felt the vertigo open beneath her again, wide and patient. Because here was the trap's true architecture: either the house was rewriting reality, or she was losing her mind, and from inside her own skull there was no instrument fine enough to tell the difference. The mirrors lagged—but maybe she misjudged. The charts lied—but maybe she misremembered. Every piece of evidence for the haunting was also, perfectly, evidence for madness, and whoever had designed this place had understood that the second explanation would do all the work of the first.

Dev would have—

She stopped the thought with the pen pressed hard enough to dent the paper. Dev, who had worked here eleven years and warned her away from patterns. Dev, whose face she could now see only in the lagging mirrors, standing in doorways of rooms that weren't there yet, looking at her with an expression she couldn't afford to interpret.

There was a mirror in the station's corner, part of an old apothecary cabinet, its glass foxed at the edges like breath on a window. Coral looked into it and saw the nurses' station a half-second late: her own shoulders dipping as she exhaled, arriving after the exhale. In the glass, on the desk beside her actual elbow, lay a sheet of paper she wasn't writing on. Not yet. The reflection showed her hand moving, lines appearing, and she watched, cold spreading through her chest, as the lagged image finished writing something her real hand had not begun.

She couldn't read it from here. The reflection's script was too small, angled away.

She wrote one more line on her private page, pressing hard: *If the mirrors show me doing something, I will not do it. Whatever it costs.*

Behind her, faint as breath, the house whispered: *seven.*

It sounded almost fond.

* * *

Ada Voss was awake when Coral came in at the end of the shift, propped in her chair by the window with a blanket over her knees and her sharp grey eyes fixed on the garden, where the first crocuses were pushing up through frost-stiffened grass in colors too vivid for the season, purple like bruises, yellow like warning lights.

"Two," Ada said, before Coral could speak. "I heard them go. Both mid-sentence." She turned her head. "You look like a woman who's been auditing."

Coral set the morning meds tray down and said nothing, which was answer enough.

"They'll have fixed your charts by now," Ada said. "They always fix the charts. Nineteen seventy-four, they rewrote mine so neatly I nearly believed it myself—my transfer date moved, my diagnosis changed, three weeks of my life filed under someone else's name." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was the smile of a woman sharpening something. "That's the trick of this place, love. It doesn't lock the doors. It edits the evidence you'd need to prove the doors were ever locked. You start doubting your own hand, and once you doubt your own hand, you're halfway to doubting your own eyes, and once you doubt your own eyes—"

"You belong to it," Coral finished quietly.

"Full marks." Ada studied her for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her voice drop, lose its performance edge, go bare. "How many are left, Coral?"

"Seven residents. Including you."

"And the count says?"

Coral hesitated. Saying it aloud felt like feeding it. "*Seven,*" she said finally. "It's caught up. It used to run ahead of the deaths. Now it matches."

Ada nodded slowly, looking back out at the crocuses. "Then we're in the endgame, and it knows it." Her hand found Coral's wrist, grip surprisingly strong, dry papery fingers closing with intent. "Listen to me. Tonight, when you've slept—if you can sleep—you're going to want to burn those notes of yours. You're going to look at your own writing and think, *this proves nothing, this is just a madwoman's diary.* Don't. The doubt is theirs. The record is yours. Hold onto the difference."

"How do I know which is which?" Coral asked, and hated how young her voice sounded.

Ada's grip tightened. "Because they never write anything true about themselves. Check your notes against the house's versions. Wherever they disagree—" the old woman's eyes glittered, fierce and lucid and terrified all at once, "—wherever they disagree, believe yourself. Even when it costs you. *Especially* then."

Outside, the frost was burning off the grass, and the crocuses stood open in the strengthening light, and somewhere below them, in the walls, in the floors, in the space between one tick of the clock and the next, Hollowmere House counted its residents softly to itself, patient as roots.

*Seven,* it whispered. *Seven. Seven.*

And in the foxed glass of the apothecary mirror across the room, Coral saw herself write one more line on a page she was not holding—one more line, and then, distinctly, deliberately, a match being struck.
