## 11. The Gate Test

The pretext was a doctor's appointment.

Coral had spent two days building it the way she built everything now—carefully, clinically, with her watch in her left hand and her heart rate ignored like a chart she didn't want to read. Dr. Halloway's surgery in Marlow Staithe, twelve miles away, for a review of Ada's cardiac medication. She'd written it on the actual form, in her own handwriting, and then photographed the form so that when the charts were altered—and they would be altered; she'd found her own forged notes twice now—she would have proof she had once been a person who told the truth.

Verity Ashcombe signed the travel authorization without reading it. That was the first strange mercy. She sat in her study beneath the portrait of the dead daughter, pen moving, and said only: "Be back before dusk. The house doesn't like to count an empty bed."

"The house can count," Coral said, before she could stop herself.

Verity looked up. Her face was the face of a woman holding water in her cupped hands—patient, exhausted, certain it would all spill eventually. "Everything here counts, Miss Oyelaran. That is rather the point of it." She signed. "Take the east corridor. The mirrors in the west hall are being cleaned."

They were not being cleaned. Coral had checked at dawn. The west hall mirrors were being *fed*, if the small dishes of milk and honey and salt that Dev used to leave—before there was no Dev—meant anything. But she took the east corridor. You learned to take the east corridor the way you learned to take your pulse: not because you believed the numbers, but because arguing with them changed nothing.

Ada was waiting in the orangery, dressed in a wool coat the color of wet slate, her hands folded on her cane like a woman attending a wedding she disapproved of.

"You look terrible," Ada said.

"I've been sleeping badly."

"Everyone here sleeps badly. It's the house breathing through us." She stood, testing her weight, and Coral moved instinctively to steady her elbow. Ada shook her off—not unkindly. "Save the touching for when I earn it. Is the car real?"

"The car is real."

"And the driver?"

"Me."

"Even better." Ada looked out through the fogged glass of the orangery at the lawn, the ha-ha, the iron gates standing open at the end of the drive like a held breath. Sunlight lay across the grass in long pale bars. It was, by any honest measure, a beautiful morning—the kind of morning that made you distrust yourself for wanting to leave anywhere. "Twelve miles," Ada said. "Do you know how far that is, in steps? Neither do I. But I intend to find out the hard way."

* * *

The drive was gravel and larch trees and light.

Coral walked beside her at nurse's pace—slower than comfort, faster than pity—and Ada set the rhythm with her cane, click and drag, click and drag, each step a small argument against the ground. The house fell behind them in stages. First the façade went, its windows flashing as they passed out of their angles. Then the roofline. Then just the chimneys, three gray fingers over the trees, and finally nothing but the drive curving ahead through the larches, and the birdsong, ordinary and obscene, and the smell of cold spring earth thawing after a long winter.

Coral had not been past the front garden in nineteen days.

She hadn't realized that until this moment, walking, counting without meaning to count, and the realization opened in her chest like a window in a sealed room. There was a world. It had weather in it. A tractor droned somewhere beyond the wall, and a dog barked twice at something that wasn't them, and Coral felt her eyes sting and blamed the wind, which was barely a wind at all.

"Fifty yards," Ada said. "That's my estimate to the gate. My knees say forty. My knees are optimists."

"We'll take it in stages. Bench by the cedar, then the last stretch."

"There's a bench?"

"There's a bench."

"Then God is merciful and the NHS is not entirely useless." But Ada's voice had thinned slightly, stretched at the edges, and Coral glanced sideways and saw what she had been trained to see and trained herself never to see: the faint blue under the old woman's lips, the breath coming a half-second too fast for the pace they were keeping.

"It's the exertion," Coral said. "We'll rest."

"Mm," said Ada, who had survived 1974 by knowing exactly when a lie was being told to her and by whom.

* * *

The bench by the cedar was real, and they sat, and for four minutes the world behaved.

Ada talked about Marlow Staithe in the sixties—a man who sold ice cream from a van painted like a cow, a flood year when the high street ran silver, her second husband's terrible singing in the church choir. Coral let the words wash over her like warm water. This was the trick, she thought. Not running. Walking. Keeping the pace of human things, stories and benches and bad singing, until the house was a place behind them instead of a thing around them.

Then Ada stopped mid-sentence.

"—and he sang *Shall We Gather at the River* every single Christmas, flat as a—" She stopped. Her hand found Coral's wrist, not for steadiness. For anchoring. Her fingers were cold in a way fingers shouldn't be in April sunlight.

"Ada?"

"Do you hear it?" Ada whispered.

Coral did.

It came up through the gravel, through the roots of the cedars, through the soles of her shoes and the bones of her feet, a sound below sound, the way you feel a train before you hear one. A number. Not spoken. *Counted.* The way a metronome counts, if a metronome had a throat and patience.

*Seven.*

No—fainter, farther, a pulse rather than a word. But present. Here. Fifty yards from the front door and forty from the gate, in open air, under an honest sky, and the count had followed them out like a dog that knows the boundary of its yard and sits down precisely at the edge of it, watching.

"That's not possible," Coral said. Her voice sounded young and stupid to her own ears. "The residents hear it inside. In their rooms. At night. It doesn't—"

"It does whatever it likes," Ada said. "I told you. I've known since '74. I got to the gate that year too." She was breathing faster now, shallow sips of air, and her free hand pressed flat against her sternum as though holding a door shut. "There were four of us. We got as far as the gatepost. Margaret Ashcombe's own sister-in-law was one of us, if you can believe it. And the count came up out of the ground like water rising in a cellar, and Harold—he was the strongest of us, drove lorries his whole life—Harold couldn't get his legs to go past the post. Not wouldn't. *Couldn't.* Like the air turned to wool."

"Ada, listen to me. Your oxygen saturation—"

"Oh, hush with your saturation." But Ada gripped her wrist harder. "Listen. I need you to know what happens next, because it happens next, and I'd rather walk into it awake."

*Seven*, said the ground. Closer now. Or louder. Coral understood with a lurch that those might be the same thing—that distance, for this thing, might be measured in volume.

"What happens next," Ada said, "is I start drowning on dry land. And then some clever little voice in my head—it sounds like me, it always sounds like me—will explain that we've forgotten something. My glasses. My pills. My good scissors. Something small and reasonable. And my legs will believe it, because they're my legs, and I will turn around, and I will walk back to that house *on my own*, and everyone—including me, by supper time—will agree that's exactly what I wanted."

"No," Coral said.

"Yes," said Ada. "So while I still have the use of my own mind, I want you to do something for me."

"Anything."

"When I turn around—and I will turn around—I want you to let me."

* * *

Coral did not let her. Of course she did not let her. She was a nurse; letting people die of anything other than their own bodies was the one sin the profession had no absolution for. She got Ada's arm over her shoulders and took her weight and aimed them both at the gate, forty yards of gravel and light, and for eleven steps it worked.

On the twelfth step, Ada's breath failed.

Not shortened—*failed*, a full stop, as though someone had put a thumb over the valve of her. She sagged and Coral caught her, lowered her onto the gravel with her coat spread under her head, and knelt, and did the things hands do when the mind has gone white: checked the airway, tilted the chin, counted the pulse, which was there, fast and fluttering, a bird trapped in paper.

"Ada. Ada, breathe with me. In—"

Ada's mouth shaped the word *in*. No air came.

And the count rose around them like floodwater finding its level. Coral heard it everywhere now—in the gravel, in the cedar's needles, in her own chest where it borrowed her heartbeat as a drum. *Seven,* it said, patient as geology. *Se-ven.* And underneath it, woven into it so finely she couldn't separate the threads, another sound: her own name, or the shape of her own name, pronounced by something that had been practicing.

She hauled Ada upright. Dragged her three steps toward the gate. Ada's eyes were open and fixed on the iron bars with an expression Coral would carry for the rest of her life whatever length that turned out to be: not fear. *Longing.* The pure, animal longing of a creature that can see the open field and cannot reach it.

Thirty-five yards. Ada weighed nothing and everything. Twenty-nine. The count said *seven* again and this time it said it in Ada's own voice, cracked and dry, and Ada's lips moved with it, helplessly, a puppet discovering its strings.

Twenty-two yards. Coral's lungs were burning. She wasn't carrying the old woman anymore; she was dragging her, heels furrowing the gravel, and the house behind them had begun—to her peripheral vision, in that dishonest corner of the eye where the mirrors lived—to grow larger, nearer, the way a reflection grows when you lean toward it.

At twenty yards, Ada breathed.

A great tearing gasp, and then another, and Coral collapsed to her knees beside her, and for ten seconds there was nothing in the world but the ragged bellows of the old woman's chest and Coral's own pulse slamming in her ears.

Then Ada sat up. Straightened her coat. Took hold of her cane.

And stood, and turned, and began to walk back toward Hollowmere House.

* * *

"On my own," Ada said, without turning around. Her voice was level. Almost cheerful. It was the worst thing Coral had ever heard. "You see? On my own. Aren't you going to help me back, Nurse? All that way, and my heart playing up. Terribly irresponsible of you, taking me out."

"Ada—"

"Margaret's sister-in-law said the same thing, at the gatepost. *I'm turning back because I want to.* And she believed it by teatime." Ada's cane clicked. Click and drag. Click and drag. The rhythm of the whole nightmare, going the wrong direction. "Don't follow me past the bench, girl. It measures you too, you know. How far you'll go. It's measuring right now."

Coral stood on the gravel with the sun on her neck and the count fading beneath her feet like a tide going out, and watched Ada Voss walk back into the house that was eating her, upright, unhurried, almost jaunty, and the house's chimneys rose over the trees to meet her like three gray fingers closing.

At the bend in the drive, Ada stopped. Looked back. For one moment the level voice cracked and the woman inside it showed through, sharp-tongued and lucid and eighty-one years old and furious.

"Nineteen seventy-four," she called. "Ask Verity what happened to the girl who carried me that far. Ask her why there's no photograph."

Then she was gone around the curve, and the birds resumed, and the tractor droned beyond the wall, and Coral stood alone on the drive of Hollowmere House with her hands still curved around the ghost-weight of an old woman's ribs.

Fifty yards. She knew the number now. The house's reach, measured in steps: fifty yards of spring morning, and not one step more.

She turned toward the gate—toward the open world, the road, the twelve miles of ordinary country between her and anywhere else—and her legs carried her four steps before they stopped, gently, reasonably, as though she had simply remembered something.

Her keys. Her patients. Her shift.

She stood there a long moment, listening to the small sensible voice that sounded exactly like her, and understood that it was not measuring whether she would go back.

It already knew.

Behind her, up at the house, a window opened. Verity Ashcombe's voice came down the drive, mild as milk:

"Miss Oyelaran. Might I have a word about the ledger?"
