## 14. Six, Five

The count had learned to move faster.

That was the only way Coral could describe it—not that the deaths accelerated, though they had, but that the counting itself seemed to have grown impatient, the way a clock sounds louder the closer you lean toward it. On her fourth night at Hollowmere, the residents had numbered eleven. Now there were six. She had stopped writing the arithmetic in her notebook because the numbers did their own subtraction whether she recorded them or not.

Two had gone in a single night—Mr. Aldous Prynne and Mrs. Ede, both of them mid-sentence, both of them found with their lips still shaped around the word *five*. Dev had covered Mrs. Ede's face with the sheet and Coral had watched his hands shake, and neither of them had said anything, because saying anything was rule three and rule three was the only rule left that either of them pretended to keep.

The house had gone quiet in a way that was not peace. It was the quiet of a held breath.

Coral walked the east corridor at two in the morning with her torch off, because she had learned that light made the mirrors worse. Without it, they were only glass, only furniture, only the ordinary vanity of a Victorian house that had loved its own face too much. With it—well. With it, the lag had grown.

Two seconds, now. She had timed it again, because procedure was the last language she spoke fluently. She would raise her hand; her reflection would raise its hand two full seconds later, unhurried, like a dancer following music only it could hear. Three seconds, in the long gallery. She had measured that too, standing in the dark with her watch open in her palm, counting the gap between her body and its echo, and the gap had widened while she watched, stretching like something being pulled gently from a wound.

But the lag was not the worst of it anymore. The lag had only been the house learning to be late.

Now it was learning to be *early*.

* * *

She noticed it first with the small things. Her reflection reaching for a door handle a half-second before she did. Her reflection turning its head toward a sound she hadn't heard yet. Small courtesies, she told herself. Anticipation. The body knows its own habits; why shouldn't the mirror?

But Coral had spent forty-one years being overlooked—in care homes where supervisors called her by the last nurse's name, in hospital corridors where doctors spoke over her head as though she were a piece of equipment, in her mother's kitchen where her own announcements of her plans dissolved into the air like breath on glass. She knew what it was to be unseen. She knew, with the precision of the wounded, what it was to be *seen*—and her reflection had started looking at her.

Not with her. At her.

She stood in the staff bathroom at three in the morning, water running cold over her wrists, and watched the woman in the mirror watch her back. The lag was three seconds here. The woman in the glass moved three seconds behind her, faithful and delayed, and then—Coral's breath caught, a small mechanical failure in her chest—the woman in the glass stopped moving altogether and looked directly at her, eyes level, patient, waiting, while Coral's own body was still turned three-quarters away, still washing her hands, still not yet facing the glass.

The reflection was looking at her from a moment she had not yet reached.

She dried her hands. She did not run. Running was what the house wanted; she understood that now the way you understand a trap's shape only after you've stepped in it twice. She walked back down the corridor at a nurse's pace, measured and unhurried, and she did not look at the tall mirror by the stair, and the tall mirror by the stair did not look at her, and both of them were lying.

* * *

The residents were falling like leaves now, and the leaves were numbered.

Ada Voss was four. Coral had learned to think of them that way—the count descending through the house like water finding its level, each death pulling the number down a rung, and Ada was the fourth-from-last, which meant Ada was close, which meant the house was close, which meant Coral had perhaps days. Maybe hours. The lawyer, Mr. Quill, with his voice like velvet laid over a blade, was due back Thursday with what he called "the concluding instrument."

Ada, for her part, treated the whole business like a card game she was losing on purpose to annoy the dealer.

"Six, five," she said that morning, when Coral came in with her tea. Ada was propped against her pillows with her silver hair loose, looking like a queen in exile, which Coral supposed was exactly what she was. "That's where we are, isn't it? I can feel it in my teeth. Like a train you hear coming through the rails before you hear it proper." She sipped her tea. "Two more gone last night. Prynne and that chatty one, Ede. The house is hungry. It's always hungry in the spring."

"You knew it would come to this," Coral said. "In '74. You knew it counted down."

Ada's eyes were clear and terrible and kind. "I knew it counted. I didn't know it *hurried*. In my day it took its time. A year between numbers, sometimes two. The Ashcombes fed it slowly then." She set down her cup. "Something's changed. Something's made it greedy. Or something's made it *afraid*." She looked at Coral a long moment. "You've been in the mirrors, haven't you."

It wasn't a question. Coral's hands went still on the blanket she was smoothing.

"What did you see?"

"Nothing," Coral said. "Nothing yet."

"Ah," said Ada softly. "Nothing *yet*. That's the worst kind of nothing, girl. The mirrors here don't show you what was. They stopped doing that years ago. They show you what's *coming*. The house likes to watch us meet ourselves." She reached out and took Coral's wrist, her grip still strong, her pulse fluttering under Coral's fingers like a bird in a closed room. "When it shows you something—and it will—remember this: a prophecy is just a door left open. You don't have to walk through it. But you do have to see it coming to step around it. So look. Look hard. And then be somewhere else when you arrive."

* * *

She saw it that night.

The long gallery ran the length of the east wing, forty feet of dark oak panel and seven mirrors in gilt frames, and Coral walked it at 3 a.m. because she could not sleep, because the house had rearranged its silence around her, because somewhere below her a number was being spoken in a voice like dust settling.

The lag was three seconds. She had stopped measuring. Measurement had been a lie she told herself, a nurse's trick for converting terror into chartable data, and the data had stopped meaning anything the night the mirrors began arriving early.

She walked the gallery. Her reflection walked the gallery, three seconds behind, faithful.

Then, in the fifth mirror, her reflection stopped walking.

Coral kept walking—three steps, four—before her body caught up to the moment the glass had already shown, before she too was standing still, before she too was looking. But the reflection in the fifth mirror had not stopped to look at her. It was doing something else. It was holding out its hand.

And in its hand was a lit match.

The flame was small and steady and orange, and it lit the reflection's face from below, and the face was her face, and it was not afraid. That was the detail that undid her—not the fire, but the calm of it. The woman in the glass held the match the way Coral held a pen when she was about to sign something she had already decided on. With certainty. With the terrible peace of a finished decision.

The reflection looked at her. Its lips moved.

Three seconds later—her time, her body, her breath—Coral's own lips moved, and she heard herself say, in the dark of the empty gallery, the words the glass had already shaped:

"*It has to be me.*"

She stood there until the match in the mirror guttered and went out, though her own hands were empty and cold, though there was no fire anywhere in the gallery except the one that hadn't happened yet. Then she walked back to her room, and she did not sleep, and she did not cry, because crying was a thing you did when you still believed the future was unwritten, and she had just watched it being written in her own handwriting on the inside of a mirror.

* * *

The charts were wrong again.

She found it at dawn, doing her rounds, the ritual that kept her sane by keeping her busy. Mrs. Ede's chart—the dead woman's chart, the one Coral had closed out herself two nights ago—lay open on the nurses' station desk, and in Coral's own slanted handwriting, in ink Coral did not own, were notes she had never written.

*Patient agitated. Count audible. Spoke of a match.*

Coral read it three times. Her hands did not shake; she was past shaking; shaking was a luxury of the first week. She checked the other charts. Prynne's: *declined evening meal, asked about fire.* Dev's handwriting on the medication log had been overwritten in her own hand, dated tomorrow. Tomorrow. The log was dated tomorrow, and it was signed with her initials, C.O., and it recorded a dose she had not yet given, at an hour that had not yet come, to a patient whose name she did not recognize.

*V. Ashcombe, 4 a.m., morphine sulphate, as discussed.*

She stood in the grey light of the nurses' station holding a document from a day that hadn't happened, in her handwriting, with her initials, and she understood at last that the house was not gaslighting her. Gaslighting was a human cruelty, a small domestic evil, a way of making someone doubt their memory. This was worse. This was the house *editing forward*—writing her future in her own hand so that when she arrived at it, it would feel like memory, like inevitability, like something she had always been going to do. The match. The morphine. The margin of the papers. The house was not showing her a prophecy.

It was drafting one. And it was using her pen.

She burned the page in the kitchen sink—actually burned it, a real match from the box by the stove, a small human flame that obeyed her—and watched the paper curl, and the smoke rose straight up in the still air, and in the dark window above the sink her reflection watched the burning too, three seconds late, and its face was not calm at all. Its face was furious.

* * *

Dev found her there at six, sitting on the kitchen floor with her back against the cupboards and the ash still in the sink.

He looked terrible. Eleven years at Hollowmere had worn him down to a quiet-eyed ghost of the man he must have been, but now there was something new under the quiet, something that looked almost like panic wearing a nurse's uniform. He crouched down beside her, not touching, the way you approach a spooked animal.

"You've seen it," he said. "The mirrors. The forward ones."

"You knew." Her voice came out flat. "You've been here eleven years. You knew they show ahead."

"I knew they showed *wrong*." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Wrong I could live with. Wrong is just broken glass. But the last month—" He stopped. Started again. "Coral, I found a roster this morning. The duty roster for next week. My name's on it. Except it isn't. It's been taken off. Every day, taken off, like someone's erasing me one shift at a time." His laugh was terrible, small, involuntary. "And I checked the mirrors to see if I was still—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Coral had seen him in the glass that morning, three seconds behind, and he had been there, and that was somehow the most frightening thing of all—that the house kept his reflection even as it deleted his name, that it was preserving him in the medium where it kept everything it intended to collect.

"Dev." She said his name and he looked at her, and she saw how much he needed her to say something true, something that was not the house's. "The mirrors showed me a match. In my hand. Lit. And I wasn't afraid in it. I was *certain*. And this morning I found a medication log in my handwriting, dated tomorrow, for Verity Ashcombe." She watched the words land. "The house isn't predicting what I'll do. It's *planning* it. And it's planning me as the one who ends the count."

Dev was silent for a long moment. The kitchen clock ticked, honest and stupid, counting nothing.

"Then we have to be faster than your reflection," he said.

* * *

They spent the day doing the thing Coral was beginning to understand was the only real rebellion left: they paid attention. To the residents. To the count. To each other. Attention, it seemed, was the one currency the house could not mint.

Five residents now. Then, at dusk, while the light went amber and then bruise-colored over the gardens, old Mr. Fenwick in the blue room stopped mid-prayer and said *five* and did not say anything after, and the number fell to four, and Coral stood in the corridor outside his door and felt the house settle around the loss like a body around a swallowed meal.

Four residents. Ada. A man named Okonkwo who no longer spoke at all. A woman, Mrs. Halloway, who sang hymns to drown out the counting and was beginning to lose. And one more, a small silent man in the west room whom Coral had never heard say a word, who only looked at the ceiling with his mouth slightly open, as though listening to something being read to him.

Three, then. Three residents, and the count at five, and the gap between the numbers closing like a hand closing.

Coral stood at the long gallery window as full dark came down, and in the black glass—window now, not mirror, though the house did not much distinguish—she watched her reflection raise its hand.

She had not raised hers.

In the glass, the woman who was her held up the match again, lit, steady, and behind her reflection—behind *her*, in the reflected corridor—the doors of the house stood open, all of them, every door down every hallway, opening outward, opening toward the gates, and the reflection smiled.

Coral turned and looked down the real corridor behind her. Every door was shut. Every door was locked.

Three seconds later, in the window, the reflection turned too, and looked at the shut doors, and its smile did not change at all.

It knew something she didn't. Or worse—it knew something she *would*.
