## 17. Zero Hour Papers

The lawyer came back on a Thursday, though by then the days had stopped meaning much at Hollowmere. Coral only knew it was Thursday because the calendar in the kitchen still said so, and because Dev's handwriting used to change the date every morning at seven, and now no one did, and the calendar had begun to feel like a lie told politely.

He arrived without a car sound. That was the first wrong thing. She heard the front bell—two chimes, patient as a metronome—and looked out the tall window beside the stairwell to see who had come, and there was no car on the gravel, no tire prints in the soft ground from the night's rain. Only Mr. Pell himself, standing on the step with his leather case held in both hands like something he'd been asked to carry for someone else. He was a narrow man, gray as ash paper, with the kind of face that seemed designed to be forgotten: features arranged in averages, nothing to hold onto. Coral had met him twice before and could not have described him five minutes after either time.

She opened the door because Ada was asleep and because refusing him felt like giving him information.

"Miss Oyelaran." He inclined his head. "I wonder if I might have a quarter hour of your time."

"You'll want Verity for that."

"Mrs. Ashcombe has already given me my instructions." A pause, delicate as a scalpel laid down on cloth. "It's you I've come to see."

The hall behind her breathed its usual breath—that faint mineral coolness, like air off a cellar floor, like the inside of a mouth. Coral stood in the doorway and let the silence do her work for her. She had learned that much here. Silence made people fill it, and what they filled it with was always the truth they'd been carrying.

Pell filled his with procedure. "There is a final instrument," he said. "Regarding the estate. It requires the presence of the last resident, one witness of staff, and—" he consulted nothing, remembered everything, "—yourself."

"Me."

"The document names you."

* * *

Ada was awake when Coral reached her room, sitting up against her pillows with her silver hair loose and her hands folded on the blanket like a woman waiting for a bus she intended to miss out of spite. The count had been quiet all morning. Four residents left now, and the house had gone strange with it—the mirrors lagging three full seconds, sometimes four, showing Coral corridors she hadn't walked yet, doors opening ahead of her hand. She had stopped looking at them except sideways, the way you look at a dog you don't trust.

"They've come for me, then." Ada said it before Coral spoke. "Pell's shoes. I know the squeak of them. Forty years I've known that squeak—he was a junior associate when they first tried to bury me in paper." Her eyes were bright and hard, river stones. "What does he want?"

"He says there's a final document. It names me."

Something moved through Ada's face—not surprise. Recognition, old and tired, like a scar aching before rain. "Then we'd best hear it read aloud," she said. "In daylight. In front of witnesses. They hate that. Paper likes the dark; it does its worst work where nobody's watching the pen."

* * *

They gathered in the morning room at two o'clock, because Verity chose two o'clock, and Verity still chose things in this house even as the house chose everything else. The light came low and gold through the west windows and lay across the long table like something spilled. Three residents remained besides Ada—old Mr. Halloway, who slept upright in his chair; the twins' mother, Mrs. Ferro, whose countdown had been running so softly for two days that Coral had to put her ear close to catch it, a whisper under a whisper, *four, four, four*; and a man named Okafor who no longer spoke but watched everything with the patience of a heron.

Verity sat at the head of the table with her hands flat on the wood, and Coral saw, with a clarity that hurt somewhere behind the sternum, how worn she looked. Not aged—worn, the way a threshold is worn, the way a prayer book's spine is worn. She had been feeding this house herself for decades and it showed in her like rings in a tree.

Pell unlocked his case. Inside lay a single document, thick as a hymnal, bound in green ribbon, and the room changed when it came into the light. Coral felt it in her teeth. The mirrors along the far wall—all of them, the whole gallery of glass that Verity insisted was merely Victorian taste—shivered once, together, like water touched by a fingertip.

"This," Pell began, in the voice men use for wills and verdicts, "is the Renewal Instrument. It has existed since 1899. It has been executed twice—in 1931, and in 1974, incompletely." His eyes did not go to Ada, but the whole sentence leaned toward her. "Its terms are simple. If the final resident signs before the count concludes, the covenant renews. The house continues. And in exchange—"

"In exchange everyone else goes free." Ada said it flatly, testing the words like coins bitten for gold. "That's the bait. Say the rest."

Pell's composure flickered—a hairline crack, quickly mortared. "In exchange," he continued, "all obligations toward current residents are discharged. Those who remain within these walls at the conclusion of the count would otherwise... continue here. Indefinitely. Signing releases them. All of them."

"And the margin," Ada said. "Read the margin."

Coral's stomach dropped before Pell moved, because she understood then that Ada had seen this document before, or its grandfather, in 1974, and had spent fifty years carrying the shape of it in her memory the way survivors carry the shape of a door.

Pell turned to the last page. And there, in the margin, written in ink the color of dried blood—or actual dried blood, Coral thought, and hated herself for thinking it—was a single line of small, precise, feminine script.

*In consideration of renewal, the following name is offered in substitution:*

And beneath it:

*Coral Adeyemi Oyelaran.*

* * *

The room went very quiet. The kind of quiet that has weight, that presses on the eardrums like deep water. Somewhere in the walls, or in the space behind the walls, or in the place where Hollowmere kept its throat, Coral heard the count turn over like a sleeper changing position. *Four.* Soft. Certain. Patient as interest accruing.

She read her own name again. Then again. There is a particular vertigo in seeing your own name in an enemy's handwriting—it is not like seeing it anywhere else. Your name is the smallest vessel of yourself, and watching someone else pour their intention into it is a violation that has no clean word in English.

"When did you write this?" Coral asked Verity. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

Verity did not flinch. That was the terrible thing—she didn't flinch, didn't look away, didn't perform a single gesture of shame. She looked at Coral with an expression Coral had seen on families a hundred times in hospice work: the expression of people who have decided that love and harm are the same act and have chosen which one to call it.

"Tuesday," Verity said. "After you took Miss Voss to the gate."

"That wasn't—"

"I know what it was. I also know what it looked like." Verity's hands stayed flat on the table, steady as furniture. "You have been copying my ledger. You have been counting my residents like losses instead of arrivals. You have been filling that woman's head with the idea that there is an outside worth reaching." A breath. "I offered Mr. Pell a simpler solution first. Removal. But the house declined. It seems it prefers you exactly where you are." Something crossed her face then, fast, gone—grief, maybe, wearing duty's uniform. "So I gave it what it actually wanted. Your name. In exchange for everyone else's freedom. Including hers."

"Including mine," Coral said.

"No." Verity's voice cracked open just slightly, and Coral caught a glimpse of the girl underneath, the one from some other decade, holding a photograph of a daughter the house had taken or kept or eaten—she still didn't know which story was true. "Not including yours. That's the point, Miss Oyelaran. Don't you see? Everyone walks out. Everyone. Except the name in the margin. That's the price. One name buys every soul in this building, and I have chosen the only person in this house who was never supposed to be in it at all."

The words landed and Coral stood very still inside them, because there was a logic there, a horrible generous logic, and generosity was the most dangerous thing a trap could wear.

"You're asking me to sign my own name into a wall."

"I'm asking you to be the exception that saves everyone." Verity leaned forward. "You told me once—you don't remember, you tell so many people so many things—that you grew up feeling invisible. That rooms closed around you. That you learned to make yourself small enough to slip through lives unnoticed." Her eyes were wet and absolutely clear. "This house noticed you, Coral. Do you understand what a gift that is? Fifty years I have fed it photographs and names and it has never once looked at anyone the way it looks at you. You were hired because it asked for you. You belong to it already. All this document does is make it honest."

* * *

Later—she couldn't have said how much later; the clocks in Hollowmere had begun disagreeing with each other by whole minutes, then quarters of hours—Coral found herself in the corridor outside the morning room with her back against the wallpaper, breathing in the pattern she taught families at bedsides: four counts in, six counts out, the numbers hers, the rhythm hers, the one arithmetic in this building that belonged to her.

Ada's cane tapped toward her down the hall. The old woman stopped an arm's length away and looked up with those river-stone eyes.

"Well," Ada said. "Now you've seen it."

"She thinks she's saving me." Coral laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, splintered. "She wrote my name in blood-ink and she thinks she's doing me a kindness."

"Oh, Verity's kind." Ada said it without irony, which was worse. "Kindness is just cruelty that's had time to think about itself. Listen to me, girl." The cane tip struck the floorboards once, sharp. "That document is real and it is exactly what it claims. Sign it, and every soul here walks free—I walk free, Ferro walks free, even sleeping Halloway, God rot him, walks free. The house renews for another term and takes one keeper instead of many. That's the bargain. It's honest. It's the only honest thing this family has ever put on paper."

"But the margin—"

"The margin is the trap, yes. But not the one you think." Ada's voice dropped, and she glanced—quickly, involuntarily—at the mirror at the end of the corridor, where their reflections stood a beat and a half behind them, two old women and one young one, arriving late to their own conversation. "The margin doesn't bind whoever's named. It binds whoever signs *beside* the name. Read your contract law, nurse. An instrument needs execution. Verity can write your name in every ink in Christendom—it means nothing until the document is signed and sealed in the presence of the house itself. She needs your hand on that page. Yours, freely given, before the count ends."

Coral stared at her. "Then why show it to me at all? Why not forge—"

"Because the house won't take forgery. It never has. That's the one decency in the whole rotten architecture." Ada smiled, and it was a terrible smile, bright and bitter as frost. "It wants consent. It has always wanted consent. Every signature in that ledger downstairs was given by someone who believed they were choosing something." She gripped Coral's wrist, her fingers strong as roots. "Which means, my dear, that for the first time since 1899, the choice is actually on the table. They need you to say yes."

* * *

Night came early, the way it had been coming all week, pooling in the corners of Hollowmere like ink finding the low places in a page. Coral did not sleep. She sat in her narrow room with the lamp turned low and the mirror covered with her spare uniform, and she listened to the count move through the house below her feet.

*Four,* it said, at intervals that were not quite regular, like a heart deciding whether to keep going.

She thought about her name in the margin. She thought about Verity's wet, clear eyes, and the awful sincerity of a woman who had spent so long loving a house that she could no longer tell whether she was its keeper or its keeper's keeper. She thought about Dev—Dev, whose room was fresh-painted and whose name was on no roster and whose face lived now only in the lagging glass, appearing in mirrors half a second after Coral passed them, a ghost with a schedule. She thought about the way the house had hired her, had *asked* for her, had spent eleven weeks making her visible for the first time in her life, and how being seen had felt like sunlight until she understood what wanted to look at her.

And she thought about the flaw in the instrument. The beautiful, stupid, human flaw at the center of it: it needed her yes.

Her watch said 11:47. In the covered mirror, muffled by cotton and polyester, she could almost feel the reflection waiting anyway—patient, obedient, hers-but-not-hers, keeping its own time.

Below her window, gravel shifted. No engine, no headlights. Just footsteps crossing the forecourt in the dark, unhurried, and stopping directly beneath her sill, and standing there. Standing there for a long time. Long enough that Coral rose and looked down through the gap in the curtain.

No one was there.

But on the gravel, pressed into the day-old rain-soft mud, were footprints facing the house—and beside them, offset by exactly one stride, a second set of footprints, identical, slightly blurred, as if someone had walked to that spot and then waited there a half-second behind themselves.

The count turned over beneath the floorboards, closer than it had ever been, almost tender.

*Three.*

Coral picked up her pen, and her notebook, and the page where she had copied the instrument's terms in her own careful hand, and she began, in the dark, to plan what she would say yes to.
