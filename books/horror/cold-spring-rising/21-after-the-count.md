## 21. After the Count

The gates of Hollowmere House were iron, and they had always looked like iron, and Ada Voss had spent fifty years believing they were iron, and so it was a small and private shock when she pushed them and they swung like paper.

Cold spring light came through them anyway. That part was true. That part had always been true—the light, the long gravel sweep, the hedges clipped into obedience, the hills beyond going on and on in their patient green the way the world does when it has not been asked to participate in anything. Ada stood on the far side of the threshold with one hand still on the gate and felt the wind move through her cardigan and into her old bones, and she understood, with a clarity she had not earned so much as purchased, that she had not felt wind in a very long time. Hollowmere's air did not move. Hollowmere's air was held, the way a breath is held, the way a lie is held in the mouth.

She turned around.

The house stood at the end of its drive the way it had always stood, grey stone and dark windows, its chimneys like fingers pressed to lips. But it was quieter now. Not silent—Ada knew better than to trust silent, silent was a costume the place wore—but quieter, the way a church is quiet after the congregation has gone home and only the building remembers what was said. The mirrors in the long gallery would be empty. She knew that the way you know a thing you watched happen. The count was finished. Zero had come and gone like a held note finally released, and whatever the house had been collecting since 1899, it had collected its last payment at the gate, at one minute past dawn, in the handwriting of a woman who had signed her own name into the margin so that an old woman could walk out.

Ada's hand went to her coat pocket. The papers were there. Coral's copy—the forged one, the decoy, the thing they had carried through corridors that rearranged themselves like a throat swallowing—was gone, burned without flame in Coral's hands. But the original, the founding document, the 1899 bargain with its column of names and its renewal clause and its margin now bearing a single signature in blue ink, that was folded against Ada's hip, and it was warm. It had been warm since the count reached zero. It was warm the way a stone is warm that someone has just stopped holding.

"You stupid girl," Ada said to the house. Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it. There was no one left to perform composure for. "You stupid, brave, invisible girl."

The house said nothing. The house had said nothing to Ada Voss since 1974, when she had faked her death in a borrowed nightgown and been carried out past these same gates in a wicker basket between two orderlies who complained about the weight of her, and she had lain in the cold of a hearse and listened to the count pause—*pause*, like a man at a door considering—and then continue without her. She had spent fifty years knowing the house let her go. Not failed to catch her. *Let* her go, the way a fisherman lets the small fish swim, because the net was for something else, something that would take fifty more years to grow to size.

And then a nurse with a lagging reflection had grown to size, and the house had reached for her, and Ada—old, sharp-tongued, dying on schedule—had finally been given something to do about it.

She walked down the drive. Her legs complained. Her lungs burned in that thin, papery way of lungs that had been counting down their own private arithmetic for months. But every step was hers. Every step landed on gravel that did not rearrange beneath her, past hedges that did not lean, under a sky that stayed where the sky was supposed to be. She had forgotten that the world could be trusted to stay put. She had forgotten that a road could simply be a road, going where it went, without opinion.

Halfway down the drive she stopped and looked back once more, because she was old and old women are permitted their rituals.

The windows of Hollowmere were dark. Not curtained—*dark*, the way eyes are dark, the way a well is dark. And in the long gallery, she knew, in the hall, in Verity's morning room with its cruel oval glass, the mirrors hung empty. Not reflecting the rooms. Not reflecting anything. She had seen it happen as they ran: the glass giving back nothing but a grey shimmer, like water with the memory drained out of it. The house had spent its reflection. It had spent it buying one more resident, one more signature, one more name in the margin, and the name it bought was the name of a woman who had never properly reflected at all, and so the house had, in the end, purchased a hole.

Ada laughed. It hurt her chest and she laughed anyway, a dry crow's laugh that startled a bird out of the hedge, a real bird, a stupid ordinary wren that flew off into the real ordinary morning.

"You bought nothing," she told the house. "You bought a nothing, and the nothing signed, and now you're empty as a drum, and I'm the only one left who knows the tune."

* * *

The village was four miles. She had planned to walk it, in the way that dying people plan things, generously, in theory. But a milk lorry came up the lane at half seven, and the driver—a boy, a child, a round-faced boy with a radio murmuring football scores—slowed without being asked, because that is what people do in the country when they see an old woman walking, and Ada got in, and the warmth of the cab nearly undid her.

"You alright, missus? You look like you've been somewhere."

"I have," Ada said. "I've been at Hollowmere."

The boy's hands stayed easy on the wheel. He didn't flinch. He didn't go quiet-eyed the way Dev had gone quiet-eyed, the way everyone within eleven years of the place went quiet-eyed. He just frowned a little, the frown of a boy reaching for a fact and finding the shelf empty.

"Where's that, then?"

Ada looked at him. She looked at him for a long time, long enough that he glanced over twice, long enough that the radio filled the silence with a crowd roaring at nothing. And she understood. The house was off the maps—she had known that since '74, when she'd tried to show a solicitor the way and the solicitor had driven in circles until she'd given up and wept. But it was more than off the maps now. It was off the *tongues*. The count was finished, the bargain spent, and the house was folding itself out of the world like a letter being put away, and the boy driving this lorry had never heard of it and never would.

"Past the ridge," she said finally. "You wouldn't know it. Most don't."

"Old place?"

"Older than it should be." She settled into the seat. Her hip ached. Her heart did its uneven, borrowed-time thing in her chest, and she was grateful for every irregular beat. "Young man. Do you have a mother?"

"Course."

"And she knows your name. Says it. Shouts it up the stairs when you're late, I'd wager."

He laughed. "Every morning."

"Then you're richer than the Ashcombes ever were," Ada said, "and you should know it every day of your life," and the boy looked at her sideways and decided she was mad, kindly mad, lorry-picking-up mad, and drove her into the village square, and would not take her money, and called her "missus" again as she got down, and Ada Voss stood in the middle of a market town on a cold spring morning and wept like a girl.

Not for herself. She was done with herself; she had spent fifty years on herself, hiding, hoarding her one escaped life like a miser. She wept for the ones who hadn't walked out. For Dev, whose name was gone from every roster and whose face lived on only in lagging glass that was now, presumably, empty glass, which meant Dev was nowhere at all, unheld, unremembered, a man erased down to the last mirror. For the residents who had counted down to zero one by one, mid-sentence sometimes, their charts altered in handwriting that wasn't the hand that wrote it. For Verity Ashcombe, who had loved a dead daughter so ferociously that she had fed her love to a house for thirty years and called it keeping the girl near, and who had stood at the gate at the end begging, *begging*, with her heart broken open like a dropped egg, and who was now—where? Inside? Folded into the letter with the house? Or standing in an empty gallery calling a name that no glass would answer?

And for Coral. Always, from now on, for Coral. Coral Oyelaran, who had spent her whole life being unseen—unseen by employers, by families, by lovers, by the world's lazy eye—and who had finally understood that her invisibility wasn't an accident or a wound but a *hiring criterion*, the house's oldest trick, and who had taken that invisibility and done the one thing with it the house never planned for: she had made herself seen. She had signed the margin. She had traded the last scrap of herself she had, her name, her reflection, her place in anyone's memory, and she had done it so that a sharp-tongued old woman could stand in a market square in the cold spring light and cry and live.

Ada wiped her face with the heel of her hand, hard, the way her mother had taught her, *none of that, Ada, none of that*, and then she did not obey her mother, and cried a little more, and then stopped, because there was work.

* * *

The work was this: the papers in her coat pocket were still warm, and warm papers are papers with something left in them, and Ada Voss had spent fifty years as a dead woman and had gotten very, very good at one thing, which was surviving institutions. She knew how they worked. She knew their grammar. And she knew—this was the thing she knew best, the thing she had learned at nineteen in a workhouse and relearned at fifty-four in a hospice that ate its residents—that an institution does not die when you burn its documents. An institution dies when its documents are *read aloud*.

The house was empty. The mirrors were empty. But the bargain was a thing of names, and names outlive houses. The 1899 document named signatories. It named the Ashcombe line, generation to generation, the keepers of the count. It named, in its margin, in blue ink, in a hand that had shaken only a little, *Coral Oyelaran*—and that name was now, by the terms Ada had read three times in the lamplight of the gatehouse, *released, redeemed, and held in trust by the bearer of this instrument*. Held in trust. Ada was the bearer. Ada was, by the cold arithmetic of the thing, the executor of a dead house's last estate, and the estate consisted of one name, and she intended to administer it.

She went first to the parish church, because churches keep records and records are the enemy of forgetting. The vicar was young, a woman with ink on her fingers and a bicycle leaning in the vestry, and Ada told her a story. Not the true story—no one would bear the true story, not yet, maybe not ever; the true story was a stone too heavy for any single pair of hands—but a true-shaped story, a story about a hospice and a nurse and a woman who gave up everything for a stranger, and the vicar listened the way young clergy listen, hungrily, and when Ada asked, "Will you enter her name in your memorial book? She has no one else to carry it," the vicar said, "Of course," and wrote, in a steady hand, in a book that would sit on a shelf in a building that had stood for six hundred years and would stand for six hundred more:

*Coral Oyelaran, who gave her name that another might live.*

Ada watched the ink dry. It felt like watching a fire catch.

"That's a start," she said.

"A start?" the vicar asked.

"Mrs. Oyelaran deserves more than a start. She deserves a noise." Ada buttoned her coat. The papers crackled against her hip, warm as a sleeping animal. "I'm eighty-one years old, Reverend, I've been legally dead since 1974, and I have nothing left to lose and one name to spend, and I intend to spend it *loudly*. There will be a solicitor. There will be letters. There may, God forgive me, be a journalist."

The vicar laughed, then saw Ada's face, and stopped laughing, and nodded, the way people nod when they realize the mad old woman is not mad, only armed.

* * *

That night Ada took a room above the Green Man, because she had money—fifty years of a dead woman's careful savings, hidden in accounts under a name no one had ever thought to connect to a wicker basket and a hearse—and because she was done sleeping under roofs that watched her. The bed was lumpy. The window rattled in the wind. The washstand mirror, small and spotted with age, showed her exactly as she was: an old woman, thin as a lath, with a face like a crumpled letter, arriving precisely when she arrived, moving precisely when she moved, half a second of nothing between her and her reflection because there was no lag in the world, not out here, not anymore.

She raised her hand. The reflection raised its hand. Simultaneous. Perfect. Ordinary.

"Hello, Ada," she said to herself, and her reflection said it with her, mouth for mouth, and she sat down on the edge of the lumpy bed and put her face in her hands and let herself grieve properly, at last, alone, with no one to perform for, the way you're only allowed to grieve when the danger is past and the cost has been paid and the person you're grieving for is someone the world will never know existed.

She grieved for Dev, who had stayed eleven years because staying felt like safety and safety had a price collected in other people's names. She grieved for Verity, whose cruelty had been love with nowhere to go, and who had, in the last moment, at the gate, in the wind, looked at Ada with her daughter's eyes and said, "You'll tell them. Won't you. Someone should tell them." And Ada had said yes. She was going to keep that promise if it was the last act of her last year, and it might well be; her lungs were doing their countdown arithmetic, and she was not afraid of it, because she had heard a real countdown once, in a house of mirrors, and she knew the difference now between a number and a summons. Her numbers were just numbers. They meant only that she was mortal, which meant only that she was human, which meant, tonight, above the Green Man, with the wind rattling the honest glass: free.

She slept. She dreamed of nothing but a long drive and hedges and a wren in a hedge and gravel under her feet, each step her own.

* * *

In the morning she bought a notebook, a fountain pen, and second-class stamps, and she sat at a table by the pub window in the cold spring light, and she opened the warm papers, and she began to write.

She wrote it all down. 1899. The founding count. The household counted down like residents, the Ashcombes sealing their bargain, perpetual occupancy paid in the dying. 1974, and a young woman named Ada Voss in a borrowed nightgown in a wicker basket. The rules, laminated. The mirrors, lagging. The charts altered in impossible handwriting. Dev, and his eleven quiet years, and the man he'd tried to save, returned by morning with no memory and one fewer year. Verity, feeding photographs to a shrine. The gate, and the fifty yards where hope ran out. The margin. The blue ink. The name.

She wrote Coral's name at the top of the first page, large, in letters that pressed through three sheets beneath, and under it she wrote: *She was real. I saw her. I am the seeing.*

Because that was the thing, Ada thought, dipping the pen again, that the house had never understood, in all its long centuries of counting: a name does not live in documents. A name lives in mouths. In the saying. In the *Coral, hand me that* and the *Coral, look at this* and the *Coral, we're going, now, run*. The house had collected names in glass and paper and kept them where nothing could say them, and that was its hunger and its prison both. But a name given away—spent, shouted, written in a memorial book and a notebook and, God help her, a journalist's column—could not be collected. It was out. It was loose. It was multiplying.

Ada Voss licked an envelope, pressed the stamp down flat, and addressed it to a newspaper she did not read and did not respect, because disreputable papers printed the stories respectable ones were afraid of.

The wind came up the street and rattled the pub window, and in the glass Ada saw the street, the square, the church tower, the wren again on the sill outside, and herself, exact, immediate, one old woman in cold spring light with a debt to spend.

She mailed the letter. Then she wrote the second one, and the third, and she did not stop until the light went gold, and even then she wrote on, because somewhere out there—she was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of a promise—Coral Oyelaran was somewhere, nameless, reflectionless, walking through a world that had forgotten her, and every word Ada set down was a hand reaching through the forgetting to touch her shoulder and say: *I know you. I remember you. You are not nothing. You were never nothing.*

And far away, in a town with no Hollowmere on any map, a woman working the night shift of her ordinary life would feel, for no reason she could name, a warmth at her shoulder like a hand, and would turn—and find no one, and smile.
