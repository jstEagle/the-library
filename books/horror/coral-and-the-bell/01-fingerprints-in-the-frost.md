## 1. Fingerprints in the Frost

The satnav gave up on Marrow Fen eleven miles before Ada reached it, which she took as a fair warning from a machine that had never lied to her before.

"Recalculating," it said, in its patient female voice, and then it said nothing at all for the rest of the drive, just glowed there on the dashboard like something sulking. Ada let it sulk. She'd grown up in fen country — her grandfather's cottage had stood not forty miles from here, back when he was alive and counting things — and fen country did not forgive a road so much as tolerate it, the way you tolerated a cat on your lap when you needed to stand up.

She drove the last stretch on memory and hedge-lines, the van's headlights carving a tunnel out of the November dark. The fields on either side were black water stitched with pale reeds, and every so often the road dipped through a hollow where mist pooled like spilled milk. Twice she passed drowned gateposts standing alone in open water, marking fences that fenced nothing now, and once — she slowed for this, though she didn't stop — what looked like the ridge of a roof, just breaking the surface of a flooded field, furred with weed.

"Someone's village," she said to the satnav, because talking to machines kept her honest. "Somebody's whole street."

The satnav declined to comment.

The farmhouse announced itself by a single lit window, square and yellow as a butter pat, and then by the smell — woodsmoke and wet earth and, underneath, the particular green rot of standing water that gets into everything in these parts, wool, bread, hair. Ada pulled the van onto the gravel and sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, doing what she always did before a job: checking her kit in her head. Torch. Ladder. Dust sheets. The good torch, the one with the beam like a searchlight, because you never knew. Gloves. And the little notebook, because Ada Hartwell did not do anything worth doing without writing it down first.

She counted the farmhouse doors from outside, the way she counted every building's doors from outside. Front door: one. Back door, visible past the lean-to: two. A stable door on the side, half of it hanging: three. Three doors, one lit window, one chimney smoking.

Satisfied — or as near to satisfied as she ever got, which was a bit like being dry in a rainstorm — she got out and knocked.

* * *

Nell Marsh opened the door before Ada's knuckles landed the second time, which meant she'd been watching the lane. She was seventy-something and built like a fence post that had decided, late in life, to put down roots; she wore a man's cardigan over a floral dress and slippers the colour of old porridge, and she looked Ada up and down with the frank appraisal of a woman who had hired tradesmen since before Ada was born.

"You're the pest woman."

"Pest controller," Ada said. "Ada Hartwell." She offered her hand. Nell looked at it, considered, and shook it once, briskly, like closing an account.

"Hartwell." Something moved behind Nell's eyes — quick, gone again, a fish under ice. "Any relation to the Hartwells out of Thurlow way?"

"My grandad came from round here originally," Ada said. "Before my time."

"Mm," said Nell, in a tone that contained an entire conversation she wasn't going to have, and stepped back to let her in. "Mind the dog."

There was no dog. Ada, who had spent fifteen years learning to read houses the way other people read faces, clocked this immediately: no dog basket, no water bowl, no hair on the rug, no smell of dog beyond the general animal warmth of any lived-in kitchen. But she said, "Right-o," and stepped inside anyway, because the alternative was standing on the step discussing dogs that didn't exist with a woman who clearly had reasons, and Ada had learned long ago that the fastest way through a strange house was to be agreeable and keep your eyes moving.

The kitchen was warm and low-beamed and aggressively ordinary. A Rayburn ticking away. A dresser with willow-pattern plates. A calendar from the agricultural merchants, three months out of date because Nell had plainly stopped turning it over sometime in August and saw no reason to resume. On the table, a teapot under a knitted cosy shaped like a hen, and two cups already out.

Two cups. Nell had been watching the lane, all right.

"Sit down," Nell said. "You'll want tea before I show you. Everyone wants tea before I show them, and then they want tea after, and mostly they don't finish either cup."

"That's a very specific prediction," Ada said, sitting.

"I've had cause to make it four times now." Nell poured without asking how Ada took it, which was correct, because Ada took it however it came. "The council sent a man in September. He lasted twenty minutes. Then a firm out of Ely — they sent two lads, and one of them left his ladder. Didn't come back for it. Left his ladder, love. You know what a ladder costs."

"What did they see?"

"That's the thing." Nell sat down opposite, folded her hands, and regarded Ada with the weary patience of a woman about to explain arithmetic to someone who was going to get it wrong anyway. "They didn't see anything. That's why they left. Men can bear most things, but they can't bear being made fools of, and whatever's in my house makes fools of professionals." She pushed a biscuit tin an inch toward Ada, a formality, an opening move. "You'll want to see the windows."

"The call said rats."

"The call said rats because the girl on the phone said she couldn't file 'other' without a supervisor sign-off, and I'm not waiting three weeks for a supervisor." Nell's chin came up. "I've been in this house sixty-one years. I know what rats look like. Rats don't press their hands against the glass."

Ada felt the familiar cold start somewhere behind her sternum, the professional cold, the one she wore like a second coat. It was a good coat. It had gotten her through wasp nests the size of rugby balls and a pigeon problem in a crematorium that she still didn't discuss at parties. "Show me," she said.

* * *

The front room was cold.

Not cool — cold, in the specific way of a room where a window has been left open all night in January, except that every window in the front room was shut. Ada could see the latches, painted over more than once, fused in place by decades of gloss. The curtains were drawn across them, heavy things, floral, and Nell went along the wall pulling them back one by one with the air of a woman unveiling portraits of relatives she no longer spoke to.

"There," she said.

Frost covered the inside of the glass.

Not condensation, not a draught finding a gap — frost, actual frost, feathered and ferned across the panes in white whorls, thick at the edges, thinning toward the centre of each window. In November, in a heated house, on the *inside*. Ada breathed out slowly and watched her breath fog in front of her face, and thought: the Rayburn is ticking away ten feet from this room, and this room is breathing winter.

"All right," she said, keeping her voice level, because the voice was part of the coat. "That's unusual."

"Unusual," Nell repeated, with tremendous restraint. "Look closer, pest woman."

Ada leaned in.

The frost wasn't uniform. Across the central pane of the middle window, pressed into the white, were handprints.

Five fingers each, splayed wide, the prints deep enough that the frost had been crushed to clear glass beneath them — as if something had laid its palms flat against the pane from the *outside*, and the frost had bloomed around the pressure, recording it. Except the prints faced inward. The fingers pointed down and slightly outward, the way a person's hands hang when they're pressing against a window to peer in. Except there was no one peering in. Except the prints were on the *inside* of sealed windows, in a locked room, in a heated house, and they were —

Ada held her own hand up beside the glass, without quite touching it.

The print dwarfed hers. Not by a little. Her hand, and she was not a small woman, vanished inside the outline the way a rowboat vanishes alongside a ferry. Whatever had pressed itself against Nell Marsh's windows had hands the size of dinner plates, fingers long and blunt-tipped, and it had pressed hard — the crush-marks in the frost were deep, deliberate, the pressure of something leaning its weight in.

"How many windows?" Ada asked.

"Eleven in the house. Prints on nine." Nell said it flatly, but Ada heard the cost underneath. Sixty-one years in this house. "Started small. One window, back in October. I scrubbed it off. Came back next morning, two windows. Scrubbed again. By the time I stopped scrubbing it was every night, more windows each time, like it was — " Nell stopped. Her jaw worked. "Like it was practising. Learning where the windows were. Getting better at it."

"Getting better at it how?"

Nell crossed the room to the far window, the one over the sideboard, and pulled its curtain.

This print was different. This one wasn't a palm pressed flat. This one was a single finger, drawn through the frost in a slow curve — and at the end of the curve, unmistakably, deliberately, a spiral. Neat as a compass had drawn it. Coiling inward, tighter and tighter, until the line simply stopped, as if the thing drawing it had run out of either patience or glass.

"It used to just press," Nell said quietly. "Now it draws. My dad always said — " and there she stopped again, harder this time, visibly swallowing a sentence whole. Ada watched her do it. Watched the fish go under the ice again.

"Your dad said what?"

"He said a lot of things. He's dead fifty years, he can say them to himself." Nell turned, and for the first time since the door opened, Ada saw fear in her face — not the sharp fear of the moment, but the worn, settled kind, fear that had moved in and paid rent. "I don't care what it is. I don't care what it wants. I've buried a husband and both my brothers and I am not going to be frightened out of my own kitchen by something that won't even show its face. So you'll tell me honestly, Miss Hartwell: is this rats?"

Ada looked at the spiral. At the handprints, big as dinner plates, pressed from the wrong side of the glass. At the frost, which had begun, very faintly, right while she watched, to bloom anew around the edges of the oldest print — filling it in, smoothing it over, the way snow fills a footprint. As if the window were healing. As if it wanted a clean slate for tomorrow night.

"No," Ada said. "It's not rats."

"Right." Nell nodded, unsurprised, almost relieved. "Then we'll have that second cup of tea, and you'll tell me what it *is*, and if you don't know, you'll say so, because I've had enough of men who don't know pretending they do."

* * *

Over the second cup — which Ada, noting it with some private satisfaction, did not finish — she asked the questions she always asked. When did it start. Who else had seen it. Any smells, sounds, cold spots, dreams. Nell answered everything with the precision of a woman who had been keeping notes herself, and Ada saw the exercise book on the dresser and didn't comment, because everyone in this line of work eventually became a person with an exercise book, and there was no shame in it.

"When exactly did it start?" Ada asked. "The date matters."

"October the ninth." No hesitation at all. "Because it was the day after the machines came. The drainage lot, digging out the fen for their new road. Big yellow things, all day, groaning and sloshing. I could hear them from the garden. Then that night — " Nell gestured at the windows with her chin. "First print. And they've been at it ever since, draining that hollow down to the mud. My dad would've had opinions about it. My dad had opinions about everything that got dug."

"What sort of opinions?"

"The sort you don't dig." Nell looked at her over the rim of her cup. "He said Marrow Fen was drowned for a reason and the reason hadn't expired. He said the water was doing a job." She set the cup down. "Course, my dad also believed the church bell rang underwater for three days after the flooding, and that the vicar's wife could cure warts, so you can weigh that how you like."

Ada smiled politely and wrote *Oct 9 — excavation begins* in her notebook, and underneath it, without quite deciding to, *bell?*

Her grandfather had been a bell-hanger, among other things. She hadn't thought about that in years.

It was full dark now, and the drive back to her lodgings — Nell had insisted she take the spare room rather than "go rattling down those lanes at night like a fool" — waited only until Ada finished her survey. She walked the house room by room with her torch, professional to her boots, checking vents and eaves and the gaps under doors, finding nothing, finding less than nothing, finding a house that was warm and dry and clean except for the cold rooms and the frosted glass. At the back door she paused, out of habit older than her career, and counted.

Front door: one. Back door, where she stood: two. Stable door: three.

Cellar door, at the end of the scullery passage, which she had noted earlier and Nell had not mentioned: four.

Four doors. She wrote it down. Four doors, and she checked the number twice against the house, walking it again, because that was what she did, that was what she had always done, since she was small enough that nobody had thought to ask her why — and if anyone ever did ask, she had a stock answer ready about fire safety, which was a lie, and she had never worked out what the truth was, only that buildings must have their doors counted, that a house whose doors went uncounted was a house where things could be *added*, and that the count must always, always come out the same.

It came out four. She wrote *4* in the notebook, and drew a small tick beside it, and felt the tick settle something in her chest that had no name.

From the front room, faintly, came the sound of Nell drawing curtains.

Ada stood in the scullery passage with her torch off, letting her eyes adjust, and looked at the cellar door at the end of it. Painted green, many times over. A normal door. A counted door. Accounted for.

And yet, as she watched — and she told herself afterward that the light was bad, that her eyes were tired, that frost did funny things in torchlight — she could have sworn the frost ferns on the cellar door's little window were arranged in a shape she recognised. A curve. A slow, deliberate spiral, coiling inward, drawn by a single long finger.

Tighter and tighter, until the line simply stopped.

"Tomorrow," Ada said quietly, to the door, to the house, to whoever was listening from whichever side of the glass. "Tomorrow I'll have a proper look at you."

Behind her, in the front room, Nell finished drawing the last curtain, and the latch of the ninth window clicked home like a full stop.
