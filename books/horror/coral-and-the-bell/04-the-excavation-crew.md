## 4. The Excavation Crew

Ruth Calloway had a rule about sites that made her skin prickle: she wrote the feeling down, dated it, and then ignored it with professional discipline. Feelings were not evidence. Feelings did not get you a keynote slot in Inverness. So when she stepped out of her car at the Marrow Fen bypass works that grey Tuesday morning and felt the back of her neck tighten like a hand had been laid there—gently, almost kindly—she took out her notebook and wrote *site feels attentive*, underlined it twice, and put it away.

"Attentive," said Dekker, reading over her shoulder because boundaries were a concept he'd filed somewhere and lost. "What's that mean, then? Like it's pleased to see us?"

"It means I have a headache, Dekker."

"You had a headache yesterday."

"Yes. They tend to recur."

He grinned at her, all teeth and foreman's confidence, a big man built like something dredged up and reassembled by an optimistic engineer. He had a hi-vis vest over a shirt that had once been white and a thermos the size of a fire extinguisher. "Well, keep your headaches to yourself. Council's already sent us one inspector." He jerked his thumb toward the site cabin, where a slim figure in a waxed coat was signing the visitor's book with the concentration of someone defusing something. "Pest control, would you believe. Farmer's got mice, or ghosts, or whatever it is farmers claim to have when they want the council to pay for things."

Ruth looked. The woman at the counter was perhaps forty, weathered, practical-looking, with the kind of stillness Ruth associated with people who worked alone in dark places for a living. She signed her name—*A. Hartwell*—and then, Ruth noticed, counted the doors of the cabin on her way through it. Her eyes flicked left, right, left again, lips moving faintly. Three doors. She seemed satisfied.

"Pest controller," Ruth repeated. "For a fen."

"Fen's got rats like anywhere else." Dekker shrugged. "More, probably. Everything out here's got more."

* * *

The bypass was going to cut straight through the eastern arm of Marrow Fen, which meant draining eleven acres of drowned pasture that had been underwater since 1974. Ruth had fought for this job the way other people fought for custody of children. Nineteen seventy-four. A whole village, flooded deliberately, church and all, its records scattered across three county archives and one private collection she'd had to be very charming to access. Nobody had ever done a proper survey. Nobody had ever been allowed to. The parish council of the nearest living village—Thornholt, four miles east, population two hundred and falling—had objected to every application for fifty years, always on grounds so vague they read like apologies: *inappropriate*, *unwise*, *against the wishes of interested parties*. Interested parties. As if the dead had a residents' association.

She'd won anyway. Grants, permits, a season of drainage and salvage archaeology before the bulldozers came. It was going to make her career. She told herself that most mornings, standing on the cofferdam with her coffee going cold, looking down into water the color of steeped tea.

Today she gave Ada Hartwell—the pest controller, who had turned up at the site office asking odd questions about frost and footprints, which Ruth had privately filed under *local color*—the grand tour, partly because Dekker was busy shouting at a pump and partly because Hartwell asked questions like someone who actually listened to answers, which was rarer than it should have been among the people Ruth met.

"That's the church," Ruth said, pointing across the drained basin to where a squat stone tower rose out of the mud like a rotten tooth. The water around it had been pumped down six feet; the tower's base was exposed now, green-black with fifty years of silt, and above the waterline the old flintwork gleamed wetly. "St. Brannoc's. Drowned with everything else. The plan in '74 was to demolish it before flooding, but—" she consulted her memory of the file, which she knew by heart, "—the demolition contractor pulled out. Twice. Then the money ran out, and they just... closed the doors and let the water do it."

"And the water didn't," Hartwell said.

"The water didn't," Ruth agreed. "It never does what you want. That's the first thing they teach you and the last thing anyone learns."

Hartwell stood with her hands in her coat pockets, looking at the tower the way Ruth had seen her look at the farmhouse windows earlier: thoroughly, patiently, as if waiting for it to say something incriminating. "You've been down there yet?"

"Not yet. Sonar first. We're not diving until we know what's under the silt." Ruth hesitated, then heard herself add, "There's something down there. On the scans. Big. Regular. Too regular for rubble."

"Show me?"

And here was the thing Ruth would think about later, in the small hours, in the way she thought about all her worst decisions: she wanted to show someone. Not the crew—Dekker thought sonar anomalies were excuses for expensive delays, and Idris had gone quiet and strange about the whole site—but someone neutral, practical, a woman who counted doors and killed rats for a living and would presumably say *it's a fallen roof beam, love, get some sleep*. Ruth wanted to be talked out of the feeling in her neck. That was all.

So she led Hartwell up the scaffold ramp to the survey hut, where Idris was hunched over his screens like a man warming himself at a fire he didn't trust.

Idris Penn was thirty-one and built like a question mark. He was the best hydrographic surveyor Ruth had ever worked with, and he was afraid of this site—not in any way he'd admit, but in the way he triple-checked his transducer mounts and wouldn't take the boat out alone and had started, in the last week, humming to himself while he worked. Old hymns, Ruth thought. Or something like hymns. She'd meant to ask him about it and hadn't.

"Idris," she said. "This is Ada Hartwell, pest control. She wants to see the anomaly."

"We don't call it the anomaly," Idris said immediately, in the tone of a man correcting a dangerous misstatement. "We call it Target One."

"Why?"

"Because 'anomaly' implies it's strange." He swiveled his monitor toward them. "This isn't strange. Strange is one-off. This is—" He gestured at the screen, at the sonar mosaic of the flooded basin rendered in greens and yellows, and tapped a shape near the church tower. "This is deliberate."

Ruth leaned in, though she'd seen it a hundred times. The shape sat beneath three meters of water and two of silt, northeast of the tower: a rectangle, roughly four meters by seven, with what looked like walls. Attached to its long side, another smaller shape. And from the smaller shape, running away into the unscanned dark, a line—straight as a ruled pencil stroke.

"A building," Hartwell said.

"A building," Idris agreed, "that is not on any map. Not the 1974 flood plans, not the tithe maps, not the enclosure awards. I've been through all of them." His voice had gone slightly high. "I've been through *all of them*, Dr. Calloway. There is no record of a structure there. Which means either everyone who ever mapped this village missed an entire building—"

"Or it wasn't there to map," Ruth finished.

Nobody said anything for a moment. On the screen, the little green rectangle sat in its drowned field, patient as a held breath.

"What's the line?" Hartwell asked.

"Path? Wall? Pipe?" Idris spread his hands. "We haven't scanned far enough. The water gets shallow that way—reeds, peat—it degrades the return. But it runs toward Thornholt." He caught himself, frowned. "Toward the living village. Which is probably nothing. Probably a field boundary. Boundaries run straight; that's the whole point of them."

"Probably," Ruth said, and heard how thin the word sounded.

Hartwell was quiet a moment. Then, in the mild tone of a woman discussing vermin: "Has anything else come up? Out of the water, I mean. Anything the dredger's brought in."

"Bones," said Idris, too fast.

"Idris."

"Livestock bones. Cattle, sheep. Dekker says it's a midden, some old farmer's dump." Idris was looking at his screens now, not at either of them. "They were arranged, though. When they came up in the grab. Arranged in a—" He stopped. Started again. "In a pattern. Spiral. Dekker says tides do that. Fen doesn't have tides, but he says it anyway, loudly, so nobody asks him anything else."

"Dekker," Ruth said, "believes the universe is a series of problems with solutions, and the solution is usually him."

"He'd get on with my sister," said Hartwell, and for the first time since Ruth had met her, something in her shoulders came down half an inch. "Can I see the bones?"

"They're bagged and tagged in the finds tent. Catalogued as T-1 through T-40-something." Ruth paused. "Why does a pest controller want to see the bones?"

"Professional curiosity." Hartwell smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Everything's connected to something. That's true of rats and it's true of everything else."

* * *

The finds tent smelled of damp canvas and freshwater mud, that particular fen smell Ruth had come to think of as the smell of 1974—of everything the water had swallowed and kept. Crates lined the trestle tables. Hartwell moved along them slowly, reading tags, and stopped at the crate marked T-01–T-14, LIVESTOCK, CONTEXT 3.

Inside, on a bed of bubble wrap, lay cattle bones. Ribs, a scapula, part of a skull. They were clean—too clean, Ruth thought, for fifty years underwater; the fen should have stained them tea-brown—and they had been packed in the spiral order Idris described, curling inward like a snail shell, like a written letter curling at the end of a sentence.

"These were found how deep?" Hartwell asked.

"Just under the silt crust. Top of the sequence. Recent deposition, relatively speaking." Ruth crouched beside the crate. "Which is odd in itself. Livestock don't wander into a flooded village. Somebody put them there. Recently-ish. Or—" 

"Or something did," Hartwell said.

"I was going to say *or they were deposited after flooding by persons unknown*, which is the academic version of the same sentence."

"Mm." Hartwell didn't touch the bones. Ruth noticed that: she looked at them closely, thoroughly, the way she'd looked at the frost prints on the farmhouse window that morning—prints on the *inside* of sealed windows, she'd said, prints too big for anyone in the house—and then she stepped back from the crate with the deliberate care of someone stepping back from a dog that might bite. "Your surveyor. Idris. How long has he been humming?"

Ruth blinked. "How did you—"

"He hums when he's working. Something hymn-like. You didn't notice because you're used to it, but it started recently, yes?" She glanced up. "People hum for reasons. Usually good ones. Sometimes to fill silence. Sometimes to drown something out."

"He's nervous about the site. It's a big contract; he's careful."

"Sure," said Hartwell, in a voice that filed Ruth's explanation neatly under a label Ruth couldn't read, and moved on to the next crate.

Ruth stayed crouched by the bones a moment longer. The spiral curled inward, rib after rib, getting smaller, getting closer to the center, to the point where the pattern began—or ended. She thought about the sonar rectangle lying patient under the water, attached to nothing, mapped nowhere, reached by a straight line that ran toward the living world. She thought about the parish objections, fifty years of them, *inappropriate, unwise, against the wishes of interested parties*, and how she'd read those words aloud at the public inquiry in a voice of gentle reason while thinking, *superstition, nimbyism, the past trying to hold the future hostage.* She had won. She had been so pleased to win.

Her neck prickled again, that gentle hand.

"Dr. Calloway." Hartwell had stopped at the tent flap, silhouetted against the grey light. "One piece of free advice, from one professional to another. When you go down to that structure—and you will, I can see you're going to—take more lights than you think you need. However many you think you need, double it."

"Is that a pest-control thing?"

"It's a dark-places thing." Hartwell pushed back the flap. "Lights are cheap. Most things that live in the dark are only brave when they can't be seen. Give them a reason to be shy." She paused. "And count your exits. Every time you enter somewhere new. Doors, gates, ways out. Count them, and remember the number."

"That seems paranoid."

"It seems paranoid right up until the day the number's wrong." She said it lightly, almost cheerfully, the way people deliver their most serious sentences, and then she was gone, boots squelching off toward the farmhouse, toward her mice, or her ghosts, or whatever it was she claimed to have.

* * *

That evening Ruth stayed late at the survey hut, ostensibly to process scan data, actually because going back to her B&B in Thornholt meant an hour alone with her notebook and the entry she'd written that morning, and she wasn't ready to underline it a third time.

Idris had gone at five, taking the boat out one last time to recover the transducer array—a fifteen-minute round trip he'd insisted on doing himself, though Ruth offered. Through the hut window she watched his headlamp bob across the blackening water toward the church tower, steady, unhurried, and she noticed—he was humming again. She could hear it faintly through the glass, or imagined she could, a thread of melody rising off the water. Not a hymn she knew. Something older-sounding. Something with the shape of a bell in it, if a melody could have such a shape.

She turned back to her screens and pulled up Target One full-screen. Four by seven meters. Walls. An annex. The straight line running toward Thornholt, toward the living, lit world, toward the B&B where her bed waited and her notebook waited and the word *attentive* waited.

She zoomed in on the annex until the pixels broke apart. Before they did, she could just persuade herself she saw something in the middle of it—a regularity within the regularity, a shape like a box, like a crate, like something set down carefully in the dark and left.

Like something stored.

On the water outside, Idris's headlamp stopped moving.

Ruth looked up. The lamp hung motionless out by the tower, a single bright point in all that darkness, and the humming—if it had ever been real—had stopped, and the silence it left behind was the specific silence of a room where someone has just realized they are not alone.

Then the lamp swung, quick and purposeful, back toward shore, faster than protocol, faster than sense, and Ruth was already reaching for her coat and her radio, telling herself it was nothing, a snagged cable, a cramp, a man being jumpy on a creepy site, nothing, nothing—

The radio crackled. Idris's voice, breathless, wrong-pitched:

"Ruth. Ruth, there's something down here. Under the boat. It's—" A pause, and in the pause, faintly, through his open mic, a sound Ruth would spend the rest of her life failing to describe except as *a bell, ringing, underwater, and no bell has been down there since 1974.*

"Get out of the water," Ruth said. "Idris. Get out of the water *now*."
