## 6. The Door, Sealed from the Inside

Ada had spent the night doing what she always did: lights on, doors counted, tea gone cold in a mug she kept forgetting to refill because her hands were busy counting again. Eleven times she'd walked the farmhouse corridor. Eleven times the count had come back correct—kitchen door, pantry door, stairs cupboard, three bedrooms, bathroom, front, back. Eight. A good honest number. A number that stayed put.

She'd slept maybe two hours, and dreamed of water standing politely on the far side of a window, waiting to be let in.

By morning the frost had retreated from the glass like something embarrassed to be caught, and Ada had almost convinced herself the whole business was condensation, bad seals, an old house breathing out at night. Pest controllers made their living on plausible explanations. Rats left grease marks along skirting boards. Mice left droppings like punctuation. Nothing left handprints on the *inside* of sealed windows, but there was a first time for everything, and if there wasn't, well, she'd invoice for it anyway.

The call from the excavation site came at half seven.

"You'll want to see this," said Dekker, before she'd even said hello. "Or you won't. Honestly, I don't know which."

"That's usually my line," Ada said.

"Yeah, well." A pause, and beneath it the sound of wind and diesel and men shouting in the flat way people shout when they're frightened and pretending it's about logistics. "Bring your wellies."

* * *

The fen had been peeled open.

That was the only way Ada could think of it, standing at the lip of the drained section with her hands jammed in her coat pockets and the cold coming up off the exposed mud like breath from a mouth. The crew had dammed and pumped a great grey bowl of fenbed dry, forty acres of it, and what lay revealed was not mud in any honest sense. It was floor. The silt had settled into long level planes, and out of those planes rose the stumps of walls—flint and brick, soft-edged after fifty years underwater, arranged in streets. Doorsteps without houses. Garden walls around gardens that were now just rectangles of darker mud. Here and there a chimney stood alone, absurd as a tooth in a gum.

"Marrow Fen," Ruth said, coming up beside her. The archaeologist had her hood up and her camera down—she wasn't photographing anything, Ada noticed, which for an archaeologist was probably the equivalent of holding your breath. "The village. They flooded it in '74. Reservoir scheme that never went ahead properly, so they just... left it drowned."

"And now?"

"Now we're draining it for a bypass, and every day it gives us something else." Ruth's voice was careful, level, the voice of someone reading from notes they didn't trust. "Come and see."

They walked out along a temporary causeway of gravel and scaffold boards, past two diggers parked nose-to-nose like animals sheltering from weather. The crew had gone quiet in that particular way Ada recognized from houses where something was wrong—not silent, but performing noise. Dekker was bellowing about pump schedules at a man who clearly already knew the pump schedule. Another chap was hammering a stake with tremendous commitment and no accuracy.

"Everyone's very busy," Ada observed.

"Everyone's very busy," Ruth agreed.

The thing they'd found stood in the middle of what must once have been the churchyard, because the church itself still loomed behind it—a black gable rising out of the shallows where the pumping hadn't reached, its tower leaning like a man listening at a door. Ada would deal with the church later. Or never, ideally.

The thing was a door.

An oak door, standing upright in the fenbed, planted in the mud as though the wall around it had simply been persuaded to leave. It was perhaps seven feet tall, dark with age and water, iron-studded, and its hinges—great curled things, strap-hinges a foot wide—had fused into a single mass of rust, so that the door and its frame were one object now, welded shut by half a century of patience.

And the planks were scarred.

Ada crouched at the edge of the trench the crew had dug around it and looked at the gouges without touching them. They ran deep into the oak, four parallel channels at a time, dozens upon dozens of them, overlapping, layered, some old and softened by water and some—she made herself look properly—some with the crisp edges of recent work. They covered the lower two-thirds of the door. Above them, at shoulder height, the wood was smoother, worn pale in a patch the size of a forehead, as though something had rested its head against the door for a very long time.

"Claw marks," Dekker said behind her. He'd abandoned his pump schedule. Up close he was broader and more tired than he sounded on the phone, with the ruddy complexion of a man whose confidence was currently running on fumes. "From inside. That's the bit everyone keeps coming back to. The grain of the wood shows the tearing came from—the pressure's from—" He gestured helplessly. "Behind."

"Where's behind?" Ada asked.

Ruth answered, quietly. "Under the fen. Under everything. There's no room behind it, Ms Hartwell. We've sonar-mapped the whole basin. It's silt and bedrock. There is no space for anything to have been standing behind that door, clawing at it. And yet."

"And yet," Ada said.

She stood up slowly. Her knees cracked, which under the circumstances felt appropriate, like the building joining in.

"Here's my professional assessment," she said. "You haven't got a pest problem. You've got a pest problem's landlord. And I'd like to go home now."

Nobody laughed. She'd been hoping somebody would laugh.

* * *

It was Dekker who insisted she stay, in his brash, over-loud way, waving a thermos at her like a contract. "You're the pest woman. You're on retainer. Parish council's paying for peace of mind, and right now nobody's got any, so earn it. Look at it properly. Tell us it's rot. Tell us it's erosion. Tell us something with a Latin name."

So Ada looked at it properly.

She circled the trench twice, boots sinking in the give of the drained mud, and told herself she was looking for woodworm, for wet rot, for the ordinary criminality of timber. What she was actually doing—and she knew it, the way you know you're walking toward a cliff edge in the dark—was looking at the grain.

Oak grain has a signature. Anyone who's worked old buildings knows it: the cathedral arches of the medullary rays, the long rivers of the growth rings, the way quarter-sawn boards throw that shimmering fleck. Doors are like faces. You can know a door the way you know a face, if you've stood in enough hallways at three in the morning counting them.

Ada had counted a lot of doors.

She stopped circling. The cold moved through her coat and settled against her spine like a hand.

Because she knew this door.

Not its type. Not its period. *This* door. The extra one. The one that appeared in her farmhouse corridor at 3 a.m., between the pantry and the stairs cupboard, plain-painted and wrong, the one she'd opened exactly once, years ago, and found a darkness that smelled of pond water and old hymn books. The one she'd counted around ever since, telling herself the count was a quirk, a comfort, a private superstition with no more weight than not stepping on cracks.

This door's grain matched that door's grain. Not similar. Matching. The same pale scar across the third plank from the bottom, the same knot low on the left like a closed eye, the same shimmering fleck where the light caught it. The door standing in a drained fenbed fifty miles from her kitchen had grown, plank by plank, from the same tree—or from the same *idea* of a tree—as the door that visited her hallway at night.

"Ms Hartwell?" Ruth said. "You've gone white. Well. Whiter."

"I need to ask you something," Ada said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, and she clung to that steadiness like a banister. "Has anyone been to my farmhouse? Any of your crew? Deliveries? Surveyors? Anyone taking measurements near Hartwell land?"

Dekker frowned. "We did a route survey up that way in October. Why?"

"Did anyone bring back souvenirs? Timber? Old fittings? Anything from the fen or the village?"

"I don't—" He turned. "Idris! Did anyone pocket anything off the survey route?"

Idris emerged from behind a spoil heap, thin and nervous, clutching a clipboard like a shield. "There was nothing to pocket. It's all underwater. Except—" He stopped himself, visibly rewound, decided honesty was the lesser terror. "Except the things that wash up. On the north bank. After storms. Bits of the village. Everyone takes a little something, it's traditional, it's—"

"What sort of things?" Ada asked.

"Bricks. Bottles. A hinge, once. Someone's got a whole mantelpiece." He swallowed. "And doors. There are doors that wash up. From the flooded houses. People prop them up in sheds, use them as worktops. It's harmless. It's just wood."

"It's just wood," Ada repeated.

The words sat in the air and refused to be comforting.

* * *

She walked the north bank that afternoon, alone, because the alternative was standing in a trench staring at a door until it stared back, and she wasn't ready for that yet, and possibly never would be. The bank was littered with the fen's leavings: a chair leg, a doll's head with its paint gone the colour of weak tea, bricks stamped with a maker's name from a kiln long cold. And doors. Three of them, propped against a fence like tired commuters—one pine, one painted green, one oak.

The oak one was small. Attic-sized. Its grain meant nothing to her, and she felt a wave of relief so strong it was nearly vertigo, followed immediately by the question she'd been outrunning since the trench:

If the big door matched her hallway door, which one was the copy?

Her grandfather had built the farmhouse. That much family lore she had—Hartwells had held the land above Marrow Fen since before the flood, and Grandad Joe had put up the house in '69, five years before the waters came. She'd grown up in it. She'd inherited it. She'd counted its doors since she was small enough that the top of the stair banister was eye-level, and her mother used to say, fondly, *you and your numbers, Ada, you're your grandfather all over again.*

*Your grandfather all over again.* She'd thought it was about the counting. Now, standing on a frozen bank with a doll's head watching her with no eyes, she wondered what else it might have been about.

Her phone buzzed. Dekker.

"Change of plan," he said, and the bravado had gone out of him entirely, leaving something younger and scared. "We tried to shift it. The door. Winched it, four-tonne strap, the digger's rated for ten. The strap snapped. Then the second one. Then the winch arm bent, and Idris says—and Idris doesn't say things like this, Idris says things like *load coefficients*—Idris says it's not that the door won't move. It's that the door doesn't want to."

"Doors don't want things, Mr Dekker."

"No," he agreed. "But I'm starting to think this one does. And here's the other thing. The clawing. We cleared the silt off the base today, full depth. You want to know how deep the scratches go?"

"How deep?"

"All the way down. Below the planks. Into the frame. Into the ground under the frame." A breath. "Fifty years' worth, minimum. Layer on layer. Something's been scratching at that door since before they flooded the village, Ms Hartwell. Since before there was a door to scratch at, according to the parish records, which Ruth pulled last night and hasn't slept since."

Ada looked out across the fen. The church tower leaned over its black water. Somewhere under that water was a village that had been drowned on purpose, and somewhere under the village was a door that had been scratched from the inside since before it existed, and somewhere in her hallway at 3 a.m. was its twin, patient as furniture, waiting for her to stop counting.

"Keep your crew off it," she said. "No digging round it. No straps, no winches, no dynamite, and I know that last one needed saying."

"It needed saying," Dekker admitted.

"And nobody touches anything that washes up on the north bank. Nothing comes home with anybody. Not a brick. Not a bottle. Especially not a door."

A pause. "Why especially not a door?"

"Because," Ada said, and heard her own voice drop into the flat calm she saved for wasps' nests and clients in denial, "I think this one already has."

She hung up and stood a moment longer in the wind. Behind her, half a mile off across the mud, the oak door stood upright in its trench, fused hinges gleaming dully, its pale worn patch at shoulder height catching the last of the light—exactly the height, she noted, of her own forehead.

The impossible, she thought, turning back toward the farmhouse, hasn't been following me at all.

It's been waiting. It put a door in my corridor years ago, and it's been leaving the porch light on ever since.

All it needs now is for me to open it from my side.

That night, at 3 a.m., Ada counted the doors eight times, and got eight every time, and understood finally that this was worse. The extra door hadn't vanished.

It had gone home.
