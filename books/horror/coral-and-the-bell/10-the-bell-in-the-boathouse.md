## 10. The Bell in the Boathouse

The ledger had led her to the water's edge, and the water's edge, as it turned out, was where Marrow Fen kept its secrets the way other villages kept their post offices: centrally, and with a certain institutional pride.

Ada stood on the shingle bank at half past nine in the morning, breath steaming, a thermos of tea in one hand and Nell's hand-drawn map in the other. The map was drawn on the back of a 1987 calendar page advertising agricultural feed, which gave the whole enterprise an air of prophecy fulfilled.

"That's the boathouse," Nell had said, tapping a wobbly rectangle with a gnarled finger. "Or what's left. My father kept his punt there before they drowned everything. Afterward it just... stayed. Things that should have floated away always stayed near that building. Bins. Deckchairs. A whole rowboat once, tied up with nobody to tie it." She'd looked at Ada over her spectacles. "You'll want to count the doors before you go in."

"I always do," Ada said.

"I know you do, love. That's rather the point of you."

* * *

The path along the fen edge was frozen hard enough to hold her weight, crunching like biscuit underfoot. Ada counted things as she walked, because counting was what she did, the way some people hummed and some people bit their nails. Eleven fence posts between the lane and the sluice gate. Four herons—no, three herons and one piece of driftwood shaped like regret. Forty-two steps from the sluice to the point where the reeds gave way and the boathouse appeared out of the mist like something surfacing for air.

It was worse than she'd expected, and she'd expected quite a lot. The building leaned into the water at an angle suggesting it had been trying to drown itself for fifty years and hadn't managed it, out of sheer spite. Its roof was moss-green corrugated iron, sagging in the middle like a tired back. The boards were silver-grey with age, soft-looking, rotten-looking—and yet every single plank sat exactly where it had been put. Nothing had fallen off. Nothing had floated away. In a landscape that ate structures for breakfast, the boathouse was being saved for something.

"Right," Ada said aloud, because talking to herself was cheaper than therapy and only slightly less effective. "Doors first."

There was the main door, double-fronted, facing the water: two.

A side door, half-hidden behind a curtain of dead ivy: three.

A hatch in the roof, presumably for hoisting boats: not a door, but she noted it anyway, because her grandfather's journal—which she now carried everywhere, wrapped in a plastic bag against the damp like a holy relic in Tupperware—had been very firm on the subject of openings. *Count all openings,* he'd written, in handwriting that started confident and got smaller and more hurried toward the end of each entry. *A thing can come through anything you haven't counted.*

She counted the windows. Four. She counted them again. Still four. Good.

Then she stood in front of the main doors and noticed, with the particular sinking feeling she'd been cultivating all week like a rare orchid, that frost ferns were growing on the *outside* of the wood. Feathery white whorls, spreading from the hinges outward, each frond perfect and symmetrical, like lace made by something with too much time and no soul.

Frost grew outside in winter. That was normal. That was physics.

But these ferns were arranged in spirals. And the spirals matched, exactly, the pattern she'd photographed three days ago in the dredged silt where the bones had come up. And the pattern in the silt had matched, exactly, the frost on the inside of the farmhouse windows.

Everything here rhymed. That was the horror of Marrow Fen, Ada was beginning to understand. Not the cold, not the dark. The rhyme. Everything answered everything else, like a call-and-response hymn sung by a congregation of one enormous invisible throat.

She put her shoulder to the doors. They opened without a sound, which was somehow more frightening than a scream would have been. Fifty years of dereliction, and the hinges moved like they'd been oiled that morning.

* * *

Inside, the boathouse smelled of creosote and old rope and, beneath both, something mineral and cold—the smell of the inside of a bell, if bells had insides, which Ada was increasingly willing to entertain.

Her torch beam swept across a floor of blackened boards. A punt lay overturned against the far wall, its paint flaked to ghost-patterns. Fishing nets hung from hooks like curtains in a house where nobody wanted to be seen. And in the center of the room, sitting on a low trolley whose wheels had long since rusted into sculpture, was a crate.

It was a big crate. Big enough to hold, say, a chest freezer, or a piano, or a church bell weighing the better part of a ton. It was built of thick oak planks, and Ada's stomach performed a slow, deliberate somersault, because she knew that grain. She'd spent an hour on Tuesday night with her nose six inches from the extra door in the farmhouse hallway, telling herself she was being ridiculous, memorizing the wood anyway. The same pale oak. The same long, flowing figure in the grain, like water seen through ice.

Someone had built this crate from the same tree—or the same trees—as the door standing upright in the drained fenbed. The door sealed from the inside.

"Oh, that's fine," she said. "That's absolutely fine. Nothing ominous about that whatsoever."

Her voice echoed longer than the room deserved. She counted the echoes. Three. She waited to see if there'd be a fourth.

There wasn't. Small mercies.

She circled the crate. There were no markings except, stenciled faintly on one side in flaking white letters: *PARISH OF ST. BRANDAN'S — MARROW FEN — TO BE HELD —*

The rest had been painted over. Or scratched over. Ada crouched and ran her thumb across the gouges and found they weren't scratches at all. They were letters, dug out deliberately, deep enough to go through the stencil into the wood. Someone had removed the last words with a knife or a chisel, carefully, thoroughly, the way you'd dig a splinter out of a child's foot.

*To be held.* Held where? Held how? Held by whom?

The lid was nailed down with what looked like hundreds of nails, driven in a pattern that was almost regular and then, when you looked properly, wasn't. Ada traced the rows with her eyes and felt the familiar prickle at the base of her skull. The nails formed letters. Crude ones, angular, the kind of letters you'd make if you'd never written before but had watched writing from a long way off.

She couldn't read them. But she could tell they were a name. Something about the spacing—the way the shapes clustered and then paused, clustered and then paused—said *name* the way a footprint says *walker.*

The frost ferns crawled over the crate's corners, thick as fur, and here was the thing that made Ada's hands go cold around her torch: the ferns were moving. Slowly. Imperceptibly, unless you watched for ten seconds and then looked away and then looked back, which is precisely what she did, four times, getting the same result each time. Growing. Reaching. The way ivy reaches for a wall. The way a hand reaches for a rail in the dark.

They were growing toward the center of the lid. Toward the name.

"It knows it's in there," Ada whispered. "Whatever's out there—it knows its own name is nailed shut in a box."

Which raised the obvious question of why it didn't simply open the box. Unless it couldn't. Unless the box, the oak, the nails, the *holding*—unless all of it was doing exactly what it had been built to do, and the frost outside the farmhouse, the prints, the borrowed crewmate humming his untaught hymn, was the sound of something throwing itself against bars for fifty years.

Ada thought of her grandfather's journal, of the entries that stopped mid-sentence in the autumn of 1974. Of the line she'd read eleven times last night, by lamplight, with the doors counted twice: *It cannot be killed. It can only be kept. We keep it with what we remember, and God forgive us, we remember so little.*

"Well," she said, straightening up and patting the crowbar on her belt. "Let's have a look at you, then."

* * *

The nails came out easier than they went in, apparently. Each one slid free with a soft sigh, like the crate exhaling after holding its breath since before she was born. Ada worked her way along the lid seam, counting nails as she pulled—one hundred and forty-four, sixteen dozen, a number that meant nothing to her and probably something to somebody—and stacking them in neat rows on the trolley, because tidiness was a form of prayer when the alternative was screaming.

Halfway down the second seam, she stopped.

The air inside the boathouse had changed temperature. Not colder, exactly. More *attentive.* The way a room changes when someone comes quietly through a door you didn't hear open.

She turned. The doorway behind her stood empty, showing grey water and greyer sky. The ivy curtain hung motionless. But the frost ferns on the inner walls had spread while her back was turned—she was nearly sure of it—and they'd spread in a direction. All of them. Every frond on every board now pointed, delicately, unmistakably, at the crate.

At her.

"No," Ada told the room firmly, in the voice she used on wasps' nests and clients who wanted to pay in exposure. "You don't get to rush me. I'm working."

The room did not reply, which she chose to find respectful rather than patient.

The last nail came free. She set the crowbar down, took hold of the lid with both hands, and paused—because her grandfather's journal had a passage about this too, and she'd read it enough times now to have it by heart. *When you meet it, it will show you what you already know. That is how it hunts. It does not invent. It remembers you better than you remember yourself.*

"Fine," she said. "I know who I am. Mostly. On good days."

She lifted the lid.

* * *

The bell filled the crate the way grief fills a house: completely, and leaving no room for anything else. Bronze, darker than bronze should be, with a patina like pond ice, and carved around its crown in letters worn shallow by decades of hands—or by one pair of hands, many times—a ring of inscription. Ada read it upside down, tilting her head, and the letters swam, and she decided she'd read them properly later, in daylight, with witnesses.

What she couldn't avoid reading was the frost.

Ferns covered the bell's skin entirely—not resting *on* it but seeming to grow *from* it, as though the cold came from inside the metal, as though the bell had been freezing from within for fifty years and the frost was merely its breath made visible. Whorl upon whorl upon whorl, spirals nested in spirals, and in the deepest whorl, right at the lip of the bell where the clapper would strike—

Ada leaned closer.

The pattern there wasn't abstract. It was writing. The same crude, angular letterforms as the nail-heads on the lid. The name again, or part of it, spelled out in frost on bronze, and even as she watched, a new frond unfurled at the end of the last letter, reaching for the next shape it needed, patient as winter.

"Don't finish that," she said quietly.

The frost continued, at its own pace, in its own time. It had been at this for half a century. It was not going to hurry for a pest controller from two counties over.

Ada knew she shouldn't touch it. Every instinct she owned, every page of her grandfather's journal, every cold-reading cliché in every film she'd ever watched on a Sunday afternoon—all of it said *do not touch the haunted bell, you absolute walnut.* She heard her own thoughts say it in her mother's voice, which was strange, because she'd been having trouble picturing her mother's face lately, though the voice came through clear as a bell—

Clear as a bell.

She touched it.

* * *

The metal was warm.

That was the first wrongness, and it arrived with such gentle, domestic shock that Ada almost laughed. Everything in Marrow Fen was cold; the cold was practically the parish motto. But under her palm the bronze held a warmth like a sleeping animal, like a hand that had been holding another hand and hadn't yet let go.

The second wrongness was the sound.

Not ringing. Not yet. A hum, felt rather than heard, rising through her fingers, up the bones of her wrist, settling somewhere behind her sternum like a swallowed note. And threaded through the hum, thin and clear as a wire, a voice.

Her voice.

*"Front door, back door, cellar hatch, cupboard under the stairs—"*

Ada snatched her hand back. The hum stopped. The boathouse was silent except for water lapping somewhere below the floor, patient as arithmetic.

She stood there for a long moment, breathing, telling her heart to be reasonable.

That was her bedtime ritual. Her private, never-told-a-living-soul ritual, the one she'd done since she was seven years old, since the year her grandfather died and the dreams started: lights on, doors counted, recited in order, every night, in the same flat little singsong. Nobody knew it. Not her exes, not her one close friend in Peterborough, not the therapist she'd seen for six weeks in her twenties before deciding the therapist was the one who needed help.

*"Front door, back door, cellar hatch,"* the bell said again, warmly, in her own voice, the way you'd greet an old friend arriving late. *"Cupboard under the stairs, airing cupboard, loft hatch—that's six, that's all six, all present, all correct—"*

And then, horribly, gently, the way a parent continues a child's sentence:

*"Good girl."*

Ada backed away until her shoulders hit the punt. Her breath came in white rags. The frost ferns on the bell had gone still—listening, she thought, listening the way the whole fen listened—and the unfinished letter at the bell's lip hung waiting, one frond short of complete.

"You've been *watching* me," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle, and she hated it. "All my life. You've been—every night, you've been *counting along—*"

The bell said nothing. It didn't need to. The warmth of it filled the boathouse now, radiating out through the crate, and Ada understood, with the calm clarity that arrives at the exact moment understanding becomes useless, that the entity hadn't learned her ritual from watching her windows.

It had taught it to her. Through her grandfather, through whatever passed between keeper and kept, through fifty years of quiet nightly transmission down a telephone line of blood. Her comfort. Her safety. Her *good girl* at the end of every frightened night.

It was a leash. It had always been a leash, and she'd held it out to the thing herself, every night, in her own voice, since she was seven years old.

Outside, across the grey water, a sound reached her—small, human, wrong. Footsteps on the frozen fen. Someone walking toward the boathouse, unhurried, through cold that would kill an unprepared man in an hour.

Ada wiped her face with her sleeve, picked up the crowbar, and went to the doorway.

Idris was coming across the ice. He was in his shirtsleeves. He was smiling, and he was counting under his breath as he walked—she could see his lips move—and when he looked up and saw her, he said, cheerfully, in a voice that was mostly his own:

"He wants to know if you'll finish the name. He says you're the only one left who reads it properly."
