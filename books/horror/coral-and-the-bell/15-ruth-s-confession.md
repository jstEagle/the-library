## 15. Ruth's Confession

Ruth Okafor had given a great many presentations in her career. She had defended doctoral theses, pitched funding boards, and once explained stratigraphy to a room of bored county councillors using nothing but a bag of flour and a rolling pin. She was, by her own estimation, good at talking.

Which was why it was so deeply humiliating that she could not, for the life of her, get the words out now.

She stood in the doorway of Ada Hartwell's room at the farmhouse — she had knocked twice, counted as a courtesy, because she'd noticed that Ada counted things and thought it polite to make herself countable — holding two mugs of tea like a peace offering and feeling, at forty-one years old, exactly like a first-year student who'd broken an artefact and hoped nobody would notice.

Ada looked up from her grandfather's journal. Her eyes were red-rimmed and steady, which Ruth had learned over the past week was simply how Ada's face worked: exhausted and unflinching at the same time, like a lighthouse in bad weather.

"You look," Ada said, "like you're about to tell me something terrible."

"I brought tea."

"That's usually what people bring when they're about to tell me something terrible."

Ruth came in anyway. She set the mugs down on the dresser, between the salt jar and the row of keys Ada kept on a nail — house key, van key, padlock key, and one small brass key that Ruth had never seen used and had privately decided not to ask about. Some doors you didn't ask about. She was learning that about Marrow Fen the way you learn a language: slowly, painfully, and mostly through embarrassment.

She sat on the edge of the chair by the window and wrapped her hands around her mug even though it was too hot, because the burn gave her something honest to feel.

"Idris is dead," she said. "Officially he's missing. But we both know."

"We both know."

"And Dekker is—" She stopped. Dekker had spent the morning standing at the fen's edge in his shirtsleeves despite the cold, talking to a radio that wasn't switched on, laughing sometimes. The site doctor had suggested rest. Dekker had suggested, loudly, that the doctor go and rest somewhere very far away. "Dekker is being managed."

"Managed," Ada repeated, without inflection.

"By me. Badly." Ruth took a breath. It went in crooked. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to let me finish before you decide what I am."

Ada closed the journal. She didn't put her hand flat on its cover protectively, the way people do when they're guarding something — or maybe she did, and Ruth just read too much into everything now. Everything in this place invited over-reading. Frost was a text here. Footprints were a text. Last Tuesday, Ruth had caught herself reading the condensation on the inside of the site cabin's window like a telegram, and had made herself stop only by reciting soil classification codes until the letters stopped rearranging themselves.

"Go on," Ada said.

* * *

"The parish warned us," Ruth said. "That's where it starts. That's the whole ugly shape of it, really — the parish warned us, and I pushed through anyway."

She had expected Ada to react to that. Ada didn't. She just waited, which was somehow worse, so Ruth kept going, because the alternative was silence, and silence in Marrow Fen had started to feel less like an absence and more like a presence taking notes.

"When the bypass route got approved, my firm was contracted for the preliminary survey. Standard work. Drain a section, core it, log anything interesting, move on." She turned the mug in her hands. "Except the sonar came back wrong. You've seen the readings. A structure under the water, intact, oriented — not collapsed, not scattered. Buildings don't stay tidy for fifty years underwater unless they're being *kept*. And there was a return off the church that Idris swore looked like a bell-shape, and I told him he was seeing what he wanted to see, and then I went home and pulled every archive I could access and found out he was right."

"The ledger," Ada said quietly. "The removal."

"The removal." Ruth laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry little bark with no humour in it anywhere. "You know what I thought when I read that entry? *Object removed from church, 1974, name redacted.* I thought: provenance gap. I thought: *career.* Do you understand? There is a drowned village under that fen with its church still standing, and a documented object of unknown significance taken from it on the eve of the flood, and no archaeologist gets a find like that twice in a lifetime. Most never get it once. I have spent twenty years sifting other people's rubbish middens and writing papers that eleven people read, and there it was. Sitting under the water. Waiting."

"And the warnings?"

Ruth set the mug down because her hands had begun to shake and she didn't want to hear the ceramic chattering against the saucer; it sounded too much like teeth.

"There was a letter in the parish file. 1974. From the vicar — Reverend someone, the ink had gone brown — formally objecting to any future disturbance of the site. Not on heritage grounds. He wrote that the village had been flooded *for a reason*, that the reason had been agreed by everyone who mattered, and that the agreement included leaving certain things exactly where they were put." She swallowed. "There was a phrase in it I haven't been able to get out of my head. He said the flood wasn't a tragedy. He said it was a *sentence*, and that the village was serving it, and that digging it up early would be—"

"—like opening a door from the outside," Ada said.

Ruth looked at her. "Yes. How did you know?"

"Because there's a door under your excavation site that's scarred on the inside. Clawed. Someone sealed it from their side and spent whatever time they had trying to get back out." Ada's voice stayed level, but her thumb had gone to the scarred palm Ruth had noticed days ago and never asked about — a ridge of raised skin across it, like a burn in the shape of a crack. "Someone opened it from ours."

The wind moved against the window. Neither of them looked at it. Looking at windows had become, by unspoken mutual agreement, a thing they did sparingly.

"So yes," Ruth said. "I read the letter. I photographed it, filed it, noted it in the risk register as *historical superstition, no material relevance*, and recommended the dig proceed on schedule. Idris wanted to slow down. I told him slowing down was how projects die. Dekker wanted to speed up, and I was the respectable one, the one with the credentials, the one whose sign-off made it all legitimate. When the dredger started coming up with bones in spirals, I wrote it up as ritual deposition, Iron Age or earlier, displaced. When the generator died and the frost bloomed inside sealed cabins, I told myself it was a ventilation fault." She shook her head slowly. "I am very good at telling myself things. It's practically the job description. We don't find the past, Ada. We *excavate* it, which sounds neutral, and isn't. You decide what comes up and what stays buried. I decided. Every signature on those permits is mine."

The kettle ticked in the kitchen below, cooling. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked once, politely, like a neighbour asking if everything was all right. Nothing was all right. The pipe knocked again anyway, hopeful.

"Why are you telling me this now?" Ada asked.

"Because Nell spoke to me." Ruth's mouth twisted. "Ninety years old, half-frozen, forgetting her own daughter's name more often than not, and she still managed to make me feel about four inches tall. She asked me — very kindly, that's the awful part, she was *kind* — whether I'd known what I was digging toward. And I lied to her. First word out of my mouth, no hesitation. *Of course not.* And she looked at me the way you look at a child who's broken something valuable and is lying about it badly, and she said, *'The fen knows when folk lie, love. It's got time to wait for the truth.'*" Ruth pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "So. Truth. Delivered late, at cost, to the person least able to afford my cowardice. That's the confession. That's all of it. I'm sorry doesn't cover it, but it's what I have, along with a Land Rover, a full fuel tank, and a sledge in the site store rated for four hundred kilos."

Ada went very still.

"A sledge," she said.

"The bronze one. For moving equipment across soft ground." Ruth lowered her hands. "I did the arithmetic last night, after Nell. The bell in the boathouse — I've seen the crate dimensions in the salvage manifest your grandfather's era filed, and I've lifted worse. Two people, a sledge, hard frost, and the fen freezing properly by Thursday if the forecast holds." She met Ada's eyes. "Let me help you take it back."

For a long moment Ada said nothing. Then, unexpectedly, she huffed — almost a laugh, thin and tired but real.

"You know what my grandfather's journal says the keeper's duty is?"

"Remembering," Ruth said. "Nell told me some of it. Keeping the count. Feeding it memory so it sleeps."

"It says the keeper works alone." Ada's jaw tightened. "It says that three times, in three different hands — his father's, his, and then instructions for mine. Alone, alone, alone. Like they were arguing with each other down the decades about whether anyone would ever be stupid enough to offer help." She looked at Ruth for a long moment, and Ruth felt herself being weighed — not judged, exactly. Weighed. Measured for trustworthiness the way you'd measure a rope before trusting it with weight. "All right," Ada said finally. "Thursday. Before dawn, while the ice is black and thick. We take the bell out to the door and we give it back."

Ruth exhaled, and it felt like the first full breath she'd taken in a week. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." Ada stood, crossed to the window, and paused with her hand on the curtain. "And Ruth — thank you for telling me. Genuinely. It takes more spine to confess than most people ever find." A pause. "But I have to ask you one thing, and I need you not to take it personally, because I ask it of everyone now, including myself."

"Ask."

"How did you get up here tonight? Which stairs did you use?"

Ruth blinked. "The back stairs. Why?"

"And you came straight up? Didn't stop anywhere?"

"Straight up. Knocked twice." Ruth smiled uncertainly. "Counted, for you."

Something in Ada's face eased, but only partway, the way a knot loosens but doesn't come free. "Good," she said. "That's good."

* * *

Ruth left at half nine, promising to be back at six with the sledge and the tow straps and a thermos of something aggressively alcoholic disguised as coffee. At the bottom of the back stairs she paused, out of Ada's sightline, and did something she hadn't done since she was a girl: she checked behind her. Just once. Just a quick turn of the head down the dark little stairwell, where the wallpaper hung in pale ribbons and the bulb had died sometime during the previous administration.

Nothing. Of course nothing. Stairwells were famously nothing-shaped.

She let herself out the kitchen door, crossed the yard to where the Land Rover sat silvered with frost, and scraped the windscreen with the edge of her card while her breath plumed. The night was enormous and clear, stars sharp as pinholes punched in tin, and the fen beyond the hedge lay white and silent under a moon three days off full. Beautiful, she thought. It's actually beautiful. That's the trap of it. Nobody warns you the haunted places are lovely.

She was halfway around to the driver's door when her torch beam caught the ground by the gate, and she stopped.

Frost lay over the yard in a smooth, unbroken sheet — smooth everywhere except for the prints.

Boot prints. Deep ones, pressed clean through the rime into the mud beneath, crisp as casts. They came out of the darkness beyond the gate, walked with unhurried confidence across the yard, and stopped directly beneath the kitchen window, where whoever had worn them had apparently stood for some time, facing the house.

Ruth crouched beside them, telling herself the whole time that there was a mundane explanation, that boots were common, that yards were places boots went — and shone the torch full on the nearest print.

Lug pattern. Diagonal bars. The distinctive worn crescent at the heel where the tread had gone bald.

She knew that pattern intimately. She had bought those boots in Keswick four years ago. She had resoled them once. They were, at this precise moment, on her feet — and they were bone-dry, and they had not left the yard since she'd parked, and the prints were sized exactly right, and they led *away* from the house, out through the gate, toward the fen. Toward the drained bed. Toward the door standing upright in the black mud with its claw-marks facing inward.

Toward it — and then, twelve feet from the gate, they stopped. Both boots planted side by side. Facing the door. As though someone wearing her shape had stood in the dark looking at it for a long, patient while.

As though it had been waiting for her to come outside and see.

Ruth stood up very slowly. Behind her, in the farmhouse, a light went on — Ada, checking, counting, doing whatever Ada did — and Ruth found herself torn between the overwhelming urge to run inside and hammer on that door, and the colder, quieter thought that arrived underneath it, calm as a footnote:

*If it can wear my boots, it may already have worn my face. And if it has, then the woman who promised to help carry the bell on Thursday might not be entirely the woman who made the promise.*

Her breath hung in the air. Out on the fen, far off, something that was not an animal and not machinery made a sound like a bell heard through several feet of water and several decades of silence — one low note, patient, almost friendly.

Ruth got in the Land Rover, locked all four doors, and sat with her forehead against the icy wheel, whispering her own name over and over like a password, trying to remember exactly when, in the last week, she had last been absolutely, provably, unmistakably herself.

She couldn't.

That was the worst of it. She couldn't.
