## 16. The Long Cold Night

Ada Hartwell had spent thirty-one years in the pest control business, and in that time she had learned one iron law of the trade: the moment you stop being afraid is the moment you get sloppy. Fear kept your gloves on. Fear made you check the seals twice. Fear was, in its way, a colleague.

So when she woke at 2:47 a.m. on the fourth night in the Marrow Fen farmhouse and felt—nothing, a flat and terrible calm where her dread should have been—she knew something had changed.

She lay still under the blankets and listened. The house ticked and settled around her like an old dog dreaming. The lights were all burning; she'd checked them at midnight, as always. Downstairs, the kitchen clock did not tick.

That was wrong. She'd wound it herself.

She got up, dressed in the dark out of habit, then turned on every lamp between the bedroom and the stairs because habit was all she had left that she trusted. The hallway light buzzed. The landing light buzzed. She counted doors as she went—one, two, three, four—and stopped at the top of the stairs to count again, because the number had come up four both times now, and for two years running the correct answer had been four, and before that, in her grandfather's day, she'd been told it had been four as well. Four was the number of the house. Four was safe.

Four was safe. She said it twice more, silently, like pressing a stamp into wax.

The kitchen clock read 3:04 and its second hand stood straight up, trembling but not falling, like a man on a ledge deciding.

"Right," Ada said aloud, because saying things aloud helped, and because there was no one left in the house to hear her except possibly the thing in the frost, and let it hear. "Let's have a look at you, then."

* * *

Nell Pargiter was sitting at her own kitchen table across the lane when Ada arrived, coat over nightdress, boots unlaced, hands wrapped around a mug of tea she had plainly forgotten to drink. The old woman's kitchen was lit like a chapel—every bulb, every candle, the oven door open for the glow of it—but the light seemed to be having no effect whatsoever on the cold. Frost ferned up the inside of the windows in patterns too regular for weather: rows, columns, something almost like handwriting.

"Nell," Ada said gently. "You shouldn't be up."

"I couldn't sleep." Nell's voice was steady, which frightened Ada more than weeping would have. "I got up to make myself a hot water bottle and I stood in front of the cupboard and I couldn't—" She stopped. Started again, slower, feeling for each word like a woman crossing ice. "I keep a photograph on the shelf above the kettle. My girl. My daughter. I look at it every morning, forty years, every morning, first thing, before the kettle even."

Ada looked at the shelf. There was a photograph there—a young woman with Nell's strong jaw and someone else's laugh, standing in front of a caravan, squinting into sun.

"I looked at it tonight," Nell said, "and I knew it was my daughter. I knew it the way you know a chair is a chair. But her name—" Her hands tightened around the mug until the knuckles went white. "Her name has gone, Ada. Not slipped. Gone. Like a tooth out of the gum. There's a hole where it was and the hole is smooth."

Ada sat down across from her. Her own heart was doing something complicated and painful that she chose not to examine.

"What was her name?" she asked carefully. "If you say it, maybe it'll settle back in."

Nell opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again, and what came out was horror, quiet and total:

"There isn't a name. I keep reaching for it and there's just—smoothness. And worse than that, love. Worse than that, I can't remember being worried about it five minutes ago. I know I was crying. My face is wet. But the crying's gone too, and I'm just sitting here like a teapot." She finally looked up, and her eyes were dry and terribly clear. "It's not taking things anymore, is it. It's tidying them away. And it doesn't hurt after, and that's the worst thing I've ever known."

Ada reached across the table and took the old woman's hands. They were freezing.

"It took my mother," she heard herself say, and then heard it again from the inside, and understood what she'd said. "I tried to think of her face just now, walking over. I could tell you her hair was brown. I could tell you she wore a green coat to church. I cannot—I cannot see her. It's like a photograph that's been left facing the wall so long you've forgotten what's on the front."

"When?" Nell asked.

Ada thought about it. That was the awful part—she had to *think about it*, reconstruct it, infer it like a detective. "Sometime tonight. Between counting the doors at midnight and waking up. There wasn't a moment. There's never a moment with it. That's how you know it's been there—it leaves no seams."

They sat together in the over-lit kitchen while the frost wrote its patient rows up the glass, and outside, across the whole drowned valley of Marrow Fen, every clock that still ran anywhere was standing at 3:04 and would stand there, though neither woman knew it yet, until morning.

* * *

At dawn Ada drove out to the site, because sitting still felt like agreeing to something.

The fen lay under a skin of new ice, thin and grey at the edges, thickening toward the drained excavation pit where Dekker's crew had torn open the valley floor. The oak door still stood upright in the mud, untouched by the explosion, untouched by anything, its claw-scarred planks wearing a rime of frost like a fur collar. Nobody was working. The site office had its lights on but its windows frosted solid from the inside, and when Ada hammered on the door Ruth Voss opened it looking like a woman who had been awake since the Flood.

"You've seen it," Ruth said. Not a question.

"I've seen it." Ada came in and stood near the heater, which was running full blast and accomplishing nothing. "My mother's face. Gone. Nell's lost her daughter's name. What have you lost?"

Ruth laughed—a short, cracked sound with nothing in it. "What haven't I? I lost my supervisor's name this morning. The man who hired me. I've worked with him six years. I rang him to report and got halfway through 'Good morning, Dr.' and then nothing, just air. And here's the thing that's going to keep me up for the rest of however long I've got—" She pulled a notebook across the desk and shoved it at Ada. "Look at the timestamps."

Ada looked. Ruth had been writing down every loss as she noticed it, time-stamped, a surveyor's log of her own mind eroding.

"They're not random," Ruth said. "They're sequential. It started with people I met most recently and it's working backward through my life like it's reading a book. Last week first. Then last month. It's going to reach my childhood, Ada. It's going to reach my mum, and I am going to sit in some room knowing I loved somebody whose face I can't find, and I won't even be able to grieve properly because grief needs a shape and it's taking the shapes."

"Why you?" Ada asked, though she suspected.

"Because I remember the most." Ruth said it flatly, without self-pity. "I've been cataloguing this place for months. Every record, every interview, every sonar scan. I'm the fullest memory of Marrow Fen that's currently walking around, and it's eating me like I'm a packed lunch." She rubbed her eyes. "Dekker's worse off. He won't say what he's lost. He's been in his cabin since three o'clock with the engine of his truck running and the radio on loud, all stations, static and pop music and shipping forecasts, like noise might fill the holes."

"And the clocks?"

Ruth looked at her strangely. "How did you know about the clocks?"

"Lucky guess. Educated. Go on."

"Every watch, every phone, every vehicle dashboard on this site reads 3:04. The ones that were switched off too. I checked a phone in a drawer that hasn't been charged in a week. 3:04." Ruth's voice dropped. "Idris found the flood records last month, before he—before the fen took him. The sluices failed at 3:04 in the morning, April 1974. The village went under at 3:04. Whatever's down there isn't just remembering that hour, Ada. It's *keeping* it. It's holding the whole valley at that minute like a breath."

Ada thought of the kitchen clock, its second hand trembling on the ledge of the twelve, refusing to fall. Refusing to move forward. Refusing to let anything move forward.

"It's not feeding anymore," she said slowly, working it out as she spoke, hating the shape of it as it formed. "Borrowing—that was feeding. Taking our memories and wearing them, growing fat on them. This is different. This is preparation. You don't eat the house you're moving into. You pack it."

"Pack it," Ruth repeated.

"Empty it. Strip it back to walls. It's clearing the ground." Ada looked out the frosted window toward the standing door, small and dark in the grey light. "It wants the valley empty of everything except itself and whatever walks through that door. And it's saving us for last, or saving us for something."

* * *

She went to see Dekker because someone had to, and because she'd learned that the brash ones were the ones who broke quietest.

His cabin smelled of diesel and cold. He sat on the bunk with the radio going—some phone-in programme, voices chattering brightly about traffic and weather, filling the little room with other people's ordinary lives—and he didn't turn it down when she came in. He looked ten years older than he had a week ago. His forearms, always bare, always brown, were mottled with cold.

"Hartwell." He nodded at the radio. "Can't stand the quiet. You notice that? Whole world's gone quiet. Birds gone. Even the generator's got a different sound, like it's whispering. So I put this on." He gestured at the chattering box. "Except now I listen to it and I can't remember why it matters. Some bloke's moaning about a roundabout in Leeds and I used to find that funny, that sort of thing, and now it's just—words. It's all just words."

"Dekker. What did it take from you?"

He was quiet long enough that the radio filled it. Then: "My brother." His voice came out sanded flat. "Younger brother. Danny. Died when we were kids—ice, a pond, the whole story, happens every winter somewhere. I carried him my whole life. Every job I ever took near water, every safety rule I ever enforced, every bloke I ever shouted at for not wearing a flotation vest—that was Danny. That was me making his death mean something." He finally looked up. "This morning I remembered having a brother. I remember the fact of him. I can't remember him. And the worst part, Hartwell—the part I'll deny saying if you ever quote me—is that it doesn't hurt. It's like the pain was part of the memory and it went with it. I've got a smooth hole where my brother was and I keep prodding it waiting for it to bleed and it won't."

Ada sat down beside him on the bunk, because there was nothing else to do, and grief shared is at least grief witnessed, even when the grief is happening in advance.

"It's coming for all of us," Dekker said. "Working backward. You feel it? Like something reading you from the end of the book?"

"I feel it."

"My money says it reaches the beginning by tomorrow night." He turned the radio up a notch, defiantly, pointlessly. "So whatever you're planning, Hartwell—and don't tell me you're not planning, you've got the face of a planner—do it fast."

* * *

She went back to the farmhouse as the light died, and made herself do the rounds.

Every lamp lit: kitchen, hall, stairs, landing, bedroom, bathroom, the little lamp in the cupboard where her grandfather's journal lived. She counted the doors—four, four, four, four, at intervals, like a sentry—and each time the count came right she felt a relief so sharp it was nearly joy, and understood, with a clarity that felt like another kind of theft, that this was exactly what her grandfather must have felt, every night of his life, and that he had never once explained it to her, and that his silence had been the kindest and cruelest inheritance anyone ever handed down.

She sat at the kitchen table with the journal open in front of her and read his entries again, the ones she'd found days ago, the ones that had rearranged her entire life into a ritual she hadn't known she was performing.

*Remembered the village today,* he wrote, over and over, decades of it. *All forty-one households. Remembered the bell ringing. Kept the count.* And once, near the end, in a shakier hand: *Stopped in '74. Told myself it was finished. Passed the duty to my son without telling him what it was. God forgive me, I passed it like a debt.*

And beneath that, the entry Ada had read a dozen times and only now truly heard:

*It does not want blood. It wants to be remembered. Starve it of that and it will take the remembering by force.*

Outside, the wind moved across the fen, and the frost on the kitchen window grew.

Ada watched it happen. She was too tired to be afraid, and perhaps that was the entity's doing too, and perhaps it wasn't, and she had no way left to check. The frost came in slow fronds from the corners of the glass, meeting in the middle, forming letters—not names this time, not the dead, not the crew, not her own half-finished name. Something else. A sentence, assembling itself letter by letter with the patience of water finding a level.

She read it as it completed, and her breath fogged the glass, and behind her breath the frost held its shape anyway:

**BRING IT HOME BEFORE THE COUNT RUNS OUT.**

Below the sentence, smaller, almost gentle, in the same spidery script:

**YOU KNOW WHERE THE DOOR IS. YOU HAVE ALWAYS KNOWN.**

Ada sat very still. Then she took up a pen and, on the flyleaf of her grandfather's journal, beneath forty years of his entries, wrote her own line in a hand that only shook a little:

*Night of the long cold. It has taken Mother's face, Nell's girl's name, Dekker's brother, and is working backward through us all. Clocks stopped at 3:04. Tomorrow I take the bell across the ice and put it back where it belongs, and if the price is every memory I have of everyone I love, then let it be paid by someone who knows what she's paying for.*

She signed it: *Keeper of the count, third of her line.*

Then she blew out nothing, turned off none of the lights, and went upstairs to count the doors one last time before dawn.

Four. Still four.

For now.
