## 18. The Door Opens Both Ways

The sledge runners sang across the ice—a thin, keening note that Ada thought might be the last honest sound in Marrow Fen.

She had the rope over her shoulder, leaning into the pull, boots finding the old road beneath the ice by feel more than sight. The bell rode behind her in its crate, lashed down with every strap she owned, and it was heavier than bronze had any right to be. It was heavier than memory. She'd stopped being surprised by that somewhere around the third mile.

Ruth walked beside her, one hand on the crate to steady it, her breath coming out in white plumes that the wind took and shredded. Neither of them had spoken for a long while. There wasn't much left to say that the frost hadn't already written on the world in letters ten feet high.

Ahead of them, where the drowned village's main street ran down toward the church, the oak door stood upright in the fenbed like a headstone for someone who hadn't finished dying.

The drained section Dekker's crew had opened had refrozen overnight—black glass, clear as a window, and through it Ada could see the door's shadow stretching down and down into water that shouldn't have been there anymore. The door stood on dry ground. Its shadow stood in forty feet of black water. She had decided, somewhere back along the ice, not to think about that too hard. Her grandfather's journal had a line about it: *The door is where it is. The rest is where it wants you to think it is.*

"Ada." Ruth's voice, low. "How far?"

"Quarter mile. Maybe less."

"And then what? We ring it like a dinner bell and hope?"

"We ring it," Ada said, "until something answers."

Ruth made a sound that was half laugh, half prayer. "You know, when I put in for this survey, I told the county board there might be medieval foundations under the fen. Career-making find, I said. I had a slide deck." She shook her head. "I did not have a slide for *the door opens both ways*."

"No," Ada said. "Nobody ever does."

The joke landed soft between them, the way jokes do at funerals—not funny, exactly, but necessary, like a handrail on a staircase going down into the dark. Ada found she was grateful for it. Grateful for Ruth, out here, hauling beside her when she could have driven away and written her report and let the fen keep its secrets. Whatever else happened tonight, Ada would remember that.

She would remember it, she thought, if she was allowed to remember anything at all.

The frost was closing in around them as they walked—the pattern completing itself, Nell had said, letter by letter, name by name—and it moved now like weather with an opinion. Ice ferns bloomed across the fen surface ahead of them and behind them, spiraling inward toward the door, and in the spaces between the ferns the ice had gone cloudy, opaque, as though the fen were closing its eyes against what it was about to watch.

Behind them, faint but persistent, came the crunch of a third set of boots.

Dekker. He'd been following since the boathouse road, sometimes fifty yards back, sometimes twenty, calling out to them in a voice that slid between registers—his own gruff bark, then something older and wetter underneath it. *Give it up, Hartwell. Let the count end. Your granddad knew when he was beaten.* Then, minutes later, raw and human and terrified: *Ada. Please. It's got most of me. Come get the rest.*

She didn't look back. Looking back felt like an invitation.

* * *

They reached the door as the sky went the color of dishwater.

Up close, it was worse than she remembered from the photographs and better than she feared, which is a sentence Ada would never have believed herself capable of thinking before this week. The oak planks were scarred by clawing—from within, always from within—and the hinges had fused into a single mass of metal, like the door had been poured rather than built. Frost covered everything except a hand-shaped patch at the center, at chest height, polished smooth by decades of touching from the other side.

Someone had spent a very long time pressing their palm against this door, hoping.

Ada set the rope down. Her shoulders screamed. She ignored them.

"Help me with the crate," she said.

Ruth didn't move.

"Ruth. The crate."

"I've been thinking," Ruth said, and her voice was wrong. Not borrowed-wrong—not yet—but careful, the way a person sounds when they're walking on ice they know is thin. "About the trade. What it offered you. The bell for your memories. Everyone you love."

"It offered," Ada said slowly. "Past tense. I'm not trading."

"But it *would*, wouldn't it? That's the shape of the bargain. One keeper's memories, and the count ends, and everyone else walks away whole." Ruth turned to face her, and her eyes were bright and feverish and full of something Ada recognized too late as resolve. "Nell can't remember her daughter's name. You couldn't remember your mother's face this morning—I saw you trying. It's taking you anyway, piece by piece, and at the end you'll have nothing left to give and it'll take the village besides. But if someone offers freely—"

"Ruth."

"—if someone offers *first*, maybe it takes just the one. Maybe that's enough. Maybe—" Ruth stepped forward, past her, toward the door, and raised her hand toward that smooth, hand-shaped patch of bare wood. "I don't have anyone. That's the thing nobody tells you about ambition—it eats the people part first. I pushed this dig through. Idris is under the ice because of my slide deck. So it should be mine to pay. It's *fair*, Ada. For once in my life I want to do the fair thing."

And Ada, who had spent three days learning to read frost the way other people read handwriting, looked at Ruth's boots.

There was no frost on them.

Every surface in Marrow Fen wore frost now—every boot, every sleeve, every eyelash. The cold wrote on everything that moved through it. But Ruth's boots were clean, and dry, and Ada understood, with a clarity that arrived like a blade sliding between ribs, that the woman standing in front of her had not walked across four miles of frozen fen.

She had *appeared* on it, somewhere back along the way, wearing Ruth's coat and Ruth's guilt and Ruth's terrible, generous arithmetic.

The real Ruth was still lying on the bank of the fen where Ada had last seen her, gasping, or perhaps she wasn't lying anywhere at all anymore.

"You're not her," Ada said quietly.

The thing wearing Ruth smiled, and the smile was almost right. That was the horror of it—not the wrongness but the nearness. It had studied them all week. It had learned how Ruth tucked her chin when she laughed, how she apologized before asking questions, how she carried guilt like a stone she'd chosen herself. It had learned all of it from the outside, the way you learn a song by listening through a wall.

"Aren't I?" it said, in Ruth's voice, with Ruth's weariness. "I'm the best part of her. The part that wanted to fix it. The part that was willing to pay. You should say thank you, Ada. I'm giving you the version of your friend that doesn't run away."

"The real Ruth ran *toward*," Ada said. "That was the problem. That was always her problem." Her hand found the crowbar lashed to the sledge. "Get away from the door."

"You misunderstand the offer." The Ruth-thing spread its hands, reasonable, warm. "I'm not here to stop you. Ring the bell. I want you to. But first, hear the terms one more time—from someone with a kinder face than his."

The fen went silent.

Not quiet—silent. The wind stopped mid-gust. The creak of the ice ceased. Even the sledge runners' song cut off like a throat closed by a hand, and into that silence, from everywhere and nowhere, from the ice below and the air above and the grain of the door itself, came a voice Ada knew better than her own mother's.

"There's my girl."

Her grandfather. Not the recorded, radio-crackled version Dekker had heard—that had been a lure, a fisherman's fly. This was the whole man: the rasp of him, the Yorkshire flatness, the little cough he made before saying anything serious. Ada's knees tried to fold. She locked them.

He came out of the dark beyond the door's edge, walking across the ice without breaking it, and he was *right*. The flat cap. The cardigan with the mended elbow. The way he stood with his weight on his heels, hands in pockets, as though the end of the world were a bus that was running late. He had died when Ada was nineteen, and she had grieved him cleanly, and she had never once, in all the years since, imagined she'd see him again—and so some traitorous cellar of her heart opened its doors wide and wept with welcome.

"Grandad," she said. Careful. Counting, the way she counted doors. *This is not him. This is not him. This is not him.*

"You've done well, love." He smiled, and his eyes crinkled, and it was unbearable. "Better than I did. I kept the count for eleven years and then I got tired, and look what tiredness bought." He gestured at the fen, the frost, the door, the whole drowning world. "But you can end it. That's the truth nobody in our family ever wrote down because they were afraid of it: it *can* end. Give it the bell and give it your remembering—all those people you carry around in you, your mum, your dad, Nell, that Ruth girl, all of them—and it lets go. Everyone lives. You just won't know them." He tilted his head, gentle as a doctor delivering news. "You won't feel the loss, love. That's the mercy of it. You can't miss what isn't in you. You'll wake up light as a bird."

"That's not mercy," Ada said. "That's just theft with good manners."

"It's a fair price."

"It's *your* price. It was always your price. You stopped counting in '74 and passed the debt down like a christening gown." Her voice cracked and she hated it. "I counted doors my whole life, Grandad. Every hotel, every house, every night. I thought I was mad. I thought it was a quirk, a fear, something broken in me. And it was *you*. It was the job. You gave a child a ritual and never told her why, and now you're standing there wearing your own face asking me to hand over everyone I love so you can finally sleep?"

The grandfather-shape was quiet for a moment. When it spoke again, the warmth had gone out of it, and what remained was older, and colder, and had never been her grandfather at all.

"He begged too," it said. "In 1974. In this same spot. He asked us to take his memories instead of the village's. Do you know what we told him?" The shape smiled with her grandfather's mouth. "*Later.*"

Ada's hand closed on the crowbar.

But it was the Ruth-thing that moved first—or seemed to. It stepped toward the door again, hand outstretched toward the smooth patch, and said, in Ruth's voice, cracking with borrowed sincerity: "Take mine instead. Take hers' too if you must, but take mine *first*. I offer freely. I offer—"

"*No.*" Ada didn't shout it. She said it the way you close a door on wind. "You don't get to be noble. You're not noble. You're a *copy*." She swung the crowbar—not at the thing, she wasn't fool enough to think she could hit it, but at the crate. The lashings parted. The lid fell open.

The bell lay in its bed of straw, and the frost ferns on its bronze skin were moving, crawling, arranging themselves into letters, into a word, into—

A name.

She couldn't read it yet. It was still forming. But the entity's attention snapped to it like a compass needle, both shapes—the grandfather, the false Ruth—turning in unison, and in that unison Ada saw the seam. Saw that they were one thing wearing two coats. Saw that it was afraid.

"You want my memories," Ada said. Her breath steamed. Her hands, on the bell's rim, were steady—her hands were always steady; it was the only inheritance she'd ever valued. "Come and count them yourself."

She lifted the bell.

It rang once, against nothing, against the air itself, and the sound went *down*—through the ice, through the fenbed, through whatever floor the world kept under its floor—and the door's fused hinges shivered like a struck tuning fork.

The grandfather-shape took a step back. The Ruth-thing's face ran like candle wax, reforming, deciding what to be next.

And out on the ice behind them, from the direction of the village, came a sound that stopped Ada's heart and restarted it in the same beat:

A second bell. Faint, underwater, ringing in answer.

And a voice—hoarse, human, furious, alive—shouting her name across the ice.

"ADA! *Wait!*"

Ruth. The real Ruth. Running out of the dark with frost in her hair and terror on her face and her boots, Ada saw with a sob of relief, filthy with true fen-mud—boots that had walked every one of those four miles, boots the frost had written on, boots that proved nothing except that she had come the hard way, the honest way, the way that could not be copied.

Which meant the thing beside Ada had been Ruth for miles. Which meant everything the false Ruth had said—the sacrifice, the fairness, the willing payment—had been designed, tuned, tailored, to make Ada lower the bell and open her heart and let the thing walk in through the front door of her.

Which meant, Ada realized, turning back to the door, that while she had been grieving her friend, the grandfather-shape had drifted closer. Close enough to touch the crate. Close enough, now, to touch *her*.

"Too late to count, love," it said, in her grandfather's voice, in her mother's voice, in her own voice, all at once. "The pattern's nearly done. Look."

Above the door, in the frost that coated the oak, the final letters of a name were writing themselves—letter by letter, curl by curl—and Ada lifted the bell again, higher, higher, ready to swing it with everything she had left, everything she loved, everything she was about to lose—

The frost finished the last letter.

And the door began, softly, patiently, from both sides at once, to open.
