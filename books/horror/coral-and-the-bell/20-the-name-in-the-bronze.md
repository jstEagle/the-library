## 20. The Name in the Bronze

The door did not open so much as admit her.

That was Ada's first thought, standing on the threshold with the cracked bell still humming against her palms: doors that swing inward are invitations, and she had just accepted one. The oak frame stood upright out of the fenbed like a gallows, and beyond it there was no drained mud, no grey February sky, no Ruth gasping and coughing behind her—only a cold that was not temperature but attention, pressing against her face like a palm testing whether she was ripe.

Behind her, Ruth said her name. Just once. Just "Ada," in a voice scraped raw from the fen water, and Ada reached back without turning and squeezed her hand once, hard, the way you'd squeeze someone before stepping into a confessional.

"I have to see it," Ada said. "All of it. That's how this works. You can't return something until you know what it is."

"That's not how returning things works at all," Ruth whispered.

"It's how returning *people* works," Ada said, and stepped through.

* * *

Inside was the village.

Not the drowned one—the real one. Marrow Fen as it had been, rendered in frost so fine it looked like lace laid over air: the high street with its crooked gutters, the post office with its red paint gone pink, the school with a hopscotch grid scratched into frozen ground. Everything white. Everything still. And everywhere, pressed into every surface like a signature repeated by a hand that never tired, fingerprints. Thousands of them. Millions. Small ones, child-sized, clustered at knee height along the walls of the school. Large ones, work-roughened, dragged down doorframes as if people had gripped them on their way past for luck.

Ada understood, with a lurch, what she was looking at. Not haunting. *Record.* The fen had kept everything the flood took, filed in ice, and the thing that lived here had spent fifty years pressing its hands against the world because it remembered having hands.

"Hello?" she said, and her voice fell flat, swallowed, the way sound behaves in churches.

At the end of the high street stood the church, whole and unbroken, its tower exactly as Nell had described it—and beneath the tower, in the belfry doorway, something was waiting.

It wore her grandfather's shape. It had worn it across the ice, had offered bargains in his voice, had learned him the way you learn a coat from the outside. But now, as Ada walked toward it—because walking away had stopped being an option somewhere around the boathouse, maybe around 1974, maybe around the day she'd first counted doors—the shape began to fail. It flickered like a bulb on its last hours. The shoulders slumped wrong. The face ran like candle wax and reformed, ran and reformed, trying on Dekker, trying on Idris, trying on a woman Ada didn't recognize and then realized, with a grief that arrived ahead of its explanation, that she *should*—

Her mother. It tried on her mother's face, and Ada felt the memory of that face go thin as paper held to light.

"No," Ada said quietly. "You don't get her. She's mine. Whatever else I owe you—you don't get to keep her."

The shape shuddered. And then, all at once, it gave up pretending, the way a tired person finally sits down, and became what it had been before anyone taught it to be anything else.

A man. Ordinary height. Ordinary build. Drowned-grey skin gone translucent with age, hair drifting around his head as if he were still underwater, still forty feet down, still ringing. He wore a bell-ringer's coat, the kind with reinforced elbows, and his hands—his poor, enormous, scarred hands, the hands that had made the prints in the frost, too large for any living resident because they belonged to no living resident—hung at his sides with the terrible patience of tools put away for winter.

He looked at her. He had been looking at her, Ada realized, since the farmhouse. Since the first print in the frost. Perhaps longer. Perhaps since 1974, when a little girl who would grow up to count doors had been carried through this square on her father's shoulders, and a man in a belfry had watched the water come up the lane and thought: *someone will remember me.*

"Thomas," Ada began, and stopped, because she didn't know why she'd said it. The name had simply arrived, the way names do in dreams.

The drowned man shook his head. Slowly. Almost gently.

*No,* said the frost, blooming across the church wall behind him in letters that formed and melted and formed again. *NOT HIM.*

"Then who?" Ada asked. "What's your name?"

The frost went wild. Letters chased each other across every surface—the school wall, the post office window, the hopscotch grid—dozens of attempts, dozens of failures, shapes that almost resolved and then collapsed like breath on glass. *J——. R——. A name starting with a sound like a bell struck underwater.* And underneath all of it, written smaller, written in the careful hand of someone who has practiced an apology for fifty years:

*THEY TOOK IT.*

* * *

She heard the bell before she understood it—the cracked bronze in her arms, cold as a heart that had stopped, and yet faintly, faintly sounding. Not ringing. *Remembering* ringing. And inside the sound, inside the crack that she and Ruth had beaten into it on the threshold, there was writing.

Ada turned the bell. The inscription ran around the crown in letters worn nearly smooth, and where the crack split them, they caught the frost-light and showed themselves:

**I WAS HERE. I RANG FOR THEM ALL. THOMAS ——— KEEPER OF THE ROPE, 1911–1974.**

The surname was gone. Not worn—*chiselled*. Deliberately, deeply gouged out of the bronze, the way you'd gouge a name out of a war memorial, the way you'd cut a face out of a photograph. Someone had taken a chisel to this bell before crating it, before rowing it out, before burying it in a boathouse under a lie about parish accounts.

And Ada understood.

"Oh," she said. The word came out very small. "Oh, you poor bastard."

The drowned man flinched at the words—or perhaps at the gentleness in them, which was worse, which was the one thing he hadn't been offered in half a century.

Because she could see it now, the whole shape of it, the way you finally see the pattern in frost when you stop trying to wipe it away. In 1974, the village hadn't wanted to flood. The village had fought. And when fighting failed, when the reservoir men came with their maps and their compensation cheques, some of the village had found something else to fight—a scapegoat, a reason, a man. Thomas, keeper of the rope, who rang the bells, who knew everyone's comings and goings, who had perhaps opposed the flooding too loudly or too strangely or simply been the wrong man at the wrong meeting. And when the waters rose anyway, they hadn't drowned him with the rest of them. They had done something older and crueler. They had sealed him in the crypt below the church—behind an oak door, bolted from the *outside*, though the claw marks said everything about which side had wanted it opened—and they had taken the bell, because a ringer without his bell cannot be called, and they had chiselled off his name, because a man without a name cannot rest, cannot be mourned, cannot even be *remembered properly*. You can't say a requiem for a blank.

They had made him into a pest. Into a problem. Into the thing you hire someone like Ada Hartwell to deal with.

And then they had flooded the village over his head and called it progress, and gone home to their new houses, and told their children nothing, and let the silence do the rest of the drowning.

"You weren't hungry," Ada said. Her voice cracked on it. "You were *starving*. They starved you. Fifty years under the water with your own name taken away, and the only food left was other people's memories, because yours had been stolen, and you—" She stopped. Started again. "My grandfather helped carry the bell out. Didn't he. And he couldn't live with it, so he invented the count. Remembering the village every day, feeding you scraps of it, keeping you asleep instead of setting you free. Because free meant angry, and asleep meant safe. He chose safe. My whole family chose safe, for three generations, and called it duty."

The frost had gone very still. Every surface. Every letter. As if the entire drowned village were holding its breath.

The drowned man took one step toward her. Then another. Up close, Ada could see that his translucence wasn't decay—it was erosion. He was worn thin, like the inscription, like the church steps, like everything that gets touched too long by too much time. There was almost nothing left of him except the wanting to be known.

Ada looked down at the bell in her arms. At the gouge in the bronze. At the empty space where a name should be.

She thought about her mother's face, and how thin it had gone, and how much more it would cost before dawn. She thought about Nell, forgetting her daughter's name upstairs in the farmhouse. About Idris smiling up through the ice. About Dekker, half-borrowed, following them across the fen with someone else's patience wearing his voice. All of it, all of it, because a village full of frightened people had decided that one man's name was the price of their comfort.

"Well," Ada said, and wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, briskly, the way she did everything. "We'll soon fix that."

* * *

The problem, of course, was that she didn't know the name either. The chisel had seen to that. She turned the bell, hunting the crack, hoping the fracture might have opened some hidden depth of the metal—but bronze doesn't hide anything. Bronze says what it says.

Except.

Except that bells are cast, not carved, and the men who cast them sign their work twice—once in plain sight, and once where only God and the founder would look. Ada heaved the bell up, grunting, cursing, her boots slipping on frost-slick cobbles, and tilted the great cracked mouth toward the light of the belfry doorway.

There, inside the shoulder of the bell, in small neat letters the chisel had never reached:

**THOMAS MERRILEWES.**

"Merrilewes," Ada read aloud. And the name went out of her like heat out of an open door.

The effect was not dramatic. That was the terrible thing. She had braced for thunder, for the fen splitting open, for the sky to tear. Instead the drowned man simply—stopped eroding. The translucency firmed. His hands, those huge scarred hands, flexed once, as if remembering they were hands and not just instruments of complaint. And the frost, the endless frost that had been writing itself across Marrow Fen for weeks—across windows, flour bins, cabin glass, the skin of the living—began, mid-letter, to stop.

Ada saw it happen on the church wall beside her. A word forming—her own name, she realized, *ADA HARTWELL*, the final line of the pattern, the deadline Nell had warned her about—and the last letter simply faded, unfinished. An *L* with no foot. A sentence that would never now reach its full stop.

Thomas Merrilewes looked at her. And his face, which had been a mask, which had been a hunger, which had been fifty years of pressing against the inside of the world—became, briefly, unbearably, just a man's face. Tired. Grateful. Ashamed, maybe, of what starvation had made him do, though Ada would spend the rest of her life unsure whether that shame was his or hers, lent to him like everything else.

*THANK YOU,* wrote the frost, in letters already softening, already melting back into weather. And then, smaller, in the careful apologetic hand:

*GO HOME, KEEPER. THE COUNT IS DONE.*

"Not quite done," Ada said. Her voice was wrecked and she used it anyway. "One more job. You're coming with me, Mr. Merrilewes. Or rather—" she hefted the bell, settling its weight against her hip like a child, like a coffin, like both—"this is. You've been kept from your bell long enough. We're going to hang it, and we're going to ring it, and everyone in whatever's left of this parish is going to hear it, and they are going to *know your name*."

The drowned man smiled. It was the worst thing she had seen in three weeks, and the best. Behind him, the church door swung open on darkness—not hungry darkness now, just the ordinary dark of a building at evening, waiting for lights, waiting for voices.

Ada Hartwell, keeper of the count, third of her line, counted the belfry steps as she climbed them. Nine. She always counted. But for the first time in her life, she wasn't counting because she was afraid.

She was counting because someone down below, in the dark, needed to hear that someone was coming—and because a bell with a name on it deserves a proper ringing, and a proper ringing needs a steady hand, and hers, God help her, was the steadiest for fifty miles.

The rope hung waiting in the tower, dusted white with frost ferns, exactly where a keeper of the rope would have left it.

Ada took hold.
