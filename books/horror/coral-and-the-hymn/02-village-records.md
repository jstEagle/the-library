## 2. Village Records

The parish office of Marsh Hollow occupied the ground floor of what had once been a schoolhouse, and it smelled the way all such offices smell: of paper gone soft at the edges, of tea made in a mug that had never once been washed so much as rinsed, and of time — not passing, exactly, but pooling. Tamsin Halloran had spent eleven years in rooms like this one. He knew their grammar. The filing cabinets arranged by decade rather than alphabet. The noticeboard with a fete poster from three summers ago still pinned hopefully in the corner. The kettle with a limescale crust like a geological stratum.

He also knew that the person behind the desk would tell him everything he needed to know within twenty minutes, provided he asked about nothing directly.

"Mrs. Aldous?" He extended his hand. "Tamsin Halloran. Hargreaves & Bell. I'm doing the survey on the old vicarage."

The woman who rose to meet him was perhaps sixty-five, small and neat as a hymnal, with silver hair pinned back and reading glasses on a chain around her neck. Her handshake was firm and brief, the handshake of someone who had decided long ago exactly how much of herself to spend on any given stranger.

"Of course you are," she said. "You're the third this year."

"The third surveyor?"

"The third *young man* from *the firm*." She said the words with a faint, dry amusement, as though quoting from a brochure she found mildly ridiculous. "There was one in March. Very tall. And one in June who wore far too much aftershave for a Tuesday."

Tamsin smiled and produced his folder. "Well, I'm afraid I'm stuck with you until Friday. I'll need access to the parish records — anything relating to the vicarage. Deeds, plans, correspondence. The building's listed, so the diocese wants a full historical picture before anyone talks about restoration."

"Restoration." Mrs. Aldous repeated the word the way another woman might have said *tax audit*. She looked at him over her glasses for a moment — an appraising look, he thought later; the look of someone measuring a doorframe against a piece of furniture she already knew wouldn't fit — and then nodded briskly. "Sit down, Mr. Halloran. I'll make tea. You don't want the records on an empty stomach. They're heavy going."

"I've read a lot of parish records."

"You haven't read *ours*."

She said it lightly, almost cheerfully, and went through to the little kitchenette, and Tamsin sat down in a chair that creaked its disapproval and told himself, as he had told himself in a hundred villages, that rural clerks were protective of their archives the way farmers were protective of their hens. It meant nothing. It was a temperament, not a warning.

He had a professional habit, honed over eleven years, of narrating away oddness. A door that swung shut on its own was a settling foundation. A cold spot in a hallway was a draught under the floorboards. A house that felt watched was simply a house that hadn't been loved lately — buildings were like dogs that way, and Tamsin had a soft spot for both. He'd learned early that the surveyors who lasted were the ones who could look at a stain shaped like a handprint on a nursery ceiling and write *damp ingress, probable roofing failure* without their pen hesitating.

His pen did not hesitate now. He opened his notebook and wrote: *Parish office. Clerk cooperative (guarded?). Records requested.*

Then he waited, and looked around, because waiting in other people's offices was where the real surveying happened.

The room had two windows, both facing the church across the lane. St. Brannoc's, according to the sign — a squat grey thirteenth-century thing with a tower that looked, even from here, slightly wrong. Tamsin squinted at it. It took him a moment to identify what was wrong, and when he did, he wrote it down immediately, because it was exactly the sort of detail that made reports interesting:

*Bell tower appears empty. No bell visible in frame.*

Odd. Every village tower had a bell; most had several, and the smaller the village, the prouder they were of them. There'd be a plaque inside, no doubt — *This bell was cast in 1740 and has rung out for every wedding, funeral, and questionable decision since.* Villages loved their bells the way they loved their grudges: publicly, and forever.

On the wall beside the window hung a framed photograph, sepia going brown, of a group of men standing proudly around something enormous and dark. Tamsin got up for a closer look. The something was a bell — a great cracked-hulled thing, taller than the men beside it, resting on timbers. Beneath the photograph, in copperplate ink on a card gone ivory with age:

*St. Brannoc's bell, removed from the tower prior to recasting. October 1911.*

"Ah." Mrs. Aldous had returned with two mugs of tea and a tin of biscuits that had clearly been purchased during a previous administration. She followed his gaze to the photograph. "That was the last time anybody in this village agreed on anything," she said. "They argued for six years about whether to recast her or let her be. Half the village said the crack was a scandal, the other half said the crack was the *point*."

"What was the point?"

"Well, that depends which half you ask." She set his tea down. "The Foundry came, took her away in a cart, and melted her down. And then—" she paused, and there was the smallest hitch in it, a stitch dropped and picked up again so smoothly that a less attentive man would have missed it entirely, "—then they sent back a new bell, and everyone pretended the whole business had been very sensible."

"And did they hang the new one?"

"They laid the foundation stone for it," said Mrs. Aldous, and drank her tea.

Tamsin waited for more. None came. He wrote: *1911 — bell melted. Replacement never hung? Verify tower interior.* His pen hovered. *Foundation stone?*

"Where's the new bell now?" he asked, casually.

"In the tower," said Mrs. Aldous.

"But there's no—"

"Sugar's in the tin," she said pleasantly, and turned to the filing cabinets.

* * *

The records were kept in a back room that had once been the school cloakroom, judging by the row of low hooks along one wall, now holding nothing but dust and one child's coat, decades out of fashion, that nobody had ever claimed. Mrs. Aldous fetched boxes down from the shelves with the efficiency of a woman who knew precisely where everything was and resented being asked to prove it.

"Deeds first," she said. "Then the vicarage correspondence. Then, if you must, the general parish files. I'd skip the general files myself. They're mostly complaints about the drainage and minutes of meetings nobody enjoyed."

"I live for meeting minutes," said Tamsin. "It's why I became a surveyor. The glamour."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. "You're not what I expected, Mr. Halloran."

"What did you expect?"

"Someone who'd take the money and go home." She said it flatly, without malice, the way you'd say *someone who'd take the umbrella and leave the wellies*, and then she left him alone with the boxes.

He started with the deeds, because he was a professional, and they were exactly as dull as deeds should be — conveyances and mortgages and a boundary dispute from 1953 that had clearly consumed the village the way wars consume nations. He photographed what he needed, noted what he needed, and allowed himself, after forty minutes, the pleasure of the general files.

The 1911 file was thin. A letter from the foundry confirming receipt of the old bell. An invoice for the new one — £84, twelve shillings, itemised down to the clapper. And then a single sheet of parish letterhead, undated but filed between October and November, written in a hand that pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through:

*The work is done and the metal is gone and I have prayed over it and I do not know what we have done. Rector Wm. Prowse asks me to record that the bell gave up her voice willingly. I cannot record it. She screamed. I heard her scream in the fire and so did nine others and we will say what we are told to say but God heard it too.*

Tamsin sat back.

Eleven years. He had read a great many things in parish archives — fevered letters about popery, furious letters about organists, one memorable eight-page denunciation of a curate's choice of hat. Clergymen's hands shook. Clergymen were dramatic. Molten bronze did make noises; he knew that; metal sang and groaned as it went, and to a frightened religious man in 1911, standing close to a furnace—

But *nine others*. Nine others had heard it too, and someone had told them what to say, and the writer had obeyed while refusing, on paper, in the permanent record, to agree. That wasn't hysteria. That was a man building himself an alibi out of ink.

He photocopied the letter. Then he sat with it a moment longer than he needed to, telling himself the extra moment was scholarly thoroughness.

The 1974 box was thicker, and out of order — someone had been through it and put it back imperfectly, the way you can always tell a drawer has been searched by a person who loves the contents. Newspaper clippings, mostly. Tamsin flipped past a harvest festival notice, a story about the new village hall, and then stopped.

The clipping was from the regional paper, September 1974. The headline ran across two columns:

**SEARCH CONTINUES FOR MISSING VILLAGE GIRL**

Below it, a photograph. A girl of about ten, gap-toothed, hair in two careful plaits, caught mid-laugh by whatever camera had caught her. She was wearing a cardigan with a pattern of small flowers, and she was laughing at someone just outside the frame, and the laugh was so alive that Tamsin felt it land somewhere under his ribs, in the unprofessional part of him.

*Coral Vane, 10, daughter of Mr. Edwin Vane of Marsh Hollow, was last seen on the evening of Saturday 14th September. Police and villagers have searched the marshes...*

He read it twice. The article was short — missing children were shorter news then, if they stayed missing — and it ended on the standard note: *Police appeal to anyone with information to come forward.* There was no follow-up clipping. In a village that kept everything — that had kept a coat on a hook and a complaint about drainage from 1961 — there was no follow-up.

No *search called off*. No *family thanks public for support*. No memorial notice, no anniversary appeal, nothing. As if the story had been amputated rather than concluded.

"Coral Vane," he said aloud, to the empty cloakroom, and the name sat oddly in the air, the way a word does when you've said it in the wrong room.

He was still looking at the photograph when Mrs. Aldous appeared in the doorway with a fresh pot of tea, and he saw — he was quite sure he saw — her eyes go to the clipping before they went to his face. Just for a fraction of a second. The way your eyes go to a bruise.

"That one's not really relevant to the vicarage," she said.

"No," Tamsin agreed. "I suppose it isn't." He turned the clipping face-down, deliberately, and watched her shoulders come down a quarter of an inch. "Just context. Villages are their history, aren't they? You can't survey a place without it."

"Mm," said Mrs. Aldous.

She refilled his tea. She did not leave. Instead she perched on the edge of the old teacher's desk, hands folded, and regarded him with the expression of a woman assembling words she had assembled many times before, for several previous young men from the firm.

"Mr. Halloran. May I give you some advice? Unprofessional advice. Free of charge, which in this office makes it unique."

"Please."

"Don't stay late at the vicarage. Do your measurements in daylight, take your photographs, and be off home before dusk. It's a damp old place. Bad for the chest. Bad for the sleep." She smiled — a real smile, warm even, and that was somehow worse. "And if you hear the bell, pay it no mind. Old towers carry sound strangely. Sound gets into the bones of a place and stays there, like damp."

"The tower's empty," Tamsin said. "I noticed. There's no bell in the frame."

"No," said Mrs. Aldous. "There isn't."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like the silence after a door closes elsewhere in a house. Tamsin found that his rationalising machinery — the well-oiled, eleven-year-old machine that converted every strangeness into a surveyor's footnote — was turning over and producing nothing. Because she hadn't denied it. That was the thing. He had handed her the perfect opening — *there's no bell* — and she had not said *of course not, don't be silly*. She had agreed, calmly, that the tower held no bell, and had not explained why a village would leave its tower empty for sixty years, and had looked at him with those kind, tired eyes as though checking whether he was going to be sensible about it.

"Mrs. Aldous," he said carefully, "what happened to Coral Vane?"

"She was lost," said Mrs. Aldous, "on the marsh. In 1974. It was very sad, and the village grieved, and it was a long time ago." Each clause arrived separately, wrapped, delivered. A parcel of sentences tied with string. "Children were lost on the marshes all over this county, in those days. The water takes what it likes. There's nothing in it, Mr. Halloran, that concerns a property survey."

"Except the vicarage is where she was last seen," Tamsin said. "Isn't it? Near it. The search started from the church."

For the first time, Mrs. Aldous looked genuinely at him — not appraising, not managing, but seeing — and what was in her face was not guilt and not fear exactly, but something older than either: the expression of a woman who had carried a bucket for fifty years and had forgotten it was heavy until someone asked about the water.

"The vicarage is a house," she said quietly. "A dead house. Whatever it was, it finished a long time ago, and the best thing anyone ever did for it was leave it alone. So do your figures, take your fee, and let sleeping dogs lie where the marsh put them." She stood, brisk again, the moment folded away like laundry. "Now. I've the church keys if you want the tower measured. Mind the steps. They're older than they look."

Everything, Tamsin thought, taking the keys, was older than it looked in this village. Including the silences.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon in the church, measuring the tower's emptiness, and it was strange work — pacing out a space built to hold a bell and holding nothing but pigeons and his own echo. The foundation stone was set into the tower's west wall at shoulder height, a slab of granite with a cross cut into it and an inscription worn shallow: *A.D. 1974 — THE BELL SHALL SPEAK AGAIN.* Someone had laid flowers at its base. Fresh ones. He noted that, and then noted that he had noted it, and did not write down the thought underneath the thought, which was: *who brings flowers to a stone?*

By five o'clock the light was going amber over the marsh, and Mrs. Aldous's advice sat in his head like a stone in a shoe. Dusk. Be off home before dusk. He packed up his equipment, walked out into the churchyard, and paused at the gate to look back at the tower — the empty frame, the darkening sky behind it, the whole structure standing against the evening like a mouth open to speak.

From the vicarage, further up the lane, he thought he heard — faintly, unmistakably, impossibly — a single note.

Deep. Bronze-voiced. Patient.

One toll, and then nothing, and then the doors of the houses along the lane began, one after another, softly, to close.
