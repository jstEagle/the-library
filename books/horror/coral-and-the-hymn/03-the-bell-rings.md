## 3. The Bell Rings

By half past five, Tamsin Halloran had convinced himself the morning had been unremarkable.

This was a professional skill. Ten years of surveying empty houses taught you that every old building made noise — pipes ticking as they cooled, floorboards shrinking in the dry air, wind finding gaps no sensible draught should fit through. He'd catalogued the sounds of dereliction the way other men catalogued football scores, and he'd learned early that the surveyor who flinched at every creak finished nothing. So when he'd heard — distinctly, unmistakably — a door close somewhere above him at nine that morning, he'd written *structural settlement* in his notes and underlined it twice, which was what he did instead of believing things.

He sat now in the Marsh Hollow Arms, the village's only pub, with a pint of something local and amber and a plate of chips going cold, and he told himself the day had gone well. The vicarage appraisal was three-quarters done. The ledger could wait for tomorrow; Mrs. Aldous had promised him access to the parish records in the morning, and there was no hurry. Nobody was queuing up to buy a rotting house on the edge of a marsh. The estate agent in Norwich had been frank about that. *"It's been on our books eleven years,"* she'd said. *"We've had four offers. All withdrawn."*

Withdrawn offers. Tamsin had made a note of that too, and then, being who he was, had made a smaller note beneath it: *probably financing.*

The pub was warm and nearly empty. A fire burned in the grate with the cheerful indifference of a fire that has never once considered the metaphysics of its fuel. Behind the bar, the landlord — a broad, ruddy man whose name Tamsin had already forgotten — polished glasses that didn't need polishing, and a dog slept by the hearth with its legs twitching through some dream of pursuit.

"Out-of-towner," the landlord had said when Tamsin arrived, not as a question but as a diagnosis, delivered to the room at large. There had been one other customer, an ancient man in a flat cap who had looked at Tamsin the way farmers look at weather they don't trust, and left without finishing his drink.

Now, at half past five on an October evening, the light outside the pub's windows had begun to fail in that particular way marsh light fails — not darkening so much as thinning, the sky going the colour of skimmed milk over water, the reeds beyond the last houses turning to black stitching against it. It was beautiful, in a way Tamsin's profession had trained him to price rather than feel. *Good views of open fenland,* he might have written. *Potential.*

He was halfway through his pint when the first door closed.

He noticed it because of the sequence. Doors closing in an empty pub make one sound; doors closing along a street make many, and this was many — a soft, heavy *thud* somewhere down the lane, followed almost immediately by another, further off, and another, nearer, and then a run of them, overlapping, the way rain starts: scattered drops, then a steady sheet. Thud. Thud-thud. Thudthudthudthud. Every house on the street, front doors shutting in a wave that moved from the church end of the village toward the marsh end, orderly as dominoes falling.

The landlord set down his glass mid-polish.

Tamsin watched him cross to the window, look out, and then — and this was the part Tamsin would turn over later, in the small hours, unable to file it anywhere — reach up and pull the curtain closed. Not glance out and go back to work. Pull the curtain fully shut, with both hands, like a man boarding a window before a storm.

"Early night?" Tamsin asked.

The landlord didn't answer. He was looking at the ceiling, or through it, his head tilted slightly, listening. The dog had stopped dreaming. It stood up, walked to the corner of the room farthest from the windows, lay down facing the wall, and put its head between its paws.

Tamsin felt the first cold thread of it then, the thing that would unravel his whole careful evening: the silence. Not quiet — pubs were never quiet, there was always the fridge hum, the fire, the tick of cooling pipes, the distant traffic of the A-road. But the fridge had stopped humming. He realized he couldn't hear the fire either, though the flames still moved. The whole room had gone acoustically dead, like the inside of a padded cell, like the moment after a gunshot in a film where the sound man pulls everything out to show you how it feels.

And into that silence, from across the marsh, came the bell.

* * *

He knew — he knew with absolute certainty, the way you know your own name — that the church tower held no bell. He had stood in that tower not six hours earlier. He had measured it. The belfry was an empty timber frame, eight oak posts black with age, and in the middle of it, bolted to a beam, hung nothing but a rusted bracket and a length of frayed rope, cut short, tied off, useless. The 1911 record he hadn't read yet would have told him why, but he didn't need it told. You don't hang a bell in a frame like that. You don't ring what isn't there.

The bell rang anyway.

It came from the direction of St. Aldhelm's, across the churchyard and over the flat fields, and it was deep — deeper than any parish bell had a right to be, a sound less heard than felt, arriving in the chest before the ear. One toll. A long pause, long enough for Tamsin to think, absurdly, *wind chime, someone's got a bloody great wind chime.* Then a second toll, and the pause again, and he understood with a lurch that the pauses were the worst part, because the pauses were patient. Whatever counted the strokes was in no hurry. It had all the time in the world, and it wanted him to know it.

Three. Four.

The landlord had backed away from the window until his shoulders met the optics shelf. His lips were moving — counting? praying? Tamsin couldn't tell — and his face had gone the grey-white of old lard. He wasn't looking at Tamsin. He wasn't looking at anything. He was doing what everyone else in Marsh Hollow was evidently doing behind their pulled curtains, which was enduring it.

Five.

Tamsin's rational mind threw up its hands and offered its final inventory: *acoustic anomaly, possibly a resonance effect from the fen, low-frequency phenomenon, there are documented cases of—* and then the bell tolled a sixth time and the sixth toll was different. The first five had come from the church. The sixth came from closer. From the lane outside the pub. From the direction of the vicarage.

Seven, from the vicarage gate.

Eight, from the garden path.

The landlord made a small sound, high and involuntary, like a kettle deciding against boiling.

Tamsin stood up. Later he would try to reconstruct why — call it professionalism, call it stupidity, call it the simple animal fact that when a sound is coming toward you, standing still feels worse than moving. What he said to himself, buttoning his coat with fingers that didn't entirely cooperate, was: *You're a surveyor. Go and survey it.*

"Don't," the landlord said. It was the first full sentence he'd managed. "Whatever you're thinking of doing, sir — don't. Get in your car. Drive back to Norwich. Don't even stop for petrol."

"It's just a bell," Tamsin said, and heard how young and thin his own voice sounded.

The landlord finally looked at him, and what was in his face wasn't madness or superstition but something much harder to dismiss: pity. Plain, human pity, the kind you give a man who hasn't read the letter yet.

"Aye," he said. "That's what we tell ourselves too."

Nine.

Tamsin went out into the cold.

* * *

The lane was empty and the light was nearly gone. Overhead the sky had cleared to a hard, starry black, and the harvest moon — two nights off full, fat and orange as a lamp — was climbing over the fen, laying a silver road across the wet fields. His breath smoked. The reeds hissed somewhere in the dark, a sound like whispering, like a crowd murmuring behind a screen.

Ten.

From ten feet away, the vicarage door.

Tamsin stopped at the gate. The tolls had stopped coming from different places now; they simply came from the house, each one seeming to originate a little further inside it, as if the bell were walking through the rooms toward the front door, unhurried, expecting to be let out — or expecting him to be let in. The windows were dark. The ivy on the east wall hung motionless in air that should have been moving it. And the door —

The door was opening.

Not swinging wide, not dramatically. Just easing inward, an inch, two inches, slow as a held breath released, with none of the groan you'd expect from swollen, fifty-year-old oak. It opened the way a host opens a door when he's been watching you come up the path and has timed it exactly.

Tamsin's entire body voted to leave. His legs had opinions. His scalp tightened and his heart was going like a bird trapped in a shed, and every instinct that had kept his species alive for a hundred thousand years screamed at him that the correct response to a door opening itself in a dead house at moonrise was to be elsewhere, immediately, ideally in a different county.

But underneath all of it, faint and clear, threading through the last of the bell's dying resonance, he heard it again — the same thing he'd heard that morning through the study wall and filed under *pipes.*

Humming. A child, humming. Four notes, rising, then a fifth that fell away like a hand letting go.

It sounded, God help him, lonely.

"Tamsin Halloran," he said aloud, to the lane, to the moon, to whatever was listening. "Property surveyor. I'm expected."

The words came out steadier than he felt, and they were true in a way he only understood afterward: he hadn't decided to say them. They had simply arrived, the way you answer when someone calls your name.

The door swung wide.

Beyond it the hall was dark, but not empty-dark — warm-dark, ember-dark, the darkness of a room with a fire banked low somewhere out of sight. And it was warm. Actual warmth flowed out over the step and touched his face like breath, carrying the smell of woodsmoke and beeswax and, beneath it, something older and sweeter that he couldn't name and wouldn't try to.

On the hall table inside, where that morning there had been nothing but dust and a dead vase, a candle was burning.

Tamsin Halloran, who had spent ten years explaining away every sound an old house ever made, stood on the threshold of Marsh Hollow vicarage with the harvest moon at his back and the door held open before him, and understood — not believed, *understood*, the way you understand gravity — that the house had been waiting for him since 1974.

He stepped inside.

Behind him, gentle as a sigh, the door closed — and somewhere below the floors, below the foundations, below the cold black water of the marsh itself, a bell that had been melted down sixty years ago tolled once more, softly, like a count beginning.
