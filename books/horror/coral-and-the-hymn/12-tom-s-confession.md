## 12. Tom's Confession

Tom Aldous had spent fifty years learning how to be small.

It was a skill, like any other. His sister had it too — Margaret could make herself so unremarkable that men forgot she was in the room while she stood at the parish counter taking their money. But Tom had perfected it differently. Where Margaret's smallness was a kind of armor, his was a penance. He had learned to sit in corners of the Fox and Hare with his back to the wall, to speak only when spoken to, to laugh half a beat after everyone else so his laughter sounded borrowed. He had learned to walk past the vicarage without looking at it, which was harder than it sounded, because the house looked at you whether you looked at it or not. He had learned, above all, not to hum. Fifty years without humming a single note, because there was a hymn he had heard once through a wall and he did not trust himself to start anything and stop where he chose.

Now, in the last week of September, with the harvest moon fattening in the sky like something being fed, Tom's body had begun forgetting how to be small. It was coming apart instead. The house was taking him back piece by piece, and it was taking him in the order he deserved: first the warmth from his hands, then the strength from his legs, then — this morning — the hearing in his left ear, so that the world arrived slightly tilted, everything leaning toward the silence on one side of his skull.

He understood what was happening. He had understood since the night Tamsin Halloran knocked on his door and asked about 1974, because the cold had started in his feet within the hour, climbing him like water filling a lock. The house knew when its secrets were stirring. It always had.

So Tom did the thing he had been rehearsing in the dark for half a century, without ever quite believing he'd have the nerve.

He put the kettle on.

* * *

The kettle was a ritual. His mother's kettle, copper-bottomed, dented on one side where — the family joke went — she'd thrown it at their father, though in truth he'd dropped it drunk and let her keep the story because her version made her happy. Tom set two cups out, then reconsidered and set out three, because you never knew with that surveyor; the man turned up like weather. Then he sat down at his kitchen table to wait, and found that waiting, for once, was easy. He had done nothing but wait for fifty years. This was simply the last thing he was waiting for.

Tamsin came at dusk, as Tom had known he would. The man had a face like a man doing sums he didn't want the answer to. He'd had it since the night the bell rang. Tom liked him for it. Most people who came asking questions about Marsh Hollow wanted the answer to be *no*, nothing here, move along, and wore disappointment like a coat. Halloran wanted the answer to be *yes* so badly it frightened him, and he kept asking anyway. That was either courage or stupidity, and in Tom's long experience the two were indistinguishable until afterward, when everyone got together and decided which it had been.

"Mr. Aldous." Tamsin stood in the doorway, hat in hand, marsh mud to his knees. "I'll be quick."

"You'll have tea first," Tom said. "I've got maybe three days left in me and I don't intend to spend them being hurried by a man who doesn't take sugar."

"I take sugar."

"Then we'll get on famously."

Tom poured. His hands shook, but they'd shaken all week, and the tea stayed mostly in the cups, which he counted as a triumph. They sat across from each other at the table, and outside the window the light went down over the marsh the way it always did, slow and reluctant, like a child at bedtime.

"You've been to see my sister," Tom said.

"She told me about the tithe. In general terms."

"In general terms." Tom smiled, and was surprised to find the smile still worked. "That's Margaret. She could tell you the sea was wet and call it general terms." He drank. The tea tasted of tin now; another thing going. "She'll have told you the village pays, and pays every year, and pays gladly, and never asks what happens to what's paid. She'll have told you that much because it lets her sleep. What she won't have told you — because she doesn't know, and I thank God nightly she doesn't know — is what the paying looks like from the paying end."

Tamsin set his cup down. "Then tell me."

"I'm going to," Tom said. "That's rather the point of the tea."

* * *

Here is what Tom remembered, and he had spent fifty years making sure he remembered it properly, because forgetting would have been the one unforgivable thing:

In October of 1974, Tom Aldous was nineteen years old and strong as an ox and stupid as a post, and Edwin Vane — who was twenty-two and already spoke like a man reading from documents nobody else had seen — came to him at the Fox and Hare and said, *We need you Friday night, and you're not to ask why till after.*

Everyone loved Edwin then. That was the part Tom needed Halloran to understand, the part no one ever believed. You had to have been there. Marsh Hollow in '74 was drowning — foot-and-mouth had gutted the farms, the young ones were leaving in numbers, old Mr. Petchey had drowned himself in the mere and the coroner called it misadventure and everyone called it what it was. And then Edwin stood up at the harvest meeting, twenty-two years old with his father's jaw and none of his father's doubt, and said the hollow would survive. Said it plainly, the way you'd say the tide would come in. And it did survive. That winter, against all sense, things turned. A grant came through. A buyer appeared for the dairy. People started sleeping again.

Nobody connected it to Coral Vane going missing. Nobody dared. The connection lived in the village the way a splinter lives in a thumb — felt whenever you pressed on it, ignored whenever you could manage, and slowly poisoning either way.

"We took her from the churchyard," Tom said now, and heard his own voice come out flat and terrible, the voice of a man reading a shopping list. "You'll want me to say we stole her. We didn't. Stealing implies she was ours to want. We dug her up out of consecrated ground — she'd died in the spring, fever, buried quiet behind the east wall because the Vanes were proud even in grief and didn't want the whole village counting their sorrow — and we carried her to the vicarage in a wheelbarrow, wrapped in sacking, at two in the morning, and I held the gate open, and I remember thinking the gate was heavier than it should have been. Like something on the other side of it was holding on."

Tamsin had gone very still. Stillness suited him; he had the look of a man built for it.

"Why the churchyard?" he asked quietly. "Why did the house want—"

"Because she'd been given to God." Tom said it before he could dress it up. "That was the rule, or the shape of the rule, or whatever you call a rule when the thing making it is a house. It wouldn't take just anyone. It wanted what had already been surrendered. A body given over and then taken back — that was the payment. We didn't understand it. We weren't meant to. Edwin understood enough, and the rest of us understood we were cold and tired and scared and that Edwin said it would save us, and when you're nineteen, that's arithmetic enough."

"There were others. That night."

"Five of us." Tom's voice caught, and he let it. "Me, Edwin, the Petchey brothers — both dead now, and good riddance to how they lived after — and Danny Foulkes, who drowned in '89, accidental-like, in the mere, same as old Mr. Petchey. The house has been collecting its witnesses slow, these fifty years. I'm the last one left, and it knows it. That's why I'm coming apart like wet paper. It wants the page back before anyone reads it."

"The page," Tamsin said.

And Tom, who had been waiting for exactly this, reached into the pocket of his cardigan — the good cardigan, the one Margaret knitted him for his sixtieth, the one he'd decided to die in because a man ought to die in something someone made for him — and brought out a folded sheet of paper, yellowed soft as cloth at the creases, and laid it on the table between them.

* * *

It was a ledger page. Tom watched Halloran read it, and watched the color go out of his face line by line, and thought: *good*. Not because he enjoyed frightening the man, but because a frightened man read carefully, and this page deserved careful reading. Every name was on it. All five takers, in Edwin's young handwriting, neat as a schoolbook. The date. The words *tithe rendered, accepted* in a hand none of them could account for — ink brown-black, dried wrong, written in a book that had been locked in the vestry cabinet all night. And beneath, added later in the same wrong ink, the names of everyone who had known and kept silent, appearing one per year, like frost forming on a window. Margaret's name was not among them. Tom had checked a hundred times across fifty years, checking not for her sake but for his own: one clean thing, one person he'd managed to protect.

"Edwin's name is second," Tamsin said. "Not first."

"He wrote the page. The house ordered it." Tom shrugged, and the shrug cost him; his shoulder cracked like green wood. "First name's mine because I held the gate. I've had fifty years to wonder if that's a coincidence. It isn't. Nothing in that house is a coincidence. It's a filing system, that's all it is. It files what it's owed and it files who owes it, and I have been *filed*, Mr. Halloran, since the night I was nineteen years old, and next week it closes my folder."

"What do you want me to do with it?"

"Honestly?" Tom laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing went on long enough that Tamsin half-rose, and Tom waved him back down with fingers that had gone the color of candle wax. "Honestly, I don't care what you do with it. Burn it, publish it, nail it to the church door like your German fellow. It's yours. That's the whole point. It stopped being mine the moment I handed it over, and Lord, but that feels good." He pressed his palm flat on the table, feeling the absence of the paper under it the way you feel a pulled tooth. "Fifty years I've carried that page the way you carry a stone in your boot. Everyone in this village carries theirs — some little stone, some folded-up thing they don't look at. And we've all convinced ourselves the carrying is noble. Necessary. The price of the hollow surviving." He shook his head. "It isn't noble. It's just heavy. And I am so tired of heavy, Mr. Halloran. I'd like to be remembered honestly. Not as a victim of the house, not as a poor old man the hollow chewed up. As what I was. A strong lad with a wheelbarrow who held a gate open for the devil's accountant because he was told it would save the farm. Remember me honestly, and let the honest remembering sting."

Tamsin folded the page away inside his coat, carefully, the way you handle something alive. "Mrs. Aldous will lose you," he said. "Your sister."

"My sister will outlive us all out of pure stubbornness." Tom smiled at the window, at the dark coming down. "But you tell her — no. Don't tell her anything. Let her read it in the church like everyone else, when the time comes, if it comes. She's spent fifty years believing her brother was merely weak. Let her find out he was wicked. It's kinder than it sounds. Weakness you have to forgive forever. Wickedness you can bury."

* * *

Tamsin stayed another hour, though neither of them said much more. Tom taught him how Mrs. Aldous took her tea — stewed, left bag in, an abomination she'd inherited from their mother along with the temper — and Halloran actually wrote it down, which struck Tom as the funniest thing that had happened in years, a grown man noting *tea: ruined, deliberately* in a notebook alongside names of the dead. When the surveyor finally rose to go, Tom walked him to the door, or rather was walked, propped on the doorframe, and the cold came in off the marsh and Tom felt it settle into him like a tenant.

"One more thing," Tom said. "The girl. Coral. You've heard her. In the walls."

"Yes."

"And?"

Tamsin was quiet a moment. "She's not what the house is," he said finally. "Whatever else is true. She's something the house took and couldn't digest. She's still — her. Asking to go home."

Tom nodded, slowly, and found his eyes were wet, and did not wipe them, because a man confessing ought to leave the evidence visible.

"That's the worst of it, you know," he said. "Fifty years I've dreaded her ghost, and she turns out to be the only innocent soul in this entire story. The house knew what it was doing, taking a child who'd sing. Everything in that place sings back at it. Even the walls." He gripped the doorframe. "Go home, Mr. Halloran. Sleep if it lets you. And when you dig — and you will dig, I can see it standing up in you like a crop — dig fast. The moon's nearly fat, and the house gets hungry near the full, and it has my scent now. It'll come for the page through whoever holds it."

"It can try," Tamsin said.

"No," Tom said, and smiled, and meant it kindly. "Don't invite it. That's the first lesson of Marsh Hollow, and I'm giving it to you free, fifty years late. Never say *it can try*. Say nothing. Keep your head down and your hands busy and your name out of ledgers." He paused. "Although I suppose we're well past lessons."

Tamsin went down the path into the dark, and Tom watched until the mud swallowed the sound of him, and then he shut the door and stood a moment with his hand flat against the wood, listening.

Far off, across the marsh, something rang once. Not the church — the church had no bell. Just a single note, low and sweet and patient, hanging in the air the way a question hangs.

Tom smiled at it, unafraid for the first time in fifty years.

"Not tonight," he told it. "Tonight I've done my work."

He put the kettle on, for one, and sat down to wait for morning, and if the tea tasted of tin, well. He'd earned a little tin.
