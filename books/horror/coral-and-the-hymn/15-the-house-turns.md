## 15. The House Turns

The refusal sat in Tamsin's chest like a swallowed stone all the way back down the lane.

He had said no. He had stood in the vicarage's front parlour — the one with the hearth that burned without wood and the armchair that faced the door as though expecting someone — and he had told the house, out loud, in the flat professional voice he used for disputing invoices, that he would not take its money, would not sign its ledger, would not forget.

*No,* he had said. *I'm not for sale.*

And the house had gone very quiet, the way a dog goes quiet before it decides about you.

Now, walking back toward Mrs. Aldous's cottage through the grey end of an October afternoon, he found himself cataloguing the silence of Marsh Hollow and finding it wrong in a new way. He had spent ten years surveying empty buildings. He knew the silence of abandonment — it had a texture to it, a settled dustiness, like a room that had exhaled long ago and didn't mind. This was not that. This was the silence of a held breath. Of forty-odd households all pretending very hard not to be home.

A curtain twitched at the Hendersons' window and snapped shut so fast it made a sound like a small gunshot.

"Charming village," Tamsin muttered. "Lovely sense of community. Very cohesive."

His voice sounded strange in his own ears, and he stopped walking.

It took him three full seconds to understand why: it echoed. Not off the hedges or the distant church tower — there was nothing near enough to bounce sound back at him — but inside his own head, a half-beat delayed, as though someone were repeating his words back to him in a room just behind his skull. Testing them. Learning the shape of his voice the way you'd learn the shape of a key by pressing it into wax.

"That's not real," he said firmly.

*That's not real,* agreed the echo, pleasantly.

Tamsin walked faster.

* * *

Mrs. Aldous opened her door before he knocked, which meant she'd been watching from the window, which meant everyone in Marsh Hollow watched their windows now, the whole village become one enormous nervous eye.

"You refused it." She said it flatly, pulling him inside and bolting the door behind him with both bolts and, he noticed, a third bolt he hadn't seen her use before. "You actually refused."

"It seemed the polite thing. It offered me eleven thousand pounds and total amnesia. I've had better offers from insurance companies."

"This isn't funny, Mr. Thackeray."

"No," he agreed, and was surprised to find his hands were shaking, so he put them in his pockets where they could shake privately. "No, it isn't. But I find if I don't make jokes, Mrs. Aldous, I start screaming, and I'm told that frightens the neighbours."

Something that might have been the ghost of a smile crossed her face and died there. She looked older than she had a week ago. She looked like a woman who had been carrying something heavy for fifty years and had recently been asked to carry it up a hill.

"The others took the offer," she said. "Every surveyor since '74. There's been six of you. The house pays well because it can afford to. It's had fifty years of tithes." She turned away, busying herself with a kettle that did not need filling. "The ones who refused—"

"There were none?"

"There was one. 1989. A young man from the council. He stayed to fight." She set the kettle down with exaggerated care. "They found his car at the marsh edge. They never found him. The ledger has his name in it, Mr. Thackeray. Crossed out in red."

"Red ink," Tamsin said. "How administrative of it."

"Stop making jokes."

"I'm trying very hard not to scream, as established."

She faced him then, and what was in her face wasn't fear anymore, or not only fear. It was the particular grim tenderness of someone who has decided you are going to die and has begun mourning you in advance while still hoping to be proved wrong. "Then listen to me properly. Tonight the moon will be more than half full. The house has been patient because it thought it was getting its tithe — Edwin was going to feed it, and patience is cheap when dinner's coming. You've spoiled the meal. And a hungry thing that has waited fifty years—" Her voice caught. "My brother Tom is dying of it right now, do you understand? It's taking him back piece by piece. What it gave in 1974, it's reclaiming. And you've just told it that no substitute is coming."

"So it comes for me instead."

"It comes for everything," she said. "But you first. You're the insult. The rest of us are only groceries."

* * *

He should have left that night. He knew it even then; some cold practical surveyor's part of his brain drew up the estimate — fuel in the tank, four hours to anywhere, the house's reach unknown beyond the parish boundary but surely finite, surely a thing like this needed roots — and recommended immediate departure with full supporting documentation.

Instead he sat in Mrs. Aldous's kitchen until nearly midnight, drinking tea he couldn't taste, and when he finally walked the hundred yards to the spare room she'd offered him, he paused at the window and looked across the dark fields at the vicarage.

Every window was lit.

Every window, in a house with no electricity connected since 1998, according to his own survey notes, which he now regarded as the funniest document ever filed.

And in the upstairs window — Coral's window, the preserved room, the mid-1974 shrine of floral wallpaper and a hairbrush with blonde hairs still in it — a small shape stood watching him. He couldn't see features at this distance. He didn't need to. He felt the watching the way you feel sun on your skin.

He raised one hand, awkwardly, the way you wave at a child on a train platform.

The small shape raised its hand too.

Then every light in the vicarage went out at once, and the temperature of the night dropped so sharply that his breath came out smoking, and somewhere beneath the ground, beneath the foundation stone, beneath the melted bell that had been ringing in no-place for two weeks, something began — softly, patiently, with the air of a thing settling in for a long-awaited meal — to hum.

Coral's hymn.

Except it wasn't Coral humming it anymore. He knew her voice now; he'd spent nights listening to it through walls, thin and sweet and pleading. This was the same tune dragged down an octave and slowed, sung by something vast that had learned the melody from her and was using it the way a thief uses a stolen coat.

*Bring me home,* the true version went. *I want to go home.*

This version had different words. He couldn't make them out yet. He suspected he would, before dawn.

Tamsin closed the curtains, wedged a chair under the doorknob, and lay down on top of the bed fully clothed with his shoes on, which was the single most sensible thing anyone in Marsh Hollow had done in fifty years.

* * *

He woke at three in the morning because the walls were breathing.

There was no other word for it. The plaster of Mrs. Aldous's guest room rose and fell in a slow rhythm, a centimetre out, a centimetre in, and with each expansion came a smell — wet clay, river silt, the green rot-smell of the marsh at low tide — and with each contraction a faint creaking, like floorboards under a heavy tread, except the floorboards were the walls themselves.

"Mrs. Aldous!" His voice came out cracked. He tried again. "MRS. ALDOUS!"

Nothing. Worse than nothing — he realized the sound wasn't leaving the room. It hit the breathing walls and came back to him folded, diminished, a shout returned as a whisper. The house — and it was the house, it had to be, the vicarage was a mile away and it didn't matter, distance was a courtesy the thing had extended and had now withdrawn — the house had reached down the lane and taken Mrs. Aldous's spare room into itself the way you'd draw a thread through a needle.

The lamp wouldn't light. His phone showed a full battery and a black screen. The window, when he wrenched at it, had fused into the wall, the frame grown over with plaster smooth as skin.

And the humming had words now.

*STAY,* it sang, in a voice like a bell being dragged through mud. *STAY AND BE CATALOGUED. EVERY HOUSE KEEPS WHAT COMES INTO IT. YOU CAME IN. YOU ARE CONTENTS.*

"I am not contents," Tamsin said, with the desperate dignity of a man arguing with a building. "I am a chartered surveyor and I am leaving a one-star review."

The walls laughed. He felt it rather than heard it, a shudder running through the plaster, and the breathing quickened, excited now, and the room began to change around him the way rooms had changed that first day — except then it had been rearranging furniture, showing off, courting him. Now it was *building*. The wardrobe stretched upward into a shape like a doorframe. The bed sank into the floor until he stood ankle-deep in mattress springs that curled around his shoes like grasping fingers. And along the walls, rising out of the plaster like fossils surfacing in eroding cliff-face, came names.

Dozens of names. Hundreds. Occupants back to 1830, the ledger's whole history rendered in relief, and among them, fresh, wet, glistening as though newly carved: **THACKERAY, E.** — and beside it, where the ledger had always left a blank column for dates, a date.

Tonight's date.

And after the date, in smaller letters, almost apologetic: *deceased.*

"Oh," Tamsin said quietly. "Right. So we're doing this."

Panic arrived, fully formed, and he let it — he ran at the breathing wall with his shoulder down and hit it and bounced off and hit it again, shouting, and the wall absorbed each blow with a soft fleshy thud that was worse than any resistance, and somewhere in the middle of the third attempt he understood that the room was not trying to kill him quickly. It was *measuring* him. Fitting him. The way you measure a coffin. The way you measure anything you intend to keep.

Through the walls — through the thing's own body — he heard the village. Doors opening. Screams starting and stopping. A baby crying somewhere with a sound that cut off mid-wail, and the silence afterward was so complete and so satisfied that Tamsin's blood went cold in a way he would remember for the rest of his life, however long the ledger intended that to be.

And underneath it all, threading through the hunger, another voice. Small. Thin. Furious.

*Let him GO.*

The breathing stuttered.

*He's MINE,* the small voice screamed, and it was Coral, it had to be Coral, her sweetness burnt away to pure fury, *you can't have him, you had ME, you had me for FIFTY YEARS—*

The room convulsed. Plaster cracked. For one instant the window reappeared, glass starred with fractures, and Tamsin threw himself at it without a single rational thought, elbows first, and went through in an explosion of glass and cold air and landed in Mrs. Aldous's flowerbed wearing most of the curtain.

The night outside was ordinary. Mercifully, obscenely ordinary. Stars. Frost forming on the hedge. The church tower a black silhouette against a moon two-thirds risen and climbing.

Behind him, the cottage windows glowed with a light that had no source, and from inside came a sound like a great indrawn breath, gathering itself for the next attempt.

Tamsin got up, bleeding, shaking, and ran for the church.

* * *

Dawn found him sitting on the altar steps with his back against the pulpit, watching the light come up grey through the east window, and here is what he had learned in the worst night of his life:

The house could reach anywhere in the parish. The house could rewrite rooms, steal voices, unmake a baby's cry. The house had written his death date in its walls and it did not write fiction.

But it had hesitated. When Coral screamed at it, it had *hesitated* — a full second, maybe two, its grip loosening enough for a man to break glass. Fifty years it had kept her, and in all that time it had never considered that she might have opinions about the arrangement.

Two days to the harvest moon. One stone to lift. One child to carry home.

Tamsin pressed his bleeding forearm with a handkerchief and looked up at the tower, where the empty frame waited where a bell should be, and spoke aloud to the cold morning air, because he was past caring who heard.

"Hold on, Coral. Two more days. Then nobody keeps anybody."

From the direction of the vicarage, faint across the fields, came a single peal of the melted bell — and for the first time, it did not sound hungry.

It sounded angry.

And beneath it, so faint he might have imagined it, a child's voice was singing — not the plea anymore, not *bring me home*, but something new, steady as a work song, a rhythm to dig by.

He got up, and went to find a shovel.
