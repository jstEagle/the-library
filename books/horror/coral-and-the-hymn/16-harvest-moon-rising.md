## 16. Harvest Moon Rising

They came for us at half past four in the morning, which told me everything about how frightened they were. Honest men knock at a reasonable hour.

I heard them first as a change in the house itself — the walls, which had spent two nights breathing around me in slow, hungry rhythms, went suddenly still, the way a rabbit goes still when the shadow of the hawk crosses the grass. Then boots on gravel. Many boots. Torches blooming orange through the lace curtains of what had once been the vicar's study.

Mrs. Aldous was beside me in the dark, her hand cold on my wrist. We'd been sitting on the floor of the nursery — Coral's room — because it was the only place in the vicarage where I could think straight, the hymn there being less a haunting than a kind of company. She'd brought tea at midnight in a thermos that must have been older than both of us, and we'd taken turns reading aloud from Tom's ledger page, saying the names into the room like planting seeds.

"They're here," she said, unnecessarily. You could have heard the mob from the next parish. There were perhaps forty of them, which in Marsh Hollow constituted nearly everyone not bedridden or under ten.

"Are you going to open the door?" she asked.

"I was rather hoping they'd knock."

They did not knock. The front door of the vicarage, which had opened for me once with terrible courtesy, now held fast against forty fists, and I confess I felt a flicker of something almost like gratitude toward the old monster before I remembered whose side it was on, and whose it wasn't.

* * *

Coral showed me the crowd through the nursery window, or perhaps through her memory of windows — the image arrived slightly wrong, colors too saturated, faces lit from beneath as if by candlelight even though they carried electric torches. A child's way of seeing a mob: enormous, bright, more frightening than it needed to be and exactly as frightening as it was.

Edwin Vane stood at their head. He'd put on a coat for the occasion, tweed, buttoned to the throat, as though this were a hunt breakfast and not the sealing of a fifty-year lie. His voice carried across the yard with the ease of a man who had never once doubted his right to be obeyed.

"Mr. Thackeray. Mrs. Aldous." He said our names the way you'd read out charges. "The council has met. The churchyard is closed. By order of the parish, effective immediately, no ground will be broken, no stone lifted, no grave disturbed. The harvest comes tomorrow night, and Marsh Hollow will meet it as it has always met it. Whole."

"Whole," repeated someone behind him, and then others took it up, and it became one of those terrible small chants that villages do, the word passed mouth to mouth until it stopped meaning anything and became only a sound, a wall made of voices.

Mrs. Aldous leaned toward the glass. "You buried a child under a bell," she called out, and her voice cracked across the yard like a whip, "and you call yourselves whole."

The chanting faltered. For one moment I saw doubt move through the crowd like wind through wheat — faces turning, a woman pulling her boy closer, an old man removing his cap for no reason he could name. Edwin saw it too. I watched him decide, in real time, that doubt was more dangerous than cruelty.

"Seal it," he said.

And they did. It was almost gentle, which was the worst part. No stones thrown, no windows broken. Men simply began to work — planks over the churchyard lychgate, chain and padlock, wire strung along the wall, a tractor dragged sideways across the gate of the old consecrated ground until the whole place wore a carapace of ordinary rural hardware. They sealed Coral's grave the way you'd seal a well suspected of drowning children: thoroughly, without discussion, without looking up at the house.

Then they turned to me.

"Mr. Thackeray," Edwin said, and now his voice had softened into something worse than threat — reasonableness. "Nobody wants trouble. Nobody wants your family getting a phone call. Go inside. Stay inside. Two nights, and then you can drive away and file whatever report you like. We'll even pay your invoice." A ripple of laughter, thin and dutiful. "The house asked for a tithe. It seems fitting it should get a surveyor."

"You're locking me in with it," I said.

"I'm locking it in with you," Edwin said. "There's a difference. Ask it which of us is the prisoner."

* * *

They nailed the door shut behind me. I heard each nail go in — six of them, unhurried, the hammer strokes spaced like a man thinking between thoughts — and then the boots receding, and then the extraordinary silence of a village holding its breath.

Mrs. Aldous had refused to leave. They'd let her stay, which surprised me until I understood: to them she was no longer a person but a symptom, a grief they'd agreed decades ago not to look at, and you don't evict a symptom, you just stop visiting. She sat down in the vicar's study among the dust sheets and poured tea from the thermos with hands that shook only slightly, and said, "Well. We wanted daylight and we've got two nights of it coming," meaning the moon, and I knew what she meant, and we drank our tea.

It was somewhere past dawn — though dawn came grey and grudging through the boards over the windows — that the house began its real work.

It started with my reflection. I caught myself in a hall mirror that had survived fifty years of dereliction suspiciously well, and the man looking back at me was smiling when I wasn't. Not a sinister smile. A hopeful one. The smile of a man who has just been shown something wonderful and is deciding whether to believe it.

"Careful," Mrs. Aldous said, without looking up from the ledger. "It starts polite."

Then it showed me my mother's kitchen. Not a vision, nothing so theatrical — just a smell, bread baking, and the sound of a radio playing the shipping forecast, and for three full seconds I was nine years old and everything was fine and nobody had ever died. I gripped the doorframe until the wood bit my palm and the kitchen dissolved like sugar in rain.

"It knows what you love," Mrs. Aldous said quietly. "That's how it gets the ones who take the money. It doesn't threaten. It reminds."

But here is the thing I want to set down honestly, because it matters: the house could not touch the nursery.

Every other room shifted and breathed and offered me my own history like a street vendor offering watches. But the door to Coral's room stayed exactly where it was, and the room inside stayed exactly 1974, and when I sat on the small bed with its crocheted blanket the humming was steady and clear and — I keep reaching for the word and the word is *warm*. The hymn ran through the walls like heat through pipes. And wherever it reached, the house flinched back.

"She claims things," Mrs. Aldous said. She'd followed me in, and stood in the doorway with her arms folded, watching the wallpaper settle like a bird settling. "My brother said it, near the end. Said the house takes and takes but it never touched her room, never moved so much as a hair ribbon. He thought it was guilt." She paused. "I think it's jurisdiction. You can't steal from a thief. She was given to the house, so the house owns her — and what the house owns, the house protects. Even from itself."

"Then I'm safest exactly where I am," I said.

"Yes," she said. "Isn't that a horrible thing? The only safe place in Marsh Hollow is inside the thing that wants to eat you, sitting on a dead girl's bed, because her ghost has decided you're hers." She looked at me then, and for the first time since I'd known her, Mrs. Aldous smiled — a dry, crooked, entirely human smile. "She's got taste, I'll say that."

* * *

Coral's memory, that second night, was different from any she'd shown me before. Until then her fragments had come like weather — sudden, fragmentary, gone before I could hold them. But sitting in her room, with the harvest moon rising fat and orange beyond the boarded window, she gave me something whole.

I was in the churchyard. Not the sealed one — the old one, before. Grass to my knees, daisies, the smell of cut hay off the marsh. A woman's hands braiding my hair — not my mother's hands, gentler, slower, a grandmother's hands. A hymnbook open on my lap, the pages soft as cloth from handling, and my own voice, small and clear, singing the words I'd been humming through these walls for fifty years without knowing they were words:

*Bring me home, bring me home,*
*over the water and under the stone,*
*the light is low and the lane is long,*
*O Lord, who waits, O Lord, who waits —*

and the last line breaking off, because that was as far as she'd ever learned it. The hymn ended mid-phrase in 1974 and had been ending mid-phrase ever since, fifty years of a sentence never finished, and I understood suddenly why the house's hunger sounded so much like hunger: it was listening for an ending too.

*I'm not scared,* Coral's voice said, and it was a child's voice, unbroken by anything, which after everything seemed impossible and true. *I was scared for a long time. Under the stone. But you knocked, and somebody answered, and that's the first time anybody answered.*

"I'm going to finish it," I said aloud, to the room, to her. "Tomorrow night. The stone comes up. We carry you home."

The warmth in the walls deepened. Somewhere below us, in the foundations, the house groaned — long and tectonic, the sound of a thing realizing its meal intends to walk out of the pantry. The floorboards bucked once, gently, like a dog shaking off water. On the little desk, the school exercise book that had lain untouched for five decades slid two inches toward me, as if pushed by a small determined hand.

*He'll try to stop you,* she said, and I didn't need to ask who. *He's had fifty years to practice being right.*

"Everyone does," I said. "It's the village sport."

And — I swear this — I felt her laugh. Not heard. Felt. A flutter in the warm air like a bird taking off, brief and bright and gone, and Mrs. Aldous looked up sharply from the ledger and said, "What? What was that?" and I said, "Nothing," and then, because lying to her seemed suddenly obscene, "She laughed at my joke."

Mrs. Aldous set down the ledger. She pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes. "Fifty years," she said, "and that's the first joke this house has laughed at. God help us if it develops a sense of humor before tomorrow."

* * *

Near midnight, the house made its last offer, and it was the cruelest yet.

It didn't show me my losses. It showed me my gains. A life in which I'd never driven down that marsh road — the appraisal done, the commission banked, a promotion, a flat with good light, a woman whose name I almost recognized, Sunday papers, a dog. Ten years of ordinary happiness rendered in perfect detail, waiting for me like a train at a platform, and all I had to do was stand up from this child's bed and walk downstairs and let the nails hold and the moon pass over and the covenant renew itself on some other family's child, some traveler's kid no one would miss, and forget.

I won't pretend I didn't feel the pull. That's the honest horror of it: it fit. Every piece of that other life slotted into the hollow places in mine with a click I could feel in my teeth.

What broke it was small. From the walls, faintly, patiently, the hymn began again — and stopped, as it always stopped, at *O Lord, who waits.* Mid-sentence. Fifty years unfinished.

"No," I said, to the house, to the vision, to myself. "No. I know how your stories end. Everyone in your ledger ends crossed out."

The kitchen-smell faded. The flat with the good light folded up like paper. And in the sudden dark of the real room, Coral's warmth gathered close around me like a blanket drawn up by someone who loved me, and I understood that the house had made a catastrophic error of bookkeeping.

It had tried to buy the one thing in Marsh Hollow that wasn't for sale — and it had done it in front of a witness.

Beyond the boards, the harvest moon cleared the marsh and laid a white road across the flooded fields, straight as a laid table. One night left. Somewhere out there, under chain and plank and padlock, a small coffin waited in the dark, and forty villagers kept watch over it, guarding the thing they most feared, because the alternative was grieving it.

Mrs. Aldous blew out the candle. In the dark she said, "The boy's coming at first light. The Hollis lad. He's got his father's spade and his grandmother's conscience."

"And the rest of them?"

She was quiet a moment. "Half of them are ashamed," she said at last. "Ashamed people are dangerous, Mr. Thackeray. They'll do anything to keep from feeling it. Including nothing." She pulled the blanket — Coral's blanket — up over us both, absurdly, tenderly. "Sleep if you can. Tomorrow we dig."

In the walls, soft as breath, the hymn hummed on — unfinished, unfinishable, waiting.

*O Lord, who waits,* it sang, and stopped.

"Not you," I whispered into the dark. "Not anymore."

The house shuddered once, all the way down to its foundations, like something turning over in its sleep — or waking up.
