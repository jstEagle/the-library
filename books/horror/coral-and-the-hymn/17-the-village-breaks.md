## 17. The Village Breaks

Margaret Aldous had spent forty-one years keeping the parish records of Marsh Hollow, and in all that time she had learned one iron law of village life: the truth could survive anything except being read aloud.

She stood in the vestry of St. Brannoc's-in-the-Marsh at half past six in the morning, holding her brother's ledger page in both hands the way you hold a bird with a broken wing — carefully, uselessly, aware that it was going to die in your grip no matter what you did.

The paper was soft as cloth from fifty years of being hidden. Tom had kept it inside the lining of his good coat, then inside a biscuit tin, then — after the drinking got bad — inside the waistband of his trousers, which Margaret had always chosen not to think about too closely. On it, in Tom's careful clerk's hand, were eleven names. Eleven men who had gone out on the night of the harvest moon in 1974 and done what needed doing.

Her brother's name was third from the top. Edwin Vane's was last, because Edwin had insisted on signing even though he'd been nineteen and had mostly held the lantern and been sick behind the wall afterward. That detail wasn't written down. Margaret simply knew it, the way you know things about people you've shared a kitchen with your whole life.

"You don't have to do this," Tamsin Halloran said from the doorway. He looked like a man who had slept badly in a house that didn't want him to sleep at all. There was a bruise along his jaw she didn't ask about. "There are other ways."

"There aren't," she said. "That's rather the point of us, isn't it? Fifty years of other ways, and every one of them was to keep quiet." She smoothed the page against the vestry table. "Tom asked me to remember him honestly. I intend to be thorough about it. He'd be mortified. It's what he would have wanted."

Tamsin almost smiled. Almost. "You're sure they'll come? To hear it?"

"They'll come," Margaret said. "I put it in the parish notices. *A reading concerning the tithe, all households required.*" She paused. "And I made the tea announcement. Two hundred cups. Mrs. Penhale is bringing her saffron buns."

"That's — I'm sorry, is that manipulation?"

"It's Marsh Hollow," said Margaret. "You could announce the Second Coming in this village and half of them would only attend if there were refreshments and the other half would only stay away if the same were true. We are a people governed entirely by baked goods and spite. It has kept us alive for centuries."

*It has kept us alive*, she thought, and the words turned over like a stone showing its wet underside. *Kept us alive by feeding it.*

* * *

By nine o'clock the church was fuller than it had been for any service since the Queen's Jubilee, and considerably quieter.

They came in their best coats because Margaret had said *required*, and in Marsh Hollow a requirement from the parish office carried the weight of law, or at least the weight of Margaret Aldous's disapproving face, which was heavier. They sat in their usual pews — the Vane pew at the front, three generations of posture and entitlement; the Penhales behind them; the Cranes; the old Dymond brothers, who sat together the way they did everything, including breathing. The boy — Josiah Crane's lad, the one who had helped dig without a word — sat near the back with his grandmother's hand clamped on his shoulder like a mooring line.

Edwin Vane sat at the front with his hands folded, wearing the expression of a man attending the funeral of someone he had personally arranged. He had tried, of course. He had come to her door the night before with brandy and reasonableness, and had left with neither.

"He'll stop you," Tamsin murmured, beside her at the lectern. "Somehow. He always has."

"Yes," Margaret agreed. "But he's never had to stop me in front of two hundred people and Mrs. Penhale's saffron buns. Even tyrants have manners about crumbs."

She opened the ledger page. Her hands, she noted with distant irritation, were shaking. She pressed them flat.

"My name," she began, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved, "is Margaret Aldous. My brother Thomas was one of eleven men who, on the fourteenth of October, 1974, took a child's body from an unmarked grave in this churchyard and sealed it beneath the foundation stone of our bell tower. I am going to read you the names. Not because I hate any of you. Because we have spent fifty years not saying them, and look what that has bought us."

Silence. The particular silence of a village holding its breath — which, she thought, was exactly how it had all started.

"Thomas Aldous," she read. "Gerald Crane. William Penhale. Robert Dymond. Edward Dymond—"

The old Dymond brothers did not flinch. They had been waiting for this, she realized, for decades — two old men sitting side by side in a cold church, waiting for their names to be said out loud so they could finally stop carrying them alone. Edward closed his eyes. Robert reached over and gripped his brother's wrist, and neither of them let go.

"—Samuel Hoskins. Peter Vane. Arthur Tregose. John Metherell. Francis Kellow. And Edwin Charles Vane."

Every head in the church turned toward the front pew.

Edwin did not turn red or rise in outrage, which Margaret had prepared for. Instead he did something worse. He nodded slowly, gravely, like a magistrate acknowledging evidence, and stood.

"Thank you, Margaret," he said, in his parish-council voice, the one that had chaired fifty years of meetings about drainage ditches and fete schedules while children's names went into walls. "Thank you for your courage. Now sit down, and let me explain to these good people why everything you've just heard was necessary."

And here was the thing — the thing Margaret would think about for the rest of her life — heads actually began to turn back toward him. Old habit. Fifty years of letting Edwin do the explaining. A few faces wore relief already, the blessed relief of being told what to think by someone whose voice had always sounded like sense.

"No," said Margaret.

It came out louder than she intended, and it cracked on the second word, and it was, she later admitted, the most effective thing she said all morning, because Edwin Vane had never once in his life been told *no* in this church.

"The covenant saved us," Edwin said, spreading his hands, reasonable as a weather report. "In 'seventy-four we were dying. Blight, fever, the marsh taking a farm every winter. You all remember. Your parents remembered. One child — one child who was already dead, already given to God — and the blight stopped. The fevers stopped. Fifty years, and not one soul lost to the hollow. Ask yourselves what your grandmothers would have paid for that bargain. Ask yourselves what you'd pay now."

"Ask Coral Vane," said a voice from the back.

It was young Josiah Crane. Forty years old, stooped from the boat work, a man who had never in living memory spoken in church except to say the responses. The whole congregation swiveled toward him and he went scarlet but didn't sit down.

"She was somebody's daughter," he said. "She was in the ground where her mother put her. And we dug her up. We dug her up and gave her to a *house*, and then we sang hymns every Sunday ten feet above where—" His voice failed. His grandmother's hand found his arm. "My dad signed that ledger. He drank himself into the marsh by 1980 and I always thought it was just the drink. It wasn't, was it. It was this."

"It was necessary," said Edwin.

"It was *convenient*," said Margaret, and something in the church shifted — a sound like a long-held note finally resolving, or perhaps like masonry settling, though St. Brannoc's had not settled in three hundred years.

Because now the others came. Not all. Never all. But Mrs. Penhale rose from her pew and said, in her small fierce voice, that her husband William had cried in his sleep every October until he died, and she had never known why, and she would like Edwin Vane to explain that to her. And old Robert Dymond stood up, still holding his brother's wrist, and said, "Necessary," in the tone of a man tasting a word and spitting it out, and said, "We held the lantern, Ed. We held the lantern while you measured the hole. Necessary," and sat down again as though the effort had cost him a year.

Half the room, Margaret thought, watching the pews tilt and divide like a tide coming in. Half the town turning on him, and the other half looking at their shoes, and both halves weeping in their own way, because that was the thing nobody warned you about the truth: it doesn't set anyone free. It just makes everyone equally wounded, and for the first time in fifty years, equally *together* in it.

Edwin's composure cracked at last — a fine hairline fracture, visible only to those who'd known him since he was a boy throwing stones at the vicarage windows. "If we break the covenant," he said quietly, "the house takes. You've seen what it takes when it's *fed*. What do you imagine it does hungry?"

"We're about to find out," said Tamsin Halloran, from the lectern, "because it's already starving. Look around you. Look at what it's taken this month. Your reflections. The baby's cry. Tom Aldous's life. It isn't protecting you anymore, Mr. Vane. It's eating you slowly enough that you call it protection."

And that was when the seal broke.

* * *

Margaret felt it before she heard it — a shudder through the floor, through the soles of her sensible shoes, up through her knees, a great indrawn breath beneath the whole churchyard, as if the ground itself had been holding its position for fifty years on instructions and had just heard, at last, permission to move.

Then the sound: a groan of old iron and older mortar from the direction of the lychgate, and through the south windows came a light that was wrong for nine in the morning — a low amber light, harvest-colored, though the sky outside stayed grey.

"The churchyard wall," someone shouted from the porch. "The wall at the far corner — it's come down!"

Not fallen. Come down — laid open, stones rolled neatly aside like a bed being turned down, opening the consecrated ground to the air for the first time since 1974. Margaret understood it in her bones before her head caught up: the seal hadn't been the wall. The seal had been the *silence*. The agreement, the not-saying, the fifty years of names unsaid — that had been the lock, and she had just smashed it with two hundred witnesses and a piece of biscuit-tin paper.

The congregation poured out into the churchyard, and Margaret followed, clutching the ledger, and saw them gather at the broken corner of the wall — the Penhales and the Cranes and the weeping Dymond brothers and the boy with his grandmother — staring down at the opened earth as though expecting it to speak.

Behind them all, apart, stood Edwin Vane, quite alone now in the middle of his own congregation, and Margaret watched him understand, with terrible slowness, that he was no longer a squire. He was just a man standing in a churchyard with everyone's eyes on him and nothing left to explain.

From the direction of the vicarage, faint and clear across the marsh, came the sound of a melted bell ringing — one peal, then another, closer than it had ever sounded before.

"It knows," whispered Mrs. Penhale. "Oh God, Margaret, it knows we're coming."

"Yes," said Margaret Aldous. She tucked the ledger page into her coat, next to her heart, where Tom had kept it all those years, and picked up the spade that Tamsin had leaned against the lychgate that morning, hopeful and ridiculous, like a man leaving an umbrella out against a drought.

"Well," she said, to the whole frightened village, in her very best parish-office voice, "we'd better get on, hadn't we. Before it finishes ringing."

And she walked toward the bell tower, and — to her astonishment, and then, somehow, inevitably — they followed.
