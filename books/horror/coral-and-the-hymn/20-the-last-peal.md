## 20. The Last Peal

I had forgotten the weight of a name.

Not my name — I had carried that like a stone in my pocket all these years, turning it over in the dark, *Coral, Coral, Coral* — but the weight of it in someone else's mouth. The weight of being called. Of hearing your mother's voice shape the two syllables of you and mean, by them, *come inside, supper's on, mind the puddles.*

The man called Tamsin was carrying me now. He had my bones wrapped in an old altar cloth — Mrs. Aldous's doing; she'd knelt in the grass and folded each small piece as though packing away a christening gown — and he walked with his shoulders hunched against the house's screaming, and every jolt of his stride knocked my ribs together like spoons in a drawer, and I have never in fifty years been so glad of anything.

Behind us the vicarage was coming apart.

I could feel it the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. The house had stopped pretending to be a house. Its walls breathed in long, wet gulps; its windows wept their glass; from its open door poured everything it had ever swallowed — voices, mostly, and the shapes of voices, and one particular hymn sung by one particular girl in one particular bedroom in the summer of 1974, which was me, which was mine, which it had kept like a pressed flower between the pages of its ledger.

It offered him things as he walked. I felt the offers go out like heat from an oven.

It showed him his father's kitchen, steam on the window, radio playing. It showed him a woman whose name I didn't know but whose absence I could taste — salt and rain and a train platform. It showed him ten years of empty rooms made full again, every appraisal a homecoming, every locked door opening onto light. *Stop walking,* said the house, in a voice like honey poured over broken crockery. *Stay. Be warm. Be remembered. You need never be nobody's guest again.*

He walked through it. All of it. His jaw was set so hard I could hear his teeth complain, and I thought — and forgive me, because I was eleven when they took me and eleven-year-olds find the strangest things funny — I thought: *he walks like Mrs. Pettifer going to collect her eggs from a fox.*

Onward, Mr. Surveyor. Onward through the offered kitchens.

* * *

The old churchyard sat at the top of the marsh path where the ground remembered how to be solid. My grave was there. Had always been there. A small plot under the yew, beside my grandmother, marked once with a wooden cross that Tom Aldous had carved badly and then, after they took me from it, replaced with nothing at all — because a grave with no body is just a hole with ambitions, and holes get filled in, and forgetting gets planted over the top like grass seed.

But the ground knew. Ground always knows. For fifty years that patch of earth had been the only place in Marsh Hollow where I could hear myself think.

They laid me down at the edge of it while the moon climbed. Tamsin's hands were shaking. The boy who had helped dig — I never learned his name that night, though I know it now, and I keep it safe — held the lantern steady, and Mrs. Aldous knelt in the wet grass with her coat off and spread it over the plot like she was tucking me in, and Edwin Vane stood apart from all of them at the churchyard gate, and did not come in.

I want to be fair to Edwin. It costs me something to be fair to Edwin, the way it costs a bone something to knit. But I was his daughter once, before I was the house's tithe, and even corrupted love leaves fingerprints, and his were all over the shape of what he'd become.

He had spent fifty years telling himself a story. I heard it through the walls often enough, in the years when the house let me listen out of spite: *the covenant saved them. Every harvest, the village lived. One child against four hundred souls — any father would do the arithmetic.* He had done the arithmetic so many times he'd worn the numbers smooth, and now they slipped through his hands like river stones, and he could not hold a single one of them still long enough to see that none of them was me.

The house had abandoned him first, you understand. That was its last cruelty and its truest confession. When Tamsin lifted me from the foundation stone's dark pocket, the house had reached for Edwin — *my faithful one, my tithe-taker, my keeper of the arithmetic* — and found nothing there worth keeping. A covenant needs two parties. The house had gotten what it wanted, or failed to, and Edwin Vane was suddenly only an old man standing at a gate in the cold, holding fifty years of receipts for goods no longer stocked.

"Come away from there," Mrs. Aldous said to him, not unkindly. She had buried her brother Tom three days past, in the new churchyard, with the whole village watching and half of them weeping honestly for the first time since 1974. Grief had cracked her voice open, and what came out of the crack was not bitterness. It was tiredness. "Edwin. Come and help fill it in. She's your girl."

And here is the thing I will tell you about my father, the thing I saw from wherever I was, the thing I want set down plainly because otherwise the story will make him only a monster and monsters are too easy:

He tried.

He put one foot inside the gate. I felt it — the ground felt it, a small tremor like a bird landing. Fifty years of arithmetic, and underneath it all, one stubborn remainder that had never divided evenly: *she was nine pounds two ounces at birth, she had a cowlick like her mother's, she sang flat on the high notes and I never told her because she was happy.*

One step. Two.

Then the marsh spoke.

Not the house — the marsh. They are different things, though the village had spent half a century confusing them. The house was the hunger. The marsh was older and colder and had no use for covenants or tithes or ledgers; it simply took what wandered onto it, the way water takes. And it said — not in words, but in that pull you feel near deep water, that tilt behind the eyes — it said to Edwin Vane: *you know the way down here. You've known it all along. We saved you a place.*

He looked at me — at the small bundle of altar cloth, at the plot under the yew — and I felt him look, the way you feel sunlight through a window. And whatever passed between us in that moment, I have decided to keep private. Some accounts are settled between fathers and daughters alone, and the moon was kind enough to look elsewhere.

He turned from the gate. He walked out along the marsh path with his coat buttoned wrong and his head up, like a man going to meet someone, and the fog came down the hill to meet him halfway, and that is the last anyone saw of Squire Edwin Vane, and I will say only this: the marsh keeps what it takes, but it does not catalogue. There are no ledgers down there. Whatever he became, he became it privately, and perhaps that was the only mercy left in Marsh Hollow that night.

Mrs. Aldous watched the fog close behind him for a long moment. Then she picked up the spade.

"Right," she said, in the voice of a woman who has read fifty years of names aloud in a cold church and intends to spend the rest of her life saying ordinary things in warm ones. "Let's get you home, Coral Vane."

* * *

Digging a grave is honest work. I recommend it to anyone who has spent time around dishonest houses.

Tamsin dug. The boy dug. By the end, half the village had come up the path with lanterns — not the mob from two nights past, with their seals and their padlocks and their fear dressed up as law, but the other half, the ones Mrs. Aldous's reading had cracked open, the ones who had spent the days since wandering their own homes like strangers, touching doorframes and asking themselves *what did we trade, exactly, and what did we buy?*

They did not speak much. They formed a line and passed the earth back bucket by bucket, and old Mr. Crake, who had been fourteen in 1974 and had held the lantern while the others worked, took the lantern again now, fifty years later, and held it the same way, and wept the whole time without making a sound, which is the hardest way to weep and the most useful.

I lay in the altar cloth at the graveside and listened to them work, and I will confess something strange: I was nervous. Fifty years of being a haunting, and here I was about to be a burial, and I found myself hoping they'd think well of me. Hoping the plot was deep enough. Hoping nobody would notice I'd been a thin child and say so. Death, it turns out, does not cure you of wanting to make a good impression. It only gives you more time to rehearse.

The moon crested the yew tree just as Tamsin set the cloth down in the earth.

I should describe what happened next properly, because everyone who tells this story afterward gets it wrong. They say the bell rang. They say it rang loud, rang furious, rang like the world ending. It did not. The melted bell — the one poured down in 1911, the one that had rung across the marsh for decades from nowhere and nothing, calling the village to its covenant the way a dinner bell calls pigs — rang once.

Once. Sweetly.

If you have ever heard a small church bell on a still evening, heard that single bronze note go out over fields and come back changed, softer, carrying the smell of the fields inside it — it was like that. It was the note my grandmother's bell had made when I was small and she'd carry me up the tower steps to see the ropes. It was the sound of being called home to supper. It went out over Marsh Hollow, over the ruined vicarage and the sealed wells and the fifty years of quiet doors, and every soul in the village heard their own name inside it — not their given names, but the name they'd had before they learned to be afraid, the name their mothers used — and the note came back down out of the sky like a bird settling, and then there was silence.

Real silence. The kind with nothing underneath it.

For fifty years the silence of Marsh Hollow had been a held breath. This was an exhale.

I felt it happen to the house. I was leaving it, and it was letting me, and as the boundary between us thinned to nothing I felt the great hungry architecture of the place sigh — a long, shuddering, relieved sigh, the sigh of a dog circling three times and finally lying down. All that cataloguing, all those ledgers, all those names entered and crossed out and entered again — it had never been malice. Or not only malice. A house alone with its hunger for fifty years, keeping records because records were the only company it could keep. Now the ledger closed. I heard it close. A sound like a Bible shutting at the end of a service.

And the walls came down — not all at once, not dramatically, but the way ivy lets go of a wall in a hard frost, quietly, piece by piece, until by morning it's only a ruin again. An ordinary ruin. Damp brickwork and rotting beams and a cellar that floods in February. A house that would appraise, if anyone were fool enough to appraise it, as uninhabitable, structurally unsound, of no further interest.

Of no further interest. I have rarely envied anything as much as I envied that house its ordinariness, in that moment, going into the ground.

* * *

They filled the earth in over me by hand.

I want to tell you what it is like, being buried properly, because I suspect very few people get to know, and it is better than you would think. Each spadeful landed soft — Mrs. Aldous made them take off their rings first, all of them, a whole line of villagers solemnly removing wedding bands and signet rings and one enormous turquoise monstrosity belonging to the postmistress, so that nothing hard would touch the cloth. The earth came down warm from the lantern-light and the working. Someone — the boy, I think — had gathered late flowers from the verge, marsh marigolds gone over to seed, and these went on top.

And the consecrated ground — oh, the ground. I had been in the house's ledger for fifty years, an entry, a line item, *one (1) child, received harvest 1974, condition: complete.* The ground did not enter me anywhere. The ground simply opened and had me, the way soil has a seed, without paperwork, without terms, without asking what I was worth. Consecration, it turns out, is not a word written over a place. It is the place itself having decided, long ago, to be kind.

My grandmother was next to me. I felt her the way you feel a hand find yours under a blanket. She did not say anything. Grandmothers who have waited fifty years for a granddaughter to come home do not waste the reunion on talk. But the yew tree leaned a little in a wind that wasn't blowing anywhere else, and I chose to take that as her voice, and I did, and it was.

Tamsin stood at the head of the grave with his hat off. He looked terrible — hollowed, grey, ten years added and ten years lost all at once, a man who had walked through every vision the house owned and refused all of them and paid the fare in full. He looked, in fact, exactly like what he was: the first person in fifty years to come to Marsh Hollow and leave it better instead of bought.

"You sang flat," he said, to the grave, awkwardly, because he was English and did not know what else to do with grief except file it incorrectly. "In the east bedroom. On the high notes. I'm told that runs in families."

I laughed. Whether anyone heard it, I couldn't say. The wind moved the yew again, and Mrs. Aldous smiled at nothing in particular, and I counted that as delivery confirmed.

Then the boy stepped forward and did the thing that undid me entirely, the thing I keep like a sweet in my cheek even now: he set a small wooden cross at the head of the plot. Carved badly. Very badly. Letters uneven, the C backwards.

*Coral Vane. 1963–1974. Sang flat. Was loved.*

Tom Aldous had carved my first cross the same way, badly, in 1974, before they dug me up and carried me to the house. The boy had never seen it. He was not born. And yet there it was, the same wobble in the letters, the same stubborn amateur heart in every stroke — as if bad carving for Coral Vane was simply a craft Marsh Hollow passed down, generation to generation, the way other places pass down bell-ringing or bread.

Mrs. Aldous put her hand on the boy's shoulder. "Your granddad would've made a mess of the spelling too," she said, and the boy grinned, and above us the moon crested fully, round and harvest-gold and done with us at last.

* * *

Afterward they walked down the hill in a loose crowd, lanterns swinging, and the village below them was dark in the ordinary way villages are dark — lamps lit behind curtains, televisions murmuring, a dog complaining about something — and I stayed a moment at the top of the path because I was allowed to, because the ground had me and the night had me and nothing needed me anywhere anymore, which is the definition of peace, I think, though I'd have to check.

From up there I could see the vicarage.

It stood in its field like a tooth after the ache goes out of it. Just a house. Just a big, damp, failing Georgian house with a sagging porch and a cellar that flooded and a draft in the east bedroom that no amount of putty would ever fix. In the morning light it would be almost boring. Surveyors would come, in time, and walk its rooms with their little machines, and the machines would find nothing but damp and subsidence, and the surveyors would feel vaguely sad without knowing why, the way you feel sad in an empty school, and they would write *uninhabitable* in their reports and drive away, and the house would stand there being nobody's business at all.

Except.

I felt it even from the hilltop, faint as a tick in a clock: one small pulse, one page turning somewhere in the ruin. Not hunger. Not anymore. Something smaller and stranger — the way a retired keeper of anything still counts the flock out of habit. The house had spent fifty years taking names into its ledger, and now it had given one back — mine, the last entry, crossed out in a fair hand — and I felt it wondering, in its dim structural way, what a ledger was *for*, if not that.

And I felt it notice the man walking down the hill with his hat in his hands.

Tamsin Halloran. Refuser of offers. Walker-through of visions. The one who had carried me home through the screaming. The house turned its attention on him the way an old dog turns its head toward the one member of the household who ever fed it by hand, and I felt the beginning of a thought form in its rafters — slow, patient, in no hurry at all, houses never are —

But that, I decided, was not my page to read.

I lay back in the good earth, in the consecrated dark, with my grandmother's hand in mine and the yew leaning over me and the marsh marigolds going to seed on top, and far away — so far away it might have been memory, might have been the last vibration of that single sweet note still travelling outward past the edge of the map — I heard the bell one final time.

Not ringing. Remembering ringing. The way a shell remembers the sea.

Then nothing, and the nothing was warm, and I slept.

* * *

They say — the village says, in the way villages say things, sideways, over fences, in the tone reserved for weather and funerals — that the vicarage stands empty now, truly empty, and that children dare each other to touch its gate and come away disappointed. They say Mrs. Aldous has taken to leaving flowers at the old churchyard on Thursdays, and that she talks while she does it, comfortably, at length, as though to someone who answers. They say the east window of the church catches the evening sun in a way it never used to, and that if you stand in the right pew at the right hour, you can almost hear — well. They say.

And they say the surveyor's car was seen leaving the valley a week later, driving slow, and that it stopped a long while at the top of the marsh road before it went on.

What they don't say — what nobody in Marsh Hollow knows yet, though the house does, and the house keeps what it keeps — is what was lying on the passenger seat when the car finally rolled out of sight beyond the hill.

A small book. Bound in faded blue cloth. A child's hymnal, water-stained, fifty years old, with a page turned down at the corner and a name written inside the front cover in pencil, in a careful eleven-year-old hand, beneath the printed words *This Book Belongs To:*

*Tamsin.*

The car drove on toward the horizon. The book rode beside him, patient as masonry.

And in the good earth under the yew, I slept, and did not dream of the house, and did not need to — because the house, diminished, ordinary, alive in the way old buildings are alive, had already dreamed of him enough for both of us.
