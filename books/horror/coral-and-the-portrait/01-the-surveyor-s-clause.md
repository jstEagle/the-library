## 1. The Surveyor's Clause

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Ansel would later consider the first small wrongness, because Tuesdays were the day she checked the mail last, after dark, when the box at the bottom of her building's stairs held nothing but takeout menus and the slow sediment of other people's lives. She had a system. She always had a system. Systems were how a person stayed upright in a world that kept adding doors.

She counted them now without meaning to: the mail slot's brass flap, the stairwell door, the door to 3B with its deadbolt hanging open an inch because Mr. Ferreira still didn't understand locks, the basement door with its chain, the roof door she'd never once opened. Five. It was five yesterday and it would be five tomorrow, and if it was ever six she would know something had happened to the building itself, some soft rearrangement in its bones, and she would move before the building finished deciding what it wanted to be.

Ansel Sandoval, thirty-one years old, professional urban explorer, amateur cartographer of places the city insisted did not exist, stood in the yellow light of the stairwell and read the letter three times.

*Dear Ms. Sandoval,* it began, in the flat serif of a law office that smelled, even through the paper, of carpet and old men. *We represent the estate of Vespera Sandoval Nystrom, deceased as of March 3rd of this year—*

Vespera. She turned the name over like a stone found in a riverbed, smooth and cold and entirely unfamiliar.

Her mother had died when Ansel was nine, taking with her every answer Ansel hadn't yet thought to ask. Her father had lasted another four years before the drink finished what grief started, and after that there had been group homes and a rotating cast of caseworkers whose names she'd deliberately forgotten, and no one, not one person in twenty-two years, had ever mentioned a grandmother. Not a photograph, not a birthday card, not a slip of the tongue. Ansel had built her whole understanding of her family the way she built her tunnel maps: from fragments, from inference, from the assumption that anything unrecorded was probably sealed off for a reason.

*—you have been named sole beneficiary of the property located at 9 Corvid Lane, contingent upon your completion of certain conditions set forth in the will.*

Conditions. Of course there were conditions. Inheritance was never a gift; it was a job posting. She'd seen enough abandoned buildings to know that ownership was just the word people used for whoever got stuck maintaining the rot.

*Specifically: the testator requires that the beneficiary conduct a complete inventory of the residence, cataloging each room individually, with photographic documentation, within ninety days of taking occupancy. Furthermore, the beneficiary shall make no alteration whatsoever to the portrait hanging in the main stairwell. This condition is non-negotiable and material to the bequest.*

Ansel lowered the letter and looked at the five doors of her stairwell until they resolved back into wood and paint and hinges, ordinary, countable, hers to count.

A portrait clause. She'd read enough strange legal language in condemned buildings' paperwork—easements, rights-of-way, deeds with names scratched out and rewritten—to know that a specific prohibition meant someone had anticipated a specific temptation. You didn't write *do not touch the painting* unless you believed, on some level you couldn't admit in front of a lawyer, that someone might want to.

*Please contact our office at your earliest convenience to arrange transfer of keys and relevant documents.*

There was no phone number. There was an address, downtown, and beneath the signature block—a name she didn't bother memorizing—a single line that had not been printed by any machine:

*She said you'd come. —M.*

Handwritten. Blue ink gone slightly brown with age, though the postmark was eleven days old. Whoever M was, they'd written that line long ago and the lawyers had simply included the page, or the note had been waiting inside the envelope like something folded into a coat pocket by a dead woman who planned ahead.

Ansel went upstairs to apartment 4C, locked her door, checked the lock twice, and put the letter on her kitchen table under the desk lamp she used for drafting maps.

Then she did what she always did when the world handed her a document that felt less like paper than like a mouth: she treated it as data.

* * *

Her apartment was small and organized around a single principle: nothing should ever be where she wasn't looking. The walls held her maps—not the official ones, the real ones. Eleven years of crawling through storm drains, service tunnels, steam conduits, and passages that appeared on no municipal record anywhere. She documented everything. She photographed everything. She kept duplicates of every file in two locations, because a person who trusted only one copy of anything was a person asking to be erased.

People assumed she explored for thrills. She'd stopped correcting them years ago, mostly because the truth sounded worse. She explored because unmapped space was unbearable. A corridor that existed but appeared on no plan was a corridor that could change while nobody watched, and Ansel had decided at some point in her teens—she couldn't remember when, which itself bothered her—that the way to keep the world honest was to watch it so closely it forgot how to lie.

She counted doors the way other people checked their phones. She checked light switches in threes: on, off, on, confirming the world responded to her in the expected sequence. When she entered a new room she touched the doorframe with her left hand, a habit so old she'd stopped remembering it was a habit, the way you stop remembering breathing.

Her therapist—two sessions, six years ago, before the money ran out—had called it hypervigilance rooted in abandonment. Ansel had called it being good at her job. Both were true. That was the thing about rituals: they worked whether or not they were symptoms.

Now she sat at her table with the letter flattened under four magnets shaped like fruit, and she made lists, because lists were prayers she actually believed in.

*Unknowns:* Who was Vespera Sandoval Nystrom? Why had no one mentioned her? Why had the house waited—because houses this specific always waited—for Ansel specifically?

*Knowns:* Corvid Lane. She knew Corvid Lane. Everyone in her line of work knew Corvid Lane, or rather knew that it ended in a chain-link fence and a hillside the city claimed was unstable, behind which sat a house that appeared in exactly zero photographs online, zero tax records she could pull, zero memories of anyone she'd ever asked. She'd tried, once, three years ago, standing at that fence with her headlamp and her camera, feeling the particular gravity a structure exerts when it does not want to be found. She'd written it up in her notes as *site 47 — access denied — do not push.* She never wrote "do not push" lightly. The house had made her write it.

And now the house had written to her instead.

She pulled site 47's folder from the map drawer—the third drawer down, the one that stuck—and spread her notes across the table beside the letter. Her own handwriting from three years ago looked younger, more confident, arrows and measurements in the tight notation she'd invented herself: distances in paces converted to meters, compass bearings, little symbols for moisture and airflow and the direction doors opened. She'd surveyed the fence line, the slope, the visible corner of the roofline through the trees. She'd noted, with the dispassion she wore like armor, that the chimney drew heat on thermal imaging despite no registered utilities connecting to the property.

Someone lived there. Someone had been living there all along, keeping the lights on in a house the city pretended not to see, and that someone had been a Sandoval Nystrom, and that someone had died in March, and the lawyers had taken until October to find her.

Or—she let the thought sit there, ugly and patient—until October to decide she was ready.

Ansel made tea she didn't drink. Outside, the city hummed its usual frequency, traffic and sirens and someone's television arguing through the wall, and she sat in the middle of it with a dead woman's handwriting and felt the specific vertigo of a map suddenly showing a room she'd never entered.

*She said you'd come.*

Said. Past tense. Which meant Vespera had spoken about her before dying, which meant Vespera had known about her, which meant somewhere in the architecture of Ansel's orphaned childhood there had been a living woman who knew she existed and chose, year after year, to remain a rumor. Ansel tested the feeling that raised in her chest and found it wasn't anger, exactly. It was closer to what she felt at the mouth of an unmapped tunnel: fury and hunger wearing the same coat.

She wanted to hate the woman. She reached for hatred the way she reached for her left hand against a doorframe, and found it wouldn't close around anything. What she felt instead was the professional assessment, cool and complete: *an unknown variable has entered my survey. Proceed with instrumentation. Do not proceed with trust.*

Ninety days. Room by room. Photograph everything. Touch nothing in the stairwell.

It was, she had to admit, almost flattering. Whoever Vespera had been, she'd understood Ansel perfectly. She'd known that the only inheritance Ansel would accept was an assignment. Not money, not sentiment, not family—*work*. A task with a beginning, a method, and an end. Catalog the house and it becomes yours. Count the rooms and they can't multiply behind your back. Document everything and nothing can pretend it was always otherwise.

The will wasn't a bequest. It was a protocol. And protocols, unlike grandmothers, Ansel knew how to love.

* * *

She called the law office the next morning from the payphone outside the laundromat, because she didn't give her cell number to strangers and because payphones recorded nothing, including her voice. The receptionist transferred her to a paralegal named Dorn, whose voice sounded like a man reading aloud in a room where no one else was present.

"Ms. Sandoval. Yes. We've been expecting your call."

"Since March?"

A pause with texture to it. "Since the will was executed, the timeline is... the estate preferred to wait."

"Estates don't prefer things. People do."

Another pause. "The testator left instructions regarding timing," Dorn said, in the tone of a man who had learned not to ask follow-up questions. "I'll be frank with you, Ms. Sandoval, because frankly the file is strange and you seem like someone who appreciates strangeness handled plainly. Mrs. Nystrom came to us fourteen years ago with the will already drafted. She paid the retainer in cash. She reviewed the document annually—annually, we're required to offer amendments, most clients decline, she never did—and every year she made one change."

"What change?"

"The date." He cleared his throat. "The date of the condition deadline. Ninety days. She moved it forward every year, as though she were counting down to something. And the inventory requirement, the room-by-room catalog—that was in the original draft, fourteen years ago. Before we could have known you existed. I mean—" he caught himself, "before we could verify your current contact information."

Before we knew you existed. Ansel watched a moth batter itself against the laundromat window, drawn by a light that had no warmth behind the glass.

"Was there a reason given? For the inventory?"

"Testators don't owe reasons. But she did leave a personal letter, to be delivered with the keys. I'm authorized to tell you it exists. I'm not authorized to summarize it." His voice dropped half a register, the way voices do when they're admitting fear they've dressed up as procedure. "Ms. Sandoval, may I offer unsolicited advice?"

"No."

"I'll give it anyway. Read her letter before you read the deed. And whatever you find in that house—count it yourself. Don't trust anyone else's numbers."

The line clicked dead before she could ask what he meant, and she stood holding the receiver in the laundromat's fluorescent buzz, listening to the dial tone like it might confess something.

Count it yourself. Don't trust anyone else's numbers.

As if numbers were the kind of thing that could lie. As if a room could be one number to you and another to the house.

Ansel hung up the phone, walked home, checked her five doors—all five, all correct—and began packing. Headlamp, spare batteries, camera, chalk, measuring tape, the good boots. She packed the way she packed for a descent, because some instinct older than her reasoning had already classified the trip: this was not a visit to an inheritance. This was a survey of hostile terrain.

* * *

On her last night in the apartment, she dreamed of a staircase.

She never remembered dreams; she considered them unmapped territory and refused to grant them legitimacy. But this one clung. A staircase going down into a house that was somehow also underground, and on the wall of the stairwell a large framed portrait of a woman whose face she couldn't see because the woman was turned away, counting something out of frame, fingers raised, lips moving. And in the dream Ansel knew—with the dream's terrible fluency—that the woman was counting doors, and that she had been counting for a very long time, and that she would keep counting until someone arrived who could take over the tally.

Ansel woke at 4:17 a.m.—she always woke at odd times and always noted them, timestamps mattered—and lay in the dark reciting her own inventory until her heart slowed. Four walls. One window. One door. One exit route via fire escape. One person, accounted for.

In the morning she took the bus to the end of the line, walked to the chain-link fence at the end of Corvid Lane, and stood before the gap in the hillside trees where the house waited with its windows shuttered and its chimney standing straight as a held breath.

The gate was unlocked. On it, nailed at eye level, hung an envelope with her name on it in blue ink gone brown with age:

*Ansel —*

*Come in. Count everything. Change nothing on the stairs.*

*We'll speak soon. I have waited so patiently to meet you.*

*— Grandmother V.*

Ansel stood in the cold morning air with the letter in her hands and felt the hair rise along her arms, because the ink was decades old and the paper was soft with time, and the handwriting was unmistakably, impossibly fresh in its certainty—as though the woman had written it yesterday, or thirty years ago, or both at once, addressed to a granddaughter who would not exist for years.

*We'll speak soon.*

The dead did not speak. Ansel had built her entire life on that foundation. The dead were data; the dead were silence; the dead were rooms you cataloged and closed.

But the gate was open, and the house was watching, and somewhere inside it a portrait hung on a stairwell wall, forbidden and waiting, and ninety days had already begun.

She touched the gatepost with her left hand—on, off, on, the world responding in sequence—and stepped through.

Behind her, at the end of Corvid Lane, the city went on pretending nothing was there. Ahead, the path climbed through the trees toward thirty-eight doors, or forty-one, or however many the house decided to admit to, and Ansel Sandoval took out her camera, and her chalk, and her measuring tape, and began, professionally, to count.
