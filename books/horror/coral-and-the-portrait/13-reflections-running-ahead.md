## 13. Reflections Running Ahead

Ansel woke at 4:17 a.m. with the certainty that she had already woken up.

Not déjà vu—she knew the texture of déjà vu the way a cartographer knows the texture of bad paper, the way it smears under the pencil. This was different. This was the feeling of having performed an action and then been asked to perform it again, like a take repeated for a director she couldn't see. She lay in the narrow bed in what she had cataloged as Room 12, Guest Bedroom (east-facing, water stain shaped like a hand on the ceiling), and watched her own breath fog the cold air, and waited to see if the fog would come first.

It didn't. Not yet.

She got up because staying in bed felt like agreeing to something. Her ritual was the same as it had been since childhood: touch the doorframe, count the doors between her and the nearest window, check that the flashlight in her left pocket still answered when she thumbed the switch. Three doors. Window intact. Light alive. The house held its breath around her, the way it had been holding its breath for days now, ever since the deed had appeared on the kitchen table with its patient pen and its wet-footed messenger.

In the upstairs bathroom, she turned on the tap out of habit more than need, and the mirror over the sink showed her a woman turning on a tap.

The lag was gone.

For eleven days the mirrors had run one second behind her—a delay she had measured with a stopwatch, a metronome app, a dropped cup filmed in slow motion on her phone. One second, every time, exact as a surveyor's plumb line. She had covered them all with bedsheets and painter's tape and, in one case, a framed map of the storm drains from 1962. She had told herself: *a lag is a debt. A thing that follows you is a thing you can outrun.*

The woman in the mirror turned off the tap before Ansel's hand moved.

Ansel stood very still. Water ran over her fingers. In the glass, the woman's hands were dry.

"Okay," Ansel said aloud, because saying things aloud made them data instead of hauntings. "Okay. Test protocol."

* * *

She spent the morning testing, because ritual was armor and measurement was prayer, and if she could put a number on the wrongness then the wrongness belonged to her and not the other way around.

The hallway mirror—the tall cheval glass in Room 3, her grandmother's bedroom, its silvering foxed at the corners like old ice—showed her raising her right hand a full second before she raised it. She lowered it; the reflection had already lowered hers. She spun in place and the reflection spun with her, but always ahead, always finished first, a dancer who had heard the music early.

She filmed it. Watched the footage back frame by frame. There it was: Ansel Sandoval, thirty-four years old, lifting her arm into empty air while the woman in the glass lifted hers into a gesture that hadn't happened yet—and then Ansel's body, obedient, catching up, performing the motion it had been assigned.

"That's not how mirrors work," she said to the empty room, and the reflection said it too—or would say it, or had said it—one second before her mouth opened.

She thought about the physics of it the way she thought about everything, in terms of flow and direction. For years the tunnels had taught her one law: water goes downhill, air moves toward the breach, everything runs from cause toward effect. You could map a city by its flows. But a mirror running ahead meant the flow had reversed somewhere. It meant that somewhere downstream, the effect had already happened, and the image was traveling backward up the pipe to meet her.

Somewhere downstream, she had already done all of this.

Her hands were steady. She noted that with professional interest, the way she'd once noted that her hands stayed steady while a tunnel collapsed behind her, which either meant courage or something broken in the fear machinery. She taped a bedsheet over the cheval glass. Then she went downstairs to look at the deed.

* * *

The deed sat on the kitchen table where it had sat for six nights, squared to the table's edge with a precision no draft would explain. The pen beside it was her grandmother's—an old fountain pen, black lacquer worn to bare brass at the grip, the kind of object that gets held by four generations of the same hand until the wear becomes a signature of its own.

Ansel had not touched it. That was the whole war, really. Everything else—the mirrors, the rooms that existed only in photographs, the lullaby missing its ending—was maneuver. The deed was the objective. Something in this house needed her name applied to that paper by her own voluntary hand, and it had been patient about it, polite about it, asking nightly in a voice like dry leaves imitating her grandmother's timbre: *Sign, dear. It's only paperwork. Everyone signs eventually.*

This morning, the deed was not blank.

The signature line at the bottom bore her name. *Ansel Miriam Sandoval*, written in ink that glistened wet, in handwriting that was unmistakably hers—the cramped ascenders, the way she crossed her t's low and fast—but signed with a flourish she didn't use, a tail on the final *l* that curled like a root seeking water.

"No," she said. Her voice came out flat and reasonable, which frightened her more than screaming would have. "No, I haven't signed."

In the dark window above the sink—glass counts, glass has always counted—her reflection nodded. Nodded *first*. And then Ansel felt her own head dip, a small involuntary bow, one second late, agreeing to something she had not agreed to.

She backed out of the kitchen without touching the paper. In the hallway her phone buzzed: a photograph she'd taken of the deed two nights ago, timestamped and unaltered, showing the signature line blank. Good. Evidence. The past still agreed with her. The past was the last territory she held.

She pinned the photo to her inventory board, next to the room-by-room grid, next to the chalk-notation key, next to Marta's phone number written on a receipt. The board was her fortress wall. Every documented fact was a stone in it.

Then she looked at the board and saw, in the lower corner, a photograph she did not remember taking: the kitchen table, the deed, the pen—and the signature line filled in, dated tomorrow.

* * *

She smashed the bathroom mirror first.

Not in rage—she wanted to be clear with herself about that, wanted the record to show method. She wrapped it in a blanket, carried it to the cellar floor, and broke it with a hammer against the concrete, wearing gloves and safety glasses, because a surveyor does not enter an unmapped space without protection. The glass came apart with a sound like a struck bell, high and wrong, and the pieces skated across the floor reflecting the bare bulb in forty directions.

Then the reflections kept going after the bulb was blocked.

Each shard showed a piece of a room. Not the cellar. A nursery—she recognized it from the photographs, the impossible Room 19 that existed only on her memory card—sunlit, dust motes hanging in the light like suspended weather. Another shard: the music room, but rearranged, the piano pushed against a wall that wasn't there yet. Another: a hallway lined with small framed mirrors, and in each tiny frame a version of this kitchen, this moment, this woman standing over broken glass.

Rooms that didn't exist. Rooms the house hadn't built yet. The shards weren't reflecting the present. They were reflecting forward.

Ansel crouched among the fragments, hammer loose in her hand, and understood with a clarity that felt like falling: the mirrors had never been showing her a delayed copy. First they'd shown her the past by one second—what she'd already been. Now they showed the future by one second—what she was about to be. And the deed, the wet signature, the photograph dated tomorrow: the house wasn't haunting her. It was *editing* her. Moving through her life the way water moves through limestone, dissolving a little of the rock, redepositing it downstream in whatever shape the current preferred.

And the current's preferred shape was a woman who signed.

* * *

She spent the afternoon destroying every reflective surface in the house, and it took longer than it should have, because there kept being more of them.

The cheval glass in Room 3. The vanity mirror in Room 7. The oval in the front hall with its tarnished frame of cast grapes. The little hand mirror in the sewing basket, the polished lid of the biscuit tin, the chrome kettle, the darkened window of the display cabinet in the dining room. She worked top to bottom, floor to floor, wrapping and breaking, sweeping the fragments into contractor bags, and the whole time the house made sounds around her—not protest exactly, but attention. Pipes ticking. Doors easing open along their hinges with soft sighs, as if the house were turning in its sleep to watch her work.

By dusk she had twenty-three bags of glass stacked in the cellar, and she stood looking at them and felt no victory at all. Because she had missed one. She knew she'd missed one, because she could hear it.

The portrait.

She climbed the stairwell slowly, flashlight beam climbing ahead of her, and there it hung at the landing's turn: the woman mid-count, lips parted, fingers raised, wearing the ring and the posture and, increasingly, the face. Eleven days ago the painted woman had been perhaps fifty. Tonight she was older than Ansel's mother had lived to be, and her eyes had begun to do something Ansel's eyes did when she was concentrating—narrow slightly at the left, a squint from an old injury Ansel had never told anyone about.

The varnish gleamed. Under the flashlight it didn't gleam like varnish. It gleamed like the surface tension on deep water.

"You're not a mirror," Ansel told it. "You're paint. You're oil on canvas and you are *inventory*."

The painted woman's raised fingers—frozen mid-count for sixty years, per the neighbor's stories, per Coral's diary with its entries naming Ansel before Ansel was born—those fingers curled. Slowly. Deliberately. One, two, three, counting down instead of up.

And in the gleam of the varnish, small and curved and perfectly clear, Ansel saw a reflection of the stairwell behind her: the steps, the rail, the flashlight—

—and a figure standing three stairs below her, wearing her grandmother's cardigan and her mother's posture and a face assembled from photographs Ansel had never taken of people she'd never met, smiling with Ansel's own smile.

Ansel whirled. The stairs were empty. Of course they were empty. The stairs were always going to be empty one second before she looked.

* * *

She called Marta from the porch, because the porch was outside the house's skin and the night air tasted like the city she actually lived in, exhaust and rain and someone's distant grill, real smells, time-stamped smells.

Marta picked up on the fourth ring, breathing the wet rattle that had gotten worse since the day she'd given Ansel the two lines of the verse and the photograph of the three girls. "You sound like my sister used to sound," Marta said, "the week before she stopped coming to the door."

"The mirrors reversed." Ansel heard her own voice shake and hated it, corrected it, flattened it into reportage. "They're running ahead now. One second ahead. And the deed—it's signed. My handwriting. Wet ink. Dated tomorrow."

A long silence down the line, long enough that Ansel checked the call was still live.

"It's not forging you," Marta said finally. "I want you to understand that, because it matters what you think it's doing. It's not making a fake. It's *previewing*. It shows you the truth one second early and dares you to disagree with yourself. That's how it got Coral. Not force. Coral just got tired of arguing with her own face."

"I'm not tired."

"No," Marta said, and there was something in her voice that might have been pride, or might have been pity. "You're not. Listen. The verse I gave you—two lines, and I told you the third comes from the house when it trusts you. I lied a little. Not about the third line. About the order. You can't wait for trust like it's weather. You have to build it. Do you understand me? Stop breaking things. Start keeping them."

"Marta—"

"My granddaughter will come by Thursday. Rosalind. She has my mother's hands and none of my fears. Let her help you." A pause, the rattle of breath. "And Ansel. When the mirrors show you signing—look closely at the pen. Look at whose hand holds it. That's all I can give you tonight."

The line went dead. On the porch step beside her, Ansel noticed, someone had left a jar of wildflower honey and a note in a shaking hand: *For the girl who counts. — M.* She hadn't heard anyone come up the walk. The honey was still warm from Marta's kitchen, which meant Marta had been here minutes ago, which meant—

Which meant the phone call had run one second ahead too, maybe. Or behind. Ansel pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until the dark went orange.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in the kitchen facing away from the table, drinking tea gone cold, and at some point she became aware that the window over the sink—uncovered, because she'd run out of sheets and because a window is also a mirror after dark—was showing her the kitchen.

Showing her the kitchen *with the lights on*, though her lights were off.

In the reflected kitchen, a woman sat at the table with her back to the window—Ansel's shoulders, Ansel's cropped hair—and the fountain pen moved in her hand, steady, unhurried, laying down the curling tail of a final *l*. The woman set down the pen. Squared the deed to the table's edge. Stood, and turned toward the window, and smiled with the whole composite face—the grandmother's cheekbones, the mother's mouth, the faceless girl from the photograph finally given features, all of it arranged around Ansel's own eyes.

One second later, Ansel's actual hand, resting on the cold tabletop, twitched toward the pen.

She snatched it back. Knocked over the tea. And then she was moving, taking the stairs two at a time, because there was one reflective surface left in the house and she had promised herself she wouldn't, and she did it anyway.

The portrait's varnish shone like a lake at dawn.

In it, the painted woman had lowered her hands. Finished counting. And she was holding a pen, and beneath her, painted in the lower margin in letters that had not been there yesterday, in ink that gleamed wet on dry canvas, was a single line of text:

*Tomorrow, then. Same time. You've seen how it ends—now let me show you how it begins.*

Below the house, far below the cellar, in tunnels Ansel had mapped and named and claimed, something began to sing the lullaby from the beginning.
