## 15. Doors Out of Order

Ansel woke on the floor of the music room with chalk dust in her hair and the taste of copper at the back of her throat. She had gone to sleep in the bedroom—she was almost certain of it. She had locked the bedroom door, wedged a chair beneath the knob, and set her camera to record. The camera lay beside her now, still running, its little red light patient as an insect's eye.

She sat up slowly, the way she'd learned to sit up in tunnels when the air felt wrong: no sudden movements, no assumptions about which way was out.

The music box sat closed on the piano where she'd left it. Morning light came through the shutters in thin gold blades. Everything looked ordinary, and that was the first lie.

She counted the doors before she stood. It wasn't superstition anymore; it was methodology. Four doors in the music room. One, two, three, four. Except last night there had been three, and the fourth door—the one between the window and the piano—was painted the exact shade of green she remembered from a hallway she hadn't stood in since she was nine years old.

Her mother had painted that hallway herself, on her knees, with a roller wrapped in a plastic bag so it wouldn't drip. Ansel remembered the smell of the paint, the radio playing low, the way her mother had let her roll one stripe near the floor where nobody would see it. *That's our secret stripe,* her mother had said. *Evidence we were here.*

Ansel did not go to the green door. Not yet. She went to the camera instead and rewound the footage with hands that were steadier than she deserved.

The recording showed the bedroom door opening at 3:14 a.m.—from the outside. The frame held empty hallway for eleven seconds. Then the view lurched, tilted, swung through corridors she did not recognize and could not have walked, and settled on the music room floor, where the image showed Ansel already asleep, curled like a comma, though she had never gotten up. The timestamp said 3:26. There was no gap. No missing minute. The house had simply decided where she would be, and her body had agreed without consulting her.

She stopped the recording. Her breath fogged the screen.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept them in the world where they had to obey physics. "Okay. You want to show me something."

The green door waited. Doors always waited. That was the whole problem with doors.

* * *

She inventoried the house first. Ritual before risk. She photographed every door on the upper floors and found the disorder spreading like frost across glass: the linen closet opened onto her first apartment, the one above the laundromat, its water-stained ceiling rendered in perfect miniature; the door to the upstairs bathroom stood ajar on a sliver of hospital corridor, fluorescent and endless; the cellar stairs, when she checked, descended into her own survey office from three years ago, complete with the coffee ring on the desk and the pinned map of the eastern tunnels—the map she'd lost, or thought she'd lost, the night she'd stopped exploring.

The house was not rearranging itself. She understood that now, standing in the doorway of her own past with her camera hanging forgotten at her side. The house was rearranging *her*. It was laying her life out room by room like a hand of cards, showing her what it held, what it knew, what it had been keeping.

It was a catalog. She recognized the form instantly, the way you recognize your own handwriting. She had been doing the same thing to the house for weeks.

"Fine," she whispered. "We're both surveyors. Let's see who maps whom."

She started at the top and worked down, photographing each impossible threshold, logging coordinates, refusing—mostly—to step through. Discipline. Data over dread. But discipline frayed at the edges, and the edges were made of memory, and memory was exactly what the house had chosen to build with.

The door under the main staircase, the one that had been locked since her arrival, stood open this morning. Beyond it: linoleum the color of weak tea. A radiator ticking. The smell of coffee and dish soap and something floral underneath, some soap or shampoo that existed nowhere else in the world except in the olfactory vault of childhood.

Ansel stood in the threshold for a long time.

Then she stepped into her mother's kitchen.

* * *

It was not a recreation. That was the horror of it, once she made herself look properly. It was not a stage set, not a diorama. The grout between the tiles was cracked in the same places. The window over the sink had the same dead fly trapped between the panes, desiccated to a husk, because her mother had never gotten around to disassembling the window and neither, apparently, had the house. The refrigerator hummed at the exact pitch she'd fallen asleep to for eighteen years, a sound she had not heard since she was twenty-two and had not known she missed until this moment, when it filled her chest like water filling a cracked vessel.

There were dishes in the rack. Two mugs. One chipped, one whole.

Ansel touched nothing. She walked the perimeter the way she walked every new space, counting exits—window, window, back door, the doorway to the hall—and the count came back wrong. There was an extra doorway. Where the pantry had been, where the pantry should be, there was instead a short corridor turning left toward a room she couldn't see into, a room lit warm and dim, amber as lamplight through honey.

And from that room, someone was humming.

The lullaby.

Not the version the music box played—not the truncated thing that stopped just short of its ending, leaving a silence shaped like a held breath. This was the whole song. The melody ran past the place where it always broke off and continued, climbing somewhere unexpected, tender, resolving through a phrase Ansel had never heard and recognized anyway, the way you recognize a face in a stranger's child. And then the final verse began—words this time, half-swallowed, hummed more than sung, but words—and Ansel's whole body turned toward the sound like a compass needle.

*Marta's verse.* Two lines Marta had taught her, dying and lucid, gripping her wrist with bird-boned strength. *The third line comes from the house when it trusts you.*

This was the third line. It was being sung to her from behind a wall in a kitchen that had been demolished a decade ago.

Her eyes burned. She hated that. She hated the house for knowing exactly which lock in her to turn.

She took one step toward the amber doorway. The humming faltered—just slightly, a hitch in the breath—and resumed.

Another step. The radiator ticked. The refrigerator hummed. The singer's voice was low and unhurried and it was—she could not stop herself from thinking it—it was her mother's voice, the specific grain of it, the slight weariness folded into the warmth, the voice that had read to her through fevers and argued with teachers on her behalf and gone quiet in the last year, so quiet, before the quiet became permanent.

Ansel walked toward the doorway. Fast now, past caring, past protocol, her boots loud on the linoleum that should have been soft with age but wasn't, nothing here aged, everything here was preserved at the precise temperature of longing—

The singing stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, clean, mid-phrase, the way a needle lifts off a record. The amber light in the far room dimmed by one degree, like a lamp turned down by a careful hand.

"No," Ansel said. Her voice cracked against the kitchen tiles. "No, wait—"

She crossed the threshold into the corridor. It was longer than it should have been. She walked it anyway, faster, and the walls breathed past her, wallpaper giving way to plaster giving way to something smoother, and the amber light ahead thinned to gray, and when she finally reached the end of the hall there was no room at all. Only the cellar stairs, descending into cold and dark, and the faint smell of wet stone, and the lullaby gone as if it had never been.

She stood at the top of the stairs with her fists clenched and her heart slamming and understood, with terrible clarity, what had just happened.

It wasn't a trap. Traps wanted to catch you. This was a lure, and lures wanted you to follow, and following meant wanting, and wanting was consent in its first, smallest form. The resident didn't need her signature yet. It was cultivating the appetite for signing. Every door it opened onto her past was a question, and every question ended the same way: *wouldn't you stay? Wouldn't you stay here, where the song is finished, where the kitchen is warm, where someone is home?*

"You don't get to use her voice," Ansel said to the dark at the bottom of the stairs. Her voice shook. She let it. "You don't get to use any of them. You're wearing them. That's all you know how to do—wear."

The dark said nothing. Somewhere below, water dripped with the patience of geology.

But her legs were unsteady when she climbed back through the kitchen, and she made herself pause at the counter, at the dish rack, and look at the two mugs. One chipped. One whole. Her mother had always taken the chipped one, had always said the chip gave it character, had drunk from it every morning of Ansel's remembered life while Ansel got the whole one.

The whole mug sat on the side nearest the amber doorway. As if set out for a guest.

As if set out for her.

Ansel walked out of the kitchen without touching it, and she did not let herself run, because running was also a kind of answer.

* * *

She spent the afternoon in the survey office that had replaced her bedroom—her real records, her real maps, pulled through time and space into the house's growing collection of her. She worked with the methodical fury of someone refusing to feel. She cross-referenced the doors' locations against her inventory photographs and found the pattern emerging like a figure rising out of developer fluid.

The house was arranging her memories in sequence. Not randomly. In order. The apartment above the laundromat adjoined the hospital corridor; the hospital corridor adjoined a door that opened onto her college dormitory; the dormitory shared a wall with the green hallway from when she was nine. Walking the upper floor east to west was walking backward through her life, room by room, year by year, toward something.

Toward the earliest thing.

She found it at dusk, at the end of the sequence: a small door, child-height, painted white, with a latch she knew the weight of in her hand before she touched it. Beyond it, if the pattern held, would be the first room she had ever known. A nursery. Or whatever had come before memory.

She did not open it. She photographed it, logged it, and nailed a survey marker to the frame—her own chalk notation, her own system, a claim staked in the only language she trusted. *Mapped. Not entered. Surveyor's discretion.*

The house, she noticed, had begun correcting her chalk marks elsewhere. Fading lines reappearing in her handwriting. Errors she'd made in week one quietly amended. The house was learning her notation. Practicing her hand.

Two cartographers, one map. She wrote that in her log and underlined it twice.

* * *

Night came down over the house like a lid. Ansel barricaded herself in the survey office with salt she didn't believe in and a fire axe she absolutely believed in, and she reviewed the day's footage, and that was where she found it.

The camera had recorded the kitchen while she stood in it. She watched herself cross the room toward the amber doorway—watched it twice, three times, frame by frame. And in the background, reflected in the dark kitchen window, in the glass over the sink with its dead fly sealed between the panes, there was a figure standing in the corridor behind her.

The singer. Half-visible, blurred, wearing a shape that might have been her mother's shape, might have been anyone's, might have been assembled from the composite of every woman the house had ever swallowed. The figure was not watching Ansel.

It was watching the mugs in the dish rack. Its head was tilted, considering, the way Ansel considered a room she was about to add to a catalog.

And then—frame by frame, she advanced it, her stomach tightening—the figure raised one hand toward the window glass, toward the reflection, and pressed its palm flat against the inside of the mirror-world, and Ansel saw that its fingers were wrong. Too long. Too many joints. A hand designed by something that had studied hands from a distance, through glass, for a very long time.

On the soundtrack, beneath the lullaby, beneath her own footsteps, there was a sound she hadn't heard live: a whisper, looping, patient, in a voice like paper being folded.

*Count with me. Count with me. Count with me.*

Ansel paused the video on the figure's pressed palm. Behind it, in the reflection, in the kitchen that existed only as memory and glass, she could just make out the wall of the amber room. And on that wall hung a frame.

A portrait. Small, crooked, painted in colors she couldn't resolve at this resolution—but she knew the composition. She had been living under it for weeks. A woman, mid-count, lips parted, fingers raised.

Except in this version, in this reflection, the woman's hands were lowered.

Finished counting.

Waiting for the next number.

Ansel closed the laptop. In the silence of the survey office, deep beneath the floorboards, beneath the cellar, beneath the mapped and unmapped tunnels, the house began—very softly, from everywhere at once—to hum the lullaby again. All of it. Including the verse.

Including the line Marta had told her could only come from the house when it trusted her.

It was offering her the ending early. And Ansel sat in the dark with the fire axe across her knees, understanding at last that nothing free had ever been offered in these walls, and wondering, with a coldness that felt almost like calm, what price the house thought she would pay for a song she was already planning to steal.
