## 10. Wren's Last Minutes

The water in the basement came up to Dee's shins and it was warm, which was worse than cold. Cold water you could explain — burst mains, storm runoff, the city drowning itself the way it did every few years. But this water was body-temperature, and it moved when she didn't want it to move, and twice now she had felt something brush past her calf going the other direction.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Marcus said from the stairs. He'd refused to come down past the third step. He stood there with his phone flashlight cupped in both hands like an offering, its beam cutting a pale cone through the dark.

"Because the tape ends where it ends," Dee said. "And I need to know how it ends."

"You could wait for morning."

She didn't answer that. Morning wasn't a thing anymore; she'd stopped believing in it around the time the clock above the studio door started running backward, its second hand ticking counterclockwise with a confidence that felt almost mocking. The storm outside had swallowed the streets whole. The station sat on its little hill of high ground like an island, and they were its population of three.

Three, she thought. It used to be two. Then Marcus had come pounding on the loading door at midnight, soaked through, saying his brother's voice had called him from a number that belonged to a man who died in March, and Jonah had let him in without a word, like he'd been expecting it. Like he'd been dreading it.

Now Jonah was upstairs keeping the mic hot because that was the rule they'd pieced together from Wren's fragments: *the line stays open*. As long as someone was broadcasting, the thing below stayed interested in the signal instead of the people. So Jonah talked to the dead and to the dark, steady as a metronome, while Dee went down into the flooded archive to find the last minutes of a reel recorded before either of her parents were born.

The archive occupied what had once been the station's record library. Metal shelving ran in rows down the length of the room, and the water had swollen the cardboard sleeves until they sagged like wet leaves. The air tasted of rust and mildew and something else underneath, something sweetish and wrong, like flowers left too long in a closed room.

The reel cabinet was against the far wall, steel, sealed. She'd found the key taped under the drawer of Wren's old desk an hour ago, wrapped in wax paper, along with a note in fountain-pen ink that had bled but not faded: *FOR WHOEVER IS LEFT. FORGIVE THE MESS.*

"Aldous," she said to the dark, working the key into the lock, "you are the mess."

* * *

She'd spent two days in the archives before tonight, back when this was still research and not survival. The station's paper trail told a story everyone at KVLT half-knew and nobody told: Aldous Wren, founder, first voice on the frequency, broadcasting from 1951 until the night in '61 when he walked into this basement and never walked out. Officially he'd retired. Unofficially, his car was still in the garage sublevel, under forty years of dust, keys in the ignition, fuel gauge on empty.

The reel she'd found — CONFESSION — DO NOT AIR — ran twenty-two minutes. She'd digitized it on a machine older than she was, listening through headphones in the empty studio at four in the afternoon with sunlight on her desk, and even in daylight it had gotten into her like cold air through a cracked window.

Wren's voice on the tape was beautiful. That was the first horror of it. Deep and warm, a radio voice from the age when radio voices were built like church organs. He spoke calmly, methodically, like a man reading his own eulogy off cards:

*"My name is Aldous Wren. On the fourth of November, 1951, I opened this station on a frequency I did not find by tuning. I dug for it. I had been told — by whom no longer matters, they are all dead or better off — that beneath the Bell Theater there was a door, and behind the door a listener who had been waiting since before the theater, since before the street, since before the name of this city. A listener who would give anything to be heard."*

*"I thought I was digging toward a story. The greatest broadcast ever made. I was thirty-one years old and I wanted the world to know my name."*

A pause on the tape, long enough that Dee had checked to see if it had ended.

*"It heard me before I heard it. That is the thing no one tells you. You do not call it. It answers the call you haven't placed yet. It knew my show's opening line before I wrote it. It knew my mother's maiden name. It knew the song I hum when I'm frightened, which I have never done on air, which I have never done in front of another living soul."*

*"I invited it. There is no gentler word. I opened my mouth on live radio and said: if anyone is listening, I am here. And something answered that had been listening since before there was a here."*

Then the tape degraded — Dee had assumed damage, water spots, magnetic rot — and Wren's voice came back changed, layered, as though two men were speaking in perfect unison through one throat:

*"It cannot enter where it is not invited by voice. That is its law, and laws are all it has. But a voice is a door, and I had left mine open for ten years. So I will do what should have been done in 1951. I will close it. From the inside."*

*"Understand me, whoever finds this. I am not banishing it. Banishment requires a power I do not have. I am doing something smaller and more terrible. I am giving it what it wants — a voice, continuous, devoted, mine — and I am wrapping that gift around the door like wire. Every hour I speak, the seal holds. It will hate me and need me in equal measure. We will be married, it and I, until one of us stops talking."*

*"Do not pity me. Pity implies I had a self worth losing. Listen—"*

And here the recording had done the thing that made Dee sleep with the lights on afterward: Wren had laughed, and the laugh had been two laughs, and then three, and then a chord of laughter, dozens of voices laughing together inside one man's mouth, and underneath them all, faintly, unmistakably, a bell.

The tape cut to static at minute twenty-two.

Static, Dee now understood, was not an ending. Static was a door held shut by someone screaming through the gap.

* * *

The lock gave with a click that sounded obscenely loud. Inside the cabinet, rows of reels stood in labeled boxes, and the labels were all in Wren's hand — dates, running times, and single words. *HUM. LISTENING. IT SPEAKS IN WEATHER. SHE SANG TONIGHT.* Decades of documentation. He had kept logging, down here in the dark, right up until—

Dee's light found the box at the bottom. No label on the spine. Just a date: 11/04/61. The night he disappeared.

Inside: two reels. One marked FINAL MINUTES in letters that started controlled and deteriorated into scratches. The other unmarked, its leader tape stained brown.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed distantly, the way you notice weather happening to someone else. She zipped both reels into the dry bag she'd brought — a stupid little precaution, a sandwich bag for the most important object in the building — and turned to go.

That was when the water moved.

Not a ripple. A current. The whole flooded floor slid toward the back wall in one smooth motion, like the room inhaling, and in the sudden shallow silence Dee saw, printed in the silt on the concrete, a pattern the water had been hiding: concentric rings, hundreds of them, carved into the floor around a central point directly beneath the studio above. Not carved. *Worn.* Something had been pacing that circle for sixty years, or standing in it, turning, turning, and the floor remembered.

At the center of the rings, scratched deep enough to survive the flood, were words. She waded closer. Her light shook.

KEEP TALKING

Two words. In handwriting she recognized, because she'd spent two days with it in the archives, because it matched the labels on every reel in the cabinet, because it matched the note taped under the desk.

Aldous Wren had written it from the other side of whatever he'd become, and he had written it recently. The edges of the grooves were clean. No silt in the cuts.

"Oh God," Marcus said from the stairs. "Dee. Dee, the water's doing something."

"I see it."

"It's going in a circle. Why is it going in a circle?"

Because something down here was walking the same path it had walked ten thousand times before, she thought. Because the flood hadn't drowned the archive. It had given him a surface to write on.

* * *

Back upstairs, the studio was a fortress of candlelight and duct-taped cables. Jonah was on air, mid-sentence, and Dee stopped in the doorway to listen, because she always stopped, because some animal part of her needed to confirm each time that his voice was still only his voice.

"—and if you're just joining us, it's a long night, friends, so get comfortable. Line one is open. Line one is always open."

He looked wrecked. Fourteen hours on the mic, coffee gone cold in a row of mugs, his bandaged hand cradled against his chest where the console had burned him pulling Marcus back from the edge of an invitation. But his voice was level. That was the miracle of Jonah Vasquez — the steadiness wasn't natural, it was *built*, laid down layer over layer like roadbed, and Dee was starting to understand what it was covering.

She held up the dry bag. His eyes flicked to it, then to her face, and whatever he saw there made him cue a record without breaking stride — an old instrumental, organ music, filling the airwaves while the dead waited politely on hold.

"Found it?" he asked, hand over his own mic.

"The ending." She set the reels on the dubbing bench and threaded the first one onto the player with fingers that wouldn't quite obey. "Jonah. It's not what we thought. He didn't trap it down there. He—"

The tape began to play, and she stopped talking, because Wren started.

The final minutes were rougher than the confession. Recorded in this basement, judging by the echo, in the dark, close to the microphone — you could hear his breath, wet and labored, and beneath it that faint bell-tone that lived in everything now.

*"Last entry,"* Wren said. *"If the word last means anything anymore. I have been speaking for six hours. My throat understands things my mind refuses to. It is learning the shape of me. Or I am learning the shape of it. The distinction stopped mattering somewhere around the second hour."*

*"To whoever plays this: the binding works. Hear me clearly — it WORKS. But it works the way a dam works, not the way a wall works. It needs mass. It needs a voice poured into it continuously, or the seal thins. Sixty years, seventy, a hundred — however long the one who holds it can hold. When the holder fails, the door opens inward, and it will come up through whatever wires are nearest, wearing whatever voices it has collected, and it will ask, politely, so politely, to be invited in."*

*"So do not fail quickly. That is my only advice and it is worthless advice, because you do not choose the hour of your failing. I can feel mine coming. It is patient. It can afford patience. It has nothing else."*

A sound on the tape then — Dee flinched, Marcus made a small noise behind her — the sound of a man weeping, except the weeping harmonized with itself, griefs stacked on griefs, and through it Wren kept talking, calm as a sign-off:

*"One more thing. If someone comes after me — and someone always comes after, that is the disease of our profession, we cannot leave a signal unanswered — know that I am still here. Not dead. Not alive. Holding. You will hear me in the hum of the wiring. You will hear me in the pause between songs. I am the reason the frequency stays warm."*

*"I traded my name for the door. It seemed a fair price. It seemed a fair price because I had already begun to disappear — that is how it hunts, it finds the ones who are already thin. Find whoever is thinnest in your station, whoever built themselves out of broadcast and cardboard, and keep them away from this tape."*

*"Keep them away from the mic."*

*"Keep them—"*

The tape ended mid-word. Not static. Mid-word, the way a phone goes dead.

The second reel was shorter. Ninety seconds. Dee played it because she had to, because incomplete knowledge had nearly killed them twice already.

It was Wren's voice, but there was no strain in it anymore. No breath. It sounded like a recording of a recording of a man, worn smooth as sea glass, and it said only this:

*"It is 2024. I know because the water told me. Someone new is answering the calls. He is kind. Kindness is the thinnest coat of all."*

Then, softer, in a register that was pure Aldous Wren, the founder, the first voice, the man who had wanted the world to know his name:

*"Whoever you are up there — I held it as long as I could. I am so tired. I have been so tired for sixty years."*

*"Please don't make me hold it alone anymore."*

* * *

Silence in the studio. The organ record had ended; Jonah hadn't cued anything else. On the board, the hold lines glowed their patient red — seven of them, eight, nine, the dead queueing like it was any other night.

Marcus spoke first, hoarse. "He's still down there. Right now. Holding the door with his voice."

"Yes."

"And he's asking us—" Marcus's jaw worked. "He's asking us to take over."

"He's asking us to help," Dee said. "There's a difference. Maybe."

"Is there?" Marcus laughed, and it came out broken. "My brother's voice is on those lines, Dee. I've heard it. What if holding the door is what lets him rest? What if Wren's been down there sixty years and the only way out is through?"

"That's exactly what it wants you to think."

"That's exactly what *he* wants us to think. Those might not be the same thing anymore. That's the whole problem, isn't it? After sixty years, whose voice is whose?"

Jonah hadn't spoken. Dee turned and found him looking at the reels the way a man looks at a mirror he isn't ready for. She knew that look. She'd catalogued it over two days of research without knowing what she was cataloguing: Wren describing a man who built himself out of broadcast, a man already thin, a man whose name was a costume.

*Find whoever is thinnest in your station.*

"Jonah," she said carefully.

"I'm fine," he said, in the voice he used on callers who weren't fine, and reached for the reel marked FINAL MINUTES, and began, quietly, to thread it onto the playback deck — because he'd understood before either of them, and Dee watched the understanding settle over him like frost, that Wren hadn't left them a warning.

He'd left them a manual.

And on the hold lines, all at once, every red light began to pulse — slow, rhythmic, patient as a heartbeat — in time with a bell none of them could hear yet.
