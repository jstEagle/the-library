## 6. Siblings Summoned

Edmund sat in his motel room with the phone face-down on the bedspread, the way you might sit across from a dog you'd been bitten by. The lamp buzzed. The ice machine down the hall clunked and sighed. Somewhere in the walls of the building—this building, a different building, an ordinary building—a pipe ticked, and he flinched before he could stop himself.

It's a motel, he told himself. Motels have pipes. Pipes make noise. That's the whole job of a pipe.

He had spent the day finishing the exterior measurements, which had gone fine, mostly, except for the twenty minutes he'd spent standing at the back fence staring at the well cap, which was wet. Not damp. Wet, the way a stone gets when something underneath it is breathing. He had touched it with one finger and then wiped the finger on his trousers like a man who had touched a stove, and he had written *well: cap intact, no visible issues* in his field notes, which was the most generous lie he had told anyone all week, including himself.

The appraisal was nearly done. The estate reading, however, required family. Family required a phone call. And a phone call required him to be a person who existed, which was, historically, his least favorite role.

He picked up the phone. He put it down. He picked it up again and opened the family group chat, which had not been used in eleven months, the last message being Marcus's *Happy Thanksgiving all* and Delia's thumbs-up, and before that a link Marcus had sent about gutters that no one had acknowledged. The chat was called **Quintanas 🏠**, a name Delia had chosen years ago, back when the emoji still felt like optimism.

Edmund typed:

**Edmund:** Hi. Sorry to text out of nowhere. Grandma's lawyer has scheduled the estate reading. Saturday the 14th, 10 a.m., Halloway & Finch offices, Millbrook. I've been doing the property appraisal this week. You should both be there.

He read it four times. It sounded like a memo. He tried again.

**Edmund:** Hey. It's been a while. I know. The lawyer set the reading for the 14th. I'm at the house this week finishing the appraisal. Hope you're both well.

Worse. Now it sounded like a hostage note written by someone being forced to perform wellness. He deleted everything and stared at the ceiling, where a water stain had arranged itself into a shape that was either a map of Ohio or a face, and he chose not to examine which.

The truth was he had rehearsed this call for ten years. In the car, in rented kitchens, on the fire escapes of a dozen apartments, he had composed the message he would send when he finally came back: *I'm sorry I disappeared. I had reasons. The reasons were terrible and also true.* But every draft had the same flaw, which was that it required him to explain the reasons, and the reasons were a sentence an old woman had said to a twelve-year-old boy in a kitchen that smelled of cinnamon and dust, and he had never once said that sentence out loud to another living soul.

So he settled on the memo. He sent it. Then he turned the phone face-down again and went to stand by the window, where he could watch the lights of Millbrook and pretend his heart wasn't doing the thing it did, the arrhythmic hopeful thing, like a dog hearing a key in a door.

The reply came in ninety seconds. Marcus had always been fast. Marcus answered emails at 6 a.m. and returned calls from the dentist's chair. Marcus's entire personality was a man standing ready by the phone.

**Marcus:** Noted.

Edmund waited. There was more. There was always more with Marcus; the *noted* was just the door slamming before the lecture.

**Marcus:** Ten years, Edmund. Ten years and you text like a survey report. "Hope you're both well." Who taught you to write like that, the witness protection program?

**Edmund:** It's been a hard week.

**Marcus:** It's been a hard DECADE, but sure, your week.

Then, forty seconds later:

**Marcus:** We'll be there. Priya will want to know about hotels. Are the twins okay to come or should we get a sitter? It's a will reading, not a séance.

Edmund stared at the word *séance* longer than it deserved. It deserved exactly nothing. It was a word. Words were not omens.

**Edmund:** Kids are fine. House is fine. Everything's fine.

**Marcus:** "Everything's fine." Great. Can't wait.

* * *

Delia took longer. Delia always took longer, because Delia read a message, sat with it, carried it around the house with her like a casserole, and answered when the answer had finished cooking. Edmund showered. He came out with wet hair and found three messages waiting.

**Delia:** We'll be there.

**Delia:** I'm not going to do this over text, Edmund. The thing where we're polite and cold and everyone pretends the last ten years were a scheduling issue. I'll be civil on Saturday. That's the best I have.

**Delia:** Also, question. And I want you to answer it like a normal person and not like a property surveyor.

Edmund's stomach performed a small, professional appraisal of itself.

**Delia:** Rosie drew a picture at school last week. Family portraits, the whole class did them. She drew our family — me, Theo, Grandma, Marcus, Priya, the twins. And then she drew an extra person. A tall thin man standing a little apart from everyone, near the edge of the paper, and she said — and I wrote this down because I didn't believe she'd said it — she said, "That's the extra grandpa. He waits by the house."

Edmund sat down on the motel bed. The springs spoke. He did not.

**Delia:** Edmund?

**Delia:** You've been at the house. Is there something at the house you want to tell me? Because Rosie has never met him. She was two when Grandma died. She has never once seen a photo of you that I know of. And yet.

And yet.

He looked at his phone for a long time. The lamp buzzed. Down the hall, the ice machine clunked, and he noticed — the way you notice your own heartbeat, suddenly and against your will — that the pipe in the wall behind the bathroom had gone quiet. Not ticking. Not knocking. Waiting, if a pipe could wait, which it couldn't, because pipes were metal and metal didn't wait, and he was going to hold onto that thought like a rope over a canyon.

He typed carefully.

**Edmund:** Kids say strange things. It's probably from a dream or something she saw on TV.

**Delia:** She drew him with your chin.

**Edmund:** I have a very common chin.

**Delia:** Edmund. I'm asking you as your sister. Is there something at the house?

There was. There was a chair that appeared in photographs like a guest who wouldn't leave. There was a well filling itself with water that had no source. There were pipes that counted, and a door with new hinges on old wood, and a dust pattern in the parlor that looked like someone had recently stood there, waiting to be measured. There was the sentence grandma had said, which he still hadn't said aloud, and which was now pressing against the back of his teeth like a tooth that needed to come out.

**Edmund:** I'll tell you everything on Saturday. I promise. Both of you. It's — there's a lot, and it sounds insane in a text, and I'd rather do it in person where you can see my face and decide for yourselves how insane I am.

A long pause. Long enough that he got up, drank water he didn't want, and checked the peephole for no reason at all.

**Delia:** Okay. Saturday. But Edmund — if you're in trouble, say so now. If someone's hurt you or scammed you or you owe someone money, I don't care what it is, just say so.

The tenderness of it undid him a little. Ten years of silence and her first instinct was still *who hurt you.* He had forgotten that about her. He had tried to forget a lot of things about them, and the forgetting had worked about as well as the well cap.

**Edmund:** Nobody's hurt me. I promise. It's the house.

**Delia:** The house.

**Edmund:** I know how that sounds.

**Delia:** I don't know how it sounds. That's why I asked.

* * *

Marcus called at 11:40 p.m., which for Marcus was the equivalent of a midnight howl at the moon. Edmund let it ring twice, lost the battle with himself, and answered.

"You're at the house," Marcus said, without hello. His voice was the same voice it had been ten years ago — flat, clipped, a man reading a pressure gauge. "Alone. At night."

"I'm at a motel. The house is twenty minutes away."

"You're *near* the house. At night. Voluntarily." A pause. In the background, a dishwasher ran, and a child's voice said something about a lost sock, and Marcus said, "One second, buddy," away from the phone, and something in Edmund's chest folded up small and aching at how gently Marcus said *buddy.* "Sorry. Twins. It's a whole ecosystem over here."

"How are they?" Edmund asked, and hated how much he meant it.

"Oliver lost a tooth and is demanding compensation. Anaya has decided she's a paleontologist and has buried things all over the yard that we're now legally required to excavate." Marcus said it in the tone of a man reporting cargo figures, but Edmund heard the pride underneath, the way you hear the sea under a floorboard. "Priya says hi. She says — " a pause, a muffled exchange — "she says you'd better have a good explanation for ten years, and also that she's making her biryani for after the reading, and you're eating some, and that's not a question."

"Tell her — " Edmund stopped. Tell her what? *Tell her thank you, tell her I've missed her, tell her I used to practice saying your names in empty apartments like a man rehearsing a defense.* "Tell her yes. Please."

"Okay." Marcus went quiet. The dishwasher hummed. Somewhere in Marcus's house a pipe knocked once — plumbing, just plumbing, just a house settling its accounts the normal way — and Edmund gripped the phone. "Delia texted me. About Rosie's drawing."

"Ah," Edmund said.

"'The extra grandpa.' That's what the kid called it. And you're telling me it's a coincidence, and you're also telling me there's something wrong with the house, and you're also telling me you'll explain on Saturday, and I want you to know that I've been a project manager for fifteen years and I know what it sounds like when a contractor says 'there's a lot of water damage but let's discuss it on-site.' It means it's bad, Edmund. It means it's bad and they don't want to say the number over the phone."

"That's — actually a pretty accurate analogy."

"I know it's accurate. That's why I said it." Marcus exhaled, long and controlled, the exhale of a man rationing his patience. "Here's what's going to happen. I'm going to assume, for the next four days, that my brother is being dramatic. I'm very good at assuming that; I've had practice. And on Saturday you're going to stand up in that lawyer's office and tell us what's going on, and if it's bad, we deal with it, because that's — " his voice caught, just slightly, on the rails of something older and softer — "that's what we do. We're Quintanas. We show up. Well. *We* show up. You've been a little — "

"I know, Marcus."

" — spotty. On the showing up."

"I know."

Silence. Not hostile silence. The other kind, the kind with a door left open in it.

"The chair thing," Marcus said finally. "Delia didn't tell me this part, I read her texts over her shoulder like a monster, but — you said something about a chair?"

Edmund closed his eyes. "Saturday."

"You said there's an extra chair. In the photos. In *every* photo."

"Saturday, Marcus."

"Because Grandma's parlor had seven chairs, Edmund. I counted them my whole childhood. Seven. I used to count them when she made us sit through the organ recitals. Seven chairs and one of them wobbled and Grandma said it was *character.*" His voice had gone up half a register, the register of a boy in a parlor being bored and safe. "So if there's eight now — "

"Saturday."

Another silence, longer. When Marcus spoke again, the project manager was gone, and what was left was just a tired man in a kitchen at midnight, holding a phone.

"Edmund. Whatever this is. You don't have to be the one holding it alone. That's — I mean, that's kind of the whole point of having siblings. You hold it together or you drop it on each other, but you don't stand off to the side of the picture holding it by yourself for ten years."

Edmund looked at the water stain on the ceiling. The face, or Ohio, looked back.

"I'll see you Saturday," he said.

"Yeah," Marcus said. "You will. And Edmund? Eat something. You always forget to eat when you're spiraling. I can hear spiraling over the phone. It's a gift."

He hung up. Edmund sat with the phone in his lap for a long time.

* * *

At 1 a.m., Delia sent one more message. No preamble, no cold open. Just:

**Delia:** Theo had a nightmare two nights ago. He's sixteen, he'd rather die than admit it, but he came into my room and sat on the floor by my bed like he was six. I asked what it was about and he said, "There was a chair in Grandma's house and someone was sitting in it, and I couldn't see his face, but I knew him." Then he went to bed.

**Delia:** I didn't connect it to Rosie's drawing until tonight. Two kids, Edmund. Two kids who've never spent a night in that house.

**Delia:** Whatever you're not telling me, tell it fast.

Edmund set the phone on the nightstand, screen glowing in the dark like a small patient lantern. He lay down. He did not sleep.

And in the wall behind the headboard — faint, unhurried, almost polite, like a knuckle rapped on a door by someone with all the time in the world — the pipe knocked twice, paused, and knocked twice more.

*Two. Two. Two children,* he thought, and then hated himself for thinking it, and then lay in the dark listening to the silence that followed, which was not an empty silence at all but the specific silence of a room in which someone has just finished speaking and is waiting, patiently, for your answer.
