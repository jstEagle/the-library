## 7. Arrivals

Marcus Quintana had a system for difficult days, and the system was this: you treated them like a Tuesday.

You got up at six. You made the coffee before anyone else needed anything from you. You loaded the car with the efficiency of a man who had once, briefly, considered a career in logistics before life handed him twins instead, and you drove with both hands on the wheel and a podcast murmuring something soothing about the history of shipping containers. Difficult days were survivable if you refused to acknowledge they were difficult. This was the entire architecture of Marcus's emotional life, and it had held up fine for forty-three years, thank you very much.

Today's difficult day was his grandmother's estate reading, which was a phrase that sounded like it belonged in a novel about rich people poisoning each other in 1920s England and not in his actual life, in his actual minivan, on an actual Saturday in October.

"Are we there yet?" said Asha, from the third row.

"We've been driving eleven minutes," Marcus said.

"That's basically forever."

"It's really not."

"I'm hungry," said Dev, from beside her, in the tone of a boy who had eaten a full breakfast forty minutes ago and would deny it under oath.

"There are snacks in the bag."

"The good ones?"

"All the snacks are the same snacks."

"They're *not*," the twins said together, with the unified outrage of a two-person committee that had never once reached consensus on anything except this.

In the passenger seat, Priya turned around to referee, then apparently thought better of it and faced front again. She was quiet in the way she got when she was thinking hard, which Marcus had learned over fifteen years of marriage to read like weather. Right now the forecast was: overcast, with a strong chance of her saying something perceptive he didn't want to hear.

"You okay?" she asked anyway.

"Fine," Marcus said. "It's just weird. Being back."

"When's the last time you were here?"

He did the math and didn't love the answer. "Nine years. Grandma's eightieth." He remembered it mostly as a series of small failures—arriving late, leaving early, standing in the parlor holding a drink he never sipped while relatives he couldn't name asked him questions about his job that he answered wrong. "The house is smaller than I remember."

"Houses usually are."

"This one isn't, though. That's the thing. I went back through my memory of it on the drive, like, deliberately, and it's bigger. The ceilings were higher. The hallway was longer." He laughed, a little embarrassed. "I used to get lost in it as a kid. Actual lost. Delia found me crying behind the water heater once when I was seven."

"And now?"

"Now I could probably still find my way around blindfolded. Which is worse, honestly. It means some part of me never left."

Priya looked out the window at the road unspooling ahead—the highway giving way to two lanes, the two lanes giving way to the gravel drive that the GPS insisted did not exist. "Your brother called you back?"

"Texted. A group thread." Marcus's phone buzzed against the console as if summoned, and he glanced down at it at a red light. The thread was titled, in Edmund's aggressively practical way, **ESTATE — LOGISTICS**. No hearts, no emojis, no *thinking of you during this hard time*. Just a spreadsheet-adjacent human being trying to manage grief through bullet points.

*Delia:* We'll be there by noon. Theo has a soccer thing until 11.
*Edmund:* Gate code is still 4-1-1-9. Same as always.
*Delia:* You remembered the gate code? You haven't been here in ten years.
*Edmund:* I appraised the property. Remembering access codes is part of the job.
*Marcus:* Since when do you do residential?
*Edmund:* Since it paid well enough.

Marcus had stared at that exchange longer than it deserved. Ten years. His brother had been gone ten years—not dead-gone, which at least came with a funeral and a casserole tradition, but *gone*-gone, the kind where Christmas cards stopped having return addresses and phone numbers changed without forwarding announcements. And now here he was, texting gate codes like he'd never left, like the decade between them was a rounding error.

"He's nervous," Priya said, watching him read.

"He's Edmund. Nervous isn't in his vocabulary. He has *concerns*, and he addresses them via email."

"You know what I mean."

Marcus did know what she meant, which was annoying. "Grandma used to say he hid so well the house lost him." He said it lightly, tossing it off, but the words came out heavier than intended, and Priya's head turned a degree or two. "She said it at dinner one time. Everyone laughed. Edmund didn't."

"And now he's the one cataloguing her house."

"Life's got a sense of humor."

The light changed. Marcus drove.

Behind them, in the third row, Asha whispered something to Dev, and Dev giggled, and Marcus caught the tail end of it in the rearview mirror—"the extra grandpa"—and felt a small cold finger trace the back of his neck, though he couldn't have told you why.

---

They arrived at noon exactly, because Marcus was the kind of man who arrived at things exactly, and the house rose up at the end of the drive looking exactly as wrong as he'd remembered: too big, too dark, its windows reflecting the gray sky in a way that made them look less like glass and more like eyes deciding whether to open.

"Whoa," said Asha.

"Whoa," agreed Dev, with reverence.

"It's a house," Marcus said. "People live in houses."

"Not houses like *that*," said Asha. "Houses like that are in movies. Movies where everybody dies."

"Asha."

"What! I'm just saying."

Priya got out first and stood looking up at the facade, and Marcus watched her face do something complicated. "There's someone already here," she said.

Edmund emerged from the side door carrying a clipboard, because of course he was carrying a clipboard, because of course his brother had responded to their grandmother's death by inventorying it. He looked—older. Thinner than Marcus remembered, grayer, with the particular hollowed quality of a man who had spent a decade being careful. But his posture was the same: slightly apologetic, slightly braced, like someone perpetually waiting for permission to take up space.

"Marcus," Edmund said.

"Edmund." They shook hands. It was absurd—they were brothers—but neither of them seemed able to convert the handshake into a hug, so it just hung there between them, professional and cold, until Priya stepped in and hugged Edmund herself, briskly, the way you'd hug a coat rack you were fond of.

"Priya," Edmund said, visibly surprised.

"You look terrible."

"Thank you. I've been sleeping at the Fairview Motor Lodge. The pillows are decorative."

"These your kids?" Edmund crouched down to twin height, which Marcus noted he did without being asked, without hesitation, like it was muscle memory. "Asha and Dev, right?"

"You remember us?" Asha said, suspicious.

"You were four. You bit me at Thanksgiving."

"That was Dev."

"It was definitely Dev," Dev confirmed proudly.

Something in Marcus's chest loosened half a turn. He watched his brother—his strange, vanished, spreadsheet-hearted brother—crouch in the gravel and negotiate with his children, and he thought: *he remembers them.* He thought: *maybe ten years wasn't as long as I decided it was.* Then he thought: *don't be sentimental, you're here for a will reading,* and put the feeling away in the drawer where he kept all the other feelings, which was getting full.

Delia's car pulled in twenty minutes later, horn tapping twice in greeting. Delia came out of it like weather—all motion and volume, sunglasses pushed up into her hair, already talking. Behind her slouched a teenager constructed entirely of hoodie and reluctance.

"Theo, say hi to your uncle Marcus."

"Hey," said Theo, to a point approximately four feet to the left of Marcus.

"He's fourteen," Delia said, as if that explained everything, which it did.

Then Theo saw Edmund, and something happened. It was small—it was the kind of thing Marcus would normally have missed entirely—but he was watching, and he saw it. The kid stopped. Not slowed. *Stopped*, mid-slouch, like a video buffering, and stared at Edmund with an expression Marcus couldn't parse: recognition, maybe, but the wrong kind. The kind you have for a stranger who's wearing a face you've seen somewhere you can't place.

"You're the one from the drawing," Theo said.

"Theo," Delia said, mortified. "We don't announce people's business."

"No, I mean—" Theo blinked, surfacing, embarrassed. "Sorry. Never mind. You just look like... never mind."

Edmund had gone very still. "What drawing?"

"My daughter drew Grandpa last week," Delia said, waving it off, herding everyone toward the door. "Except she's never met Grandpa, obviously, so it's just, like, a generic old guy, and Theo's been calling it 'the extra grandpa' because she drew him *next to* Grandma in the picture, and honestly the whole thing is—Theo, stop dragging your feet, you'll track gravel in, Mom kept this place spotless—"

But Edmund wasn't walking toward the door anymore. He was standing in the driveway with his clipboard against his chest, looking at Theo the way a man looks at a door he thought was locked and has just heard click.

"What did she draw him sitting in?" Edmund asked quietly.

Delia paused. "What?"

"The picture. Your daughter drew Grandma. What was the old man sitting in?"

"A chair?" Delia frowned. "A big wooden chair. Why, does it matter—"

"It doesn't," Edmund said, too fast, and turned toward the house. "We should go in. Mr. Voss will be waiting."

Marcus exchanged a glance with Priya. Her expression said: *you saw that too.* His expression said: *we'll discuss it later, preferably never.*

The twins ran ahead, their footsteps loud on the porch boards, and the front door—unlocked, unlatched, waiting—swung inward before anyone touched it.

Nobody remarked on that either. Which was, Marcus would think later, the last moment the family got to not remark on things.

---

Mr. Voss had been Grandma's lawyer for thirty years, and he had the specific qualities such longevity requires: a cardigan the color of oatmeal, a voice designed to make contracts sound like lullabies, and the emotional range of a filing cabinet. He sat at the head of the dining room table with papers squared in front of him, and the family arranged themselves around the table in the unconscious choreography of people returning to assigned seats after many years.

Marcus ended up where he'd always sat as a boy, facing the window. Priya beside him. Twins across, kicking each other under the table with metronomic regularity. Delia next to them, already crying a little, because Delia cried at commercials. Theo at the far end, hood up, earbud in one ear, present in body only—which is to say, a normal teenager.

And Edmund took the seat nearest the kitchen doorway. Not at the table's edge. Near the exit. Marcus noticed and filed it away with everything else.

The parlor, Marcus noted, had seven chairs. He counted without meaning to—an old habit from childhood, counting the chairs, because Grandma used to say a room should always have a seat for whoever might need one. Seven chairs, arranged around the cold fireplace, plus the settee. Plenty of seating. The room felt watched regardless.

"Before we begin," Mr. Voss said, shuffling pages, "I want to express my condolences. Your grandmother was a remarkable client. Meticulous. She updated this document every year, you know. Every single year, without fail, right up until—" he consulted nothing, remembering precisely, "—the eleventh of March."

"She died in April," Delia said.

"Yes," said Mr. Voss, and moved on with the smoothness of a man who had learned which rocks not to turn over.

He read the standard provisions first—the small bequests, the dishes to Delia, the tools to Marcus, the books divided by subject—and the family nodded along in the warm, sad fog of routine grief. It was almost pleasant. Almost a Tuesday.

Then he cleared his throat, and something in the quality of the silence changed.

"The remainder of the estate—the house, the land, the contents, and all associated holdings—my client declined to divide among heirs." He adjusted his glasses. "Her instruction, given verbally and confirmed in writing, was as follows. Quote: *'Everything goes to whoever the house chooses.'*"

A beat.

Then Delia laughed—a wet, startled bark. "I'm sorry, what?"

"That is the operative language," Mr. Voss said, with dignity.

"The *house* chooses? Houses don't choose. Houses are load-bearing structures with outdated plumbing."

"Your grandmother was quite specific. She reviewed the phrasing three times. She wanted no ambiguity." He peered over his glasses. "She also instructed me to tell you, and I quote again: *'You'll understand soon, and I'm sorry about that.'*"

The laughter curdled in real time. Marcus heard it happen—the exact moment when Delia's laugh stopped being about the absurdity and started being about the fear underneath it, the way a joke stops being funny when you notice the teller isn't smiling.

"Mom was sharp," Marcus said carefully, "but she was ninety-one. Maybe the document—"

"Your grandmother was sharper at ninety-one than I have ever been at any age," Mr. Voss said mildly, "and she knew exactly what she was signing. I witnessed it myself." He gathered his pages. "That concludes the formal reading. There's a codicil I'm to deliver only if certain conditions arise, and I sincerely hope they don't, because the conditions involve terminology I'd have to look up."

"What terminology?" Priya asked.

"'Occupancy,'" said Mr. Voss, and stood, and shook hands all around, and left—left, Marcus realized afterward, faster than a seventy-year-old man should move, and without taking the coffee Delia offered him.

The family sat in the ringing aftermath.

"So," Delia said finally. "Split it four ways and sell. The house doesn't get a vote."

"Agreed," Marcus said. "Motion carried."

"Seconded," said Priya.

"Thirded," said the twins, who understood nothing and sensed everything.

Only Edmund hadn't spoken. He sat with his hands flat on the table, staring at the doorway to the parlor, and his face had the color of paper.

"Edmund?" Marcus said. "You okay with selling?"

His brother didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was quiet and even, the voice of a man reading off a clipboard inside his own skull.

"Do you hear that?"

Everyone listened. The house ticked and settled around them, the ordinary sounds of an old building in autumn wind. And beneath it—faint, rhythmic, patient—something knocked in the pipes. Once. Twice. Three times.

A pause, long enough to doubt it.

Four.

"Old plumbing," Marcus said. "Houses knock. It's thermal expansion, it's pressure, it's—"

"Nine," Edmund said softly.

"What?"

"There are nine of us in this house." Edmund was looking at the ceiling now, tracking the sound as it moved through the walls like something walking the halls on the far side of them. "You, Priya, the twins, Delia, Theo, me. Eight." He swallowed. "It just knocked nine times."

The pipes went silent, as if satisfied. As if the count was correct.

Under the table, Asha grabbed her brother's hand. In the parlor, unobserved by anyone, the eighth chair sat facing the doorway, angled—had it always been angled?—toward the dining room, waiting for its occupant to be called.

* * *

Marcus didn't sleep that night. None of them did, though they'd scattered to separate rooms with the determined cheerfulness of people pretending a sleepover was fun. Around two in the morning he gave up, went downstairs for water, and passed the parlor doorway.

He stopped. Backed up.

Moonlight lay across the floor in long pale panels. The seven chairs stood where they'd always stood. He counted them twice, three times, telling himself the number would resolve, that exhaustion was playing tricks, that rooms didn't gain furniture.

Eight chairs.

The new one was high-backed, wooden, carved with initials worn nearly smooth by generations of hands. It faced the doorway. It faced *him*.

From somewhere deep below the floor—below the cellar, below the foundation, below anything with a right to exist—a sound rose through the pipes. Not knocking this time. Slower. Rhythmic. Almost gentle.

Almost like a name.
