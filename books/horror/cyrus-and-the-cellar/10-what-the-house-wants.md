## 10. What the House Wants

**Edmund**

Edmund laid the photographs out on the dining room table like evidence at a trial, which, in a way, they were. His own trial, probably. He'd arranged them in chronological order—parlor, parlor, parlor—eight shots of the same room, and in every one of them the eighth chair stood at the end of the row, high-backed, patient, facing the camera like it had paid for the portrait session.

"You're going to want to sit down," he said. "Although, honestly, I'd understand if nobody wanted to sit down in this house ever again."

Marcus took the chair nearest the door. Priya stood behind him with one hand on each twin's shoulder, a posture Edmund recognized from airport departure gates. Delia hovered at the edge of the table with her arms crossed, and Theo—Theo dropped straight into the seat beside Edmund, close enough that their elbows touched, which was more physical contact than Edmund had received from any family member in a decade.

"Okay," Marcus said. "Show us the thing you drove three hours to show us. The thing that couldn't be a phone call."

Edmund slid the first photograph across. "Count the chairs."

Marcus counted. Edmund watched his brother's lips move, which would have been funny under almost any other circumstances. "Seven," Marcus said. "So?"

"Now look at the picture."

Marcus looked. His jaw did something complicated. "That's—no. That's a printing thing. A lens thing. Delia, tell him that's a lens thing."

Delia hadn't spoken. She was holding the second photograph, then the third, her thumb moving across each one like she was checking a scar for tenderness. "Edmund," she said quietly, "when did you take these?"

"Three nights ago. During the appraisal."

"And this chair—" She tapped the eighth chair in the third photo. "It's not in the room. I grew up in this room. There were seven chairs my entire childhood, and one of them wobbled and Grandma put a folded matchbook under the leg, and I *know* that chair, Edmund, and this is not it."

"This one's taller," Theo said.

Everyone looked at him.

"The extra chair," Theo said, with the flat certainty of a teenager stating the obvious. "It's taller. And the back has carving on it. Letters." He leaned over the photo. "Q-something. It's worn down."

"You have very good eyes," Edmund said, and did not mention that he'd needed to enlarge the image on his laptop to see the same thing, and that when he had, he'd had to go sit in his car for ten minutes with the radio on.

"Or," Marcus said, "he's fourteen and he's playing along with whatever this is." He turned to Edmund, and Edmund watched ten years of stored-up brother arrive all at once in his face. "Because here's what I know, Ed. You disappear for a decade. Not a call at Christmas, not a card, nothing. Then Grandma dies, and suddenly you're *back*, and within a week you've got the whole family gathered in her house to look at—what? A chair? A chair you could have bought at any thrift store in the county and photographed yourself?"

"Marcus," Priya said.

"No, I want to hear it. Because there's a version of this where our brother faked some photos to get everyone in one room, and honestly? That would be the *least* disturbing explanation available."

Edmund took a breath. He'd rehearsed this part on the drive over, the reasonable part, the part where he stayed calm and professional and didn't sound like a man who had recently watched a water meter run backward. "I considered that I was wrong," he said. "I considered it very seriously. So I went back. I retook the photos. Different lighting, different lens, phone camera instead of the good one. The chair was in every frame." He pulled the ledger from his bag and set it beside the photographs, and the room got quieter in a way rooms shouldn't be able to do on command. "And then I found this."

Delia reached for it first. Her fingers hovered over the cover without touching. "Grandma's handwriting," she said.

"Open it," Theo whispered.

She did. And for a long moment the only sound was pages turning, and the small involuntary noises Delia made—not gasps, exactly. More like the sounds of someone finding their own name in a stranger's diary. She stopped at the final entry and read it aloud, because some sentences are too heavy to carry alone.

"*Held off as long as I could. It wants the invisible one.*"

Priya's hands tightened on the twins' shoulders. "The invisible one," she repeated. "That's—"

"It's a phrase," Marcus said quickly. "Grandma said weird stuff. Grandma called the refrigerator 'the cold pantry' until the day she died. It doesn't mean—"

"It means me," Edmund said.

The word landed in the middle of the table like a dropped glass.

**Delia**

Delia looked at her brother—really looked, for the first time in ten years, which was its own small horror—and thought: *of course it's you.* Not because Edmund was forgettable. He had never been forgettable. He was the one who'd taught her to skip stones, the one who could name every bird at the feeder, the one who'd held her hand through the whole drive home after her appendix burst and told her jokes so bad she'd laughed and cried at the same time. He was unforgettable. He had simply made himself unfindable, and there was a difference, and apparently the house could tell the difference.

"You left," she said. It came out harder than she meant. "You left and you never told us why. Not a fight, not a letter, nothing. You just evaporated. And now you're telling us Grandma wrote that the house wanted the invisible one, and you're standing there looking like—like you *knew*. Like you've known for ten years."

"Delia," Edmund said.

"No. No, you don't get to do the quiet thing. You're very good at the quiet thing, Edmund, you've had a decade of practice. What did you know?"

The question hung there, and Delia watched her brother decide—she could actually see him deciding, like a man standing at the edge of cold water—and then the pipes in the wall knocked.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Everyone froze. The twins grabbed each other. Theo's head came up like a bird dog's.

"It does that," Edmund said, very calmly, which somehow made it worse. "It's been doing that. It's counting."

"Counting what?" Priya asked.

"Occupants."

The knocking paused. The pause went on so long that Marcus actually laughed—one sharp, disbelieving bark—and said, "See, this is a prank, this is a *prank*, there's a—"

Four.

**Theo**

Theo had known since the dream. He hadn't told anyone about the dream, because in the dream he'd been sitting in the parlor in the dark, and the tall chair at the end of the row had been occupied, and the person in it had been wearing his uncle's face but older, and instead of being scared he'd felt *sorry* for it, the way you feel sorry for a dog left in a car. The dream hadn't felt like a warning. It had felt like a message that had taken a wrong turn and ended up in his head by mistake.

Now the pipes knocked four times and his mother's face went gray, and Theo felt the strange calm of a person whose nightmare has finally introduced itself politely.

"It counted us," he said. "Four. Mom, Uncle Marcus, Aunt Priya, me. It's counting who's in the room."

"That's enough," Marcus said. "Theo, upstairs. Take the twins upstairs."

"No," Theo said.

"Theo—"

"It's not going to hurt us." He heard himself say it and was surprised by how sure he sounded. "It's *counting*. You don't count things you're going to break. You count things you're keeping track of. It's like—" he searched for it— "like attendance. It's taking attendance."

"Wonderful," Marcus said. "The house takes attendance. Great. Priya, back me up here."

But Priya wasn't looking at Marcus. She was looking at the ledger, and her expression was doing something Theo had only ever seen on his mother's face when a patient's chart didn't match the patient. "Marcus," she said slowly. "The dates in here. Every ten years or so. 1911. 1921. 1951. 1981." She looked up. "That's not a grocery list. That's a schedule."

"It's an old woman's notebook."

"With our family name in it," Priya said. "Over and over. Quintana, Quintana, Quintana. Silas Quintana, 1951. Ada Quintana, 1981." She turned the ledger around so everyone could see. "Marcus. Your family has been *in the book* for a hundred years."

The knocking came again. Five. Six. Seven. Delia's hand flew to her mouth—Theo's mom, who never cried in front of anyone, whose motto was *we don't do drama, we do solutions*—because seven was her, and everyone in the room knew it, and nobody said it, and the not-saying was louder than the knocking.

Eight. Priya.

Nine. Marcus.

And then the house was quiet, and Edmund said, in a voice like a man reading his own diagnosis, "Nine. There are nine of us on the property. It's got all of us."

"Except," Theo said.

Everyone looked at him.

"It knocked ten," he said. "Just now. After nine. I counted. It did one more."

The silence that followed had a texture. Theo watched the adults do the math and arrive at the same place he'd already been sitting for three days: there were nine people in the hall, and the house had counted ten, and the tenth one was the chair, or the thing in the chair, or the thing that *sat* in the chair, and it had been here the whole time, patient as furniture, waiting for the rest of the roll call to finish.

"Okay," Marcus said, and his voice cracked in the middle of the word, and Theo almost felt bad for him. Almost. "Okay. Everyone out. Now. Cars, driveway, we drive until the tank's empty and we figure this out at a Denny's like normal people."

They went. All of them, fast, the twins crying, Priya herding, Delia clutching the ledger against her chest like a shield. Edmund was last, and Theo hung back to walk with him, because someone had to, and because Theo had a question he couldn't ask in front of the adults.

"Uncle Edmund. In my dream. The guy in the chair." He made himself say it. "It was you."

Edmund stopped at the front door. He looked down at Theo for a long moment, and his face did something Theo had never seen an adult face do—it stopped pretending. Underneath all the calm was something exhausted and ancient and sad.

"I know," Edmund said. "I've seen him too."

Behind them, down in the dark of the cellar, something began to knock—not the pipes this time. The door. Slow, patient, almost polite.

Like a host, checking whether anyone was coming to dinner.
