## 18. Becoming Visible

Priya laid the letters out on the parlor floor like a hand of cards she was afraid to play.

There were eleven of them, yellowed at the folds, each addressed in grandma Rosa's careful cursive to a Madame Serafina Voss, Medium & Spiritual Consultant, of a P.O. box in a town that no longer appeared on maps. Priya had read them three times through the night while the rest of the house pretended to sleep, and by the third reading she had stopped being frightened and started being furious—furious at how practical the letters were, how much like a recipe, like instructions for assembling a bookshelf. *Speak the truth aloud where the walls can hear it. Forgive what is owed. Say each other's names as if you mean them, because the house will know if you don't.*

"It's not a spell," she said, when they had all gathered on the parlor rug in the gray morning light, bleary and unwashed and suspicious. "It's the opposite of a spell. Spells are about hiding things. This is about—" she searched for the word, "—disclosure."

"Disclosure," Marcus repeated flatly. He sat with his arms crossed, a man attending a seminar he did not believe in. "We're going to fix a haunted house with disclosure."

"We're going to try," Priya said.

"The knocking slowed last night," Theo offered from the window seat. He had his knees pulled up, his phone dark in his lap for once. "After Mom found the letters. I timed it. It used to knock every forty seconds or so. Last night it was more like every two minutes."

Nobody asked him how he'd been awake to time it. Nobody wanted to know what he'd been listening for.

Edmund stood apart from the group, near the fireplace, holding letter number seven as though it might bite him. He had read it four times. It was dated March 1998—the month before he left—and it ended mid-sentence: *I have told him nothing because a boy who knows he is chosen will either run or resign himself, and either way the house*—

The sentence just stopped. As if Rosa had been interrupted. As if she'd looked up from her desk and seen something standing in the doorway of her study, patient, waiting to be acknowledged.

"Edmund." Delia's voice, gentle. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm here," he said, which was not quite true, but he came anyway, folding himself down onto the rug between his brother and sister for the first time in ten years. The twins immediately rearranged themselves so that one sat on each side of him, an honor guard of six-year-olds. Marisol patted his knee twice, businesslike, the way you'd pat a horse you'd decided to trust.

"All right," Priya said. "The letters say we do this together, out loud, in the room with the most photographs. The house watches through them, apparently. Madame Voss was very insistent on that point."

"The parlor," Edmund said quietly. "The room with eight chairs."

"The room with eight chairs," Priya agreed.

Marcus snorted, but he didn't leave. That was something.

* * *

They began badly.

The first truth spoken aloud in the Quintana parlor was delivered by Marcus, who cleared his throat with the gravity of a man sworn in before Congress and said, "I resent that Dad left Mom before she got sick, and I have never said that out loud, and saying it makes me feel like a bad person, which maybe I am."

The pipes knocked once. Everyone flinched.

"That wasn't a knock," Theo said quickly. "That was—it sounded almost like approval? Like a nod?"

"It sounded like plumbing," Marcus insisted, but his ears had gone red, and Priya reached over and took his hand, and he let her.

Delia went next. She had prepared, because Delia always prepared; Edmund could see the folded paper in her hand and loved her desperately for it and wished she would put it away. She put it away. She closed her eyes instead.

"I forgave you years ago," she said to Edmund, eyes still shut, "for leaving. Or I told myself I did. But I didn't forgive myself for being relieved when you went, because when you left, Grandma's attention came off me, and I was nineteen and I wanted to be invisible too, just for a little while, and then Theo was born and I couldn't afford it anymore." She opened her eyes. "So I'm sorry. Not for what I did. For what I wished."

The knocking in the walls paused—a real pause this time, a held breath—and then resumed slower than any of them had ever heard it. Almost gentle. Almost like a lullaby tapped out by someone who only knew percussion.

Marisol, who had been waiting with the patience of a landmine, raised her hand.

"You don't need to raise your hand, sweetheart," Priya said.

"I do if it's important." She stood up, all four feet of her, in the middle of the parlor, facing the wall with the photographs. "My truth is that I ate the last popsicle on Tuesday and I blamed it on Joaquín and he got in trouble and I didn't say anything even when he cried." Her chin wobbled. "That's my truth."

Joaquín looked at his sister with the expression of a man who has waited his whole life for this moment. "My truth," he announced, "is that I'm glad she told, AND my truth is that I broke the blue cup and hid it in the yard and told everyone the raccoon did it."

"There is no raccoon," Marcus said faintly.

"There is NOW," said Joaquín, with terrible confidence.

And the house—there was no other way to describe it—the house seemed to chuckle. A soft double-knock in the parlor wall, quick and light, like laughter rendered in wood. The twins dissolved into giggles. Even Marcus's mouth twitched against his will.

Theo spoke last of the children. He didn't stand. He stayed curled in the window seat, looking at the eighth chair across the room, and he said, "I see it. The chair. I've seen it since before we got here—in dreams, in drawings. And my truth is that I'm not scared of it. I'm scared that everyone thinks I should be, and that being the kid who isn't scared means I'm the weird one again." He shrugged. "But somebody in this family has to look directly at things. Apparently it's me."

The chair did not move. But the light through the curtains shifted, briefly, warmly, the way light does when a cloud lets go of the sun, and Theo nodded slowly, as if a question had been answered.

Then it was Edmund's turn, and the room went quiet around him the way water goes quiet around a stone.

* * *

"I don't know how to do this," Edmund admitted. His hands were shaking. He put them flat on his knees. "Ten years of practice not saying things. It turns out the skill doesn't reverse."

"Start small," Delia said.

"My name is Edmund Aurelio Quintana." The middle name landed in the room like a dropped plate; nobody had heard it spoken since the funeral of the uncle it belonged to. "I was named after a man who drowned in 1951, and Grandma never explained why she gave me his name, and I never asked, because asking questions in that woman's house felt like reaching into a wall socket." He exhaled. "Small truths first. Fine. Here's another: I rehearse conversations. Before every phone call, every visit, every—everything. I write scripts in my head so I can't be caught off guard, because being caught off guard means being visible, and being visible—" He stopped. Started again. "Being visible used to feel like standing in a doorway during a lightning storm."

Priya made a small sound, half sympathy, half recognition, and Edmund realized with a jolt that she understood—not the house part, but the hiding part. Maybe everyone in this family understood the hiding part. Maybe that was the whole cursed inheritance, passed down like the good silverware.

"I watched you all from a distance," he went on, and now the words were coming faster, loosening, ten years of sediment breaking up. "Birthday photos on Marcus's feed. Delia's work anniversary posts. Theo's science fair—I drove past the school that day. I sat in a parking lot for forty minutes trying to make myself go in, and then I bought a coffee I didn't want and drove home and told myself next year." His voice cracked. "Next year. Every year. Next year."

"Edmund." Marcus's voice was rough. "Why?"

And there it was—the question Edmund had spent a decade avoiding, arriving at last in the warmest possible room, surrounded by people who were, at that moment, doing the bravest thing any of them had ever done, which was simply telling the truth with the walls listening.

"Not yet," Edmund said. "I promise I'll answer it. Fully. Soon. But not yet, because if I say it now I'll say it wrong, and the letters say the house knows when you mean things, and I need—" his breath hitched, "—I need to mean it right."

Marcus stared at him for a long moment. Then, to everyone's surprise including his own, he nodded. "Okay. But I'm collecting. You owe me one truth, plus interest."

"Plus interest," Edmund agreed.

The knocking had slowed to almost nothing. In the silence, the house itself seemed to lean in—warm air moving through the rooms without a source, the smell of bread baking in a kitchen where no one was baking, dust motes rising in the window light like slow applause.

Delia laughed suddenly, wetly, wiping her eyes. "Is it me, or does the house like us better when we're honest?"

"It likes us better when we're *here*," Theo said quietly. "Really here. I think that's the whole thing. I think that's all it ever wanted."

For one hour—one golden, impossible hour—the Quintana house was almost kind.

They kept going. Priya confessed she'd been terrified of becoming a mother until the day the twins were born, and that she still sometimes stood outside their door at night just to hear them breathe, and that she'd never told anyone because strong women weren't supposed to admit fear. Marcus admitted he'd taken over the family responsibilities after Edmund left partly out of love and partly out of spite, and that the spite had curdled into a suit of armor he no longer knew how to take off. Delia told the story of the day Theo was born and how she'd whispered their grandmother's name in the delivery room like a prayer and a curse at once. The twins took turns confessing increasingly minor crimes with increasing solemnity, until the parlor smelled of bread and salt tears and lemonade someone had fetched from the kitchen, and the pipes knocked only occasionally, softly, like a heartbeat keeping easy company.

Edmund laughed. Actually laughed—at Joaquín's deadpan account of the raccoon conspiracy—and the sound startled him so much he pressed his hand to his own chest, checking whether it belonged to him.

He had forgotten what this was. Not the haunting. The *this*. The noise and warmth of a family in a room, all of them unguarded at once, none of them performing. Ten years he'd imagined these gatherings from the outside, and his imagination had gotten it wrong in every particular: he'd pictured elegance and tension, and the reality was lemonade spills and a six-year-old's sock with a hole in it and his sister crying and laughing in the same breath.

*This,* he thought. *This is what I traded.*

The clock on the mantel—he had never heard it tick before, though it must always have been ticking—ticked on, companionable, and the afternoon light lay across the parlor floor like something poured, and for sixty minutes the Quintana house was just a house, old and odd and full.

Then the well surged.

* * *

There was no mistaking it for plumbing. The sound came up through the foundations and out through every pipe at once—a deep, wet, muscular rush, like something enormous turning over in its sleep, followed by a gout of water hammering upward through the walls. The lemonade shivered in its glasses. The photographs rattled on their hooks. The warmth drained out of the room so fast it felt stolen.

Everyone froze.

"What was that?" Priya whispered.

"The well," Edmund said. He was already on his feet, already moving toward the hall, and the family moved with him, a single reluctant organism. At the front window, Theo pressed his face to the glass.

"It's louder," he reported. "You can hear it from here. Like a river under the lawn."

Edmund opened the front door. The afternoon had gone strange—the light too thin, the air carrying that loam-and-sweetness smell from the cellar, stronger now, sweeter, like fruit going over. From behind the house came the sound of water moving with intent, filling, climbing, impatient.

The pipes in the hallway knocked. Once. Twice. Three times.

Then, distinctly, in a rhythm they had all learned to dread, they spelled out a pattern Theo translated aloud, his voice very small:

"It says... 'soon.'"

Nobody spoke. Behind them, in the parlor, the mantel clock ticked on, indifferent. The warmth did not come back.

Marcus put his hand on Edmund's shoulder—roughly, but it stayed there. "One hour," he said. "We get one hour of being a family, and then it starts banging on the pipes like a landlord."

"It's not angry," Theo said. He was still looking out at the backyard, at the place beyond the fence line where the capped well waited. His breath fogged the glass. "That's the worst part. It's not angry. It's just..." He hunted for the word.

"Hungry," Edmund said.

"No." Theo turned around, and his eyes were older than fifteen, older than the house. "*Eager.*"

In the sudden silence that followed, every pipe in the Quintana house knocked at once—a single synchronized beat, like a drumroll beginning—and beneath the floorboards, faint but unmistakable, the water rose another inch.
