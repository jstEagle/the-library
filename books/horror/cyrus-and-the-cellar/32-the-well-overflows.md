## 32. The Well Overflows

Edmund had set his phone alarm for 5:40 a.m., which was the time he'd woken up every working morning for ten years without an alarm at all. The alarm was a formality now, the way the tie he'd laid out on the dresser was a formality, and the way the thermos of coffee Delia had pressed into his hands last night—"You'll want something warm where you're going"—was a formality. The house had stopped pretending to be subtle around midnight, when the knocking in the pipes had shifted from his name to something shorter, more insistent, a rhythm like a finger tapping a watch face.

He lay in his childhood bed—his childhood bed, which still smelled faintly of cedar and pencil shavings—and listened to it.

*Knock-knock-knock.* Pause. *Knock-knock-knock.*

COME.

It wasn't menacing. That was the strange part, the part he kept turning over like a stone looking for something ugly underneath. It sounded almost polite. Like a taxi idling outside. Like a colleague leaning into your office doorway: *Ready when you are.*

"Five more minutes," Edmund said to the ceiling, because if you couldn't be wry at the end of the world, when could you be?

The pipes knocked twice. Almost amused.

He got up.

* * *

The window in his old room faced east, toward the back yard, and what he saw there stopped him with one leg in his trousers.

The well had overflowed.

Not dramatically—not a geyser, not a flood. The water had simply risen past the stone lip sometime in the night and continued rising, spilling outward in a slow, glassy sheet that spread across the yard like spilled milk finding its level. It covered the grass, the flagstones, the rusted swing set frame, all of it, a skin of water perhaps half an inch deep stretching to the fence line and beyond, catching the first gray light and holding it.

And the grass was dry.

Edmund watched for a full minute. Dew sat on the blades of grass *above* the water. A spider's web strung between the swing set poles held its beads of moisture undisturbed, though the web's lower strands passed through the flood as if the water weren't there at all. Where the sheet of water met the dry ground beyond the fence, it simply stopped—a clean edge, like paint poured to a line.

"It's not real water," he said quietly. "Or it's realer than real. I don't know which is worse."

His breath fogged the glass. Below, the water caught his reflection and held it, and for a moment he thought the reflection looked older than he did. He decided not to think about that. There would be time—or rather, there wouldn't, which was the point.

* * *

By the time he came downstairs, dressed, the kitchen already smelled of coffee and, improbably, pancakes.

Priya stood at the stove in her robe, flipping batter with the serene competence of a woman who had decided that if her family's last morning together was going to happen, it was going to happen fed. Marcus sat at the table staring into a mug. The twins were asleep on the parlor floor still, curled together under the quilt grandma had made, and Theo sat at the counter with his sketchbook open, drawing the window, drawing the yard, drawing the water that didn't wet anything.

"You're up early," Priya said, not turning around. Her voice was carefully light. "I'd have thought today of all days you'd sleep in."

"I've never slept in," Edmund said. "It's genetic. Grandma used to say the Quintanas treat mornings like appointments."

"She did." Priya slid a pancake onto a plate and set it in front of him without being asked. "Eat. You'll need it."

"Priya, I don't think digestion is going to be my problem today."

"Eat anyway." She turned then, and her eyes were red-rimmed but steady. "Humor me. Humor us. We spent all night deciding how to do this, and we decided: no drama. No speeches. You go down there like it's a Tuesday. So it's a Tuesday. Have breakfast on your Tuesday."

Edmund looked at the pancake. It was slightly burnt on one edge, the way every pancake he'd ever eaten at this table had been slightly burnt on one edge, because the third burner ran hot and nobody in the family's history had ever adjusted for it. He picked up his fork.

"Tuesdays," he agreed. "Worst Tuesdays on record."

Marcus snorted into his coffee. It was a small sound, half-sob, but it counted.

* * *

They gathered in the back yard at seven, all eight of them—the six living Quintanas and attachments, plus Mrs. Okonkwo, who arrived at the gate at ten to seven exactly, carrying a folding chair and a thermos, because she had announced yesterday that she would attend and Mrs. Okonkwo attended things the way weather happened.

She could not cross the property line. They had tested it gently, offering her arm, and she had smiled and declined, and none of them had pushed it. She set her folding chair just beyond the fence, on the neighbor's side, facing the well like a spectator at a parade.

"Witnessed contracts," she reminded them, settling in. "Everything done in the open. Everything seen. Go on, now. I'll be here."

The water had spread overnight until the entire yard was that impossible shallow mirror, and the morning sun had come up enough to turn it gold. The twins walked out into it barefoot, shrieking—not from cold or fear but delight, because their feet came down dry, every step, dry footprints blooming behind them on the surface of the water like the yard was showing off.

"It likes them," Theo said quietly, beside Edmund. He had his sketchbook under his arm. "Or it's being polite. For the occasion."

"For the occasion," Edmund agreed. His voice came out steadier than he expected. "That's the thing I finally understand about this place, Theo. It's not cruel. Cruelty would almost be simpler. It's *lonely*. It's been lonely since before any of us, and it doesn't know any other way to ask."

Theo looked up at him. The boy had grandma's eyes, Edmund realized—not the color, the *way* they saw. "And you're going to keep it company."

"I'm going to keep it company," Edmund said. "Somebody has to. Apparently I've been training for the job my whole life."

* * *

The cellar door stood open.

It had been sealed since Edmund was nine—painted shut, padlocked, then nailed, in escalating generations of parental anxiety—and last night, while the family slept in the parlor around the eighth chair, it had simply swung wide, the old hinges silent, the darkness beyond it warm and breathing. Not the cold breath of a grave. The warm breath of a house in summer, of loam and cut grass and, underneath, something sweeter, like apples going soft in a bowl.

Edmund stood at the top of the stairs with his tool bag over his shoulder.

He had packed it the night before, sitting cross-legged on his childhood bed like a boy packing for his first day of school. Tape measure. Level. Pencil. The little brass plumb bob grandma had given him when he got his surveyor's license, the one she'd held up and said, *"So you can always find true down, Eddie. Everything else is negotiable."* He'd hesitated over the plumb bob longer than anything else. Then he'd packed it. If he was going to spend eternity taking measurements, he was going to take them properly.

"You look like you're going to work," Delia said, coming to stand beside him. She had insisted on doing his collar, and her fingers were trembling so badly she'd had to start three times, and neither of them mentioned it. "That's the most depressing thing I've ever seen. My brother, dressed for a shift."

"I am going to work," Edmund said. "Long-term placement. Terrible commute. The benefits package is a haunted house."

Delia laughed, and then she cried, briefly and efficiently, the way she did everything, and then she hugged him hard enough to creak ribs. "Ten years," she said into his shoulder. "You idiot. Ten years, and I get you back for one week."

"You got me back for one week," Edmund said, "and it turns out that was the whole job. I'm sorry it took the end of the world to clock in."

"Theo drew you," Delia said, pulling back, wiping her eyes with her wrist. "Before any of this. Before the reading. 'The extra grandpa,' remember? He says you were always in the pictures. You just hadn't arrived yet." She touched his cheek. "You're visible now, Eddie. Whatever happens down there. You're the most seen man in this family's history. Don't you dare forget it."

"I won't," Edmund said. And found, to his surprise, that it was true.

* * *

Marcus came last.

His brother had aged five years in five days, gray coming in at the temples like frost, but he stood straight, and he put out his hand, and when Edmund took it Marcus pulled him into a hug instead, rough and brief, the kind men give each other when they've run out of decades.

"I pumped that well until my hands bled," Marcus said. "I traded my wedding day for nothing. And you're still the one going down there, and I hate it, and I'm grateful, and I don't know how to hold both of those at once."

"You don't have to," Edmund said. "That's the whole point of family. Somebody holds it for you."

Marcus laughed wetly. "Grandma would've loved that line."

"Grandma wrote it. Basically. In ledger form."

They stood together at the top of the stairs. Below, the lamplight moved in the dark where there was no electricity—had been no electricity for forty years—swaying gently, patient as a porch light left on for someone expected home.

The pipes knocked. Once each, soft, from everywhere at once. Not COME anymore. Just attention. Just the house, clearing its throat.

"Any last words?" Marcus asked. "For the record. Mrs. Okonkwo's witnessing."

Edmund considered. Behind him he could hear Priya telling the twins to stand still, and Theo's pencil moving fast across paper, and Delia blowing her nose with dignity, and Mrs. Okonkwo calling out, "Beautiful morning for it!" in the tone people use for funerals and graduations alike.

"Yeah," Edmund said. "Tell them I'm home."

He meant it as a joke. It came out as a prayer.

Then he shouldered his tool bag, found true down with his feet the way grandma taught him, and started toward the stairs—and the lamplight below rose to meet him, and the water in the yard went utterly, perfectly still, every reflection in it sharpening at once, as if the whole property had leaned in to watch him descend.
