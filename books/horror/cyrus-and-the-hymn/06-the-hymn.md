## 6. The Hymn

The call came in at 3:47 a.m., which Sebastian would later understand was not a time but an appointment.

"The Hour of the Wolf" had been running on fumes for the last hour—Sebastian had played two songs from the deep cuts bin and read out a text from a night-shift nurse in Anacostia who said the hospital's vending machine had started dispensing only grape soda, every slot, as if the machine had made a decision. Sebastian loved calls like that. The city at four in the morning was a soft animal with its belly up, full of small absurdities, and Sebastian's whole gift—the one that had paid for the mortgage on a house they'd never wanted—was making each of those strangers feel like the only person awake.

"Line three, you're live with the Wolf."

There was a pause on the line. Not dead air—Sebastian knew dead air the way a doctor knows a flatline. This was *held* air, air someone was keeping in their lungs on purpose.

"Hello?" The voice was low, genderless in the way voices are at the bottom of a bad connection. "Can you hear me?"

"Loud and clear, caller. You've got the Wolf until sunrise. What's on your mind?"

"I have something for you." A breath. "Not a request. A delivery."

Sebastian grinned at the ON AIR light. "We don't take deliveries after midnight, friend. Station policy."

"It's four notes." The voice was very calm now, calm the way water is calm right before it goes over the edge of something. "I need you to pass them along."

"Pass them along to who?"

"Whoever's next."

And then the caller hummed.

Four notes. Sebastian would spend the rest of what remained of their life trying to describe them and never quite managing it, because they were not a melody so much as a *shape*—the first note low and round like a stone dropped into deep water, the second climbing a half-step with a kind of reluctance, the third hanging suspended longer than any human lung should allow, and the fourth—

The fourth note didn't end. It just stopped being audible, the way a star stops being visible when you look directly at it.

The hum lasted perhaps six seconds. The board showed the call duration ticking past forty seconds, which meant the caller had spent most of it breathing, or pretending to.

Then silence, and the dial tone, and the line going dark on the console without Sebastian ever having screened it, because there had been nothing to screen—no caller ID, no hold light, no record of the line ever opening.

Sebastian sat back in the chair. The studio hummed around them: the transmitter's whine through the wall, the refrigerator in the break room, the old clock radio that ran four minutes fast because Sebastian found it funny to be slightly ahead of schedule forever.

"That was—" Sebastian said to the microphone, and heard their own voice come out strange, flattened, careful. They cleared their throat and tried again, reaching for the warmth, the velvet, the thing they did. "That was line three, folks, with what I can only assume is the world's least effective prank call. Four notes? Buddy, I've had callers recite their grocery lists with more dramatic structure."

The joke landed somewhere in the empty studio and died there. The red ON AIR light regarded Sebastian steadily.

They played a song—something loud, something with drums—and while it played they pulled up the call log. Line 3. Duration: 00:00:41. Origin: no data. Recording: yes, always, FCC rules.

Sebastian queued it up and listened on headphones.

Their own voice: *"Line three, you're live with the Wolf."*

A pause.

*"It's four notes. I need you to pass them along."*

And then the hum—and here Sebastian pulled off the headphones so fast the cable cracked against the console, because underneath the four notes, woven into the spaces between them, there was a second sound. A choir of them. Dozens of voices, maybe hundreds, humming the same four notes in unison so perfectly aligned that they collapsed into what sounded like one throat—one enormous, patient throat.

The song ended. Sebastian put the headphones down on the desk like they were handling something venomous.

"You're tired," they told themselves out loud, because saying things out loud had always been how Sebastian sorted true from false. "You've been sleeping in a dead woman's house for two weeks. You're hearing choirs in phone static. This is a you problem, not a them problem."

But even as they said it, they noticed that the four notes were still there.

Not playing. *Present.* Sitting behind their sternum the way a swallowed coin sits, a small dense weight that hadn't been there before 3:47 a.m. When Sebastian breathed in, the inhale seemed to bend around it. When they spoke, they could feel their vocal cords arranging themselves around it, accommodating it, making room.

*Pass it along,* the caller had said. *To whoever's next.*

"To whoever's next," Sebastian repeated, and hated the way their mouth shaped the words like they were reading them off a card.

* * *

The drive home took nineteen minutes at that hour, down Georgia Avenue with the streetlights strobing across the windshield in their slow amber metronome. Sebastian did what they always did on the drive: talked to themselves, ran bits, tested phrasings for tomorrow night's show. It was a habit older than the show itself—Sebastian had narrated their own life since childhood, a running commentary, a soundtrack, the constant proof that a voice was still coming out of them.

Tonight the commentary kept snagging.

Every few sentences, unbidden, the four notes would surface—not in Sebastian's ears but somewhere deeper, in the meat of the throat, in the little chamber behind the nose where you feel a song you're about to sing. And each time it surfaced, Sebastian's mouth would start to shape the first note before they caught it and bit down.

By the time they pulled into the driveway of Adaeze Okafor's house, they had hummed the first note aloud twice without deciding to.

The house stood black against the graying sky. Two weeks in, Sebastian still hadn't unpacked more than a suitcase's worth; the lawyer had been merciless about the timeline, and the will had been merciless about everything else—*the house passes to my great-grandchild, who will know what it is for.* Sebastian did not know what it was for. Sebastian knew it had eleven rooms, a roof that needed work, and a hallway mirror that blinked late, and they had filed all of that under *grief, exhaustion, old wiring* and moved on, because moving on was the family specialty.

The cat was waiting on the porch.

This was wrong. Bismuth—a gray tabby with the emotional range of a tax form—did not wait on porches. Bismuth waited by the food bowl, at distances calibrated to communicate maximum indifference. But there he sat on the top step, tail wrapped tight around his feet, staring at the front door with his ears pinned flat.

"Hey, buddy." Sebastian climbed the steps, keys out. "Did you lock yourself out again?"

Bismuth looked at Sebastian. Then at the door. Then back at Sebastian, and made a sound Sebastian had never heard him make—not a meow, not a yowl, but a low, vibrating complaint, almost subsonic, like a motor failing to turn over.

Sebastian opened the door. Bismuth flowed between their legs—not into the house, but *past* it, down the steps, off the porch, and under the boxwood hedge at the yard's edge, where he stopped and turned around and stared back with the flat yellow patience of a creature that has decided something and will not be argued with.

"Bis."

Nothing. The hedge trembled once in the windless morning and went still.

Sebastian stood in the doorway holding a bag of groceries and a bag of cat food and felt, for the first time since Marisol's sister had called the station, something that wasn't confusion. It was colder and older than confusion. It was the feeling of standing in a river and realizing the current has changed direction.

Cats knew things. Everyone said it, nobody believed it, and yet every culture on earth had some version of the animal that refuses to cross the threshold. Sebastian stood there and let the draft from inside the house move against their face—it was flowing *out*, warm and faintly sweet, like the exhalation of something sleeping—and thought: *if I were a cat, would I come in?*

The four notes stirred behind their sternum, gentle as a hand on the shoulder.

"No," Sebastian told them, out loud, to the empty hall. "Absolutely not. We are not doing this."

The notes settled, patient. They could afford to be patient. That was the thing Sebastian couldn't stop noticing, the thing that made the skin of their arms prickle even as they stepped inside and shut the door: the hum wasn't insistent. It didn't push. It simply *waited*, the way a held chord waits for resolution, and it had all the confidence in the world that resolution would come, because resolution always comes. That was what music was.

Sebastian put the groceries away. They did it slowly, deliberately, naming each item as they shelved it—*oat milk, eggs, the good bread*—because naming things out loud was a rope hand over hand, and letting go meant sliding down into whatever was waiting at the bottom of the quiet.

From under the boxwood hedge, faintly, Bismuth watched the lit kitchen window.

* * *

Sebastian didn't sleep. They tried—they showered, they took the melatonin that mostly worked, they lay down in the bedroom that still smelled like fresh paint over old smoke—but every time they drifted toward the edge of sleep, the four notes rose to meet them, and each time they surfaced with their lips already parted around the first note, already halfway to singing it.

At some point they gave up and sat on the edge of the bed with the lamp on and opened the voice memo app on their phone.

"This is a test," they said. "It is—" they checked the clock— "5:52 a.m., and I am going to describe what's happening to me in plain language, because plain language is how you keep from losing your mind. Item one: a caller with no origin hummed four notes and asked me to pass them along. Item two: I cannot stop hearing the four notes. Item three: my cat is living in a bush. Item four—"

Item four was the mirror.

Sebastian had hung a towel over the hallway mirror two days ago, after the blinking thing, after they'd stood in front of it at two in the morning waving one arm and watching the reflection complete the motion a beat behind, smooth and unhurried, like a swimmer a stroke back. They'd told themselves: old glass, warped silvering, parallax. They'd hung the towel and felt stupid and gone to bed.

Now, sitting on the edge of the bed, they realized they could hear the four notes *coming from the direction of the hallway.*

Not in their head. In the hall. Faint, muffled by the towel, but directional, external, real—or real enough that Sebastian's body reacted to it as sound, turning their ear toward the door.

They got up. They told themselves seventeen different reasonable explanations on the twelve-foot walk to the hallway, and none of them survived contact with the actual moment of pulling the towel off the glass.

The mirror showed the hallway. The hallway lamp, the framed photo of Adaeze mid-performance, mouth open, eyes closed, radiant. Sebastian, standing in pajamas with a towel in their hands, looking wrecked.

For one full second, everything was normal.

Then the reflection's mouth moved.

Sebastian's mouth did not move. Their jaw was locked, their breath held, their whole body a held note. But in the glass, their reflection's lips parted and shaped four syllables with unhurried precision, and though no sound crossed over, Sebastian knew—knew in the marrow, in the place where the hum lived—exactly what it was saying.

The four notes. Mouthed. One, two, three, four. Then the reflection smiled, closed its mouth, and became an ordinary mirror again, showing an ordinary exhausted person holding a towel.

Sebastian hung the towel back up. Their hands were steady, which frightened them more than shaking would have. Somewhere below the fear, below even the dread, a small treacherous part of them had *recognized* the four syllables the reflection mouthed, had felt its own throat ache to answer, and that part was not afraid at all. That part was homesick.

"Okay," Sebastian whispered to the towel-covered mirror. "Okay. New plan."

* * *

The new plan lasted until noon and consisted of coffee, a call to the station manager to take the night off—"family stuff, yeah, the house stuff"—and a long sit on the back steps with Bismuth, who permitted himself to be picked up out of the hedge and held, rigid as a plank, for exactly four minutes before demanding release.

Sebastian talked to him. This was not new; Sebastian talked to everyone, everything, the microphone, the dashboard, the dark. What was new was what they talked about.

"Somebody gave me a song, Bis," they said. The cat groomed his shoulder with surgical disinterest. "And I don't think it was a gift. I think it was a seed. I think somebody planted something in me at 3:47 this morning and now it's growing, and I don't know what it grows into, and I can't cut it out because it's not *in* a place, it's in—in the part of me that sings. Which is basically the part of me that's *me*, you know?"

Bismuth paused mid-groom. Looked at the house. Looked at Sebastian. Resumed grooming, with the air of a creature confirming a suspicion.

"And here's the really fun part." Sebastian laughed, and the laugh came out with the first note riding on top of it, a faint harmonic they hadn't intended, and they clamped their mouth shut so hard their teeth clicked. "Here's the really fun part. Sometimes when it plays—which is constantly, by the way, it's like a song stuck in your head except your head isn't where it's stuck—I want to pass it along. That's the instruction, right? Pass it along. And I catch myself thinking about who. Nkiru. My producer. The nurse who texted about the vending machine. Random people. I'm standing in my kitchen composing a mental list of people to *give it to*, Bis, like it's a chain letter, like it's a blessing, and some part of me thinks it *is* a blessing. Some part of me wants everyone I love to hear it."

The yard was very quiet. Even the traffic on Georgia Avenue seemed to have agreed to keep its distance.

"So that's the trap, isn't it," Sebastian said quietly. "It doesn't make you do anything. It makes you *want*. It makes wanting feel like generosity."

Bismuth stood, stretched, and walked to the far corner of the yard, where he sat down facing outward, on watch, done with the conversation.

Sebastian stayed on the steps until the light went long and gold, and the four notes played on behind their ribs, patient, patient, patient, and around six o'clock—when any reasonable person would be thinking about dinner—Sebastian's phone buzzed in their pocket with a voicemail notification, and they saw with a lurch that they had, at some point in the last hour without noticing, dialed a number.

Nkiru's number.

The call log showed an outgoing call, 5:41 p.m., duration zero seconds. No memory of dialing it. And beneath the notification, the four notes hummed their approval, soft as a purr, and Sebastian understood with total clarity that the list they'd been composing in the kitchen had not been their idea—that the hum had simply been waiting for them to say the names aloud, and that tonight, whether they chose it or not, they were going to call the one person whose voice they knew better than their own, and either warn her, or deliver.

They pressed play on the voicemail they didn't remember leaving.

Their own voice, thick and strange, at 4:07 that morning: *"Nkiru. It's me. I know—I know it's late, I know we don't—just listen. There's a song. Someone gave me a song and I can't get it out and I think it's from her, I think it's from the house, I think—"* a pause, and underneath the pause, barely audible, bleeding through from somewhere in the room behind Sebastian's sleeping body, four notes hummed in a chorus too large for any room— *"—please pick up. Please, Nkiru. Tell me you recognize it. Tell me what she was singing."*

The message ended. The yard darkened. Behind Sebastian, in the covered mirror in the hall, something smiled.

And thirty miles away, in a city archive that smelled of dust and cold iron, Nkiru Adichie was listening to that voicemail for the third time, headphones on, going pale, because she did recognize it.

She'd seen it written down once, in a folder marked ADAEZE OKAFOR — RESTRICTED, on a page torn from a journal, above a date: 1923.
