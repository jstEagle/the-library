## 8. Adaeze's Bargain

The city archives smelled the way all old buildings smell when they think no one is listening: dust and paper and something faintly sweet underneath, like flowers left too long in a closed room.

Nkiru Adichie had been coming here since graduate school. She knew the building the way other people knew their own kitchens—the elevator that stuck between floors three and four, the reading room with its green-shaded lamps and its rules about pencils only, the basement stacks where the truly strange material lived, uncatalogued, unloved, waiting for someone with a grant deadline and a stubborn streak. She had written her dissertation on gospel music of the Great Migration. She had spent four years of her life inside Adaeze Okafor's recordings.

She had not spent four years of her life inside Adaeze Okafor's family.

That distinction had a name, and the name was Sebastian Okafor, and Nkiru was not thinking about him. She was thinking about him constantly, which was different, she told herself, professionally different, the way a doctor thinks about a symptom. The voicemail had come in at 4:07 that morning—she'd checked the timestamp twice, needing the precision of it, the clean arithmetic of a time when normal people were asleep—and Sebastian's voice on it had been wrong in a way she couldn't file under any category she owned.

*Nkiru. It's me. I know—I know I don't get to call you at four in the morning anymore, but something is happening in this house and I need you to tell me if I'm crazy, because you're the only person who knows enough about my family to—*

And then, underneath the words, so far down she might have imagined it: singing. Four notes, hummed low, looping. Not a melody she recognized, exactly. More like a melody that recognized *her*—that turned slightly toward her ear as she listened, attentive, patient.

She had played the voicemail eleven times. On the eleventh play she had caught herself humming along, and had stopped so abruptly she bit her tongue.

So now it was 9 a.m., and the archives had just opened, and she was standing at the reference desk with her researcher's card and a story she wasn't sure how to tell without sounding like the kind of person who gets escorted out of archives.

"I'm looking for probate records," she said. "Estate files. Anything connected to the Okafor family—Adaeze Okafor, the singer. Especially anything unusual. Deeds, contracts, correspondence."

The archivist, a woman named Mrs. Ferreira with reading glasses on a beaded chain and the unhurried eyes of someone who had heard every kind of research question, tapped a pencil against her teeth.

"Okafor," she said. "The gospel singer? We get requests about her maybe once every few years. Students, mostly." She paused. "You're not a student."

"No."

"Most of them ask about the voice." Mrs. Ferreira said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "There are stories. You know how there are stories."

"I study those stories," Nkiru said. "Professionally."

Something in Mrs. Ferreira's face shifted—a door opening a crack. She lowered her voice. "There's a box. Estate miscellaneous, 1923 through 1971. It came in with a property transfer decades ago and nobody has ever requested it. It's not in the finding aid. Frankly, half of what's in it made the last cataloguer uncomfortable, and he was a man who catalogued dental records for fun." She wrote a number on a slip. "Basement B, aisle fourteen. Pencils only. And—" she hesitated, then seemed to decide against whatever caution she'd been about to offer, and simply handed over the slip instead.

* * *

The box was smaller than she expected. Acid-free these days, but originally a hatbox or something like it, cardboard gone soft at the corners, tied with string that had been retied by an archivist's careful hands sometime in the seventies. The label read ESTATE MISC. OKAFOR, A. in faded ink, and beneath it, in a different hand, smaller, almost apologetic: *do not separate.*

Nkiru carried it to one of the basement's two reading tables, under a bulb that buzzed at a frequency just below headache. She untied the string. The knot came apart too easily, as if it had been waiting.

On top: a funeral program from 1971, Adaeze Okafor's own, with a photograph Nkiru knew from album covers—the proud chin, the headwrap like a crown, the eyes that looked slightly past the camera as though listening to something behind the photographer. Beneath the program, a bundle of letters tied in ribbon. Beneath the letters, a leather ledger, its spine cracked white. And beneath the ledger, wrapped in yellowed tissue paper like something being hidden rather than preserved—

A contract.

Nkiru knew documents. She could date paper by its tooth, ink by its bloom, and everything about this document said 1923 except for the feeling of it, which said nothing at all, which was itself wrong. Paper had a presence. This had an absence, like pressing your hand against a window and finding the glass warmer than the room.

She put on cotton gloves anyway. Ritual mattered. Ritual was how you told the archive, and yourself, that you were still in control.

The header read:

*Agreement made this 14th day of March, 1923, between ADEZE OKAFOR of [address], hereinafter the SIGNATORY, and the party known as THE CHOIRMASTER, hereinafter the SECOND PARTY.*

No address for the second party. No name beyond the title. Where a legal description should have been—*of the parish of*, *residing at*—there was only a line, and on the line, in ink darker than the rest, as though written later or by a heavier hand:

*whose residence is wherever voices are kept.*

"Okay," Nkiru said quietly, to the bulb, to herself. "Okay."

She read it three times. The first time her eyes skated off certain clauses the way water skates off wax; she had to go back with a finger under each line, forcing herself word by word, and even then some sentences seemed to rearrange slightly between readings, the way a face in a crowd seems to turn toward you and then hasn't.

The terms, as best she could hold them steady:

The Signatory, Adaeze Okafor, did hereby convey unto the Second Party *the shadow of her voice*—and the phrase was not metaphor, the contract insisted on that, italicized itself, underlined twice in a hand that shook: *the shadow entire, being that part of the voice which remembers and repeats, which answers when called, which persists after the breath that made it.*

In exchange, the Second Party granted the Signatory *a voice without shadow: a voice that shall be believed.* Not beautiful—that word appeared and was struck out, replaced in the margin in Adaeze's handwriting with *better than beautiful*. Believed. A voice that could make anyone believe anything it said, or sang, or whispered into a darkened hall.

There was a payment schedule. That was the part that turned Nkiru's hands cold against the table. Contracts had terms; she'd expected terms. But this clause was written like a hymnal, verse and refrain:

*Payment shall fall due upon the death of the Signatory, and thereafter upon the death of each who inherits the shadow, until the debt is sung whole.*

*Each who inherits.* As if the shadow were furniture. As if it passed down a bloodline like a chin or a temper, accruing interest.

And then the final clause, the one her eyes kept refusing, the one she finally pinned to the page with both thumbs:

*The Second Party may call upon the Signatory and her heirs to sing. They will answer. They will always answer. This is the nature of a shadow: it belongs to the thing that casts it.*

At the bottom, two signatures. One was Adaeze Okafor's—Nkiru knew that signature intimately, had seen it on royalty statements and concert programs, the confident loop of the A, the decisive crossbar. It was here, but shrunken somehow, cramped into the space as if the hand that wrote it had been trying to make itself small.

The other signature was not written. It was *hummed onto the page*—there was no other way to describe it, a line of ink that rose and fell in four notes, looping, and Nkiru's stomach dropped through the floor of the building, through the foundation, into whatever the basement was actually sitting on top of.

Four notes. The same four notes bleeding through Sebastian's voicemail.

She sat back. The bulb buzzed. Somewhere in the stacks, a pipe ticked as it cooled, or warmed, or counted.

* * *

She should have photographed it and left. That was the professional move. Document, preserve, withdraw, analyze in daylight among living colleagues and coffee.

Instead she opened the ledger.

It was Adaeze's journal—she recognized the handwriting from the margins of the contract, from letters Nkiru had squinted at on microfilm years ago. The entries ran from 1923 to 1969, and they were not diary entries in any soft sense. They were records. Payments received. Powers used. Costs incurred.

*March 1923. Sang at the Abyssinian hall tonight and told them, with the voice, that they had never heard anything so fine. Two hundred people believed it absolutely. I believed it myself for one full minute afterward, which frightened me more than the other thing.*

*June 1924. Used the voice on Mr. Ellison to make him forget he ever had a soprano worth mentioning. Her career ended quietly. Mine did not. I do not write down his name anymore because when I do, I hear him humming.*

*1929. The Second Party visited again. It does not knock. It is simply present in the room where the piano is, the way dampness is present in a wall. It asked if I am satisfied with our arrangement. I said yes, with the voice, and then alone in the dark I said no, without it, and I do not know which of those words it heard.*

*1946. My son has the gift. Not mine—the other one. He can make strangers feel heard. Strangers weep on his shoulder and cannot say why. I know what that is. I know whose coinage that is. I have begun writing warnings and hiding them where a child would look and an adult would not.*

*1954. It came for him early. He bought us thirty years with counter-melodies. I do not know where he learned them. I do not know how long thirty years buys when the creditor owns the clock.*

*1968. Grandchild shows the gift already, at nine years old. Talks to the radio. The radio talks back. God forgive me, I taught them none of this and they have all of it.*

*1969. To whoever of my blood reads this: the voice you have is not yours. It was purchased before you were born with a piece of you that you will miss without knowing its name. Break it or feed it. There was never a middle.*

Nkiru closed the ledger with hands that were not quite steady.

The inheritance revealed as a debt. She had come looking for context—dates, corroboration, the sober scaffolding a scholar builds around a frightening intuition—and instead she had found the intuition itself, notarized. Sebastian's gift for radio, that uncanny thing, that ability to make a stranger at 3 a.m. feel like the only person alive—purchased. Inherited. *Coinage.* And the price of the coinage was a shadow, a second self, held somewhere by a party whose residence was *wherever voices are kept*, and the party had begun calling in the loan.

Marisol. The dead callers. The four notes. She didn't know the details yet—Sebastian's voicemail had collapsed into static and that humming before explanations could happen—but she knew shape when she saw it. She had spent her life studying how belief moves through crowds, how a voice can fill a room past its walls. Something had been filling rooms through the Okafors for a hundred years, and now it wanted the whole house.

She photographed every page. The flash seemed louder than it should have been, and once—she would tell herself later it was the shutter lag, old camera, cheap camera—once the image on the little screen showed the contract with a fifth line of text that was not on the paper, a line that read, in Adaeze's cramped hand: *he is reading over your shoulder, child. Hurry.*

When she looked up from the screen, the actual page had no such line.

She packed the box, retying the string with the archivist's original knot, and was halfway to the stairs when Mrs. Ferreira's voice stopped her.

"You found it, then."

Nkiru turned. The older woman stood at the foot of the stairs with her arms crossed, and behind the reading glasses her expression was not curiosity. It was recognition—the specific, weary recognition of someone seeing a pattern complete itself.

"My grandmother worked the desk upstairs in the sixties," Mrs. Ferreira said. "A woman came in once, near the end—famous singer, everyone knew her face. Adaeze Okafor. She asked to deposit a box with the estate papers, and my grandmother said estates went through the county, not the library, and the woman looked at her—" Mrs. Ferreira paused, choosing the words carefully, the way you choose footing on ice. "She looked at her the way you look at someone when you're trying to warn them and can't say the warning out loud. Then she paid twenty dollars to leave the box anyway. My grandmother kept the twenty dollars in an envelope for forty years. Said it felt wrong to spend it. Said it felt like it was *listening*."

"Mrs. Ferreira," Nkiru said. "The second party in that contract. The Choirmaster. Is there anything else—in the collection, anywhere in the building—about what it actually *is*?"

The archivist was quiet for a moment. The bulb overhead buzzed. Down in the stacks, very faintly, a pipe ticked—and Nkiru noticed, with the particular clarity that arrives too late to be useful, that the ticking had rhythm. Four beats. Rest. Four beats.

"What it *is*," Mrs. Ferreira repeated. "I can't tell you that. But I'll tell you what my grandmother told me, the one thing she ever said about that box, and she made me swear not to repeat it until someone came asking." She took off her glasses, which somehow made her look younger and more afraid. "She said: the singer didn't sell her soul. Souls are common. She sold the echo. And an echo, young lady, is the part of you that keeps answering after you've stopped calling."

She put her glasses back on. "Return the box by five. Pencils only." And she climbed the stairs, leaving Nkiru alone in the buzzing light with a phone full of photographs and one thought repeating like a needle stuck in a groove:

Somewhere, a shadow of an Okafor voice had been answering calls for a hundred years.

And last night, at 4:07 a.m., it had answered *her*.
