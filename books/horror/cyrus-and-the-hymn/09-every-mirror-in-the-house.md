## 9. Every Mirror in the House

The hallway mirror had been the first. Now it was the least of them.

Sebastian woke at four in the afternoon—sleep had become something that happened to them rather than something they did—and lay in bed listening to the house breathe. Old houses breathed. Pipes ticked, joists settled, the radiator muttered its one long complaint. But this house breathed like a chest, slow and deliberate, and lately Sebastian had begun to suspect it was breathing *around* them, the way a mouth breathes around a held tongue.

They got up. They washed their face. They did not look at the bathroom mirror.

This was the discipline now: the towel draped over the glass before brushing teeth, the shower taken with eyes on the tiles, the small choreography of a person learning to live sideways through their own home. It had been three days since the blink that finished without them. Three days of pretending that was exhaustion, or stress, or the particular madness of inheriting a house you never asked for from a woman you'd met exactly twice, both times at funerals.

Three days was as long as the pretending lasted.

Because that morning, making coffee in the kitchen, Sebastian had caught movement in the window over the sink—the old black glass that reflected the room at night and showed only the yard by day. Except it wasn't day yet. The light outside was still that bruised pre-dawn gray, and the window was doing its mirror work, and in it Sebastian saw themselves standing at the counter.

Standing at the counter, smiling.

Sebastian's face, at that moment, was doing nothing of the kind. Sebastian's face was slack with sleep and dread, jaw loose, eyes raw. But the reflection smiled—a wide, easy, radio-host smile, the smile Sebastian wore for strangers at two in the morning when a caller said *I don't know why I'm telling you this* and Sebastian said *because I'm listening*—and the smile was wrong in a way Sebastian couldn't have named if they'd tried. It was too practiced. It was a smile worn by someone who had studied Sebastian's smile for a very long time and gotten almost all of it right.

Almost.

Then the reflection's lips moved. No sound came through the glass, but the shapes were clear, patient, unhurried, like words spoken underwater:

*You're late.*

Sebastian dropped the mug. It broke against the tile, and the sound was ordinary and real and for one merciful second the world snapped back into single file—reflection, action, same second, no gap. Then the window went dark with morning coming up over the fence, and there was nothing in it but the yard and the broken mug and Sebastian's own shaking hands, reflected honestly, a full second behind nothing at all.

That had been six hours ago. Since then, Sebastian had walked the house.

They hadn't meant to take inventory. They'd meant to pack a bag, get in the car, drive to Nkiru's, pound on her door, say *I know we haven't spoken in two years but something is wrong with me.* They'd even gotten the bag down from the closet shelf. But the bag was in the bedroom, and the bedroom had a mirror—a tall oval thing in a tarnished silver frame that Adaeze Okafor had presumably gazed into while she sang her way into the hearts of congregations along the whole eastern seaboard—and the mirror was uncovered, and Sebastian made the mistake of walking past it.

Their reflection turned its head first.

Not much. A half-second of lead, the way a bad video call lags, the image trailing the body like a coat caught on a nail. Sebastian stopped. The reflection kept turning, completed the motion, caught up, and then—settled into place—it looked at Sebastian with an expression of mild, courteous interest, the look of a shopkeeper waiting to see what you'll break.

"Okay," Sebastian said aloud, because saying things aloud had always been how they anchored themselves. "Okay. That's—that's fine. That's a trick of the light."

The reflection did not say anything back. It just watched them with those borrowed eyes, and Sebastian understood, with the cold certainty of a fever breaking, that the lag had spread. Whatever had lived in the hallway mirror for three days had learned to be everywhere the glass was.

They backed out of the bedroom. They took the stairs facing forward, which felt absurd and necessary, like descending a ship's ladder in a storm. And then they stood in the front hall, keys in hand, ready to leave—

—and heard, from the parlor, the sound of humming.

Four notes. Rising, falling, rising again. The hymn-fragment the caller had hummed into their headphones five nights ago, the one that had lodged itself behind Sebastian's sternum like a swallowed coin, surfacing at red lights and in the shower and once, horribly, mid-broadcast, so that Sebastian had to talk over their own throat for a full minute until it sank again.

It was coming from the parlor. Where the big cheval mirror stood.

Sebastian did not go into the parlor. Sebastian went to the hall closet instead, and found the dust sheets Adaeze had left folded on the top shelf like a gift—or an instruction—and began, methodically, hands steady in the way that hands go steady when the alternative is not, to cover every mirror in the house.

* * *

There were more mirrors than any reasonable house needed.

Sebastian counted as they worked, because counting was a thing to hold onto. The hallway pier glass: one. Bathroom vanity: two. Bedroom oval: three. The cheval in the parlor: four. A round hand-mirror in a drawer of Adaeze's writing desk, its handle wrapped in yellowed ribbon: five. Two framed mirrors in the guest room that reflected each other across the narrow space, creating that infinite corridor effect, and Sebastian covered them last and fastest, because standing between them even briefly meant standing inside a tunnel of delayed selves, versions of themselves receding into the dark each one a beat behind the last, and in the deepest reach of that corridor, something had been waving.

Six, seven. The little mirror set into Adaeze's jewelry box, which opened on a hinge and could not be draped, so Sebastian shut it and put the whole box in the kitchen freezer, which was not logical but felt right, felt like putting a tooth under ice. Eight: a smoky antique mirror above the fireplace in what must have been Adaeze's sitting room, a room Sebastian had avoided entirely until now, and entering it was like entering a held breath. The furniture was still arranged for company. A teacup sat on the side table with a ring of ancient tannin at its bottom, as if the last guest had stepped out mid-sip a hundred years ago and simply never come back.

The mirror above the mantel was the largest in the house, and when Sebastian lifted the sheet toward it, their reflection raised its arms a full second early.

Before Sebastian moved. Before Sebastian's own hands began to lift.

The reflection reached up, grasped the edge of a sheet that wasn't there yet, and paused—poised, waiting, patient as arithmetic—and Sebastian stood frozen beneath it with the actual dust sheet crumpled in their arms, and the reflection waited, and waited, and then slowly, deliberately, lowered its empty hands and shook its head.

*No,* said the shape of its mouth. *No, no.*

Sebastian threw the sheet over the frame anyway, blind, by feel, and stood there gasping in the sudden brown dimness of the shrouded glass, heart slamming, and thought: *it wanted me to think it was ahead of me now. It wanted me to know the lag goes both ways.*

Or worse. Worse was simpler: the reflection hadn't been predicting anything. The reflection had simply been living its own Tuesday, in its own time, one second offset from Sebastian's, and in that second it had decided to pick up a sheet, and changed its mind, and none of it had anything to do with Sebastian at all. The idea arrived whole and terrible: *the mirror world isn't reacting to me. It's just going about its business. And its business is getting more crowded.*

They finished the sitting room. They did the small mirror in the upstairs landing, and the one built into the wardrobe door, and a hand-glass wedged behind the radiator like contraband. Eleven mirrors. Twelve. Thirteen, if you counted the darkened television screen in the den, which Sebastian covered too, because these days every black surface was a candidate, every polished thing a door left ajar.

By the time they were done, the house looked like a place where furniture was in mourning. Sheets everywhere, ghost-shaped, hanging still in the closed-air rooms. Sebastian walked the halls and the sheets did not stir, and somehow that was worse than if they had.

* * *

They called the station at seven to say they wouldn't make the night's broadcast. Their producer, Dev, took it badly.

"You've missed two nights already. You never miss nights. You told me once you'd do the show from your own deathbed."

"I might have to," Sebastian said, and heard how it sounded, and tried to laugh, and the laugh came out thin and wrong, a paper copy of a laugh.

"Seb. What's going on? You sound—" A pause; Dev choosing his words with unusual care. "You sound like you're in a tunnel."

"I'm in the house. My great-grandmother's house. The acoustics are—" Sebastian stopped. Because that wasn't it. The line wasn't echoing. Something else was happening, something they recognized from the other end of a different kind of call: underneath Dev's voice, faint as breath on a baby monitor, there was a second voice. Not speaking. Breathing. Slow, tidal, keeping time with nothing.

"Dev, I'm going to go."

"Take the week," Dev said. "Take two. Just—come back, okay? The show needs you. Honestly, the listeners need you. Half of them only survive the night shift because of you."

"That's what worries me," Sebastian said, and hung up before he could ask what that meant, because Sebastian didn't fully know, only that it was true: somewhere in the last two weeks, the show had stopped being a service Sebastian performed and started being a door Sebastian propped open, and everyone who called in, everyone who listened, was standing in the doorway with them.

They sat on the kitchen floor after that, back against the cabinets, in the one room with no reflective surfaces larger than the toaster. The cat—Biscuit, thirteen pounds of indifferent affection—had not come home in four days. Sebastian had put food out; the food stayed. Animals knew things. Biscuit had looked at the house from the porch on that last night with an expression Sebastian had never seen on a cat before, an expression of apology, and left, and had not returned, and Sebastian missed the stupid animal with a physical ache, because the animal had been the last vote in favor of the house being merely a house.

The phone rang.

Sebastian stared at it. The landline—the old rotary thing hardwired into the wall, which had rung twice before, both times with dead callers, both times with messages that had come true in the worst possible ways. It rang with a patience that felt rehearsed.

They let it ring nine times. Ten. On the eleventh, they picked it up, because not answering had become its own kind of torture, and because some traitor part of them still believed the phone was their instrument, their territory, their altar. Voices were Sebastian's country. Even now. Especially now.

Static. Then, layered under the static, the choir—not singing, just sustaining, a chord held so long it stopped sounding like voices and started sounding like weather. And threaded through it, close, intimate, a voice that was almost Sebastian's own:

*"You can't cover them all."*

"Who is this?" Sebastian asked, though the question had died of embarrassment weeks ago.

*"There's one in the music room,"* the voice said gently, the way you'd remind someone of an umbrella left behind. *"Behind the piano. She kept it for a reason."*

The line clicked dead. The dial tone came back, ordinary, indifferent.

Sebastian sat on the kitchen floor for a long time afterward, watching the last light fail in the window over the sink, and did the math they'd been avoiding. The music room. Locked since the day they inherited the house, key nowhere, door painted shut or something worse than painted. Every reel-to-reel tape behind it labeled in Adaeze's handwriting with dates spanning a century. And now a mirror behind the piano, kept—*kept*, the voice had said, kept on purpose—for a reason.

The house had been herding them for days. Sebastian could see the shape of it now: the lag spreading mirror to mirror like frost across glass, forcing them to shroud every surface, driving them room to room, and every path through the shrouded house bent, eventually, toward the one door they hadn't opened. The sheets weren't protection. The sheets were walls of a maze.

Sebastian stood. Found the flashlight. Found the crowbar Dev had given them as a gag gift three Christmases ago—*for when the critics come*—and carried it down the hall past all the draped ghosts of themselves, to the door at the end of the ground-floor corridor, the one with the brass knob gone green and the wood swollen around the frame like the house had grown scar tissue over it.

The air near the door was cold. Not draft-cold. Held-cold, the cold of a room that has been keeping its breath.

"Fine," Sebastian said to the door, to the house, to whatever was one second behind everything. "Fine. Let's see what she kept."

They raised the crowbar—and stopped, because from the far side of the locked door, faint and clear, a piano played a single note. Middle C. Tuned. Patient.

Waiting for a duet.
