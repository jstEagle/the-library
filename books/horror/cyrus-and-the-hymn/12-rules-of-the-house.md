## 12. Rules of the House

The journal gave up its secrets grudgingly, the way old people give up opinions—only when cornered, and always with a note of contempt.

Sebastian sat at the kitchen table with the book open under the brightest lamp in the house, a pot of coffee going cold beside them, while outside the windows the night held its shape like wet clay. It was past four. Or it had been past four for a long time. The clock on the stove said 4:17 and had said 4:17 twice already, which Sebastian was choosing not to think about.

Nkiru slept upstairs—or lay awake upstairs, which amounted to the same thing in this house—and Sebastian read alone, because someone had to know what they were dealing with before the sun came up. If the sun came up. The journal's handwriting shifted across its decades: Adaeze's bold, slanted script giving way to a grandson's careful pencil, then to Sebastian's grandmother's looping cursive, each generation adding to the record like people adding sandbags against a flood that never crested, only rose.

And in the margins of every hand, repeated like a refrain worn into a hymnal, were the rules.

*It cannot enter a room where its name is not spoken.*

*It cannot cross salt.*

*It cannot be seen directly without claiming the voice of the one who sees.*

Three rules. Three lines of defense. Sebastian read them five times, then a sixth, mouthing the words, feeling something almost like hope unclench in their chest—the first hope since the dead callers had started singing in unison on their show, since the mirrors had begun lagging like bad video feeds, since the house had sealed itself against a dawn that might never come.

Rules meant boundaries. Boundaries meant the thing could be managed. Sebastian Okafor had spent fifteen years managing the impossible on live radio—grieving callers, prank callers, callers who confessed to crimes mid-song request—and they knew how to work inside constraints. You learned where the edges were, and you performed right up against them.

"Okay," they said aloud to the empty kitchen, because talking helped, because their voice was the one thing they'd ever trusted. "Okay. Let's test your fine print."

* * *

The first rule seemed almost too easy. *It cannot enter a room where its name is not spoken.* Which meant, presumably, that if you simply never said the name—if you kept "Choirmaster" out of your mouth—the thing couldn't follow you from room to room. A quarantine built out of silence. Sebastian could work with silence. Silence was half their job; they called it dead air, and they'd spent a career filling it, but they knew its texture, knew how to move through it without panic.

They started with the kitchen. They stood in the doorway and said the name once—"Choirmaster"—feeling the word leave their mouth like something spat, and then they walked deliberately through the house saying nothing, testing whether the air changed, whether the pressure behind their sternum shifted, whether the temperature dropped the way it did in movies when ghosts came through walls.

Nothing. The hallway stayed a hallway. The living room stayed a living room with its sheets draped over the mirrors like furniture in mourning. Sebastian walked the full circuit, lips pressed shut, and ended back in the kitchen breathing hard and almost laughing.

"It works," they said. Then stopped. Because they'd just said *it*, not the name, and the rule said—

The rule said the name had to be *spoken*. Not by whom.

Sebastian stood very still in the kitchen doorway and listened to the house.

At first there was nothing, just the refrigerator's hum and the wind doing something theatrical with the chimney. But underneath—faint, patient, coming from everywhere the way water comes through a foundation—was a voice. Not singing. Reciting. The name, over and over, in a register too low to properly hear, felt more than heard, vibrating in the fillings of their teeth and the bones behind their ears.

*Choirmaster. Choirmaster. Choirmaster.*

The thing was speaking its own name. In every room. Continuously. It had been, Sebastian realized with a cold slide of understanding, probably since the moment Nkiru first read the name aloud from Adaeze's journal two nights ago. The rule wasn't a wall. It was a formality—a legal requirement the entity satisfied itself, the way a landlord satisfies an ordinance by posting a notice no tenant reads.

Sebastian grabbed the coffee mug and hurled it at the wall. It shattered beautifully. The voice didn't pause. Somewhere upstairs, Nkiru's floorboard creaked, and Sebastian made themselves breathe, made themselves pick up the shards, because breaking things felt good and they couldn't afford to feel good about anything tonight.

One rule down. Two to go.

* * *

Salt. Of course salt. Every tradition, every folklore seminar, every late-night documentary Sebastian had ever produced a segment around agreed on salt: purification, preservation, the mineral that keeps meat from rotting and spirits from crossing. And the journal confirmed it in Adaeze's own hand, underlined twice: *SALT AT EVERY THRESHOLD. IT CANNOT CROSS. I HAVE SEEN IT STOP AT THE LINE LIKE A MAN AT THE EDGE OF WATER.*

There was still salt in the house. Sebastian found a box of kosher salt in the pantry, absurdly domestic, and poured thick white lines across every doorway on the ground floor—kitchen, hall, living room, the door to the basement stairs. They worked methodically, kneeling on the hardwood, pouring with the care of a person laying down a lifeline, and when they finished they stood in the center of the hall surrounded by their small white borders and felt, for the second time that night, something adjacent to safe.

Then they tested it. That was the whole point. Trust but verify—that was radio, that was life.

The salt line at the front door was the thickest, a fat unbroken ridge of crystals three inches wide. Sebastian crouched before it and spoke to the empty street visible through the sidelight window, to the dark, to whatever stood in the dark listening.

"You can't cross this," they said. "Right? That's the deal. So let's see the deal hold."

For a long moment, nothing. The streetlight at the curb buzzed. A moth threw itself against the glass with small dry clicks.

Then the salt began to move.

Not blown, not scattered—*dissolved*. The crystals darkened, grayed, went translucent as ice in spring, and shrank, each grain diminishing in place until the line thinned to a thread, then a smudge, then a faint damp stain on the wood, as though a hundred years of time had passed over it in a single breath. Sebastian watched the entire defense rot in real time and understood, with a lurch, what they were looking at. The entity hadn't crossed the salt. It had simply waited. Salt preserved things—but everything the salt touched was still *in time*, and time belonged to the Choirmaster the way the night currently did. It hadn't broken the rule. It had outlasted it, the way it had outlasted Adaeze, outlasted her son, her granddaughter, everyone who had ever drawn a line against it and died comfortably believing the line would hold after them.

Sebastian ran to the kitchen doorway. The line there was thinning too, graying at the edges, dissolving grain by grain with a sound like distant rain. They snatched the box and re-poured, hands shaking, doubling the lines, tripling them, and the fresh salt held—for now, for minutes, maybe for hours—but the arithmetic was brutal and simple. There were eleven boxes of salt in the house. The night did not appear to have an end scheduled.

In the living room, behind its sheet, one of the covered mirrors laughed. Softly. Politely. Like an audience member who knows better than to heckle.

Two rules down. One to go. And the last one, Sebastian already knew, was the worst, because it wasn't a defense at all. It was a trap with a warning label.

* * *

*It cannot be seen directly without claiming the voice of the one who sees.*

Which meant you could fight it blind or fight it mute. Those were the options. Sebastian turned the sentence over and over at the kitchen table, coffee gone bitter and cold, looking for the loophole the way they'd look for the catch in a contract—which, they supposed, was exactly what they were doing, because that's all any of this was: contract law, inherited, with souls as collateral.

You couldn't look at it directly. Fine. But what counted as direct? A reflection—did a reflection count? The mirrors lagged; the mirrors were compromised; the mirrors were practically its waiting room. What about seeing it through a camera? Through the studio's monitor feed? What about peripheral vision, the glimpse at the edge of sight that isn't quite a look?

The journal, maddeningly, offered testimony instead of definitions. Adaeze's grandson had written, in careful pencil: *Uncle Emeka saw it in 1949, full on, face to face. He lived forty more years but never spoke again. He sang, though. God help us, he sang, all day, hymns none of us knew, in a voice that was not his.* And beneath that, in Sebastian's grandmother's hand: *Mama looked at it through the hallway mirror in '81 and lost her singing voice only—not her speech. Partial view, partial price. The rule scales.*

The rule scales. Sebastian sat with that. Their grandmother had paid a partial toll and considered herself lucky. Uncle Emeka had paid in full and spent four decades as a haunted flute.

So the question became experimental, and Sebastian hated themselves a little for what they were about to do, and did it anyway, because they were their great-grandmother's blood and curiosity in this family had always outrun sense.

They took their phone. They opened the camera, flipped it to the front-facing lens, and held it up so the screen showed the kitchen behind them—the table, the lamp, the ruined salt line, the doorway to the hall. If the rule scaled, then a mediated image should cost less than a direct gaze. A photograph of a storm doesn't get you wet.

"Testing," they said stupidly, to the phone, to the journal, to the night. "One, two."

And in the phone's screen, in the doorway of the reflected hallway, something was standing.

It was tall in the way that architecture is tall—it occupied the doorframe the way a column does, with the same indifference to human proportion. It wore robes that might have been vestments or might have been the idea of robes, rendered in shadow. Where its face should have been there was a mouth. Only a mouth, wide and serene, and around the mouth, faintly, arranged in rows that receded further than the hallway possibly could, faces. Dozens. Hundreds. Some of them Sebastian knew from photographs in the journal. One of them, at the outer edge, wore Adaeze's famous smile.

On the phone screen, the mouth opened.

Sebastian's thumb slammed the power button. The screen went black. They stood in the kitchen shaking, waiting—listening to their own voice inside their chest like a man checking his pockets after brushing against a thief.

Still there. Still theirs. They said the alphabet forward and backward. They sang eight bars of something from church, off-key, graceless, alive. Their voice held.

They laughed—one cracked, disbelieving note of it—and looked down at the dead phone in their hand, and the laugh died in their throat.

The camera app was open again. Black screen, yes, powered off—but the little green indicator light above the lens was glowing. Steady. Patient. Recording.

And beneath the refrigerator hum, beneath the wind, beneath the low recitation of its own name that filled every room of the house, Sebastian heard—very faintly, very fondly, coming from everywhere and nowhere, from the phone and the walls and the lagging mirrors and the space just behind their own ears—a new sound being added to the archive.

Their own voice. Saying *testing, one, two.* Played back. Kept.

The rule hadn't failed. The rule had worked perfectly. It had claimed exactly what it could reach: not Sebastian's voice, but Sebastian's *voice*—the recording, the artifact, the broadcast self, the version of them that lived in microphones and transmissions and the ears of strangers at 3 a.m. It had taken a sample. It was building a choir part.

The green light pulsed once, gently, like a slow blink. Like a promise.

Upstairs, every door in the house opened at the same instant—a soft chorus of clicks, twelve latches turning in unison—and from the direction of the music room, the one that had been locked for a century, came the unmistakable sound of a piano bench being pulled back from the keys.

Sebastian stood in the kitchen holding a dead phone that was somehow still listening, and understood that the rules had never been armor. They'd been a syllabus. The house was teaching them, lesson by lesson, exactly how little the rules were worth—and walking them, door by opening door, toward the one room where the final exam was already tuned and waiting.
