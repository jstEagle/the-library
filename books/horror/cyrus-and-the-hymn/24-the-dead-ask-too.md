## 24. The Dead Ask Too

The phone started ringing at 2:47 a.m., and I knew before I answered it that it wasn't the Choirmaster.

I'd learned its call by then. The entity's calls came with a pressure behind them, a sensation like a hand cupped against the back of your skull, and the line always carried that faint wet undertone, as if the voice were traveling through something organic. This was different. This rang the way phones used to ring when I was a kid—patient, almost shy, the way a caller rings when they're not sure they should have called at all.

The house phone. The old rotary in the front hall that hadn't been connected to anything since before I inherited the place. I'd checked the jack myself, weeks ago, after the mirrors started lagging. There was no wire. There was no service. And still it rang, brass bell shivering inside its dark wooden throat, filling the hallway with a sound so ordinary it felt obscene.

Nkiru was asleep on the couch in the study, or pretending to be. We had stopped really sleeping days ago; we took turns lying down with our eyes closed while the house rearranged itself around us. I stood in the hallway in my socks, watching the phone ring, and I thought about all the rules from Adaeze's journal. Salt lines. Closed doors. Never look directly. None of them said anything about answering.

Maybe that was the trap. Maybe everything was the trap now.

I picked up the receiver.

"Hello?"

Static first—the deep, oceanic static I'd come to know—but underneath it, faintly, a woman breathing. Not the measured, choral breathing of the dead callers on my show. This was ragged. Human. Someone catching their breath after running a long way.

"Sebastian." A pause. "Oh, God. It worked. Sebastian, can you hear me?"

My mouth went dry. "Who is this?"

"It's Marisol."

* * *

I sat down on the hallway floor because my legs decided the question for me. The receiver was cold against my ear, colder than plastic should be, cold like a stone left out overnight.

"Marisol Reyes," I said stupidly, because saying names was the only thing I knew how to do anymore. "You called my show. Three weeks ago. You wanted me to play 'Bésame Mucho' at your funeral."

"You played it," she said, and there was something in her voice—not gratitude exactly, more like relief with grief folded into it, the way you fold egg whites into batter, gently, so it doesn't collapse. "You played it, and everyone sang along, and for about four minutes nobody at my funeral was afraid of me. Do you know what that did? Do you know what that bought me?"

"I don't understand."

"No," she said. "Of course you don't. You're still alive. Understanding comes later, and by then—" She stopped herself. I heard her take a breath she didn't need, an old habit persisting past its purpose, and that small human stubbornness nearly broke me. "Listen to me carefully, because I don't know how long they'll let me hold this line. The ones who've been calling your show—the messages we asked you to pass along—"

"The lies," I said. "The door-opening lies."

Silence. Then, very quietly: "So you know."

"Nkiru found the contract. 1923. My great-grandmother sold our voices' shadows to something called the Choirmaster, and every generation since has fed it, and right now it's using my show as its throat, and half the city is humming its hymn in their sleep. So yes. I know some things."

"Then know this one." Her voice dropped, went urgent, went thin as wire. "We didn't want to lie to you. None of us did. But we don't get to choose what we say when it dials through us. It wears our voices the way you'd wear a coat. The widow on Delancey Street—the message about the garden—it wasn't her husband speaking. It was never him. And when she believed it, when she opened that little door of joy in her chest, the Choirmaster walked through it, and three days later she was ours. She's in the rows now, Sebastian. Second row, alto section. She won't stop crying, and the Choirmaster won't let her stop singing, and both sounds come out of the same mouth."

My hand had gone numb around the receiver. Or maybe the receiver was numbing my hand. With this house, lately, it was hard to tell which was the instrument and which was the held thing.

"The rows," I repeated. "I saw them. In the mirror. All the Okafors, standing in formation."

"That's what harvest looks like from the inside. Not a field. A choir. It doesn't kill us—it tunes us. Every Okafor who ever died with that pact hanging over them got collected into its harmony. My great-aunt. Your grandfather's brother who drowned in '61. Babies. Babies, Sebastian, it keeps the babies in the back row where nobody looks." Her breath hitched. "A hundred years. We've been its choir for a hundred years. Singing the hymn into every ear it aims us at. Opening doors in strangers' hearts so it can walk through and add them to the count. That's how it grows. That's why it wanted a radio host in this generation. Why do you think the gift landed on you? A voice that makes strangers feel heard? It didn't give you that gift out of generosity. It built itself a broadcasting tower and waited for the bloodline to produce someone who'd sit behind a microphone at 3 a.m. and open the door wide."

The hallway seemed to tilt. I pressed my free hand flat against the floorboards, against the honest grain of wood, and tried to hold on to the fact of it.

"When you called my show," I said. "Your request. Was that real? Was any of it you?"

Another silence, longer. When she spoke again her voice had changed—smaller, stripped of urgency, just a woman talking from somewhere very far away.

"That part was me. That's the cruel joke of it. When it lets one of us through clean—a whole minute, sometimes two, when it's distracted or feeding elsewhere—we only ever want one thing. To be remembered as ourselves. You gave me four minutes of a song and a room full of people singing, and for those four minutes I was Marisol Reyes, who liked cilantro too much and cried at commercials, and not row three, second soprano, vessel number forty-one." She laughed, and the laugh cracked down the middle. "You want to know the worst part? It knows that's what we want. It lets us through on purpose. A real moment buys the next lie credibility. Our love is its currency. It spends us."

* * *

The line clicked, hissed, and for a horrible moment I thought she was gone. Then another voice came through—younger, male, hesitant—and I understood that the phone was being passed, hand to hand, down some corridor I couldn't see.

"Sebastian? It's Theo. Theo Okafor. I died before you were born—1961, the lake. They tell the story wrong, by the way. I didn't cramp. I heard singing under the water, and I followed it down, and by the time I realized the song was a mouth it was too late to swim." He paused. "I'm telling you that so you understand: it hunts us too. Being dead doesn't make you safe from it. Being dead makes you available."

"Theo." My great-grandmother's journal had mentioned him once, in a single devastating line: *Lost August's boy to the water. The Choir took him whole.* "Why are you calling? Why now?"

"Because the hymn is almost ready. Can't you feel it? The house is tuning itself. The piano downstairs, the way the rooms keep folding toward the music room—it's building to the performance. And when dawn finally comes—if it ever comes—it will put the page in front of you and the hymn in your head and it will feel like the only possible thing to do. Like duty. Like love for your family, even." His voice hardened. "Don't. Whatever it costs, whatever it promises, whoever it threatens—do not sing it. The hymn isn't a binding. Bindings have two ends. It's a harvest home. Singing it doesn't trap the Choirmaster in our bloodline. It completes the collection. Every Okafor, living and dead, gathered into one chord, forever. Your voice. Nkiru's, if she's standing close enough. Any child you might have had. All of it, folded into the rows, and the door closes, and there is no counter-melody from inside. Believe me. We've looked for a hundred years."

"We found Adaeze's warning," I said. "In the mirror-writing. She told us the same."

"Did she tell you what it cost her?" Theo's voice gentled. "She's been trying to reach this side of the glass since 1938. Every mirror in every house we've haunted, she's been pressing words through, one second late, always one second late, because the lag is the only crack in the wall the Choirmaster couldn't seal. It thinks the lag is its own feature. Its private waiting room. It never imagined the dead would learn to write in the gap." A dry, terrible laugh. "Adaeze was always smarter than the thing she bargained with. Just not smart enough before she signed."

"Is she there? Can I talk to—"

"She's holding the line open. That's costing her more than you can imagine, and when it ends she'll pay for it in rehearsal hours you don't want to picture. So listen, and let her rest." I heard him shift, fabric against fabric, or maybe just static arranging itself into the shape of comfort. "There are forty-one of us on this call. Forty-one Okafors, whispering down one dead telephone wire, taking turns wearing the connection. Some of us haven't spoken in our own voices since the year we died. Do you understand what that means? The Choirmaster takes your voice first. Before anything else. You spend eternity with words in your chest and no way to shape them. Tonight, for a few minutes, we have our mouths back. So we're going to use them."

And they did.

They came one after another, down the decades, each voice distinct as a fingerprint. A woman named Constance who died in 1949 and wanted someone to know she'd forgiven her sister, finally, after sixty years of silence on her side of the glass. A man named Emeka, 1987, who asked whether the mango tree in the yard still stood—I looked out the kitchen window and told him yes, huge now, taller than the roofline, and he made a sound I will carry until I die, a sound like a man setting down luggage he'd been carrying across a century. A child, seven years old, 1954, who only said *tell Mama the dog remembers me,* over and over, until a woman's voice gently took the receiver away.

They weren't asking for messages, though. That was the thing. They weren't calling the show. They were calling *me*, and what they wanted, all of them, in a hundred different phrasings, was the same single sentence:

*Don't sing.*

"Don't sing the hymn." "Whatever it offers, don't sing." "It will show you our faces and promise us peace. Don't sing." "It will wear Grandpa's voice. Don't sing." "It will cry. It has learned to cry. Don't sing."

"But the alternative," I said at last, when the chorus of refusal had worn itself down to breathing, "the journal says if I refuse, it takes the whole line anyway. The dead souls entirely. Isn't the hymn at least—"

"At least what?" Theo again, weary. "At least a cage instead of a grave? Sebastian, listen to me. The choice it shows you is a stage set. Refuse the hymn and it harvests us—yes, that's true, that's the threat, and it will perform the threat beautifully. But here's what it doesn't advertise: if you sing, it harvests us *and* you *and* everyone downstream of your blood until the sun burns out. The hymn doesn't save us from the harvest. The hymn *is* the harvest. The only version where anyone gets free is the one where the collection stops growing. Where one Okafor stands at the piano and does the unthinkable thing instead of the dutiful one." He paused. "Adaeze spent fifty years trying to say this plainly and the Choirmaster kept garbling her words in the glass. So I'll say it plain, while I have a mouth: break the line. Break it even if it breaks us. We've been instruments for a hundred years. Let us be finished."

* * *

Somewhere in the walls, something shifted. Not the house-folding groan I'd grown used to—this was smaller, sharper, like a needle lifted off a record. The temperature in the hallway dropped five degrees in a single breath.

"They know," Marisol whispered, suddenly close again, as though she'd been handed the receiver at a run. "It's noticed the line. Sebastian, listen—the counter-melody, the one from the journal, the torn page—there's a reason it needs a voice given freely to silence. The hymn is a chord, right? One massive chord, every voice locked in unison, that's how it holds us. Unison is prison. Harmony is a door. The counter-melody moves *against* it, dissonance deliberately chosen, a note that refuses to resolve—and a refusing note sung by a willing throat will crack the chord open. But it has to be willing. It has to be given. That's the price, and it's the only price, and no one can pay it for you, and—" her voice splintered— "and I am so sorry. I'm sorry we opened doors for it. I'm sorry about the widow on Delancey. I think about her every day, which is a strange thing to say, because every day down here is the same day, but I think about her anyway. If you find a way to end this—end it for her too. She was only in the rows for a week. She still remembers being a person. Help her remember longer."

"Marisol—"

"One more thing." Fast now, urgent, the connection degrading around her voice like a road washing out. "When it comes for you—and it will come tonight, it will dress the offer up in duty and family and the faces of everyone you've lost—look at its hands. Nobody ever looks at its hands. It shows you faces because faces are what you trust, but it conducts with its hands, and the hands are old, Sebastian. Older than Adaeze. Older than the contract. The hands are the only part of it that's real, and they are *tired*, and I have watched them for a century wondering if even it remembers what it was before it became hunger." A breath. "Maybe that's nothing. Maybe it's just a dead woman's wishful thinking. But if there's anything left in that thing that was ever a singer, ever a person who lost their voice the way we lost ours—then maybe the counter-melody could reach it too. Maybe nothing is only hunger all the way down. Worth knowing. That's all. Worth knowing before you choose."

The line began to fill with a sound I recognized with full-body dread: the hymn, distant, gathering, the Choirmaster calling its choir home. Around Marisol's voice I could hear the others starting to fade, forty-one presences being reeled back into formation, and someone—Constance, maybe, or the mother of the boy with the dog—was weeping without sound, which is the only way they weep down there.

"Go," I said. "All of you, go, don't let it punish—"

"Punishment's just more rehearsal," Theo said, and laughed, and the laugh was defiant and exhausted and utterly human. "Sebastian. Last thing. From all of us." The voices layered then, briefly, not in the Choirmaster's locked unison but in something ragged and free and unbearably alive, forty-one dead Okafors speaking over each other the way families speak at holidays:

*Don't sing.*
*Break it.*
*Break it even if it breaks us.*
*Be the refusing note.*

Then the dial tone. Long, flat, final—the honest sound of a line with no one on it, which somehow hurt worse than screaming would have.

I sat on the hallway floor with the dead receiver in my lap for a long time. Down the hall, the study door stood open, and Nkiru stood in it, wrapped in a blanket, watching me with eyes that already knew.

"Well?" she said.

I set the phone back in its cradle. My hands were shaking. Through the window at the end of the hall, the sky beyond the glass was still the color of a closed eye, though somewhere far above it, I knew, the sun was climbing anyway.

"They're not its choir," I heard myself say. "Not really. They're its hostages. And they just went on strike." I looked up at her. "Nkiru—they told me to break it. All forty-one of them. Even knowing what refusal might cost them." I swallowed. "And Marisol told me something else. Something about the counter-melody. About what kind of voice it needs—and I think I finally understand what we're actually holding in that blank page."

Behind her, at the end of the hall, the covered mirror breathed—one slow ripple, like a sheet over a sleeping mouth—as something in the house began, very quietly, to count down.
