## 40. Coda: The Request Line

The request line rings once.

That's all it takes now—one ring—and Nkiru is out of her chair before the sound finishes, because she has learned in the last two months that the body knows things the mind refuses to admit it knows. Her hand hovers over the blinking light on the board. The studio is empty except for her and the hum of equipment that should be off but never quite is; the ON AIR sign glows red above the glass like a small stubborn heart.

It's 3:14 a.m. The Hour of the Wolf's old slot. Nkiru has noticed that the strange calls only come in this hour, and she has decided—deliberately, the way you decide not to look down from a ledge—not to make anything of it.

She presses the button. "Request line."

Silence. But not dead air. Dead air has a texture she knows intimately now, the texture of a held breath, of a room where something is listening. This silence is different. This one is almost shy.

"Hello?" she tries. "You're on with the Wolf."

*The Wolf.* She still says it sometimes, out of habit, though Sebastian hasn't spoken a word since that morning in October when the counter-melody took its price mid-note and left them conducting with their hands, tears cutting through the dust on their face, mouthing the final bars to a choir no one else could see. Nkiru had watched from the doorway of the music room as the last chord unraveled—not faded, *unraveled*, thread by thread, until the house exhaled and every mirror in it snapped back into time like a bone setting.

She'd thought, in those first seconds, that she would trade anything to hear Sebastian's voice again. She'd meant it. She still means it. And yet when she holds that wish up to the light these days, she finds something complicated living inside it, something that looks a little like gratitude and a little like shame, because the silence bought them everything—the city, the dead, the dawn—and because some part of her, the archivist part, the part that spent years cataloguing other people's ghosts, understands that some prices are not losses so much as transformations. Sebastian didn't lose their voice. They gave it away, freely, which is the only way anything true was ever purchased in that house.

"Anyone there?"

A long pause. Long enough that she counts her own heartbeats against the second hand of the studio clock. Seven. Eight. Nine.

Then, soft as breath against a microphone that isn't there, four notes.

Nkiru goes very still.

She knows them instantly—not the Choirmaster's hymn, God, no, she would know that melody if it were played by a dying music box at the bottom of the ocean, and she would run. These are different. These are four descending notes, warm and slightly ragged, the opening phrase of a theme song that a late-night host used to whistle under the intro of every broadcast for six years, the little signature that thousands of insomniacs across the city came to associate with being heard at the loneliest hour of the night. Sebastian's theme. The one they used to hum while making coffee, while brushing their teeth, while reading Nkiru's footnotes aloud in a voice pitched low just to watch her pretend not to smile.

The notes hang in the air and then fade into the hiss of the open line.

Nkiru's throat tightens. She looks through the glass at the couch in the corner of the studio—the one Sebastian insisted on keeping even after they stopped hosting, the one they curl up on most nights now like a cat reclaiming a favorite chair—and sees that Sebastian is awake. Sitting up. Head tilted, the way they tilt their head when a song catches them, when a caller says something that lands somewhere deep.

They don't know why they're smiling. Nkiru can see that too. It's the smile of someone who has heard their name called from another room and turned around too late to catch who said it—a smile made entirely of echo.

"Sebastian," she says quietly, and covers the mic with her palm, though it doesn't matter; nothing she says on this line has ever mattered to anyone but the two of them. "Do you hear that?"

Sebastian nods slowly. Then shakes their head. Then shrugs, and the shrug says everything their hands have learned to say in two months of signing and scribbling and pressing their palm flat against Nkiru's sternum when words failed: *I don't know. Maybe. Does it matter?*

The line crackles. Not static—static belongs to the Choirmaster, to the century of borrowed voices, and Nkiru flinches from it even now—but the ordinary crackle of an ordinary phone line carrying an ordinary distance. Somewhere on the other end, faintly, there is a room tone. A kitchen, maybe. A clock ticking. The acoustic signature of a place where someone is sitting alone at night, holding a phone, working up the courage to speak.

Or to hum.

"Who is this?" Nkiru asks, and her voice comes out gentler than she intends, the voice she uses in the archives when she's handling something fragile. "You don't have to say. You can just... stay on the line. That's allowed here. That was always allowed here."

Another pause. Then, so softly she almost misses it, the four notes come again—and this time there is something underneath them, something that makes Nkiru's eyes sting. A second voice. Barely there, more intention than sound, harmonizing a third below the melody the way two people do when they've sung together so long they no longer need to look at each other.

Sebastian is off the couch now. Crossing the studio without a sound, barefoot on the carpet, drawn like a tide. They stop beside Nkiru's chair and lean down toward the monitor speaker, and their face does something complicated—hope and grief and recognition fighting for the same square inch of skin.

Their lips move. No sound. But Nkiru reads it easily now; she is fluent in the language they built between them out of necessity and love.

*Is it me?*

"I don't know," Nkiru whispers. And then, because lying to Sebastian is a door she swore never to open again: "I think maybe it doesn't matter whose voice it is. I think maybe it's everyone you ever reached. Calling back."

* * *

Here is what Nkiru Adichie has learned about doors in the past year: they are never really closed, only unlatched from different sides.

She thinks about this a great deal lately, in the way of people who have survived something and now must metabolize it for the rest of their lives. She thinks about Adaeze Okafor, who wanted power over audiences and paid for it in blood-voices across four generations, and who spent fifty years trying to warn the descendants she'd doomed, scratching warnings into mirror-lag and journal margins, breaking her own heart against the thing she'd built. *Break it or feed it,* the journal said. *There was never a middle.* But Adaeze had been wrong about that, or incomplete. There was a third option, and it was Sebastian, standing at a piano at the edge of real dawn, singing a melody written for two voices with only one throat, giving away the last of themselves mid-note rather than let Nkiru pay for a sin that wasn't theirs.

Nkiru had screamed when it happened. She remembered that with terrible clarity—screamed Sebastian's name as the note climbed and then simply stopped, as if a hand had closed gently over a bell. And Sebastian had turned, mouth open, nothing coming out, and smiled at her. Smiled, and kept conducting, hands carving the air, leading a counter-melody that needed no voice anymore because it had become the room itself, the walls themselves, the dawn pushing through windows that hadn't seen morning in weeks.

Afterward, in the kitchen, sitting on the floor amid the dust of a hundred years of reel-to-reel tapes, Sebastian had laughed silently until they cried, and Nkiru had held them and felt the laughter move through their chest like birds taking flight. No sound. All of it real.

The grief for the voice came later, in waves, and Nkiru learned to ride each one without trying to fix it. Some nights Sebastian woke reaching for their own throat, mouth shaping words that had nowhere to go, and Nkiru would take their hands and press them flat against her own collarbone so they could feel her voice humming there, borrowed, temporary, a bridge. *Say it through me,* she'd sign. And Sebastian would, silently, and somehow it worked, the way most impossible things work when two people refuse to stop trying.

The letters started arriving in November. Listeners who half-remembered. A nurse in Yaba who swore that during the worst week of her life, a voice on the radio told her the truth about herself and it saved her. A man who'd driven his car into a ditch outside the city and heard, through the dead radio of a dead engine, four notes and a promise. Children who hummed a melody they couldn't name. None of them could explain how they knew. All of them wrote the same sentence in different hands: *I don't know why, but I feel like someone kept me.*

Nkiru archived every letter. Of course she did. It's what she does with things that matter.

And now the request line rings at 3:14 a.m., and four familiar notes drift out of it like smoke, and Sebastian stands beside her chair looking at the ON AIR light with an expression Nkiru has catalogued before—in the old photographs of Adaeze, backstage, listening to her own recordings with her head tilted, unable to decide whether the voice coming out of the machine was hers anymore.

* * *

"Stay with me," Nkiru tells the line. "I'm going to put you through. Just for a minute. Nobody's listening except us. Nobody's ever really listening except us."

She works the board the way Sebastian taught her, years ago, back when they were still trying to teach each other how to be loved instead of merely admired. The fader slides. The line opens into the studio proper, into the microphone that hangs above the mixing desk like a patient bird, and the four notes fill the room properly now, round and close, and Nkiru realizes with a jolt that they are being hummed *live*, that whoever—or whatever—is on the other end is breathing in time with them, pausing between phrases, the pauses shaped exactly like the pauses Sebastian used to leave so the city could breathe.

Sebastian sits down slowly in the host's chair. Their chair. They haven't sat in it since October. Their hands settle on the mixing board the way hands settle on the shoulders of an old friend, and Nkiru watches them close their eyes.

Then Sebastian does something that stops her heart.

They raise one hand—just one, fingers loose—and begin to conduct. Small movements, private ones, following the four-note phrase as it cycles, and with each pass their gestures grow more certain, more specific, adding dynamics that aren't audible but are *implied*, shaping a performance only they can hear. And the voice on the line follows. Every cue. Every lift of the wrist, every gentle closing of the fist. The hum swells where Sebastian's hand rises. Softens where it falls.

Nkiru stares. Her skin prickles from scalp to sole.

Whatever is on that line, it can see him. Or remember him. Or *be* him, in some register she has no instrument to measure. The archivist in her wants documentation, timestamps, corroboration. The woman who loves Sebastian wants only one thing, and it is this: that whatever remains of the voice they gave away—scattered into the city, folded into a thousand strangers' memories, released with the dead Okafors into whatever comes after—has found its way home tonight, the way lost dogs do, the way songs do, circling back to the window it was born behind.

The phrase ends. Holds. Fades.

And then, into the silence, the voice speaks.

Not words. Not quite. More like the shape of words, the way wind through a doorway can sound like your name if you're lonely enough. Two syllables. Soft. Almost amused.

Almost fond.

Nkiru leans toward the monitor. "Say that again," she whispers. "Slower."

But the line has gone quiet—that shy quiet again, the quiet of someone who has said what they came to say. There's a click, distant and polite, like a receiver being set down carefully. The light dies. The request line sits dark and ordinary, just a phone line at 3 a.m., connected to nothing but the city's sleeping switchboards.

In the corner of the studio, the couch waits. On the wall above it hangs the new mirror—bought in December, hung by Nkiru's own hands, plain walnut frame, honest glass. It reflects the room exactly, in real time, without opinion. Sebastian checked it every day for the first month. Now they check it only sometimes, the way you test an old injury.

Tonight, in the mirror, Nkiru sees them both: herself at the board, and Sebastian in the host's chair, smiling at the dark request line with an expression of such unguarded tenderness that it hurts to look at directly.

Sebastian turns to her. Signs, slow and clear:

*Did you recognize it?*

"Yes," Nkiru says. "It was your theme. Your four notes." A pause. "And something else under it. Singing harmony."

Sebastian's hands hover. Then:

*Was it me?*

Nkiru considers lying. Considers the kind, clean answer. But they built this on honesty scraped raw, on the wreckage of a relationship that died once because Sebastian gave strangers more truth than they gave the person beside them, and she will not preside over a second death.

"I don't know," she says. "Maybe it was the part of you that lives in other people now. Maybe it was Marisol. Maybe it was Adaeze, finally singing something she chose. Maybe it was the city, saying thank you." She reaches over and rests her hand on top of Sebastian's, on the cold fader of the mixing board. "Or maybe the door isn't locked. Maybe it's just closed, and things knock sometimes, and we get to decide whether it's a haunting or a visit."

Sebastian looks at her for a long moment. Then they laugh—silent, shoulders shaking, eyes bright—and sign one last thing, the smallest possible gesture, two fingers flicking upward from the chin, the sign they invented themselves in the first week, meaning *come here*, meaning *stay*, meaning, in the private grammar of the two of them, *I am not afraid*.

Outside the studio glass, the city sleeps. Somewhere in it, a thousand people who were once entranced, once harvested, once sung to sleep by something hungry, turn over in their beds and dream of nothing at all. Somewhere in it, a phone sits dark on a table in a house that no longer rearranges itself, in a hallway where a mirror reflects a man waving at himself in perfect, boring, miraculous synchrony.

The request line stays dark for the rest of the hour.

But at 3:59 a.m., one minute before the wolf's hour ends and the world's begins, it lights up one final time. One ring. Nkiru answers on reflex, hand moving before thought, and holds her breath.

No hum this time. No voice. Just the sound of a room somewhere—a kitchen, she'd swear it, a kitchen with a ticking clock—and beneath it, barely, the sound of someone laughing without any sound at all.

On the couch, Sebastian smiles without knowing why, and reaches for Nkiru's hand, and the red ON AIR light burns steady above them both, keeping watch until sunrise—which, these days, always comes exactly when it's supposed to.
