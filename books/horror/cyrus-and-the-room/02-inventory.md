## 2. Inventory

The clipboard came out at 7:40 a.m., before the coffee finished cooling in the truck's cup holder. Freya Eriksen had a system, and the system was the point: room by room, wall by wall, every defect logged, photographed, measured, and timestamped. A house was a ledger. You read it left to right, top to bottom, and when you were done you knew its value down to the dollar and its failures down to the hairline crack.

She clipped the site plan to the board, checked her camera's date stamp — *OCT 14* — and stepped over the threshold into the Aldous farmhouse for the second day running.

"Okay," she said to no one. "Let's see what you're hiding."

The mudroom smelled of cold ash and mouse. She logged it: *Mudroom — plaster deterioration, NE corner, approx. 30% loss. Floorboards cupped, moisture intrusion suspected.* The window here was painted shut; she noted that too. She worked fast because speed was how she stayed honest. Slow surveyors started editorializing. They'd write *charming original wainscoting* instead of *wainscoting, original, water-stained*. Charm was not a line item. Charm was how appraisers got attached, and attachment was how they got sloppy.

She photographed the coat hooks first. Six of them, iron, screwed directly into the studs at two heights — four high, two low. The low ones were child height. She'd noticed them yesterday and filed it under *period detail*. Today she stood in front of them a moment longer than the system allowed, looking at the way the lowest hook had been polished bright by use while the others held their rust like a secret.

*Move on,* she told herself, and moved on.

* * *

The kitchen took forty minutes.

It should have taken twenty-five. But the pantry door wouldn't stay open for photographs — it swung shut whenever she turned away, slow and polite, like someone closing a door in another room so as not to disturb her — and she ended up bracing it with her tripod case just to get clean shots of the shelving. On the third shelf from the bottom, the wood was worn through in a single spot, a shallow crescent, as if something heavy had been set there and taken down ten thousand times. A crock, maybe. A jug.

Or a child who liked to sit in pantries.

Freya wrote *shelf wear, consistent with stored goods* and heard how thin the sentence sounded in her own head.

The kitchen window faced the back field, where the grass grew in a strange ring around the depression that marked the old well — she could see the cap's steel disc catching the morning light, dull as a coin at the bottom of a pocket. Yesterday the draft rising through the sealed cap had been cold enough to feel against her ankle through her boot. Today she kept her distance from that side of the room without deciding to.

*Window, north face — glazing putty failed, sash weights missing.*

Her pen paused over *missing*.

Sash weights didn't go missing. They sat in their cavities for a hundred years doing nothing. But both cavities in this window were empty, and when she reached her flashlight up into the frame, the light found only dust and a small drift of something pale and fibrous that she chose, professionally, not to examine.

* * *

By eleven she'd cleared the ground floor: parlor, dining room, the narrow stair hall, a back sitting room whose wallpaper had been hung over older wallpaper over older wallpaper still, three generations of pattern pressed flat like leaves in a book. In the parlor she found the fireplace damper welded shut with rust and, wedged behind the coal scuttle, a child's marble — cloudy glass, one blue swirl — which she photographed in situ, then in her palm beside a coin for scale, then returned exactly where it lay. Protocol. Everything stays until the estate attorney says otherwise. Everything gets documented, nothing gets touched.

She was good at protocol. She'd built a fifteen-year career on being the person who never pocketed so much as a bent nail.

The staircase was the next problem.

Yesterday, measuring the stair run for the floor plan, she'd recorded thirteen risers. She remembered it specifically because odd numbers always made her double-check, and she'd double-checked, and it was thirteen. This morning she counted fourteen.

She stood at the bottom and counted again, touching each riser with the toe of her boot like a rosary. Fourteen. Same rise, same run, same oak treads worn pale down the center line. One more stair than yesterday, distributed so evenly across the flight that nothing looked added. Nothing looked wrong. That was the worst part. If a stair had been inserted, the geometry would have to give somewhere — a taller riser, a shorter tread, some place where the math flinched. There was nothing. The staircase simply disagreed with her notes.

Freya climbed it twice more, counting aloud the last time, her voice flat in the empty house: "Eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen."

From upstairs, silence. And beneath the silence, faintly, from somewhere below the floors — below the basement, below even that — the sound she'd catalogued yesterday as *settling*. A long, slow exhalation, like water finding its level in the dark.

She wrote *stairs: verify count* and underlined *verify* twice.

* * *

The upper floor gave her three bedrooms and a bath, and she worked them in order, smallest to largest, because that was the system.

The small bedroom first. Its window looked out on the well. She noted the view without comment, though her pen hesitated — views got described in marketing copy, not surveys, and yet something in her wanted to write *the room watches the well* and couldn't say why. The closet held forty years of dust and one wire hanger. Under the window, the floorboards bore a cluster of small dents, round-ish, irregular — the kind of wear a child leaves kneeling in the same spot night after night, looking out.

The middle bedroom had been a sewing room once; a treadle machine base remained, its iron legs furred with rust. In the corner, water staining spread across the ceiling in a shape she photographed three times because the shape bothered her. It looked like a handprint. It did not look like a handprint if you were a professional. She filed it as *ceiling stain, active leak suspected, roof inspection recommended*, and her eye went back to it anyway, twice, the way a tongue goes back to a chipped tooth.

The large bedroom — the master — was where the plaster gave.

She'd flagged it yesterday: an area along the east wall, maybe three feet square, where the lath showed through like ribs. Moisture had done it, she'd assumed, or simple age. But standing there this morning with her camera raised, she noticed what she'd missed: the damage wasn't random. The plaster hadn't crumbled. It had been *opened* — the edges too clean, the break pattern deliberate, a rough rectangle punched through and then, judging by the dust drifts on the floor below, never repaired. Someone had made a hole in this wall and left it open for fifty years.

Freya crouched. The cavity beyond the lath was dark and dry and smelled of paper.

Her flashlight beam went in and stopped mattering, because the light wasn't what found it. Her fingers did — reaching past the lath edge, professional habit already composing the log entry (*wall cavity, debris, inspect for structural damage*), and then her fingertips touched cardboard.

A box. Small. Shoebox-sized, shoebox-soft, its corners gone to mush, its lid collapsed inward under a fine silt of plaster dust.

For a moment she didn't move. The house was quiet around her in a way that felt less like emptiness than like held breath — the particular quiet of a room where someone is listening to see whether you'll be gentle.

"It's all right," she said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because surveyors didn't talk to houses, and because she'd said it softly, the way you'd speak to something frightened.

She drew the box out with both hands.

* * *

She knelt on the drop cloth she always carried and worked with a photographer's patience. First: wide shot of the cavity. Then the box in situ. Then gloved hands lifting the lid — it came away with a sound like a sigh — and photographing the contents before touching any of them.

Three things.

A hair ribbon, faded to the color of weak tea but once, clearly, red. It was tied in a bow. Not knotted carelessly, not stuffed in — tied, carefully, the loops even, the tails matched, the way you'd tie a ribbon if you expected to untie it again soon. Fifty years in a wall and it was still waiting to be undone.

A child's shoe, single, leather, its buckle green with verdigris. Size small enough that Freya's whole hand covered it.

And folded into eighths, soft as cloth from handling before it was hidden, a newspaper clipping.

She unfolded it under the camera's eye, documenting each fold, and the headline came into focus letter by letter:

**SEARCH CONTINUES FOR MISSING VOSSTAD GIRL, 9**

*Local — Authorities and volunteers continued Friday to search the river bottoms and outbuildings along the county road for Maren Vosstad, nine, daughter of Halvard and Sigrid Vosstad of Millbrook Road, last seen the evening of June 21st near the Aldous farm...*

June 21st. Solstice. The date snagged in her mind like a burr, though she couldn't have said why.

Below the text, half the photograph survived the fold and the years: a girl's face, grainy, solemn, dark-haired, eyes that looked at the camera the way children look at cameras when they've been told to stand still and aren't sure why. The caption fragment read *Maren, pictured last spring.*

Freya became aware that she had been holding her breath. She let it out slowly and it shook, only slightly, only at the end.

*Missing girl. Nine years old. Last seen near the Aldous farm.* Hidden in the master bedroom wall, wrapped in a wall that had been opened and never closed: a ribbon tied for later, one shoe, and the only public record that this child had ever existed at all.

The system said: photograph, log, replace, report. The estate attorney would be notified. The sheriff, possibly. The items were evidence — or heirloom, or trash; the law would sort it out. The system was clear, and the system had never once failed her, and she knelt there on her drop cloth in the dead man's bedroom with the clipping in her gloved hands and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she was not going to follow the system.

She didn't decide it. That was the terrible part. There was no decision, no weighing, no moment of choice she could later examine and regret. Her hand simply moved — glove to coat pocket, clipping to lining, smooth as a card trick — and then the clipping was against her hip, warm through the fabric, and the box held only a ribbon and a shoe, and Freya Eriksen, fifteen years unblemished, had stolen from a crime scene.

*Not stolen,* something in her corrected. *Kept safe.*

She photographed the box again, empty now, and wrote the log entry in her smallest, most controlled handwriting: *Wall cavity, master bed., E wall — child's effects (ribbon, shoe), concealed. Recommend notification of estate counsel.* No mention of the newspaper. The omission sat on the page like a black tooth.

* * *

She finished the upper floor in record time and hated herself for the haste. Bathroom: *fixture loss, subfloor rot suspected.* Back bedroom: *closet rod fallen, plaster keying failure, N wall.* Every entry correct, every measurement taken, and underneath them all, in the pocket over her hip, the folded paper pressing its little rectangle of weight against her like a hand.

At the top of the stairs she stopped. Counted the risers going down. Thirteen.

She counted again. Thirteen.

Fourteen going up, thirteen coming down, and the staircase serene about it, unbothered, as if numbers were a courtesy it extended to visitors and revoked when they left.

Freya stood very still on the landing. Below her, the stairwell dropped away into the dimness of the hall, and beyond that the basement door stood ajar at the bottom — she'd closed it yesterday, she was certain she'd closed it, she remembered the latch clicking — and from the dark beyond it came the sound again, clearer now, patient as tide: water moving where no water should be, in a house whose well had been sealed since before she was born.

She thought of the ribbon, tied for later. Of the girl in the photograph, standing still because she was told to. Of a hole in a wall, opened fifty years ago by hands that meant to come back and never did.

"Who are you?" she asked the house quietly.

The draft rose up the stairwell from the basement and stirred the hair at her neck, cold and mineral-smelling, like the air off deep water. It felt, absurdly, like an answer beginning.

In her pocket, the clipping seemed to warm against her hip. Down in the dark, the water sound gathered itself, and held, and waited — and Freya realized she was already reaching for her recorder, wanting to capture it, wanting proof, wanting to ask it something, though she could not yet have said what.
