## 15. The Judge's House

The tape sat in my coat pocket like a stone I'd swallowed.

I had listened to it eleven times by the time I reached the Rennick place, eleven times through the forty minutes of Edmund Aldous's voice crawling out of the dark of that well — *she wouldn't stop crying, Halvard, she wouldn't — no, you do it, you're the one with the hands —* and then the other voice, younger, clipped, the voice of a man who had never once in his life been interrupted: *Calm down, Edmund. She's a child. Children are quiet when you give them something to be quiet about.*

That was Judge Anders Rennick. That voice had sentenced forty-one years of defendants from the bench of the Third District. That voice had retired with a plaque in the county courthouse lobby and a scholarship named after him at the high school. That voice had told a crying girl, in the winter of 1974, that children were quiet when you gave them something to be quiet about.

His house sat at the end of a lane of bare maples, brick and symmetrical, the kind of house that had been designed to communicate that nothing inside it had ever gone wrong. A flagpole. A mailbox with his name stenciled in careful capitals. Smoke rising straight from the chimney, unhurried, as if even the fire here had learned courtroom decorum.

I parked at the curb and did not move for a long moment. My hands were shaking — I noted that the way I noted everything now, cataloguing, measuring, the habit of the surveyor turned on my own body. *Tremor, bilateral, amplitude moderate. Cause: the tape.* I made myself say it out loud, in the car, alone: "I'm Freya Eriksen, and I'm here to take a measurement."

It had become a kind of prayer. I wasn't sure anymore who it was addressed to.

The door opened before I knocked. Not Rennick — a woman, his wife presumably, small and silvered and polite, who looked at me the way you look at weather. "He's in the study," she said, as if I'd called ahead. As if the house had called ahead. "He's been expecting someone all morning. He won't say who."

I found that interesting. I found everything interesting now, in the way you find things interesting when you have stopped being able to feel them as anything else.

* * *

The study smelled of pipe tobacco and lemon oil. Judge Rennick sat behind a desk that could have slept two people, an old man assembled out of good posture and pressed flannel, and when he looked up at me his face did the thing I had seen on a hundred property owners' faces — the flicker of assessment, *what does she want, what will this cost me* — before settling into courtesy.

"Miss Eriksen." He knew my name. That should have frightened me more than it did. "The Aldous appraisal. I heard you'd been out there." He gestured to a chair. "Sit. Would you like coffee?"

"No, thank you, Your Honor."

"Retired," he said, with the modesty of a man who had never once let the title go. "But I appreciate it. So. The estate. The Voss boy's people finally getting around to selling?"

"The estate is being appraised," I said. "That's all I can speak to."

"Of course. Confidentiality." He smiled. His teeth were very even. "You know, I grew up two miles from that farm. Knew Edmund Aldous slightly. Sad business, what happened to his family. Sad business all around, 1974."

"Tell me about it," I said.

He folded his hands. "There's not much to tell. A girl went missing. The father was looked at, as fathers always are. Nothing came of it. The well was searched — I remember that, the whole county remembers that, fire department, deputies, divers when there was still water in it. Nothing." He spread his hands, a small benediction of emptiness. "Sometimes there is simply nothing, Miss Eriksen. Grief without a villain. It's the hardest kind for people to accept. They'd rather invent a monster than live with an accident."

"Was it an accident?"

The smile stayed exactly where it was. That was the first wrong thing. A man told his family's shame was an accident would frown, would lean back, would show some seam. Rennick's face was a wall with the paint still wet.

"Why don't you tell me why you're here," he said. "You drove two hours. You asked for this meeting through my clerk, which was unnecessary — my home number is listed. You're a surveyor, not a reporter. So you're not here about the girl." He tilted his head. "You're here about the house."

"The house and the girl are the same thing," I said.

Something moved behind his eyes then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "That's a strange thing for an appraiser to say."

"I've been saying a lot of strange things lately." I reached into my coat. "Your Honor, do you know what a laser measure is?"

"I can't imagine I do."

"It's a device that bounces light off a surface and times the return. It's never wrong. It can't be wrong — it's physics. And in that house it returns a different number every time I stand in the same spot. The rooms move. The floor plans redraw themselves overnight. There's a well in the basement under a steel cap that was sealed in 1974, and last week it was warm." I set the recorder on his desk between us, small and silver and ordinary. "And three nights ago, it spoke."

"Miss Eriksen—"

"It spoke with Edmund Aldous's voice," I said, "and it spoke with yours."

I had rehearsed this moment in the car and it went nothing like the rehearsal. In the rehearsal, he denied it, I played the tape, he broke. In the room, he simply looked at the recorder for a long moment, and then he looked at me, and his face did something I will be describing to therapists for the rest of my life: it *emptied*. Like a room being cleared. Like a house being prepared for sale.

"Turn it off," he said quietly.

"I haven't turned it on."

"Then leave it in your pocket." He stood. For the first time I saw how old he was, how the posture was load-bearing. "Young woman, whatever you think you found out there, whatever you think you heard — and I know what those devices are, I've read the studies, they pick up radio, they pick up plumbing — I'm going to tell you what I tell every witness who walks into my courtroom with a story they love too much: enthusiasm is not evidence. Grief makes people credulous. You've been alone in a decaying house too long, and someone — I'd guess the neighbor, Halvard, he was always soft in the head about that family — has been feeding you a ghost story." He came around the desk. "Now. I'm going to ask you to leave, and you're going to leave, and you're going to take your little machine and your strange ideas back to whatever firm employs you, and you're going to finish your appraisal of a farmhouse, because that is what you are: a woman who measures houses."

"I don't measure houses," I said. "I measure what houses are worth. And this one—" I picked up the recorder and pressed play.

*—she wouldn't stop crying, Halvard, she wouldn't—*

I will spend the rest of my life being glad that I was watching his hands and not his face, because hands don't know how to lie. His hands went to the desk and gripped its edge, and the knuckles went the color of the maple trees outside, and when the second voice came — *calm down, Edmund* — his hands let go, all at once, like something cut.

I stopped the tape.

The silence in the study was the deepest silence I have ever stood inside. Deeper than the well. Deeper than the house at 3 a.m. when the water sounds stop. It was the silence of a man hearing his own voice come back to him across fifty years from the one place he had spent fifty years making sure nothing could come back from.

"That's not me," Rennick said.

"Acoustics," I said. "Radio interference. Plumbing."

He sat down. It wasn't sitting so much as a controlled collapse, a man folding along lines that had been creased in advance. He put both hands flat on the desk, and he stared at them, and for a long time the only sound was the chimney drawing smoke and my own heart, which I could hear the way you hear a laser measure tick.

Then he said: "How much of it is on there?"

"All of it," I said. "From the crying to the cap going on."

He nodded, slowly, like a man confirming a diagnosis. And then Anders Rennick, forty-one years on the bench, retired with honors, began to talk, and I stood in his study and felt the cold clarity come down over me like a cap over a well.

* * *

"She was going to tell," he said. "That's the whole of it, if you want the whole of it. Edmund had been running the fraud for two years by then — the crop subsidies, the insurance claims, the equipment that burned and got paid for twice. I set it up. I was thirty-one, I was the county's rising man, I knew every form and every man who signed them, and Edmund knew every acre and every lie an acre can be made to tell. It was elegant. It was going to put him out of debt and put me through the state attorney's race." His hands were still flat on the desk. He looked at them as if they belonged to the story and not to him. "And his daughter found the ledger."

"Maren."

"She was fourteen. She was — God, she was *sharp*, she was the sharp one in that family, everyone said it. She'd been keeping her own books since she was ten, little columns in a school notebook, what her father owed the feed store, what he owed the bank. She found the real ledger. The one Edmund kept because I told him to keep it — I told him, *never write it down*, and he wrote it down, because men like Edmund need to write things down, it's how they hold on to the shape of what they've done." A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh. "She brought it to me. Did you know that? She walked four miles to this house — not this house, the old one, but to *me* — with her father's ledger in her school satchel, and she put it on my table, and she said, 'Mr. Rennick, I think my daddy's going to jail, and I don't know what to do.'"

The chimney drew. Somewhere in the house a clock ticked, and I thought: *the house on Voss Road has a clock too, and its hands move.*

"And what did you do?"

"I told her I would look into it," Rennick said. "I told her to go home and say nothing, and I would look into it, and she believed me, because I was the man everyone believed. That was my whole talent, Miss Eriksen. People believed me." He looked up, finally, and his eyes were dry and terrible. "I drove out that night. I told Edmund his daughter had the ledger and knew what it was. I thought — I have told myself for fifty years that I thought — we would send her to an aunt in Duluth until it cooled. That was the plan. That was the *plan*."

"What happened to the plan?"

"Edmund panicked." The words came out of him like stones from a chute. "She'd hidden the ledger — no, she hadn't, she'd left it with a school friend, a boy she trusted, and Edmund went to get it and the boy's mother was there and there were words, and Edmund came back certain the whole county would know by morning. And Maren was crying, and Edmund was shouting, and I—" His voice did something then, dropped through a floor. "There was a rope in the barn. There was always rope in a barn. And the well was dry — it had been dry since August, we'd checked it in the summer over the water-rights dispute, it was a hundred and ten feet of dry stone with a room at the bottom, an old spring chamber, the masons who built the house cut it in 1880-something—"

"You knew there was a room."

"I knew there was a room," he said. "*I* knew. That was the thing, that was the whole obscene thing — the searchers didn't know, the divers didn't know, the county map didn't show it, because the Aldous deed had a metes-and-bounds error from 1931 that dropped the well parcel from the survey, and I was the one who found that error, I was the one who told the fire chief the well was only sixty feet and solid silt at the bottom, because I was the man who read the deeds." He was shaking now, the fine steady tremor of a machine under too much load. "I stood at the top of that well and I heard her at the bottom and I *sealed the cap*, and then I went home and I slept, Miss Eriksen, I slept like a man who had done a hard day's necessary work, and in the morning I called the sheriff myself and reported the girl missing, and I sat with Edmund while he wept, and I held his hand, and everyone in that church looked at us and saw two grieving men."

The recorder lay between us on the desk. I had stopped it long ago. I found I was standing — I didn't remember standing — with my back against his bookshelves, as far from him as the room allowed, and my hands were in my pockets, and in one pocket was the ribbon and in the other was the ring, E.A., and I realized I had brought them with me the way you bring a weapon or a relic, without deciding to.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "You could have thrown me out. You almost did."

He was quiet for a while. When he answered, his voice had changed — flattened, procedural, the voice of a man reading a finding into the record.

"Because it's December," he said. "Because for two weeks I have dreamed of water. Because my wife says I talk in my sleep, and she says I answer questions." He looked at the recorder. "Because that house has been *measuring* me, Miss Eriksen, since the day I put that cap on, and I have spent fifty years waiting for the day it finished. And because—" his hands finally left the desk, and covered his face, and the great upright posture went out of him all at once, and what was left was just an old man in a warm room with a cold thing inside him — "because I heard her voice on your little machine, and she sounded *patient*, and I know what that patience is. It's the patience of a thing that has all the time in the world and knows it does. The solstice is in nine days. The well is filling. I've known it was filling since Tuesday, because I've been hearing it, in the pipes, in the walls of this house, and this house is two miles from that one and there is no water between them that I ever found on any survey."

He lowered his hands.

"Whatever it wants," he said, "it wants it before the water reaches the top. And I am eighty-one years old and I cannot go down those stairs. But you can. And you will. And I will give you whatever you need — the ledger's location, the truth, my confession, notarized, recorded, on the courthouse steps if that's what it takes. I'll give you the whole of it. I'll give you *me*."

"That's not mine to take," I said. "I'm not the one you owe."

"No," he agreed. "But she sent you. Didn't she. The girl. She's been sending people for fifty years — Halvard, the surveyors, the boy who ran away from the place in '88 — and everyone who got close enough to hear her got turned away or turned around, because I was still here, still believing myself, still *believable*." He stood, unsteady, an old tower finally admitting the wind. "She stopped sending. She started *keeping*. You're the first one the house let all the way in. Ask yourself why, Miss Eriksen. Ask yourself what you have that the others didn't."

I knew. I had known since the night I woke with silt on my cuffs. I had the ribbon. I had the ring. I had her things, and a thing given into your keeping is a thing you are answerable for.

"I need the truth," I said. "All of it. Where the ledger is. What was done to the file. What Halvard burned. Everything, in order, on the record."

Rennick nodded. He opened a drawer of the great desk — the drawer I would later understand he had opened every December for fifty years, the way a man checks a wound — and took out a key, small and brass and bright with handling.

"Then come with me," he said. "There's something in my cellar you should measure first."

* * *

We went down the stairs of the Rennick house, the judge ahead of me with a flashlight, and I want to set down exactly what I felt, because this document is the only place it will ever be true: I felt nothing. That was the horror of it. Not revulsion — revulsion is a heat, and there was no heat in me. I walked behind the man who had sealed a living child into a well and I felt the flat professional calm of a surveyor approaching a boundary marker, and I understood that this was what the house had been making of me for three weeks. Not a witness. An *instrument*. Something that could be carried into the dark and would not shake.

His cellar was clean. Concrete floor, shelved preserves, a furnace humming its one note. And in the far corner, under a moving blanket, a shape.

He pulled the blanket off.

A ledger. Bound in black oilcloth, tied with twine, the size of a Bible. Edmund Aldous's handwriting on the cover in faded pencil: *Accounts, 1972–74.* And beneath the ledger, arranged with a care that made my stomach finally, finally turn, a child's other shoe — the mate to the one I had found in the wall — and a school notebook with columns in a fourteen-year-old's hand, and a coat. A small coat, wool, with a name tape sewn inside the collar: *M. VOSS.*

"He kept them," Rennick said. His voice was very small in the concrete room. "He sent them to me in '75, after the estate was settled, and I was supposed to burn them, and I couldn't. I told myself it was insurance." He laughed, and it was the worst sound of the whole long day. "Fifty years, and I called it *insurance*. You spend enough years in the law, Miss Eriksen, and you learn to call anything by its legal name. It's how we sleep."

I knelt and did not touch the coat. My hands hovered over it the way you hover over a sleeping animal.

"The solstice is in nine days," I said.

"I know."

"The well is filling."

"I know."

I stood, and I took the ledger, and the twine, and I looked at the old man who had been the law in this county for forty-one years, and I said the thing I had driven two hours to say and had not known until this moment that I would say it:

"You're coming back to the house with me. Tonight. And you're going to stand at that cap, and you're going to say her name into it, and then—" I heard my own voice go quiet, go cold, go down the well. "And then we're going to open it."

Rennick looked at the flashlight in his hand, at the stairs, at the small coat on the shelf, and I watched the last of the judge go out of his face like a lamp in a window at dawn.

"She won't want me there," he said.

"She's waited fifty years," I said. "She can wait nine days more. But the water can't — and neither, Judge, can you. Because there's one more thing the ledger needs, and one more thing she's owed, and you're going to give them both before the solstice, or I will put you in that well myself."

I had never said a sentence like that in my life. I stood in his clean cellar holding a dead man's accounts and I meant every word, and somewhere two miles away, in a house that was listening, I felt — I will swear to this, I will swear it on any survey, any Bible, any well — I felt the water stop rising for one full minute, like a held breath.

Like a girl, hearing her name.
