## 6. What the Register Remembers

The archive room at St. Aldhelm's occupied the lowest floor of the old library, a windowless vault where the heating had never been updated and the air tasted of cold paper and iron shelving. Vesper had been given a key on their first week — *for curriculum research*, Corvane had said, with that smooth, untroubled smile of his — and had used it every evening since, long after the last bell, long after the corridors emptied of everything but the hum of the boiler and the small, industrious sounds of a building digesting its day.

Vesper worked by lamplight with a thermos of black coffee going cold at their elbow, and a system.

The system was everything. Vesper had built it the way other people built shrines: carefully, and in the belief that someone was watching. One ledger per academic year, arranged chronologically along the east wall, 1924 through the present. One notebook for anomalies. One for cross-references. One — the thinnest, the one Vesper touched least often, the way you avoid touching a bruise to confirm it still hurts — for Idris.

Idris Osei. Third form. Marked absent on the fourteenth of October, present on the thirteenth, and by the morning of the fifteenth, gone from the register entirely, as if the ink had been drunk back into the page. Vesper had photographed the register before the erasure and after. The photographs were the only proof the boy had ever been enrolled. Vesper kept them in a fireproof box under the bed in the small flat the school provided, and some nights, lying awake, Vesper thought about the fireproof box the way other people thought about a gun in the nightstand. Not because it would save them. Because it was the only thing in the flat that agreed with them.

*You are not imagining this,* the photographs said. *He was here. He was here. He was here.*

Tonight, the plan was simple, and simple plans were the ones Vesper trusted most: pull every register from every year, and read them the way one reads scripture — not for the story but for the pattern beneath the story. If the vanishings were random, then Vesper was a madwoman standing in a cold room cataloguing noise. But if they weren't random — if there was a shape to it, a rhythm, a *rule* — then the rule could be learned, and anything that could be learned could be raced.

Vesper had stopped believing in coincidences around the time Idris's name had dissolved. What remained was arithmetic.

* * *

The registers from the early decades were the hardest. Handwritten in copperplate that faded to brown, the ink of the 1920s gone the color of dried blood. Vesper wore white cotton gloves and turned pages like a surgeon, and the lamplight made the columns of names swim — *Aldous, Bennett, Carrow, Dunn* — a hundred years of children reduced to ink and attendance.

Vesper read the way water finds cracks: patiently, along every line, and then, at 1949, stopped.

October. The register for the autumn term of 1949 had a gap. Not a name crossed out — St. Aldhelm's had its share of ordinary departures, transfers, illnesses, and these were all dutifully annotated in the margins by teachers with initials and dates. This was different. This was four names, in a row, with nothing beside them at all. No annotation. No transfer code. Just a blank space where the explanation should be, and then — Vesper checked three times, heart ticking — the register resumed as though nothing had happened. The next page began mid-alphabet, as if the four names had been excised and the seam smoothed over.

Vesper photographed the page. Then the page before. Then, because the hands had started to shake slightly, the whole ledger, front to back, forty-eight exposures, the shutter clicking in the silence like a small, insistent heartbeat.

Four names. October. 1949.

Vesper pulled the 1924 ledger down next — the school's founding year — and read the autumn term twice. Nothing. Clean. But 1925 — the *second* year — had three names gone in October, same blankness, same smoothed-over seam, and Vesper sat back on the cold metal chair and looked at the ceiling and breathed.

Three names. October. 1925.

Twenty-four years apart. Almost a quarter century.

Vesper did not let herself feel anything yet. Feeling came later, at home, in the dark, where it always came. In the archive there was only the work. Pull the next ledger. 1974.

The 1974 register was typed — a clattering Remington had done the honors, and the letters were uneven, the *e*'s riding high — and the autumn term of 1974 contained a wound so obvious it made Vesper's throat close. Five names. October. No annotations. And beside the register, clipped to the inside cover with a rusted paperclip, a memo on school letterhead, typed and initialed by a headmaster whose name Vesper did not recognize: *Regarding the recent reorganization of the third form, the affected families have been notified and no further correspondence will be entered into.*

*The affected families.* Vesper read the phrase until it stopped being words. Five names. October. 1974. Twenty-five years after 1949. Forty-nine years after 1925.

Vesper did the arithmetic three times, because arithmetic was the only thing in the room that could be trusted, and each time the numbers came out the same: every twenty-five years, in October, in the autumn term, names went into the registers of St. Aldhelm's and did not come out.

And 1999.

Vesper knew about 1999 already, in the way you know about a scar on your own body — the headmaster before Corvane, the one who had 'resigned suddenly,' the rumors Vesper had dismissed as school folklore. Vesper pulled the ledger and found the autumn term of 1999 and read it standing up, because sitting felt suddenly too much like rest. Six names. October. No annotations. And in the margin, in a different hand, in pencil, so lightly it was almost not there at all, someone had written: *it is nearly full.*

*It is nearly full.*

Vesper photographed the pencil note four times from four angles, as if the light might reveal a signature, a confession, a name. The light revealed nothing. The pencil note revealed everything: whoever had written it had known. Had *counted*. Had stood in this same cold room, or one like it, ninety-nine years into the school's history, and had understood that the vanishings were not random, were not tragedy, were not even, properly speaking, a mystery — they were a *tally*. A count being kept by something that did not keep registers, because it did not need them. Something that took in autumn, every twenty-five years, and stopped — Vesper felt the understanding arrive whole, cold, and complete, like a stone dropping into still water — stopped when it had taken one hundred.

One hundred names. One hundred children, every quarter century, and then silence for a generation while the school forgot, or pretended to forget, or was made to forget — and then the autumn came round again, and the taking resumed.

Vesper sat down. Stood up. Sat down again. The coffee had gone completely cold and Vesper drank it anyway, because the ritual of it steadied the hands.

Four cycles. 1925. 1949 — no. Vesper recalculated, mouth moving silently. 1925, then 1949 was only twenty-four years — but school years straddled the calendar, and if the count ran autumn to autumn, then 1925 to 1950 would be twenty-five, and the 1949 register's October entries might belong to the 1949–50 academic year. Vesper checked. They did. The ledger was organized by academic year. The pattern held. 1925. 1950. 1974 — twenty-four again, but the same straddle, the same academic-year drift. 1999. And now.

Now. This year. This October. This autumn term, in which Idris Osei had been marked absent and then unmade.

Vesper pulled the current register — the one on her desk upstairs, the one she'd photographed before and after — and laid the photographs out on the archive table in a row, and counted.

Not the names that had been erased. Vesper had only ever seen one erasure firsthand. But the *absences*. The strange, unexplained, unannotated absences that had been accumulating since September, the ones Corvane had waved away with his talk of fluctuating enrollment, the four empty desks in third-period English that no one would look at. Vesper had documented every one. Of course she had. Documentation was the only prayer she knew, and she had been praying all term without knowing to whom.

She counted the absences. Then counted them again, marking each on a fresh page, cross-referencing against the yearbook photographs, the staff memos, the bus rosters, the lunch accounts — the small, dumb, beautiful bureaucracy of a school, which kept records of everything, even the things it pretended not to see.

Four students unaccounted for since September. Idris made five.

And then, at the bottom of her own list, in her own handwriting, the entry she had been avoiding for two weeks: *Whitcombe, Gerald — teacher, resigned September 30th. No forwarding address. No exit interview. Personnel file missing.*

Five students and a teacher. Six.

Vesper sat very still in the cold room and let the arithmetic finish itself, because it was going to whether she permitted it or not.

The historical cycles averaged five to six names per autumn — 1925 had taken three before stopping (a small cycle, an incomplete one, Vesper noted, and filed the anomaly), 1950 had taken four, 1974 five, 1999 six. The counts varied. The *stopping point* did not. Every cycle ended at one hundred — she was sure of it now, sure in the way she was sure of her own name — because the pencil note said *it is nearly full*, and you did not write *nearly full* about a thing that stopped at six.

But nearly full meant the count did not begin at zero each cycle.

Vesper stared at the page. If the count did not reset — if the bell, or whatever kept the tally, carried its number forward across the centuries, across the gaps, across the generations of forgetting — then the six names taken this autumn were not the beginning of something. They were the *end* of something. The last six strokes of a tally that had been running since before the school had a name.

She needed to know how far along it was. She needed the earlier cycles' totals, the exact numbers, the missing pieces — and the archive did not have them. She had checked. The ledgers before 1924 were gone, transferred in the sixties to some county repository and lost, or destroyed, or *taken*, and the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome: *or never existed, because the school itself was built around something older.*

The lintel. Danny Okafor's photographs, which he'd pressed into her hands behind the gymnasium with the furtive urgency of a man passing contraband: a stone lintel stamped with the school's founding date, carved into stone that was fifty years older than the school itself. The school had not founded the thing beneath it. The thing beneath it had been waiting, and the school had been built on top of it like a lid on a well.

Vesper spread Danny's photographs across the table, next to the register photographs, next to her own notebooks, until the whole surface was a map of a hundred years of erasure, and stood over it with her hands flat on the cold wood, and did the final arithmetic.

Ninety-four names taken across four documented cycles — she counted them one by one, from the ledgers, name by name, four plus five plus six plus the partial three, and then the ones the damaged early pages wouldn't yield, the gaps she had to estimate, and the estimate came out between ninety and ninety-four, and she wrote *94* and circled it and hated it for being an estimate.

Plus six, this autumn. Idris. The four empty desks. Gerald Whitcombe.

One hundred.

Vesper's breath fogged faintly in the cold air of the archive, and for a long moment she could not move, could only stand there with her hands flat on the table like a woman bracing against a wind only she could feel.

The count stood at one hundred. It was finished. It was *over* — and yet the school stood, and the children slept in their beds, and Corvane smiled his smooth smile, and nothing had ended, nothing had convulsed, the world had not stopped.

Unless.

Unless the count was not yet at one hundred. Unless her estimate was wrong — ninety, ninety-four, the damaged pages, the gaps — unless the true number was ninety-five, ninety-six, and the tally was not finished but *nearly* finished, and the pencil note's *it is nearly full* was not a record of the past but a warning addressed, across a century, directly to her.

Vesper sat down and began again, name by name, cycle by cycle, slower now, checking each entry against the yearbooks, the admissions records, the parish rolls Danny had helped her copy from the church office — because if the number was ninety-six, then four names remained, and four names was a race, and races could be won. If the number was one hundred, then everything she'd done was already too late, and the only thing left was to know that, cleanly, and decide what a person did with the time after the end.

She worked until the small hours. She checked every name three times. She threw out the estimates and kept only what was documented — her religion, her rule, the law she had made for herself in the first week: *only what can be proven, only what can be shown, only what another person could look at and see the same thing.*

When she finished, the number at the bottom of the page, in her small, precise handwriting, was ninety-six.

Ninety-six names, across a century, taken in autumns, every twenty-five years, by something that lived beneath the school and kept its count in a register older than ink.

Ninety-six.

Which meant four.

Vesper looked at the number for a long time. Then she took a fresh page and wrote at the top of it, in letters that did not waver, because the hands had gone past shaking into something colder and steadier:

*Four remaining. Four names between us and whatever happens at one hundred. Find them. Find them before the bell does.*

She underlined it twice. She photographed the page. She locked the photographs in the fireproof box in her mind, and began, methodically, to make the list of everyone at St. Aldhelm's who had gone missing this autumn — not just the students, not just the staff, but anyone, anyone at all, whose name had appeared in one record and not in another, because the pattern was clear now, and the pattern had a shape, and the shape had a hunger, and the hunger was four names from being full.

Outside, far off across the dark grounds, the old bell tower stood against the sky, empty and disconnected these fifty years, its bell removed, its rope gone, its silence a lie the whole school told together.

Vesper packed the ledgers back onto their shelves in perfect order, because order was the last prayer left, and turned off the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the building breathe.

Then she climbed the stairs toward the cold and the morning, carrying the only map that existed of the thing beneath the school, and thinking, with a clarity that felt almost like peace: *four names. I can find four names. I can find them faster than it can.*

She did not yet know that one of the four had been in the building all along, sweeping its corridors every night, keeping its secrets, holding in a locked drawer the one ledger older than all of these.

She did not yet know that the bell — disconnected, removed, gone — had already begun, in the dark beneath the gymnasium, to count.
