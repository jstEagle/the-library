## 9. The Buried Wing

The air that came out of the door was warm.

That was the first wrong thing. Danny had described the draft at dawn as cold — chalk dust and cellar chill — but that had been hours ago, before the bolts withdrew, before the corridor beyond had decided, apparently, to make itself comfortable. Now the air rising through the excavation pit smelled of floor wax and pencil shavings and something faintly sweet, like the inside of a violin case. It smelled, Vesper thought, like the last ten minutes of a school day, when the light goes amber and the building itself seems to exhale.

"You don't have to go in," Danny said. He was crouched at the lip of the bronze door, camera hanging forgotten against his chest. Behind him, Marta stood at the far edge of the pit with her arms wrapped around herself, as far from the opening as the shoring would allow. "Okafor's rule of excavations: anything that's warm underground is either a pipe or a problem."

"Or a classroom," Vesper said.

They had brought lights. Danny had insisted — two headlamps and a work lamp on a cord, because whatever the wing was, it was still, in some sense, a basement. Vesper took the lamp. The cord played out behind them, a lifeline of yellow extension cable, absurd and comforting, the most ordinary object in the world trailing into the least ordinary place.

The threshold was a foot of dressed stone, and then the floor changed to linoleum — the same honey-colored linoleum as the east wing above, the same worn path polished into it by decades of feet. Vesper's headlamp found the corridor walls: plaster, wainscoting, a fire-notice in a plastic frame. The notice was yellowed. The evacuation plan printed on it showed a floor plan of St. Aldhelm's that matched the east wing exactly, except that the stairwell at its end descended, in this version, one flight further than any stairwell Vesper had ever climbed.

The corridor was lit. Not brightly — a soft, sourceless daylight, the kind that comes through overcast windows, and when Vesper turned and looked, there were the windows. Tall, arched, set into the left-hand wall at intervals of exactly fifteen feet, exactly where the east wing's windows stood fifteen feet above a foundation that had no business containing windows at all.

Through the glass was a sky. Grey-white, evenly clouded, without a single seam or drift. Vesper watched it for a full minute. Nothing moved. Not the clouds, not the light, not a bird, not a leaf. It was not a view of the sky. It was a photograph of the sky, printed at the scale of the horizon, and the light it shed was as steady and as dead as the light in a dentist's office.

"Same day," Danny said quietly. He had raised the camera, and Vesper heard the shutter click three times. "The day it was built, maybe. The day they closed it. It's been that afternoon in here for fifty years."

Vesper wrote it down. The notebook came out of their coat the way it always did, without decision, the pen already uncapped. *Windows: overcast, static. Light: constant. No shadows move.* The pen felt heavier here. That was superstition — Vesper knew it was superstition — but the knowledge didn't help. Above ground, the notebook was a record. Down here, Vesper could not shake the feeling that it was a conversation, and that something on the other side of the page was answering in a voice too low to hear.

They walked. The corridor ran forty meters and turned, and the turn was the turn outside Room 114, and Room 114 was there, door open, exactly as it stood above — except that above, Room 114's door had a brass number screwed on crooked, and here the number was straight, and newer, and there was a small paper sign taped beside it at a child's eye level. A drawing. A door, with a handle on the inside, and a stick figure waving through the little window.

Vesper stopped. Danny's lamp swung up beside theirs.

"That's his," Danny said. "That's the drawing from the locker. Or it's — it's the same drawing."

"It's the same hand," Vesper said. The letters on the sign were careful, uneven, a nine-year-old's best printing: *WELCOME.* Below it, smaller, as though added later by a different, older hand: *we kept your seat.*

The classroom door stood open on desks in rows. On each desk, an exercise book, open. A lesson half-finished: *Compare the two poems. Which speaker do you believe, and why?* The handwriting in the nearest book was a child's, three lines written and a fourth abandoned mid-word. The pencil lay across the page where it had been set down, not dropped — set down, with the small deliberation of someone who intended to come back after break.

Vesper did not touch it. Vesper's whole life was touching things — turning pages, lifting registers, pressing flowers flat between leaves of record — but here, in this room where everything had been kept ready, touching felt like trespass. Like walking into a house where the family had only just stepped out, and the kettle was still warm.

"Vesper." Danny's voice had gone strange. He was standing by the teacher's desk, and he had turned his camera around to show the screen.

The date stamp read 1974.

"It's been doing this since the door opened," he said. "Every shot. Look — the room, the books, it's all —" He scrolled. Every frame, the same soft light, the same grey sky through the same windows, and the colors of everything slightly faded at the edges, the way prints fade in an album. "The camera thinks it's 1974. Or the room does, and the camera's just being polite."

"Put it away," Vesper said, and was surprised by their own voice — not fear, exactly. A kind of courtesy, extended to the room.

They went deeper. The wing unfolded with the patient logic of a building that had been copied by something that loved it: corridor, stairwell, corridor. The stairwell descended the extra flight from the fire notice, down into a lower hall where a trophy case held cups engraved with names Vesper did not know and dates from years that had never been on any calendar — 1951, 1938, 1926 — the founding date from the lintel, and before it, and before it, a school that had always been here, buried, waiting for the one above to grow down and meet it.

The classrooms were all the same and all different. Same desks, same half-finished lessons, same pencils laid down with the promise of return. In one, a geography map of Europe hung slightly askew, its borders decades out of date. In another, a reading corner with cushions gone flat in the shape of the children who had sat there. In every room, the small paper signs. *WELCOME. WE KEPT YOUR SEAT. YOU'RE LATE BUT THAT'S ALRIGHT.* The handwriting changed from sign to sign — different children, different years — but the sentiment never varied, and it was that, more than the dead sky or the 1974 photographs, that made the back of Vesper's neck prickle. Not menace. *Anticipation.* The wing had been expecting someone. For a long time. With the terrible patience of a thing that has nothing else to do.

They found the dormitory wing last, or it found them — a corridor of small rooms with narrow beds, each made with hospital corners, each with a folded change of clothes on the chair, each with a name taped to the door in a child's printing. Vesper walked the corridor slowly, reading the names, and their pen hovered and did not write, because writing them down felt like ringing a dinner bell, and Vesper was suddenly, unreasonably certain that everything down here was hungry in the gentlest possible way.

Halfway down the corridor, a door stood ajar, and from behind it came a sound that did not belong to any photograph of 1974.

Humming.

Danny's hand closed on Vesper's shoulder. Marta, who had followed them down after all — Marta, who had sworn at the threshold that she would not set one foot inside, was somehow three steps behind them, her face grey, her lips moving around what might have been a prayer or an apology — whispered: "That's a child. There's a child in there. Fifty years, and there's a *child*—"

The humming stopped.

Footsteps, light, on linoleum. The door opened the rest of the way.

Idris Okafor stood in the doorway.

Vesper had seen his school photograph eleven days ago, in the file that no longer existed: a small boy, all elbows and ears, gap-toothed, nine years old. The boy in the doorway was taller. His hair had grown out into curls that no one had trimmed. His school jumper was the right uniform, the right house color, and it fit him, and it had been mended at one elbow with stitches in a slightly different wool — mended recently, mended *here*, by hands that had kept him clothed and cared for through weeks that had passed for him like weeks.

He was perhaps eleven. He had been gone for four months.

"Hello," Idris said. He looked at Vesper with the frank, assessing gaze of a child sizing up a supply teacher, and then he nodded, satisfied, as if a question had been answered correctly. "You're the substitute. Miss Adeyemi said you'd come. She said, *one day someone will come through the door and they'll have a notebook, and that will be you.*" He frowned slightly, the way children frown when reciting something they've been told rather than understood. "She said you write everything down. She said that's how we'll know you're real."

Vesper's pen was in their hand. Vesper did not remember taking it out.

"Idris," Vesper said. "Idris Okafor. Year Five. Marked absent on the fourteenth of October. Your mother reported —" The sentence died. What could it say? *Your mother reported you missing, and the school told her you had never been enrolled, and she began to doubt her own photographs.* "Your mother misses you."

"I know." He said it without drama, the way children say the enormous things. "I miss her. But it's alright here. We have lessons." He stepped back from the door, an invitation, a host. "You came at a good time. It's nearly break. Do you want to see the common room? We've got a table tennis table. Danny —" and he turned to Danny with a smile of pure recognition, and Danny made a sound like a man being struck, "— you found the door. Well done. We could hear the digging for ages. Miss Adeyemi said, *they'll get through when they're meant to.*"

"Idris." Danny crouched, slow, as though approaching something wild, though the boy was nothing but a boy, gap-toothed still, one front tooth newly arrived and slightly too big. "Son. It's me. It's your — I'm Danny. Danny Okafor."

"I know," Idris said kindly. "You're the foreman. You dig things up. You're not supposed to be down here, though." He said it without reproach, the way a child reports a rule. "Visitors have to be remembered properly or the bell gets confused." He glanced down the corridor, toward the buried heart of the wing, where Vesper now noticed, for the first time, a sound so faint it might have been the building settling, or a clock, or a pulse: a low, bronze hum, felt in the teeth more than heard. "It's been counting. Miss Adeyemi says it's nearly finished. She gets sad when she says it, so don't ask her."

"Counting what?" Vesper asked.

Idris looked at them with mild surprise, the way a child looks at an adult who has asked why the sky is up.

"Us," he said. "It counts us. That's how we stay." He smiled then, gap-toothed and warm, and held the door wider, and the light from the dead windows lay on him like the light of a photograph, and Vesper understood with a clarity that felt like falling that this was the horror, that this had been the horror all along: not the dark, not the sealed bolts, not the tally marks scratched by desperate fingers. The horror was a mended jumper. The horror was a table tennis table and a kept seat and a child who was *alright here.* The horror was hospitality, offered without condition, by something that had been keeping its guests for fifty years and had every intention of keeping them forever.

"Miss Adeyemi's in her classroom," Idris said. "She's been getting ready for you. She'll want to meet you before break ends." He paused, and his face did something old — a flicker of the adult he would never be permitted to become, or had already, in here, slowly become. "She said to tell you: *bring the register. He'll understand what it's for.*"

Down the corridor, in a classroom with a dead grey sky in its windows, a light was on.

Vesper wrote it down. The pen shook, and Vesper let it shake, and the words went onto the page anyway, because that was the whole of who Vesper was: *She is expecting me. She has been expecting me for three months. She knows my name.*

The bell hummed, low and patient, somewhere in the walls.

It sounded, Vesper thought, like a throat clearing before a count.
