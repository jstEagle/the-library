## 17. Priya Runs

Priya had been counting the doors.

That was the thing nobody understood about her — not the teachers at St. Aldhelm's, not her mother, who called her *difficult* the way other mothers said *asthmatic*, like a condition to be managed. Priya counted things because counting was how you kept the world from lying to you. Fourteen steps from the gate to the front door of their house. Eleven tiles in the bathroom, one cracked. Three hundred and sixty-four days since Arjun walked to school and did not walk home, and everyone — everyone — began saying his name in the past tense.

So she counted the doors in the buried wing as she sat on the floor of the corridor outside Miss Adeyemi's classroom, knees pulled up, watching the adults argue in low voices near the bronze door far behind her. There were nine doors on this side of the corridor. She was sure of it. She had counted them twice, once with her eyes and once by touching each handle, cold brass under her fingers like the noses of sleeping animals.

There were eleven now.

She counted again. Twelve.

The corridor was breathing them into existence the way her breath made ghosts on cold glass — quietly, without effort, without caring whether she watched. And down at the far end, past the multiplying doors, past the classroom where the overcast light never changed, she could see him. Arjun. Fifteen years old and still fifteen years old, sitting at a desk with a half-finished maths worksheet in front of him, chewing the end of his pen the way he'd chewed it every night at the kitchen table while their mother pretended not to watch him with that soft, ruined look.

He looked up. He saw her. He smiled.

Priya stood up so fast the world tilted.

* * *

She didn't remember deciding to run. Her body decided for her, the way bodies do when they've been holding a scream for a year. Her shoes slapped the linoleum — the same linoleum, she realized wildly, the same scuffed grey-and-cream pattern as the corridor above, as if the wing had been built from a memory of the school rather than the school itself — and the doors blurred past her, handles winking, and she counted them without meaning to, seven, eight, nine, ten, and there had only been twelve a moment ago, there had only been—

Arjun's classroom door was open. Of course it was open. Everything here was open, welcoming, hospitable, a hotel where no one ever checked out. She burst through it and he was rising from his desk, pen still in hand, worksheet fluttering to the floor, and his face —

His face was the worst part. Not frightened. Not confused. *Pleased.* The face of someone whose package has finally arrived.

"Priya?" he said, and his voice hadn't changed at all, hadn't aged a single day, and that broke something loose in her chest that she'd been keeping bolted since the funeral-that-wasn't-a-funeral, since the memorial service where the headmaster spoke about him in that smooth oiled voice and her mother gripped her hand so hard the bones sang. "Priya, you're — how are you *here*?"

"You're coming home," she said. "Right now. Get your stuff."

"Get my—" He laughed, and the laugh was Arjun's laugh, warm and startled, and it hurt worse than crying would have. "Priya, I don't have stuff. I mean — I have stuff, but it's all..." He gestured vaguely at the desk, the room, the unchanging grey light pressing against windows that showed nothing but cloud. "It's all here."

"Then leave it." She grabbed his wrist. His skin was cool, cool like the corridor air that smelled of chalk dust, and underneath the coolness he was still solid, still her brother, still the boy who'd taught her to ride a bike by running alongside and letting go without telling her. "Mum's alive. She's — she waits by the phone every night, Arjun, she still sets a plate out sometimes, she'll deny it but I've *seen*—"

"Priya." Gently, the way you'd calm an animal. "You shouldn't be here."

"Don't tell me what I shouldn't be." Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated being fourteen, hated being small, hated that her hand shook around his wrist. "I counted the days. Three hundred and sixty-four. Tomorrow it's a year and I'm not doing a year, I'm not doing the stupid assembly where they say your name and everyone does that sad face, I'm taking you *home*."

Something moved in his expression then — a flicker, quick as a fish turning — and for just a second he looked past her shoulder toward the door, toward the corridor, and she saw fear there after all. Real fear. Fear with a direction.

"Priya," he said, very quietly. "How did you get in? Did someone bring you?"

"Danny. And Marta. And the new teacher, Nakamura, they're all back by the door arguing about—"

"*Nakamura.*" He said the name strangely, tasting it. "That name's been going round. Everyone keeps... hearing it." He frowned, and Priya watched with a creeping horror as her brother's face did something faces weren't supposed to do: it went briefly blank, like a screen losing signal, and then reassembled itself. "Sorry. What were we — you said Mum sets a plate?"

"Arjun."

"I'm fine," he said, too fast. "I'm fine, it's just, sometimes names go through me, it's nothing, it's actually quite peaceful once you—"

"We're leaving." She hauled on his arm. He weighed more than she remembered, or she was weaker than she remembered, grief had been eating her all year like a moth in a jumper. "Come *on*."

And that was when the corridor changed.

* * *

She felt it before she saw it — a pressure in her ears, like descending in a plane. When she dragged Arjun to the classroom door and looked out, the corridor was longer.

Not much longer. Twenty feet, maybe thirty. But wrong the way a funhouse mirror is wrong: the proportions subtly off, the ceiling a fraction lower, the doors multiplied along its length until they stood shoulder to shoulder like a crowd. Doors where there had been wall. Doors where there had been windows showing the eternal overcast. Every handle turned toward her, every door slightly ajar, and from the gaps came smells — chalk dust, floor polish, school dinners, rain on tarmac — the whole archive of St. Aldhelm's breathing out at her.

"It does that," Arjun said behind her, flatly. "When someone tries to leave."

"You've tried?"

"Everyone tries. At first." He said it the way you'd say *everyone cries at weddings*. "The corridors get longer. Then you get tired. Then you stop trying, and honestly, Priya, it's not so bad, the lessons are easy, Miss Adeyemi takes care of us—"

"Miss Adeyemi resigned in September. It was in the newsletter. There was a card everyone signed."

"That's what they told you." Something hard came into his voice, the first hard thing she'd heard from him since she arrived, and it gave her hope precisely because it was angry. "They told you a lot of things, didn't they. They told you I transferred. They told you my records were a clerical error. They told you—" He stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "Sometimes I can't remember which parts are true. That's the trick of it. You hold on too tight to being angry and it wears a hole right through the memory, and then the bell..."

"The bell what?"

But he was already shaking his head, backing away from the doorway, and Priya made her decision the way she made all her decisions: completely, immediately, and without consulting anyone.

She ran.

She hauled him into the corridor and ran, dragging him, both hands locked around his forearm, and the corridor ran with them, lengthening beneath their feet like taffy, doors peeling open as they passed so that she caught glimpses — a dormitory with rows of small beds, children sleeping in perfect synchrony; a science lab where beakers held water that had been mid-pour for months; a stairwell going down into dark that smelled of wet stone — and every glimpse lasted exactly long enough to be refused. Behind them, the classroom door swung shut. Ahead, the corridor simply continued, generating itself, and the far end where the bronze door stood stayed exactly the same distance away no matter how fast she ran.

"No," Priya gasped. "No, no, no—"

"Priya, stop, it doesn't like it when—"

"I DON'T CARE WHAT IT LIKES!"

Her voice went down the corridor and came back wrong, flattened, as if the walls were drinking the echo. The lights — those gentle, sourceless fluorescents — dimmed a shade. Just a shade. Just enough to notice. Somewhere far behind them, deeper in the wing than any bell should reach, something began to hum. A gathering sound. A throat clearing.

Then she heard running footsteps behind them, and Danny Okafor's voice, hoarse and furious: "*PRIYA!*"

* * *

He caught them at the corner where the corridor bent — except it hadn't bent before; she would have counted the corners, she counted everything — and his hands closed on her shoulders, big builder's hands, calloused and shaking.

"What did I say," he panted. "What did I *say*. Stay by the classroom. Stay where we could see you."

"He's my brother!" She twisted in his grip. "You've got a camera full of proof and a ledger and whatever plan Nakamura's got, and meanwhile he's been *here*, Danny, he's been here for a YEAR—"

"And if you pull him out the wrong way, you know what happens?" Danny's voice dropped, and it was worse than shouting. He crouched to her level, and up close she could see he hadn't slept, that his eyes were rimmed red, that his hands weren't shaking from anger alone. "Marta remembers what happens. She was here thirty years ago. The last person who tried to take someone out by force—" He stopped himself. Swallowed. "It doesn't end well. The wing doesn't lose. It just adds."

Arjun had gone very quiet beside her. When she looked at him, he was staring down the corridor the way they'd come, and his face had that flickering quality again, signal lost, signal found.

"There was a boy," he said slowly. "Before me. Someone tried to carry him out. I think — I think we all agreed to forget it, because remembering it made the nights longer." He blinked. "Why am I telling you this? Why do I keep *telling* you things—"

"Because I'm your sister and you trust me."

"Because you keep *looking* at me," he said, and there was wonder in it, and terror, "and every time you do, it's like — like waking up. Like the fog clears for a second and I remember there's an outside, there's a kitchen, there's a crack in the bathroom tile, third one from the door, you used to—"

"—sit on it so it wouldn't pinch," Priya whispered.

The humming behind them swelled.

* * *

Danny got an arm around each of them and started moving them back the way he'd come, fast, not running — running was forbidden now, she understood that instinctively, the wing punished running — but walking with terrible purpose, the way you walk when the sea is coming in. The corridors fought them. Doors opened in walls that should have been solid, offering shortcuts that Priya knew better than to trust; the floor tilted almost imperceptibly uphill so that every step cost double; and the light dimmed another shade, and another, until the fluorescents were the colour of old teeth.

"How did you even find us?" Danny muttered.

"I followed the smell of chalk," Priya said. "It gets stronger the closer you get to the classrooms. Didn't anyone else notice?"

Danny glanced down at her, and for a moment something like respect crossed his exhausted face. "You count things, don't you. Nakamura said you might. Said your file was full of teacher comments — *distractible, obsessive, fixates on details*." He almost smiled. "Best damn skill set in this building."

Behind them, the humming resolved into a tone. A single note, deep and bronze, felt in the sternum more than heard. The whole wing leaned toward it, the way plants lean toward a window.

"That's early," Arjun breathed. His hand had found hers without either of them deciding it, the way it used to cross the road. "Danny, that's *early*. It's not supposed to — Marta's toll was three days ago, it waits, it always waits—"

"Maybe it's done waiting," Danny said grimly.

The note swelled again, closer now, and Priya felt it pass through her like a hand through smoke, and everywhere around them the wing *shivered*. Doors rattled in their frames. In the dormitory they passed, the sleeping children turned over in unison, forty small bodies moving as one, and did not wake. The false daylight beyond the impossible windows darkened like a bruise.

"Ninety-eight was Marta," Danny said, mostly to himself, counting on fingers scarred by twenty years of scaffolding. "Ninety-eight. Which means this is—"

"Ninety-nine," Arjun whispered.

The toll came.

It wasn't a sound so much as a verdict. It rolled up through the floor, through the soles of Priya's shoes, through her bones, and every door in the corridor slammed shut at once — a gunshot volley of oak and brass — and in the sudden silence afterward, the wing exhaled. The light steadied. The corridors stopped growing. Somewhere far away, in the dark above the buried ceilings where no stairs led, the great bell hung silent again, satisfied, patient, *hungry*.

One name remained.

Priya stood in the dim corridor holding her brother's hand, and she understood — with the cold, total clarity of a girl who counts everything — what one name meant. It meant the adults' arguments were over. It meant the ledger and the plans and the sacrifices-being-negotiated-in-whispers had all collapsed into a single night. It meant that somewhere in this wing, someone was going to stand in front of that bell and feed it a whole life, every memory, every name, until it choked — and that everyone here, the living and the looping alike, was looking at the clock.

"One toll left," Arjun said. He wasn't looking at the dark. He was looking at her, and his grip on her hand tightened, and his eyes were clear — fully, painfully clear — for perhaps the last time. "Priya. Listen to me. When it rings tomorrow — whatever Nakamura told you, whoever walks into that belfry — you make sure someone writes down what really happened. Promise me. It's the only thing that survives this place. Being written down."

Above them, faint as a heartbeat, the bell began, very softly, to hum again — warming up, getting ready, learning the shape of the name it had been promised.

Tonight, Danny said. It had to be tonight.
