## 1. Head Count

The bus to Hollowmere ran once a day, and it was late.

Bram Kavanagh watched the fog come down off the hills in long gray sheets, folding over the road like something being tucked in for the night. The driver — a heavyset man with a face like a closed door — hadn't spoken since they left the junction forty minutes ago. When Bram had asked how much farther, he'd gotten a shrug and a grunt, which Bram chose to interpret as *not far* rather than *don't ask again*.

The sign when it finally appeared was older than Bram expected, paint flaking off letters that read WELCOME TO HOLLOWMERE — POP. 1,204. Someone had scratched out the number and written nothing beneath it. Just a raw groove in the metal, as if whoever held the knife had changed their mind about what should go there.

"School's up the hill," the driver said, the first full sentence of the trip. "You'll want your coat."

"I have my coat."

"You'll want it anyway."

He wasn't wrong. The fog wasn't like fog anywhere else Bram had been. It didn't drift; it *arrived*, settling on skin with a damp weight that felt almost deliberate, like a hand checking whether you were solid. Bram pulled their collar up and started walking.

The town itself was pretty in the way old photographs are pretty — everything slightly softened, slightly out of focus, as if Hollowmere couldn't quite commit to being fully present. Stone cottages with slate roofs. A church spire poking through the mist like a needle through cloth. A main street with three shops, a post office, and a bakery whose window display looked untouched since some earlier decade. No litter. No loitering teenagers. No sound except their own footsteps and, somewhere behind the houses, water moving — a river or a stream or something larger, going about its business in the dark.

Bram had taken the job the way people take jobs they need: quickly, gratefully, without asking too many questions. Twelve years teaching English in cities that kept eating themselves and rebuilding uglier, two schools closed out from under them, one marriage dissolved amicably into silence. When the listing appeared — *Hollowmere Secondary, permanent post, housing provided* — Bram had read it three times looking for the catch.

There had to be a catch. Towns like this didn't advertise for teachers. Towns like this grew them from seed.

But the salary was real, the cottage came with the post, and the reference check had come back within an hour, which should have been the first warning. Nobody checks references that fast unless they're afraid you'll change your mind.

Hollowmere Secondary sat at the top of the hill like a headstone. It was a long, low building of gray stone, built in stages over a century, each addition slightly mismatched with the last, so the whole thing looked like a sentence someone had kept adding clauses to without ever finding a period. Ivy climbed the east wing. The windows were tall and dark, and though it was barely four o'clock, half of them were already lit — warm yellow squares floating in the fog, like the building was showing its teeth.

A woman was waiting at the front doors. She was small and square-shouldered, maybe fifty, with iron-gray hair cut short and reading glasses pushed up into it. Her cardigan was buttoned to the throat despite the fact that she was standing indoors.

"Bram Kavanagh." She said it as a statement, not a question, and offered a hand dry and firm as a ledger's spine. "Marguerite Osgood. Vice principal. Welcome to Hollowmere."

"Thank you. It's — quieter than I expected."

"It usually is." She turned and led them inside before Bram could decide whether that was a joke.

* * *

The interior smelled of floor wax and chalk dust and, underneath both, something faintly mineral. Damp stone. Old water. The corridors were wide and high-ceilinged, lined with photographs of graduating classes going back decades, row upon row of solemn faces in Sunday clothes. Bram slowed in front of one from 1983. The class was small — maybe thirty students — and every single child wore the same expression: not unhappiness exactly, but a kind of listening. As if the photographer had asked them all to be quiet at the same moment and none of them had ever quite stopped.

"Staff room's through there," Osgood said, not slowing. "Your classroom is Room 4, end of the east corridor. Keys are on your desk. Timetable's pinned above the board. Any questions?"

"A few. Class sizes—"

"Small." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *expensive*. "You'll have nineteen on your register across four year groups. English is consolidated here; we run a compact program."

"Nineteen. In a school this size?"

"The school serves the whole valley." She opened a door onto a small office — hers, evidently, given the wall of filing cabinets and the desk so tidy it looked staged. On the desk, facing the door, sat a thick green book.

Osgood picked it up and held it out.

"Attendance ledger," she said. "You'll take your own register daily, but the master copy lives with me. I'm showing it to you now because new staff always ask eventually, and I'd rather you asked me than asked each other."

Bram took it. It was heavier than it looked, bound in green cloth gone soft at the corners, and it fell open naturally near the middle, the way well-used books do.

The pages were ruled in columns: name, year, date, mark. Present marks were neat ticks in blue ink. And the absences —

The absences were lines. Single horizontal strokes drawn through names in red ink, one per child, clean and final, like cuts. Not *absent*. Not *left*. Not *transferred*. Just struck through, the name beneath still legible but cancelled, the way you'd cancel a cheque.

Bram turned back a page. More red lines. Forward. More. They thumbed toward the front of the book, past 2019, past 2015, past a decade of entries, and the red strokes kept coming — sparse at first, one or two a term, then more frequent, then, in certain years, clustered so thick the columns looked wounded.

"How many students does this school actually have?" Bram asked.

"Currently? Nineteen on your register. Forty-one enrolled total."

"And how many—" Bram weighed the book in their hand. "How many names have been crossed out?"

Osgood took the ledger back with both hands, gently, the way you'd take a photograph of someone dead from a grieving relative.

"Families move," she said. "Rural districts see turnover. People go somewhere better."

*Somewhere better.* The phrase landed strangely, and Bram couldn't have said why — only that it sounded rehearsed, worn smooth, like a stone passed hand to hand until it lost its edges. Like everyone in Hollowmere had been handed the same sentence and told to carry it.

"Of course," Bram said. Professional. Pleasant. The voice they used for parents at open nights and inspectors and anyone whose worry might become their problem. "I just want to make sure I understand the community I'm joining."

"You will." Osgood slid the ledger into a drawer and locked it — actually locked it, a small brass key from her chain — and when she looked up her expression had rearranged itself into something almost warm. Almost. "You come recommended, Mr.— Ms.—"

"Bram is fine."

"Bram." She tried it out, found it acceptable. "We've had teachers leave mid-term before. It reflects poorly on the children. Consistency matters here. Routine matters. The children respond to routine."

"What kind of routine?"

"Assembly, Monday mornings, eight sharp. All students attend. All staff attend." A pause, fractional, but Bram caught it — a hitch in the rhythm, like a skipped stitch. "You'll see."

"That's not quite what I asked."

"No," Osgood agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

She walked Bram out then, back along the corridor of staring class photographs, and at the doors she stopped and turned, and for a moment the fog outside pressed against the glass behind her like something leaning in to listen.

"Two things," she said. "Housekeeping, really. First — the east wing closes at six. The boiler room, the old chapel storage, all of it. Keep out. Structural survey pending. Second—"

She hesitated. For the first time since the bus stop, Marguerite Osgood looked less like an administrator and more like a woman standing in a doorway at night, listening to her own house creak.

"Keep your doors counted and your lights on," she said. "Both of them."

"Both of what?"

"Doors. Lights." She smiled, and it reached her eyes the way frost reaches a window — from the outside in. "You'll find the habit useful. Everyone here does. Goodnight, Bram. Cottage key's under the mat. Number four, Church Row. You can't miss it — it's the one with the porch light working."

* * *

The cottage was small and cold-smelling, all dark beams and low ceilings, furnished by someone who had never met its tenant: a sofa, a table, two chairs, a bed made up with sheets still creased from their packet. Bram set down their bags, found the fuse box, and went room to room switching on every light — hallway, kitchen, bedroom, even the little box room that would serve as a study. Then, remembering Osgood's face, they went back and checked the front door was locked, then the back, then stood in the middle of the sitting room feeling faintly ridiculous, a grown adult counting doors in an empty house.

Four doors, including the cupboard under the stairs. Three windows with curtains. One porch light, switched on, throwing a pale cone into the fog where the garden should have been.

Through it, Bram could just make out the shape of the hills, and below them, at the bottom of the town, the glint of water. The quarry, according to the map in the bus station — disused, fenced off, half-full. Children were warned away from it, presumably. Children were always warned away from water.

They ate toast standing up. They unpacked one bag and left the other for tomorrow. And all evening, while the fog pressed its face against the windows and the house ticked and settled around them, Bram found their mind returning to the same image: the green ledger falling open in their hands, the red strokes running down the columns like rain down glass.

Families move. People go somewhere better.

Nineteen students. Forty-one enrolled. And a book full of names that no one would explain, crossed out in ink the color of something bleeding slowly.

At ten o'clock, writing lesson plans they weren't reading, Bram heard it for the first time — faint, distant, coming from the direction of the school on the hill. A sound they'd have called wind if wind could keep time. Rising, falling, rising again. Voices, maybe. Or one voice, doubled and doubled until it became many.

It stopped at what felt, absurdly, like the end of a line. Mid-phrase. Cut off, deliberately, the way you'd stop yourself saying something dangerous.

Bram sat very still in the lamplight, pen hovering, and listened to the silence where the sound had been.

Then they got up, checked the doors again — four — counted the lights — six — and went to bed with the lamp burning, telling themselves it was diligence. Professional habit. Nothing more.

In the morning, nineteen names waited on a register, and Bram Kavanagh intended to learn every one of them, and to notice — carefully, quietly, professionally — exactly which desks stayed empty.
