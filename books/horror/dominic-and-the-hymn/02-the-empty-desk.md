## 2. The Empty Desk

The first thing I learned about Hollowmere was that the fog didn't lift so much as it got bored and moved to another street.

I'd been teaching for six years before I came here — two in Leeds, four at a comprehensive outside Manchester where the worst thing that ever happened was a boy setting fire to the microwave with a fork. I knew how schools smelled: floor polish, cheap deodorant, the sweet rot of forgotten packed lunches. St. Brannoc's Academy smelled like all of those things too, but underneath it there was something else, something mineral and cold, like the air that comes up from a cellar when you open a door you've never opened before.

Wren Halloway sat third from the window in my Year Nine English class, and for the first five weeks she was my favorite kind of student — not the loudest or the cleverest, but the one who listened like listening was a skill she intended to master. She had a gap between her front teeth she covered with her hand when she laughed, which meant she spent a lot of time covering her mouth. She wrote her essays in blue ink because black ink was "a funeral color, sir," and she underlined nothing, because "if it matters you'll remember it."

On the Friday before half-term she handed me an essay on *Lord of the Flies* that stopped mid-sentence on page three. The sentence ended: *the boys stop being afraid of the beast and start being afraid of each other, which is worse, except in Hollowmere it's different because we already know what's under—*

And then nothing. Just the blue pen pressed hard into the paper, a small crater of frustration or hurry, and after it a doodle in the margin: a row of little vertical lines with horizontal strokes through them. It took me a moment, turning the page toward the window light, to recognize them as musical staves. Empty ones. Five lines, no notes.

"Run out of steam?" I'd asked her, handing it back.

She'd looked at the essay like she'd never seen it before. Then she laughed behind her hand and said, "Sorry, sir. I'll finish it after break."

She did not finish it after break.

* * *

Half-term week itself passed quietly. I stayed in my rented cottage on Veil Street — every street in Hollowmere is named for something the town would rather forget; there's a Drowned Lane and a Gallows Hill and a Sorrowfield Road, and nobody finds this strange but me — and I marked forty sets of books and read half a novel and let the fog press against the windows like a face.

When I came back on the Monday, the school felt wrong in a way I couldn't name until second period, when I took the register for 9B and reached Wren's name and the silence where her voice should have been lasted one beat too long.

"Wren Halloway?"

Nothing. Pens kept scratching. Someone coughed.

"Anyone know if Wren's off today?"

A girl in the front row — Maren Oduya, sharp-eyed, perpetually unimpressed — said, without looking up, "She moved away."

"Moved away? Over half-term?"

"That's what everyone says." And then, in exactly the same flat tone, as though reciting a line from a form letter: "Her family went somewhere better."

I waited for someone to laugh, or elaborate, or roll their eyes. Nobody did. Thirty faces looked back at me with the mild patience of people waiting for me to move on to the next name, and the horrible thing — the thing I still think about at two in the morning — is that none of them seemed sad. Not one. In Manchester, when a kid left mid-year, the class held a small funeral for them: cards, a shared bag of sweets, someone crying about the group project now doomed. Here, Wren Halloway's absence landed on 9B like a leaf on water. A ripple, then glass.

I marked her absent and told myself there were a hundred explanations. Families moved. Towns like Hollowmere were dying on the vine — I'd seen the boarded shops on the high street, the estate agent's window full of houses photographed in better weather. People left for work, for grandparents, for somewhere with functioning streetlights. Somewhere better.

That phrase again. Where had they all gotten the same words?

* * *

The desk was gone by Tuesday.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only weapon I had, early on, and I hoarded it. On Monday afternoon, Wren's desk was still there — third from the window, second row, its surface carved with a lopsided heart and the initials *WH + ?* — and her exercise book was still in the tray beneath it, because I'd checked. I'd stood at that desk for a full minute on Monday after school, holding her half-finished essay, telling myself to look through her other books for context, some note, some clue, and then deciding that would be invasive, inappropriate, the act of a paranoid newcomer, and I'd put the essay in my bag instead and gone home.

On Tuesday morning the desk was gone. Not just empty — gone. There was no gap in the rows, no rectangle of cleaner floorboard where it had stood. The desks were arranged in their usual horseshoe, twenty-nine of them, evenly spaced, and the arrangement looked natural, looked like it had always been twenty-nine, and when I counted twice and then a third time with my finger touching each desktop like a man checking his own pulse, Maren Oduya watched me do it and said, "Sir, are you okay?"

"Was there a desk here yesterday?" I asked. "Third from the window. Wren's desk."

Maren frowned — genuinely frowned, a real flicker of confusion crossing her face — and said, "Whose desk?"

And that was the moment. Not the missing girl. Not the identical phrases. That moment, standing in a warm classroom smelling of pencil shavings while a fourteen-year-old looked at me with honest bafflement, that was the first time I understood that Hollowmere was not a town that lost children. It was a town that erased them.

I said something bland — "Never mind, long week" — and taught the lesson, and taught it well, and my hands did not shake until I was alone in the corridor afterward, holding the doorframe, breathing like I'd run there.

* * *

I did what any reasonable person would do. I asked questions.

The school office has a card index of current students, updated termly. Wren Halloway's card was there — I saw it, blue-bordered, her date of birth, her address on Sorrowfield Road — but stamped across it in red, diagonally, was WITHDRAWN, and the date field read simply: *half-term.* No day. No reason. No forwarding address. When I asked Mrs. Tallow, the office administrator, a woman built like a warm loaf of bread who had worked at St. Brannoc's for thirty years, she smiled at me with genuine kindness.

"Gone somewhere better, love," she said. "Happens all the time, this time of year."

"All the time? How many left last year?"

The smile stayed exactly where it was. Nothing behind it moved. "Families come and go," she said. "You'll want to update your mark book, Mr. Kavanagh. Vice Principal Corrigan doesn't like discrepancies."

Vice Principal Corrigan was the one who'd warned me, my first week, in his office that smelled of wet carpet: keep your doors counted, keep your lights on, and don't be a hero about attendance. I'd thought he was eccentric. Towns develop folk wisdom; teachers inherit superstitions like furniture. Now his advice sounded less like folklore and more like a survival manual, and I hated him a little for knowing things he hadn't said.

I tried Wren's house. Sorrowfield Road, number eleven, a narrow terrace with a neat front garden and a wind chime made of spoons. The curtains were drawn. The garden was dead — not autumnal, not untended, but dead, brown to the roots in October, every plant, even the ivy. A neighbor across the road was washing her car in the drizzle, and when I called over the wall and asked if the Halloways had moved, she turned off the hose and gave me the smile. The same smile. The town's smile.

"Somewhere better," she said. "Lovely family. You'll want to speak to the school about pupil records, though. Data protection."

There was no van in the drive. No removal boxes visible through the frosted front-room glass. The milk bottles on the step had gone green with age.

I walked home through fog thick enough to chew, and I made myself a promise on Drowned Lane, out loud, to no one, which should have embarrassed me and didn't: *I am not going to look away from this.*

It's strange, the promises that end up costing everything. At the time it felt small. It felt like professionalism.

* * *

That night I sat at my kitchen table with Wren's essay under the lamp, and I finally read it properly — not as a teacher hunting for a grade, but as whatever I was becoming, which I didn't yet have a word for.

The assignment had been persuasive writing: *Is fear more powerful than reason? Discuss with reference to the text.* Most of the class had written competent garbage about Piggy and the conch. Wren had started the same way, and her opening paragraphs were good — genuinely good, the sentences of a kid who read past her reading level and resented being told to cite sources. But somewhere in paragraph four the essay stopped being about the book. I could see the exact line where it happened, because her handwriting changed, loosened, sped up, the way handwriting does when a person stops performing and starts confessing:

*Everyone says the island is made up but I think Golding knew about places like here. The boys stop being afraid of the beast and start being afraid of each other, which is worse, except in Hollowmere it's different because we already know what's under*

Under. Full stop. Pen-crease. As if she'd been interrupted — or had interrupted herself, the way you flinch away from a hot stove.

Below the abandoned paragraph, in smaller writing, squeezed into the margin as if it were too dangerous to give its own lines, she'd written:

*I hear it in assembly. Everyone sings the hymn and I can feel the floor humming under my shoes like the song goes DOWN instead of out. Miss Pryce says I'm imagining it but Miss Pryce hums it in the staff room when she thinks no one is listening and she always stops at the same place. They ALL stop at the same place. Why does everyone stop at the same place?*

*I found the pretty song under the floor. It knows my name.*

Then, underneath that, in handwriting gone suddenly careful, almost childlike, letters formed the way you form them when you're copying something exactly:

*Do not sing the last verse. Do not learn the last verse. If you can hear it learning you, cover your ears and count the doors.*

My skin did something it had never done before — a slow cold crawl from the base of my skull down between my shoulder blades, like a fingertip drawing a line. I sat in my kitchen on Veil Street with the lamp on and every light in the house burning, because some part of me had already obeyed the instruction without deciding to, and I read those last lines maybe thirty times.

*If you can hear it learning you.*

Not *if you can hear it.* If you can hear it *learning you.* A fourteen-year-old had understood, before she vanished, that the song wasn't something you listened to. It was something that listened back. Something that studied you. Learned you the way you learn a piece of music — repetition, refinement, until you could perform it perfectly.

And *count the doors.* Corrigan's rule. Keep your doors counted. The same instruction, decades apart, worn smooth like a stone passed hand to hand. Which meant Corrigan knew. Which meant the adults had all been given the same warning and had all decided the correct response was to pass it along in fragments and never, ever ask why.

I got up and counted the doors in my cottage. Front, back, four interior, the cupboard under the stairs. Seven. I counted them again. Seven. I stood in the hallway with my hand on the cold latch of the cupboard door, feeling like a madman, feeling like the sanest man in England, and I counted them a third time because counting was the only prayer anyone had taught me since I arrived.

Seven doors. All present. All shut.

From somewhere below the floor — below the kitchen, below the cellar hatch I hadn't opened, below the foundations, below sense — came a sound so faint I could have invented it. I've spent months deciding whether I did. A hum. Three notes, rising, gentle as a music box winding down. The kind of melody you'd call pretty, if you heard it anywhere else. Anywhere but under your feet at midnight in a town that erases its children.

It stopped before I could be sure.

I didn't sleep. I sat at the table until the window turned grey, Wren's essay in front of me, and when the fog thinned enough to show the hills I made two decisions. The first was that I would find out what happened to Wren Halloway, and I would not stop, and I would not be smiled at into silence, and if the whole town wanted to pretend she'd gone somewhere better then I would go somewhere worse — down, if I had to, following the song wherever it pooled.

The second decision I didn't make so much as notice I'd already made: I stopped singing in assembly. Permanently. Whatever that hymn was, whatever it carried in its verses like a river carries silt, I would stand among the singing children with my mouth closed and my eyes open, and I would watch.

Because here is what I couldn't stop thinking, as the grey morning came up over Veil Street: Wren had written *it knows my name,* and then she was gone, and her desk was gone, and her card was stamped, and thirty children had forgotten her face in a single weekend.

But I had her essay. Blue ink. Half-finished. Proof that she had existed, that she had feared, that she had counted doors.

They'd erased Wren Halloway everywhere except in the hands of the one adult stubborn enough to keep her homework.

At eight fifteen I walked to school through the fog with her essay in my bag against my ribs like a letter I was delivering on her behalf, and as I crossed the yard, the bell began to ring — and beneath the bell, faint and patient, from everywhere and nowhere, from under everything —

three notes, rising.

Pretty as anything.

Waiting to be finished.
