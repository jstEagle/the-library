## 5. Rules We Made

The rain hadn't stopped since the night on the porch. It came down in gray sheets that turned the world outside Bram's windows into something half-erased, and Dominic sat at their kitchen table like he'd always lived there — patched coat, too-wide smile, hands folded with the patience of someone who had waited thirty years and could wait thirty more.

Bram had not slept. They'd made tea they didn't drink. The mug sat cooling between them like a border neither wanted to cross.

"You're not going to ask me if I'm real," Dominic said.

"I already know the answer changes depending on the day."

"That's fair." He turned the mug a quarter-turn, so the handle faced Bram instead of him. A small courtesy. He'd done things like that when they were children — small courtesies, invisible to everyone else, as if he were practicing being a person. "Ask me something better."

So Bram did what any reasonable adult would do when confronted with an impossible boy on their porch: they tested him.

* * *

The quarry was behind the Kavanagh house in Ashford, back when Ashford still had a working mill and Bram still had two parents who spoke to each other. Bram was seven the summer they met him. He'd been standing at the edge of the water, which everyone said was bottomless, which everyone said had taken a boy some years before Bram was born — though no one could ever quite remember whose boy it was, or exactly when.

"You fell in," Dominic told them once. "Almost."

"I did not."

"You climbed down to look at the flooded machinery and the rock gave way. I caught your wrist." He held up his own wrist now, across the kitchen table, thirty years later, as if presenting evidence. "You never told anyone because you weren't supposed to be down there."

That was true. That was true, and Bram had never told a living soul, and the memory rose up whole and wet and terrifying: the green-black water, the rusted ladder rungs, the small strong grip that hauled them back from the edge of everything.

"Nobody knew," Bram said quietly.

"I knew. I was there." Dominic smiled his too-wide smile. "I'm always where the song is."

"The song."

"Hymn of Hollowmere. You've heard it. They sang it at assembly yesterday — you stopped halfway through the second verse. Good instinct, by the way."

Bram's skin prickled. "You were there?"

"I'm always there when it's sung. Every assembly, every funeral, every wedding where somebody's grandmother insists on the old hymns. I stand at the back and listen." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't match the lightness. Nothing about Dominic matched anything else about him; that was the whole problem with him, then and now. "It's how I find my way around. Like — you know how a dog can hear a whistle nobody else can? I hear the song. And where the song is sung, I can be."

"And where it isn't?"

His smile thinned. "Nowhere. I'm nowhere."

* * *

When Bram was eight, they made rules. Children do this with imaginary friends — it's how you keep the magic orderly, how you keep it from spilling into the parts of life where magic would be embarrassing. Bram had written them in crayon on the back of a wallpaper sample and pinned it inside their closet door:

1. DOMINIC CANNOT LIE.
2. DOMINIC CANNOT COME IN THE BATHROOM.
3. DOMINIC HAS TO LEAVE WHEN MOM CALLS ME FOR DINNER.
4. NO SINGING AFTER DARK.

Rule four had been Dominic's idea. Bram remembered that now, sitting in the Hollowmere kitchen with rain hammering the windows, remembering a summer evening when they'd been singing made-up songs on the porch steps and Dominic had gone very still, the way a rabbit goes still, and said, *not that one, don't finish that one,* and Bram had laughed and asked why, and Dominic had looked at the darkening yard and said, **because songs are doors, and some doors open outward.**

"What happened to rule four?" Bram asked aloud.

Dominic's head came up sharply. For a moment he looked genuinely frightened — the first honest expression Bram had seen on him since the porch. "You remember."

"Crayon. Inside the closet door. My mother painted over it when we moved."

"She painted over a lot of things." He leaned forward. "Listen to me, Bram. I need you to listen the way you listened when you were seven, before you learned that listening is embarrassing. The hymn they sing here — Hymn of Hollowmere — it has four verses."

"Three," Bram said. "The hymnal only has three. I checked."

"The hymnal is wrong on purpose." His voice dropped. "There's a fourth verse. Nobody sings it. Nobody is *allowed* to sing it. And you must never learn it — not the words, not the melody, nothing past the third verse. If someone hums it near you, walk away. If you catch yourself humming it, stop. Bite your tongue if you have to."

"Why?"

"Because the fourth verse isn't a verse. It's a net." He spread his hands. "Songs are doors, Bram. Most doors open both ways. That one only opens inward."

The word *inward* sat in the kitchen like a third guest. Outside, the rain kept up its gray arithmetic against the glass.

"The children who disappear," Bram said. "Wren Okafor. The others in the ledger."

Dominic said nothing. His silence had a texture to it — Bram remembered that too, the specific quality of a Dominic-silence, which was not emptiness but restraint, a held breath.

"You know something."

"I know rule one," he said finally. "I cannot lie. But nobody ever made a rule saying I have to answer."

* * *

They played the game after that, though neither called it a game. Bram circled the kitchen slowly, asking questions, watching him the way you watch a witness.

"What did I call the quarry water?"

"The tea. You said it looked like steeped tea, and I said it looked like drowned tea, and you cried because you thought that was funny and you didn't know why you were crying."

True. God help them, true.

"What did I break the summer I was nine?"

"My fault, technically. I dared you to jump the drainage ditch on Mrs. Pell's bicycle and you broke your arm in two places. You told your mother you fell off the curb. She believed you because she wanted to believe you were careful, and I sat with you in the emergency room under the fluorescent lights where I couldn't follow, and I waited outside for six hours, and when you came out with the cast you drew a little door on it and told me that was so I could get in."

Bram stopped circling. There was a tightness in their throat that had nothing to do with fear. "I'd forgotten the door."

"I didn't forget anything," Dominic said softly. "That's rather the whole trouble with me."

One more. The hardest one. Bram sat down across from him again and folded their hands the way he'd folded his, mirroring him without meaning to.

"Why did you leave? When I was eleven. One day you were there and then you were just — gone. No goodbye. I grieved you, Dominic. I grieved you like a death, and everyone told me I'd simply grown out of an imaginary friend, and I spent twenty years half-believing I'd invented the whole thing, invented *you*, and that grief was just embarrassment wearing a costume."

Dominic looked at his hands. When he spoke, each word came out carefully, like a man crossing ice.

"I didn't leave. The song left. Your school stopped singing — budget cuts, the music program ended, the assemblies got shorter. And I can only be where the hymn is sung." He raised his eyes. "I stood in an empty music room for three years waiting for someone to play the piano. Then even the piano was gone. And after that I was nowhere, Bram. Nowhere, and aware of it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I didn't abandon you. I was *unmade by neglect.*"

The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere down the street a car hissed through standing water.

"And then?" Bram whispered.

"And then three weeks ago, in a town called Hollowmere, four hundred children opened their mouths at morning assembly and sang the first verse of a hymn written in 1983 by a man named Aldous Vane. And somewhere in the nowhere, I heard it." His too-wide smile returned, but it trembled at the edges now, and Bram saw — really saw, for the first time since the porch — how much effort the smile cost him. "And I started walking. It took me three weeks to get here. I don't recommend the nowhere."

Bram laughed. It escaped before they could stop it, cracked and wet, half sob. Because that was the thing about Dominic — he had always been funny in the exact shape of the truth, and thirty years hadn't sanded that off him at all.

"I believe you," Bram said. "God help me, I believe all of it."

"Don't." He said it fast, sharp. "Believe the parts you've tested. Trust the rest provisionally. That's the deal we're making tonight — provisional trust, renewed daily, revocable at will. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Then here's your homework, teacher." He counted on his fingers, and Bram's chest ached at the gesture, because he'd done exactly that when they were children, laying out the rules of some game only the two of them could play. "One: keep counting heads. Whatever the vice principal says, count them yourself, every day, and write the numbers down somewhere he can't find. Two: stay in the room during assembly, but keep your mouth shut after verse two. Three—" he paused, and for a moment the flickering candle-flame of him seemed to gutter, "—three: whatever you start to feel when the whole school sings together, that pull in your chest, that wordless wanting — don't follow it. It isn't yours. It's the song, fishing."

"Fishing for what?"

"For people who'll sing the fourth verse without knowing they know it." He stood, and the kitchen light did something wrong around him, bending toward him slightly, like the light itself was leaning in to listen. "It's late. Rule three still applies, by the way — you should sleep, and I should... go wherever I go."

"Where do you go?"

"Nowhere," he said cheerfully. "It's not so bad. Lots of reading."

He was at the door when Bram caught him — not with a hand, because they suspected a hand would pass through, but with a question.

"Dominic. The boy in the quarry. Before me. The one who drowned."

He went very still.

"Was that you?"

The rain filled the silence. Dominic turned, and his face did something complicated — grief and guilt and love all trying to fit through the same too-small doorway.

"No," he said. "But I think I might be what's left of the song he drowned in."

And then the porch light buzzed, and he was gone, and Bram stood alone in the kitchen with cold tea and a head full of crayon rules, understanding suddenly, completely, that rule four had never been about protecting Bram at all.

It had been about protecting everyone else — from Bram.

Outside, far off toward the school, faint through the rain, someone was humming.
