## 15. Ellis Vane

The county archive opened at nine on a Tuesday, which meant I had four hours to kill and a parking meter that judged me by the minute. I fed it quarters and sat in the van listening to the tapes again, because apparently I'd become the kind of man who did that — who sat alone in a vehicle at seven in the morning listening to a dead child answer questions nobody had asked out loud, and thought of it as *preparation*.

There was a version of me, eleven years ago, who'd have called this a job. Find the pest, remove the pest, invoice the pest's next of kin. That version of me had a rule about houses like the Halloway place: you don't sleep in them, you don't name them, and you never, ever answer when something in the walls says your name.

The rule had a flaw, I'd realized somewhere around the fourth night of recordings. It assumed the something in the walls wanted to hurt you. This one didn't. This one wanted something much worse.

It wanted to be *known*.

* * *

The archive was a converted fire station with the smell of a dentist's waiting room and the lighting of an interrogation. A woman named Carol ran the local records desk — Carol with the reading glasses on a beaded chain and the expression of someone who had heard every possible excuse for why a grown man needed forty-year-old property documents and had found none of them interesting since 1996.

"Vane," I said. "Ellis Vane. Vanished from a house on Calder Row in 1974. I want everything."

"Missing persons is county sheriff's."

"I know. I've been to the sheriff's. They gave me a case number and a suggestion to move on with my life, which was generous of them, since it wasn't my life." I set the case number on the counter. "But the property records would be here. Deeds, permits, anything attached to the address. And if a child went missing from a house, sometimes there's an incident report filed with the municipality. Health and safety. That kind of thing."

Carol looked at me for a long moment over the top of her glasses. I got the sense she was deciding whether I was a journalist, a grieving relative, or a lunatic, and that all three categories had stood at her counter before.

"Calder Row," she said. "The house with the—"

"Sealed wall cavity, yes."

"Sunroom next door." She said it the way people say *the Kennedy thing*, a shorthand for a story everyone in town half-remembers. "My mother used to say the Aldous family never let their kids play over there. Never said why. Mothers in that neighborhood just said *not that house* the way you'd say *not the road*." She pulled a ledger toward herself. "You a relative?"

"Contractor," I said. It wasn't even a lie. It was the truest thing I'd said to anyone all week.

* * *

Here is what the paper trail looked like, laid out on a scarred oak table under a fluorescent tube that buzzed in B-flat, which I know because the recorder in my pocket picked it up and I checked the pitch later, because I have become a man who checks the pitch of things:

The house on Calder Row was built in 1931. Sold in 1968 to Gerald and Maureen Aldous. There were permits for renovations in 1971 — a wall moved, a cavity created, signed off by an inspector whose signature deteriorated over the years into a single confident loop, the signature of a man who had stopped looking.

There was no permit for the neighbor's sunroom. The neighbor, a Mr. Harlan Finch, built it in 1975, one year after Ellis Vane stopped existing on paper. The permit application was dated four days after the disappearance. Four days. He'd had the slab poured before anyone had finished putting up flyers.

And then there was the diary.

It wasn't a diary, officially. It was entered in the archive as *personal effects, Aldous estate, 1988* — a notebook in a box of notebooks that nobody had catalogued because Carol's predecessor had died and Carol had inherited four decades of boxes. She found it for me because I'd done the thing you do with archivists: I'd been honest about what I was looking for and patient enough to let her enjoy the hunt. She came back from the stacks at eleven-forty with a cloth-bound book and an expression I'd last seen on my own face, listening to the tapes.

"You'll want to sit down," she said.

"I've been sitting down for three days, Carol."

"Not like this."

Gerald Aldous wrote like an accountant, which is to say he wrote like a man who believed the universe kept ledgers and could be audited. The early entries were banal — lumber prices, his wife's health, a dispute over a fence. Then, March 1971, after the renovation:

*The room is ready. M. says we were fools to wait so long. The man from upstate was very clear: it wants a shape and a door and it will do the rest. He was also clear about the cost, and I confess I did not believe him until Tuesday.*

There was no explanation of what *Tuesday* was. There never is. The universe keeps its ledgers in a shorthand only it can read.

The entries continued, monthly, then weekly. Aldous wrote about *feedings* in the tone other men use for boiler maintenance. The man from upstate had told them what the room ate — not food, not blood, nothing so Gothic. It ate *attention*. It ate being thought about, dreamed about, feared. A room that was remembered grew. A room that was forgotten starved. And a room that was *fed a child* —

I'll give you Aldous's own words, because I've tried to paraphrase them and every paraphrase sounds like something I invented, and I need you to understand I didn't invent any of this:

*The man said a child is best. A child's sleep is deep and undivided. He said the room would grow fat and quiet and never trouble us again, so long as we kept giving it the one and took nothing back. M. found one through the church. A foster placement, the family glad of the respite. The boy is quiet. The boy sleeps. God forgive us, the boy sleeps so well.*

Ellis Vane was eleven years old in the summer of 1974. A foster placement, respite care, three weeks with the Aldous family while his mother — a widow, I'd learn, though the file didn't say it — was in the hospital. He was reported missing on the 14th of August. The sheriff's report, which I'd already read, noted that the boy had a history of running away, that he'd been *difficult to place*, that the Aldouses described him as *withdrawn*.

Withdrawn. I turned that word over for a long time. A child who sleeps too well. A child the neighbors' mothers wouldn't let their kids near. A child who, I was now fairly sure, had been carried — sleeping, or past sleeping — through a door that had been built for exactly that purpose, into a cavity that had been shaped for exactly that purpose, and fed to something that the Aldouses had invited into their walls the way you invite a tenant, because the rent it paid was peace.

The last entries in the diary were short.

*It is done. The room is quiet. M. says we must never take anything back and never give it a name. Names are handles. He said that too.*

*The Finch man has bought the lot next door. He wants to build. I have told him to build elsewhere. He laughed at me.*

*He has agreed to keep the box. He understands the arrangement. I do not like that he understands it so readily.*

*The room is dreaming. Sometimes I hear it through the wall. It sounds like the boy. M. says it will fade. It has not faded.*

The diary ended in 1976. Maureen Aldous died in 1981. Gerald in 1988, alone, in a flat across town, and the notebook went into a box that went into a basement that went into Carol's care, and for thirty-six years the only testimony of a murdered child sat in the dark next to tax assessments for a hardware store that burned down in 1979.

I sat at the table for a long time after I finished reading. Carol brought me tea I didn't drink. The fluorescent light buzzed its B-flat, patient as everything else in this story.

"You look like a man who's found what he was looking for," Carol said, "and wishes he hadn't."

"That's the whole job description," I said. "Pest control."

* * *

Finding June Vane took two days and one very uncomfortable phone call.

The sheriff's file listed next of kin as of 1974: a mother, Ruth Vane, deceased 1990. No siblings listed. But missing-persons files are compiled by people in a hurry, and I've learned that the people a case forgets are usually the ones who remember the case. I went back through the foster placement records — Carol again, bless her and her beaded chains — and found the intake interview. Conducted with the child and, separately, with a *maternal aunt*, one line, no name, the interviewer having run out of interest somewhere around *maternal*.

But the aunt had signed. June Vane. And June Vane, if she was alive, would be seventy now, give or take.

I found her the way you find anyone from that generation: a wedding announcement from 1981, a husband with a distinctive surname, a phone listing that survived into the digital era, and finally — because the husband had died in 2019 and the house had been sold — a care facility in Marlow, forty minutes east, where the receptionist was either bored or kind and told me that yes, June Aldous — she'd kept the married name — was a resident, and yes, she had her wits about her, and no, I couldn't just walk in, and then, when I said the words *her brother Ellis*, a silence long enough that I heard the receptionist's chair creak.

"She doesn't talk about him," the receptionist said finally. "But she'll want to talk about him. She's been waiting fifty years for someone to ask."

* * *

Marlow House was one of the good ones, or as good as they get — plants that were real, a smell of coffee instead of disinfectant, a sitting room with actual sunlight in it. June Aldous sat by the window in a chair that had clearly been chosen for its view of the garden, and when the aide led me over, she looked up at me with eyes the same pale grey as the sky outside and said, before I'd said a word:

"You've been in the house."

Not *hello*. Not *who are you*. She looked at my hands — I realized later she was looking at the scratches, the ones I'd gotten from the lath when I'd opened the cavity — and then at my face, and she said it again, quieter, like a diagnosis.

"You've been in the house. Sit down, love. My legs aren't what they were but my memory is a spiteful old thing and it's kept everything."

I sat. For a moment I couldn't speak. Fifty years of waiting for someone to ask, and here I was, the someone, and all I could manage was the question I'd carried out of the archive like a stone in my chest.

"Mrs. Aldous — June — did you know what the Aldouses were going to do?"

She didn't flinch. I'll give her that. She'd had fifty years to practice not flinching.

"Know?" she said. "I drove him there. I carried his suitcase. He was eleven and he had a cowlick he fought every morning and he was *furious* about the placement, not scared, furious, because he was eleven and furious was his whole personality that summer." Her hands were folded in her lap, knuckles like a topographic map. "Ruthie was in St. Mary's with her heart. Three weeks, they said. I had two of my own and a husband who drank, and the church arranged it, and the Aldouses had a *nice house*, everyone said so, a nice house on Calder Row, and I put my nephew in it and drove away and that was the last time I saw him walking."

"Did you ever suspect—"

"Suspect." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as old tape. "Love, I *knew*. Not then. Then I knew nothing, I was thirty-one and drowning. But afterward. When he ran away — that's what they said, he ran away, an eleven-year-old with four pounds fifty and no coat, in August, and the sheriff closed the file in *nine days* — I went back. I went back and back and back. I knocked on that door until Gerald Aldous threatened me with a solicitor. And I'll tell you what I saw, Mr. Dunmore, and you can decide if I'm a silly old woman."

I said nothing. I'd learned by then that June Aldous required no encouragement, only an audience and time.

"The house was *wrong*. Not dirty-wrong, not sad-wrong. It was wrong the way a face is wrong when the smile doesn't reach it. And Maureen Aldous — she'd put on weight, or she looked like it, she had this *fullness* about her, this well-restedness, and Gerald the same, and their own children, the two girls, they'd stopped coming to the window when I knocked. Children don't stop coming to the window. Children come to the window even for the postman." She turned to face me fully. "And I said to myself, June, that house is eating something, and I didn't know what, and I was ashamed to say it out loud because it sounded mad, and by the time I stopped being ashamed, Ruthie was gone and the trail was cold and everyone who could have told me anything had stopped answering."

"Harlan Finch," I said. "The neighbor."

Her mouth went thin. "Finch. Yes. I went to him too, in '75, when the sunroom went up. He was pleasant as pie. Told me the Aldouses were odd ducks and I should let the past lie. And I stood in his garden and I felt it — under the slab, under that horrible little glass room, I *felt* it, the way you feel a draft. And I thought, that's where he is. That's where my Ellis is. And I called the sheriff and they sent a constable who looked at the sunroom and looked at me and said, ma'am, would you like me to call someone for you?" Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "Fifty years, Mr. Dunmore. I have been the madwoman of this story for fifty years. Tell me why you're here. Tell me you're not here to be polite."

So I told her. The tapes. The second voice. The name it had answered to — not *Ellis*, it had never once said *Ellis*, and I watched her absorb that, watch it land like a blow she'd been bracing for since 1974. The handprints on the inside of the sealed wall, small, left-handed, at the height of an eleven-year-old. The diary. *The boy sleeps so well.* The box under the sunroom slab — I hadn't opened it yet, hadn't dared, but I told her what the radar showed, and what the voice had asked for, in its patient, childlike, wrong way: *return what was taken. The name and the bones.*

June Aldous listened without moving. When I finished, she sat looking at the garden for a long time, and the sunlight moved maybe an inch across the carpet, and then she said:

"His name wasn't Ellis."

I waited.

"Ellis was what Ruthie called him because she hated her father-in-law and the name was her small revenge. But he was baptized something else. His full name — the real one, the one on his christening record, the one Ruthie never put on any school form because she said the world had taken enough from that family already —" her voice caught, and she pressed her folded hands flat, and went on. "The Aldouses told the room his name. I'm sure of it. That's what the diary means — *never give it a name*. A name is a handle. They gave it the handle and then they took the handle out of the world so no one could ever use it back. That's why the file says Ellis. That's why everything says Ellis. They erased the true name so the room could keep him."

"And you know it," I said. "The true name."

"I have known it for seventy years, love." She looked at me then, and her eyes were not a madwoman's eyes. They were the clearest, most terrible eyes I have ever been on the wrong side of. "I was there when he was baptized. I held the candle. Now. Before you ask me for it — and you will ask, and I will give it, so don't make a ceremony of it — you'll answer me one question first, and you'll answer it true."

"Ask."

"The voice on your tapes. The one that answers questions no one asked." Her hands tightened on each other. "Does it sound like a boy who's angry? Or a boy who's waiting?"

I thought of the tapes. The slow exhale at 3:14. The patient voice, the questions it answered that no one had asked, the way it had said Ivo's childhood nickname — *Ivo, Ivo, sold his shadow for a pillow* — not like a threat. Like a reminder. Like something that had been waiting in the dark for so long it had forgotten how to be anything but patient, and was so tired, and was so *hungry*, and had been hungry for fifty years in a box under a sunroom while the world called it a runaway.

"Waiting," I said. "It sounds like it's been waiting."

June Aldous closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet, and she was smiling, and it was the worst thing I saw all week.

"Then there's still time," she said. "There's still time to tell him he was loved. Wait here, love. Don't go anywhere."

She rang the little bell by her chair, and when the aide came she said, in a voice like a woman ordering tea, "Fetch my jewelry box, dear. The blue one. And my coat, if you don't mind. It seems I'm going to be useful after all."
