## 19. The Name

The room let me in the way a mouth lets in food.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had left by then. It wasn't a door opening. The wall simply stopped being a wall where I needed it to be, and the hallway beyond it stopped being a hallway, and there was a threshold under my feet — actual wood, actual grain — and I stepped over it because the alternative was standing in the Halloways' kitchen at three in the morning holding a tin box and a locket like the world's saddest delivery man.

Behind me, Mara stood at the end of the hall with Wren on her hip. She didn't say goodbye. We'd agreed there would be no goodbyes, because goodbyes are answers, and this house answered back.

"Three nights," she'd said instead. "You said three nights."

"Two," I said. "It's generous tonight. Enjoy it while it lasts."

That's the thing about me they never put on the van magnets: I joke when I'm terrified. It's not bravery. It's a pressure valve, and if you ever meet me at a funeral and I'm making you laugh, understand that inside I am screaming in a key only dogs and dead children can hear.

The door sealed behind me with a sound like a held breath finally released.

* * *

The room was bigger than the house.

I know how that sounds. I've had thirty years to make peace with the fact that some rooms don't care about load-bearing walls or county assessor records or the basic courtesy of geometry. This one had swallowed its own dimensions the way it had swallowed everything else — quietly, completely, without leaving so much as a bulge in the wallpaper.

And the wallpaper. God help me, the wallpaper.

Faded yellow roses on cream, water-stained in the corner above where the radiator used to be. My mother's rental on Corliss Street, 1989, reproduced down to the stain shaped like a rabbit if you squinted, which nine-year-old Ivo had squinted at every night for eleven months. The room had been building this out of me for weeks, feeding on my sleep through the recorders same as it fed on the Halloways', taking measurements. Here was the chair — brown corduroy, one arm worn shiny, angled toward the television that wasn't there but left a rectangle of cleaner paint anyway. Here was the smell: cigarettes and instant coffee and the particular damp of a radiator that never quite worked. Here, on the nightstand, a glass of water with a spoon in it, because my mother stirred sugar into everything she couldn't finish.

"You could have just asked me to describe it," I said out loud. "I'd have charged you less."

The room didn't answer. Rooms never do. But the air got warmer, the way a room gets warm when someone in it is pleased with you, and I decided I hated that more than anything else that had happened so far, including the footprints, including the tapes, including the moment on night two when Wren had looked up from her cereal and said, in perfect unison with something behind the wall, *he knows the way in.*

I walked toward the center of the room carrying the box against my chest like a football. The floorboards knew my weight. That was the worst part, maybe — not the wrongness of the place but the rightness, the horrible hospitality of it. Every creak was a creak I recognized. Somewhere ahead, past the chair and the ghost of a television, the light went strange, thickening the way light does before weather.

Dev was there.

He stood frozen mid-stride, one foot raised, arms loose, head tilted at an angle no awake neck would choose. His eyes were open. His eyes were open and they were looking at nothing, which was worse than if they'd been looking at me, because it meant whatever he was watching was somewhere I couldn't follow yet. Frost had formed on his eyelashes. In July. His breath came out in small white flags of surrender.

"Hey, kid." I kept my voice level, the way you do around spooked animals and armed teenagers. "Your mom's going to kill you about the curfew. Just so you know what's waiting."

Nothing. Not even a blink. But the frost on him breathed with him, in and out, and I took that for a pulse and made myself stop checking.

"It's rude to leave guests standing," said a voice behind me.

I turned around slowly. You learn to turn slowly in this line of work. Fast turns invite things to show you what they look like.

She was small. Eight years old, maybe nine, wearing a dress from a decade before I was born — a cotton thing with a dropped waist, the kind you see in sepia photographs. Her hair was dark and fine and cut in a bob that someone had loved once, cut with care, with bangs trimmed straight across a forehead still round with childhood. And her face —

Her face was starving.

Not metaphorically. The skin sat close over the bones the way skin does on the very old or the very ill, stretched and translucent at the temples, and her eyes were too big for their sockets, dark and wet and furious, and her mouth was a thin line drawn by someone who had run out of patience sometime around 1975. She wore Ellis Vane's face the way Dev wore his sleepwalking — from the outside, borrowed, ill-fitting. Underneath it something else moved. Something that had been hungry for fifty years and had recently discovered appetite wasn't the same as satisfaction.

"You brought them," she said. Her voice was the voice from the tapes, patient and childlike and wrong, but closer now it had texture, a rasp at the bottom like a recording played too many times. "You brought my bones."

"I brought your bones," I said. "And something else."

I held up the locket. Inside it, a curl of hair, fine and dark, snipped by a seven-year-old girl named June who had never stopped looking. The entity's eyes fixed on it and the whole room flinched — actually flinched, the wallpaper rippling like pond water, the corduroy armchair shivering on its ghost-legs.

"That's mine," she said.

"That's yours," I agreed. "June kept it. Fifty-one years. She wants you to know she never stopped looking for you."

"She forgot my name."

"No," I said. "They made her forget. There's a difference, and I've got proof of the difference, and we're going to get to that. But first—"

The temperature dropped so fast my breath joined Dev's. The room leaned in. Every stain in the wallpaper became a rabbit; every rabbit became an eye.

"SAY IT," the thing wearing Ellis said, and the voice split into harmonics, a child's voice braided with something older and larger underneath, the landlord behind the tenant. "SAY MY NAME AND GIVE ME BACK WHAT'S MINE."

"Yeah, see, here's my problem." I knelt down and set the tin box on the floor between us, slow, deliberate, palms visible. Old pest-controller habit: never make sudden moves around anything cornered. "Here's my problem, Ellis. Because that's not your name. That's the name on the case file, the name the Aldous family gave the police in '74 when they told everyone you ran away. A nine-year-old doesn't run away in bare feet in January. A nine-year-old doesn't run away and leave her shoes by the back door and her coat on the hook. Somebody wrote *Ellis Vane, runaway* in a file and closed the drawer on you, and I think you've been choking on that lie for half a century."

The fury in her face flickered. Underneath it, for one second, something lost surfaced — a child's confusion, the expression of someone being told the world isn't what they were promised.

"June remembers," I said. "June is seventy now, and her memory's got holes in it like everything that old, but she remembers a full name. A real one. Given at a christening, written in a family Bible, and then scrubbed off every record the Aldouses could reach, because a runaway needs no grave and no stone and no questions." I reached into my jacket pocket and took out the photograph I'd photographed off June's mantelpiece, printed at a twenty-four-hour pharmacy by a bored clerk who had no idea. "But Bibles keep. Sisters keep better."

I held it out. The little girl in the picture stood on a porch step beside a taller girl, both squinting into sun, and beneath it in faded ballpoint: *Ellis Marie Vane-Okafor, age 6, with June.*

The room went absolutely still. Stillness has a sound. It's the sound of a held breath in a house with lungs.

"Vane-Okafor," the entity whispered. The harmonics fell away. What remained was small and wondering and broke something in my chest I'd need to look at later. "Mama called me—"

"Lissie," I said. "She called you Lissie. June remembers that too. June remembers everything they couldn't burn."

* * *

I'm going to tell you what happened next as straight as I can, because bearing witness was the whole point, and because if I dress it up in cleverness you'll miss what it cost.

I opened the tin box.

The bones inside were small. That's the sentence. That's the whole of it and it will never be enough. Small bones, wrapped in what had once been a child's dress — the same dress, I realized, the same dropped-waist cotton, rotted nearly to lace. They had put her in the ground under the neighbor's slab with her shoes off, the way you'd put away something you meant to come back for, except they never came back, because coming back would mean admitting what the room had cost them and what they'd paid it with.

The Aldouses hadn't been victims. That was the last lie to die. Chapter fifteen's research, June's testimony, the diary in the archive — Gerald Aldous writing *the room asks so little and gives so much* in a hand that never trembled until the entries stopped. They had fed it deliberately. A child, chosen, carried next door in the winter of 1974, given to the cavity behind the plaster in exchange for — what? Luck. Health. The extension that never cracked. A life that went on being comfortable while a girl's name was sanded off the world.

"They took your name," I said to her, and my voice did something embarrassing, cracked down the middle like drywall, and I let it, because she deserved to hear it break. "They took your body and hid it under concrete. They took your sister's memory of you and filed it under *runaway.* And then they died comfortable in their beds, Ellis. Both of them. In their beds."

The thing wearing her face began to shake.

"I was here," she said. "I was so quiet. I did everything they asked. I was *quiet.*"

"I know."

"Nobody came."

"I came," I said. "Fifty-one years late, and I'm not the one who should have come, and none of that is fair, and I'm not going to stand here and pretend saying words fixes it. But I can do this."

I lifted the bones out, all of them, with both hands, the way you carry something that used to be somebody. I laid them on the floorboards of a room built out of stolen memory, in the shape a small girl makes when she's sleeping on her side, knees up, fists tucked. I unwound the rotted dress around them as gently as I've ever done anything. Then I opened the locket and set the curl of hair where a pillow should have been.

"Ellis Marie Vane-Okafor," I said. "Known as Lissie. Daughter. Sister. Person. You were taken from your home in January 1974 by Gerald and Marion Aldous, who fed you to this room on purpose and lied about it until they died. You did not run away. You were nine years old and you did nothing wrong. You were quiet because they made you be quiet, and it was never, not for one single day, your fault."

The room screamed.

Not the entity — the room. The wallpaper tore itself off the walls in long yellow-rose ribbons that dissolved before they hit the floor. The corduroy chair collapsed inward like a lung exhaling fifty years. The false window, the one I hadn't even noticed, the one showing Corliss Street in permanent dusk, shattered into light and then into nothing. Everything the room had built out of me and out of Dev and out of fifty years of a stolen girl's loneliness was coming apart at the seams, because a room is only a lie, and lies cannot survive being witnessed out loud.

Through it all, the entity stood over the small arrangement of bones, and she was crying, and the tears were the first honest thing in the building.

"Mama called me Lissie," she said again, to herself, to the bones, to the dark coming up through the dissolving floor. "Lissie. Lissie. I'm Lissie."

"Yeah," I said. "You are. And June says hello. And June says she never stopped."

Dev gasped.

It was a drowning-man gasp, whole-body, and he folded forward onto hands and knees, frost sloughing off him in sheets, and I was on the floor beside him before I remembered deciding to move. His eyes found mine. They were his own eyes again, sixteen and terrified and furiously alive.

"Ivo," he croaked. "Ivo, I couldn't — I was *in* it, I was—"

"I know. Up. Can you stand? Good. Stay behind me anyway."

Because the room was collapsing, yes. But rooms that are dying do what dying things do.

They grab.

The floor tilted — not physically, worse than physically, *conceptually*, the idea of down relocating itself — and the darkness beneath the dissolving boards rose up like water finding a hole, and in it I felt the shape of the terms I'd agreed to at the sealed wall. Himself for Dev. The bones returned. The name spoken. All done, all done, all done — and now the bill.

The room reached for Dev first. Of course it did. It had held him longest; it knew his weight.

I put my hand flat on his chest and shoved.

He went backward through a doorway that was already mostly memory, through the threshold, out — and I heard Mara scream his name on the other side, heard the beautiful ordinary collision of mother and son hitting the hallway floor together, heard Wren laughing the way six-year-olds laugh at the fall-down-go-boom of it all, and the sound of it was the best thing I have ever heard and I knew, even then, even falling, that I'd be keeping it.

Then the floor finished forgetting itself, and I went down into the dark with a dead girl's name in my mouth and her bones' warmth still on my hands, and the last thing I saw from the outside of the world was the room-that-wasn't folding shut around me like a hand closing over a coin.

Somewhere below — or within, or after — something small and hoarse whispered:

*Lissie. My name is Lissie.*

And the dark, for the first time in fifty years, listened.
