## 13. Edmund Forgets His Name

The raincoat was hanging on the hook by the back door where it had lived for three days now, dripping a little, though it hadn't rained since Tuesday. Gideon noticed the drip the way you notice a clock that's stopped — not the sound itself but the absence of what should follow it.

Edmund was sitting at the kitchen table with a glass of water he never drank from. He'd been doing that more often lately. Holding things without using them. A boy performing the idea of being alive.

"Hey," Gideon said, and lifted the raincoat off the hook. "Look at this. Remember this?"

They turned it around slowly, the way you'd show a child something precious. The yellow had gone pale as butter left out. The hood was frayed at the edge where thirty years of waiting had worn it thin.

Edmund looked at it.

Gideon watched his face do something terrible and small: nothing. No flicker. No recognition. Just the polite blankness of a stranger being shown an object by someone who clearly expected it to mean something.

"It's yours," Gideon said. "You wore it. The day we met — well, the day I met you again, after — you were standing in the culvert and it was raining and you had this on."

"I don't remember wearing it," Edmund said.

The words were flat. Not cruel. That was the worst part. There was no grief in them because there was nothing in them yet to grieve. It was like watching someone read their own name off a list of the dead.

"You're Edmund," Gideon said. "Edmund Pryce. You were born in 1966 on Larkspur Street. Your mother's name was Alma and she sang while she cooked and she burned the rice every single time and you loved her anyway. You disappeared in October of 1974. You've been under the town ever since. You came up onto my porch because I believed you back into the world when I was six years old, and you waited, and you waited, and you are *here*—"

"Gideon." Edmund's voice was gentle. "Why are you crying?"

Because you just called me by my name like it was a rope thrown across a gap, Gideon thought. Because you said it right, but you said it the way you'd say the name of someone you'd been told about.

"I'm not," they said, and wiped their face. "Eat your breakfast."

He didn't eat his breakfast. He held the fork and turned it over and studied the tines like he was trying to remember what forks were for, and Gideon stood at the counter with their back turned and understood, with the cold clarity of a surveyor reading a fault line, exactly what was happening.

The bell was taking him back. Inch by inch. Toll by toll.

And somewhere beneath Harrow's Bend, something else was taking *them*.

* * *

It started with the doors.

Gideon had counted doors their whole life. It was the first thing their grandmother taught them, before she taught them letters: *count the doors, Gideon, always count the doors.* Every room entered, every threshold crossed, a number spoken under the breath. It had seemed like a family quirk, one of Ruth's oddnesses, like her refusal to own a clock or her habit of salting the doorstep on the first of every month. Gideon had built a whole identity on top of it — the methodical explorer, the mapper, the person who documents everything because everything can be lost.

Three days ago, they had walked into the kitchen and realized they didn't know how many doors were in the house.

They'd stood in the doorway with coffee going cold in their hand, running the count, and come up with seven, then nine, then four, and each answer felt true for half a second and then dissolved like a name on the tip of the tongue. They'd had to walk the house twice, touching every frame, whispering numbers, until the count settled at eleven and held.

Eleven. It had always been eleven. But for one long moment, standing in their own kitchen, Gideon Vasquez had not known how many doors there were in the world.

Now it was happening again, in smaller ways. They'd signed a receipt at the gas station and their signature had come out wrong — not messy, *wrong*, the letters in an order that wasn't theirs, a looping scrawl that belonged to some other hand. They'd caught themselves answering to a name they couldn't quite hear. Twice in the night they'd woken standing in the hallway facing the cellar door, feet bare and cold, no memory of getting up, and both times the door had been unlocked, and both times they had locked it with hands that shook.

And under their fingernails, faint but persistent, the smell of wet clay.

They scrubbed in the shower until their fingers bled a little. The smell stayed.

*You've been chosen,* the dream-keepers had sung, faces like riverbed mud, voices layered like a choir heard through water. *The ledger has a blank line. Blank lines want filling.*

Gideon stood in the shower with the water hammering their shoulders and thought: if it takes me, if it wears me down to a robe and a song, who anchors Edmund? Who remembers him?

Nobody. That was the answer. Nobody in Harrow's Bend remembered Edmund Pryce except a woman dying in a house full of locked rooms and a man who was currently forgetting how many doors his own house had.

So Gideon did the only thing they knew how to do.

They got a notebook.

* * *

Not just any notebook. Gideon owned dozens — field books, waterproof survey pads, the Rite-in-the-Rain kind with the yellow covers, because a document that dies in a drizzle isn't documentation. They chose the good one, the one they'd used to map the storm drains, and they sat down at the kitchen table across from Edmund, and they opened it to the first clean page, and they wrote:

**EDMUND PRYCE. Born June 3, 1966, Larkspur Street, Harrow's Bend. Mother: Alma Pryce. Father: Daniel Pryce, killed in the mill accident, 1971.**

"Edmund," they said. "I need to ask you some questions. And I need you to answer even if the answers feel wrong. Especially then."

He looked up. For a moment — one bright, terrible moment — his eyes were fully his own, and Gideon saw the eight-year-old who had waited thirty years on a porch step, and behind him the older boy he'd never gotten to be, and behind *him* something vast and patient and ringing.

"Okay," Edmund said.

"What did your mother call you when you were in trouble?"

A long pause. His brow furrowed, and Gideon watched him reach for it the way you reach for a stair in the dark — the foot finding nothing, the lurch of the drop.

Then: "*Eddie-bell.*" A small, startled laugh escaped him, surprised out of wherever it had been buried. "Because — because she said I rang through the house like a bell. Never stopped moving, never stopped making noise. Eddie-bell."

Gideon wrote it down. Their hand was steady. Their hand was the steadiest thing in the room.

"What did you want to be when you grew up?"

"A bell-ringer." He said it fast, then frowned. "No. That's not — I wanted to be a train engineer. Why did I say bell-ringer?"

"Because something's been telling you what you are for fifty years," Gideon said quietly. "Train engineer. Write it down." They wrote it. **Wanted to be a train engineer. Was afraid of the dark under the porch. Loved the smell of rain on hot asphalt.**

"Keep going," Edmund said. His voice had changed. There was hunger in it now, a starving-boy hunger, and Gideon understood that this was food, that memory was the only meal the bell couldn't take from him as long as someone else kept serving it.

So Gideon served it.

They worked through the afternoon and into the evening, question and answer, answer and ink. What was your dog's name? (*Biscuit. He was a bad dog. He ate a whole loaf of bread off the counter and hid under my bed for two days.*) What was the first movie you saw in a theater? (*Herbie. I put my gum under the seat and my mother made me go back the next day and find it.*) What did you hear when the bell took you?

That last one stopped them both.

Edmund's face went still. The kitchen clock — the only clock Ruth would allow in the house, wound weekly, its tick deliberate as a heartbeat — filled the silence.

"I don't want to write that one down," he said finally.

"We have to."

"Why?"

"Because if it's written down, it happened. And if it happened, it's yours. It doesn't get to be the only thing you remember, Edmund. It doesn't get to be the *whole* story." Gideon turned the notebook toward him. "Look at this page. Look how much of you there is. One bad thing is just one page in a book."

Edmund looked. He read his own life in Gideon's cramped, careful hand — the surveyor's hand, the hand that mapped drains and counted doors — and something in his shoulders came down from around his ears.

"She burned the rice," he said softly. "Every time."

"Every time."

"And she still called me Eddie-bell." He touched the page, very lightly, the way you touch a thing you're afraid will vanish. "Write that she forgave herself for the rice. She told me once. She said, *a burnt pot feeds you same as a clean one.*"

Gideon wrote it. **Alma Pryce forgave herself for the rice.**

Outside, far off, so low it might have been a truck on the highway or might have been nothing, something tolled.

Edmund flinched. The pen skittered from Gideon's fingers.

When Gideon picked it up, Edmund was looking at them with an expression they'd never seen on his face before. Pity.

"Gideon," he said. "What's my name?"

The question landed wrong. Off-key. Like a bell struck by a cracked clapper.

"Edmund," Gideon said. "Edmund Pryce."

"No." He shook his head slowly, and his eyes had gone vague again, tide going out. "No, I mean — *you*. What's *your* name? You were saying it a minute ago and then—" He pressed his palms against his eyes. "There was a word and now there's a hole where the word was. Gideon, why is there a hole? Why do I keep looking at you and seeing a *robe*?"

The clay smell rose in Gideon's own nose, sudden and thick, and beneath it, fainter than breath, a tone they felt in their teeth rather than heard.

They knew then that the erosion ran both ways. The keepers weren't just recruiting them. They were hollowing them — scooping out Gideon Vasquez door by door, name by name, signature by signature, preparing the vessel. And Edmund, bound to the bell, tethered to Gideon's belief, was going down with them. Two candles in one draft.

Unless the record held.

Gideon opened the notebook to a fresh page and wrote, in block capitals, pressing hard enough to emboss the next five pages:

**MY NAME IS GIDEON VASQUEZ. I AM THE GRANDCHILD OF RUTH VASQUEZ. I COUNT DOORS BECAUSE SHE TAUGHT ME TO. I AM NOT A KEEPER. I AM NOT A ROBE. I HAVE ELEVEN DOORS AND ONE FRIEND AND HIS NAME IS EDMUND PRYCE AND HE IS REAL.**

They read it aloud. Then they made Edmund read it aloud, twice, until his eyes cleared and he could say *Gideon* like a rope thrown across a gap instead of a word learned from a list.

"There," Gideon said, and their voice almost held. "Now we're both written down. Now we exist on paper even if we stop existing anywhere else."

Edmund reached across the table and took the pen. Slowly, tongue between his teeth, in a child's careful cursive fifty years out of practice, he added a line:

**EDMUND PRYCE WAS HERE. HE WAITED. HE WAS FOUND.**

* * *

They taped pages to the walls that night. It looked like madness — a house papered with handwriting, names and dates and burnt rice and a bad dog named Biscuit — but Gideon had spent their life learning that structures hold or fail based on what's written in their bones, and they intended to write these bones full.

By midnight the kitchen was an archive. By two a.m., the hallway. Edmund fell asleep on the couch surrounded by his own evidence, one hand curled around a page that said **HE LIKED THE SMELL OF RAIN ON HOT ASPHALT**, and Gideon sat watching him breathe and let themselves, for exactly one minute, feel the size of what was coming.

Then they checked the date on their phone, and the minute ended.

Five nights until the harvest moon.

They were gathering the loose pages into order when they found the one that wasn't theirs.

It was in the middle of the stack, wedged between **EDMUND PRYCE'S MOTHER SANG WHILE SHE COOKED** and a blank sheet. The handwriting was Gideon's own — unmistakably, the same cramped surveyor's print — but Gideon had no memory of writing it, and it said:

**THE LEDGER HAS ROOM FOR ONE MORE NAME. BRING THE BOY HOME WHERE HE BELONGS. YOU KNOW THE WAY. YOU'VE ALWAYS KNOWN THE WAY.**

Below it, in the same hand, a tally mark. Fresh. Wet enough to smear under Gideon's thumb.

Down the hall, in their sleep, Edmund began to count — softly, steadily, the way children count when they're frightened and trying not to show it — and Gideon stood in their papered kitchen holding a page written by their own disappearing hand, and listened to him count past one hundred, past two hundred, past any number a sleeping child should need.
