## 1. Dead Air

The red ON AIR light had been burning for two hours and eleven minutes when the phone line blinked.

Felix Ashgrove saw it from across the studio — a small amber pulse on the board, patient as a heartbeat. He was mid-sentence, something about the weather turning cold enough to smell the winter coming through the mountains, and he let the line ring twice more before he patched it through. That was the ritual. Two rings minimum. It made the callers feel like they'd earned it.

"You're live with Felix Ashgrove on KLMR, the night shift. Who's this?"

"Hi, Felix." A woman's voice, warm and slightly hoarse, like she'd been talking all day and hadn't minded. "It's Marta."

"Marta." He said it like he knew her, because half the time he did. The graveyard shift at a community station meant you learned your regulars the way a bartender learns theirs. "Let me guess. You've got a request."

"I always have a request." A small laugh. "But I mostly just wanted to say thank you. For last week. The night I couldn't sleep — you played that whole set of old folk songs, remember? You said something about how music is the only thing we do that goes both directions in time."

He remembered. He remembered saying it because he'd been proud of it, and because nobody else had been awake to hear him say it.

"That's a lot of remembering for one call," he said. "You want me to play something for you tonight?"

"Actually—" and here her voice did something strange, dropped down into a register that made him sit up straighter in his chair, "—I wanted to ask you about a song. An old one. From where my family's from. Do you know any harvest songs?"

"Honestly? Not really. I know 'John Barleycorn' if that counts."

"No." She said it fast, too fast. "Not that kind. The other kind. The kind with a verse people don't sing anymore."

The studio hummed around him. The transmitter's low drone, the tick of the console clock, the hiss of the open line. Outside the glass, the dark parking lot sat empty under one sodium light, and beyond it the hills were just a blacker shape against the black sky.

"Marta," he said carefully, "that sounds less like a request and more like a riddle."

"It's not a riddle. It's a debt." Another laugh, but thinner now, frayed at the edges. "Sorry. Sorry, I'm being weird. It's just — I haven't been able to stop thinking about it since I died."

The word went through him like a needle finding a nerve. He laughed — a short, reflexive bark of a laugh, the kind you do at funerals when someone says the wrong thing.

"Since you *what*?"

"Since I died," Marta said again, patiently, the way you'd repeat an address to someone who wasn't writing it down. "Six days ago. Tuesday. My sister found me Wednesday morning. I don't blame her for taking so long; we didn't talk much anymore. But I heard your show that night, Felix. I heard it after."

He looked at the console. The call screen showed LINE 1 ACTIVE and nothing else. No caller ID. No number. No duration counter ticking up, which it should have been doing, which it always did, even on blocked calls. Just the word ACTIVE sitting there like a held breath.

"Okay," Felix said, and his voice came out steady, which surprised him, because his hands had gone cold on the faders. "So this is a bit. This is a Halloween bit, right? Except it's October third, so somebody's early this year."

"I'm not a bit, Felix."

"Sure. And how does the bit work? Voice changer? Somebody who sounds like—"

"Like what?" she asked, genuinely curious. "You don't know what I sounded like. We never met. You know my voice from the phone and nothing else. So tell me — how would you know if I got it wrong?"

That stopped him. Because it was true. He knew Marta the way he knew all his night listeners: as a voice, a set of opinions about songs, a habit of calling between one and two a.m. when the world got quiet enough to hear yourself think. If someone else had picked up this line and said *it's Marta*, he would have believed them without question. There was no way to check. There never had been.

Except.

"Last week," he said slowly. "You called last week too. About your garden. Your tomatoes came in bad and you blamed the soil."

"And you told me tomatoes are just grief with seeds in them," Marta said, "and I laughed so hard I woke up my neighbor's dog."

He had said that. He'd forgotten saying it until she said it back, and now it came back whole, the exact words, the exact cadence, and underneath it something else began to move — a cold, slow thing, rising through him like water coming up through a basement floor.

"Where do you live, Marta?"

"You know I can't say that on air."

"I'm asking off the record. I'm asking as a friend."

A pause. Long enough that he checked the line was still active. It was. ACTIVE, no numbers, no timer.

"Farrow County," she said finally. "North of you. Little town called Hollowmere. Though I suppose it isn't mine anymore, is it? Nothing's mine anymore. That's the part nobody warns you about. You spend your whole life thinking the worst part of dying will be the pain or the leaving, and actually it's the inventory. Standing there counting up everything that used to be yours and realizing none of it follows you out."

"Marta." He pressed his palm flat against the console, grounding himself against the cool metal. "I need you to listen to me. Whatever's going on — whoever put you up to this — it's not funny. It's cruel, actually. If this is a prank, it's a cruel one, and if it's not a prank—"

"If it's not a prank," she echoed softly, "then you're talking to a dead woman on live radio, and you should probably decide pretty quickly what that makes you."

"What what makes me?"

"The kind of person they can talk to."

*They.* The word landed wrong. Not the way a word lands wrong in a sentence — the way a floorboard gives under your foot when you thought it would hold.

"Who's *they*, Marta?"

"The others. The ones who call you. The ones who are going to keep calling you." Her voice had changed again, and he realized with a lurch that it sounded tired now, worn, like a recording played too many times. "I got to go first because I loved the show most. Isn't that funny? Loyalty has a queue."

"There aren't others. You're the first call I've gotten tonight."

"Not tonight," Marta said. "Soon. One after another, until you understand. That's how it works. That's how it worked for Edith."

His grandmother's name, out of nowhere, out of a stranger's mouth, out of a dead woman's mouth, and every hair on his arms stood up under his headphones.

"What did you say?"

But she was already somewhere else, her voice drifting, unfocused, like someone talking while looking out a window at nothing. "It's so loud out here, Felix. I don't know why nobody says how loud it is. All those voices in the field, singing that song, and they only ever sing it up to the part everyone knows, and then they stop, all together, like a door closing, and then they start over. Over and over. They're waiting for someone to finish it. They've been waiting so long."

"Marta, stop. What field? Whose voices?"

"The wheat remembers better than we do," she said. "That's the whole problem. That's the whole *everything*. We cut the verse out and we thought cutting it out meant it was gone, but a song doesn't die just because you stop singing it. It just gets hungry."

The console clock read 1:47. The ON AIR light burned its steady red. Somewhere behind him, in the rack of equipment, a relay clicked — a small mechanical sound, utterly ordinary, and it made him flinch hard enough to knock his elbow against the desk.

"Marta, I want you to hang up now," he said. "I want you to hang up, and I want you to—"

"To what?" she asked, and there was real gentleness in it, terrible gentleness. "Go home? Lie down? Felix. Sweetheart. Think it through."

He couldn't answer that. His mouth had gone dry and his thoughts were moving in circles, chasing their own tails: *prank, prank, it's a prank, it has to be a prank,* and under the chant, quieter, the other voice, the honest one, saying *no caller ID, no timer, no number, no number, no number.*

"Do you want to know the worst part?" Marta said. "It's not scary, once you're here. That's the trick of it. Everyone thinks it'll be scary. It's just quiet, and then it's loud, and then it's the song, and the song is almost nice. It's almost like coming home. You'll hum it before you know you're humming it. Everybody does."

"Don't," Felix said.

"It goes like this."

And she hummed.

It was a simple melody — five notes, maybe six, rising and settling, rising and settling, the shape of something meant to be sung by many voices working in rhythm, scythes in wheat, feet in rows. It should have been nothing. It should have been any folk tune from anywhere, forgettable as weather. But it caught in him immediately, hooked behind his ribs like a fishhook, and worse — worse — some deep animal part of him recognized it. Not from anywhere he could name. Not from any memory he could reach. Recognized it the way your body recognizes falling.

He realized his own lips had parted. That he was breathing in, preparing, without any decision to do so, to hum along.

He clamped his jaw shut. Biting down so hard his molars ached.

"Marta." His voice came out cracked. "Marta, stop humming."

She stopped. The silence on the line was enormous.

"See?" she said. "Almost nice."

"Please hang up. Please. I'm asking you. Whatever you are, whatever this is — please hang up the phone."

"I will," she said. "One more thing first. Tell Marta—" and she paused, and corrected herself with a small, sad laugh, "—tell *my sister* the verse isn't her fault. She was there, you know. In '89. She was just a kid. Kids don't choose. Nobody chooses, really, that's the lie the families tell themselves so they can sleep. They choose for us and then they call it tradition."

"Whose families? Marta, whose—"

"Tell her," Marta said, and her voice was receding now, thinning out like a signal losing strength, stretching across distance it shouldn't be able to cross, "the verse isn't her fault. And Felix — when the chair shows up—"

Static swallowed her. Not gradually. All at once, like a door slamming, and the line went dead in his ears with a click that sounded horribly final, horribly physical, like a bone setting.

For a long moment Felix sat perfectly still in the red light.

Then he moved. Fast, clumsy, knocking his headphones half off. He pulled up the call log on the console screen, fingers slipping on the mouse, and stared at it.

LINE 1 — INCOMING CALL — 1:31 AM.

No caller ID. No number. No duration. Every other entry on the log had a duration, even the hang-ups, even the wrong numbers, three seconds, eleven seconds, forty seconds, a little column of honest arithmetic running down the screen. His call with Marta had lasted sixteen minutes. The column beside it was blank. Not zero. Blank. As if the system itself had declined to witness it.

He scrolled. He refreshed. He rebooted the console, which took four minutes and which he spent standing with his arms wrapped around himself in the middle of the studio like a man waiting for a verdict. When the system came back up, the entry was still blank.

*Prank,* he told himself. *Somebody spoofing the line. Some app, some voice-changer thing, kids do it, people do it, it's a whole genre of YouTube video, dead-voice calls the late-night DJ.* It explained everything except the things that mattered. It didn't explain the tomatoes. It didn't explain Edith. It didn't explain the blank column, and it especially didn't explain the fact that he could still hear the melody, note-perfect, looping in his head, five notes rising and settling, rising and settling, and that his foot was tapping it against the floor of the studio without his permission.

He looked down and stopped his foot.

At 2:00 exactly, the automation took over — syndicated programming, a smooth voice from three states away talking about classic country — and Felix signed off early for the first time in six years at KLMR. He wrote it in the log as *equipment issue.* Then he sat in his car in the empty parking lot with the engine running and the heater blowing cold air because the engine hadn't warmed yet, and he took out his phone, and his thumb hovered over the search bar for a long time before he typed:

*hollowmere*

The town existed. Of course it existed. He'd known it existed his whole life without ever once thinking about it — the place his grandmother Edith had come from, the place she'd left young and never gone back to, the place she'd gone quiet about whenever anyone asked, in that particular way she had of going quiet, folding a subject up and putting it away like linen. He'd driven past the turnoff a hundred times on trips north. He'd never taken it.

The search results were ordinary. A historical society. A church with a website last updated in 2019. A grain co-op. A listing for an inn. And then, fourth result down, an obituary from the Farrow County Courier, dated six days ago.

*Marta Osgood, 61, of Hollowmere, passed away peacefully at her home on Tuesday evening.*

Tuesday evening. Six days ago. The night she'd missed his show — he remembered that too now, remembered noticing the absence, the night feeling strangely unattended, like a shop with the lights on and nobody inside.

Felix sat in the cooling car and read the obituary three times. *Predeceased by her parents. Survived by her sister, Delores.* No mention of children. No mention of much at all. Short obituaries for small towns, lives summed up in ninety words.

*Survived by her sister, Delores.*

*Tell my sister the verse isn't her fault.*

The heater had started blowing warm. Felix didn't feel it. He was listening to the melody again, whether he wanted to or not, five notes rising and settling, and beneath it, faint as a station drifting in from far away, he could have sworn he heard something else — many voices, singing together in the dark, up to a certain point, and then stopping. All together. Like a door closing.

And then starting over.

He turned the key off. Sat in the silence. Told himself, one more time, out loud, in the empty car, in the empty lot, in the voice he used on air when he needed to sound like everything was fine:

"It's a prank."

The words didn't echo. But somewhere out past the fence line, past the dark hills, in a direction he refused to identify, something that might have been wind moving through dry stalks kept perfect time with the song in his head.

Two nights later, the second call would come. But that night, Felix Ashgrove drove home with the radio off, and did not sleep, and hummed nothing, absolutely nothing, all the way to his door.
