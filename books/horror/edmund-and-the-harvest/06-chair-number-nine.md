## 6. Chair Number Nine

The key was where Ruth Calloway said it would be: under the third flowerpot from the door, in a dish that had once held butter, now holding thirty years of rain and one brass key gone green at the teeth.

Edith's cottage sat at the end of Latch Lane, the last house before the fields began. It was smaller than Felix remembered, though he'd only been inside it twice as a boy, both times in summer, both times with the windows open and the smell of baking bread pushing out into the lane like a hand offering welcome. Now the windows were dark with grime, and the air inside was cool and still, the particular stillness of a house that has been holding its breath.

He stood in the doorway a long moment, letting his eyes adjust. Dust hung in the light that came through the kitchen's back window, turning the air into something almost solid, almost navigable. He had the absurd thought that he could walk on it.

"You're just a house," he said aloud, and his voice sounded wrong in the room, too loud, too human, like a shout in a library. "You're just a house where an old lady lived."

The house said nothing. Houses never do. But he felt it listening.

He'd told himself, on the drive over from the inn, that he was here for closure. That word again—closure, the great anesthetic of the modern grief industry. He'd told himself he was sorting his grandmother's effects because someone had to, because Priya wouldn't, because the county would eventually come through with clipboards and skip bins and turn a life into landfill. He'd told himself a great many things. It was a skill he'd honed over two weeks of dead women calling his radio show, of men dissolving into static mid-sentence, of photographs growing furniture like tumors.

He was here because the chairs were here.

He knew it the way you know a thing at three in the morning, without evidence, without argument, with the simple dumb certainty of the body. The chairs were here, and they had something to tell him, and he was tired of being told things secondhand.

The kitchen was first, because kitchens are where houses keep their honesty. Edith's kettle still sat on the stove, a dented copper thing he remembered from childhood, and her cups hung on hooks beneath the cabinet, and her calendar—her calendar was still turned to the month she died. October. Someone had written in the little squares in her cramped, slanted hand, the handwriting of a woman who had grown up before print was cheap: *Choir practice. Dentist. Choir practice. Choir practice.*

Choir practice. Three times in one month.

Felix stood looking at the calendar for a long time. His grandmother had not sung in a choir in his lifetime. She'd hummed, occasionally, tunelessly, the way old women hum, and when he'd once asked her what the song was she'd stopped humming so abruptly it was like a door slamming, and she'd looked at him with an expression he'd spent thirty years filing under *old people are strange* and had spent the last two weeks slowly re-filing under something else entirely.

He took the calendar off the wall. It went into his bag, along with the kettle—no, not the kettle, that was ridiculous, he wasn't going to start looting the house of its soul—and he moved into the front room.

* * *

The front room was bare.

That was the first thing. Not empty—bare. There's a difference. Empty is when a room has nothing in it. Bare is when a room has had something taken from it, recently, deliberately, and the room remembers. The wallpaper showed pale rectangles where pictures had hung. The floorboards showed darker patches where a rug had lain. The windows had been washed, recently, by someone who cared, and the light came through them clean and cold and fell on the table in the center of the room like a spotlight on a stage.

And around the table, nine chairs.

Felix counted them twice. Nine wooden chairs, plain, dark, old—the kind of chairs that get made by hand and then outlive the hands. They were arranged with a precision that was somehow worse than disorder would have been. Not pushed in, not pulled out. Set. Each one equidistant from the table's edge, each one facing it, each one empty.

He walked the room slowly, the way you'd walk a room where a stranger was sleeping. He touched the first chair. The wood was cold and smooth, worn to a polish along the arms where hands had rested for decades, or longer. He touched the second. The same. The third, the fourth. By the fifth he had stopped pretending he was doing anything other than what he was doing, which was taking attendance.

Eight chairs. Then the ninth.

The ninth chair was different. Not visibly—same wood, same make, same age, or so he thought at first. But when he put his hand on its back, the cold of it went through his palm and up his wrist and into the meat of his arm, a cold with a direction to it, a cold that was going somewhere. And the chair was not worn. The arms were unmarked. The seat had never held weight.

It was a new chair, in the way that an empty crib is a new thing. Made for someone. Waiting for someone.

Felix took his hand off it carefully, the way you'd take your hand off a sleeping animal, and stepped back, and his heel caught the leg of the eighth chair and the sound it made—a short wooden knock, once—went through the silent house like a shot, and he stood frozen, half-expecting the room to answer.

The room didn't answer. But somewhere in the walls, or under the floor, or in the space behind the plaster where houses keep their oldest silence, something shifted. Not moved. *Shifted.* The way a sleeper shifts. The way a listener shifts.

"Okay," Felix said. His voice was steady. He was almost proud of it. "Okay."

He photographed the table. Nine chairs, bare room, clean light. He photographed it the way he'd photographed the pictures in the house in town, the way he'd recorded the calls, because documentation was the raft he'd been clinging to for two weeks now: *if I can record it, it's data; if it's data, it's explainable; if it's explainable, I'm not crazy.* He knew the raft was waterlogged. He knew the photographs would probably show eight chairs, or eleven, or none. He took them anyway. You cling to what you have.

It was while he was crouched to get a low angle that he saw the hymnal.

It was on the table. He was certain—it was absolutely certain—that it had not been on the table when he entered the room. He had photographed the table from the doorway before walking it, a habit now, and the table in that first frame was bare wood and nine chairs and nothing else. He checked. The photo showed nothing. The table in front of him showed a book.

It was small, bound in black cloth gone soft and grey at the corners, and it had no title on its cover, which was the first wrong thing, because hymnals always have titles, they wear their titles like churches wear crosses. He picked it up. It was heavier than it looked, in the way of old books, the way of books that have been held open in the same places for so long that the binding has learned those places, has memorized them, has become a shape made of use.

He opened it.

The pages were thin as onion skin, printed in a typeface from another century, numbered in a hand at the top corner in pencil: someone had catalogued the songs. 1 through 44. And between 44 and 46—between one song and the next—there was a gap. A wound. Pages 45's stubs stood up from the spine like the stumps of teeth, ragged, brown-edged, razored. Not torn—*razored*, cut with a blade, carefully, by someone with time and a steady hand and a reason.

The stubs were maybe a quarter inch long. He tilted the book toward the window and could just make out, on the deepest stub, the descenders of letters. The bottoms of words, amputated at the ankle. A tail of a *g*. The foot of a *y*. The ghost of a word that had been *singing* or *dying* or *digging*, and would never say which.

Felix sat down in the eighth chair—it was the farthest from the ninth, a choice he made without examining it—and held the hymnal in both hands and turned, with a fingertip, to the songs that remained. They were harvest songs, most of them. Songs about reaping and binding and the stacking of sheaves, songs about the land's long patience, songs with the kind of titles you find in folk archives: *The Barley's Patience. The Long Field. Come All Ye Faithful Laborers.* Song 44 was called *The Hollowmere Harvest Hymn*, and it was complete—verses one through four, a round, cheerful, brutal little tune about cutting and carrying and the mercy of the scythe—and then it ended, and the page turned, and the stubs began.

The song had a fifth verse. The song had had a fifth verse, and someone had cut it out of this book with a razor, and the cut was so old the surrounding pages had yellowed around it like skin around a scar, and Felix sat in his dead grandmother's front room holding the scar and understood, with the calm of a man who has stopped fighting the water coming over the gunwales, that this was the verse. This was the verse the dead callers hummed and never finished. This was the verse that had been cut out of a town's memory so thoroughly that even the photographs had started growing chairs.

He turned back to song 44 and read the fourth verse again, slowly, aloud, in a whisper, because some part of him wanted to hear how the rhythm would run, wanted to feel the meter reach for the line that wasn't there:

*"The field is fed, the field is glad,*
*the scythe has done its part,*
*and what we owe the hungry ground*
*we pay—"*

And the line stopped. Because the verse stopped. Because the hymnal was a hymnal and not a séance, and it could only give him what it had.

But his mouth, his stupid traitor mouth, had already shaped the next word. He'd felt it coming up his throat, felt his lungs lean into it, felt the note assemble itself somewhere behind his sternum like a passenger leaning toward a door. The word was *with*. He knew it was *with* the way you know the next note of a song you haven't heard in thirty years. The song was in him. It had been in him since the first call, since Marta—since the dead woman on the line had hummed her six notes into his headphones and hung up the world.

He closed the hymnal. He put it in his bag, next to the calendar. He did not sit in the ninth chair. He was proud of that, later, and ashamed of the pride, because pride implies a choice freely made, and he was beginning to understand that in Hollowmere the choices had all been made long ago, by other people, and what was left was only the choosing of the hour.

* * *

He stayed in the cottage until the light went.

He told himself it was to search the rest of the house, and he did search it—Edith's bedroom with its quilt folded back with hospital neatness; the small back room with its boxes of photographs, which he did not open, because he had seen enough photographs; the pantry with its jars of preserves dated in a hand that stopped two years ago. But underneath the searching was the truth, which was that he did not want to be on Latch Lane after dark, and did not want to be on the road either, and so he did the thing people have always done in houses at the edge of fields: he waited for the night to come to him, behind walls, with the door locked and the lamp lit.

He found an oil lamp in the pantry and lit it, because the electricity had been cut years ago, and the lamp made the front room golden and small and turned the nine chairs into an audience. He sat in the eighth chair with the hymnal on his knees and his recorder running on the table—running always now, the red light a small religion—and at some point, without deciding to, he began to hum.

It was the six notes. Marta's six notes, the fragment every caller carried like a coin they'd died holding. He hummed them low, barely voiced, and the lamp flame leaned, and he stopped, and the flame righted itself, and he told himself it was the door, it was a draft, it was the breath of a house settling on its stones.

Then he heard the field.

It started the way the calls had started: at the edge of hearing, in the register where you can't be sure. A thread of sound coming through the walls, through the dark, from the direction of the wheat. He turned off the recorder's playback and held his breath and listened, and it resolved the way a radio signal resolves when you tune past the static—a melody, a line of voices, many voices, singing in unison.

The harvest hymn. Verse one. Verse two. The voices were old and young, male and female, and they sang with the flat, patient precision of people who have sung this song more times than living throats could manage, and the sound came across the field and through the cottage walls and into the golden room as if the walls were made of the same stuff as the night, which is to say: no obstacle at all.

Felix sat absolutely still. Every animal instinct he owned was telling him the same thing: *do not sing, do not hum, do not keep time with your foot, be stone.* He was stone. He was the stoniest man in the county.

Verse three. The lamp flame leaned again, steadily now, leaning like a listener.

And then the choir reached the fourth verse, and Felix's blood went cold, because the fourth verse was where the song had always ended—four verses, that's what the hymnal said, that's what the hymn was—and the choir did not stop. The melody reached the end of the fourth line and stepped over the edge of it, the way his own mouth had stepped toward *with*, and the voices went on into the cut place, into the missing verse, singing words he could not make out at that distance, words the razor had taken from the book but could not take from the dead.

And in the middle of the choir—threaded through it, rising out of it, a voice he knew—someone was humming.

Six notes. Between the verses. A woman's voice, low and sure, humming the fragment the way you'd hum something you couldn't quite remember the words to, and Felix knew the voice. He had spent eleven years behind a microphone; he knew voices the way a jeweler knows stones. It was Marta. It was the first caller, the devoted listener, the woman who had died six days before she'd phoned him, humming in the dark of a wheat field in a village he'd never been to until this week.

He stood up. The chair didn't scrape; he made sure of it; he lifted himself out of it like a man lifting himself out of a dream, and he went to the window, and he put his eye to the gap in the curtain.

The field was moving. Not the wheat—the *dark*. The dark above the wheat was moving, or the dark was full of shapes the way deep water is full of shapes when you look down from a boat, and the singing came up out of it steady and unhurried and vast, dozens of voices now, and the missing verse rolled over the field like weather, and Felix stood with his hand pressed flat against the cold glass and did the only thing left in his arsenal, the thing he'd been doing for two weeks, the thing that had brought him here:

He recorded it.

The recorder's red light glowed on the table like a votive. The choir sang. And then the choir did the thing that ended his career as a man who believed in explanations.

It stopped.

Mid-verse. All of them, all at once, the way a room goes quiet when the door opens. The silence came across the field and pressed against the cottage like a tide going out, and in the silence Felix heard—clearly, closely, at a distance of no more than a few feet, though the field was two hundred yards away—a voice, right behind him, in the room, say:

*"Tell Marta the verse isn't her fault."*

He turned.

The room was golden and empty. Nine chairs. A lamp. A table. The recorder's red light. Nothing else. But the voice had been *there*, in the air behind his shoulder, warm as a caller's breath against a studio mic, and it had been the second caller's voice, the man who'd dissolved into static asking him to carry a message, and the message had been delivered, and the delivery had been witnessed, and there was no caller ID for the dead and there was no duration on the log and there was nothing, nothing, nothing left to file this under but the truth.

The choir started again. And now, as it sang, the voices began to separate, the way instruments separate when your ear learns a piece of music, and Felix stood at the window and took attendance, because attendance was all he had left.

Marta, humming her six notes. The second caller, steady and low. And others—voices he didn't know, voices from the boxes of photographs he hadn't opened, voices that had been singing this song in this field since before the razor, since before the gap in the records, since 1923 or earlier, a congregation with a century of practice and no last verse anyone living could sing.

And then, beneath them all, under the choir like a bass note under a hymn, he heard his grandmother.

He couldn't have said how he knew. He just knew, the way you know a hand on your shoulder in the dark. Edith was in the field. Edith was singing. And the part she was singing—the line that kept surfacing, over and over, patient as tide—was not from the hymnal at all.

It was the missing verse. She was singing it *to him*. Not performing it. *Teaching* it. And every time the line came around, the cold of the ninth chair reached across the room and found him, and the lamp flame leaned, and somewhere behind his sternum the note assembled itself again, patient, ready, waiting for his mouth to open.

Felix snapped the recorder off.

"No," he said, to the room, to the field, to the note behind his sternum. "I'm not singing it. You hear me? Whatever this is, whoever's listening—I'm not singing it."

The lamp flame stood up straight. The choir went on, soft and vast, and the last thing he heard before he gathered his bag and his recorder and left the hymnal's scar behind in the dark house was the choir's newest sound, added that night for the first time, threaded under the missing verse like a signature under a contract:

His own name. Sung by a hundred dead voices. In harmony.
