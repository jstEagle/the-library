## 7. What Marta Knew

The letter came first, and then the phone call, and I have thought about the order of those two things every day since, because I believe the order mattered.

The letter arrived on a Tuesday in a plain white envelope with no return address, postmarked from a town forty miles south of Hollowmere that I have never lived in. Inside was a single sheet of lined paper, torn from a spiral notebook, and written on it in my sister's handwriting—Marta's handwriting, which is to say the handwriting of a dead woman—were four words:

*Tell him about 1923.*

I sat at my kitchen table in Spokane and read it eleven times. Then I put it face-down under the fruit bowl, because there are things you do not leave looking at you while you make coffee.

The phone rang at 9:14 that night.

I want to say I didn't know who it would be. That's not true. I knew. I'd known since the letter, and maybe before the letter, if I'm honest—if I go all the way back to the obituary I clipped six months ago and kept in the drawer with my mother's recipes, telling myself I was keeping it for the funeral home records, telling myself a lot of things. When the phone rang I looked at it the way you look at a door when you already know what's standing behind it, and I picked it up anyway, because Marta never once called me after she died and I was not going to miss her now.

"Del." It wasn't a question. The voice was young, male, careful—the voice of a man holding something breakable. "My name is Felix Ashgrove. Edith Ashgrove was my grandmother."

"I know who you are," I said.

A pause. In the background of his silence I could hear a radio, faintly, some late-night station hissing between songs. He was calling from a studio or a room full of equipment. A man who had learned to keep recordings running.

"You've been expecting me," he said.

"Since Tuesday." I pulled the letter out from under the fruit bowl, though he couldn't see it. "You got something too, I take it. Something in her hand."

Another pause, longer. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone rougher. "How did you know that?"

"Because that's how it works with us, Mr. Ashgrove. The dead don't call strangers. They call family. And they don't waste words." I turned the letter over in my fingers. The paper had gone soft at the edges, like it had been handled a hundred times before it reached me, though it had come straight from the mailbox into my kitchen. "What did yours say?"

"'Ask Del,'" he said. "'Del knows the verse.'"

I laughed. I couldn't help it—it came out of me like something escaping, high and wrong in my own kitchen, and I heard him flinch through the line. "The verse," I said. "Right. Yes. Del knows the verse. Del has always known the verse. Do you know what I did with the verse, Mr. Ashgrove? I burned it. I wrote it out when I was nineteen years old so I would never forget it, and then I burned it in a coffee can on the fire escape of an apartment in Boise, and I watched every scrap go black, and I still know it. Every word. You can't burn a thing like that. That's the first thing you should understand. Whatever you're planning—and I can hear it in your voice, you're planning something—you need to understand that this doesn't live on paper. Paper is just where it rests."

"Mrs.—"

"Just Del."

"Del." He breathed. Through the line I heard him settle, the creak of a chair, the click of something being switched on—I'd bet my house it was a recorder; I'd have done the same. "I'm going to tell you what I know, and you're going to tell me if I'm crazy. Fair?"

"Nothing about this is fair," I said. "But go ahead."

* * *

He told me about the calls. I'll give it to you the way he gave it to me, as close to word for word as I can hold it, because I wrote it down afterward the way our family writes everything down, in the notebook I keep in the freezer behind the venison, where a fire won't find it.

He told me about a woman named Carol Denning who called his show and talked to him about the weather in a town she hadn't been alive in for six days. About a man who asked him to tell Marta the verse wasn't her fault. About the call logs that showed nothing—no numbers, no durations, no record at all except his own memory and, later, his own tapes.

"Marta," he said. "That was your sister."

"My sister died in June," I said. "Heart failure, they said. She was sixty-one. She'd had a bad valve since she was forty and the doctors said every year might be the year, and every year it wasn't, until the year it was." I stopped. My kitchen was very quiet. Outside, a neighbor's dog was barking at nothing, the way dogs do in October. "Except she didn't die of heart failure. Did she."

"No," Felix said quietly. "I don't think anyone in Hollowmere dies of what the certificate says."

"There it is," I said. "There's the sentence I've been waiting thirty years for someone else to say out loud."

And then—because he had earned it, because he had said the sentence, because he was Edith's blood whether he wanted to be or not—I told him.

* * *

I told him about 1923 the way my grandmother told me, sitting on the porch of a house outside Hollowmere in the summer of 1974, shelling peas into a bowl and speaking in the flat, unhurried voice of a woman describing drainage problems. That's how they all talk about it, the ones who stay. That flatness isn't calm. It's scar tissue.

In 1923, Hollowmere had the best harvest in the county's history and the worst winter anyone could remember. The wheat came in heavy and the money came in heavy and then the snow came early and stayed late and by March half the town was hungry and the other half was proud, which is worse. The founding families—the Vanes, the Calloways before that branch went soft, the Merricks, the Lowes—held a meeting in the hall that the historical society still pretends was about seed grain.

It wasn't about seed grain.

There was a man among them—not a founder, a hired man, a thresher named Aldous Wren, no relation to the Vanes despite the name, though the Vanes liked to imply one—who knew songs. Old ones. Songs from before the county had a name, brought down through families that worked fields the way other families worked iron: knowing what was in them. And he taught them a song. A harvest song, sweet as anything, the kind children could learn in an afternoon, and they sang it together in the field that spring, all of them, the whole town, because it was pretty and because Aldous Vane's grandfather said singing made the wheat grow, and everyone laughed and sang louder.

The song was a contract. That's the part nobody understood until it was too late. Every verse was a term. The planting verses promised labor. The growing verses promised care. And the last verse—the one that comes after the reaping, the one about what the field keeps—that verse promised payment. Sing it whole, in the field, at harvest, and the field takes what it's owed: one life, given willingly, sung into the ground. One life, and the town eats for another generation. Full bins. No blight. No rot. Weather that bends around Hollowmere like water around a stone.

"They didn't know," I told Felix. "That's what my grandmother swore, and I believe her, because she had no reason to lie to a nine-year-old on a porch. They sang the whole song at the harvest festival in 1923, all of them together, hundreds of voices, and Aldous Wren led it, and when the last note went into the ground, the field took him. Right there. Between one verse and the amen. He just—stopped. Folded down into the stubble like a coat falling off a hook. And the ground drank him, and the next year's harvest was the finest anyone had ever seen, and the founding families sat down in the hall and did the arithmetic."

"The arithmetic," Felix repeated. His voice had gone very careful again.

"One life per generation," I said. "Against two hundred lives fed. That's the arithmetic, Mr. Ashgrove. That's what they wrote in the ledger your historical society doesn't have anymore. And here's the thing you have to understand about people—they didn't feel like monsters. Monsters get to feel like monsters. They felt like practical men. They felt like stewards. They told themselves the field chose, that the song chose, that it was God's arrangement and they were just the bookkeepers. And then, very quietly, over about ten years, they discovered that the song didn't choose at all. The song takes whoever sings the last verse alone in the field. Anyone. Any voice. So they started choosing for it."

* * *

Here is where I have to slow down, because this is the part I have never said to another living soul, and my mouth knows it.

They chose the poor. Not out of cruelty—or not only out of cruelty, though cruelty grows in the dark like anything else—but out of strategy. A singer who wouldn't be missed. A thresher with no family. A widow whose kin had moved away. A girl from the wrong side of the creek. Someone whose absence could be explained—a runaway, a drowning, a heart failure—and whose lack of people meant no questions, no inheritance disputes, no one standing at the edge of the field demanding answers. The founding families never sang it. Not once in a hundred years. Not a Vane, not a Merrick, not a Lowe. They held the song like men hold a lease on other people's lungs.

"And my grandmother?" Felix asked. I could hear him not breathing.

"Edith Ashgrove was the last one," I said. "Nineteen years ago. And she wasn't chosen, Mr. Ashgrove. That's what nobody left alive understands, and it's why I'm telling you this instead of hanging up. Edith volunteered."

Silence on the line. Long enough that I checked the screen to see if the call had dropped. It hadn't. There was just a man in a dark room somewhere holding a phone against his face and trying not to make a sound.

"She volunteered," he finally said.

"For a girl named Priya," I said. "Priya Chandra. Sixteen years old. Her family had come to Hollowmere for work and stayed, which is what happens to people there—they arrive for work and the work becomes a wall. The Board chose her. Vane himself signed the entry—I know because Marta saw the ledger once, through a door she should never have looked through, and she carried what she saw like a stone in her chest for forty years. And Edith Ashgrove, who was sixty-eight years old and had buried a husband and had no one left but a grandson she saw twice a year, walked into the hall and told Aldous Vane that the Board had made an error. That the girl was a child. That if the field wanted a voice, it could have hers, and she'd thank them to keep their arithmetic out of it."

"Oh, God," Felix said. It didn't sound like swearing. It sounded like prayer arriving late.

"She sang it on the last night of harvest," I said. "Alone, in the field, the way it has to be done. And the wheat took her gently—I'll say that much for it, the field is honest even when the men aren't. She lay down in the stubble and the ground took her and the harvest came in golden and the town ate well and Aldous Vane wrote her name in the ledger with a steady hand. And Marta—" my voice did something I didn't authorize, cracked sideways, and I let it, because some things shouldn't be smoothed over—"Marta was Edith's friend. Had been since they were girls. And Marta spent the rest of her life trying to figure out how to end it instead of feed it, and the Board watched her do it, and when her year came up in the rotation—because yes, Mr. Ashgrove, there's a rotation, they plan decades ahead, they have a list—they took her. Heart failure, the certificate says. She was found at the edge of the north field."

The dog outside had stopped barking. My kitchen was so quiet I could hear the refrigerator thinking.

"So when a dead man calls your radio station and says *tell Marta the verse isn't her fault*," I said, "that's why. Because it wasn't. She spent forty years trying to unmake a contract, and the penalty for breach is written into the terms. The song protects itself. The founders built it that way—Wren's song, their ledger. You can't burn it, you can't refuse it, you can't run far enough. I ran three states and it mailed me a letter in my dead sister's handwriting."

* * *

"Then tell me how," Felix said. "Tell me how to end it."

And here, Mr. Ashgrove, is where I have to be honest with you, because you deserve better than the flat porch-voice, and because Marta would haunt me properly if I shortchanged you.

I don't know. Nobody knows. That's the truth of it. Marta believed there was a way—she used to say a contract is only as strong as its wording, and the wording of this one is a song, and songs can be sung wrong, sung incomplete, sung by the wrong mouth. She spent her last twenty years collecting fragments. She interviewed the old people before they died. She copied what she could of the razored hymnal before the Board got to it. She had a theory—I remember her saying it on this same phone, twenty years ago, her voice low like the walls of her kitchen had ears, and maybe they did—that the covenant needed three things to bind: the verse, the voice, and the consent of the bloodline. Take away any one of them and the whole thing sags. But she never found the missing piece. Or she found it and it found her first. I've stopped being able to tell those apart.

"What I know is this," I told him. "You're in the ledger now. You may have been in it since the day you were born—Edith traded herself for Priya, but debts like that don't cancel, they transfer, and the Board keeps tidy books. If you go into that field and sing the last verse, you die, and Hollowmere eats for another generation, and Vane writes your name in a steady hand. If you refuse, the field starts collecting on its own schedule, and it is not gentle, and it does not limit itself to one. And if you try to run—" I touched the letter, still lying on my table, soft-edged and patient. "Well. You've seen how far running gets you."

"Then what am I supposed to do?"

"Listen," I said. "That's all I've got, and I think it might be everything. Marta always said the song wanted a voice—but she also said, near the end, when she was tired and past caring who heard her, that a song is only half sound. The other half is hearing. Everyone in Hollowmere for a hundred years has been so busy making sure nobody hears the last verse that they forgot to ask what happens if somebody does. Whole. All the way through. From a mouth that never joins in." I closed my eyes. "She said the dead have been trying to finish the song for a hundred years, Felix. Every singer the field took is still in there, still humming, still holding their piece of it. Maybe they're not prisoners. Maybe they're waiting for an audience."

"That's insane," he said. Without heat. The way you say a thing you're afraid might be true.

"Probably," I agreed. "So was a woman walking into a wheat field to die in place of a sixteen-year-old girl, and the wheat said yes. Insanity and holiness wear the same coat out here. Ask the field which one it was."

We were quiet together for a while, two people three hundred miles apart listening to the same hum on the line—that faint tone underneath every long-distance call, except I have never believed it was only electricity, not since June.

"Felix," I said, before he could hang up. "One more thing, and then I'm going to pour myself a drink and sleep with the lights on like a grown woman. The chair. You've seen the chair."

Not a question. I heard him stop breathing entirely.

"In the photographs," he managed.

"In the photographs. An empty chair at every table, in every picture, going back further than film should allow. We called it the Waiting Chair. My grandmother said it was the field's seat at every table—the guest of honor, the one the meal is ultimately for. As long as the chair stays empty, the debt stands. It's the covenant showing itself to the bloodline, because the bloodline is who it's waiting on." I turned the letter over one more time. Four words in my sister's hand. *Tell him about 1923.* Underneath them, fainter, pressed so lightly I'd missed it on the first eleven readings, a fifth line had surfaced sometime between the mailbox and midnight, the way things surface:

*The ninth chair is his.*

"Go home, Felix," I said. "Go back to your grandmother's cottage, and count the chairs, and whatever you find sitting in the ninth one—don't sit in it yourself, and don't look away from it either. And when the choir sings tonight, and it will sing, listen. Listen all the way to the end. Marta believed the last verse was never actually cut out of the world. Only out of the paper."

The line hummed. Somewhere behind his breath, faintly, through his window or his walls or the open door of a studio, I heard it—three notes, rising, the first phrase of a song I have spent thirty years refusing to hum.

"Don't sing along," I whispered.

"I won't," he said.

But we both heard it, in the pause before he hung up: his voice, saying *I won't*, had fallen without either of us noticing into the exact cadence of the second phrase, and neither of us mentioned it, and that silence is the thing I will regret longest.

The line went dead at 10:03. The call log, he told me later, showed nothing at all.

I poured the drink. I slept with the lights on. And in the morning there was a sixth line on the letter, pressed lighter than the fifth, in handwriting that was not my sister's—older, rounder, patient as weather:

*Come home, child. Bring nothing you love.*
