## 17. Foundation Stone

Priya hadn't slept. She'd sat in the cottage kitchen with the razored fragment flat on the table under a flashlight beam, reading it until the words stopped being words and became a shape, a keyhole, and then she'd read them again because the alternative was thinking about what came next.

Felix slept in the chair by the window, his head tipped back, the wax cylinder case cradled against his chest like something he was afraid someone would take from him in the night. Even asleep he looked like a man holding a door shut.

At four-forty she woke him without touching him—just said his name once, quietly, the way you'd wake a dog you weren't sure of. His eyes opened all the way at once, no fog, no confusion. He'd been sleeping like that for days now. Ready to be awake.

"It's time," she said.

He looked at the window. The sky beyond it was still black, but not empty black—the east had a bruise of grey in it, the color the sky got before dawn when dawn wasn't sure it wanted to come.

"The stone's on the north side," Priya said. "Under the hall's foundation. Del told me where. The Board laid it in 1923 with the verse inside, sealed in lead, because lead keeps a promise." She folded the fragment into a tin and put the tin in her coat pocket. "They wrote it down so they'd never have to carry it in their mouths."

Felix stood, rolled his shoulders, and winced. "And if we're wrong?"

"Then we're wrong at dawn instead of at midnight," Priya said. "I'd rather be wrong in daylight."

Outside, the air had weight. Not cold exactly—heavy, the way air gets before a storm that never breaks, pressure sitting on the skin like a hand learning your shape. The wheat around Edith's cottage had gone still. Not calm. Still. There was a difference, and Priya felt it in her teeth: calm is quiet because nothing's happening; this was quiet because everything was waiting.

They took the shovel from the cottage shed and walked into Hollowmere with the sky going grey above them and the village absolutely silent. No dogs. No birds. Priya noticed that most of all—the birds were gone, and she couldn't remember when they'd left.

The Board hall stood at the end of Church Lane, a long low building of dark timber that had been old before anyone alive was born. Its windows were shuttered. Its door was closed. But as they crossed the lane Priya saw light moving behind the shutters—not lamplight, which flickers, but a steady glow, like something burning without consuming anything.

"Someone's in there," Felix whispered.

"Everyone's in there," Priya said. "That's the point. They'll be watching the field. They think the harvest comes for the field first."

She found the north wall by feel, counting stones from the corner the way Del had described it in her letter—*nine courses up, third stone from the corner, the one that doesn't match.* It didn't take long to find. The stone was paler than its neighbors, almost white, and when Priya pressed her palm against it the surface was warm. Warm as skin. Warm as a forehead before fever.

Felix drove the shovel blade into the mortar line and the mortar crumbled like bread. Nine courses down the stone came free, and behind it was a cavity lined with lead sheeting, and in the cavity a box no bigger than a hymnal, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark.

Priya unwrapped it with hands that wouldn't quite obey her. Inside was a page—real paper, heavy stock, written in iron-gall ink that had faded to the brown of dried blood. At the top, in a different hand, older and more careful:

*The Verse of Proxy, 1923. To be spoken by the chosen, never by the signers.*

Below it, the full final verse. Whole. Unrazored. And beneath the verse, nine signatures.

Priya read the names aloud without meaning to. "Vane. Calloway. Ashgrove—" She stopped.

"Ashgrove," Felix repeated.

"Your family signed too," Priya said. "Edith didn't just sing it, Felix. Her people wrote it. That's why she took my place. That's why she never let me sing. She was paying her own family's debt."

Felix didn't say anything. He held out his hand for the page, and Priya gave it to him, and he read it through twice, lips moving but voiceless, and then he folded it along its original creases and put it inside his jacket, over his heart.

"Read it," Priya said. "Don't sing it. Read it. Like a document. Like a court record."

"I know," Felix said. "Del told me. Ruth's cylinder told me. I know what I have to do." He looked up at the greying sky. "But not here. Out there. Where they can see."

* * *

The field at dawn was the wrong color.

Priya had grown up with that wheat; she knew every shade it owned, gold to green to the pale straw of August. This was none of them. It had gone the deep red-brown of things drying from the inside, and it moved without wind, a slow tidal sway, all one direction, toward the treeline, away from the village—as if the field itself were leaning toward something coming.

They stood at the field's edge where the ground gave way to stubble, and Priya took out the tin with the razored fragment, and Felix took out the page, and between the two of them the song lay complete for the first time in a hundred years.

"They're here," Felix said.

She'd heard them too—the crunch of boots on the lane, unhurried, deliberate. Aldous Vane came first, walking with his cane even though Priya would have bet money he didn't need it anymore, dressed as though for church, three men behind him and two women, the remnant of the Board, their faces lit orange by torches they carried though the sun was already bleeding over the hills. Behind them, further back, others—villagers, dozens, some holding torches, some holding tools, some holding nothing at all, drawn out of their houses by whatever the Board had let slip or could no longer contain.

"Mr. Ashgrove," Vane called across the stubble. His voice was pleasant. It was always pleasant. "Miss Suresh. You've been busy."

"Give it back," one of the women said, and her voice cracked in the middle. "The stone. Give it back and maybe it stops."

"Maybe it stops," Priya echoed. She stepped forward so she stood slightly ahead of Felix, an old habit from a lifetime ago, standing between her cousin and the thing in the wheat. "You've had a hundred years to make it stop. A hundred years of Martas. Of Ediths. Of children nobody would miss. And every year you sang *thank you* over the harvest supper and went home to your warm houses."

Vane's eyes went to Felix's jacket, to the shape of the folded page pressing against the cloth. "You don't understand what you're holding, boy. That verse isn't a weapon. It's a dam. Tear it open and the water goes everywhere. Sing it, give the fields their singer, and Hollowmere lives another generation. That's the bargain. It has always been the bargain."

"Then sing it yourself," Priya said.

The silence that followed had texture. The torches crackled. Somewhere behind the Board, a villager began to cry.

"You know I can't," Vane said, and for the first time his pleasantness slipped, and underneath it Priya saw something ancient and terrified. "The signers' blood can't carry it. That's the whole design. We built it so it *couldn't* be us. So it would always be—" he gestured, vague, disgusted, at the villagers, at the poor houses at the village's edge, at the unmarked graves, "—someone suitable."

"Someone expendable," Felix said.

"Someone *available*," Vane snapped, and the mask was fully off now, and what was underneath was worse than cruelty—it was bookkeeping. "One life, Mr. Ashgrove. One life per generation, and a town fed and prosperous for twenty-five years. Show me the arithmetic where that isn't mercy."

Priya laughed. She couldn't help it. It came out of her like something breaking. "Mercy. My grandmother-figure chose the wheat at fifty-one so I could run. Marta chose it so her sister could leave. And you call it arithmetic." She pulled the razored fragment from the tin and held it up. "We have all of it now, Aldous. Every word. And we know what you did with the key page—you hid it in a wall because you couldn't bear to hold it, and you couldn't bear to burn it, because burning it means the debt comes due on *you*."

The field moved.

There was no wind. There had been no wind all morning. But the wheat bent as one stalk, all of it, a bowing, a curtsy, and from somewhere deep in the red-brown mass came a sound Priya had heard once before, at seventeen, through a closed window, on the night Edith walked out: singing. Dozens of voices. Hundreds. The dead callers, the dead singers, the dead of a century of ledgers, rising through the grain like heat off a road.

Among them, clear as a bell over static, Felix's callers. Marta's hum. The man who'd asked Felix to tell her the verse wasn't her fault.

The villagers at the back began to scatter. Some of the Board dropped their torches. But Vane stood his ground, and his remnant stood with him, and one of the men drew a knife—a short, ugly, practical thing—and pointed it not at Priya, not at Felix, but at the folded page in Felix's jacket.

"Last chance," Vane said softly. "Burn it. Bury it. Forget you ever dug. The fields will find another way, but they'll find it slower, and maybe not in your lifetime, and isn't that all any of us can—"

"No," Felix said.

It wasn't loud. But it cut through the choir, through the wheat, through everything, because it was the voice of a man who talked to strangers in the dark for a living and knew exactly how much a single word could carry.

"No," he said again. He took the page from his jacket and unfolded it, and held it up so the whole field could see it, so the Board could see it, so the rising sun could catch the brown ink and make it shine. "You wrote this to be sung by proxy. By the poor. By the childless. By seventeen-year-old girls. Never by you." He turned to face the wheat. "So hear it the way you wrote it. Spoken. By proxy. By *me*—and I'm not signing, and I'm not singing, and I'm not yours."

Behind him the choir swelled, and Priya grabbed his arm—

"Felix," she breathed. "Now. Before it takes the words back."

He drew one breath, huge, the breath of a man about to go under and come up changed, and the wheat rose around them all like a green-brown wave deciding, and Aldous Vane lunged forward with his hand outstretched and screaming a word that might have been *no* or might have been a name—

—and Felix opened his mouth, and spoke.
