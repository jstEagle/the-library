## 18. The Last Verse

The wheat had stopped pretending to be wheat.

Felix saw it from the field's edge as the sky went the color of a bruise—stalks standing too straight, too dense, packed shoulder to shoulder like a crowd waiting for something. The harvest-dark gathered above it in a slow spiral, and inside that spiral the choir was singing, all of them now, every voice he'd heard through the phone lines over those first terrible weeks: Marta's low hum, the man who'd wanted Marta told she wasn't at fault, a dozen others he'd never known in life but knew now by their harmonies.

And beneath them, threading through like a wire pulled taut, his grandmother's voice. Edith Ashgrove, singing the verse she'd sung once and paid for with everything after.

Priya stood beside him with the razored fragment clutched against her chest like a child. The paper had gone soft at the folds, translucent with handling, thirty years of being carried away from Hollowmere and back again. Behind them, at the treeline, the foundation stone lay cracked open in its pit of turned earth, and the original page—the 1923 verse, written in Aldous Vane's grandfather's careful hand—was folded into Felix's jacket, over his heart, where it felt less like paper than like a coal.

"You shouldn't have dug it up."

Vane's voice came from behind them, and Felix turned slowly. Aldous Vane stood at the field's edge with six men behind him—founding-family faces, some old, some barely grown, all of them holding blades that caught no light because there was almost no light left to catch. Vane himself held nothing. He didn't need to. His hands were clasped behind his back like a man inspecting a garden party, and only his eyes moved, quick and hungry, tracking the folded page in Felix's jacket the way a dog tracks a wound.

"The covenant is insulted," Vane said. "You can feel it. We can all feel it." One of the younger men behind him made a small sound—not quite a sob—and Vane didn't turn to look at him. "The fields have come early. They're in my house, Felix. In my daughter's room. Singing." For one moment the courtesy cracked and something older showed through, something frightened and vicious. "You did this."

"No," Priya said. "Your family did this. A hundred years of it. You just never thought you'd be on the bill."

Vane ignored her. He took a step forward, onto the field's edge, where the soil was black and soft and gave slightly under his polished shoe, and Felix watched him do it without flinching, which told Felix more than anything else could: Aldous Vane had never once been afraid of the field. Why would he be? No Vane had ever been asked to feed it.

"Here is what happens now," Vane said, and his voice smoothed out again, reasonable, almost kind. "You sing the verse. Tonight, at the edge, before the dark comes down. And you live. That's the offer. Not just live—you'd be the warden. The keeper of the covenant. Edith's chair, filled properly, honored. The Board would be yours someday. The town would be yours." He spread his hands. "Or you don't sing, and the fields take Hollowmere entire. Every soul in it. I've seen what *entire* means, Felix. My grandfather wrote it down. It isn't death. Death would be a mercy they don't extend."

"And if I refuse?" Felix asked, though he already knew. He'd known since the cottage, since Edith's silent mouth shaping words across the table: *the verse was a lock.*

"Then I take the choice away," Vane said simply. "We've done it before. We'll hold you down and put the song in your mouth ourselves. The fields aren't particular about willingness—they never have been. Willingness is just cheaper." He smiled, and it was the smile from the dinner, the one that had made Felix understand how a century of murders could wear a cardigan and serve good wine. "But I'd rather not. I'd rather you chose it. It matters to them, I think. The singing. They like it given."

Behind Vane, the youngest of the men—a boy, really, maybe nineteen, with the Calloway jaw and none of Ruth's kindness—was crying openly now, blade shaking in his fist. The harvest-dark had reached the tops of the trees. The choir's song swelled, and Felix felt it in his teeth, in the fillings, in the hollow of his throat where his whole life a voice had lived and which the song wanted so badly it ached to feel wanted.

*His is the only one left that fits.* Del had said it. Edith had shown it. Nine chairs, eight full, and the ninth holding his shape.

"Felix." Priya's hand found his sleeve. Her voice was very quiet. "Whatever you're going to do. Do it fast. I can hear it under the song. The other thing. The hunger thing. It's getting closer to the town."

He could hear it too. Under the choir's melody there was a lower sound, vast and patient, the sound the wheat must make when it closes over someone—not wind, not rustle, but a kind of swallowing.

Felix took the page from his jacket.

Vane went still. The whole line of founding families went still, six statues with knives. Even the choir seemed to lean in, the melody thinning to a held breath.

"Good," Vane breathed. "Good. Now—"

"I'm not going to sing it."

The words came out steadier than Felix felt. He unfolded the page. The 1923 handwriting was beautiful—copperplate, confident, the hand of a man who'd written a murder contract and slept well after. The verse ran fourteen lines. Felix had read it eleven times since dawn, silently, learning it the way you learn the shape of a key: not to use it, but to know it.

"Then what—" Vane started.

"I'm going to read it."

Silence. Even the choir stopped.

"You'll give them the words," Vane said slowly, "and think that saves you? The words are nothing without the voice. That's the whole architecture of it, boy. My grandfather built it. Words are the lock. Voice is the turning. You can recite it until doomsday—"

"I know," Felix said. "That's the point."

He looked past Vane then, out into the wheat, at the place where the choir stood though no bodies showed—just a bending in the stalks, a hollow in the dark shaped like people. He found the bend that leaned closest, the one that had been nearest the treeline all night, patient, waiting for him the way she'd waited through every photograph.

"Edith," he said. "I need you to listen. All of you. Listen to the whole thing. Don't sing. Just—receive it. Like a letter."

And Felix Ashgrove, who had spent twenty years talking to strangers in the dark and calling it a living, lifted the founders' own page into the harvest-dark, and read.

---

*"O field that we have fed with blood,*  
*O furrow opened, o beloved ground,*  
*take now the voice we cannot spare,*  
*and keep the singer, safe and bound.*"

His voice cracked on *bound*. He kept reading.

*"One verse each generation, freely given,*  
*one throat to pay the acreage its due.*  
*What the signers sow, the chosen reap—*  
*the many fed by what the few go through."*

The wheat began to move. Not toward him—around him, a slow rotation, the whole field turning like a great listening ear. The harvest-dark dropped lower. Somewhere behind him Priya made a sound like prayer or protest, and he loved her for it and didn't stop.

*"No signer sings; no founder falls;*  
*the poor, the lone, the friendless pay—*  
*this is the pact, this is the law,*  
*and none may break it, none may say—"*

He faltered. Because there, in the twelfth line, in the copperplate confidence, was the flaw—or not the flaw, the hinge. The verse itself named its own injustice. The founders hadn't hidden what they'd done. They'd written it into the song, plain as a signature, certain no one would ever read it aloud without singing it, certain the words would always arrive wrapped in a willing voice, dissolved into melody, unexamined. A hundred years of singers had carried these words in their mouths and never been able to *say* them.

Until now.

Felix lifted his head. Vane had taken two steps forward without seeming to notice, his face working, his composure finally, fully gone.

"Stop," Vane said. "Stop reading. You don't know what you're—"

"—and none may say," Felix continued, louder, over him, over the rising wind, "*what the field has heard.*"

The last two lines:

*"So let the debt return to source:*  
*let what was sown in blood be thence*  
*reaped by the hands that set the price—*  
*the signers' voices, the founders' expense."*

He stopped.

For three heartbeats, nothing. The field stood still. The dark hung. Vane stared at him with an expression Felix would remember for the rest of his life—the expression of a man watching a document he believed unreadable being read aloud in court.

"It doesn't work that way," Vane whispered. "It needs a voice. It needs a *singer*. Words without a voice are nothing, they're inert, they're—"

"They're testimony," Felix said.

And the choir sang.

Not the harvest song. Something else—something that had been waiting under the melody for a hundred years, the way the verse had waited under the hymnal's stubbed page. The dead callers' voices rose together, Marta and the man and the nameless dozens, and under them Edith's voice, clear and unmistakable, and what they sang was the verse—his reading, his cadence, his flat unsung delivery—but *voiced*, given breath by throats that owed the fields nothing because the fields had already taken everything.

The words came back to the field wearing voices. The lock heard its own mechanism described, precisely, completely, by instruments that were not throats-for-hire.

The covenant had required a singer. It had never specified whose voice delivered the words—only that they be given whole, and truly, and heard. The founders had assumed no one would ever find a loophole because no one had ever read their beautiful handwriting closely enough to see that they'd written their own indictment into the anthem.

The wheat turned black from the roots up.

It happened fast, a darkness flooding the stalks like ink through water, and the harvest-dark overhead didn't descend—it *reversed*, pulling upward, drawn back into the field as the field drew something else up out of the ground. The soil along the far edge split open in a long seam, and from it rose the smell of 1923: cut wheat and lamp oil and old rain and, underneath, faintly, sweetly, wrong.

"Oh," said the young Calloway boy, dropping his blade. "Oh, God, Grandfather, what did you *do*—"

"What did *we* do," said another man, older, backing away. "Aldous. What did we *all*—"

"Don't," Vane snarled, wheeling on them. "Stand together. It needs consent, it needs—"

"It has consent," Felix said quietly. "Yours. You signed every entry in the ledger. You chose every singer. You fed it for forty years with your own hand." He nodded toward the blackening wheat, which was moving now, parting, opening a corridor into its depths like a throat deciding. "You wrote the terms, Aldous. I just read them back."

Vane looked at the field. Then—at the last moment, horribly human—he looked at Felix, and there was no chairman in his face at all anymore, no Board, no family, just an old man standing at the edge of a hole he'd spent his whole life believing other people would fall into.

"You could sing instead," Vane said. "Take the warden's chair. Save us. Save *yourself*. The ninth chair is yours, it will always be yours, the fields want you, they've *always* wanted—"

"That's exactly why I won't," Felix said. "They want me because I fit. Because I'm alone enough, grieving enough, expendable enough. Your system always found someone like me, Aldous. Someone with no one to miss them." He glanced at Priya, standing rigid beside him, the fragment pressed to her chest, tears cutting through the dirt on her face. "Not anymore."

The wheat took the first of them before Vane finished turning.

It didn't lunge. It didn't grab. The stalks simply closed around the oldest signer—the Halloway man, Felix thought, the one with the orchards—and when they parted, he was gone, and from somewhere deep in the black field came his voice, singing, high and terrified and entirely his own, the sound of a man discovering at the end that he'd had a voice all along and had only ever used it to say yes.

The others broke. Three ran for the trees and the wheat let them go—it wasn't finished sorting, Felix understood; it was reading its own ledger now, matching names to entries, and the runners might yet outrun their signatures. Two fell to their knees at the field's edge, praying aloud, and the wheat passed over them like a tide passing stones, because kneeling, it seemed, was a kind of testimony too.

Which left Aldous Vane, alone on the black soil, and the corridor open in front of him, and the choir singing his name—not menacingly, not gloatingly, just *accurately*, the way a clerk reads out the final entry.

Vane straightened his cardigan. Felix would think about that later, in the weeks after, in the dreams: that of all things, facing the field his family had built, Aldous Vane straightened his cardigan.

"My grandfather," Vane said, "believed the land belonged to those who could make it bear. Whatever this is—" he gestured at the dark, the choir, the closing corridor, "—it's just arithmetic. Debts and payments. And debts," his voice climbed, fraying, "can be *renegotiated*—"

The wheat took him mid-word.

There was no scream. There was something worse: silence, one full second of it, the whole field holding its breath. And then, from deep in the black stalks, Aldous Vane began to sing—in a voice that was suddenly, horribly, unmistakably his own, rich and trained and terrified, singing the harvest song complete for the first time in a century, every verse including the last, because the fields had finally found a Vane willing to give them his voice, and they had not asked.

Beside Felix, Priya sank down onto the black earth, and the fragment slipped from her fingers, and neither of them moved to catch it.

The choir's song changed. Felix felt it before he understood it—the melody loosening, the wire pulled taut for a hundred years suddenly going slack. The hunger-thing under the music, the vast patient swallowing, faded like a tide going out, and what remained was just... voices. People singing in a field at dusk. Grieving, maybe. Resting, maybe. Free of the terms, if not of the ground.

The ninth chair, Felix knew, was emptying.

He turned toward the cottage road, toward the table that had haunted every photograph of his childhood, and that was when he saw her—one figure standing apart from the dissolving choir, at the field's edge, in the last grey light. Small. Straight-backed. Wearing the blue dress from the photograph on the mantel.

Edith didn't speak. She never would again; some prices weren't refunded. But she raised one hand—not waving, not beckoning—and pointed, firmly, past Felix, past Priya, toward the treeline.

Toward the three runners, still crashing through the dark, still carrying their signatures into the world.

The fields, Felix realized, weren't finished reading the ledger either.

* * *

They followed the pointing hand.

Priya found the first one herself, fifty yards into the trees, huddled behind a fallen oak with his phone screen glowing uselessly in his hand—no signal, of course; Hollowmere's towers had been singing all night. He was one of the ones who'd knelt at the end, Felix saw; he'd crawled here after. When he looked up at them his face was wrecked and wet and, underneath the terror, disbelievingly alive.

"It let me go," he whispered. "It looked at me and it *let me go*. Why? I signed. God help me, I signed every year, I sat at the table, I—why am I still here?"

Felix crouched down to his level. Behind the man, deeper in the trees, the second runner was visible, sitting against a trunk, breathing. Alive. Both of them alive, both of them spared, and Felix understood it a half-second before he said it, the way you understand a song's meaning just before the chorus proves you right.

"Because the ledger needed witnesses," he said. "Men who could testify to what was written in it. The fields took the ones who fed them. They left the ones who can tell everyone else what the feeding cost." He stood. His legs ached; his throat was raw from reading; he had never felt better in his life. "Ruth Calloway kept a secret archive for thirty years. She's going to need help finishing it."

The man stared at him. Then, slowly, he turned his head toward the field—toward the black stalks settling back into ordinary gold-green, toward the distant sound of voices that were only voices now—and began, haltingly, terribly, to talk. Names. Dates. Entries. Thirty years of dinners where the wine was good and the agenda was murder, pouring out of him into the dark like water finding its level.

Priya came to stand beside Felix. Together they listened, two survivors collecting testimony under the trees while the dead sang softly behind them and the wheat, ordinary at last, bent and whispered in a wind that smelled only of rain coming.

"We should write this down," Priya said at last. Her voice was hoarse. Her hand found his and held on. "All of it. Before anyone can bury it again."

"Yeah," Felix said. "I know someone with a transmitter."

Far off across the valley, in the direction of the historical society, a light was burning in a window that should have been dark—and moving, urgently, from shelf to shelf to shelf, like someone racing to get everything down on paper before morning came.
