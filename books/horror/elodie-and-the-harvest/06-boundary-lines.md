## 6. Boundary Lines

The county line was a rusted sign on a leaning post, half-swallowed by honeysuckle, and it read WELCOME TO HARLOW COUNTY in letters someone had shot through the O's decades ago. August stood at the edge of the shoulder with the engine idling behind him and thought: this is where rationality goes to die. A line on a map. Paint and rust and a drainage ditch full of June heat.

"Drive," Maren said from the passenger seat.

"I'm driving."

"You're standing."

He was. He got back in, put the car in gear, and looked at the three of them crammed into his sedan like refugees: Maren up front, jaw set, her dream-journal open on her knees; Devan slouched behind her with his arms crossed, radiating the sullen fury of a man who had been dragged out of bed at four in the morning; and Tomas, sixteen, in the middle of the back seat, still soft-faced, still carrying the particular patience of the youngest sibling who has learned that no one listens to him anyway.

They had argued about bringing Tomas for two hours before dawn. Maren had won by pointing out that if residency was what bound them — blood, proximity, acknowledgment — then leaving him home alone in Hollowmere was not safety but exposure. August hadn't liked the logic. He'd liked even less that he couldn't refute it.

"Slow," Maren said. "I want to watch him cross."

August took the car over the line at fifteen miles an hour. The tires made the same sound they'd made on every mile of road since the farmhouse. Nothing changed. The honeysuckle stayed honeysuckle. The ditch stayed a ditch.

Then he heard Devan say, very quietly, "Tomas."

August looked in the mirror.

The boy was still there. That was the first thing — he was *there*, seated, seatbelt fastened, hands in his lap. But he was wrong the way an old photograph is wrong when the light hits it at an angle: the colors gone thin, the edges bleeding into the upholstery behind him. His face had the grainy, underexposed quality of a print left too long in a drawer. When he raised his hand — slowly, confusedly, the way you check whether you're dreaming — August could see the headrest through the fingers.

"Tomas." Maren had turned fully around. "Say something."

"I feel—" The voice came from far away, or from underwater, or from both. "It's like being erased. Like someone's rubbing me out."

"Back," Devan said. "Go back, go back—"

August swung the car around so hard the tires barked. They crossed the line again going the other direction, and it was like watching a photograph develop in reverse: color flooding back into Tomas's face from the center outward, edges sharpening, the headrest solidifying behind him until there was nothing to see through his fingers at all. He sat breathing hard, gripping his own wrists, checking himself the way you check for a wallet after a pickpocket brushes past.

"It knew," Tomas said. "The second we crossed. It didn't chase us. It just started... subtracting."

Nobody spoke for a while. The car idled on the Hollowmere side of the line, and August stared at the rusted sign as if it might explain itself.

*Residency is defined by blood, proximity, and acknowledgment.* Maren's words from two nights ago, delivered over the family ledger with its columns of names and dates, one harvested per generation, always after the papers signed. He had wanted to believe the ledger was superstition dressed up as bookkeeping. But the ledger had arithmetic, and arithmetic didn't care what you believed.

"The township," Maren said. "Not the county. I crossed into Harlow County last spring for a wedding. Nothing happened." She tapped the map spread across her knees — she'd drawn the boundary herself, in red pen, from old plat records. "It's the township line. Hollowmere proper. We're not trapped in our lives. We're trapped in the *jurisdiction*."

"That's insane," Devan said.

"Yes," Maren said. "And?"

Devan had no answer. August watched him in the mirror — the big brother who'd spent thirty years pretending none of this was real, now sitting in a car that had just demonstrated otherwise, on a boy who had begun to fade like bad film.

"We test it once more," August said. "On foot. Slowly. I want to know exactly where the line sits."

"No," Tomas said.

Everyone looked at him. The boy's voice had steadied.

"You want to find the exact spot where I stop existing so you can write it down." He wasn't accusing them. He was just tired. "Fine. But I'm not walking across it twice today. Once was enough."

Maren reached back and took his hand. August turned the car around and drove them home, and in the rearview mirror Tomas watched the county-line sign recede like something drowning.

The church archive was in the basement of St. Brigid's, which was no longer a church — it had been deconsecrated sometime in the nineties and sold to the historical society, which meant the archive was technically a museum, which meant August could walk in during open hours and ask for records without explaining why a schoolteacher needed sixty-year-old parish documents.

Father Anselm — not a father anymore, just Anselm, a retired archivist with reading glasses on a chain and the unhurried manner of a man who had catalogued ten thousand dead people's paperwork — led him down the stairs past shelves of deed boxes and baptismal registers.

"Hollowmere families," Anselm said. "Kavanagh. You'll want the probate copies and anything touching land covenants. Covenants are the interesting ones. Everyone thinks the interesting papers are wills. Wills are just wishes. Covenants are promises, and promises get enforced."

"How do you mean?"

Anselm smiled faintly. "This county has land covenants older than the county. Some of them have conditions attached that lawyers stopped trying to interpret around 1900. There's one on your grandmother's parcel — I've seen it referenced. Never seen the original." He pulled a box off the shelf and blew dust off the lid. "But references point somewhere. Follow the references."

The reference pointed to a folder labeled, in faded iron-gall ink, *KAVANAGH — INSTRUMENT OF ACCOMMODATION, 1965.*

Accommodation. Not sale, not lease, not easement. Accommodation. As though the land were doing someone a favor.

August carried the folder to the reading table under the basement's single window and opened it with the care he'd use on a student's exam — the same professional detachment, the same refusal to be moved before all the evidence was in.

The document was four pages. The first two were legalese of a kind he half-recognized: metes and bounds, consideration paid and received, parties of the first part and second. The party of the second part was named only as *the Count, his heirs and assigns,* which should have been disqualifying right there — no lawyer drafts a contract with an entity, only entities draft contracts with entities — but the clerk's hand was steady and the seal was the county's own, pressed deep and brown with age.

The third page listed terms. August read them three times.

*One soul of the Kavanagh line, tendered per generation, upon finalization of inheritance and lawful residence of the offering within the bounds described herein.* The bounds described were the township of Hollowmere, its lines drawn with surveyor's precision, each corner marked by coordinates he could have plotted on Maren's map. *Deferral may be granted indefinitely so long as the hearth-fire of the principal dwelling burns without interruption, it being understood that a kept fire is a kept promise, and a kept promise defers collection.*

His grandmother's hearth. Sixty years without going cold. He had thought it was superstition, sentiment, an old woman's stubbornness. It had been a stay of execution, renewed daily, for six decades.

And then the fourth page, and the signatures.

There were two. The first was a name he didn't know — a flowing, confident signature that seemed to press harder than ink should press, as though the pen had been driven through the paper: *Aurelius Vane.* The witness line beneath it held the same name again, in the same hand. A contract witnessed by itself. August felt the hair rise on his arms and told himself it was the basement's chill.

The second signature was small, careful, and heartbreakingly young:

*Eleanor Kavanagh.*

His grandmother. Nineteen years old, if the date held — dated sixty years ago almost to the week. She had signed away one descendant per generation to keep the rest alive, and she had come home and lit a fire and never, not once in sixty years, let it go out.

August sat with the page for a long time. Grief and resentment had been tangled in him since the funeral; now a third strand wound in, and it was worse than either: understanding. She hadn't believed in superstitions. She had *known*. And she had let him storm out at seventeen, calling her a madwoman, because telling him the truth would have been worse than losing him.

Below Eleanor's name, the witness line.

*Witnessed:* and then a signature in a plain, unremarkable hand, the kind of handwriting you'd see on a permission slip.

*W. Pruitt.*

August read it twice. Then a third time, because the name wouldn't settle into sense.

Walter Pruitt. Walter Pruitt was on his class roster. Seat eleven. One of the three desks that stood empty every morning, one of the three names the principal had waved away with a folder of transfers whose destination lines were blank.

He checked the date against the enrollment records in his head — he couldn't help it, the teacher's reflex, the arithmetic of age. If Walter Pruitt had been old enough to witness a contract in 1965, he would be seventy-something now. Not eleven. Not enrolled in the fifth grade at Hollowmere School.

Unless the Count's heirs and assigns included more than the Count.

Unless the roster itself was part of the instrument.

August closed the folder, thanked Anselm, and climbed the stairs into the June light feeling the township's borders close around him like a hand. The trap wasn't supernatural. That was the horror of it, the thing he would carry up those stairs and out into the ordinary afternoon: the trap was *legal*. Signed, sealed, witnessed, filed. Every escape route out of Hollowmere ran through a boundary that had been surveyed and recorded before he was born, and the entity holding the paper had never needed to chase anyone. It only ever had to wait for the paperwork.

That night he called Maren from the farmhouse, the hearth burning behind him because he no longer dared let it do anything else.

"Maren. The witness on the contract. Look at your ledger — is there a Pruitt in it? Any Pruitt, any generation?"

A pause. Pages turning. Then her voice came back flat and careful, the voice of someone reading their own death warrant.

"There's a Pruitt marriage in 1964. And August — every entry after that, every harvest, every signing. The witnessing hand is the same."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying Walter Pruitt isn't a student who transferred." The fire popped in the hearth, and down the line, Maren's breath went shallow. "He's how it keeps count. And tomorrow morning, he'll be sitting in your classroom — and he'll know you've been to the archive."
