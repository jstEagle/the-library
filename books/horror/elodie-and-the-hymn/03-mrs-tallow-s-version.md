## 3. Mrs. Tallow's Version

The house at the end of Tallow Lane leaned like a tired man against a wall, its thatch sagging over windows that had gone the color of weak tea. Oscar Amberdale stood at the gate for a moment before knocking, letting the recorder in her bag stay switched off. She had learned something in three days of Hollowmere: the villagers were generous with their songs right up until the machine appeared. Then the songs became shorter, or they stopped mid-phrase, or — most often — they simply weren't there anymore, replaced by a polite smile and an offer of tea she was expected to refuse.

Mrs. Tallow opened the door before she knocked.

"You're the one writing it all down." The old woman's voice was dry as seed husks. Her eyes were pale blue and startlingly direct, set in a face like crumpled parchment, and she looked at Oscar the way a farmer looks at weather coming in from the west — not hostile, just measuring what it would cost her.

"Folklorist," Oscar said. "I record songs. Stories. I'm not writing anything down that you don't want written."

"Everything gets written down eventually." Mrs. Tallow stepped back from the doorway, which Oscar understood was an invitation, though it had the warmth of a cell door left unlocked. "Come in, then. Mind the cat."

There was no cat. Oscar looked anyway, out of politeness, and saw only a worn patch on the second stair where generations of feet had polished the wood to the color of dark honey. The cottage smelled of lavender and lamp oil and, beneath both, something older — pond water, maybe, or stone that hadn't dried since it was laid. Every house in Hollowmere had that undertone. She'd stopped noticing it by the second day, which troubled her more than if she'd kept noticing.

The front room was small and fiercely tidy. A fire burned low in a black-leaded grate. On the mantelpiece stood a row of photographs so old they had gone sepia and then gone further, into a brown so deep the faces seemed to be drowning in it. Oscar counted seven frames. In six of them, children.

"You'll want me to sing," Mrs. Tallow said. It wasn't a question. She lowered herself into a chair by the fire with the careful economy of someone whose joints had opinions, and folded her hands in her lap. They were large hands, work-worn, spotted like a thrush's egg. "Dan Hobb will have told you I'm the one with the old ones still in my head. He's right. I've got more songs than teeth, and I'm keeping both till I go."

"I'd be grateful for whatever you're willing to share."

"Willing." The word came out with a small, sharp edge, like a sliver of glass in bread. Mrs. Tallow gazed into the fire. "That's a funny way of putting it. Everyone here is willing, girl. That's the trouble with this village. We're all too willing."

Oscar took out her recorder but didn't switch it on yet. She had learned that too: let them begin first. Let the song exist once in the air, unrecorded, belonging only to the room. Then ask permission. It felt superstitious, and she distrusted how easily superstition had taken root in her since arriving, the way moss takes root in a damp wall — without being asked, without effort, simply because the conditions were right.

"Start wherever you like," she said.

Mrs. Tallow started with a harvesting song, all call and response, the kind that needed a dozen voices to work properly and sounded strange sung alone, like hearing one hand clapping. Her voice was thin but true, and when she reached the refrain her hands moved unconsciously, miming the sweep of scythes, the gathering of sheaves. Oscar watched the old woman's body remember what her mouth was doing and felt the familiar ache of her trade — the beauty of things that existed only in passing, that died a little every time they were sung and could never be sung the same way twice.

Then, without pause, without announcing it, Mrs. Tallow began another tune.

It was different from everything else Oscar had collected in Hollowmere. Slower. Minor key, or something adjacent to minor — modal, old, the kind of melody that predated the idea of major and minor altogether. Four bars, rising and falling like breath, like water swelling against a weir. And under the melody, though Mrs. Tallow sang no words, Oscar heard words anyway, hovering just below audibility, the way you can hear your name across a crowded room. A hymn. She knew it was a hymn the way you know a stranger is grieving: not by evidence but by atmosphere.

Four bars. Five. The fire ticked. Somewhere outside a rook called once and thought better of it.

On the sixth bar, Mrs. Tallow stopped.

Not trailed off. Not forgot. *Stopped* — the way a clock stops, all at once, every part of her going still. Her hands, which had been moving gently in her lap, froze mid-gesture, fingers curled around nothing. Her eyes had gone wide and fixed on some middle distance where, presumably, something was standing.

The silence lasted four seconds. Oscar counted them. It was a habit; silences in interviews had lengths, and the lengths meant things. This one was the length of genuine fear.

"We don't sing that twice." Mrs. Tallow's voice came out flat and quiet, stripped of everything performative. Her hands had begun to shake — not the tremor of age, which is a fine, constant vibration, but a coarse shaking that moved up from the wrists. She pressed them flat against her skirt to still them and mostly failed. "You understand me? Whatever happens, however it starts, we don't sing it twice. Once is giving. Twice is asking."

"Asking for what?" Oscar kept her voice level. Professional. Inside, something had begun to lean forward, the way it always did, hungry and slightly ashamed of itself.

Mrs. Tallow turned and looked at her properly for the first time. Up close, her pale eyes had flecks in them like iron filings, and they went through Oscar rather than at her, as though checking the room behind her for eavesdroppers.

"For an answer," she said.

* * *

Oscar walked back to the Grey Lamb through streets going blue with dusk, turning the phrase over. *Once is giving. Twice is asking.* It was the first time anyone in Hollowmere had named the rule outright. Three days of interviews, eleven residents, and every one of them had done the same thing: sung each piece exactly once, beautifully, completely, and then closed like a shop at closing time. When she'd asked Dan Hobb about it his first evening, he'd laughed and said country folk didn't hold with encores, songs weren't fireworks. But he'd laughed a half-second too late, and his eyes had gone to the window while he did it, and now she understood that laugh for what it was — a door held shut with a joke.

She asked him again that night, in the Grey Lamb's back parlour, over a supper of ham and eggs she barely tasted.

"Twice is asking," Dan repeated. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, fifty-some years old, with forearms that remembered farm work and a face that had decided long ago to give away as little as possible. He cut his food into very small pieces before eating it. "Who told you that?"

"Mrs. Tallow."

"Ah." He put down his knife and fork, aligned them parallel on the plate's rim, and looked at her with something that might have been pity in a man less guarded. "Edith Tallow doesn't say things for effect, Miss Amberdale. If she told you that, it's because she judged you needed telling. Which means—" He stopped himself. Took up the knife again. "Which means she thinks you'll hear it somewhere else anyway."

"Hear what somewhere else?"

"The hymn." He said it lightly, carelessly, the way you'd say *the weather*, and the lightness was the loudest thing in the room. "Old tune. Nobody sings it. That's all there is to tell."

"Nobody sings it, but everyone knows it."

"That's Hollowmere for you." He smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "We keep a lot of things we don't use. Like good china. You'll finish your eggs."

Upstairs, in the narrow room under the eaves with its sloped ceiling and its bed that creaked like a boat, Oscar sat cross-legged with her recorder and listened back through the day's material. The harvesting song, complete. Two ballads from Mr. Fen, the schoolmaster, delivered in a papery tenor with the precision of a man who had once taught children to parse sentences. A lullaby from the Postmistress, sung so quietly the microphone had barely caught it, trailing off at the end into humming and then into nothing, as though the singer had fallen asleep inside her own song.

And then Mrs. Tallow's hymn. Four bars. Five. Six, incomplete, cut off at the top of a phrase that clearly wanted to resolve downward and never got the chance.

Oscar played it eleven times. She transcribed it onto staff paper by lamplight, working out the intervals by ear, and the more she worked the stranger the tune felt in her hands — not difficult, exactly, but *slanted*, like a staircase in a dream that looks normal and puts your foot down wrong. The sixth bar ended on a suspended note, unresolved, a question mark made of sound. Every musical instinct she had screamed that the next note was obvious, inevitable, one step down, home — and that the tune was unbearable precisely because it never arrived.

She caught herself humming the resolution. Just the single missing note, under her breath, testing it.

The room felt colder. She told herself it was the eaves, the draft, October pressing at the little window. She wrote the note into the transcription anyway, bracketed, marked *implied*. Then she looked at the bracketed note for a long time and erased it, and felt foolish, and wrote it back in.

Sleep came late and went early. In the grey hour before dawn she surfaced from a dream she wouldn't remember — something about water, something about a door under water — and lay listening to the inn settle, and thought, with the strange clarity of the half-awake: *I should check the recorder.*

She'd left it running. She always left it running now; it had become a compulsion she wasn't examining, the way you check a lock twice. She reached for it on the bedside table, thumbed it on, and pressed playback to the overnight file.

Forty minutes of silence first. Room tone. The inn breathing. Then, at 2:17 a.m., her own voice, thick with sleep, saying words she had no memory of saying:

*"Where does the sixth bar go?"*

A pause. Long enough to be an answer in itself.

Then a second voice. Thin. Young. A girl's voice, unhurried, coming from nowhere the microphone could locate — not across the room, not near the machine, but somehow *inside* the recording, the way a voice sounds underwater or through a wall, present and muffled at once.

*"Down,"* the girl said. *"It goes down. Everything in that song goes down. That's why they stopped singing it. They didn't want to follow."*

Oscar lay absolutely still, the recorder warm in her hand, her heart performing slow, deliberate work.

Rationalization arrived on schedule, dutiful as a milk cart. Radio interference. A dream-voice, her own pitch shifted by sleep. The microphone picking up something from the street — a child, no, no child would be out at 2 a.m. in a village of nineteen people, a night bird then, a bird that sounded like a girl. She catalogued the explanations one by one and found, with mounting unease, that she believed none of them, and that the failure of belief felt less like fear than like recognition. As if some part of her had been waiting for the second voice the way you wait for a train you pretend not to be meeting.

Her sleeping self spoke again on the tape. *"Sing it for me. The rest."*

And the girl's voice — Elodie's voice, though Oscar didn't know the name yet, wouldn't learn it for days — began to sing.

It was the hymn. Mrs. Tallow's hymn, the four bars and five bars and the broken sixth, and then onward, past the place where the old woman had stopped cold, into territory no living resident of Hollowmere had entered in fifty years. The seventh bar. The eighth. The melody resolved downward, exactly as Oscar's instincts had promised, note after note descending like footsteps on a stair, like water finding its level, and the words came with it now, faint but unmistakable, in a register between speech and song:

*Hush now, hush, the day is done,*
*the pond holds fast what pond has won.*
*No cage, no chain, no locked-up door —*
*only love, and nothing more.*

It was the gentlest thing Oscar had ever heard, and it was the worst. Because it wasn't a threat. It wasn't a curse. Sung like this, soft and patient and infinitely sad, it was a lullaby — a lullaby sung by someone who had been left alone with it for a very long time, who knew every note the way a prisoner knows every crack in a wall, and who was singing it, at 2:40 in the morning, to a sleeping stranger in a rented room.

The hymn ended. The tape ran on in silence for a moment. Then the young voice, very close now, almost tender, said:

*"You hummed my note tonight. Under your breath. I felt it."*

A pause, in which Oscar's own breath on the recording hitched — the sound of a sleeper dreaming of falling.

*"Ask Edith Tallow,"* the voice said, *"what they buried under the chapel floor. Ask her kindly. She's been carrying it longer than anyone."*

The tape hissed. At 3:04 a.m., her sleeping self murmured one last thing, so quietly the microphone nearly lost it:

*"Elodie."*

Just the name. Twice, as if confirming a signature.

Oscar sat in the grey light with the recorder cooling in her hands and the transcription of six bars lying on the quilt, and she understood that the question she had come to Hollowmere to ask — *what songs do you have?* — had been answered, comprehensively, by something that was not among the living. Outside her window, mist was coming off the fields in slow ropes, and down the lane the chapel stood black against the paling sky, and beneath its floorboards, under forty years of dust and prayer books and dead flies, something waited that had just been given permission, at last, to speak.

She reached for her notebook and wrote, in handwriting that shook only a little:

*Day 4. There is a rule: we don't sing that twice. There is a hymn nobody finishes. There is a girl who finishes it for us.*

*Find out who Elodie is.*
