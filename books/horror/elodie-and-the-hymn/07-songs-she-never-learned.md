## 7. Songs She Never Learned

The humming started on the fourth morning.

Oscar noticed it the way you notice a draft — not as an event but as a change in the room's temperature, something felt at the back of the neck before it was understood. She was standing at the Grey Lamb's narrow window with a mug of tea going cold in her hand, watching mist peel off the mill pond like skin off milk, and she realized she had been making a sound for some time without knowing it. Four notes, then a pause, then four more. A melody with a shape she recognized the way you recognize a face in a crowd of strangers — familiar and entirely unknown at once.

She stopped. The silence after felt wrong, like a held breath.

She didn't know the tune. That was the simple fact of it, and she tested the fact the way she tested everything, professionally, with a folklorist's suspicion of her own memory. She knew perhaps three hundred traditional melodies by heart — Child ballads, wassails, the long winding laments of the Welsh borders — and this wasn't any of them. It wasn't modal in any way she could name. It moved strangely, stepping where she expected leaps, pausing where she expected motion, and there was one phrase near its middle that turned downward at the end like a sentence that couldn't finish itself.

She hummed it again to be sure. It came out whole, note-perfect, effortless — the way *Happy Birthday* comes out, the way only songs learned in childhood come out.

She set down the tea. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; that was the thing people said about her work, that Oscar Amberdale never flinched, never editorialized, just recorded what was there. But her hands were steady now in the way a rope is steady when something on the other end is pulling.

*Radio interference,* she told herself, though the thought made no sense and she knew it made no sense. Songs don't transmit. Tunes don't lodge themselves in a sleeping brain through static. But she had been telling herself versions of that sentence for days now — interference, suggestion, coincidence, fatigue — each explanation thinner than the last, each one a coat of paint over a crack that kept widening.

She got out her notebook instead. Writing things down was her form of prayer: if it existed on paper, it existed outside her head, and whatever was happening could be studied rather than merely endured.

*Oct 9. Woke humming unknown melody (4+4 bars). No source identified. Asked Mrs. Tallow about hymn fragments — she refused again. Note: check parish records re: music in village schools.*

She underlined nothing. Underlining was for conclusions, and she didn't have one yet. What she had was a feeling she hadn't let herself name since the second night, when the thin young voice had answered questions she'd never asked aloud: the feeling of being *watched from inside*, of her own skull containing a room she hadn't furnished.

* * *

That night she dreamed of stairs.

Not the Grey Lamb's stairs — she knew those, thirteen steps with the seventh loose, a fact she'd catalogued her first morning here the way she catalogued everything. These were different. Narrower. Steeper, worn hollow in the centers of each tread by decades of the same feet. The banister was oak gone dark with handling, and there was a nick in it at shoulder height, a chip shaped like a little boat, and she knew — with the calm, total certainty of dreams — that Elodie had made that chip with a penknife when she was nine years old, whittling while she listened to her mother talk downstairs, and that she'd hidden the knife afterward in the gap behind the third stair because her father had forbidden knives after the incident with the hens.

Oscar woke with the layout of the house printed behind her eyes. Not vaguely, not as dream-logic smears, but precisely: kitchen at the back with the range against the north wall, parlor to the left with the good chairs nobody sat in, the narrow bedroom above with two beds and a window that stuck unless you lifted the latch first and pushed the sash while pulling the frame — a trick, she understood, that only worked if you knew it, a trick Elodie had discovered and never told anyone.

She lay in the dark of the Grey Lamb doing the arithmetic she did not want to do.

There were no houses in Hollowmere she hadn't entered. She'd interviewed every remaining resident in their own kitchens, recorder between them like a small black guest, and she had a cartographer's memory for rooms. None of them matched. The house in her dream — the house whose staircase she could walk in the dark, whose window-sticking trick she knew — did not exist anymore, or existed somewhere she hadn't found, or existed in 1974, which amounted to the same thing.

She got up. She wrote it all down: the stairs, the nick, the knife behind the third step, the window. Her handwriting went strange halfway through, smaller, rounder, letters leaning left the way a left-handed child writes when no one has corrected her. She stared at the page for a long time before she recognized what she was looking at.

It wasn't her handwriting. It was handwriting she'd seen once, in the parish register, signing a school prize list from 1971: *Elodie Amberdale, first form, singing.*

She closed the notebook. She did not sleep again.

* * *

In the morning there was mud on her feet.

She'd gone to bed clean — she remembered washing, remembered the particular cold of the Grey Lamb's water, remembered toweling her feet dry before getting under the blankets, because cold feet were the one luxury she allowed herself to complain about. Now her soles were grey-brown, damp, gritted with fine silt, and there was a smear of it on the sheet near the foot of the bed, a print half-formed, toes pointing toward the door.

Toward the door. As if she'd gotten up, walked out, and come back.

She checked the lock. Locked from inside. She checked the window. Latched. The inn was quiet around her, Dan Hobb's radio murmuring downstairs, the pipes ticking their morning ticks, and everything was exactly as it should be except for the mud, and the mud was enough.

She knelt and touched it. Fine, dark, silty — pond mud, she thought, though she couldn't have said how she knew, except that she did know, the way she now knew the layout of a dead girl's house. It smelled of rot and green water and something underneath both, faintly sweet, like hymnals stored too long in a damp place.

She washed her feet in the basin and watched the water cloud, and she said aloud, to the empty room, in the reasonable voice she used for difficult informants:

"I'm listening. If you need me to hear something, say it plainly."

Nothing answered. But the silence had a quality she was learning to recognize — attentive, patient, the silence of someone waiting politely for you to finish talking so they can begin.

* * *

The dreams came every night after that, and they stopped being dreams.

They were memories. That was the only honest word for them, and Oscar had promised herself, years ago, when she chose this profession, that she would never lie to herself about evidence. The nights unfolded in first person, in a body smaller than hers, with hair in her face and a loose tooth worrying her tongue, and they were ordered, dated, continuous — Tuesday followed Monday, autumn followed summer, and the summer was 1974.

She learned the village as it had been. The bakery that was now a ruin had been warm at five in the morning; she knew the smell of it, yeast and coal smoke. The mill still turned. There were forty-one children in the school and she — Elodie — knew all their names, and through her, Oscar began to know them too: Thomas Fen who couldn't whistle, Sarah Prew who bit her nails to the quick, the Hobb boy who cried during thunderstorms and denied it furiously ever after. Old Mr. Fen, who had returned her stolen recorder with his careful note, appeared as a young man with ink-stained fingers teaching a roomful of children to sing, and his voice was kind, and he called Elodie "my best singer" in front of everyone, and she glowed.

Oscar woke from that one with tears already drying on her face, grieving for a man's youth she had never witnessed, in a village she had known for nine days.

The erosion was not violent. That was what frightened her most, when she let herself think about it at all. Nothing tore. Nothing screamed. Things simply *transferred*, quietly, the way water finds the low places — names, smells, the weight of a school satchel on one shoulder, the specific loneliness of being eleven and clever in a place too small to hold cleverness. She'd catch herself reaching for a pencil with her left hand. She'd stand at the window and think of the mill pond as *ours*, with a child's flat possessiveness, before catching the word and holding it up to the light, appalled.

And threaded through all of it, always, the hymn. Fragments of it surfaced in the dreams — sung in a classroom, hummed over dishes, mumbled into a pillow — and each fragment fit another, and the melody grew in her like something gestating. The phrase that turned downward at the end, the unfinished-sounding one, recurred most often. In the dreams, Elodie sang it slightly wrong, rushing it, and in the dreams someone — Mr. Fen, gently — corrected her: *No, love. Slower. It's not a warning, it's a goodnight.*

A goodnight. Oscar wrote that down and looked at it for a long time. Every instinct she had about the village's buried ritual — and by now she had stopped pretending to herself that there wasn't a ritual, that there wasn't a bargain, that the drowned girl and the fifty fat years and the rule about not singing twice weren't all one architecture — every instinct said binding, sealing, a song used as a lock. But Fen's correction suggested something else underneath. A lullaby wearing a cage's clothing. A comfort that had been bent into a mechanism by frightened people who needed it to do a job it was never written for.

She was starting to feel protective of the idea. And then, faster than she wanted to admit, of the girl.

* * *

On the ninth night, Elodie spoke to her directly in the dream, and it was not like the recordings.

The recorder-voice was thin, flattened, coming from far away through water or wire. This was close. This was a girl sitting on the end of a bed in a narrow upstairs room — the narrow room, the sticking window — swinging her feet, which did not reach the floor, and looking at Oscar with an expression of frank, unchildlike assessment.

"You're dreaming me wrong," Elodie said. "I didn't hate the pond. I liked it, before. I used to swim there in June when it was warm at the top. You keep making it cold in your version."

Oscar tried to speak and found the dream permitted it, barely. "You know what I'm doing."

"Course I do." A shrug, sharp-edged. "You're the one who doesn't. You keep writing in your book like I'm weather. Like I'm a thing that happened to the village." She picked at the quilt — the quilt was blue and white, hexagon patterns, one hexagon patched with flour-sack cotton, and Oscar knew without being told that her grandmother had made it and that the patch was where the coal spark had burned it in '68. "I'm not weather."

"No," Oscar said. "You're not."

Elodie studied her. Up close her eyes were brown, ordinary, chapped-lidded, a child's eyes that had been made to hold too much. "You say my name sometimes," she said. "When you're writing. You say it quiet. Nobody's said it since Gran died." The feet stopped swinging. "Why do you say it?"

Because we share a surname, Oscar almost said. Because you drowned and my sister died and I was eleven too and I have spent twenty years learning how to carry the dead properly, and you are the first one who has ever carried anything back. Because someone should.

What she said was: "Because forgetting you was the worst thing they did. Worse than the rest of it. And I don't intend to do it."

Something moved across the girl's face — not a smile, nothing so settled, but the shadow of where a smile might live someday, in a life that had been denied the practice. "They'll stop you," Elodie said. "They stopped everyone. They'll take your tires and your tapes and then they'll take worse. They're scared, and scared people in Hollowmere do what they did to me." She slid off the bed, small and solid and heartbreakingly real, and stood close enough that Oscar could smell pond water and soap and childhood. "But you should know something before they start."

"Tell me."

"The book isn't lost." Elodie's voice dropped, went urgent, went young. "They think burying it finished it. It didn't. A song given back keeps the giver silent — that's what my gran said, that's why they took it out of my hands, so I couldn't sing it right, so it would stay *theirs*, wrong, forever. But it's still mine. It's still under the floor where they put it, and it's been waiting fifty years for somebody with my name and my stubbornness to come and pick it up." She reached out and took Oscar's hand, and her grip was cold and certain. "Will you?"

The dream thinned at the edges even as she asked, the room going soft, the blue quilt dissolving into dark, and Oscar heard herself answer — heard the word leave her mouth in the dream and, she was nearly sure, leave her mouth in the rented room at the Grey Lamb, aloud, into the dark, into the running recorder she had not switched off:

"Yes."

* * *

She woke at dawn with mud on her feet again, and this time there was more of it — a full print on the floorboards, heel and arch and five clear toes, pointing not at the door but at the window, at the east, at the chapel whose tower she could just see above the rooftops, grey against a grey sky.

She sat for a long time with the notebook open on her knees. Then she wrote, in her own handwriting, carefully, the way you sign something:

*Oct 12. I know where the hymnbook is. I agreed to go and get it. I don't yet know what I've agreed to give in return, and I'm going anyway.*

Below that, after a moment, she added one more line, and reading it back she understood that the erosion had passed some quiet threshold, that the border between her life and Elodie's had developed a gate, and that she had opened it herself:

*She was my student. She loved that hymn. Somebody has to sing it right.*

Downstairs, a car engine she didn't recognize turned into the yard — sleek, city-clean, wrong for Hollowmere the way a scalpel is wrong for a kitchen drawer. Through the window she watched a man in a good coat step out and look up at the inn, checking something on a clipboard, and she felt the clock she hadn't known was ticking begin, unmistakably, to tick.
