## 14. Pryce's Precedents

Dan Hobb had not broken into anything since he was fourteen years old, when he and Tommy Sedge had pried open the old grain shed on a dare and found nothing inside but wasps and a rusted plough. Fifty years later his hands remembered the shape of the crime anyway — the flat of the thumb testing a door handle, the small animal patience of standing in shadow, waiting for a light to go out.

Pryce's car sat at the edge of the Grey Lamb's lot, silver and immaculate under the sodium lamp, its paint so clean it seemed to have been washed that morning by someone whose job it was to make sure no speck of Hollowmere ever touched it. The man himself had gone up to his room at half past nine with his briefcase and his bottle of expensive water and his shoes that made no sound on the inn's stairs. Dan had watched him climb them from behind the bar, drying the same glass for ten minutes, and had thought: *he sleeps like a man who has done this before.*

That was the thing about Pryce. It wasn't the suit or the paperwork or the way he said *aspirational mixed-use* without flinching. It was that he never once looked at Hollowmere the way an outsider looks at a strange place — with that flicker of unease everyone else carried, even the surveyors, even the priest who'd come out from Ashford in the nineties. Pryce looked at the village the way a butcher looks at a carcass he has cut up many times. Familiarity without wonder. Dan had grown up around men who knew exactly what Hollowmere cost, and they all had a certain look, a certain careful way of not saying things. Pryce had it too.

So at eleven o'clock, after last orders, after Mrs. Tallow's nephew had helped her up to her room and the fire had been raked down to its orange eye, Dan took the spare key ring off its hook, walked out into the cold, and let himself into a stranger's car.

He told himself it wasn't breaking in. He owned the ground the car stood on. He was only looking. But his heart went like a boy's anyway, quick and guilty, and he sat down in the driver's seat — which smelled of leather and something faintly chemical, some air freshener engineered to smell like nothing at all — and pulled the glove box latch.

Maps. A torch. A laminated card with emergency numbers. And beneath those, a grey document wallet, soft with handling, its corners rounded the way paper gets when one person opens and closes it over and over across many months.

Dan hesitated. Not out of conscience — conscience had left this village a long time ago, or rather it had been paid for, in 1974, in full — but out of a superstitious dread so old it predated language. His father had taught him that there were doors you did not open, drawers you did not search, names you did not say twice. His father had taught him that while holding a girl's hymnbook underwater until she stopped moving. Dan had spent his whole life obeying that teaching, and it had bought him nothing but a bar full of empty stools and a face in the mirror that looked more like his father's every year.

He opened the wallet.

* * *

The first file was labelled with a place name Dan didn't know: **WRENLOW, OXFORDSHIRE**. Inside were photographs, a site plan, a purchase summary printed on Meridian letterhead, and then — Dan turned the page slowly, the way you turn a stone to see what's underneath — something that did not belong in any corporate folder.

A handwritten sheet. Old-fashioned ink, careful schoolroom hand. A list of names, maybe forty of them, each with a date of birth and a date of death, except that most of the death dates were blank. At the top, underlined twice:

*What Wrenlow was called before it was called Wrenlow: nothing. There is no earlier name. I have looked. That is how thoroughly it is gone.*

Below that, in different ink, added later, shakier:

*I am the only one left who knows the bell was cracked in 1911 and recast. I am the only one who remembers that the Hendersons' dog was called Captain and that he hated thunder. When I die, Captain never existed.*

Dan read it three times. The words didn't get easier. They got heavier, like wet sand filling his pockets.

He flipped back to the Meridian documents. The purchase summary was dry as flour — acreage, access rights, compensation schedules — but there was a section near the end, marked *Heritage & Continuity Protocols*, and Dan read it with his mouth gone dry and his ears roaring.

*In cases where local memory presents a contamination risk to adjacent developments (see Appendix C: Anomalous Retention Events), the following procedures apply. Full acquisition must precede demolition by no fewer than ninety days. All physical records are to be consolidated and destroyed under supervision. Residual custodial persons are to be identified, catalogued, and left undisturbed. Relocation of custodial persons is NOT recommended. See precedent files.*

*Custodial persons.* Dan said it aloud in the dark car, just to hear how it sounded, and it sounded obscene.

The second file was **MARLBRIDGE, SOMERSET**. Thinner. Photographs of a village green with a maypole, a row of cottages, a church. A note in the same corporate hand: *Acquisition complete 1987. Demolition 1988. Memory decay confirmed within eleven months. One custodial person identified; declined relocation; status: isolated, deteriorating. No further action required.*

And then a photograph Dan would carry to his grave, because it was the kind of photograph that doesn't leave: a woman standing in a doorway of a house that was clearly already half-demolished — rubble to her knees, roof beams open to the sky behind her — and she was smiling. Not madly. Just politely, the way you smile at a census taker. As if the camera were a visitor she was glad to see, because visitors were rare, and she had been alone for a very long time among things only she could remember.

Under the photograph, someone at Meridian had typed a single line, the bureaucratic equivalent of whistling past a graveyard:

*Custodian remains cooperative. Recommend no contact.*

* * *

Dan sat in the cold car with the engine ticking as it cooled and understood, with the slow terrible clarity of a man watching floodwater come up his lane, what he was looking at.

Three villages. He found the third file at the bottom of the wallet — **ASHCOMBE, KENT**, 1962, the oldest — and it followed the pattern, photographs and protocols and a custodian listed only by initials, *E.M., b. 1909*, with a final annotation in faded ink: *Deceased 1998. Memory terminated. Site clear.*

Memory terminated. Like a line on a vet's chart.

His hands were shaking now, and not from the cold. Because here was the arithmetic his father's generation had never written down, the part of the bargain that had been kept from the children and the grandchildren, the fine print that fifty years of silence had hidden: Hollowmere was not the first. Hollowmere was a *precedent*. Whatever had been done here in 1974 — whatever his father and Tom Sedge senior and the Reverend Loxley and six other men had done at the mill pond with their coats off and their hats held to their chests — it had been done before, elsewhere, and Meridian knew how it worked. Knew it the way a surgeon knows anatomy. Had *procedures* for it.

And the procedures included leaving someone behind.

He thought of Oscar Amberdale upstairs in room three, with her tape machines and her notebooks and her stubborn, hungry kindness. He thought of the way Elodie's name had begun to surface in the parish records like a body rising in a pond. He thought of the phrase from the Wrenlow file — *residual custodial persons are to be identified, catalogued, and left undisturbed* — and he thought: *that's her. That's what they have planned for her. She thinks she's a folklorist. She's already on a list.*

There was one more thing in the wallet. A slim folder, newer than the others, its tab marked in fresh ink: **CURRENT ASSETS — ACTIVE**. Dan opened it and found Hollowmere's own file, and his own name in it, and Mrs. Tallow's, and Fen's, each with a line beside it — *cooperative, cooperative, uncooperative* — and then a page headed *Custodial Projection*, and beside Oscar Amberdale's photograph — they had photographed her, when? At the bus stop? Through the inn window? — a typed assessment:

*Subject displays elevated retention markers consistent with prior custodial profiles. Familial resonance noted (see 1974 incident record). Projected suitability: high. Recommend natural integration. Do not inform.*

Do not inform.

Dan closed the folder. He sat there a long moment in the smell of nothing-at-all, listening to his own breath fog the windscreen, and felt something shift in his chest that had not moved in fifty years. It was not courage exactly. It was closer to the feeling he'd had at his father's funeral, standing at the graveside, realizing that the man could never again tell him what to be silent about.

* * *

He almost put everything back exactly as he'd found it. Almost. But the Wrenlow file had one loose page he hadn't examined, tucked behind the handwritten list, and when he lifted it out his breath stopped.

It was a follow-up report, dated four years ago. *Custodial person: MARGARET VANE, b. 1931. Status: living. Location: [redacted]. Notes: Subject has not been observed in company of another person since 1994. Neighbours in nearest settlement describe her as "the woman who talks to herself." Subject retains total recall of Wrenlow: 212 residents, 96 buildings, 1,400+ years of recorded and unrecorded history. Subject reports the memory "does not fade." Subject reports this as a complaint.*

At the bottom, in pen, someone had added a line that had no business being in a corporate file, and Dan wondered if Pryce himself had written it, late at night, in some motorway hotel:

*Cost per annum of maintaining subject in situ: negligible. Cost of what she carries: incalculable. Note for successors — do not visit. She will ask you to remember something. You will not be able to refuse her, and you will carry it home with you.*

Dan put the papers back in order, closed the wallet, latched the glove box, and got out of the car into the freezing dark. Above the village the stars were out in their indifferent thousands, and beyond the chapel the mill pond lay black and still, and somewhere in the space between them a girl who had been dead for fifty years was waiting for a book.

He stood in the car park with his hands in his pockets and made himself say it plainly, the way his father never had, the way the whole village never had, because plain speech was the one thing the bargain had forbidden and Dan Hobb was done paying that particular tithe.

"They're going to leave her," he said to the night. "They're going to give her Elodie's place and call it integration."

The pond said nothing. The chapel said nothing. But as he turned toward the inn's yellow windows, he saw a light moving behind the frosted glass of the residents' lounge — a thin, bobbing glow, like a candle carried carefully through a dark house — and heard, faint and clear through the gap under the door, a young voice humming four bars of a hymn.

The same four bars Mrs. Tallow could never finish.

Except that this time, the voice didn't stop. It went on, past the place where fifty years of fear had always cut it off, into the fifth bar, the sixth, the seventh — singing the whole verse through, sweet and steady, as if it had finally found someone it trusted enough to finish for.

Dan stood on the step with his hand on the latch, and understood that whatever Oscar Amberdale had learned tonight, she hadn't learned it from Pryce's files.

She'd learned it from the girl herself. And tomorrow, God help them both, she would want to go to Wrenlow.
