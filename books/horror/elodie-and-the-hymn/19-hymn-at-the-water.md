## 19. Hymn at the Water

The pond did not look like a place where anything had ever drowned.

That was the first cruelty of it, Oscar thought, standing at its edge in the grey hour before dawn with the hymnbook pressed against her chest. The water lay flat and silvered, holding the last of the night in its skin, and around it the reeds stood patient as a congregation. A heron watched her from the far bank with one yellow eye. Somewhere behind her, up the lane, Hollowmere's chimneys were beginning to breathe their thin smoke into the cold, and she could smell woodsmoke and wet earth and — beneath both, faint as a memory of a memory — something sweet and stagnant that had been there all along, under everything, since 1974.

Her feet were bare. She didn't remember taking her shoes off. The mud between her toes was cold enough to ache, and she was grateful for it, because pain was proof of a body, and she wanted very much to be certain of her body for what remained of this morning.

The book against her ribs was warm. Warmer than it should have been. It beat — not a pulse exactly, but a slow tidal pressure, like something breathing inside a wrapping of leather and swollen paper. Fifty years underwater and then fifty years beneath the chapel floor, and still it lived. Still it remembered every hand that had torn it from a girl's grip while she went down.

"Elodie," Oscar said aloud. Her voice came out small over the water and did not echo. The pond drank sound the way it drank light. "I have it."

The reeds moved. There was no wind.

She had felt the girl following her from the chapel — not as a presence beside her but as a warmth along her left side, the way you feel a companion walking close without touching, the displacement of air where another person should be. All the way down the lane, past Fen's dark cottage, past the leaning sign of the Grey Lamb where Dan Hobb stood in his doorway with his fists clenched at his sides, watching her go and not stopping her because there was nothing left to stop her with except words, and words had been spent on this village for fifty years and had never once been enough.

Dan had said only one thing as she passed him. He'd said it quietly, the way you tell someone the truth when you're ashamed of it:

"She'll ask you. And you don't have to say yes. Whatever else happens, Oscar — you don't *have* to."

She hadn't answered him. She'd been afraid that if she opened her mouth, what came out would be a lie.

Now she stood at the water's edge and the sky was going the colour of old pewter, and Elodie stepped out of the reeds.

Not out of the water. Out of the reeds, on the bank, on the land, wearing the same dress Oscar had seen in the parish photograph — a smocked cotton thing gone grey with pond-water and decades, hem dark, sleeves too short for arms that had stopped growing in October of 1974 and would never grow again. Her hair hung in two flat ropes past her shoulders. Her face was the face from the records and also not: younger in some ways, older in others, the way a voice sounds different on tape than it did in the room. She looked at Oscar with an expression of such ordinary, exhausted patience that Oscar's throat closed.

"You're cold," Elodie said. Her voice was the voice from the recordings, thin and young, but here, unmediated, it had a texture to it — a grain, like wool. "You've been cold for three nights. You keep waking up and putting your socks back on and then taking them off again because your feet hurt."

"I didn't know you noticed that."

"I notice everything now." Elodie glanced at the pond, and something moved through her face — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The way you look at a house you grew up in. "It's part of it. Being the one who remembers. You hold everything, even the things people would rather you didn't. Even the small shames." She looked back at Oscar. "Even yours."

Oscar's stomach turned. Three nights of dreams, mud on her feet, melodies in her mouth that belonged to a dead girl — and underneath all of it, the thing she had never said aloud to anyone, the thing about her sister, the hospital corridor, the way she had been relieved, just for one flickering second, when the phone rang and it meant she wouldn't have to sit vigil one more night. Elodie knew. Elodie had been carrying it for her, gently, the way you carry something breakable for someone whose hands are shaking.

"I'm sorry," Oscar said.

"Don't be." Elodie tilted her head toward the book. "Is that it?"

Oscar lifted it. In the growing light she could see the water damage properly for the first time — pages fused into slabs, cover bloomed with black mould shaped almost like ferns, the gold lettering worn to ghosts. *The Hollow Hymnal.* Someone had written that on the cover once, in a child's careful hand, and someone else had tried to scratch it out.

"It's heavy," Oscar said. "It wasn't this heavy in the chapel."

"It gets heavier the closer it comes home." Elodie held out her hands, then stopped herself, drew them back. "No. Not yet. Sing it first."

"The whole thing?"

"The whole thing. The right way." Elodie's chin came up, and for a moment she was every inch the girl Mrs. Tallow had described — the one who sang four bars and made the kitchen feel warmer, the one Mr. Fen had taught, the one they'd had to drown because she was starting to understand what the hymn was actually for. "They sang it wrong, that night. They sang it like a lock. Four frightened people on the mill bridge, singing a lullaby like a chain around my wrists, and I went down hearing it wrong, and I've been hearing it wrong for fifty years. Every version in this village is wrong. Every song they sing once and refuse to sing twice — wrong, all of it, warped by what they did." Her voice cracked, and the pond rippled in rings from the centre outward, though nothing had touched it. "Sing it the way it was written. Please. I want to hear it true before—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to.

Oscar looked down at the book. The fused pages would not open — she knew that without trying; she had tried, in the chapel, in the dark, with her fingers bleeding from the nails of the boards. But she didn't need the pages. She had spent six nights with headphones on, cross-referencing fragments from fifty years of tapes: Mrs. Tallow's four bars before the shaking started; the half-hummed phrase from the man who sold eggs; the snatch of melody Dan's father had sung in his sleep in 1976, recorded by accident on a reel-to-reel that had sat in the parish chest until Oscar found it; and underneath them all, threaded through every recording she'd made in this village, the second voice, patient, humming along, correcting, waiting.

She knew the hymn. She had known it for days without admitting it. It lived in her chest now the way the book lived against her ribs.

"Will you sing with me?" Oscar asked.

Elodie smiled, and it was such a young smile, such an unbearably ordinary eleven-year-old smile, that Oscar nearly lost the melody before she'd begun.

"If you start it," Elodie said. "I can't start it. That's the rule. I could never start it. That was always the problem."

Oscar breathed in. The cold air tasted of pond and smoke and iron. And she began.

It was not a hymn, she understood now, in the singing of it. The churchmen had called it a hymn because it fit in a hymnbook, because it had verses and a refrain, because Mr. Fen had written it down in notation neat as stitching. But sung true — sung slow, sung soft, sung the way you sing to a child who is crying and cannot say why — it was a letting-go. *Lay it down, lay it down, the day is done and done.* The first verse was for setting down burdens. The second was for forgiving debts. The third — the third was the one that had terrified them in 1974, the one that had made four adults on a mill bridge sing faster and harder until the comfort curdled into command — the third was for dying well. For being allowed to stop. For the permission to be finished.

*Lay it down, lay it down, you may rest, you are free,*

*and what is remembered is loved,*

*and what is loved is never lost.*

Oscar's voice wavered on the third verse and Elodie's came in under it, lifting it, and together they were two voices on a grey morning at the edge of black water, and the village behind them went quiet — not empty-quiet, not dead-quiet, but listening-quiet, the quiet of a congregation, of a family gathered in a kitchen, of everyone who had ever refused to sing a song twice standing at their windows with their hands over their mouths.

Mrs. Tallow was at her window. Oscar knew it without turning. She could feel the old woman's attention like a hand on her shoulder, trembling, mouthing the words she had sworn never to sing again.

The hymn ended. The last note went out over the water and did not echo, and the silence that followed was full rather than empty, the silence after an amen.

Elodie was crying. Standing on the bank in her grey dress with her ropey hair and her eleven-year-old face, crying the way children cry — entirely, without shame, her whole body in it.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's how it goes. Oh — oh, that's how it *goes*—" She pressed her palms to her eyes. "Fifty years. Fifty years I've been humming the wrong notes in the dark, thinking the wrongness was me."

"It was never you."

"No." She lowered her hands. Her eyes were clear now, and older than her face, and kind. "It was them. It was always them. And that's why—" She stopped. Drew breath she did not need. "You know what comes next."

Oscar's arms tightened around the book. The warm weight of it pulsed against her sternum like a second heart, and she thought: *I could throw it in the pond right now and run. I could be on a train by noon. Pryce offered me money. The world outside Hollowmere doesn't know my name and doesn't want anything from me except the next novel.*

"Yes," she said.

"You don't have to." Elodie's voice was fierce suddenly, and it cracked the air like ice on a puddle. The reeds bowed. The heron lifted off the far bank in a clatter of grey wings. "Do you understand what you'd be agreeing to? Do you actually understand? Because I can show you. I can put it in your head the way they put themselves in mine, and you'll see Wrenlow, you'll see the custodian there, ninety years old and alone in a house that isn't there anymore, remembering every doorknob of every door of every house that no longer exists, forever, because someone has to, because that's the shape of the bargain — a song given back keeps the giver silent, but a song given back needs a keeper, needs someone to hold the memory or it dies screaming instead of sleeping—"

"Elodie."

"—and you're *young*, you have a life, you have books unwritten, you have a sister you haven't finished grieving, you have—"

"*Elodie.*"

The girl stopped. The pond stilled.

"I read the file," Oscar said quietly. "Pryce's file. On Wrenlow. I know what a custodian is. I know what it costs. I've known since Tuesday, and I drove to Wrenlow on Wednesday, and I sat across a kitchen table from a woman who hasn't been seen by another living soul in thirty years, and she held my hands and told me not to take your place unless I meant it." She swallowed. "I meant it then. I mean it now. And I will spend the rest of however long I last meaning it, so you can stop trying to save me. It's my turn. You were eleven. Nobody gave you a choice. Somebody's giving me one, and I'm choosing this, and the reason I'm choosing this is—" Her voice broke, and she let it. "Because you waited fifty years in the dark humming the wrong notes, and the first thing you did when you finally got a voice was worry about whether my feet were cold. If that's what remembering makes you, then let me be that. Let me be that forever. I can't think of anything better to be."

Elodie stared at her.

Then she laughed — a wet, broken, astonished laugh, the laugh of a child who has just been given the one thing on her list she never wrote down because she didn't believe it existed.

"You're sure," she said. It wasn't a question, but she needed it to be one anyway.

"I'm sure."

"Say it, then. Say it so the water hears it. The water keeps the terms."

Oscar knelt at the edge of the mill pond. The mud took her knees gently, cold and soft as a bed. She held the hymnbook out over the water with both hands, and it beat against her palms like a heart trying to get home, and she said, clearly, in a voice that carried across the flat silver surface and into the reeds and up the lane to the listening windows of Hollowmere:

"I, Oscar Amberdale, give back this song. I will keep what must be kept. I will remember what must be remembered. Every name. Every note. Every year. As long as I am, it will be. This I choose. This I give."

The pond took the book.

There was no splash. The water simply rose to meet it — rose a foot above the surface, smooth as poured glass, cradling the hymnal the way two hands cup water from a stream — and then lowered it down, down, down, until the silver dimmed to green dimmed to black, and the book was gone, and the beating Oscar had felt against her ribs was gone with it, and for one vertiginous moment she felt hollowed, emptied, a bell with no clapper.

Then the pond began to shine.

Light came up through it from below — not reflected dawn-light but something older, warmer, the colour of lamplight through curtains, of a kitchen at evening, of every safe room anyone had ever loved — and Elodie stepped forward onto the water and did not sink. She walked to the middle of the pond as easily as walking a hallway, and the light rose around her ankles, her knees, her waist, and she turned back to look at Oscar, and she was smiling, and she was coming apart.

Not horribly. Not like drowning. Like light on water — that was the only way Oscar would ever be able to describe it afterward, and she would try many times, in notebooks, on tapes, in the small hours of fifty years of keeping: Elodie came apart the way a reflection comes apart when you touch the surface, breaking into pieces of brightness that were somehow more herself, not less, each fragment carrying a piece of her — the smile, the patience, the four bars of hymn, the worry about cold feet — scattering wide across the pond and up into the pewter sky and out into the reeds and the lane and the village, into the chimneys' smoke, into the mouths of everyone watching from their windows, so that for one moment every person in Hollowmere tasted spring water and heard a child laugh and remembered, all at once and completely, a girl named Elodie Amberdale who had loved a hymn and been failed by everyone who should have protected her.

Before the last of her went, she crossed the water back to the bank — one step, two, the light streaming off her like silk unravelling — and she touched Oscar's face.

Her hand was warm. That was the unbearable thing. Fifty years drowned and the hand was warm, and it smelled faintly of beeswax and pencil shavings, of a classroom, of Mr. Fen's music lessons, of being taught and being loved.

"Thank you," Elodie said.

And then she was everywhere and nowhere, light on water, and the pond settled, and the dawn came up honest and grey-gold over Hollowmere, and the birds — which had stopped mid-flight, hanging in the air like notes on a staff — finished their arcs and landed and began, all together, to sing.

Oscar knelt in the mud for a long time. Long enough for the sun to clear the hedgerow. Long enough for her tears to dry salt on her face. When she finally stood, her legs shook, and the emptiness where the book had beaten against her heart was already filling with something else — heavier, vaster, quieter. Names. Doorknobs. Dates. The exact pitch of Mrs. Tallow's hum. The number of steps from the Grey Lamb's door to the chapel. Everything. All of it, settling into her like sediment into still water.

Up the lane, a car engine started.

She knew that engine. She had heard it idle outside the inn for eleven days, patient as a spider. And she knew — the new knowledge settling into her bones told her without being told — that the car was pulling away from the chapel, where Dan Hobb and half the village stood in front of the machines, and that Pryce was driving not toward the demolition site but toward the Grey Lamb, and that he was carrying a folder with her name on it, and that whatever clause had voided the contracts overnight, Pryce was not a man who accepted clauses.

He was coming to see the new custodian.

Oscar wiped her face, picked up her recorder from the bank — still running, forty minutes of hymn and light and a girl saying *thank you* — and walked up the lane to meet him.

* * *

The pond stayed bright all day.

Villagers came to its edge in ones and twos, hatless, silent, some of them weeping, none of them speaking, and by afternoon word had travelled the length of Hollowmere that the girl was gone — truly gone, unbound, released — and that the outsider woman had gone into the water's debt in her place. Old Mr. Fen arrived last, moving slowly with his stick, and stood a long time looking at the place where the light had been.

Then he took off his cap, and — his voice cracked and thin and true — he sang the hymn.

Once.

And then, to the astonishment of everyone on the bank, including himself, he dried his eyes, cleared his throat, and sang it again.

Twice. Deliberately. Joyfully. The rule broken at last, freely, by the oldest man in the village, and the sky did not fall, and the crops did not fail, and the pond only shivered a little, the way water does when it is happy.

Oscar stood beside him and sang the second time with him, and thought: *this is what it is. This is what I chose. Not punishment. This.*

She was still singing when she saw the dust of Pryce's car coming down the lane, and felt, in the vast new archive of her memory, the exact weight of the folder on his passenger seat, and understood — with a custodian's perfect, terrible clarity — that the bargain was ended but the story was not, and that somewhere in the files Pryce carried was a page listing what happened to villages whose custodians were still alive when the developers came back.
