## 1. Fingerprints

The call came at eleven in the morning, which Ivo Voss would later think of as the last reasonable hour of his life.

"Voss Pest and Structural," he answered, because the sign on his van said so, and because he had learned long ago that a man who trusted numbers should keep his words countable too.

"This is Renner. Harrow estate, out past Kettlewick. We've got a situation with a door."

Ivo wrote it down. *Harrow estate. Door.* Two facts, cleanly separated. "What kind of situation? Rodents in the frame? Wasps?"

"No." A pause on the line, the kind of pause that costs money. "The door's underground. We're doing the excavation for the new foundation — the old cellar was bricked over sometime in the last century, nobody knew it was there. We broke through yesterday. And this morning there are fingerprints on it."

"Fingerprints," Ivo said. "On a door that's been sealed in the dark for a hundred years."

"On the *inside* face of it, Mr. Voss. Fresh ones. In the dust."

Ivo looked at the form on his clipboard, at the neat columns waiting to be filled: *Nature of infestation. Points of entry. Recommended treatment.* He had been doing this work for nineteen years and had never once written *fingerprints* in the first column. He found that his pen hovered over the box longer than it should have.

"I'll be there by two," he said.

* * *

The Harrow estate sat at the end of a lane that the county had stopped maintaining sometime before Ivo was born, a long gray scar of gravel and standing water. The house itself was a great slumped shoulder of a building, three stories of slate and lichen, its windows like closed eyes. But Ivo barely glanced at it. His attention went where it always went: to the structure, to the ground, to the pit.

They had opened the earth behind the house's east wing, a rectangular excavation maybe four meters deep, shored with timber and steel plate. A ramp led down into it. At the far wall of the pit, half-emerged from the soil like something surfacing from sleep, stood a door.

Ivo stood at the pit's edge for a moment before descending, and he made himself do what he always did, what his father had taught him without ever explaining why: he counted.

One door. One approach ramp. Seven workers visible — no, eight; one crouched by the generator shed at the pit's rim, mostly hidden. Four shoring walls. One door.

He wrote the numbers in his notebook because writing them made them true. Numbers were the load-bearing beams of the world. You could lean your whole weight against them and they would hold. His father had counted doors every night of his life — front door, back door, cellar hatch, garage — tapping each lintel twice with two fingers as he passed, and when young Ivo had asked him once, *why*, his father had said only, "So you know they're all still doors," and had never said anything more about it, ever, not even on his deathbed, when there had been time for so many other things.

Ivo climbed down into the pit.

The foreman, Renner, met him at the bottom, a broad man gone gray early, with plaster dust worked permanently into the creases of his knuckles. Up close Ivo could see that Renner hadn't slept. There was a specific quality to that kind of tiredness — not the sag of physical exhaustion but the hollowed look of a man who had spent the night lying still and listening.

"You're the pest man?" Renner said. "I want to be straight with you. I don't think this is pests."

"Then why call me?"

"Because you were the only number in the book that said *structural and investigative*. And because my men won't go back down here until somebody official looks at it." He wiped his hands on his thighs, though they were clean. "Come see."

The door was oak, black with age, set into the earth itself rather than any surviving frame — the wall around it was packed soil and rubble, the remains of whatever passage had once led to it collapsed or demolished. It was a small door. That was the first thing Ivo noticed, the thing that put the first hairline crack in his professional calm: it was small. Not child-sized exactly, but low, five foot three perhaps, narrow across the shoulders. A door built for someone slight. Someone who expected to pass through it often.

And it was sealed from this side. Three iron bolts, thrown. The heads furred with rust, but the bolts themselves bright where they'd been drawn shut hard. Around the door's perimeter, a bead of what looked like wax — candle wax, poured deliberately, in a continuous unbroken line, the way you'd seal a letter.

"Somebody locked this from the outside," Ivo said. "Bolted it, waxed it. Standard containment."

"That's what we thought too." Renner's voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of a man reciting something he'd rehearsed so he wouldn't have to feel it. "Except look at the bolts again."

Ivo looked. He crouched, took out his penlight, angled it low across the metal the way he did when tracking rodent runs through insulation, looking for the sheen of compressed fibers, the directionality of disturbance.

The rust on the bolt heads was disturbed. Scratched. Recently.

From the inside.

He straightened up slowly. "These were thrown from the other side."

"Now you understand my morning."

* * *

The dust on the door's face was fine and even, a century of silt settled through whatever cracks the collapse had left, laying down a skin of gray velvet. And printed into that velvet, on the inside face of a door buried alive for generations, were fingerprints.

Small ones.

Ivo knelt in the mud with his kit open beside him — brush, tape, cards, the whole modest forensics of a man who mostly documented rat gnawing — and he felt, distinctly, the sensation of the ground tilting a degree or two beneath him. He ignored it. He brushed. He lifted. He carded.

Eight distinct prints, arranged in two vertical rows of four, positioned precisely where fingers would fall if a hand had been pressed flat against the wood. Pressed and held. Held long enough, and pressed hard enough, that the whorls had registered clean through the dust into the grime beneath.

He held the lift card up to the light.

The prints were small. Smaller than any of the workers'. Smaller than his own. A woman's size, or an adolescent's, or — his mind supplied, unhelpfully, and he put the thought down the way you'd put down something hot — or a child's.

But it wasn't the size that stopped him. It was the pressure gradient. He'd done enough print work to read how force distributed across a hand. These prints were deepest at the fingertips and dragged slightly downward at the edges, the way prints look when a hand is pressed flat and then *pulled*, as if whoever had made them had been trying to pull the door toward them. From inside.

Or push themselves through it.

"Renner," he said, without turning around. "How many people have touched this door since you broke through?"

"Nobody. I told them all hands off. That's why I called you same-day — I wanted it documented before anybody muddied it."

"And before this morning? When it was still buried?"

"It was buried, Mr. Voss. Under four meters of clay and rubble. Nobody's touched that door since before my grandfather was born." Renner came and stood beside him, arms crossed, staring at the small pale ghost-hands on the black wood. "That's the part my guys can't get past. Those prints weren't there yesterday. I checked the door myself at end of shift. Clean. Just dust. And this morning—" He stopped. Started again. "This morning Marta went down to fetch a level she'd left, and she came up white as milk and wouldn't say why, and when I went down, there they were."

Ivo said nothing. He was counting again, involuntarily now, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Eight prints. Two hands. One pair. Pressed from within a sealed space, in dust that had lain undisturbed for a hundred years, sometime between yesterday evening and this morning.

There was no column on his form for this.

* * *

He spent two hours in the pit, and the crew watched him from the rim the whole time, eight faces in a row along the edge like crows on a fence. He measured the door — 158 centimeters tall, 71 wide, oak, iron-banded. He photographed the bolts, the wax seal, the prints, the drag-marks in the dust below them where fingertips had slipped. He tested the hinges — frozen solid, useless, the door hadn't swung in living memory, which meant nothing had come *through* it, which meant—

Which meant whatever had pressed its hands against the inside of that door was still on the other side of it.

He caught himself thinking the sentence and made himself finish it, because unfinished thoughts were worse than finished ones. His father had taught him that too, in his wordless way: count everything, leave nothing hanging, because a mind that leaves a number missing will spend the whole night filling the gap with something worse than the truth.

Still on the other side of it.

He tried the rational roads one by one, walking them to their ends. A worker had lied, had touched the door, had made a hoax of it. But the prints were small, and the smallest hand on site belonged to Marta, and he'd lifted her prints from her own tools for comparison, and they didn't match — different arch pattern entirely, and she was right-handed while the drag suggested a left-leading press. A child had gotten onto the site overnight. The site was fenced, alarmed, and the nearest dwelling was two kilometers down the lane; also, no child's prints anywhere else in the dust, none on the ramp, none on the shoring. The prints were old, pre-dating the burial. But the dust over them was undisturbed, and dust doesn't lie about sequence — the prints lay *in* the topmost layer, crisp-edged, days old at most.

Every road ended at the same place: a small hand, pressing outward, recently, from inside a room that had been sealed and entombed for a century.

Ivo Voss sat down on an overturned bucket in the corner of the pit and did something he almost never did during working hours. He drank his coffee cold, all of it, in one long swallow, and stared at the door.

It stared back, in its way. Not with eyes. With the absence of them. With the patient blankness of a surface that had been touched and could be touched again.

"Mr. Voss?" One of the workers had come down the ramp — a young one, freckled, nervous, holding his hard hat against his chest like a hat at a funeral. "You're the expert. So tell us straight. What d'you reckon made those?"

Ivo considered the inventory of honest answers. *A person. A dead person. Something wearing the shape of a hand.* He chose the answer that was truest and least useful.

"I don't know yet," he said. "That's not a dodge. It's the finding. Whatever else is true, the evidence says the door was sealed from both sides — bolted and waxed from out here, and then, later, much later, handled from in there. Somebody wanted it shut, and somebody else wants it open."

"Or the same somebody," the young worker said quietly, "at two different times."

Ivo looked at him. The kid shrugged, embarrassed.

"My gran used to say that about our old place," he added. "That the ones who lock things in and the ones who want to let them out are usually the same family. She said every seal has two signatures."

Ivo's father had never said anything like that. His father had never said anything at all, which was its own kind of signature.

* * *

By late afternoon the light had gone the color of weak tea, and the wind had picked up out of the west, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't arrived yet and might not arrive at all, just circle the valley all night like something looking for a way in. Ivo packed his kit. He climbed out of the pit. At the top he turned and looked back down, and made himself count one more time.

One door. Eight workers. Four shoring walls. One ramp.

Then he saw it — the thing that sent him home with his headlights on in the dusk and his hands at ten and two and his jaw aching from clenching.

Below the fingerprints, near the bottom edge of the door, in the dust, there was a new mark. Small. He'd have sworn on his license it hadn't been there at noon. A single print, low down, at the height a kneeling child might reach.

Not pressed flat this time.

Curled. The way fingers curl when they're waving.

He got in the van. He drove. Behind him the Harrow estate stood in the failing light with its windows like closed eyes, and somewhere under four meters of clay and a hundred years of silence, something small-palmed and patient waited for the counting man to come back.

His phone buzzed at a red light. Unknown number. He answered, because a man who trusts counts answers everything, just to know the total.

"Ivo." A woman's voice, old and dry and unmistakable — his aunt Greta, whom he hadn't spoken to in nine years. She didn't say hello. She said:

"What door have they got you looking at?"

He didn't ask how she knew. Some part of him, the part that had inherited his father's silence, already understood that she would know.

"There's a job," he said carefully. "Out past Kettlewick. Old cellar door, unearthed during excavation—"

"Don't take jobs with old doors." The words came fast, worn smooth, like a prayer said ten thousand times. "Ivo. Listen to me. Your father had a list. He kept it in the green Bible. Doors he was never allowed to touch, houses he was never allowed to enter, names he was never allowed to say after sundown. The Harrow name is on that list. It's been on it since before you were born."

The light turned green. Ivo didn't move. Behind him, a car honked, once, twice, and swung around.

"Greta—"

"The last of us is supposed to go when it calls," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle like old wax. "And it's overdue, Ivo. It's fifty years overdue."

The line went dead.

Ivo sat in the idling van in the middle of the empty intersection, and after a long moment, without quite deciding to, he reached over and counted the doors of his own vehicle. Two. Driver, passenger. Both shut. Both still doors.

Then he drove home, and left every light in the house burning, and did not sleep.
