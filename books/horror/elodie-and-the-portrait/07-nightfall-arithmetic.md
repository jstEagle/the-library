## 7. Nightfall Arithmetic

The sun went down at 4:47, though it never truly rose that day to begin with. Mara Kell stood at the edge of the excavation pit with her survey tablet against her hip and watched the storm eat the horizon in long grey swallows, and she thought: *that's wrong*. Not the storm itself — storms came early to the coast in November — but the way the light had failed, as if someone upstairs had pulled a curtain over the whole sky an hour ahead of schedule.

She had been mapping the site for eleven days. She knew the sun's angles here the way she knew the lines of her own palm. The Harrow estate sat on a shelf of land above the river flats, and at this latitude, this time of year, true sunset should have come at 5:12. She had checked twice. She had the almanac open on her tablet and the numbers did not care what her eyes insisted on. Twenty-five minutes of daylight, missing. Gone somewhere. Taken.

*Everything here is taken,* she thought, and then made herself stop thinking it, because thoughts like that were how sites got into your blood.

She turned back toward the work lights strung along the pit's rim. They burned yellow-white against the gathering dark, and beneath them the unearthed cellar door lay where it had lain for two weeks now — flat on its timber cradle like a patient on a table, its iron bolts thrown, its wax seals cracked but not broken, its inner face still bearing nine small fingerprints that no one could explain and no one would talk about after dark.

Mara had measured those prints herself, on the second day, before anyone told her not to. Nine fingers' worth of presses, small ones, a child's hand or nearly, pressed hard enough into the old paint to leave ridges like fossilized ferns. Pressed from *within*. She had run the geometry three times. There was no ambiguity in it. Whatever had left them had been standing inside a room that had been sealed shut since before the house above it was built, pushing outward, patiently, the way roots push.

She was a surveyor. She dealt in boundaries. And every boundary on this site had gone soft.

***

She found Ivo Voss in the estate's mudroom, counting.

He didn't call it counting. He called it "doing a walk," and he did it the way other men checked their phones — absently, compulsively, his flashlight beam touching each doorframe as he passed. Front door. Interior hall. Pantry. Cellar stair. His lips moved slightly at each one, and Mara had learned not to interrupt him mid-walk, because when she had, early on, he'd lost his place and started over from the beginning, and there had been something in his face then — a flicker of genuine fear, quick as a struck match — that had told her this was no quirk.

Tonight he was on the pantry door when she came in, and she stood in the doorway and watched him finish. Four doors. He exhaled through his nose, pocketed the flashlight, and only then turned.

"You're doing the walk more often," she said.

"The house is bigger than it looks." He said it lightly, but his eyes went past her shoulder to the hallway behind her, ticking off the doors there too, silently, the way a man counts money he's afraid of losing. "Storm's coming in fast."

"Storm's been coming in fast for six hours. It hasn't moved." She held up her tablet so he could see the radar loop. The mass of weather sat offshore like a sleeping animal, rotating slowly, neither advancing nor retreating. "It's just *there*, Ivo. Sitting on us."

He looked at the screen longer than the image deserved. "Maybe it's waiting for something," he said, and she couldn't tell if he was joking, and that was the worst part — that she couldn't tell anymore, about anything he said, whether the joke was a joke or a report.

"Denny wants everyone in the great room by six," she said. "Headcount, hot food, nobody alone. His words."

"Good words." Ivo rubbed the back of his neck. In the mudroom's single bulb his face looked older than it had eleven days ago, carved rather than lived-in, the lines around his eyes deeper. He was a careful man, a precise man, a man who trusted counts and seals and torque specifications, and she had loved that about him first — the way his certainty had felt like a handrail in a world of loose gravel. But the handrail was shaking now. She could see it shaking. "Mara—"

"Don't." She said it gently. "Whatever you're going to say — don't say it in the mudroom. Mudrooms are for boots."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, preserved under glass. "Where should I say it?"

"Nowhere yet. Later. When we've counted everybody and the number comes out right." She heard her own voice and hated how much it trembled at the edges. She was the steady one. That was her role here; she'd built it plank by plank, the calm professional woman with the laser level and the dry humor, and the planks were groaning. "Because I've been counting too, Ivo. All day. And my number doesn't come out right."

His head came up sharply. "How many?"

"Nine." She said it flat, factual, surveyor's diction. "Nine souls on site. Me, you, Denny, Okafor's crew of four, the cook, and Pell." She paused. "And every time I count, I get nine, and every time I count, it feels like I'm miscounting. Like the number has a hole in it. Like—" She stopped, because saying it aloud gave it weight, and weight was what gravity used.

"Like the number's incomplete," Ivo said quietly.

"Like the number's being *held open*."

They looked at each other across the mudroom, across eleven days of shared coffee and cautious warmth and the slow, deliberate construction of something neither of them had named yet, and Mara saw him arrive at the same place she'd arrived at hours ago, alone, staring at her tablet in the grey failing light: the house wasn't haunted the way stories said houses were haunted. Houses didn't rattle chains. Houses *waited*. This one had waited under the ground for longer than the ground had records, and now it had been opened — not broken open, *unearthed*, invited up into the light by men with machines who thought they were digging foundations — and it was setting its table.

"Six o'clock," Ivo said finally. "Great room. We'll count together."

"We'll count twice," Mara said. "I always count twice."

***

The great room of the Harrow estate had been built to impress people who no longer existed, and it still tried. Its ceiling rose two full storeys into shadow; its windows, tall as church glass, showed nothing now but the black pressure of the storm and their own reflections, ghostly and doubled, so that the room seemed to hold more people than it did. Someone had gotten a fire going in the enormous hearth, and the cook — a silent, competent woman named Ruth who had stopped humming on day four — had laid out stew and bread on trestle tables, and the smell of it was almost enough to make the room feel like shelter.

Almost.

Denny Okafor stood near the fire with a clipboard he wasn't reading. His crew sat in a rough crescent around the heat: Tomas and Reyes arguing softly about football, Brandt staring into the flames with the fixed attention of a man watching something inside the fire, and the fourth man —

Mara's eyes snagged on the empty chair.

"Pell?" Denny said, before she could ask. "Anyone seen Pell?"

"He went out to check the generator line," Tomas said. "Hour ago, maybe."

"In *this*?"

Tomas shrugged, and the shrug had a bad texture to it, defensive and thin. "Said he heard it running rough."

Nobody said what they were all thinking, which was that the generator had died two nights ago on a full tank and restarted on its own at dawn, and that Pell had been strange ever since — quiet in a new way, not sullen but *attentive*, as if listening to a radio the rest of them couldn't hear. Mara had caught him twice standing at the pit edge looking down at the covered door with an expression she could only describe as *recognition*, and both times, when she'd spoken to him, he'd answered a half-second late, like sound traveling a long distance.

"Nine," Denny said, marking his clipboard. "Eight here, plus Pell. Nine."

The number landed in the room and sat there.

Mara felt it again — the hole in it. Eight men and women around a fire built for a crew of ten, and the arithmetic of the space itself insisting on one more. She glanced at the table. Ruth had set it for dinner later, and Mara counted the places without meaning to, the way she counted everything now, the way the whole site had taught her to count: plates, cups, spoons.

Ten settings.

"Ruth," she said carefully. "How many are we?"

Ruth didn't look up from the pot. "Nine," she said. "You asked me this morning."

"How many places did you set?"

Ruth looked at the table then, and Mara watched the question reach her, watched her count, watched her face do the thing faces did on this site lately — the small collapse, the recalibration. "I set nine," Ruth said slowly. "I know I set nine. I count everything twice. It's how I—" She stopped. Her hands had gone still on the ladle. "There's ten."

The fire popped. Somewhere overhead, in the dark upper floors of the house, a door swung gently on its hinges — once, twice, settling — though no one had been up there, and no wind reached the interior, and every window was latched.

Ivo was beside her suddenly. Mara hadn't seen him cross the room, but he was there, close, his voice low. "Twice," he murmured. "Count it twice."

"I did. Ten both times." She kept her voice level for the room's sake, but under the table her hand found his sleeve and gripped it. "Ivo. The house set a tenth place. Or the house let Ruth think she set nine. Either way—"

"Either way it's expecting company."

"And it knows our number better than we do."

***

Later — after the stew, after Denny had posted the watch schedule and sent Brandt and Reyes up to bunk in the east wing over their own objections, after the fire had burned down to a red economy of coals — Mara found Ivo in the archive room with a lamp and a stack of old deeds, and she closed the door behind her and stood with her back against it for a moment, just breathing.

The archive smelled of paper and cold stone. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, stuffed with the sediment of centuries: lease agreements, harvest ledgers, letters browned to lace. Ivo sat at the scarred oak table with documents spread around him in his precise grids, and he looked up when she came in, and whatever he saw in her face made him push back his chair.

"Mara."

"I can't sleep up there." She said it simply. "The east wing. Every door up there has that feeling. You know the feeling. The one where the door is a mouth and the room behind it is a throat."

"I know the feeling."

"And I keep thinking about the tenth place at the table, and I keep thinking about Pell out there in the dark checking a generator line that doesn't need checking, and I keep thinking—" Her voice betrayed her, cracked down the middle, and she pressed on through the crack because stopping would have been worse. "I keep thinking that if something happens tonight, I will have spent eleven days not telling you the one true thing I know, because I was rationing it. Because it felt too big to spend all at once. Because I thought we'd have time."

Ivo stood. He crossed the room slowly, the way a man moves toward something he's afraid will startle, and stopped close enough that she could see the lamplight reflected twice in his eyes.

"Say it," he said. "We'll ration it together."

"I love you." The words came out quiet and complete, no flourish, no performance — a measurement, a bearing taken, a line drawn true between two points. "Not the cautious version. Not the site-flirtation version where we trade coffee and pretend we're being subtle. The real one. I love you, Ivo Voss, and I have since the day I watched you dust a dead man's doorframe for prints with hands steadier than mine have ever been, and I am telling you now, tonight, because tomorrow the number might be eight, and I refuse — I *refuse* — to let anything take this day from me unspent."

For a moment he didn't move. Then his hand came up and touched her jaw, careful, the way he handled evidence, the way he handled things that mattered.

"I started counting doors when I was six years old," he said. "My father taught me without teaching me. I never knew why. I thought everyone's father made them walk the house at night. I thought the counting was just... what love looked like, in our family. Vigilance." His thumb traced her cheekbone. "I have spent my whole life believing that if I counted faithfully, faithfully enough, nothing bad would happen. And then I came here, and I found fingerprints on the inside of a sealed door, and for the first time in forty years the counting doesn't hold. The math is wrong, Mara. The math has been wrong since before I was born."

"So we make new math."

"There's no such thing."

"There is." She turned her face into his palm. "It's two people who count each other instead. One and one. That's the whole equation. Everything else is the house's arithmetic, and I'm done doing its homework."

He laughed — a small, broken, genuine sound, the first one in days — and then he kissed her, and it was not cautious either. It was the kiss of two people standing in a lit room while the dark outside learned their names, and it tasted of stew and woodsmoke and the specific salt of fear transmuted into something braver.

When they broke apart, she kept her forehead against his.

"One and one," he whispered.

"One and one," she agreed. "Two. A number the house didn't ask for."

Outside the archive door, in the corridor, footsteps passed — slow, unhurried, bare-sounding on old boards. Neither of them had heard anyone approach. Neither of them heard them leave. The lamp flame leaned sideways and righted itself, and in the silence after, very faintly, from the direction of the pit, came a sound that might have been wind and might have been a voice, calling a name down into the earth.

Pell's name.

And then — softer, patient, almost tender — a name Mara didn't recognize, spoken in a tone that made her blood go cold and her hand find Ivo's and grip it:

A name that sounded, unmistakably, like *Voss*.
