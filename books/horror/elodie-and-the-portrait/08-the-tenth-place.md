## 8. The Tenth Place

Denny Okafor had fed crews on a hundred sites, and he knew the arithmetic of hunger the way he knew the weight of his own hands. Nine men on the Harrow job meant nine plates, nine forks, nine chairs dragged up from the estate's mismatched collection — and one man short, because Pell had walked off at four o'clock without his coat and hadn't come back.

So when Denny came down into the long dining room at half past six with a crate of supplies under one arm and found the table set for ten, he stopped in the doorway and let the cold walk up his spine one vertebra at a time.

The china was old, cream-colored, hairline-cracked with age, and it had come out of the sideboard cabinets that Ivo Voss had personally checked and re-checked that afternoon. Denny had watched him do it. Watched him run his finger along the cabinet seams, press the doors shut, count something under his breath. And now ten place settings gleamed in the lamplight, arranged with a precision no caterer would bother with: forks aligned to the table edge like survey stakes, knives turned inward, glasses filled with water so still it looked painted.

Ten settings. Nine men.

The tenth chair was pulled out slightly, angled toward the head of the table, as if someone had just risen from it — or was about to arrive.

"Voss," Denny called, and his voice came out flatter than he wanted. "Voss, get in here."

* * *

Ivo came at his usual unhurried pace, but Denny saw the man's eyes go to the table first, before they went to Denny, and saw them move across it the way a man counts money he suspects is short. One. Two. Three. His lips moved faintly. At ten they stopped moving altogether.

"That wasn't like this an hour ago," Denny said. "I ate lunch in this room. There were crumbs on this table. There were nine of everything or nothing at all."

"I know." Ivo set his hand flat on the tablecloth, and Denny noticed it trembled once before going still. "The cabinets were sealed. I checked them myself. Twice."

"You counted them."

"I counted them." A pause. "Maybe that's the problem."

Denny laughed, because the alternative was worse. "You're telling me the house is listening to your counting? That's — no. No, that's not how houses work. Houses don't set tables. Somebody set this table. Pell—"

"Pell is missing."

"Pell wandered off. Pell does that. Pell's got a wife who texts him forty times a day and he leaves his phone in the truck every time." But even as he said it, Denny was looking at the tenth setting, at the glass of water that had not rippled, not once, though the floorboards under their feet shivered with the wind working at the storm shutters outside. "Somebody set this table," he said again, quieter.

"Yes," Ivo said. "And somebody's expected at it."

* * *

They searched for Pell the way you search for anything on a site gone dark — badly, in circles, calling a name into wind that ate it. The generator had died at sundown despite a full tank; Ivo had checked the fuel line twice with a flashlight clenched in his teeth, muttering numbers like prayers. The crew had gathered in the kitchen with propane lamps and bad coffee, and Denny had organized the search parties with the brisk competence he used to keep fear at arm's length: Ruiz and Calloway take the east wing, Marek and the boy take the grounds, I'll take the pit.

Nobody wanted the pit. Everybody knew it. He took it anyway, because that was the job — not pest control, not surveying, not whatever strange contract had brought the Voss man here with his sealed doors and his inherited dread. The job, as far as Denny Okafor had ever understood it, was simple: you go where the work is worst, and you bring your people home.

The excavation pit lay behind the house like a wound in the lawn, floodlit once, now lit only by two work lamps strung on a generator that wasn't running. The lamps burned anyway. Denny noticed that and made himself stop noticing it. Below, the unearthed cellar door stood open at the bottom of the trench — they'd opened it that morning, against every instinct Denny owned, because the client's lawyers insisted and because a door is just a door until it isn't.

Pell stood at the pit's edge.

He stood very straight, coatless in the wind, his shirt snapping around him, and he was facing the open door below as if it were a stage and he'd been waiting for his cue. The work lamps threw his shadow long across the grass, and the shadow pointed away from the light, away from the door, back toward the house — which was wrong, Denny thought distantly, shadows point away from light, that's what shadows do, except this one seemed to be pointing with intent, like a finger.

"Pell." Denny kept his voice level, the voice he used on spooked apprentices on high steel. "Hey. Buddy. You're missing dinner."

Pell didn't turn. "It asked about you," he said.

The words dropped through Denny like a stone through still water. "What did?"

"It wants to know if you've eaten." Pell's head tilted, birdlike, considering. "It sets a fine table. It has had a long time to practice. Nobody ever stays for all the courses."

"Pell. Look at me. Tom." Using the first name felt like grabbing a rung in the dark. "Your wife's been calling. Your phone's in the truck. Let's go get your phone."

"She calls and calls." Pell smiled without turning around, and Denny saw the smile only in profile, a white crescent that sat wrong on his face, too wide by a degree, held too long. "That's how she loves me. With noise. It doesn't mind the noise. It says noise is just another way of counting."

Denny stepped closer. The grass was wet and cold through his boots. Ten feet. Eight. Close enough now to see that Pell's eyes were open and dry and fixed on the middle distance, on some point below the world, and that his lips kept moving after each sentence ended, shaping silent syllables, like a man translating.

"Tom." Six feet. "We're leaving. All of us. Tonight. Job's done, we're done, and Monday you can tell me what the hell got into you."

"He counts," Pell said softly. "The little man with the careful hands. He counts the doors every night, and the house has learned the song of it. Do you know what a gift that is? For sixty years nobody taught it anything. And now—" Pell's breath fogged the air, though the temperature had been falling all evening and Denny couldn't say when it had started feeling like a throat instead of a night. "Now it knows the route. Room to room. Light to light. It will walk it backward, someday soon, from the last lamp to the first. It is very patient about walking."

Denny grabbed his shoulder.

He'd wrestled drunks off ladders and pulled men out of trench collapses; he knew the feel of a human body, its give and its resistance, the animal warmth of it. Pell's shoulder was cold. Not dead-cold, not meat-in-a-cooler cold, but cold the way stone is cold in a cellar — a cold with depth to it, a cold that had been cold longer than Denny had been alive. And beneath his hand, through the fabric, he felt something shift. Not muscle. Not bone. Something adjusting itself inside Pell the way a tenant shifts in a chair that was never built for him.

"Pell," Denny said, and shook him, gently, then less gently. "Tom. Come back now."

Pell turned his head.

Only his head, the rest of him staying fixed toward the pit, so that the turn was slow and smooth and impossible, vertebrae rotating past any angle a neck should own, and his face came around to meet Denny's with an expression of mild, courteous interest — the look of a landlord answering the door.

"He holds on to things," Pell said.

The voice was Pell's voice and it was not. It was layered, the way a chord is layered — Pell's flat Midwestern vowels underneath, and above them something older, drier, patient as sediment, speaking through the same mouth the way water speaks through the same pipe it has rusted for a century. Each word arrived slightly after the lips finished moving, as if traveling a great distance to get there.

"Denny Okafor," the layered voice said, tasting the name. "The one who fetches. The one who goes where the work is worst. We have watched such men before. They are useful. They hold the ladder while others climb down."

"Let him go." Denny's grip tightened. His own voice sounded small and young to him, and he hated that, and he gripped harder anyway. "Whatever you are. Whatever arrangement somebody made with you a hundred years ago. That man has a wife. She makes noise because she loves him. You don't get to keep him."

A pause. In the pause, Denny became aware of the silence of the pit below them — not empty silence but attended silence, the silence of a full room listening, and he understood with total, sick certainty that the open door at the bottom of the trench was not open onto a cellar.

"We keep nothing," the voice said, almost gently. "We are kept. That is the whole of the bargain, and the whole of the grief. Ask the little counting man. His family wrote the terms. His family signed them in a hand very like his own." The layered voice warmed by a fraction, the way a host warms when the guest finally asks the right question. "But you did not come to talk terms. You came to fetch. So fetch."

Pell's body swayed. For one instant the layers thinned and Denny saw only Tom Pell underneath, terrified, drowning-eyed, mouthing one word over and over — *run, run, run* — and then the ancient patience settled back over his face like a lid.

"He cannot stay," it said. "The tenth place is set, and the seat is warm, and the house has waited fifty years for a full table. But there is room for negotiation. There is always room. That is what the ledger is *for*."

"What ledger?" Denny heard himself ask, and despised himself for asking.

The smile widened by that same unbearable degree. Behind Pell's eyes, deep in the dark of them, something leaned forward.

"Ask the counting man," it said. "Tell him the renewal date has passed. Tell him the account is overdue. Tell him—" and here the voice dropped, and every work lamp on the site dimmed as one, and the shadow at their feet stretched back toward the house like a hand reaching for a door handle, "—tell him his father left the bill unpaid."

Then Pell's knees buckled, and Denny caught him, all hundred and seventy pounds of ordinary freezing human weight, and the layered presence was gone so completely that it might have been imagined — except the tenth place setting waited in the dining room, and the water in the tenth glass had begun, very slowly, to ripple, though nothing in the world was moving but the storm coming down off the hills toward them.

Denny hauled Pell up the slope toward the kitchen lights, dragging him half-carried, shouting for Voss before they reached the door, shouting it into the wind like a warning shot:

"VOSS! It knows your name! It knows your father! Get out here — it wants to talk terms!"

Behind them, at the bottom of the pit, the unearthed cellar door swung slowly shut — from the inside — with a sound like a book closing.
