## 9. Doors Within Doors

Ivo Voss had counted doors for thirty-one years, and in all that time the count had never changed on him.

That was the point of it. That was the whole architecture of his sanity: the world was a ledger of fixed quantities, and as long as you kept your sums honest — four doors in your flat, eleven in your father's old house on Vinter Row, one hundred and six in the Harrow estate, not counting outbuildings — the world could not quietly rearrange itself around you while you slept. His father had taught him the ritual without ever teaching him its reason. *Count before bed,* the man would say, standing in the hallway with his coat still on, smelling of cold air and something older than cold air, *and count again at waking. If the numbers hold, you're safe.* He never said what happened when they didn't. He died before Ivo could ask, and Ivo had spent half his life performing a rite whose meaning had been buried with him.

At 6:40 in the morning, three days after the fingerprints, Ivo stood in the east service corridor of the Harrow estate and watched the count break.

*One hundred and seven.*

He counted again. He counted the way he did everything — slowly, with the pad of his thumb against each doorframe like a priest touching a rosary bead. Kitchen passage. Butler's pantry. The narrow door behind the copper that led to the ice room no one had used since the war. One hundred and seven doors, where yesterday there had been one hundred and six.

He walked the corridor twice more before he let himself understand what he was actually seeing. It wasn't a new door. The house hadn't grown. The door at the end of the corridor — the one to the old linen press, paint-blistered, hinge-squealing, so ordinary it had never once earned a second glance — was *wrong*. Not in its shape or its size. In its light. It sat in its frame the way a word sits in a sentence after someone has rewritten it overnight, grammatical but foreign, and when Ivo put his hand flat against its panel he felt warmth there, faint and specific, like the residual heat of a palm pressed to the other side.

He took out his kit because taking out his kit was what he did instead of screaming. Brush, tape, powder. The black dust bloomed across the wood and there they were: fingerprints, small ones, child-sized, pressed into the *inside* face of the door — which meant pressed into the linen press, which meant pressed from within a cupboard forty inches deep that had been empty since 1953 according to the inventory, and which Ivo himself had opened and inspected two days ago and found holding nothing but the smell of cedar and disuse.

The prints were fresh. The powder clung to ridges still tacky with something that wasn't quite oil and wasn't quite sweat. And they were arranged wrong — not smeared by pushing, not clustered at the handle where a trapped person would claw. They were laid out in a neat vertical column down the center of the panel, evenly spaced, five prints descending like notes on a scale. Like someone on the other side had been playing the door. Like someone had been *counting*.

Ivo's mouth went dry. He closed the kit. He did not open the linen press. Some animal ledger in his brainstem told him that opening it would change the sum, and he was not yet ready to learn the new number.

Instead he did what his father would have done. He began the full circuit of the house.

* * *

It took him two hours, and by the end of it his hands were shaking so badly he had to grip the banister on the second-floor landing like a drunk coming off a week's bender.

Every door. Every single one of the hundred and six original doors — bedroom doors, closet doors, the tall French doors in the conservatory, the little painted door of the doll's house on the nursery shelf, even the cabinet fronts in the scullery, even the *car* doors of the crew's van parked outside, he discovered later, though he couldn't make himself walk out to check until noon — every one of them wore the same fresh column of small fingerprints on its inner face.

Inner faces. That was the detail that finally made him sit down on the top stair with his head between his knees, breathing through his nose in long controlled draughts, the way Mara had taught him when she'd found him hyperventilating over a wasp in his van last spring. Inner faces only. A door has two sides; these prints were always on the side facing away from the pit, away from the cellar, away from the thing under the ground. As if the house's skin had been turned inside out in the night. As if every door in the building had become an inside surface of something much larger, and something on the far side of that larger skin had been walking along it, touching each membrane, testing each one, leaving its signature the way a surveyor leaves a stake.

Or the way a keeper leaves a mark.

"No," Ivo said aloud, to the empty landing, and the word came out smaller than he intended.

Because here was the thought he could not put down, the thought that followed him down each staircase like a patient footstep: the prints were small. Child-sized. He had measured them on the first day, in the cellar, with calipers, because measurement was his religion. Nineteen millimeters across the knuckle span. He had run them against the crew roster — Denny Okafor's big hands, the twins who worked the excavator, young Pell with his narrow wrists — nothing matched, and he had filed the non-match as *historical*, as in *old print preserved somehow*, because historical was a category that didn't require him to imagine anything still having hands.

But these were fresh. Fresh and nineteen millimeters across, and there was only one explanation left standing, and it was this: whatever made the prints had been a child once, long ago, and had never grown, because it had never been allowed to, or had never needed to, and it was now going door to door through the whole body of the house the way a child goes door to door on a morning in December.

Checking who was home.

* * *

He found Mara in the survey tent at nine, bent over her drafting table with her sleeves pushed up and graphite smudged along her forearm, and for a moment — just a moment, one held breath — looking at her was like surfacing. She was real in a way the house had stopped being. She had sawdust in her hair and a pen tucked behind her ear and when she glanced up at him her whole face did the thing it did, the small unguarded rearrangement, and Ivo felt the count in his chest steady by one.

"You look like you slept in the van again," she said.

"I didn't sleep." He lowered himself onto the folding stool across from her table. "Mara. I need to show you something, and I need you to tell me I'm wrong."

"That's usually Denny's job." But she was already capping her pen, already giving him her full attention, because she was like that — she gave people her whole attention the way other people gave spare change, except she never ran out. "Show me."

He showed her the photographs on his phone. Forty-one doors photographed, timestamped, the powder-dusted columns of small prints marching down each inner face. He watched her expression move from professional interest to puzzlement to the particular stillness she got when a contour line refused to close. She zoomed in on the nursery shot — the doll's house, its tiny front door no bigger than a stamp, wearing its own microscopic row of prints.

"Ivo." Her voice had gone quiet in the way she reserved for things she was afraid to say plainly. "These are all on the inside."

"Yes."

"Of every door."

"Every door I checked. And they weren't there yesterday. I know they weren't there yesterday because I checked forty-one doors yesterday, following my route, and—" He stopped. The sentence had a cliff at the end of it and he could feel the drop.

Mara heard it too. She set the phone down carefully, aligning its edge parallel with her table edge, because order was how both of them prayed. "Following your route," she repeated.

"My father's route." Ivo rubbed his eyes. "It's a ritual. Counting the doors, checking them, every night, in the same order. Ground floor east to west, then up, then the attic last. He did it. His father did it. I used to think it was just — a tic. A superstition. Keepers of the house, that kind of thing." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, hollow, like a door swinging on a dry hinge. "Except we were never keepers *of* the house, were we. We were keepers *for* it. And a keeper does rounds. A keeper walks the same path every night and touches every threshold and—"

"—teaches it the route," Mara finished.

The tent flap breathed in the wind. Somewhere out in the pit, the excavator sat silent, yellow and absurd, a toy abandoned mid-game.

"It's not learning the house," Ivo said. "It's learning *me*. My habits. My sequence. Last night the lights failed floor by floor, bottom to top, exactly in the order I check them — Denny noticed, he told me this morning, he drew me the timeline. And now the doors. Every door I've touched, every door I've counted, has its prints on the inside, like something has been walking my circuit behind me. All these years I thought the counting was armor. A wall I built out of numbers." He looked at his hands, at the black dust still worked into the creases of his knuckles no matter how he scrubbed. "It's not a wall. It's a trail. I've been laying it down since I was six years old. Every night. Every house. Every job. I've spent my whole life teaching it how I move."

Mara reached across the table and took his wrist — not gently, exactly, but firmly, the way you grab someone standing too near an edge. "Then stop moving," she said. "Break the pattern. Tonight, sleep somewhere else. Sleep in the tent, sleep in the van, sleep in the middle of the field if you have to. Don't count. Don't check. Let tonight be the first night in thirty-one years the route goes unwalked."

"And if the route isn't optional?" The question fell out of him before he could weigh it, and he heard what it really was: not a question about logistics but about ownership. *Whose habit is it now?* "My aunt called me the night before I took this job. First time in nine years. She said, don't take jobs with old doors. I asked her why and she hung up. Mara, my family has been doing this rite for six generations. You don't do anything for six generations unless stopping costs something."

"Then we find out what it costs together," Mara said, "instead of paying alone for another fifty years." She held his wrist a moment longer, thumb over his pulse, counting it, he realized — counting him, in her own language, her own ritual — and then released him and picked up her pencil as if the matter were settled, as if defiance were simply another line to be drawn and staked.

He loved her so much in that moment that it frightened him more than the doors did. Because the house had already shown him what it did with things that mattered: it put them in columns. It counted them.

* * *

Dusk came early, the storm clouds stacking up over the western ridge like slate being unloaded, and at 4:50 in the afternoon — hours before any honest darkness — Ivo stood in the cellar doorway and understood, finally, what he had been looking at since the first day.

The unearthed door was right where it had always been, at the back of the excavation, bricked over until the crew's crowbars found it. Sealed from within: bolts thrown on the inside, wax poured into the lock from the inside, hinges pinned from the inside. For three days he had framed it as a boundary. A container. The lid on the jar. Everything else — the prints, the generator dying with a full tank, Pell wandering toward the pit murmuring that he was expected — he had filed under *leakage*, as if the door were imperfectly sealed and the thing inside were seeping through the cracks.

Standing there now, with the cold breathing up out of the dark behind the timbers, Ivo saw the error of his arithmetic.

A boundary keeps two things apart. But a mouth —

A mouth is a boundary too. Lips meet; inside and outside touch; and the resemblance ends there, because a mouth is not built to separate. It is built to admit. It is the place where the outside world is taken in, transformed, made part of another body. And every feature of the sealed door — the bolts thrown from within, the wax poured from within, the prints pressed from within — described not imprisonment but *swallowing completed*. Something had gone in. Something had drawn the door shut behind it the way you close your lips. The seal wasn't keeping it in.

The seal was keeping the flavor of it in. Until it was wanted.

"Oh," Ivo said softly, to the pit, to the grey light failing above him. "You're not the wall. You're the mouth. You've been the mouth the whole time."

And the rest of the house — the hundred and six doors with their fresh inner prints, the corridors, the stairwells, the linen presses and pantries and nursery cupboards — those weren't walls either. They were throat. The whole estate was one continuous interior, gullet and gut, and every door was a valve along it, and the small patient hands had been walking the length of the swallow, checking each valve, finding them all — every one, without exception — open from their side.

All except one. He knew it before he turned around. He felt it the way you feel a missing tooth.

Behind him, at the top of the cellar stairs, the door to the archive stood shut, and its inner face — he had checked it an hour ago, following his route, because he couldn't not — was clean. No prints. No warm palm-heat. The only door in the house that the small hands had not touched, and Ivo understood that too, with the clarity that comes at the bottom of a fall: the archive was where the ledger lived. The ledger with his name in it, six generations deep. The house hadn't overlooked that door.

The house was saving it. The way you save the last page.

From somewhere above him — the kitchen, maybe, or the pantry, or some space between rooms that had never appeared on any survey — came a sound that Ivo's professional mind catalogued automatically, uselessly, as *impact, wooden, repeated*: a small fist knocking on a door. Then another door. Then another. Working along the corridor. Descending.

Knocking on the insides of doors, patiently, in sequence, in an order Ivo knew by heart.

His order.

And beneath the knocking, so faint he might have invented it, a voice — small, child-sized, worn smooth as sea glass — counting.

"...one hundred and four. One hundred and five. One hundred and six."

A pause, at the top of the cellar stairs, directly above him.

"One hundred and seven," said the voice, and Ivo realized he was standing under the one door in the house he had never counted, because it had been bricked over for a century, because it had not existed in his father's route, because it was new — and the voice above him, patient and pleased, was waiting for him to come up and add it to the list.
