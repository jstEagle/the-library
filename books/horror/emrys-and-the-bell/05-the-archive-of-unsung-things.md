## 5. The Archive of Unsung Things

The parish records were kept in a room behind the vestry that smelled of cold stone and older paper, and the woman who kept the key was named Mrs. Aldous, though everyone in Hollowmere called her Tam's sister, as if she had no name of her own.

"You'll want the west shelf," she said, unlocking the door with a key that hung from her waist on a length of braided wool rather than a chain, as if metal near these documents had been deemed inadvisable at some point in the room's long history. "Parish registers, harvest ledgers, the coroner's copies. Mind the damp. And mind—" she paused, one hand still on the door, "—mind that you put everything back where you find it. Tam's particular about order."

"I'm particular about order too," Vesper said, and meant it. Order was the whole architecture of her trade: the index card, the cross-reference, the footnote. She had built a career out of believing that if you arranged enough small true things in a row, they would eventually spell something larger.

Mrs. Aldous looked at her for a moment longer than courtesy required. There was something in her face — not hostility, nothing so useful as hostility — that reminded Vesper of the way the villagers had watched her since the night old Bess Corrow had stopped singing mid-verse and begged her to erase the tape. A watchfulness without edges. The look of people waiting to see which way a weather front would break.

"Mind the damp," Mrs. Aldous said again, and left her alone with the dead of Hollowmere.

* * *

The room was smaller than it sounded when the door closed — no, Vesper corrected herself, listening. Smaller than it should have been. The stone walls seemed to sit a little closer once she was inside, the way a house settles around an occupant, or the way a mouth closes gently around a word it has decided to keep. She shook off the thought. Two nights of bad sleep and one impossible boy on her porch, and already her mind was furnishing empty rooms with atmospherics. That was the occupational hazard of folklore: you spent so long among other people's fears that your own began to dress themselves in borrowed clothes.

She set up her work the way she always did: notebook open, pencils sharpened, the tape recorder — the new one, bought in Leeds after she'd erased Bess Corrow's song as asked — sitting silent and obedient on the table. Daylight came through a single high window, thick and greenish, filtered through glass so old it had begun to flow. It fell across the shelves like light through water.

The registers went back further than she'd expected. Further than they should have, honestly; the earliest volume was dated 1602, its vellum stiff as bark, its ink gone the brown of dried blood. Hollowmere had never been more than a village — four hundred souls at its flood, fewer than two hundred now — but it had kept its records with the devotion of a place that had decided early on that forgetting was the one sin it could not afford.

Vesper began, as she always began, with the harvest.

Her grant proposal had been elegant in the way proposals must be: *A comparative study of agrarian festival songs in isolated upland communities, with attention to transmission variance.* She'd written it in the glow of Bess Corrow's kitchen, before the old woman's voice had cracked and her hands had started shaking and she'd gripped Vesper's wrist hard enough to leave marks and said, *don't ever ask me to sing it twice, girl. Not you. Not anyone.*

Transmission variance. That was the scholarly term for what happened when a song changed as it passed from mouth to mouth — verses dropped, names swapped, refrains bent to fit new harvests and new grievances. It was supposed to be gradual, generational, the slow erosion of oral memory. What Vesper wanted from Hollowmere was a clean case study: a village isolated enough, old enough, stubborn enough to have preserved its songs nearly intact.

What she found in the registers was not songs at all. Not yet. But it was a pattern, and patterns were the raw ore of scholarship, and for the first hour she felt the old clean thrill of it — the hunter's joy, the sense of the world agreeing to make sense.

It started with the deaths.

The parish register recorded burials, naturally, but it also recorded — in a cramped later hand, squeezed into margins and between lines, as if someone had been keeping a second, unofficial ledger inside the official one — the disappearances. Vesper counted eleven of them across three centuries. Eleven souls who had walked out of Hollowmere and not walked back, each noted with a brevity that read, to modern eyes, like callousness, but which Vesper recognized as something else: the brevity of a community that did not want to say more than it had to.

*1791. Michael Wrack, shepherd, 34 years. Went from his own doorstep. Never found.*

*1834. The Tredwell child, 6 years. Taken from the lane by the church. Never found.*

*1877. Sarah Nance, 19 years. Gone from her bed, door locked within. Never found.*

*1903. Thomas Ibbetts, 41 years. Never found.*

*1929. The Pellow twins, 8 years. Never found.*

Eleven entries. Eleven dates. Vesper wrote them all out on a fresh page, because that was what she did, because arrangement was prayer in her religion, and then she sat looking at them while the green light moved slowly across the desk.

Every date fell in late September or early October. Every single one.

She checked against the almanac data she carried in her head from years of festival research — harvest moons, the full moon nearest the autumn equinox, the moon under which every agrarian community in Britain brought in the last of the grain and sang the songs that thanked whatever needed thanking. Eleven disappearances. Eleven harvest moons. The odds of coincidence were the kind of odds scholars got to invoke only when they wanted to be laughed out of a conference hall.

"Okay," Vesper said quietly, to the room, to herself. "Okay."

Her heart was doing the thing it did when a thesis assembled itself — quick, bright, greedy. Because this was *material*. This was the kind of buried structure folklorists dreamed of: a village whose deepest trauma was calendrically bound to its most sacred festival, and nobody had ever written it down, because the village itself had done the burying. She could see the paper taking shape. *Hollowmere and the Hungry Moon: Festival, Fear, and Communal Memory in an Upland Parish.* She could see the keynote invitations.

She reached for the next volume, and that was when she noticed the witness statements.

They were bound separately — a thin folder of loose pages, undated as a group but individually marked, kept apart from the registers the way you keep a thing apart when you can't decide whether to destroy it. Depositions, mostly. Statements taken by constables or clergymen after each disappearance, transcribed in various hands across three centuries. Routine inquiries, most likely, preserved by accident or by some clerk's dogged completeness.

Vesper read the first one standing up, and was still standing when she finished it, and was still standing, unmoving, when she finished the fourth.

*Statement of Anne Wrack, wife of the missing, taken the third day of his absence:* "He stepped out to see to the gate, not five minutes past full dark. I heard him stop on the step. He said — he said, *there's a boy on the porch.* I thought he meant one of the Halloran lads come round for cider. But when I followed him out there was no one. No one at all. Only the gate swinging."

*Statement of the Reverend Mr. Quill, regarding the Tredwell child:* "The mother is firm in her account. The girl was at the window watching the lane. She called out, *Mama, there's a boy on our porch,* and her mother, busy at the loom, told her to leave off. When she looked again the window was empty and the latch was turned from within."

*Statement of Mrs. Nance, elder, regarding her daughter:* "Sarah came down pale as whey and said she'd seen a boy sitting on the porch wall, and that he'd smiled at her crooked, and she didn't like it. I told her there's been no boys on that road since the Hallorans moved away. She laughed and said, *then I'm seeing things,* and went up to bed. Door locked within, as stated."

Crooked smile.

Vesper's hand had gone cold on the page. She set the folder down, deliberately, the way you set down a thing that might be alive, and made herself breathe, and made herself think like a scholar, because thinking like a scholar was the only tool she had and she intended to use it until it broke.

Oral tradition mutated. She knew this better than almost anyone alive. A detail like *a boy on the porch* — strange, vivid, portable — was exactly the kind of detail that spread through a community's telling and retelling until it attached itself to every story in the corpus. It was contamination, cross-pollination, the folklore equivalent of a meme. Three centuries of witnesses describing the same improbable figure didn't mean the figure existed. It meant the village had a story-shape, and grief poured into story-shapes the way water pours into low ground.

That was the explanation. It was a good explanation. It was the explanation she would print.

But explanations are for daylight, and the light through the high window had gone from green to grey, and Vesper stood in the cold stone room with the folder open in front of her and admitted, privately, in the place where she kept the things she'd never say aloud at conferences, that the explanation did not help. Because she had seen that crooked smile. Last night. On her own porch. On a face that had not aged a day in twenty years, wearing a coat she had described to him herself when she was seven years old and lonely enough to build a person out of spare parts and wanting.

"There's a boy on the porch," she said softly, testing the words. They tasted like a nursery rhyme. They tasted like an invitation.

She read the rest.

The pattern held through every deposition, across every century, in every hand: someone saw a boy on a porch, or heard a family member speak of one, and within hours — sometimes minutes — that household was short one soul. In 1929, the mother of the Pellow twins had left a statement so brief it was almost a scream: *"They said there was a boy on the porch and they went out to play with him. They went OUT TO PLAY WITH HIM."* The capital letters were the transcriber's, the only violence in the whole quiet archive.

And then, at the bottom of the folder, a final page in a different category entirely. Not a deposition. A letter, unsigned, undated, but the paper was recent — post-war, Vesper guessed, maybe fifties. The handwriting was careful, educated, the hand of someone writing fast and trying to write calmly.

*"To whoever keeps these records after me. I have served this parish forty years and I will tell you what the registers will not. We do not lose people to the moor or the mine or the mill pond. We lose them to the porch. Every harvest moon it comes to a door and waits to be seen, and the seeing is the summons, and the summons cannot be unsaid. Old Marta's grandmother told me the songs hold it back — sing them once, fully, and it stays fed and stays outside. But the songs are failing. Fewer remember the verses every year. I have done what I can. God forgive us all for what we let that child do in 1961, and God keep the Okafor girl wherever she was sent, because I believe to my soul the thing has been waiting for her to come back."*

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above the ceiling, in the space between the vestry roof and the sky, a bird shifted its feet on the slates.

*1961.* Vesper did the arithmetic without wanting to. She had been born in 1952. In the autumn of 1961 she had been nine years old, and she had spent three days — three days the village had explained to her parents as a fever, a wandering, a child lost on the moor and found again — and she had never been able to account for those days herself, not truly, not even now. There was a well in them somewhere. She knew that much. A well and a voice and words spoken aloud that she had spent twenty years pretending she couldn't remember.

And then her parents had taken her away from Hollowmere for good, and the village had let them go without a word of protest, and no one had ever visited, and no one had ever written, and she had told herself for twenty years that it was because small places forgot people easily.

The letter said otherwise. The letter said they hadn't forgotten her at all. They'd been *waiting* for her.

Vesper became aware, gradually, of a sound beneath the silence — a faint sibilant hiss, steady as breathing. She turned. Her tape recorder sat on the table where she'd left it, switched off, its reels still, its little red eye dark.

The hiss stopped when she looked at it.

She stood for a long moment, one hand pressed flat against the cold stone of the wall, grounding herself in the mineral fact of it. Then she gathered her notes, squared the folders on their shelf exactly as she'd found them — mind the order, Tam's particular about order — and let herself out into the grey afternoon, locking the door behind her with Mrs. Aldous's borrowed key.

The village street was empty in the way streets are empty when they are not actually empty. Curtains hung very slightly displaced at windows. A man stacking firewood had paused mid-stack. Somewhere a dog was barking, far off, and then, abruptly, wasn't.

Marta was waiting at the corner by the well-path, arms folded tight against the wind, and her face, when Vesper reached her, was the face of someone who had drawn a conclusion some time ago and had been dreading having to say it out loud.

"You've been in the records," Marta said. It wasn't a question.

"I've been doing my research."

"Aye." Marta glanced up the street, down the street, at the low sun bleeding into the moor. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing, worn smooth as a stair-stone by generations of use. "Then you'll have found the dates. And you'll have found the boy. And I expect you're standing there telling yourself it's just stories, the way clever people do."

"Marta—"

"My grandmother sang the binding songs," Marta said. "Her mother before her, and hers before that, back and back till the names wear out. Once each, never twice. That's the whole law of this village, Vesper, and we've kept it for three hundred years, and kept *you* fed at the edge of it without ever telling you why." She stepped closer. Up close her eyes were wet and furious and terrified all at once, and much older than her face. "Harvest moon's tomorrow night. So you'll listen to me now, and you'll listen good: pack your tapes, pack your car, and be over the ridge before the light goes. Because whatever's been waiting on your porch —"

She stopped. Her gaze had gone past Vesper's shoulder, down the empty street, and everything in her face closed like a door.

"— it's already counting the days," she finished quietly. "And so, I think, is he."
