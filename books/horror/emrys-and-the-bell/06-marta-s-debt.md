## 6. Marta's Debt

Marta Fenwick had baked bread that morning the way her mother had taught her, and her mother before her, and her grandmother before that — with salt in the dough and a pinch thrown over the left shoulder, and never once, not ever, a hum while the oven door was open. The rules were so old they had worn smooth as river stones. She did not remember learning them. She remembered only that there had never been a time without them.

The bread was for the Okafor girl.

She told herself it was neighborliness. A woman come back to Hollowmere after twenty years, renting the Hollis cottage alone, no larder stocked, no fire laid. Any decent person would bring bread. But Marta knew, kneading the dough in the grey light before dawn, that the bread was an apology she could not speak aloud, and a warning she could not say straight, and a payment besides — because everything in Hollowmere was a payment, had been for two hundred years, and Marta's family had always been the ones who kept the ledger.

Her hands knew the work too well to shake, but they shook anyway.

* * *

The cottage sat at the end of Larkspur Lane where the lane gave up and became field, and Marta walked its length with the basket on her arm and counted the doors she passed the way her father had taught her to count them: one, and don't look back; two, and don't look back; three, and if you hear your name, let it be someone else's. The morning was bright and ordinary. Rooks argued in the elms. Somewhere down in Wray's Field a tractor coughed and caught. It was the kind of morning that made the old rules feel like superstition, the kind of morning that had fooled better women than her into thinking Hollowmere was just a village, small and poor and forgetful.

Marta had never once been fooled. That was her inheritance too.

She knocked on the cottage door — twice, softly, knuckles only — and heard footsteps inside, and then Vesper Okafor opened the door looking exactly as she had at nine years old and nothing at all like it. Taller, of course. Sharper through the face. But the eyes were the same: dark, watchful, set slightly wide, the eyes of a child who had learned early that rooms held more than furniture. There were shadows under those eyes now, fresh ones, and Marta's stomach dropped before the girl said a single word.

"Missus Fenwick." Vesper managed something like a smile. "You didn't have to—"

"Bread." Marta pushed the basket into her hands before courtesy could make either of them stand in the doorway longer than doors allowed. "And I'll come in, if you're offering tea, because I've things to say and I'd rather not say them standing where the lane can hear."

Something flickered across the young woman's face — the folklorist's instinct, Marta saw, the scholar catching the scent of a story — and beneath it, fainter, the other thing, the child-thing, the fear. Twenty years away and the fear still lived in her like a tenant who'd never been given notice.

"Come in," Vesper said.

Marta stepped over the threshold left foot first, the way you did, and did not look at the corners of the room until she had checked them, quickly, the way you also did.

* * *

The kitchen of the Hollis cottage had not changed since Marta had last stood in it, which was the year of the bad frost, which was the year after the girl had gone away. Same range, same dresser, same hairline crack running from the window frame toward the ceiling like a river on a map of nowhere. Marta sat where she could see both the door and the window and began talking about nothing — the weather, the price of feed, Mrs. Aldous's hip — while the kettle worked itself up to a whistle, and all the while she watched Vesper arrange her tape recorder on the table between them like a small black altar.

"You'll want me for your songs," Marta said, when the silence had stretched long enough.

"I'd be grateful, yes. The harvest set especially. Old Nan Preece started 'The Barley Bride' for me yesterday and then—" Vesper hesitated. "She stopped. Mid-verse. She asked me to erase the tape."

"And did you?"

"I did."

"Good," Marta said, and meant it with her whole chest, and saw that Vesper noticed the weight of the word and was turning it over, examining it, the way she'd turned over stones in the beck as a child looking for what lived underneath. That was the trouble with this one. She had always looked underneath.

"Marta—" the girl said, and then corrected herself, careful, "Mrs. Fenwick. In my research, when a tradition stops mid-performance, it usually means one of two things. Either the song is broken — lost verses, forgotten lines — or the song is *doing something*, and the singer is afraid of finishing it."

The kettle screamed. Marta got up and took it off the heat mostly to have something to do with her hands, and poured the tea, and set the cup down, and sat, and folded her hands in her lap where they could worry at each other unseen.

"My family has sung the harvest songs for six generations," she said. "Fenwicks before us were the Wrays, and the Wrays before them were the ones who built the wall around Wray's Field, though nobody remembers why the wall's there now except us. We sing at the harvest home, we sing at the gleaning, we sing at the moonrise supper. Every verse, every song, once." She lifted her eyes. "Never twice."

Vesper had gone very still. It was the stillness of a bird that has heard the cat's bell and is deciding whether the garden is worth dying in.

"Why never twice?"

"Because a song sung once is a story," Marta said. "A song sung twice is an invitation. You don't ring a bell once to call a dog home, girl. You ring it till it comes."

"That's folklore logic. Sympathetic principle. Repetition as invocation, it's documented in dozens of traditions, the Orkney wak songs, the—"

"I don't know your books." Marta's voice came out harder than she intended, and she softened it. "I know what my grandmother told me, and she had it from hers, and hers had it written down in a hand that shook so bad you could barely read it, and that woman wrote it because she'd seen what happened the year the singing stopped. 1847. Look in your parish records if you don't believe me. Look at what's missing from them."

"What's missing?"

"The Wrays," Marta said. "All of them. Eleven souls. And the Aldouses of that time, seven. And a tinker's family passing through, four, though nobody counted them proper. One harvest moon. One night the songs went unsung, because the whole village was down with fever and there weren't enough voices left to carry the tune." She drank her tea because her mouth had gone dry as ash. "In the morning, the ones who recovered couldn't say where their neighbors had gone. Couldn't say, or wouldn't. My great-great-grandmother could, but she was eight years old and nobody believed children in those days any more than they believe them now."

Vesper's recorder sat between them on the table. Marta looked at it. It was not recording — she checked, she always checked, the little red eye dark — but she moved her teacup so it sat between her mouth and the machine regardless.

"Kept fed and contained," Vesper said quietly. Not a question. "That's what the songs do. That's what you're telling me."

"They keep it fed," Marta said. "The containing is more... hopeful."

* * *

She told the girl what she could bear to tell, and some of what she could not, and the telling took the morning and most of the strength she had.

She told her how the songs were a ledger, each verse naming a debt and deferring it — *the barley we cut, the wheat we bound, the first fruit off the bough* — how every line was a coin paid out so that nothing larger would be collected. How the rule of once-only was the hinge of the whole arrangement: a verse named the offering, and the offering was made by the naming, and to name it again would be to offer it again, and what the village offered again and again, generation upon generation, was not barley.

She told her that the Fenwick women sang the longest song, the one called 'The Keeping,' alone, at the full dark of every harvest moon, standing at their own doorstep facing north, and that they sang it once and only once and then went inside and locked the door and did not sleep until dawn. Six generations. Her daughter had the voice for it. Her granddaughter, God help them all, already hummed it in her sleep.

"And it works?" Vesper asked. Her pen had stopped moving. "Whatever it is — it stays?"

"It stays fed," Marta said again. "Fed things drowse. Fed things forget. It's not a fence, girl, it's a trough, and we've been filling it for two hundred years, and a trough only works so long as somebody keeps carrying water." She looked at her own hands, knotted and brown, her mother's hands, her grandmother's. "My turn's coming. This moon. And after me, Bethan's. And I lie awake some nights doing the arithmetic of it, how many moons are left in me, how many in Bethan, whether my granddaughter will be singing at a doorstep in fifty years or whether the thing in the dark will finally have eaten through everything we owe."

The words hung in the kitchen. Outside, a rook called once and was answered, and even that sounded like counting.

"There's something else," Marta said, and here her voice failed her for the first time, and she had to begin again. "There's something I have to ask you, and you're not going to like it, and I need you to listen anyway. Because yesterday evening I saw what was sitting on your porch."

Vesper's face did something complicated. Denial first, then the collapse of denial, then a terrible steadiness. "You saw him."

"A boy," Marta said. "Sitting on the step with his knees drawn up, patient as Sunday. Dark-haired. Smiling crooked, like his face couldn't decide." She had not been able to make herself look directly at him; she had crossed to the far hedge and hurried past with her eyes fixed on the middle distance, and even so the sight of him had followed her home and sat with her through supper and lain down beside her in the dark. "Twenty years ago there was a boy like that seen on this lane, and folks said the Okafor girl's fancy had taken to wandering. Children's fancies do wander, they said. Harmless. And then the girl went away to her aunt's and the boy was seen no more, and everyone agreed that was the natural end of it."

"But he's back," Vesper said. Her voice was flat and careful, a floor tested for rot. "He hasn't changed. Not a day. He's exactly—" She stopped. Swallowed. "He remembers everything."

*Aye*, Marta thought. *That's the horror of it, isn't it. Not that it waits. That it keeps accounts.*

"Then hear me," she said, and leaned forward, and took the girl's cold hands in her own rough ones, because some things had to be said skin to skin or not at all. "Whatever he was to you — playmate, brother, shadow, sin — whatever bargain was struck, however young you were, however lonely — none of that matters now. What matters is the moon. Five nights hence, the harvest moon rises, and on that night the debts come due and the songs are sung and the doors are counted and every soul in this village sits up with their lights burning. You will not be safe in this cottage. You will not be safe anywhere inside the hollow, and the hollow goes further than the maps say. So pack your tapes and your books and that machine there, and you get in your car, and you drive until the moon can't find you, and you don't stop for anything that calls your name in a voice you know."

Vesper pulled her hands free. Not unkindly. But free.

"I came here to record the songs," she said. "The real ones, complete. Whatever's happening in this village — whatever you've all been carrying — it deserves to be written down. Documented. Understood. That's what I *do*, Mrs. Fenwick. And I think—" the folklorist's light kindled in her face, that hungry scholarly light that Marta had come to dread worse than any boy on any porch — "I think the songs and the disappearances and the well at Wray's Field and whatever's on my porch are all one story. And I think it's a story that ends if people like you keep refusing to sing the second half."

"The second half is what we're *refusing for*," Marta said, rising, her chair scraping. "You clever girl. You clever, doomed girl. Do you think we haven't wondered? Do you think six generations of women haven't stood on their doorsteps wondering what the rest of the song says? My grandmother found a scrap of it once, written inside a Bible no family would claim, and she read three words aloud before my great-aunt clapped a hand over her mouth, and for a week after, the dogs wouldn't bark and the milk soured in the churn and something walked the lane at midnight whistling a tune it had no business knowing." Her breath was coming short. "Curiosity is how it gets you. Curiosity is the shape of its teeth."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The kettle ticked as it cooled. On the table the tape recorder sat with its red eye dark, and Marta thought: *it hears even when it isn't listening, everything in this village hears, the walls themselves have been eavesdropping for two hundred years.*

"I won't leave," Vesper said at last, gently, the way you tell a child there's no such thing as monsters. "But thank you. For the bread. For the warning. For trusting me with it."

Marta looked at her — at the shadows under her eyes, at the recorder, at the stubborn set of her jaw that was so like her father's and would be the death of her the way it had nearly been the death of him — and felt something in her chest go quiet and cold and clear, the way it did before a storm.

"Then I'll tell you the last of it," she said, "and you'll wish I hadn't. The Fenwicks don't just sing to keep it fed. We sing to keep it *here*. Contained, I said, and you asked how, and I lied by not answering. We don't keep it in the hollow, girl. We keep it in the *village*. In the ground under it, in the walls of it, in the space between one counted door and the next. The songs aren't a fence around it. They're a fence around *us* — and it's inside the fence with us, and it always has been, and the only mercy in the arrangement is that it sleeps as long as it's fed." She was at the door now, left foot first, not looking at the corners. "So when you sit up on moonrise night with your lights burning, understand what the lights are for. They're not to keep the dark out."

She paused with her hand on the latch.

"They're so it can see us paying."

Outside, the morning had gone strange and gold, the rooks silent in the elms, and far off in Wray's Field the tractor had stopped. Marta walked the lane home counting doors — one, and don't look back; two, and don't look back — and at the third door she heard, very clearly, from behind her, a child's voice humming a harvest tune she knew, keeping perfect time, one verse and then beginning it again.

She did not look back. But she walked faster than she had in twenty years, and when she reached her own doorstep she stood a long moment with her key in the lock, listening to the second verse unfold — the true one, the forbidden one, the one her grandmother had read three words of — sung sweet and clear and patiently wrong, in a voice like a boy's, waiting to be finished.
