## 15. Sunrise Arithmetic

The moon was a coin held against the dark, and Vesper could not stop looking at it.

She sat on the floor of the cottage's back room with her knees drawn up and her notebook open across them, and she did what she had always done when the world grew too large: she made it small. She made it numbers.

Moonrise had been at 6:41. She had watched it come up over Wray's Field from Marta's kitchen window while Marta stood at the sink with her back turned, washing dishes that were already clean, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. Moonset — she'd looked it up in the almanac she kept in her field bag, the one she'd bought in a secondhand shop in Aberystwyth a decade ago and never once needed until tonight — would be at 4:17.

Nine hours and thirty-six minutes.

She wrote it down. *9:36.* Underlined it twice. Then she began the other arithmetic, the kind that couldn't be checked against an almanac.

*What happens at 4:17 when the moon sets unpaid?*

She knew some of it already. She'd seen the beginning of it an hour ago, when Emrys had sung the harvest song through a second time, standing in her yard with his hands in his pockets like a boy waiting for a bus, his voice light and clear and perfectly, horribly accurate. The dogs had gone silent — not barking, not whining, just *gone*, as if the village had never contained a dog at all. The lights had failed house by house, not all at once but in sequence, west to east, like someone walking the street and blowing out candles. And Marta had come pounding up the path, hammering on the door, screaming *what did you let him do* through the wood until her voice cracked, until Vesper had opened the door and found the old woman gray-faced in the moonlight with her coat buttoned wrong and her eyes full of a terror so old it had gone smooth as river stone.

*He knows the words now,* Marta had said. *He's sung it twice. There's nothing between him and the door anymore except the hours.*

Except the hours. Except the arithmetic.

Vesper turned the page. Her pen hovered.

Because here was the thing she kept circling and refusing to land on: Emrys did not sound like a monster. He sounded like a boy who had been left outside for twenty years, knocking politely, and who had finally been told — by the whole village, in the language of withheld songs and counted doors and nailed windows — that no one was ever coming to let him in.

And he was hungry. That was the part she couldn't make small. Hunger didn't care about fairness. Hunger didn't care that the debt was hers and the punishment had been distributed to everyone else. When the moon set and the ledger came due, hunger would do what hunger does.

She wrote: *Options.*

Under it, she listed them the way she'd once listed the doors of her childhood house, counting them every night before sleep because her mother had said a counted house was a safe house:

1. Run.
2. Pay.
3. Find another way.

*Run.* She crossed it out almost immediately, but her pen lingered, honest enough to admit why she wanted it. The car was in the lane. The roads out of Hollowmere were single-track and unlit, but she'd driven worse. She could be past the county line by midnight, in a motorway service station by two, telling herself the story everyone in this village had been telling themselves for two hundred years: *it isn't my debt, I didn't know, I was only passing through.*

But she had watched Marta button her coat wrong. She had heard the dogs go silent. And she had understood, sitting in the dark with the tape recorder hissing on the table, that running wouldn't end the debt — it would only change whose porch he waited on next. Debts like this didn't lapse. They transferred. That was the whole architecture of Hollowmere; she saw it now, saw how the entire village was built like a relay team, each generation carrying the burden until their arms gave out and passing it forward. If she ran, the baton would fall, and whatever caught it when it fell would not be gentle about picking it up.

She crossed out *run* so hard the pen tore the paper.

*Pay.* She stared at that word for a long time. Outside, the wind moved through the hedges with a sound like slow breathing. What paying meant, she understood well enough now — Emrys had explained it with terrible gentleness, sitting on her porch step in the moonlight, visible only to her, patient as furniture. *You promised me your life,* he'd said. Not menacingly. Almost apologetically. *I only ever asked for what you offered.* And she had been nine years old and invisible and so lonely that loneliness had stopped being a feeling and become a place she lived, and she had knelt at the old well in Wray's Field and spoken into the dark: *stay with me forever. I'll give you anything. I'll give you everything I am.*

A child's hyperbole. A child's bargain. And the thing in the well had taken it as contract law.

If she paid, she died — or worse than died, if the disappearances in the parish records meant anything, if the accounts of children who walked out of their houses on harvest moons and were never found meant anything. But the village lived. Marta lived. Marta's grandson lived, the boy with the gap teeth who had waved at Vesper from a garden wall on her first morning here, before she'd known what waving at a stranger in Hollowmere might cost.

Her hand shook, and she set the pen down, and she pressed the heels of both palms against her eyes until she saw constellations.

That was when the knock came.

Not a pounding. Not Marta's desperate fists. Three soft raps, spaced evenly, at the front door — the same rhythm, she realized with a lurch, that he had used when they were children, when he'd come to her bedroom window in the evenings and knocked three times so her parents wouldn't hear, so this would stay theirs.

*Ours,* she thought bitterly. *Even then I was making bargains I didn't understand.*

She didn't answer. She didn't need to. The door didn't open — he never opened doors, that was one of the rules, one of the few — but his voice came through the wood anyway, quiet and clear, the way it had come through all night after her first dusk here, when she'd fled inside and counted the doors twice and listened to him remember things only she could know.

"You're doing sums," Emrys said. "I can hear the pen."

"Go away."

"You've torn through the paper twice." A pause. "You always pressed too hard. Miss Ellery used to say you wrote like you were trying to leave a mark on the other side of the page."

Vesper got up. She crossed the room and put her hand flat against the door, not because she wanted to touch where his voice was but because she wanted to feel whether the wood was cold. It wasn't. It was warm, faintly, the way the stone rim of the well had been warm, and that was worse.

"There are nine hours," she said. "Less. What do you want?"

"To talk arithmetic with you." She could hear the crooked smile in it. Twenty years, and she could still hear the smile. "You were always better at it than me."

"I'm not doing this."

"You listed three options. I felt you cross out the first one — that was honest of you. Running doesn't suit you; it never did. You're a girl who counts doors. You need to know where all the exits are precisely so you can choose not to use them." The warmth of the wood pulsed faintly under her palm, or she imagined it did. "So it's option two or option three. Pay, or find another way. Shall I tell you what I know about option three?"

"There is no option three."

"There is," Emrys said, "and it's the reason I'm here instead of already collected. It's the reason any of us are ever anywhere. Debts like ours aren't names, Vesper. They're shapes. A shape needs filling. The village has spent two hundred years pouring songs into the shape to keep it full — one verse each, never twice, a spoonful at a time, forever. It was never mercy. It was rationing." His voice stayed level, almost kind. "The shape is nearly empty now. But a shape can be filled with anything that fits. A life fits best. Any life."

The cold went through her slowly, the way water goes through cloth.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard—"

"*No.*" She took her hand off the door as if it had burned her, which in a way it had. "I know what you're going to say. I've known since the well. You're going to say I can name someone. Put a name into the shape instead of mine. Someone who owes less. Someone who—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Someone smaller."

Silence from the other side of the door. Then, very quietly: "It wouldn't have to be a child."

"That's not the point."

"It's exactly the point. It's the only point there's ever been. The village fed me children for a hundred years before you were born, Vesper — did Marta tell you that part yet, or is she saving it? Did Tam? They fed me the Okafor girl's classmates' older brother. They fed me the miller's daughter. They sang their little fence-songs and called it keeping safe, and what they were doing was choosing, every generation, whose child was worth less than the rest." Now something moved in his voice, something that wasn't hunger, something rawer. "I'm offering you the same choice they made. The same choice they'll offer *you*, before this is done — wait and see. Name someone. Anyone. The debt transfers. The village goes on singing its half-verses and nailing its windows and pretending it's innocence. Everyone lives. Isn't that what you want? Everyone lives?"

Vesper stood in her dim back room with her notebook on the floor and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she found — to her surprise, to her horror — that she was not tempted.

Not because she was brave. She had spent nine days in this village discovering how much of her courage was actually curiosity wearing a coat. But because she could see the shape of the trap, and it wasn't Emrys's trap. It was the village's. It had always been the village's. *Name someone.* That was the engine under everything — under the unsung second verses, under the counted doors, under the way Tam and the elders had buried a nine-year-old girl's three lost days and called it protection. Every soul in Hollowmere had survived by agreeing, silently, generation after generation, that the debt should land somewhere else. On the miller's daughter. On the missing. On the girl who talked to nobody.

She thought of Marta, buttoning her coat wrong. Marta, whose family had carried the binding songs like a yoke, who had begged Vesper to leave not out of kindness but out of the exhausted arithmetic of inherited guilt — *if she leaves, maybe it won't be our turn again.* Marta, who had pounded on the door tonight and screamed *what did you let him do*, and not once, not once, had asked *are you all right*.

"No," Vesper said again, and this time her voice was steady, and she noticed that steadiness the way you notice solid ground after a long fall. "I'm not naming anyone. Not a stranger. Not Marta's grandson. Not Tam, though God knows he's earned it. Not anyone. You want to know what option three is, Emrys? Option three is that I find out what this actually is. All of it. What you were before the well. What the songs really say — the whole verses, not the amputated ones they sing at harvest. What name you had before I gave you one." She bent and picked up her notebook, and held it against her chest like a shield, which was ridiculous, and which she did anyway. "Because I don't believe you're what they say you are. I've listened to you for nine days. Monsters don't remember what Miss Ellery said about my handwriting."

Another silence. Longer. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — smaller, careful, the voice of a boy at a window hoping not to be sent away.

"The second verse," he said. "They cut it out two hundred years ago. Before my time. Before anyone's time that's still living. I've never heard it whole. Do you know what that's like? To be named by a fragment?" A breath that was not a breath. "There's a copy in the parish chest. Old Tam keeps the key around his neck. He sleeps with it. He'll die with it."

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because you refused," Emrys said simply. "Nobody's ever refused before. They always either ran or named." The warmth in the door faded, degree by degree, like a hand withdrawing. "The moon sets at ten past four. I'll be at the well. Whatever you find — or don't — I'll be there. I've waited twenty years, Vesper. I can wait six more hours."

Then the presence behind the door was gone, the way a note is gone after it finishes sounding, and the cottage was just a cottage, cold and ordinary, and Vesper was alone with her notebook and her arithmetic.

She looked down at the page. Under *3. Find another way*, her own handwriting had appeared without her quite remembering writing it — she must have scrawled it in the middle of everything, mid-refusal, the pen moving on its own:

*The second verse. The parish chest. Tam's key.*

She checked her watch. 9:52. Six hours and twenty-five minutes until moonset. Less until the village woke, if it slept at all tonight, which she doubted; she could see candlelight moving behind the curtains across the lane, figures crossing rooms, doors being counted.

Tam slept at the far end of the village, past the church, in the low stone house with the yew tree. An old man with a key on a cord around his neck, sleeping — if he slept — above a chest that held the only complete version of a song that had been cut apart to keep a hungry thing hungry.

Vesper picked up her field bag. She put the tape recorder inside, and the almanac, and the notebook with its torn pages, and she paused at the front door with her hand on the latch, listening to the wind move through Hollowmere like slow breathing, like something waiting patiently to be fed.

Then she opened the door, and stepped out into the moonlight, and went to steal a song.
