## 20. Sung Twice

The moon came up the color of a peeled egg, and Vesper walked toward it with the tape recorder held against her chest like something she'd stolen.

She had left the archive at half past nine, the true second verse copied out in her own hand on a page torn from the parish ledger — a theft, technically, though she suspected Tam would have let her take it, would have pressed it into her hands with both of his old ones and wept, if she had asked. She hadn't asked. Asking meant conversation, and conversation meant the elders finding one more hour to offer her silence in, one more bargain to bury her inside. She was done being buried. Twenty years ago they had put her under without a shovel; tonight she intended to dig herself out loud.

The streets of Hollowmere were empty in the way that only a village expecting to die can be empty. Every door she passed wore its preparations: nails driven fresh into frames, salt lines like grey scars across thresholds, chairs wedged under handles from the inside. Behind the windows, candlelight trembled. She could feel the eyes on her — dozens of them, pressed to gaps in curtains, watching the Okafor girl walk down the middle of the road with a machine that hummed and a piece of paper in her pocket and no fear on her face at all.

Let them look, she thought. Let them see what their grandmothers' songs were for.

Marta caught her at the corner by the smithy, coming out of the dark so suddenly that Vesper's hand flew to her throat. The older woman's face was wet, her shawl clutched at her collarbone like a wound.

"You found it," Marta said. Not a question. Her eyes went to Vesper's pocket, where the folded page made a small rectangular shadow against the cloth. "The second verse. Oh, girl. Oh, you foolish, brave girl."

"Go inside, Marta."

"He'll be waiting for you."

"I know." Vesper adjusted the strap of the recorder on her shoulder. "That's rather the point."

Marta grabbed her wrist then, her grip shockingly strong, her fingers cold as well-water. "Listen to me. Whatever he shows you at the end — whatever face he wears — you sing it anyway. Do you hear me? He will show you himself as a child. He will show you hungry. He will show you *sorry*. And it will all be true, and none of it will matter, because the name doesn't care what's true. It only cares who says it."

"Marta—"

"I fed him," Marta whispered, and her voice broke clean through the middle. "My mother sang, and her mother before her, and every harvest moon I stood at my window and I sang the first verse and I cut the second off my tongue like a rotten tooth, and I told myself it was mercy, keeping him contained instead of — instead of what the well used to do. But it wasn't mercy. It was just a smaller cage." Her tears fell onto Vesper's wrist, warm as blood. "You're the only one who can open it. And you're the only one who shouldn't have to."

Vesper covered the woman's hand with her own. "Then we're even," she said quietly. "You kept the cage. I built it."

She walked on. She did not look back, but she heard Marta's footsteps retreating fast, and once, faintly, the sound of a door being barred behind her — counted twice, locked twice, the way Hollowmere had always done things.

* * *

Wray's Field opened before her like a held breath.

The corn had been cut weeks ago, and the stubble caught the moonlight in ten thousand small silver blades. At the field's center, black against the luminous sky, stood the well — its cap of stones still thrown down, its rim still warm, she knew, though she could not yet say how she knew. And sitting on the low stone wall at the field's edge, exactly where a boy would sit if a boy had been waiting twenty years for someone to come home, was Emrys.

He didn't stand when she approached. That was the thing about him, the thing that had undone her from the first night on her porch: he never performed. No rising, no spread arms, no theatrical menace. He simply sat, crooked smile already in place, hands loose between his knees, as though she had only been gone a moment — as though the intervening decades were a door she'd stepped through and stepped back out of, and he had kept her seat.

"You brought the paper," he said.

"You can see it?"

"I can see everything you carry." His head tilted, that familiar birdlike tilt she had invented for him at seven years old because she'd read it in a book about robins. "I always could. That was the arrangement, Vess. You got someone who saw you. I got someone worth seeing."

She stopped three paces from him. Close enough to speak softly. Far enough, some animal part of her insisted, to run — though she had learned in these last days that running from Emrys was like running from your own reflection; it moved when you moved, and it was already wherever you arrived.

"The song," she said. "The whole song. Both verses. You know what happens when I finish it."

"Yes."

"And you know what happens to *you*."

His smile flickered — not away, but deeper, into something older and sadder, a groove worn in the stone of his face. "Yes," he said again. "Either I answer to what I was before you named me, or I come apart entirely. A name spoken truly is a door, Vess. It opens both ways. Whatever walks out of me might not fit back in."

"Are you afraid?"

Emrys considered this with the grave seriousness he had always given her questions, even the silly ones, even *do robins dream*, even *if I died would you die too* — God, she had been a morbid child, and he had answered every morbid question like a priest.

"I don't know what afraid is," he said finally. "I know what waiting is. I've done waiting for a very long time, in a great many shapes. Before you, I waited in the well. Before the well, I waited in the ground under it. I was never anything, Vess. That's the truth your pretty archive won't hold. I was hunger with nowhere to go, and your village fed me children to keep me quiet, and then a lonely girl climbed up on the rim and said *stay with me forever*, and for the first time in the history of that hunger, there was somewhere to go." He looked up at her, and his eyes in the moonlight were the exact brown she had chosen for him, age nine, from a box of crayons. "You didn't make a monster. You made a shape. And the shape learned to love the girl who gave it. That part was real. Everything else is arithmetic."

"Then let me do the sum," Vesper said.

She set the recorder on the stone wall beside him. Her hands were steady — she noted this with distant surprise, the folklorist in her observing the subject even now, cataloguing her own pulse rate, her own dry mouth, her own strange calm. Fear had not left her. Fear had simply been spent, paid out over three nights of doors and hissing tapes and a voice in the dark reciting her childhood back to her, until what remained was not courage exactly but a kind of cleared ground. Nothing left standing between her and the act.

She unfolded the page.

The true second verse was written in a clerk's faded iron-gall ink, and beneath it, in the margin, some long-dead vicar had scrawled a single line in Latin that the archive had mistranslated for two hundred years. *Nomen verum solvit quod falsum ligavit.* The true name unbinds what the false one bound. Emrys had never been his name. It was the name she had given the hole in her life, and holes do not answer to names — but the hunger wearing the shape of him did, and had, all along, worn it obediently, patiently, lovingly, waiting for someone brave enough or desperate enough to call it by what it actually was.

And she knew. She had known since the well, since she'd seen the carved names around the rim and felt the stone warm under her palm like skin. The original name was carved there, third from the oldest, worn nearly smooth by centuries of frightened thumbs. She had read it aloud once already, alone, testing it, and the field had gone silent in a way fields do not go silent — no insects, no wind, the very stubble seeming to lean away.

"Do it here?" she asked him. "Or on the porch?"

Emrys stood. Up close he smelled, faintly, impossibly, of chalk and cut grass and the inside of her childhood bedroom — the smell of an invention, the smell of a friend.

"The porch," he said. "You promised me forever on a porch step. It seems right that forever ends where it started."

* * *

They walked back through Hollowmere together, and the village watched them come, and not one door opened and not one voice cried out. Vesper understood why. This was the night the villagers had rehearsed for generations without admitting it: the night the debt came walking up the main road in plain sight. They watched the Okafor girl and the boy who did not age pass candlelit window after candlelit window, and they understood that whatever happened next would happen because of her, and to her, and that their own salvation or ruin hung on whether she sang.

At her gate, Emrys stopped.

"This is where I leave you to it," he said. "A threshold matters. Even now. Especially now." He smiled the crooked smile, and for a moment — one unbearable moment — he was nine years old again, every inch of him, the friend she'd built out of loneliness and robin-books and crayon-brown eyes, the friend who had sat with her through every dinner party where she was invisible, every school year where no one learned her name. "Thank you for seeing me, Vess. Nobody else ever managed it. Not really. They saw the hunger. You saw the boy."

"You were the boy," she said. "For twenty years, you were the boy."

"And now I get to find out if I was ever anything else." He stepped back into the shadow of the hedge, and his voice followed her up the path, soft as a hand on her hair. "Sing it loud, Vesper. Sing it like you mean to be heard. Because you do. You always did."

Her porch was exactly as it had been on the first night — the same boards, the same sagging rail, the same view down the lane to Wray's Field where the moon was climbing now, huge and white and patient as an unpaid bill. She set the recorder on the step. She took out the page. And she thought, absurdly, of her mother, who had died believing her daughter had invented nothing worse than an imaginary friend; of her father, who had signed the village's papers and carried the secret like a stone in his chest for thirty years; of the children whose names were in the parish ledger under *harvest moon*, whose songs had been sung once each, carefully, like doses of medicine, to keep the thing in the well drowsy and shaped and fed.

No more doses. No more cage. Tonight the account settled, one way or the other, in full.

Vesper Okafor stood on the porch where her childhood had ended, faced the harvest moon, and began to sing.

Her voice cracked on the first line. Of course it did. She was not a singer; she was a collector of songs, a notator of other people's voices, and her own voice sounded strange and thin in her ears, a stranger's voice, a child's voice. But the melody was in her bones — it had been in her bones since Marta hummed it to her across a kitchen table, since the old woman in chapter one of her notebook had sobbed and begged her to erase the tape — and the melody carried her where her nerve failed. *The reaper walks at harvest-tide, the reaper knows his due.* The first verse rose over the rooftops of Hollowmere, and behind her, house by house, the candles guttered — not blown out, but bowing, leaning toward her like a congregation.

She sang the verse the way the village had sung it for two hundred years: once, completely, naming what had been invited in. And then, before the silence could close over it, before the old reflex could bite the song off at the root —

She sang it again.

The effect was immediate and physical. The air over Wray's Field seemed to fold. Down the lane, doors shuddered in their frames — the counted doors, the nailed doors, opening or straining toward opening, she couldn't tell and didn't stop to learn. Somewhere a dog began to howl and was abruptly, totally silent. The moonlight thickened until it lay on the stubble like snow, like wool, like something breathing.

And Vesper sang the second verse.

It was shorter than the first. Crueler. It had no celebration in it at all — it was a ledger-line, an accounting, a summons dressed in four lines of ballad meter, and as she sang it she understood at last why every generation of Hollowmere women had cut it from their tongues. It did not bind. It *called*. It reached down through the shape, through the boy, through twenty years of patience and love and crooked smiles, and it gripped the hunger underneath by whatever a hunger has instead of a throat.

The last note left her mouth.

The silence afterward was the largest thing Vesper had ever stood inside.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere — from the well, from the porch boards beneath her feet, from the hollow of her own chest — came a sound like a great bell struck underwater, and the moonlight itself seemed to turn and *look* at her.

"*Speak,*" said the night, in a voice that was not Emrys's, that had never been anyone's, that was the sound a hunger makes when it is finally addressed after two hundred years of being merely fed.

Vesper's knees nearly went. She gripped the rail. She thought of Marta's warning — *he will show you sorry, and it will all be true, and none of it will matter* — and she planted her feet on the boards where a small girl had once sat with an invisible friend and promised him forever.

"You were never Emrys," she said, and her voice held, barely, wonderfully. "Emrys was mine. I made him. I keep him." She drew breath, and spoke the carved name — the old one, the true one, the name worn smooth by centuries of terrified thumbs around the rim of a warm stone well — into the moonlight, complete, unhurried, aloud.

The name left her mouth and the world flinched.

Down in Wray's Field, the darkness above the well began to gather, to churn, to *answer* — a vast unshaped attention rising toward the surface of things, the hunger beneath the boy, summoned at last by its own name, forced to choose between answering and dissolving. On the porch step, beside her, the air grew cold and then colder, and she felt Emrys arrive beside her without hearing him come — felt him stand at her shoulder, facing his own origin, his hand finding hers in the dark, small and cold and gripping tight.

"Whatever happens," he whispered, in the voice she had given him, the only voice she would ever call his, "don't let go until it's finished."

Above the well, the gathered dark drew itself up to its full and terrible height — and began, slowly, to speak her name back.
