## 7. What Was Taken

The reel cases smelled like a library that had drowned.

Beatrix had them spread across her kitchen table by midnight, forty-one of them, salvaged from the flooded archive in milk crates lined with dish towels. She'd spent the drive home arguing with herself about the wisdom of bringing them into her apartment—the same apartment where, two nights ago, every radio had whispered her name in unison—but the alternative was leaving them at the station, alone, in a building that hummed through its foundations like a struck tuning fork. Her kitchen had a kettle and a cat and a deadbolt. It felt like the lesser risk.

The cat, a gray, judgmental creature named Biscuit, had taken one look at the crates and relocated to the top of the refrigerator, where he now watched her with the expression of a landlord observing a tenant make poor decisions.

"Your concern is noted," Beatrix told him.

The reels themselves were mostly ruined. Water had gotten into the archive decades ago and dried in slow brown tides, leaving the tape in some cases fused into solid bricks that clicked when she shook them. She'd catalogued them anyway, out of the same compulsion that had her cross-referencing obituaries at three in the morning: *E. VANE — 03/12/74 — REQUEST LINE. E. VANE — 04/02/74 — OPEN LINES. E. VANE — 06/19/74 —* and then nothing, the labels trailing off into blankness like a sentence that had been interrupted.

It was on the fourth case that she noticed the labels were wrong.

Not wrong, exactly. *Layered.* Under the pen that read E. VANE in a slanted, confident hand, there was another hand—smaller, cramped, pressed so hard into the cardboard it had nearly gone through. She'd taken it for watermark, for shadow, for the ordinary damage of fifty years in a wet basement. But holding the case under her desk lamp and tilting it, she saw the indentations clearly, a palimpsest of pressure, writing hidden beneath writing.

She fetched a pencil from the junk drawer—the soft 6B she used for marking scripts—and held it sideways, shading lightly across the cardboard the way she'd done as a kid, revealing the secret messages her cousin hid inside birthday cards.

Words rose out of the gray like bodies surfacing.

*If you are reading this, you have my chair.*

Beatrix sat down very slowly.

* * *

The writing was on the inside faces of the cases, she discovered, and on the spines beneath the paper labels, and on the bottoms, and once—mirrored, as though written by someone practicing—on the clear plastic window itself. Emrys Vane had used the archive as his diary, hiding his words under his own professional hand the way he must have hidden everything else: in plain sight, in the margins, in the spaces between the songs.

She worked until four in the morning, pencil in one hand, a legal pad in the other, transcribing in the order of the dates. Biscuit came down off the refrigerator around two, sniffed a reel case, and left the room entirely. Beatrix chose not to take this personally, or rather, she chose to take it exactly as personally as it deserved.

The earliest entries were almost funny. That was the thing that kept undoing her—the *warmth* in the handwriting, the little jokes. She had spent a week terrified of Emrys Vane, and here he was, fifty years gone, being charming at her.

*March 1974. The new transmitter is a beaut. Clean signal all the way to the county line. Ruth's predecessor—what was his name, the one with the laugh—says I'm the best thing to happen to this frequency since electricity. High praise from a man who thinks the weather is a government broadcast.*

*April. Someone called tonight asking me to play a song for his wife, except his wife has been dead two years and he swears she requested it. I played it. What else do you do?*

*May. The calls are getting stranger. Not the cranks—cranks I can handle, cranks are the salt of this business. I mean the others. The ones who sound like they're calling up from the bottom of a well. They ask me to say their names. Just that. Say my name, they say, please, say it, I'm starting to lose it. I said them. It felt like the right thing to do. It felt like prayer.*

Then, in June, the handwriting changed. It got smaller. Tighter. As though the man writing it was trying to take up less room in the world.

*I went down there.*

*I know how that sounds. I know what you're thinking, future person, whoever you are, because I would be thinking it too. But you have to understand: the humming started in February, and by May I could feel it through the floor of the studio, in my teeth, in the fillings the dentist gave me in '68. And the callers—the well-voiced ones—they were getting clearer. Closer. Like the well was filling up. Like they were rising.*

*I followed the hum to the east wall of the basement, past the boiler, to a place where the concrete goes soft. I don't have better words. Soft. Like skin over something. And there was a door.*

*Black oak. No hinges on my side. No handle. And warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm.*

*I knocked. God help me, I knocked, and something knocked back, and it knew the rhythm of the station ID. Our rhythm. Three slow, two fast. It had been listening to us for years.*

Beatrix stopped transcribing. She got up, put the kettle on, and stood watching it as though it might boil differently if supervised. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. She'd expected shaking. Instead there was this terrible clarity, this sense of reading a letter addressed to her across half a century, and the letter beginning, *if you are reading this, you have my chair.*

The kettle screamed. She made tea she didn't drink and went back to the table.

* * *

*July 1974. I have made an arrangement. I want it on the record—I want it SOMEWHERE, even if the somewhere is the inside of a tape case—that I understood what I was doing. I was not tricked. I was not possessed. I was a man with a microphone and a mortgage and a voice people loved, and I made a trade like men have made trades since the beginning, and I told myself the usual lies, that it was small, that it was victimless, that I could stop.*

*Here is the trade. The thing below eats memory. Not flesh—flesh means nothing to it, it doesn't even have a shape to put flesh in. It eats the remembering. And the dead, the newly dead, they're richest. All that fresh grief attached to them, all those people upstairs saying their names. It can't reach up. It has no voice of its own. It can only listen.*

*But I have a voice. I have a transmitter.*

*It wants recordings. The voices of the recently dead—answering machines, home movies, the little cassette tributes families make. It wants me to play them on air, late, low, buried under the music where no one notices. It drinks them through the signal. In exchange, my career continues. My health continues. The station thrives.*

*The first one was a man named Albert Coyle, died of his heart in the parking lot of the VFW. His daughter brought a tape of him singing at her wedding to the station, wanted me to play it on her anniversary. I played it. It was a lovely thing to do. It was the worst thing I have ever done. Halfway through the song I felt the building lean, the way a dog leans into a hand, and I understood that something had just eaten, and that I had fed it, and that it was grateful.*

*Grateful things keep you. That's the trap nobody warns you about. Not that it will hurt you. That it will be kind.*

Beatrix read that line three times. *Grateful things keep you.* She thought of the ratings, of the 3 a.m. spike, of Ruth's spreadsheet with its impossible numbers. She thought of her own strange, charmed tenure at the station—the contract renewed without negotiation, the salary bumps she'd never asked for, the way the building's ancient elevator had stopped breaking down the month she arrived.

She had thought she was good at her job. It was possible she was *kept.*

She wrote that down on the legal pad, then crossed it out, then wrote it again, because crossing it out felt like lying.

* * *

*August. It's not enough anymore. I could see this coming the way you see weather coming, and I let it come anyway, because the alternative was the hum coming back into my teeth.*

*It wants more. It wants the names spoken aloud now, not just the voices. It wants me to remember them on air—say their names, tell one true thing about them—and I understand finally what the callers were asking me for, all those months ago, before any of this. They weren't haunting me. They were warning me. Say my name, I'm starting to lose it. They knew. The dead below know exactly what happens down there, and they were begging a stranger on the radio for the one thing that could save them, and I thought they were cranks.*

*I've started keeping a ledger. Names, dates, sources. If I'm going to be a thief I will at least be an honest one. Two hundred and six names so far. No. That's a lie I tell myself. That's the number I can bear. The true number is higher. The true number has no bottom, because the debt compounds—every voice it eats makes it stronger, and every time it's stronger it can pull harder on the ones still fresh, still hovering, still not finished leaving. It doesn't kill anyone. I want that understood too. It has never once killed anyone. It only waits at the door of the dying like a creditor, and takes what they carry, and then takes them.*

*I am the fence. I am the middleman. I am the man at the pawnshop window, and every night I open the till.*

*September. My hands shake now unless I'm on air. On air they're perfectly steady. It keeps the instrument in tune. I've begun to hate the microphone the way you'd hate a hand that isn't yours.*

*October. I tried to stop. I want that on the record too. I went three nights without playing anything, and on the third night the callers came, and they weren't the gentle ones. A woman read me my mother's maiden name in a voice like gravel in a bucket, and told me the thing below had it, had her, had everything I've ever fed it, and that it would give it all back the day I gave back the voices. All of them. Every recording, played back into the earth, at the door, in full.*

*It can be undone. That's the one mercy. What is taken can be returned. But it must be returned freely, all at once, and the one who returns it stands at the door, and the door opens both ways, and the thing below is very hungry and very patient and it will offer you anything, anything at all, to stop halfway.*

*I'm not strong enough. I want that on the record. Whoever you are, sitting in my chair with my tapes: I was not strong enough, and I'm sorry, and I'm going to try one more thing, and if it doesn't work, the ledger is in the cases, all of them, every case, and the last reel is labeled FINAL BROADCAST, and it is not a figure of speech.*

*It knows I'm writing this. I can feel it listening the way you feel sun on your neck. It thinks the diary is charming. It thinks I'm charming. Grateful things keep you.*

*November 14th, 1974. Tonight I play them all back. Every voice. Two hundred and six and the true number, all of them, into the earth, at the door. If you're reading this, it worked, and the cellar is quiet, and you can burn these cases.*

*If you're reading this and the cellar is not quiet, then it worked halfway, and I am down there, and so are they, and the debt has passed to you the way debts pass, quietly, in the night, with a signature you don't remember signing.*

*Say my name. Please. I'm starting to lose it.*

*—E.V.*

* * *

The kitchen window had gone from black to the deep blue-gray that isn't quite dawn. Beatrix sat surrounded by forty-one reel cases and eleven pages of legal pad, and she did the math she had been avoiding since the first shaded-in word.

Two hundred and six names, and the true number. Five decades of compounding. The dead calling her show, asking to be remembered, and her remembering them—Dolores, the drowned boy, the man who wanted his wife to know where he'd left the good scissors—and every name she spoke aloud had been a meal, or a mercy, or both, and there was no way to know which from up here. Maybe there was no difference. Maybe remembering the dead *was* feeding them, the only food they could be given, and the horror wasn't that the thing below ate memory but that Beatrix had been doing the same thing, lovingly, professionally, for years, and calling it a radio show.

She thought about the door, sealed from the inside. She had assumed it was locked to keep something in.

Now she understood it was locked to keep something *from getting out*—or rather, to keep someone from doing exactly what Emrys had tried to do. The thing below had learned from November 1974. It had eaten the attempt. It had eaten Emrys mid-broadcast, eleven minutes of breathing on tape, and then it had sealed its own door and waited fifty years for the debt to ripen and pass to the next voice on the frequency.

The ledger was in the cases, Emrys had written. All of them. Every case.

Beatrix looked at the forty-one boxes spread across her table, at the pencil shavings and the dish towels and the tea gone cold as a stone, and she understood that she was not holding a diary. She was holding an inventory. A manifest of stolen goods, itemized in a dead man's hand, with her name now effectively on it.

Someone had to give it back. That was the whole of it, the terrible simplicity at the bottom of the well. The voices could be returned—freely, all at once, at the door, by someone willing to stand in a doorway that opened both ways—and if no one did, the debt would simply keep compounding, quietly, in the night, until there was no one left on the frequency to pass it to.

She gathered the pages, squared their edges, and weighted them with the sugar bowl. She would need help. She would need the ledger complete, every name, every case, and she would need to understand the rules better than Emrys had, because Emrys had known the rules and died anyway.

The phone rang.

She stared at it. It was 5:12 in the morning. Her show wasn't for hours. The phone rang again, patient, unhurried, and through the receiver's cheap speaker, before she even lifted it, she could hear the line was already open—no dial tone, no ringback, just a soft shuffling quiet like someone settling into a chair.

Beatrix picked it up.

"Dolores?" she said.

A long pause. And then a voice she remembered from the obituary page, clearer than it had ever been on air, said: "You found his writing. Good. Now listen, honey, because there are rules, and Emrys only ever learned them the hard way."
