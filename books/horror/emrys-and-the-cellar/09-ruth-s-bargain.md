## 9. Ruth's Bargain

Ruth Okonkwo had a system for bad days, and the system was coffee.

Not good coffee—she'd given up on that fantasy sometime around 2009, when the station's budget meeting had devolved into an argument about whether they could afford both toilet paper and electricity, and she'd learned to drink whatever the break room machine produced as long as it was hot and brown and technically liquid. Bad days called for two cups before nine. Terrible days called for three, plus the emergency biscuit tin she kept in her bottom drawer, behind the files marked TAXES 2011–2014, which no one had ever opened because no one wanted to look at them, including her.

Today was a three-biscuit day.

She sat in her office at 7:40 in the morning, watching the sun come up over the parking lot through blinds that needed replacing, and thought about Beatrix Yarrow's face when Ruth had told her, six days ago, that the ratings anomalies were probably just a glitch in the new metering software.

*Probably just a glitch.* Ruth almost laughed. Almost. It came out as something more like a cough, which was fine, because there was nobody in the building yet except Marcus from engineering, and Marcus wore headphones even in the bathroom, so effectively it was just her and the coffee and the biscuit tin and fifty-one years of something she'd spent eleven years pretending not to know about.

The ledger was in the safe.

She hadn't opened the safe since March. She knew this because the safe was where she kept the station's insurance documents and her spare reading glasses and a photograph of her brother that she allowed herself to look at exactly twice a year—his birthday and the anniversary—and March contained neither. The combination was written on a Post-it inside her desk calendar under "Dentist, Tues," because Ruth believed in security through tedium. Nobody had ever robbed this station. There was nothing worth stealing except the transmitter, and anyone who tried to steal a transmitter would find out quickly that it weighed four hundred pounds and hummed in a key that made your fillings ache.

She dialed the combination anyway. Her hands were steady. That surprised her a little. She'd expected them to shake.

Inside, on top of the insurance binder, wrapped in a dish towel patterned with lemons, was the ledger.

It wasn't hers. That was the thing people didn't understand, if anyone had ever understood anything about this, which they didn't, because Ruth had never told anyone, not once in eleven years, not her ex-husband Gerald who'd left anyway, not her accountant, not the priest at St. Brendan's whom she'd stopped attending specifically so she wouldn't be tempted to confess. The ledger belonged to the station. Or to the thing under the station. The distinction had stopped mattering to her around year three.

Emrys Vane's handwriting covered most of it—a spidery, elegant script that slanted left like it was trying to escape the page. But the last third of the book was different. Newer entries in other hands. Five other hands, if you counted carefully, and Ruth had counted carefully, many times, usually at three in the morning, usually after a glass of the cheap sherry she bought exclusively for this purpose because drinking good sherry while reading a book of damned names felt like blasphemy against both the sherry and the names.

Her own hand was in there too.

She turned past Emrys's final entries—the ones where the letters got smaller and smaller, like a man writing himself down to nothing—and found the page she was looking for without needing to look for it. She could have recited it. Sometimes, in the shower, she did.

**Received: one name, freely given, by signatory R. OKONKWO. In exchange: continuance.**

Below that, in ink that had gone brownish over the years, her own handwriting, younger and less certain than the hand she wrote with now:

**Thomas Okonkwo. My brother. He is already gone. I am only lending what cannot be kept.**

That last part had been a lie, and she'd known it was a lie when she wrote it, and the thing below had known it was a lie, and it had accepted the name anyway, because the thing below did not care about the quality of your rationalizations. It cared about the name. Names were currency. Everything else was packaging.

Ruth closed the ledger and ate a biscuit.

* * *

Here was how it had happened, in the version she told herself, which was the only version available since she'd never told anyone else:

Eleven years ago, the station had been dying. Not haunted-dying—boring-dying, the ordinary kind, the kind that killed half the independent stations in America during those years. Advertising revenue down forty percent. The building's roof leaking into Studio B. Two hosts quit in one season, citing "creative differences," which in both cases meant the paychecks bounced. Ruth had taken the manager job at thirty-four because she loved radio, genuinely loved it, loved the intimacy of it, the way a voice in the dark could feel like a hand on your shoulder, and within eighteen months she was lying awake doing arithmetic that always ended in the same number, which was zero.

Then came the night of the fire sale. A developer wanted the lot. The frequency would have been sold off, absorbed, deleted. Ruth had sat in this same office until two in the morning with a bottle of wine and the station's books spread out like a patient on a table she had no skill to operate on, and she had cried, actually cried, big ugly sobs, because the station mattered. Because people called in at three in the morning and said things to Beatrix's predecessor—to poor Marcus Delgado, who'd lasted eight months before fleeing to Phoenix—that they'd never said to their wives or their mothers or their God. Because the Hollow Signal was a lighthouse, and lighthouses don't get to close for renovations.

And then the phone on her desk had rung.

At two in the morning. On a line that shouldn't have been connected anymore—the request line went straight to the studio, and the studio was empty, had been empty for hours.

She'd answered it. Of course she'd answered it. She was a radio person; answering ringing phones was not a choice, it was a reflex, like breathing or flinching.

The voice on the other end had been dry and papery and patient, the way old paper is patient, and it had said: *You want the station to live.*

Not a question. Ruth had said yes anyway. She remembered saying it clearly, remembered the taste of the word, because it was the last honest thing she said for a very long time afterward.

*There is a book,* the voice said. *In the safe. You will find instructions. One name buys continuance. Choose well.*

"I don't understand," Ruth had said. "Choose—who? What does it—what happens to—"

But the line had already gone quiet in that particular way, the dead-air way, the way a line goes quiet when the thing on the other end has stopped pretending to be on a phone at all.

She'd found the ledger. She'd read Emrys Vane's instructions, written across two pages in that escaping script, and she'd understood maybe sixty percent of it, and the forty percent she didn't understand had somehow been worse than the sixty percent she did.

One name. Freely given. A voice the thing below could keep, could feed on, could wear thin as a coin passed through too many hands. In exchange: the station lived. Money arrived—she never learned from where; checks from a holding company whose registered address turned out to be a PO box in a town that had been underwater since the fifties. Advertisers returned. The roof got fixed. The frequency survived.

All it cost was a name.

Ruth had spent four days deciding. Four days of telling herself she wouldn't do it, couldn't do it, that there were lines and this was one. And all four days, some cold practical clerk in the back of her skull had been drawing up the list.

Not strangers. That was the first rule she set herself, and it was the rule that damned her, though she didn't see it then. She refused to feed it a stranger. Some anonymous insomniac, some crank caller, some name pulled from the obituaries of a neighboring county—no. If she was going to do this monstrous thing, she would do it with someone she loved, so that the cost would be real, so that she would carry it honestly.

Which meant, when the list was done, when every name had been weighed, there was really only one candidate left standing.

Tommy.

Her little brother, who had died fourteen months earlier at forty-one, heart failure in his apartment in Sacramento, alone, three days before anyone found him. Who had played bass in a bar band all through the nineties and never made it and never minded, or claimed never to mind, in that way that meant he minded enormously. Who used to call her every Sunday, and whose calls had simply stopped, and the silence afterward had been the largest thing in Ruth's life, larger than grief, larger than the station, larger than the ocean.

He was already gone. That was what she'd told herself. That was the sentence she'd built her whole cathedral of justification on: *he is already gone, I am only lending what cannot be kept.* The thing below took voices of the recently dead—Emrys's notes were clear on that, the fresher the better, voices still warm, still remembering themselves. Tommy was fourteen months gone. His voice was in voicemails she'd saved and home videos and one cassette of his band's demo tape that she still kept in her nightstand. But Tommy himself was beyond reach. Whatever the thing below got, it wouldn't be him. It would be an echo of an echo. A shell transaction. Victimless.

She'd known better. God help her, she'd known better even as she wrote the name, sitting right here in this chair, at three in the morning, with the sherry untouched because her hand wasn't steady enough to pour.

Because here was what Emrys's notes said, in the part she'd understood all too well: the thing below didn't eat flesh and didn't eat souls, exactly. It ate memory. It ate the self. And the dead it took weren't finished dying—they were *in passage*, in that gray country between, where a soul is still mostly memory, still mostly the story it tells about itself. Take a voice from that country, and you take the story. You take the teller's ability to keep being told.

Tommy hadn't been beyond reach. Tommy had been *midway*. And she had sold him out of it.

* * *

"Ruth? You in here?"

Beatrix's voice came through the office door, and Ruth's spine went rigid so fast she heard it click.

She shoved the ledger into the drawer—not the safe, no time for the safe, the lemon towel and everything—and put her elbow on the desk in what she hoped was a casual posture and not the posture of a woman hiding a book of damned names. The biscuit tin she kicked shut with her heel.

"In here," she called. "Door's open."

Beatrix came in carrying two coffees, which was suspicious on its own, because Beatrix Yarrow did not bring people coffee. Beatrix Yarrow brought people questions shaped like coffee. She was wearing her usual uniform—cardigan the color of oatmeal, hair doing its own thing—and she had the particular careful blankness on her face that Ruth had learned, over eleven years of managing broadcasters, meant *I know something and I'm deciding how much to show you.*

"Morning," Beatrix said, setting a cup down. "You're here early."

"I'm always here early."

"You're not. You're chronically fifteen minutes late and you blame the traffic on Route 9, which I've noticed is also your excuse on Sundays, when there is no traffic on Route 9." Beatrix sat down across from her, uninvited, and wrapped both hands around her cup. "I found something in the archive."

"Did you." Ruth's voice came out level. Eleven years of management had trained her voice to come out level in any weather. It was possibly the only thing she'd gotten from this arrangement that she could call a gift.

"Reels. From 1974. Emrys Vane." Beatrix watched her over the rim of the cup. "Does that name mean anything to you?"

"Nope." Too fast. Ruth heard it land too fast, the way a dropped glass lands too loud. She took a sip of coffee to cover it and burned her tongue, which she deserved.

"His time slot was mine," Beatrix said. "Exactly mine. Three to five, weeknights. He vanished mid-broadcast. Left eleven minutes of tape that's just breathing." She paused. "And there's a ledger, Ruth. Hidden in the reel cases. With names in it. A lot of names. And signatures."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere down the hall, Marcus's headphones leaked a faint tinny drumbeat, absurdly cheerful.

"A ledger," Ruth repeated.

"Entries going back decades. Different handwriting. Different—" Beatrix stopped. Ruth watched her decide something, watched her jaw set the way it did when she was about to say the true thing instead of the kind thing, which was Beatrix's defining flaw and, Ruth suspected, the reason the thing below had chosen her. "Different *signatories*. People who gave it names. People who are probably still alive. Probably still working here."

And there it was. Ruth set down her coffee. Her hands, damn them, were finally shaking now, when it was too late for shaking to do any good.

"Beatrix," she said, "whatever you think you've found—"

"It's my brother."

The words came out of Ruth before she could stop them, before she could deploy a single one of the defenses she'd maintained for eleven years, and once they were out she discovered she didn't want them back. That was the terrible secret about confession: it felt like falling, and falling, after you've been gripping a ledge long enough, feels almost like relief.

Beatrix blinked. "Your—"

"Page ninety-one." Ruth pulled open the drawer. Her hand hovered over the lemon-towel bundle, and for one more second—one last cowardly second—she considered lying, deflecting, sending Beatrix away to dig alone. Then she thought of Tommy, wherever he was or wasn't, whatever was left of him, wearing thinner every year like a sweater washed too many times, and she picked up the ledger and held it out.

"Page ninety-one," she said again. "Read it. Read all of it. You're going to anyway, and I'm tired, Beatrix. I have been so tired for eleven years."

Beatrix took the book the way you take something hot. She turned to the page. Ruth watched her eyes move down it, watched her face do several things in sequence—confusion, recognition, horror—and waited for the moment. It came when Beatrix reached the entry in Ruth's own handwriting, the brown-inked lie, *lending what cannot be kept.*

"Thomas Okonkwo," Beatrix read aloud. Then, quietly: "Your brother. The one in the photo in your—"

"The one who's dead, yes." Ruth stood up and walked to the window, because she could not say the next part facing another human being. The parking lot glittered in the morning sun, ordinary as bread. "Fourteen months dead when I gave his name. I told myself he was beyond its reach. I told myself it was a formality, a signature on something already signed." She laughed, and it came out awful. "You know what the worst part is? It worked. The money came. The station lived. We fixed the roof. And every year on his birthday I call his old number in Sacramento just to hear 'the number you have dialed,' because that's the only place his voice exists anymore that isn't *down there*."

Silence behind her. When Beatrix spoke, her voice was gentle in a way that was somehow worse than anger. "Ruth. What did you think the names were *for*?"

"I thought—I don't know what I thought. Continuance. That's the word it uses. I thought they were fuel. Payment." Ruth turned around. "What are they for, Beatrix? You've read further than I ever let myself."

Beatrix looked down at the ledger, and Ruth saw her swallow.

"They're prisoners," Beatrix said. "Or—or guests. Dolores called me again last night. She's lucid now, she can explain it. The thing eats memory, Ruth. It eats *them*. Slowly. Unless someone above remembers them aloud—says their names, tells their stories—their selves just... wear away. Like yours has been wearing away." She looked up. "Every name in this book is someone down there, forgetting themselves, waiting for somebody up here to say their name. And you've had eleven years to say his."

Ruth sat back down. The chair received her like it always did, like nothing had happened, like the world was furniture.

"I know," she said. "I know exactly what I've done. Do you want to know why I never said it? Why I never once, in eleven years, said his name out loud in this building?" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Because I was afraid that if I reminded it that he existed—if I drew attention to him—it would notice him. Feed on him harder. I thought forgetting him *up here* might protect him *down there*. That's what I told myself. That keeping him secret was keeping him safe." Her hands came down. "And maybe that's even true. Or maybe I just couldn't stand to grieve him properly, because grieving him properly would mean admitting what I'd done, and I could live with the crime as long as I never had to look at it. Everyone pays differently, Beatrix. That's the whole rotten economy of this place. Emrys paid with his life. Whoever signed before me paid however they paid. I pay with Sundays. Every Sunday I pick up the phone to call my brother and remember that I'm the reason I can't."

Beatrix was quiet for a long moment. Then she turned the ledger around, back toward the beginning, toward Emrys's spidery hand, and began, slowly, to count.

"How many," she murmured, mostly to herself. Her finger moved down columns of names. "How many are still... down there. Still remembering. Still waitable." Her finger stopped. Ruth watched the color change in her face, watched her go from pale to something past pale, and thought, distantly, *ah. There it is. There's the next one.*

"Beatrix?"

Beatrix didn't answer right away. She was staring at a single line near the end of Emrys's final entries, and her lips had gone white, and when she finally looked up at Ruth, her eyes were the eyes of someone reading their own name on a list they'd hoped was strangers.

"There's an entry here," she said. "The last one. Dated fifty years ago, but it's—it's addressed forward. It's addressed to whoever comes next." She swallowed. "It says: *To the one who inherits the hours. It knows your name already. It chose you before you were born. Check the door.*"

Outside, far below the floor, beneath the foundation, beneath the raw earth where no cellar should be, Ruth felt—actually felt, through the soles of her shoes, through the floor, through eleven years of willful deafness—the building begin, very softly, to hum.
