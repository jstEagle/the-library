## 18. Return

The door did not look like a door anymore. It looked like a mouth that had been holding its breath for fifty years.

Beatrix stood before it in the raw-earth corridor the excavation crew had opened and then abandoned, the blackened oak sweating in the dark, and she thought—of all the useless things a mind produces under pressure—of her grandmother's pressure cooker, how the valve would rattle and hiss and the whole kitchen would hold still until it finished. That was what the cellar sounded like now. A held breath with a valve in it. The volunteers' voices came down through the building above her like water through a ceiling crack, thin but constant: *Marcus Ellery Hale. Marcus Ellery Hale.* Somewhere overhead, a retired schoolteacher was keeping a dead man anchored to the world with nothing but her reading voice and a cup of cold coffee.

Beatrix set the reel-to-reel on a milk crate she'd carried down herself, because the dead deserved better than a plastic folding table. She threaded the first tape. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. She'd expected them to shake. Instead they moved with the same automatic competence she felt when she cued up a caller at 3 a.m., and she understood, with a small cold click, that the show had trained her for this. Twenty years of handling other people's voices gently. It had all been rehearsal.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the door, to the dark, to herself. "Let's give it back."

She pressed play.

The first voice was a man's, warm and grainy with tape hiss, saying his own name—*Walter Osgood*—and then, because the dead on these recordings had always been more than names, telling a joke. A bad one. A groaner about a fish and a priest that Beatrix had heard Emrys play on the air in 1974, three weeks before Walter died and the reel went into the earth.

The door drank it.

She felt it happen more than heard it—a pull, like standing near an open window in winter, the heat of the room leaning toward the cold. The voice went out of the speaker and into the oak and was gone, and in the same instant something came back through her, up through the floor, into the soles of her shoes, her knees, her spine, and took.

It was small, the first toll. A phone number. Her childhood phone number, the one with the exchange name she'd never told anyone, the one she still recited to herself sometimes the way other people recited psalms. It was simply not there anymore. She reached for it and found a smooth blank space, like a tooth socket, and she stood in the dark with the tape hiss running on and thought: *that's the price. That's the exchange rate.*

One voice for one memory. She'd known, in the way you know things you refuse to look at directly, that it wouldn't be free. Emrys's logs had been clear enough, in their cramped, deteriorating hand: *it doesn't want the tape. It wants what the tape is made of. It wants the remembering.*

Two hundred and six voices. She had counted them twice, then a third time at Ruth's kitchen table while Ruth sat across from her with her arms folded and her face like a closed ledger, saying, *You do this, you do it knowing what it costs. I'm not paying it. I paid once already.*

Beatrix threaded the second reel.

* * *

The tolls did not arrive in order of importance. That was the cruelty of it—or the mercy, she could never decide, and later she would stop trying to decide. The cellar took what it took.

The fourth voice cost her the name of her third-grade teacher. The ninth took the smell of her grandmother's kitchen—cardamom and coal smoke—leaving the kitchen itself intact, a room she could walk through but not breathe in. The seventeenth was the first hard one.

The voice belonged to a girl named Adaeze, nineteen, recorded in 1981, singing half a verse of a song in Igbo before the recording cut off. Beatrix played it into the door, and the door took her mother's face.

Not her mother. Not the woman, not the love, not the sound of her voice or the weight of her hand. Just the face. Beatrix stood there and knew her mother had had a face, obviously, everyone did, and she knew she had loved her, and she knew the love was still there, enormous, undiminished—but the face itself was a white room. She tried to picture her and got a shape, an outline, a person-shaped absence wearing an apron in a kitchen she could no longer smell.

"Oh," she said quietly. Her breath fogged in the cellar cold. "Oh, that's how it works."

The thing below was not stealing her past. It was eating the *proof*. It took the evidence of having been someone, and left the fact, the way a burglar leaves the furniture and takes the photographs.

She thought about stopping. She wants it noted, for the record she would later broadcast to the entire listening area, that she thought about stopping, seriously, for the length of one full breath. Above her, faint as a radio in another room, the schoolteacher's voice said, *Adaeze Nwosu. Adaeze Nwosu,* and Beatrix understood that the girl in the reel was, right now, at this moment, being held in the world by a stranger who had never met her and would never know her name's weight. The volunteers were holding two hundred and six people up with their tired voices. All Beatrix had to do was pay.

She threaded the next tape.

* * *

Somewhere around the fortieth voice she lost her first winter.

She knew she'd had one—everyone has one, a first winter, snow or no snow, some earliest cold—but the specific one, the one that had been hers, the one with the details that made it *hers* and not just any winter, was gone. She sat down on the milk crate for a while after that one. The door hummed. The oak had gone from black to something like deep red in the light of her work lamp, and it was warm now. It had been cold for fifty years and now it was warm, and she tried not to think about what a door is like when it's warm.

"You're welcome," she told it, because her father—no. Because someone had taught her manners. Someone had. She could feel the shape of the lesson without the lesson, the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue. "You could at least say you're welcome."

The door said nothing, but the hum changed pitch, and Beatrix chose—with the last of her humor, which she guarded now like rationed chocolate—to believe it was laughing.

Around the eightieth voice, she stopped feeling the tolls immediately. There was a delay now. She'd play a voice into the dark, and the dark would take its payment, and she wouldn't know what was missing until hours later, or she'd never know, and some future version of herself would reach for something—a face, a summer, a street name—and find the socket smooth and empty and understand, with distant grief, that this was the bill for a voice she'd already returned.

She started keeping a list. Not of what she'd lost—she couldn't know that—but of what she still had. She'd whisper it between reels, an inventory, a roll call of her own: *My name is Beatrix. I live above a laundromat. I like my coffee burned. I have a scar on my left thumb from a can lid. I have a scar on my left thumb. I have a scar—*

Around voice one hundred and twelve, she checked. The scar was there. She laughed, and it came out cracked, and it echoed down the corridor, and the door drank the echo too, and she decided not to bill it.

* * *

The middle hours are hard to describe, and Beatrix, who had made her living describing things, would later tell her listeners honestly: *I can't do it justice. It was like being unwound. Like being a sweater and having someone patiently, politely, one stitch at a time—*

Voice one hundred and forty was a drowned boy. Not her drowned boy—there had been more than one, the cellar was not particular—but a boy named Tomas, 1993, whose recording was mostly silence and then, near the end, a small wet laugh. When she played it into the door, the toll was the sound of her own mother saying her name. Not the face this time. The voice. The specific way her mother had said *Beatrix*, with the second syllable rising, like a question, like an invitation: *Beatrix? Come see. Beatrix? Come here.*

She sat with that one a long time. The tape hiss ran on. Overhead, faintly, someone new had taken the phones—a young man with a soft voice, reading *Tomas Reyes, Tomas Reyes*—and Beatrix realized the boy in the reel and the boy in the roll call were the same boy, and that the stranger upstairs was holding him, and that she was the one letting him go, and that both of those things were acts of love, and that love had never once in her life been simple.

"Tell your mother yourself," she whispered to the reel, to the boy, before she threaded it. "That's what I'd say. Tell her yourself. You get to now."

She pressed play. The dark took him gently, she thought. She chose to think that.

* * *

By voice one hundred and ninety, she was keeping herself together with procedure. Cue the reel. Announce the name aloud—she'd started doing this somewhere in the hundreds, giving each voice a send-off, a tiny eulogy, *Walter Osgood, fisherman, terrible jokes, going home*—press play, let the door drink, note the toll or fail to note it, thread the next.

Her middle name went somewhere in there. She discovered it the way you discover a stain on a shirt you've worn all day: someone else's eyes would have caught it, but she only found the absence when she reached for it, introducing herself to herself in the dark. *Beatrix—* and then nothing. A blank where a name had been, a name she'd been given by—by someone, by a woman, by the faceless woman in the apron, for a reason now also gone.

She cried then, a little, efficiently, the way she did everything in the small hours. And then she wiped her face on her sleeve and said to the door, "You know what the funny thing is? You're taking my middle name, and I never even liked it." A pause. "I liked it fine. I must have. People don't keep things they hate for forty years."

The hum was almost a purr now. She hated that she could read its moods. She hated more that some tired, hollowed-out corner of her had started to find the company—any company—comforting, and she made a mental note, in the ledger she was keeping of her own remaining self, to be worried about that later.

Voice two hundred and five was a woman who had died in 1969, before Emrys, before everything, whose voice had been down there the longest, and whose recording was just her humming. Beatrix played it, and the toll was the color of her first apartment's kitchen wall, and she barely noticed, and that frightened her more than the mother's face had.

One left.

* * *

She knew which one it was. She'd saved it. Reel one, the oldest, the one labeled in the cramped deteriorating hand: *E. VANE.*

The man who started this. The man who made the bargain, who fed the cellar its first names to keep his career and then his life, whose cowardice had become a machine that ran for fifty years on other people's voices and other people's grief. She had every right, she told herself, to feel what she felt, which was not hatred, exactly. It was the feeling you have for someone who turns out to be exactly as weak as you are afraid you might be.

She threaded the reel. Emrys's voice filled the corridor—younger than she'd imagined, warmer, a radio voice with a laugh buried in it, saying his own name like it was a joke he was in on: *Emrys Vane, and you're listening to the Hollow Signal, and it's a cold night, friends, but we're warm together—*

The door leaned in. She felt the whole cellar tilt toward the speaker like a crowd toward a stage.

And Beatrix, one hundred and ninety-five memories lighter, her mother a shape in an apron, her middle name a socket, her first winter a rumor—Beatrix pressed stop.

The silence rang. Above her, two hundred and six names had been read, and were being read again, the volunteers circling back, holding the line. The door hummed, waiting, patient as a creditor. Two hundred and five voices returned. One debt outstanding. And the last voice on the tape—the one the dark wanted most, the one it had been waiting fifty years for—was still hers to give or to keep.

She put her hand on the play lever.

From behind the door, thin as smoke under a crack, a voice she recognized from the reels said, clearly, pleadingly:

"Wait."
