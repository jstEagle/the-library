## 21. Sign-Off

The transmitter room smelled like warm dust and forty years of coffee that had soaked into the carpet and become, essentially, part of the building. Beatrix stood at the console with her hand flat on the ON AIR light—unlit, patient, a small red heart waiting to beat—and let herself feel the weight of what she was about to do.

Behind her, stacked on a folding table Ruth had dragged down from the break room, sat the archive. Two hundred and six reels. She had counted them three times, because counting was a kind of prayer and she had learned this month that prayers could be made of anything: tally marks, tape hiss, the names of the dead read aloud by strangers who owed them nothing.

The volunteers were gone now. All thirty-one of them. They had read names from midnight until dawn for six nights running, holding the dead anchored while Beatrix did the work below, and when it was finished they had gone home to their lives like people leaving a funeral that had, against all odds, turned into something like a wake with better snacks. Denny from the all-night diner had hugged her. The insomniac with the ferret had left a note taped to the studio door that said *YOU'RE THE BEST THING ON THE RADIO AND I MEAN THAT LITERALLY, THERE'S NOTHING ELSE ON.* She had kept the note. She intended to keep it forever, or at least as long as her memory held out, which was now a genuinely uncertain quantity.

She touched her own name in her mind and found it intact. *Beatrix.* Her middle name was still gone—she'd traded it for a drowned boy's voice, somewhere in the dark under the studio, and she'd do it again—but the rest of her was there. Most of her. Enough of her.

Ruth appeared in the doorway with two cups of coffee and the expression of a woman who had been crying in her car and had done a competent job of hiding it.

"You're really going to say all of it," Ruth said. It wasn't a question. It hadn't been a question for three days.

"All of it. My name. Your name. Your brother's name—"

"Don't." Ruth set the cups down. Her voice cracked in the middle of the word and she let it, which was new, which was the whole point of the last week. "No. Say it. I told you to say it. I'm just—give me a second to be a person about it."

Beatrix gave her a second. She gave her several. Outside the transmitter room window, the town was waking up the way it always did: delivery trucks, the bakery's first batch, a man arguing gently with a dog about a squirrel. None of them knew yet what was coming over the airwaves at nine o'clock. By ten, everyone would know. By noon, there would be a line outside the station, some of it angry, some of it grieving, some of it—she hoped, she had to hope—grateful.

"His name was Peter," Ruth said finally. "Peter Okonkwo. He was going to be a teacher. I signed him away to save a building, and the building wasn't even worth it. That's the stupidest part. It was never worth it." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, like she was annoyed at them. "Say it right. Say it like he mattered."

"He mattered," Beatrix said. "I'll say it like that."

Ruth nodded, picked up her coffee, and left. At the door she stopped. "For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—I always liked your show. Even before I knew what it was for." A pause. "Especially now."

Then she was gone, and Beatrix was alone with the console and the reels and the last hour of a life she had built specifically so that no one would ever look at her, about to be dismantled in front of everyone.

It should have felt like dying. It felt, instead, like taking off a coat she'd forgotten she was wearing.

* * *

At nine o'clock exactly, she pressed the light.

"This is The Hollow Signal," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because inside she was a house with all its windows open in a windstorm. "My name is Beatrix Yarrow, and tonight I'm going to tell you everything. There won't be a request line. There won't be callers. For the first time in the history of this show, it's just going to be me, talking to you, for as long as it takes. If you stay, thank you. If you leave, I understand. If you come back later, I won't be here, and I'll explain why."

She took a breath. Somewhere in the building, she thought she felt the floor hum—one low, attentive note, like something listening from a great depth. But it wasn't hungry anymore. It was just listening. There was a difference, and she had learned it the hard way, and she was going to teach it to everyone within range of this frequency.

"Fifty-one years ago, a man named Emrys Vane sat in this chair and made a bargain," she began. "He was scared, and broke, and alone, and something under this building offered him a deal: feed it the voices of the dead, and it would keep his career alive. Then his career. Then, eventually, him. He said yes. I want you to hear that part clearly, because I need you to understand that he wasn't a monster. He was a tired man at three in the morning who wanted his life to keep going, and the thing below knew exactly how to ask."

She told it all. The excavation, the door of blackened oak sealed from the inside. The reels in the flooded archive, labeled in a dead man's careful hand. The callers who had died the week before they phoned in—Dolores, who had known her childhood; the drowned boy, Marcus, who only wanted his mother to know he hadn't been scared. She said Marcus's name the way his mother had taught her to say it, with the soft middle, the way you'd say it across a dinner table. She said Dolores's name, and Dolores's late husband's, because Dolores had asked her to, the last lucid night, before the remembering pulled her back down.

And then she read the ledger.

It took two hours. Two hundred and six names, five decades, read slowly, with the town attached to each one the way Emrys's logs had recorded it: *Marion Ellery, 1979, the florist on Delancey. Thomas Wren, 1983, taught shop at the middle school. Peter Okonkwo, 2011, was going to be a teacher.* She stumbled twice. Once on a name that had been her own great-aunt's—she hadn't known until she read the ledger, and the grief of it arrived sideways, late, like mail forwarded from a house she'd moved out of decades ago. Once on Ruth's brother, where she stopped, and said, "Peter Okonkwo mattered," and let the silence carry it the rest of the way.

The phones stayed dark. She had pulled the lines herself. But she could feel the town listening—she could feel it the way she had once felt the dead listening, except this was warm, this was the living, this was thirty thousand people sitting in kitchens and trucks and hospital waiting rooms with the radio on, holding still.

When the last name was read, she told them about the toll. Her own. What she had paid, name by name, voice by voice, to give the dead back themselves. She told them she couldn't remember her middle name anymore, or her first winter, or the smell of her mother's kitchen, and she said it without self-pity, because it wasn't pity she felt. It was more like the feeling of having carried something heavy up a long staircase and finally setting it down.

"And here's the part where I tell you what happens next," she said. "This show ends tonight. The frequency goes back to the commission. The archive goes into the ground with the door, sealed properly this time, from the outside, with witnesses. I'm not going to tell you where I'm going, because the whole architecture of this thing was built on one woman's voice and one woman's silence, and I'm done supplying both." She smiled, and hoped it carried. "You never needed me, by the way. That's the secret. You never needed a vessel. You just needed somebody willing to say the names out loud, and any one of you can do that. All of you can. Please do. Not for the thing below—it's quiet now, and I intend for it to stay quiet. For each other. The dead don't need much. They never did. They just need to not be forgotten, and that's a job for the living, and you're all so much better at it than you think."

She looked at the reels one more time. Then she played the last thing she had to play.

Eleven minutes of Emrys Vane, breathing.

She let all of it run. No talking over it. The breathing was thin and steady and human, and somewhere in the middle of it, listeners across the county heard what she heard: a man's voice, very faint, saying *thank you* once, and then just breath, and then, at the very end, a sound that might have been a laugh—small, disbelieving, relieved—the laugh of someone stepping out of a house he'd been locked in for fifty years.

Then silence. She let that run too. A full minute of dead air, which every engineer she'd ever worked with would have called a sin, and which she now understood was sometimes the most honest thing a frequency can carry.

* * *

The sun was coming up when she carried the last reel out to the truck.

Ruth met her at the loading dock with a cardboard box—Beatrix's things from the studio, such as they were: the ferret man's note, a mug shaped like a skull that had been a gift from a listener in 2019, a photo of a dog she'd never owned that callers had somehow decided was hers. Ruth set the box in the passenger seat and then, awkwardly, hugged her. It was brief and fierce and slightly too hard, the hug of a woman who had spent years managing a monster and had forgotten how to do the softer thing and was doing it anyway.

"You could stay," Ruth said into her shoulder. "Rebuild. Different show. Different name."

"It was never the show," Beatrix said. "It was the door. And I'm done being architecture."

Ruth stepped back. Her eyes were red but her voice was level. "The station's done anyway. I called the commission this morning. Told them everything. They're pulling the license—there's a hearing, there'll be investigations, the owners, all of it. The whole rotten ledger." She almost smiled. "Fifty years of quiet rot, and it took one woman reading names for two hours to bring it down. You'd have made a hell of an auditor."

"I'd have made a terrible one. I liked the cranks too much."

They stood there a moment in the cold gold light. Somewhere down the block, the bakery man was arguing with the dog about the squirrel again. The world was doing its ordinary, obstinate, beautiful work.

"Go be nobody," Ruth said. "You'll be great at it. You've had practice."

Beatrix laughed—a real laugh, the first one in weeks that didn't have a hum underneath it—and got in the truck, and drove.

She did not look in the rearview mirror at the station, because she didn't need to. She had spent a month learning that the past keeps fine without being watched, so long as somebody, somewhere, remembers it kindly. She had done her part. The town would do the rest.

* * *

The last thing she did before leaving was small, and she did it at a rest stop two counties over, standing by a vending machine in the gray morning.

She took out her phone, and she dialed the old request line one last time. It was disconnected—she'd pulled the lines herself—but she dialed it anyway, and listened to the flat, polite tone of a number that no longer went anywhere, and felt nothing rise up to meet her. No hum. No third voice. No dead woman asking to be remembered.

Just the tone. Just the ordinary, empty, merciful quiet of a line that had finally been allowed to rest.

"Goodnight, Dolores," she said to it. "Goodnight, Marcus. Goodnight, Emrys."

She hung up. The vending machine hummed, and it was only a vending machine. The highway hummed, and it was only trucks. Somewhere behind her, a town was waking up to a story it would argue about for years, and ahead of her was a coastline she'd never seen, and a day job, and a life with no frequency in it at all.

She got back in the truck. She turned the key. On the radio, a morning show she'd never heard before was playing a song she didn't know, and the DJ's voice was bright and meaningless and alive, and Beatrix Yarrow—no middle name, no show, no archive, no mask—turned the dial until she found static, and left it there, and drove toward the water with the windows down.

The dead did not call again.

But that night, in a motel outside a town whose name she hadn't learned yet, a stranger's radio in the next room crackled once, softly, in its sleep—and a woman's voice, warm and familiar and not hers, said *thank you for remembering*—and Beatrix smiled at the ceiling, and did not pick up the phone, and slept until morning.
