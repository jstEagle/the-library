## 1. The Backwards Prayer

The night shift at Hollow Pines Hospice began, as it always did, with the smell of lavender and bleach fighting for dominance in the hallway, and Ada Brightwater losing the argument she'd been having with herself for eleven years: that this was just a job.

She clocked in at 10:47 p.m., three minutes early, because being early was a kind of armor. She hung her coat on the hook by the nurses' station — the one nearest the window, where the frost had already begun its slow fern-work across the glass — and she took the leather ledger from the bottom drawer of her desk, where it lived beneath a false bottom made from an old clipboard and a lie about tax records.

The ledger was real calfskin. It had been her mother's before it was hers, though her mother had never written anything in it but grocery lists and one address in a hand Ada didn't recognize. Ada had filled the rest. Fourteen volumes now, shelved in her apartment like scripture, each spine dated in gold ink. This was volume fifteen. The cover had gone soft as an ear at the corners.

She opened it to Eleanor Marsh's page.

*Eleanor Marsh, 84. Admitted 14 March. No children. One niece in Tucson who calls on Sundays. Diagnosis: pancreatic, metastatic. Prognosis: the honest kind.*

And below that, in Ada's small, ruled handwriting, the column that had no medical justification whatsoever:

*Night 1: 312.*

Ada checked her watch. 11:02 p.m. She walked the hall to Room 4, moving quietly out of habit rather than necessity — the other residents were asleep, or past the point where sleep and its opposite made much difference — and she stood in the doorway with her pen uncapped, the way other people might stand at the edge of a church.

Eleanor Marsh was awake. Eleanor Marsh was always awake now, had been for six days, lying on her back with her hands folded on the blanket like a woman posing for a portrait she couldn't see. Her eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling, on some coordinate above the water stain, and her lips were moving.

"—three hundred eleven," said Eleanor Marsh, in a voice that was not quite hers.

Ada wrote it down.

This was the ritual, and she would not have called it that, not under oath, not to Ruth, not to Dr. Okafor who signed off on her charting without reading it. She would have called it observation. Documentation. Patient-reported phenomena, noted for continuity of care. But her pen moved in the dark room with the reverence of a censer, and she timed her visits to the minute, and she had never once missed a night, and if those things were not prayer then Ada did not know what prayer was, because she had stopped going to the Methodist church at nineteen and nothing else in her life had ever asked this much of her, or given back so little, or given back so much.

"Three hundred ten."

The voice was the problem. That was what she'd write, if she ever wrote the truth instead of numbers. Eleanor Marsh had a smoker's rasp, thirty years of Virginia Slims ground into her vocal cords, a voice like a screen door in autumn. What came out of her mouth at night was smooth. Unblemished. A young voice, or something that had never been young at all, something that had only ever heard voices described. It came out level and unhurried, with the cadence of someone reading aloud in an empty house, and it did not pause for breath, and it did not stumble, and in six days it had never once skipped a number.

Ada had watched people die for eleven years. She had seen the rattle, the pull, the long pauses between breaths that stretch like taffy until the family in the corner stops pretending they're checking their phones. She knew every way a body winds down. None of them involved counting.

It had started, as far as she could reconstruct, with Mr. Aldous in 2013, counting down from 61 over three nights, and Ada had assumed it was delirium, the brain tidying itself before the lights went out. Then Mrs. Ferro, from 208. Then the Hendricks man, from 1,004, over nine nights, steady as a metronome, and Ada had started keeping the ledger because the numbers were *consistent*, and consistency was the one thing in hospice work you could hold onto, the one thing that didn't rot or weep or ask you to hold its hand while it told you about the dog it'd had when it was nine.

She had told herself for years that she documented the Count because it might mean something. Pattern recognition. Early-warning data. Maybe the count's rate of descent correlated with time remaining; maybe someday she'd be able to tell a family *your mother has four nights left* with certainty instead of kindness dressed up as certainty.

But standing in Room 4 at 11:09 p.m., listening to a dead woman's throat produce a living stranger's voice, Ada admitted the thing she only ever admitted at this hour, in this doorway, in the dark: she wasn't documenting the Count. She was attending it.

There was a difference. Attendance implied obligation.

"Three hundred nine."

Ada glanced at the monitor. Eleanor's heart rate was 54 and lazy, her oxygen 93 on two liters, her temperature normal. By every instrument in the room, the woman was fine. By every instrument except the one that mattered, which was the one hanging open in the middle of her face, producing numbers like a well produces water — from somewhere deep, somewhere cold, somewhere with no bottom anyone had sounded.

Ada sat down in the vinyl chair beside the bed. She always sat. You didn't stand through a service.

"How are you tonight, Miss Marsh?" she asked, softly, though she knew better than to expect an answer. The counting voice did not converse. Once, early on, Ada had asked Mr. Hendricks directly — *sir, can you hear me? do you know you're doing this?* — and his eyes had rolled toward her, wet and terrified and unmistakably his own, and he had mouthed, around the numbers pouring out of him like water around a stone: *I can't stop it.* Then: *six hundred ninety-one.* His eyes had closed again, and he hadn't looked at her again for the remaining eight nights, and Ada had learned her lesson, which was that there were rooms you didn't knock on twice.

So she sat, and she listened, and she wrote.

*11:14 p.m. — 309. Voice unchanged. Vitals stable. Resident non-responsive to verbal stimuli but presents no distress markers. Skin warm. Hands folded, self-positioned (observed).*

That last detail she'd added two residents ago, after noticing it in three patients in a row: the hands folding themselves. Not clasped, not gripping the blanket — folded, right over left, thumb over thumb, the posture of a child saying bedtime prayers. Ada had done it herself as a girl, kneeling at the side of her bed in the farmhouse, in the corduroy quiet of the country dark, hands folded exactly that way, saying her prayers to — 

She stopped the thought there, the way you stop at the top of stairs in the dark, sensing one more step than your foot expects.

Right over left. Thumb over thumb. Every one of them.

"Three hundred five."

Ada wrote the number. Her pen scratched in the silence, and the sound of it seemed to please the room somehow, though rooms don't have moods, she told herself, rooms don't listen, and then she wrote that down too, in the margin, in smaller script, the way you write the things you're ashamed of: *rooms don't listen.*

But she had been writing that in margins for fourteen volumes, and the marginalia was starting to outnumber the data, and lately she had begun to suspect that the ledger was less a record than a rehearsal. That all these years of precise columns and exact times and gold-inked spines had not been science at all but liturgy — the same words in the same order at the same hour, offered up to something that appreciated order, that *fed* on it, maybe, the way anything is fed by devotion whether or not the devotee knows the name of the thing at the altar.

Because here was the thought she could not put down, the one that came to her at 3 a.m. on nights like this, wearing the face of a question: *the dying know their number.*

Not approximately. Exactly. Mr. Hendricks had begun at 1,004 — not 1,000, which would have meant nothing, but 1,004, specific as a serial number. Mrs. Ferro had started at 208. Eleanor Marsh had woken from a nap on Tuesday, six days ago, opened her eyes, and said "three hundred twelve" in that borrowed voice, and Ada had known — had felt it land in her chest like a coin dropped down a well — that the number was not random. That somewhere inside Eleanor Marsh, or somewhere inside whatever was speaking through Eleanor Marsh, a total had been calculated. A duration. An appointment.

Counting backwards from a number only they seem to know. That was how Ada had first phrased it, years ago, in the privacy of volume seven. She'd read the sentence back a dozen times since, and each time it gave her the same small vertigo, because the sentence contained a question she had never let herself finish asking: *know from where?*

You couldn't count down to zero unless zero was a place.

"Two hundred ninety-eight," said the voice in Eleanor Marsh's throat, and Ada realized forty minutes had passed. Time did that in Room 4. It pooled and went still, like water in a cupped palm. She flexed her fingers, stiff around the pen, and noticed she had transcribed every number without missing one, and that her handwriting had grown very neat, neater than her daytime charting, neater than it needed to be, as though neatness itself were the offering.

At midnight, Ruth appeared in the doorway with two coffees and the expression she reserved for Ada's rituals, which was fondness armored in skepticism.

"You're doing the vigil thing again." Ruth set a cup on the bedside table. She was sixty, built like a fence post, unbothered by everything except the state of the breakroom microwave. "Okafor asked me if you're okay. I told him you're the most okay person I know, which worried him more."

"I'm charting," Ada said.

"You're *kneeling*," Ruth said, "in a chair." She looked at Eleanor — at the folded hands, the fixed eyes, the lips shaping the next number — and Ruth, who had worked hospice for twenty-two years and claimed to have made peace with everything, looked away faster than she needed to. "I don't know how you sit in here. Gives me the creeps, that counting business. I told you, it's terminal lucidity. Brain doing inventory. Nothing but static with a rhythm to it."

"That's one theory," Ada said.

"It's the *correct* theory." Ruth drank her coffee like a woman daring the world to contradict her. "Numbers aren't prayers, Ada. They're noise."

"Then why," Ada said quietly, "does it never skip?"

Ruth didn't answer. She stayed another few minutes, filling the silence with talk of her grandson's football game, of the storm due Thursday, of anything at all — and Ada understood, with a tenderness that surprised her, that Ruth talked the way some people cross themselves. Against the dark. And when Ruth left, promising to check back at two, the room settled around Ada again like silt.

"Two hundred eighty-one."

Ada turned to a fresh line. And because it was midnight, and because the frost on the window had climbed halfway up the glass and made a pattern like feathers, like wings, like something vast settling against the building to keep warm — she allowed herself the question. Just for a moment. Just in the privacy of her own skull, where even the ledger couldn't read it.

*What happens at zero?*

Fourteen volumes. Thirty-one residents. Every one of them had reached the end of their number, and every one of them had died — that part held, that part was medicine, death remained reliably death — but the *manner* of it. That was the column she had never dared add. Because in fourteen volumes she had recorded onset times, rates of descent, vocal qualities, vital signs, and never once — not once in eleven years — had she written down what they said at the end. At the last number. At the moment the count completed and the voice, having finished its recitation, sometimes paused — a half-second, a held breath — before letting go.

They said something. All of them. She had heard it thirty-one times and recorded it zero times, and she knew exactly why: because a record is a claim that something happened, and once she wrote those words down in her careful gold-ruled hand, she would have to admit what they sounded like. She would have to admit they sounded like gratitude.

"Two hundred seventy-four," said Eleanor Marsh, in the voice of something patient, and outside the window the frost kept climbing, feather by feather, wing by wing, and Ada Brightwater bowed her head over the ledger, uncapped her heart the way she uncapped her pen, and kept the count.

* * *

At 3:40 a.m., the descent slowed.

This was new. In fourteen volumes, the rate had been constant within each resident — a number every ninety seconds, or every two minutes, metronomic to the point of machine. But Eleanor's voice had begun to space itself, stretching from ninety seconds to two minutes to nearly five, and Ada found herself leaning forward in the vinyl chair, pen poised, feeling something she refused to name and named anyway: *anticipation.*

"Two hundred sixty-one."

A gap. Long enough that Ada checked the monitor — heart rate 51, drifting down, slow and calm as a rowboat coming to shore. Then:

"Two hundred sixty."

Another gap. Outside, the wind had died completely. November should have been rattling the gutters; instead the night lay against Hollow Pines like a held breath, and Ada became aware of a smell she couldn't place at first, faint and green and impossibly out of season — cut hay, sun-warm, summer-heavy, seeping under the door of Room 4 as though the building itself were dreaming of July.

Her pen hovered. In the margin, in the small ashamed script, she wrote: *smell of hay. November. impossible.*

"Two hundred fifty-nine."

Ada looked at Eleanor's face. For six days it had been slack, absent, a rented room. Now something had shifted behind it — not a return, not Eleanor surfacing, but a *tilt*, as if whatever spoke through her had turned its attention, briefly, toward the doorway. Toward Ada. Toward the ledger open on her knee with its columns of faithful numbers, fourteen volumes of them stacked in an apartment six miles away, every digit entered in order, every night attended, nothing missed, nothing skipped —

Like a count.

The thought arrived whole, the way ice arrives on a pond: suddenly, everywhere, all at once. *Fourteen volumes. Thirty-one residents. Eleven years of unbroken attendance.* If documentation was prayer, then Ada had been praying longer and more devoutly than anyone alive, and prayers accumulate interest, and debts come due in the season of harvest —

Where had that phrase come from?

Ada sat very still. The phrase sat with her, familiar as a hymn, and she could not find its source. It felt like something a child would know. It felt like something *she* had known, once, kneeling at a farmhouse bedside with her hands folded right over left, thumb over thumb, saying her prayers to someone whose name lived now in the sealed room of her memory, behind a door she had locked so long ago she'd mistaken the lock for the wall.

"Two hundred fifty-eight," said the voice, gentle as a lullaby, and Ada Brightwater wrote it down, because that was what she did, because it was all she knew how to do, because somewhere in the marrow-deep place where vocation becomes compulsion she understood that she had not chosen this ledger any more than Eleanor Marsh had chosen her number.

The hay-smell thickened, sweet and green and wrong.

And in the margin, without deciding to, Ada wrote the question that would follow her home at dawn, waiting on her porch with thirty years of patience and a frayed corduroy cuff:

*Who taught them to count?*
