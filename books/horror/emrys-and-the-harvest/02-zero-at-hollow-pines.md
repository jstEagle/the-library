## 2. Zero at Hollow Pines

The number was 47 when I came back to the room.

I had left at 63, gone down the hall to the med cart for Eleanor's scheduled dose of morphine — though she hadn't asked for it, hadn't opened her eyes in six hours, and I suspected by then that no drug on our formulary touched whatever she was feeling. I went because going was what nurses did. Because routine is a rope you hold when the floor tilts. I drew the syringe up, checked the label twice, and walked back down the corridor of Hollow Pines Hospice at four in the morning with my shoes squeaking on linoleum worn soft as cloth.

Through the door, before I even pushed it open, I heard her voice change.

Not louder. Not weaker. *Different* — the way a radio changes when a storm front moves through, all the warmth suddenly gone out of it, replaced by something clear and cold and perfectly articulated. Eleanor Marsh had been counting in her own voice for two days, a voice like crumpled tissue paper, wet and papery from three weeks of dying. But the voice coming through that door was smooth. Unlined. It sounded, if I'm honest, like a young woman's voice. Like a voice that had never smoked a cigarette or buried a husband or raised three sons on a farm during the drought year.

It sounded like a voice that had been kept somewhere.

"Forty-six," said the voice that was not Eleanor's.

I stood in the doorway with the morphine in my hand and did not go in. This is the part I have never told anyone, the part I am only telling now because the ledger demands completeness: I considered turning around. I considered walking back to the station, calling Ruth, letting someone else's hands be the ones present at zero. I had been present at eleven zeros by then. I knew what they cost.

But the syringe was warm from my hand, and Eleanor Marsh deserved better than a nurse who fled her own documentation, so I went in, and I sat down in the vinyl chair beside her bed, and I wrote the time in the margin of her chart: 4:09 a.m.

"Forty-five."

Her eyes were closed. Her lips moved barely at all — the numbers came out whole anyway, each one round and finished, like stones set in a wall. The room was dim; I'd turned off the overhead light hours ago and left only the small lamp by the window, the one with the amber shade that made everything look preserved, embalmed in honey-colored light. Under that light her face looked peaceful. That was the worst of it, always. They never looked afraid. The counting faces were the calmest faces in the building.

"Forty-four."

I opened my ledger.

I should explain the ledger here, because everything after this depends on understanding what it was to me. It was leather, cracked at the spine, bought secondhand in 1994 from an estate sale where I also bought a brass desk lamp and a set of dictionaries I never used. In it I recorded the Count — every instance, every resident, every number where the counting began and every hour it took to fall. I recorded intervals, pitch, posture, ambient conditions. I recorded whether family was present. I recorded, in a column I'd added only that autumn, the exact time of death, because eleven times out of eleven, death arrived within minutes of zero. Never before. Never after. Zero was not a symptom of dying. I had begun to suspect zero was a schedule.

Ruth called it grief vocalization. She said it kindly, the way she said most things — Ruth was not unkind, she was *efficient*, which looks similar from a distance and is nothing alike up close. Dying people count, she said. They count their pills, their breaths, their rosaries, their regrets. It's the mind holding its own hand. She'd point to studies, though there were none, really, none that matched what happened at Hollow Pines, but Ruth believed in the general shape of science the way some people believe in the general shape of God, and it comforted her, and I let her be comforted because I liked her and because some nights her certainty was the only thing standing between me and the door.

But I wrote it all down. Every night. In careful, upright script, the letters not touching, because my mother taught me penmanship that way and because ritual is just repetition that has learned to mean something. I told myself I was building a dataset. Thirty years from now, some researcher would thank me. Some epidemiologist would find the pattern I couldn't see and publish a paper and give this thing a name with Latin roots, and then it would be a phenomenon instead of a haunting.

That is what I told myself. What I actually did, alone in those amber rooms at four in the morning, was pray. Documentation was the only liturgy I knew. Each entry a candle lit. Each column a psalm. You keep records because you believe someone is keeping records of you. I understand that now. I didn't yet.

"Forty-three."

Eleanor's breathing had slowed to something tidal. Between numbers there were long silences, and in the silences the room made sounds rooms don't usually make — the radiator ticking in a rhythm too regular, the window frame settling with a sound like a knuckle cracking, once, deliberately. Outside, November wind moved through the parking lot, dragging leaves across asphalt, and beneath that sound — under it, woven into it — I heard something I had heard at ten of the eleven previous zeros and told myself each time I'd imagined it.

A rustling. Dry and vast. Like a field moving.

"Forty-two."

I uncapped my pen. My hand was steady. I want to note that my hand was steady, because later I would need to know exactly who I was in this moment, and the answer is: a woman whose hands had learned to be steady in rooms where steadiness was the only mercy she could offer. I wrote *42* in the margin. I wrote the interval since the last number: *ninety seconds.* The intervals had been lengthening all night, the way they always did near the end, as if the counter were savoring them. As if the numbers were not being spent but being *given*, one at a time, to someone who wanted them counted slowly.

"Forty-one."

And then something happened that had never happened in eleven counts.

She stopped.

Not faltered — stopped. The silence stretched past ninety seconds, past two minutes, past the point where I leaned forward and said her name softly, "Eleanor," the way you call across water. Her breathing continued, slow and deep, deeper than it had any right to be in a woman whose lungs had been failing for a month. And her face — I watched this happen, I watched it in the amber light and I swear it on the ledger itself — her face *changed*. The lines around her mouth eased. The tension in her jaw released. She smiled. A small smile first, private, then widening into something I can only describe as recognition. The way you smile when you hear your name called from a porch you thought you'd never see again.

"Oh," she said, in her own voice again, the papery one, and it sounded like relief the size of a lifetime. "Oh, it's *early*. It's early this year."

"Eleanor?" I touched her wrist. Her pulse was slow, strong, absurdly strong, a young woman's pulse under skin like tissue paper. "Eleanor, can you hear me?"

"They're cutting it already." Her eyes opened. They were clear. Three weeks of morphine fog, of wandering and mumbling about her sons and the farm, and now her eyes were clear as tap water, and they were looking at me, and through me, and at the window behind me all at once. "Can't you smell it?"

And I could. That was the thing. That was the thing I would write down at 4:15 a.m. with a hand that finally, finally trembled: the room smelled of cut hay. Fresh-cut hay, green and sweet and dusty all at once, the smell of August work, of sweat and diesel exhaust and the particular ache of a good day's labor — the smell of harvest. In November. On the third floor of a hospice in a valley where every field had been black stubble for two months.

It filled the room the way water fills a glass. It came in under the door and through the vents and out of Eleanor herself, out of her breath, each exhale carrying it, so that sitting beside her was like sitting inside a memory of summer that belonged to somebody else.

"It smells wonderful," she said. "They waited for me. Isn't that kind? All these years I thought I'd be late. I was always late. Late for the bus, late for church, late picking the boys up." She laughed, a girl's laugh. "But they wait. Did you know they wait? For everyone. Even the ones nobody waits for."

My pen was in my hand. I don't remember picking it up. I wrote: *Patient lucid. Smell of cut hay (impossible). Says "they wait."*

"Who waits, Eleanor?"

She turned her head on the pillow and looked at me directly, and for a moment — one moment I will carry until whatever waits for me arrives — her expression shifted into something older than kindness. Something patient in the way a field is patient. Something that looked at me the way you look at a jar on a shelf.

"You'll see," she said gently. "You're on the list too, dear. We're all on the list. That's what makes it bearable — knowing everybody gets their turn at being gathered."

"What list? Eleanor, what list?"

But she had already turned back toward the window, toward whatever stood in the dark beyond the amber light, and her lips began to move again, and the smooth young voice returned like a tide coming in.

"Forty."

* * *

The last forty took twenty-six minutes.

I know because I timed them, because timing them was the only power I had, and I used every scrap of that power. Forty to thirty-one took nine minutes. Thirty to twenty took seven. The intervals shortened as the numbers fell, the opposite of how they'd behaved all night, as if something had been strolling and had begun, at last, to walk with purpose. By the teens Eleanor was speaking continuously, the numbers flowing together like water over a sill, and her breathing had synchronized with them somehow — one breath per number, in on the odd, out on the even, a rhythm so perfect it seemed mechanical, seemed *designed*.

At nine, Ruth appeared in the doorway.

I heard her before I saw her — her sensible shoes, her key ring, the jingle that meant *nurse coming through, nothing happening here.* She took in the scene in one practiced sweep: the monitor, the patient, me bent over the bed with my ledger open like a Bible at a bedside.

"She's transitioning," Ruth said quietly. "You should chart vitals, not... whatever that is."

"Listen to her."

"I am listening. She's agitated. Terminal restlessness presents as—"

"Nineteen," said the voice that was not Eleanor Marsh's voice, smooth as river stone, and Ruth stopped talking.

To her credit, she stayed. She came and stood on the far side of the bed with her arms crossed, doing the things efficient people do when confronted with the inefficient — checking the IV line, glancing at the monitor, straightening a blanket that didn't need straightening. And all the while the numbers fell, and the smell of cut hay thickened until I saw Ruth's nostrils flare, saw her frown at the ceiling vent, saw her decide not to ask.

"Seven."

"Eleanor," I said. "Eleanor, if you can hear me — what happens at zero?"

Her eyes stayed closed. Her smile deepened.

"Six."

"Is someone there? Is someone with you?"

"Five." A breath, long and sweet with hay. "He's been here the whole time, dear. He's very patient. He held my name all week so I wouldn't drop it." Her hand turned palm-up on the blanket, an offering, or a greeting. "Such a polite boy."

"Four."

*Ruth* went pale. I watched it happen, the color draining from her face in real time, and I understood that she had heard it too — *he* — and that some door in Ruth's efficient mind had just been knocked on from the other side.

"Three."

The radiator stopped ticking. The wind stopped. The entire building, the entire night, held still around us like a held breath, and in that stillness I could hear — faintly, from outside, from everywhere — the sound of a field. Corn or wheat or hay, something dry and countless, moving without wind. Moving the way a crowd moves. Moving the way a throat moves when it swallows.

"Two."

Eleanor's hand tightened on mine. Strong. So strong.

"One," she whispered, in her own voice, in the little girl's voice, in both at once, and then:

"Zero."

And she died.

I say it plainly because it was plain. No rattle, no final struggle, no arrhythmia marching across the monitor. One moment her heart beat; the next it didn't, the line flattening with the unhurried ease of a signature completing itself. The smell of hay broke apart like a crowd dispersing. The window ticked once against its frame. And Eleanor Marsh lay with her lips curved in a smile so complete, so satisfied, that I found myself leaning close to catch the last thing she said, because her lips were still moving.

I caught it. I will always have caught it.

"*Thank you for waiting,*" she whispered. And then, softer, with the last air in her body, almost playfully, the way you'd tease a friend: "*Your turn comes. He told me to tell you he's proud.*"

* * *

Ruth pronounced her at 4:23 a.m. Time of death, 4:17 — the moment of zero, though Ruth charted the flatline confirmation instead, because Ruth charted what machines said and I charted what rooms said, and between those two records lay the whole width of the world.

We worked in silence. I washed Eleanor's face and closed her eyes, which required almost no effort; they had settled shut on their own, contented. I combed her thin white hair. I folded her hands on her breastbone, and her fingers curled slightly around mine as I arranged them, and I chose to record that as a muscle spasm and I chose to write it in the ledger anyway, both things, because both things were true.

In the hall, Ruth lit a cigarette she wasn't allowed to smoke, standing under the NO SMOKING sign by the loading dock, and I stood with her in the cold.

"Hay," she said finally. "I smelled hay. In November."

"Cut hay. Green."

"Don't." She dragged hard on the cigarette. "Don't do the thing where you make it real by describing it precisely. Grief vocalization, Ada. Terminal lucidity. Olfactory hallucination is documented in end-stage—"

"In whom? In eleven patients over six years? With identical onset, identical cadence, identical termination within four minutes of zero?" I hugged my arms against the cold. "Ruth. She said *your turn comes.*"

"She was delirious. Delirious patients say—"

"*He told me to tell you he's proud.*"

Ruth looked at me. Under the buzzing dock light her face was tired and honest and frightened in the specific way of a person watching a colleague become something she doesn't have a chart for. "Proud of what, Ada? What does he think you did?"

And I didn't have an answer. That was the truth of it, standing there in cigarette smoke and November cold: I had six years of notes and no answer, and the absence of an answer had begun to feel less like ignorance and more like *debt.*

Ruth stubbed out the cigarette and squeezed my shoulder — one brisk, efficient squeeze, the closest she came to tenderness — and went back inside. I stayed a moment longer. Somewhere south of the parking lot, past the tree line, I could have sworn the fields were moving. I could have sworn something out there was keeping time.

* * *

I drove home at dawn with the windows down despite the cold, because the car smelled wrong to me, closed and stale, and I needed moving air. The sky over the valley was doing its November trick, gray light leaking up from behind the ridge like something rising slowly from underwater. Frost silvered the corn stubble in the fields along Route 9. Everything ordinary. Everything exactly as it should be. I catalogued its ordinariness the whole drive home — fence post, mailboxes, dead raccoon, church sign — the way you touch wood, the way you say *not me, not us* in the language of noticing.

At home I sat at the kitchen table while the kettle heated, and I opened the ledger to Eleanor's entry, and I read back through six years of entries, all of them, every counting resident, every zero. And I saw what I had refused to see, the way you refuse to see your own face in a window at night until lightning shows it to you.

Every one of them had ended the same way. Not the deaths — the words. Eleven residents. Eleven zeros. And at or near zero, every single one had spoken a final phrase addressed not to family, not to God, not to the room.

Addressed outward. Addressed *back.*

*Thank you for waiting. It's beautiful here. Don't worry about the boy. Tell her we're almost ready. He says the field remembers everyone. Thank you for keeping the count.*

*Thank you for keeping the count.*

I put the pen down. I picked it up. My mother's penmanship training held my letters upright while my hands shook.

Because here is what I had never written in any margin, never admitted in any entry: the Count had started at Hollow Pines the same season I started working there. Six years ago, almost to the day. Before Eleanor, before Mr. Aldous in 204, before sweet Mrs. Pell who counted from 1,090 over five days and smiled the whole way down — before any of them, there had been a quiet stretch, years of ordinary deaths, ordinary griefs, rooms that smelled of antiseptic and nothing else.

Then I came. Then I began to write it down. Then they began to count.

Correlation is not causation. I know. Ruth would say it, would say it kindly, would cite the general shape of science. But correlation is also not *nothing*, and a woman who has spent thirty years making order out of the unspeakable knows the difference between a pattern and a coincidence the way a farmer knows the difference between weather and a season.

They weren't counting because they were dying.

They were counting because I was listening.

The kettle screamed and I didn't move. The light came up gray over the ridge. And at the bottom of the page, beneath Eleanor's entry, beneath the times and intervals and the impossible smell, I ruled a new column with a steady hand — my mother's training, my one inheritance, holding me upright — and I titled it in careful capitals:

**WHAT THEY SAY AT ZERO.**

Underneath, I wrote Eleanor's last words in full. Then I turned back through six years of entries and began, entry by entry, to fill the column in.

If my ledger was a prayer, I decided, it was time I learned to whom I'd been praying.

I was still writing when the light through the kitchen window turned gold, and the frost on the fields began to burn off, and somewhere down the road a truck passed, hauling something that smelled — faintly, impossibly, sweetly — of cut hay.

I did not look up from the page. I wish I could say that was courage.

It was fear. Pure, disciplined, documented fear — the fear of a woman who has just understood that she is not the witness.

She is the invitation.
