## 2. A Village That Won't Look Up

Lena woke on his second morning in Hollowmere to the sound of a village that was trying very hard to be quiet.

It wasn't silence. Silence, he knew from a decade of fieldwork in places that had forgotten how to speak, was its own texture—wind through bare hedges, the tick of old timber settling, water moving somewhere underground. Hollowmere had all of that. But beneath it ran something else: a deliberate hush, like a houseful of people tiptoeing past a sleeping child's door. Doors opened and closed softly. Footsteps on the lane outside his window at the Grey Wether Inn fell with an odd, measured care, as though the cobbles themselves might answer back.

He lay for a while under the eaves, listening to Mrs. Aldous downstairs arranging crockery with exaggerated gentleness, and thought about the night before.

Old Tam Hedley had sung for him in the inn's back parlour, three verses of *The Lark Above the Marle* before his voice simply stopped—not trailed off, not faltered, but stopped, clean as a cut rope—and he'd sat there with his mouth still shaped around a word that never came, staring at Lena with an expression Lena hadn't been able to name until now, lying in the grey light. It was apology. The old man had looked at him as if he owed him money. As if by stopping mid-verse he had cheated him of something, and was sorry, and would do it again anyway, and was sorry for that too.

*Every one of them stops,* Lena thought. *At different points in different songs—but always mid-verse. Never at the end of anything.*

He'd recorded eleven singers in two days. Eleven songs, eleven amputations. And not one of them could tell him why, or wouldn't. They grew polite and distant, offered him more tea, asked after his journey, and the subject closed over like water.

He got up, dressed, and decided to stop asking the living and go and bother the dead instead.

* * *

St. Brannoc's stood at the crown of the village on its low knoll, a squat grey building with a roofline like a held breath. It was older than its own records, the kind of church that seemed less built than accumulated, stone laid upon stone across centuries until the original shape was only a rumour inside the final one. Its lychgate leaned. Its yew trees, black-green and vast, had been dropping their poisonous needles into the churchyard longer than anyone could count.

And above it all rose the tower—square, sturdy, unremarkable except for one thing that Lena's eye found immediately and refused to release.

There were no bells in it.

He'd noticed it yesterday without registering what he was noticing: the tower's louvred openings were dark and empty, no bronze shapes visible behind them, no glint where metal caught the sun. A tower built to hold bells, made for bells, named in the parish accounts for its bells—and hollow as a cup.

The churchyard gate gave its small complaint as he pushed it open, and somewhere among the headstones a blackbird started up, then thought better of it and went quiet. Lena walked between the graves, reading names out of habit. Hedley. Marle. Oduya—there was that name again, on a weathered slab from 1849, and again on a newer stone, and again on a fresh granite marker so recent the grass hadn't grown back over it. Oduya was woven through this ground like thread through cloth.

The south door was unlocked. He let himself in.

Inside, the air was cool and smelled of stone dust, candle wax, and something faintly mineral underneath it all, like the inside of a cave. Light came down in dusty shafts through windows whose glass was mostly clear, patched here and there with coloured panes gone soft with age. The pews were old oak, worn to a dull shine along their edges by generations of shoulders and hands. Everything about the place spoke of long use and careful keeping.

Except the tower.

Lena stood in the nave and looked up at the tower arch, that great rounded doorway of stone opening onto darkness above. Rung bells left traces. He'd documented enough campanologies-in-miniature across the northern counties to know what to look for: the scarring on beams where bell frames had bolted in, the grease-darkened pits in floors where bearings had turned, the smell of tallow and brass that clung to belfries like pipe smoke to curtains.

He climbed the narrow stair in the tower's southwest corner, his hand trailing the wall, the steps worn into shallow smiles by centuries of feet. At the top, a low wooden door stood ajar.

The ringing chamber was empty.

Not merely quiet—empty, in the way a room is empty when something has been deliberately taken out of it. There was no frame. No wheels, no headstocks, no garter holes, no stays. No bell pit below, no trace of the great timbers that should have carried the weight of bronze. The floorboards were pale and unworn where they should have shown the scuff-marks of ringers' feet.

But against the far wall hung a rope.

It descended from the dark of the louvres above—from the empty space where a bell should have been—down through a hole in the ceiling, past a wooden guide called a slider that showed no wear at all because nothing had ever run against it, and coiled its lower length neatly on the floor. It was a proper bell rope, sally and all: the fluffy grip of red-and-white wool at the bottom, plaited tight and bright.

Someone had re-plaited that sally recently. The wool was clean, unfaded, almost new.

And the rope itself—the long body of it running up into the darkness—was worn smooth. Not frayed, not aged into brittleness, but polished, the way a stair rail is polished, the way a pew edge is polished. Polished by use. By many hands, over many years, pulling.

Lena stood very still in the empty ringing chamber and felt the first true cold of his visit settle into him, not from the air but from the arithmetic of it. A tower with no bell. A rope with no bell to ring. A sally kept new, and a rope kept smooth, and a village full of singers who couldn't finish a verse.

He reached out and touched the rope. It was cool and slightly waxy under his fingers, and it gave, very slightly, when he pressed—a faint sway, as if somewhere far above it, in the dark louvres where nothing hung, something had shifted its weight.

"Careful."

Lena turned so fast his shoulder struck the doorframe.

A woman stood on the stairs below him, half in shadow. She was perhaps fifty, tall, with close-cropped silver-flecked hair and skin the deep brown of well-watered earth. She wore a plain grey cardigan over her clerical collar, and she held a battery lantern in one hand and a duster in the other, as if she'd been interrupted mid-rounds. Her eyes were steady and very dark, and they went first to his hand on the rope, and stayed there a moment too long.

"You must be the folklorist," she said. "Mrs. Aldous said you'd come poking eventually. I'm Maren Oduya. Vicar here."

"Lena Eriksen." He took his hand off the rope and immediately wished he hadn't—it felt like a retreat, like being caught at something. "I'm sorry, I didn't think anyone would mind. The door was open."

"The door is always open," Maren said. "That's rather the point of a church." She climbed the last steps and came into the chamber, and Lena saw that she moved carefully here, the way you move in a room where the furniture is familiar but fragile. "Mind is a strange word for it. Nobody minds. But we do notice."

"I was looking for the bells," Lena said. "The parish accounts mention a ring of five. Where are they?"

"Gone," Maren said.

"When?"

She set the lantern down on the sill of the louvre opening, and morning light came through the slats and striped her face. "You've been asking questions in my parish for two days now, Mr. Eriksen. How's that going?"

He blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"The songs. The singing. Old Tam, the Ferrier sisters, young Will Prowse. You've sat in their kitchens with your little machine, asking them to sing. How has that gone for you?"

Lena felt heat climb his neck. "They stop," he admitted. "Mid-verse. Every time. And they won't say why."

"No," Maren agreed. "They won't." She picked up the duster and, absurdly, began dusting the empty sill, slow strokes through the motes of light. "You're a scholar. So let me put it to you as a scholarly matter. In most places, a question is a hole. You dig it, you see what's in it, you fill it back in or you don't, and life goes on. Yes?"

"That's one way of putting it."

"In Hollowmere," Maren said, "a question is a tally. Some questions are counted as answers. Do you understand me?"

"Not even slightly."

"Good," she said, and something in her voice was almost warm, almost amused, and entirely serious. "That's the right answer. Most outsiders say *of course I understand*, and then they go on doing exactly what they were going to do anyway, only louder." She stopped dusting and looked at him directly, and her eyes were kind and tired and old in a way her face wasn't. "Mr. Eriksen. This village has been good to you so far. Fed you, housed you, sung to you as much as it safely can. That warmth you keep writing down in your notebook—that isn't weakness. It's a wall. We build it around every visitor, and we build it fast, and we build it high, and we mean it kindly, and you will thank us for it someday whether you ever know why or not."

"The warmth is a wall," Lena repeated. "Built around me."

"Around everyone who doesn't belong here yet." She said the word *yet* lightly, the way you'd say it about tea, about weather, and the lightness of it frightened him more than anything else she'd said. "Now. I'll show you the accounts, since you'll only go digging otherwise. 1911. The bells came down—all five—and were sold to the foundry at Ashcombe and melted. There was a fire at the foundry that winter; the metal was lost entirely. That's the official record and it's true as far as it goes."

"And as far as it doesn't go?"

Maren smiled, and it was a real smile, sad at the corners. "As far as it doesn't go is the rest of the story, and the rest of the story isn't mine to give you. It's the village's. And the village gives nothing away whole." She lifted the lantern again. "Come down. I'll make you coffee in the vestry, and I'll answer three questions—any three you like—as honestly as I'm able. That's the custom for visitors. Three answers, freely given. After that, everything else you learn here, you'll have to earn the way the rest of us earned it."

"Which is how?"

"By staying," Maren said simply, and led the way down the worn steps, and did not look back to see whether he followed.

* * *

The vestry was a small room off the chancel, book-lined and cluttered, smelling of paper and polish. Maren made coffee in a dented percolator that wheezed like an old dog, and they sat on either side of a desk scarred by two hundred years of ink knives and cup rings, and Lena turned his recorder over in his pocket and did not take it out. Something told him that would be counted.

"Ask," Maren said.

"Why does every song in this village stop mid-verse?"

"Because finished things get counted," Maren said. "And what gets counted gets collected." She said it flatly, factually, the way you'd say *because the road floods in winter*. "Next."

Lena's pen hovered. He had a dozen questions queued—about the bell, about 1911, about the rope worn smooth in an empty tower—but he heard himself ask, instead: "Who is 'we'? Who does the counting?"

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Outside, through the vestry's small window, he could see the churchyard, the leaning stones, the black yews. Somewhere beyond them, the village lay in its careful hush.

"That's your second," she said quietly. "And I'll answer it truly, which is why I wish you hadn't spent it on that one." She wrapped both hands around her own cup. "We don't know. That's the honest truth, Mr. Eriksen. Whatever counts us has been counting us longer than the parish register, longer than the church, longer than the name Hollowmere. My family has served it—kept its books, if you like—for as far back as our name goes. But we didn't make it, and we can't stop it, and we don't worship it. We just... keep the accounts. So that when it comes to collect, it collects fairly. That's all the mercy we've ever been able to negotiate."

"What happens when it collects?"

"That would be your third," Maren said, "and I don't think you want to spend it. Not today. Not on that."

Lena set his pen down. His hand, he noticed distantly, was not quite steady. "Then I'll save it."

"Wise." She rose and refilled his cup, and the kindness in her movements was unmistakable and utterly immovable, a kindness with bedrock under it. "You'll want to look at the archive, I expect. Parish chest, in the north transept. I won't stop you—I'd rather you read it here than hear it twisted elsewhere. But Mr. Eriksen—" she paused, cup in hand, and the light through the window caught the grey in her cropped hair, "—when you find what you find, remember that the people in those pages were frightened, and they loved each other, and they did the best that anyone has ever managed in this place. Judge them gently. Someone may have to judge us the same way, someday."

"Us?"

"My family keeps the count," Maren said. "I told you. I'm the present keeper." She smiled again, that sad-cornered smile. "Drink your coffee while it's hot. Visitors always let it go cold, the first day. Too busy writing."

* * *

He stayed in the vestry another hour, drinking coffee that did indeed go cold, turning over her words. Then he crossed the church to the north transept, where a great iron-banded parish chest squatted beneath the shallowest of the stained windows, and Maren—without being asked—brought him the key and left him alone with it.

The documents inside were dry and orderly and terrible.

He read the churchwardens' accounts for 1911 with his heart beating oddly fast: entries for the taking-down of the bells, the cartage to Ashcombe, the payment received. Five bells, gone in a single autumn. And beneath the financial record, loose among the papers, a folded sheet in a different hand—an older hand, cramped and urgent—listing names. Forty-one of them. Each name followed by a number, and each number followed by a single word:

*Counted.*

He recognized some of the surnames. Hedley. Marle. Prowse. Names still walking around the village outside these walls, buying bread and pulling weeds and refusing to finish songs.

And at the bottom of the folder, a photograph. Card-mounted, foxed with age, the faces soft as memories. Villagers—forty or fifty of them—standing in a wide ring around the mouth of a well, dressed in the black-and-white of another era. Their heads were bowed. Their mouths were open.

All of them. Mid-song.

Lena sat back on the transept floor, the photograph trembling slightly in his hand, and became aware—slowly, then all at once—of the quality of the silence in the church around him. It was not the friendly hush of the nave that morning. It was attentive. It was the silence of a room where someone has just realized you've been reading their diary, and has not yet decided what to do about it.

Above him, through the tower's empty louvres, the wind moved.

And the wind, or something riding it, hummed—low, patient, and unmistakably melodic—the first four notes of a verse that no one in Hollowmere would finish for him.

Lena looked up at the dark square of the tower arch, alone in the church, holding the faces of the counted in his hand.

The humming stopped, as if satisfied. As if his looking-up had itself been noted, entered somewhere in a ledger, ticked and totalled.

Outside, far off across the fields, a rook called once and fell silent, and the afternoon light through the stained glass went suddenly thin and cold, like the light at the end of a day that has already decided how it will end.
