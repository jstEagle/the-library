## 3. Nineteen Eleven

The parish archive lived in the vestry's back room, behind a door that Maren Oduya unlocked with the reluctance of a woman opening a coffin she'd rather not look into again.

"You'll find what you're looking for," she said, handing him a ring of keys heavier than any he'd ever been given. "That's what worries me."

Lena had smiled at that—professionally, warmly, the smile he used on archivists and gatekeepers from the Hebrides to the Carpathians—and told her that worry was the tax every historian paid. She hadn't smiled back. She'd stood in the doorway a moment longer than necessary, her hand resting on the frame as if the room might bolt, and then she'd said, "The lamp works. The window doesn't open. If you need anything—" and left the sentence unfinished, which by now Lena recognized as the village dialect of mercy.

He heard her footsteps recede down the flagstones, unhurried, deliberate, like someone counting them.

* * *

The room was small and dry and smelled of paper dust and candle smoke that had soaked into the plaster decades ago and never left. Shelves ran floor to ceiling on three walls: parish registers bound in cracked leather, boxes of correspondence tied with ribbon gone the color of weak tea, ledgers with brass corners greened by the touch of a hundred years of hands. A single high window let in a blade of grey light that fell across a reading table like something laid out for examination.

Lena set up his equipment first—habit more than necessity. The recorder went on the table, its small red light burning. He liked having it running even when he was alone. It made him feel witnessed, or at least recordable. Somewhere in his life there would be evidence.

Then he began.

The registers came first, because they always did. Baptisms, marriages, burials—the three great tides of parish life, recorded in ink that faded from confident black to apologetic brown as the decades turned. He worked backward from the present, letting the names wash over him: Oduya, Halloway, Bracken, Fenwick, Marsh. Old families. Deep roots. The same surnames recurring generation after generation like a chorus returning to its refrain.

It was the burial register for 1911 that stopped him.

Not because of the deaths—there were only four that year, an ordinary harvest for a village of Hollowmere's size—but because of the marginalia. Someone, writing in a hand so fine it looked embroidered, had entered beside each name not a date or a cause but a single word:

*Counted.*

Besold, Agnes — *counted.*
Besold, Thomas — *counted.*
Fenwick, Mary — *counted.*
Oduya, Silas — *counted.*

Lena sat very still. Outside, wind moved through the churchyard yews with a sound like cloth being drawn slowly through water.

Four names. Four words. And then, further down the page, in the same embroidered hand, a fifth entry that was not a burial at all:

*Ringer, unnamed — counted twice.*

Counted twice. He read it five times. The ink of *twice* was darker than the rest, pressed hard enough to emboss the page, as though the writer had needed the pen to bear weight the words themselves could not.

"Right," Lena said aloud, to the empty room, because saying things aloud kept the blood moving and the imagination leashed. "Let's see what you were counting."

* * *

The correspondence boxes gave him the year in fragments.

A letter dated March 1911, from the Diocesan Surveyor to the Vicar of Hollowmere, regarding "the matter of the bell." The surveyor's prose was bureaucratic and slightly irritated, the prose of a man asked to do something he found distasteful. The bell, it seemed, was to be removed from the tower and melted down—not recast, not sold, *melted*, the metal to be broken into ingots and buried. There was no reason given. There was a phrase, though, underlined twice by some later reader whose pen had torn the paper: *"given the circumstances of its sounding."*

The circumstances of its sounding. As if the bell had done something. As if ringing were an act with consequences, a crime committed in bronze.

A second letter, April 1911, acknowledged receipt of the metal and noted, in a postscript that had clearly been added after the main text dried, that the workmen "declined to remain overnight and requested payment in advance." A third, from May, was almost illegible with haste—a vicar's hand shaking, or pressed, or both—thanking God that "the tower stands silent and may it so remain, for we have learned what the silence costs and will pay it gladly."

Pay the silence. Not pay for it. Pay *it*. Lena wrote the distinction in his notebook and underlined it, and felt the small cold pleasure of a scholar closing his fingers around a fact—and beneath the pleasure, fainter but persistent, the feeling he'd been carrying since Mrs. Besold had gone grey mid-verse in her front room two days ago. The feeling of being watched by the material itself. Of papers arranged not randomly but *expectantly*, the way a table is laid before guests arrive.

He shook it off. He'd felt this way in the Vatican archives, in the wrecked library of a flooded seminary in Norfolk. Old rooms did this. Old rooms collected attention the way attics collected heat.

But the Vatican had never made him check over his shoulder.

* * *

It was in the third box—a miscellany, unlabeled, its contents jumbled as if someone had once rifled through it in panic and never restored order—that he found the list.

It was written on the back of an order of service, the printed hymn numbers still visible through the paper like bones beneath skin. The list was long. Forty-one names, in several different hands, some contemporary with 1911 and some much later—the most recent in ballpoint, the ink still blue-black, the handwriting one Lena recognized.

Maren Oduya's.

His own breath sounded loud in the little room. He scanned the names. Some had dates after them; most did not. And down the margin, beside eleven of the forty-one, ran that word again, in that fine embroidered hand or its descendants: *counted.* Beside one name near the bottom—Ivy Oduya, no date—the word appeared twice, the second instance struck through with such violence the paper had nearly parted.

Ivy Oduya. Counted. Uncounted? Or counted beyond counting?

Maren's family name. Maren's careful, hidden, generational ledger, kept on the back of a hymn sheet like a sin scribbled on a confession card.

Lena photographed it—all of it, front and back, raking light and straight-on—and as the shutter clicked he had the distinct, absurd sensation that the flash had been answered. That somewhere in the dark behind the shelves, something had blinked.

He did not turn around. He was proud of that, later, and ashamed of it too.

* * *

The photograph was in an envelope marked only with a date: *29 October 1911.*

There was a single print inside, sepia going the color of old honey, and Lena held it up to the window's grey blade of light and forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

Villagers stood around the mouth of the well. He counted them twice—twenty-two, twenty-two again—and noted that they formed a perfect ring, evenly spaced, holding hands or nearly holding hands, their faces turned inward toward the well's dark circle. Men in collarless shirts. Women in aprons and shawls. Children between their elders, small hands swallowed by large ones. The well itself was a stone lip at the center of the image, and beyond it the village rose in soft focus: the church, the tower, the crooked line of cottages.

Every face was turned to the camera—or rather, past it. Every mouth was open.

Not smiling. Not shouting. Open in the particular way mouths are open in the middle of a sung note, caught between one syllable and the next, and Lena, who had spent fifteen years studying exactly this, who could read a song from a face the way other men read weather from clouds, knew with absolute certainty that these people were singing. All of them. Together. At the well.

And the photograph had been taken at the moment the verse broke off.

He couldn't have said how he knew that. But he knew it the way you know a chord is unresolved, the way your ear aches for the note that doesn't come. Every open mouth in that picture was waiting for a word that never arrived. Twenty-two people frozen mid-breath, mid-verse, mid-something, and the photographer—God, the photographer—had chosen this instant. Had waited for it. Had wanted the record to show precisely where the song stopped.

Lena turned the print over. On the reverse, in pencil, in a hand he now knew intimately—the embroidered hand, the hand of the burial register, the hand of the list—he read:

*They sang it whole. We will not make that mistake again.*

Below that, smaller, pressed so lightly it was nearly an absence:

*The bell remembers the tune. That is why it had to die.*

The lamp flickered. Or the window did. Or the light simply changed, the way light does, and Lena told himself it was the way light does.

He set the photograph down with exaggerated care, the way you set down a sleeping animal, and became aware—fully, bodily aware—that the room had gone quiet in a new way. Not the quiet of an empty space. The quiet of a held breath. The shelves seemed to lean in. The paper smell thickened. Somewhere above him, in the tower directly overhead, the empty belfry creaked once, softly, like a throat clearing around a word it had been forbidden to say.

"The bell remembers the tune," Lena repeated aloud, and his voice came out smaller than he intended. "You melted it. You buried the metal. And it's still remembering."

The recorder's red light burned steadily on the table, faithful, useless. He glanced at it and saw—no. He didn't see anything. A trick of the eye, a shadow of the lamp's filament. Except that the shadow had been shaped like a figure standing just beyond the shelf's end, patient, at the exact distance a person stands when they are waiting for you to finish reading.

Lena packed the photograph, the letters, the list, the register transcriptions into his bag with hands that were almost steady. Almost. He left the keys in the door as Maren had shown him, and stepped out into the nave, where the afternoon light fell through stained glass in pools of ruby and salt-white, ordinary and forgiving, and the silence of the church was merely silence, merely stone, merely an old building keeping its old secrets the way all old buildings do.

Behind him, in the archive, the lamp clicked off. He had not touched it.

He stood in the aisle a moment, listening to his own heart conduct its foolish percussion, and told himself the practical things: the wiring was ancient, the switch was temperamental, fear was a folklorist's occupational disease, and there was nothing in that room but paper and the past, and the past could not watch anyone.

Then, from the tower overhead—faint, brief, unmistakable—came a sound like a single bronze note, struck once, deep in metal that no longer existed, and cut off before it could resolve.

Cut off mid-verse.

Lena Eriksen walked out of the church into the grey afternoon without running, which cost him more than running would have, and did not stop until he reached the vicarage gate, where Maren Oduya was already standing in the doorway as if she had known the hour of his return, her face pale and set, her eyes going from his bag to his face with terrible recognition.

"You found the photograph," she said. It was not a question.

"Yes," Lena said. "And the list. And the register. Vicar—who is the ringer? The unnamed one? *Counted twice?*"

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked older, and kinder, and far more afraid.

"Come inside," she said. "And mind the dusk, Mr. Eriksen. It rings at dusk."
