## 9. Ida Hears It Too

Ida Oduya had learned to lie about the bell the way other children learned to ride bicycles: badly at first, with bruises, and then so smoothly that no one could remember when she hadn't known how.

It was a matter of timing. When the true bell rang — the one everyone heard, the toll that came out of the empty tower like a stone dropped into still water — she bowed her head with the rest of them, quick as anyone, her braid swinging forward to hide her face. That was easy. That was the whole village doing it together, a hundred heads dipping like wheat under wind.

The hard part was the other ringing.

The other ringing came whenever it liked. It came during arithmetic, during washing-up, in the grey hush before dawn when the house was nothing but creaking floorboards and Tomas's boots by the door. It came thin and far away, the way a tune comes through a wall from a neighbour's radio, except there was no radio, no neighbour, no wall between Ida and whatever was on the other side of the sound. Just the bell, small and patient, ringing inside her ears rather than outside them.

*Come and be counted, Ida.*

That was what it said. Not in words, exactly. In the way a smell says *bread*, or the way cold says *winter*. The meaning arrived whole, without language, and it had been arriving since she was seven years old, which was the same age her Uncle Tomas's brother — her mother's brother, the one nobody mentioned, the one whose photograph lived face-down in a drawer — had gone down to the well.

She had never told anyone. Telling would make it real, and if it was real, then it was happening again, and if it was happening again, then it wanted her, and Ida had already worked out, with the cold clear arithmetic children are so frighteningly good at, that everything in Hollowmere that wanted something eventually got it.

So she lied. She bowed her head at the false ringings and said nothing at the true ones, and she got very skilled at looking bored, because bored was the safest thing a girl could look.

Until the folklorist came.

* * *

His name was Lena Eriksen, and he was the strangest thing Hollowmere had produced in Ida's lifetime. He had a machine that ate songs — he'd shown her once, let her speak into the little round mouth of it and played her own voice back, thinner and farther away, like a ghost of herself — and he asked questions nobody else would ask. He asked Aunt Maren about the verse where every song stopped. He asked old Mrs. Callow why she went pale. He walked through the churchyard reading gravestones out loud like they were letters from friends.

And he looked up.

That was the thing. That was the thing Ida could not stop watching. When the bell rang at dusk that first week — the great toll, the one that froze the village — every head in the lane bent down, every lantern dipped, every conversation died mid-word. And Lena Eriksen stood in the middle of the road with his face tipped back toward the empty tower, and the sound went into him, Ida could see it go into him, the way water goes into dry ground, and he did not bow. He looked up at the place where a bell should have been and wasn't, and his mouth made a small shape, and Ida thought: *he hears it the way I hear it. From underneath.*

After that she watched him the way you watch weather. From the gate. From behind the hedge. From the top of the stile with her chin on her arms.

He caught her at it on a Thursday, coming down the lane from the Callow farm with his recorder bag over his shoulder, and instead of pretending not to see her the way adults usually did, he stopped.

"You've been surveilling me," he said. "For six days."

"I don't know what that means."

"It means you're better at it than I am." He shifted the bag. Up close he smelled of rain and machine oil and something else, something like the inside of churches. "You're Maren's niece. Ida."

"You know my name."

"I know everyone's name here. That's my job." He smiled, but it was one of his wrong smiles, the kind that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Names are the only things this village will give me for free."

Ida looked at him for a long moment. Below them, down the lane, the well cover sat in its circle of stones, and she did not look at it, because she had practiced not looking at it the way other people practice instruments.

"Do you ever," she said, and stopped, and started again, smaller: "Do you ever hear it when it isn't ringing?"

The change in his face was very small and very fast, like a fish turning in dark water. "When what isn't ringing?"

"The bell."

Lena Eriksen crouched down slowly, bringing himself to her height, which no adult in Hollowmere had done since she was small enough to be carried. His eyes were the colour of tea left too long, and they were frightened, and they were kind, and both of those things at once made her chest hurt.

"Why do you ask?" he said quietly.

"Because I do," said Ida. And having opened the door, all of it came out at once, ten years of held breath going out of her in a rush: "Not the big one. The big one everybody hears. But there's another one, a small one, inside my ears, and it rings whenever it wants, at school, at night, in the bath, and it knows my name. It doesn't say it. It's more like — you know when someone stands behind you and you can feel them even though you haven't turned around? It's like that, but with my name. Like being called from a room you're already half in." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going anyway, because she had started and there was no stopping now. "Aunt Maren sings the half-songs every night and I always thought that was just what families did, but last week I sang one all the way through in my head, just to see, just the once, and the bell rang three times before morning and the water in the well rose past the second mark and nobody knew why except me."

The lane was very quiet. Somewhere a rook complained in the elms.

Lena Eriksen did not tell her she was imagining it. That was the first miracle. He did not pat her head or say *there, there* or glance nervously toward the house the way adults did when children said true things. He sat down right there on the stile step, bag between his knees, and looked at her with his whole face.

"How long," he said, "since you were seven?"

Ida's stomach dropped. "How did you—"

"Because that's when your uncle's brother went down," he said gently. "I read the register. There's a gap in it, a name scratched out, and the date is eleven days after your seventh birthday. And you flinch every time anyone says the month." He rubbed his jaw. "I'm sorry. I notice things. It's an unpleasant habit."

"He drowned," Ida whispered. "They said the well was dry. They said he fell and there was nothing down there. But the well wasn't dry, was it. It was full of something else."

"No," Lena said. "I don't think it was ever empty at all."

* * *

He did not take her straight home. Later, Ida would understand that this was its own kind of decision — that he weighed it, sitting there on the stile with a twelve-year-old's confession sitting in his lap like something breakable, and chose to walk her the long way round, past the church, because there were things she needed to see and he needed to show them to someone who wouldn't look away.

Inside the tower, the rope hung down through the gloom, worn pale and smooth as bone along its middle length.

"Feel it," he said.

She did. The wood was glassy under her fingers, polished by decades of hands, and it hummed faintly, the way a struck string hums after the note is gone. Ida snatched her hand back.

"It remembers," she said.

"Everything here remembers." Lena stood beside her, looking up into the dark where the bell was not. "Your aunt told me some questions count as answers. I think she meant that asking them moves something. But I think listening does too." He turned to her. "Ida. Has it ever asked you to come down? To the well?"

She wrapped her arms around herself. "Every night. Since I was seven. It says come and be counted. It says—" Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "It says the others are tired of waiting, and a young voice carries further."

The silence after that had weight. Lena put his hand on the tower wall, steadying himself, and Ida watched him understand it — watched the comprehension move across his face like frost moving across a window, slow and total and terrible.

"It's not haunting the village," he said. "It's recruiting. It's been recruiting for a hundred years, and every generation it gets one candidate, and the family hides them, and sings the half-songs, and prays it picks someone else next time." He laughed, one short unhappy note. "And now it's picked you, and your aunt has spent her whole life singing lullabies to a counting-house, and your uncle has spent his whole life drinking so he doesn't have to hear what you hear."

"Don't talk about Uncle Tomas like that."

"I'm sorry." And he sounded sorry. "But Ida, listen to me. Listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once and then we're never going to say it again where walls might be awake." He crouched to her level again, and took both her hands in his, and his hands were cold and shaking slightly, and she realized with a jolt that he was more frightened than she was. "It wants you because you can hear it. But hearing goes both ways. Everything that listens can be listened to. So when it calls your name — and it will call tonight, it always calls at night — you don't answer, and you don't sing, and you don't count anything, and above all you don't wonder what's down there. Wondering is a door. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," said Ida. And then, because she was her mother's daughter and her aunt's niece and honesty ran in the family like a vein of ore: "But it's not fair. Everyone else has somebody. The drowned have each other, and the villagers have each other, and even the bell has the whole sky to ring in. I've just got a name that something else wants." Her eyes were hot. "Nobody ever wanted me. Only it."

Something moved through Lena Eriksen's face then that Ida had no word for yet — she would find the word years later, standing at a graveside, and the word was *recognition* — and he squeezed her hands once, hard, and said:

"That's not true anymore. As of today you've got me, and I've got you, and Aunt Maren, and a table with your name on it. Whatever that thing below thinks it's offering, it's offering a counterfeit. Real belonging doesn't ask you to drown first." He stood, pulling her up gently by one hand. "Now come on. Your aunt will have the kettle on and your uncle will pretend he hasn't noticed we're late, and tonight I'm going to sit outside your door with the recorder running, and if that bell so much as whispers your name, I'm going to record it, and I am going to learn what it is."

* * *

Dinner at the Oduya house was brown stew and brown bread and the particular silence of people who had spent their lives not saying things. Tomas ate with his shoulders up around his ears. Maren watched Ida with the sharp soft attention of a heron over water. And Lena — Lena talked. About songs, about the archive, about the melting of 1911, filling the silence the way you'd fill a crack in a wall, and Ida understood that he was doing it on purpose, laying bricks of ordinary words between her and the well, between all of them and the well, and that this too was a kind of singing.

Afterward, Maren washed dishes and Ida dried, and in the low clatter of it Maren said, without looking up: "He told you. Didn't he. About the register. About your uncle's brother."

Ida's hands went still on the plate she was drying. "You knew I could hear it."

"I suspected." Maren set a dish in the rack with terrible gentleness. "You were seven, and you stopped humming. Children who can hear it stop humming, because humming is halfway to singing and they learn to be afraid of their own throats. My mother stopped humming at seven. Her father before her." She finally looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and loving all at once. "We keep the count, Ida. We've always kept the count. And I would sooner burn this house down and salt the ground than let it have you."

That night Ida lay in bed listening to the house settle, and to the small sounds of Lena Eriksen in the chair outside her door, recorder on his knees, awake and keeping watch like a soldier who had decided her war was his.

At half past two, the bell rang inside her ears.

*Come and be counted, Ida,* it said, patient as tide. *Come and be counted. We have kept a place for you. We have kept it a long time.*

And for the first time in five years, Ida did not lie still and pretend to sleep. She sat up in the dark, hugged her knees, and answered it in her head, clearly, in the voice she used for arithmetic and for truth:

*I'm not coming. I've got a table now. Find somebody else.*

The bell rang once more — softer, almost puzzled, like a hand knocking on a door that had never before been answered — and then, beneath the fading tone, Ida heard something new. Something that made the hair rise along her arms and her breath go shallow in her chest.

Down beneath the bell, down beneath the hum of the patient faces, another voice had joined the calling. A man's voice, thin and strained, as though shouting up a very long shaft — and it was not calling her name at all.

It was calling *his.*
