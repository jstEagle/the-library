## 11. Tomas Breaks

The grave had been dug twice.

Tomas Oduya remembered the first time, eleven years ago, when the ground had been soft with October rain and the sexton's spade went through the earth like a spoon through bread. He remembered standing at the lip of it with his hat in his hands and thinking, absurdly, that the hole looked like a doorway someone had left open in a hurry. He remembered Maren's hand on his arm, and Ida—then a baby, then a weight against his shoulder, smelling of milk and wool—and he remembered that he had not cried, because the half-verse was being sung behind him and everyone knew you did not finish anything on burial day. Not the song. Not the grief. You left both open at the middle, like a door, like a mouth, and you walked away from them and did not look back.

That was the rule. That had always been the rule.

The second digging had happened last spring, when the frost heaved the headstone crooked and the ground beneath it sank into a shallow trough, as though the earth were settling around something that had shifted its weight. The sexton had topped up the soil without ceremony, and Tomas had watched him do it, and neither of them had said what they were both thinking: that the ground over Sera Oduya was not resting easy, and that in Hollowmere nothing rested easy, and that this was simply the price of living above the old well the way a man lives above a cellar full of water he has agreed never to look at.

Eleven years. He had kept the rule for eleven years.

He told himself, walking up the churchyard path that grey afternoon with his collar turned up against a wind that smelled of iron and coming rain, that he had not come to break it. He told himself he had only come to clear the moss off the stone, because he was a tidy man and the stone was his wife's name and a name should be legible. He carried a stiff brush in one hand and a jar of water in the other, and if anyone asked—and no one would ask; the village had learned to ask Tomas Oduya nothing—he would say exactly that.

The churchyard was empty. The bell tower stood above it, hollow as a tooth, and the rope hung down inside it, motionless, worn pale and smooth by hands that were mostly gone now. Tomas did not look at the tower. He had spent fifty-eight years in Hollowmere learning not to look at things: the tower, the well, the middle verses, the faces of singers when the silence came down on them like a lid. Not-looking was a skill here, practiced daily, passed down like a recipe. He was very good at it.

He knelt at the grave.

*SERAFINA ODUYA*, said the stone. *BELOVED WIFE AND MOTHER*. And below that, cut small, the line Maren had insisted on, the one the stonemason had argued about for a week because it wasn't scripture and wasn't custom and wasn't safe: *HER SONG IS NOT FINISHED.*

It had been meant as comfort. It had been meant as protection. A verse unfinished is a door unlatched but not opened; a name unsung is a candle unlit. For eleven years Tomas had read those words every Sunday and felt the small cold comfort of them, the way you feel a wall at your back in the dark.

Today he read them and felt something else entirely.

He brushed the moss away. His hands were steady. He wet the stone and scrubbed the lichen from the letters until *SERAFINA* stood out clean and grey and certain, and then he stayed kneeling there, brush in hand, and the wind moved through the yew trees with a sound like a long exhalation, and somewhere far below the churchyard—below the soil, below the clay, below the dry throat of the old well—the village held its breath without knowing it.

"Sera," he said.

His voice came out rough. He cleared his throat and tried again.

"Sera. It's me."

He talked to her sometimes. Everyone in Hollowmere talked to their dead; it was practically agriculture. But he always talked in pieces. *Ida's grown. The roof leaks on the north side. Maren sends her love.* Fragments. Offerings small enough to pass under the lintel of the rule. Never a whole memory, never a whole sentence where a whole sentence might become a whole feeling, and a whole feeling might become—

He knew what it might become. Every child in Hollowmere knew, the way children know fire is hot before they understand why.

But today the fragments would not stay fragments. Today, kneeling in the wet grass with her name clean under his hands, the whole of her rose up in him at once, the way floodwater comes up through a floor all at once instead of seeping, and Tomas Oduya, who had not wept at her funeral, who had not wept at the christening or the harvest or the eleven anniversaries since, felt his chest crack along a seam he had not known was holding him together.

She used to sing while she kneaded bread. That was the first thing. Her forearms dusted white to the elbow, flour in the creases of her knuckles, and her voice low and unhurried, working through the ploughman's rounds and the miller's song and the one about the girl who walked the riverbank—the songs every woman in Hollowmere sang, and every one of them she stopped at the same place, mid-verse, mid-breath, the way they all did, and he had loved even that, loved the little hitch in her, the way the tune went up toward its ending and then folded itself closed like a hand refusing a gift.

She used to hum to Ida at night. She used to laugh with her whole body, tipped back, eyes shut. She used to say his name in the dark—not *Tomas* the way the village said it, flat and functional, a word for passing salt—but softly, twice, the second time lower, like an echo agreeing with the first. Nobody in Hollowmere said a name twice. She'd done it anyway, once, early, before she understood the rules as deeply as her bones came to know them, and he had felt it go through him like warm water, and neither of them had ever spoken of it.

*Tomas. Tomas.*

He heard it now, in the wind, in the yews, in the space between the stones.

And Tomas broke.

It was not dramatic. There was no falling to the knees—he was already kneeling—and no cry that startled birds. It was quieter than that and more total, the way a dam does not explode so much as simply stop pretending. He put both hands flat on the cold granite above her name and he began to sing.

He sang the ploughman's round, the one she sang at the kneading trough, and he sang it past the place where it always stopped. The verse rose toward its ending the way it always did, and the old reflex clamped down on him like a hand on the back of his neck—*stop, stop, you do not finish, you have never finished, no one finishes*—and he sang through the hand. His voice cracked and steadied and cracked again. The words came out of some sealed room in him that had been locked for eleven years, and they were not beautiful. They were ragged and too loud and they scattered a crow off the yew tree, and he did not care, because each word was a stone of her, the flour on her arms, the weight of the baby, the twice-said name in the dark, and he had carried them all this way alone and he could not carry them one more Sunday.

He finished the round. The last note fell into the grass and lay there.

Nothing happened. The wind blew. The tower stood empty. And Tomas, shaking, wiping his face with the heel of his hand, felt—for one unbearable minute—whole. Whole the way he had not been since she died. The grief did not vanish; it settled, the way silt settles, and became for the first time something he could hold instead of something that held him.

"I'll come again Thursday," he told the stone, and his voice was hoarse and ordinary and his own. "I'll bring the brush."

He walked home through the blueing dusk with his collar up, lighter than he had been in eleven years, and if he noticed that the rooks had all gone silent along the lane, or that the air had taken on the taste of deep places, wet stone and old iron, he filed it away among the things a man in Hollowmere agrees not to notice.

Behind him, in the churchyard, the grass over Sera Oduya's grave was trembling, very slightly, like the surface of water.

* * *

Maren knew before he said a word.

He came in stamping the rain off, and Ida looked up from her homework at the kitchen table, and Maren stood at the stove with a wooden spoon gone still in her hand, and the three of them existed for a moment in the particular silence of a house where something has already happened.

"You've been to the churchyard," Maren said.

"Been to clean the stone." He hung his coat on its peg. "Moss on the letters."

"Tomas."

"Moss, Mare."

She turned. She was four years younger than him and had been carrying the parish the way he carried the farm, and she looked at him now with an expression he had seen her wear over dying congregants and flooded fields: tenderness braced for impact. "What did you sing?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped glass. Ida's pencil stopped.

"The round," Tomas said. "The ploughman's."

"All of it?"

He should have lied. Fifty-eight years in Hollowmere, and he knew exactly how the lie went—*the usual, the half, the way we all do it*—and it was right there, ready, cheap as bread. He looked at his sister and then at his daughter, whose face had gone carefully blank in the way children learn blankness in this village before they learn their letters, and he found he could not do it. He had spent the whole afternoon refusing to leave things at the middle. It seemed a poor time to start again.

"All of it," he said. "Every verse. Twice through the chorus."

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them, she said, quietly, "Go and look at the well."

"What?"

"Go and look at the well, Tomas. Now. Before dark."

The old well stood at the end of the lane behind the smithy's ruin, ringed in stones older than the parish register, capped with an iron grate that had been welded shut in 1911 and re-welded twice since. No one went there after dark. No one went there at all, really, except on the two Sundays a year when Maren walked out and laid rowan sprigs on the grate and stood a while with her head bowed, doing whatever it was vicars did instead of admitting what their churches were built on top of.

Tomas took the lantern because Maren's face told him to, and he walked down the lane through the rising rain, and Ida started to follow and Maren caught her shoulder and held her, gently, absolutely, at the door.

The well was where it had always been. The grate was shut. The stones were black with wet.

And the sound was wrong.

There should have been no sound. The well had been dry within living memory—dry as a dead lung, that was the whole point of it, that was the bargain the village had made in 1911 when they'd melted their own bell and given the fire a ringer instead. Dry, and silent, and capped. But as Tomas crouched at the ring with the lantern held out, he heard, beneath the rain, beneath the wind, a sound he had heard only once before in his life, the day they buried Sera: a low, wet, patient shifting, like something turning over in sleep.

He lowered the lantern through the bars.

Far below—farther than the light should have reached, farther than the well had any business being deep—the beam touched water. Black, still, and rising. He could see the old depth-marks cut into the shaft's inner wall, the ones from before, the ones the archive photographs showed: *VII, VIII, IX*. The water stood at nine. Eleven years ago it had been dust.

As he watched, the surface shivered—though no rain reached it down there—and climbed, slow and smooth, past the base of the ninth mark, and stopped, waiting, the way a tide waits between waves.

Tomas sat back on his heels. The lantern shook in his fist and threw his shadow huge and broken across the wet stones.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, Sera. What have I done."

He knew what he had done. He had known since the churchyard, in the part of him that had sung anyway, that would sing it again, that would sing it every Thursday until he died. That was the horror of it and the truth of it, the thing Maren would try to make him understand and fail: the count did not run on malice. It ran on love. It had always run on love. Grief was the toll and wholeness was the payment and the well drank both, and he had walked up that path with his wife's name in his hands and paid without flinching, gladly, and the water had risen to meet him like a guest taking a offered chair.

He walked back up the lane slowly, and the rain came down harder, and behind him the well made its low patient sound, filling.

* * *

By midnight, the whole village knew.

It moved the way bad news moves in Hollowmere, which is to say silently and completely, porch to porch, lamplit window to lamplit window, without a single raised voice. People came out with lanterns they did not pretend were for anything but watching the well. They stood in the rain at a respectful distance, forty strong, and no one spoke, and the water climbed. Past ten. Toward eleven. The marks had never been meant to measure anything but themselves; no one alive knew what number ended the measuring. That knowledge had gone into the furnace in 1911 with the bell.

Maren stood at the grate with her cassock soaked through, and Tomas stood beside her because she had not sent him away, and Ida stood between them with her hand buried in her father's coat because no one had thought to tell her to stay indoors and no one had the heart to say it now.

"It was the round," Tomas said to the darkness, to Maren, to the water. "Her round. I sang it whole. I'm sorry. God forgive me, Mare, I'd sing it again."

"I know," Maren said.

"That's the worst of it. That's the thing I can't—" His voice went to pieces and he let it. "Eleven years I left her at the middle. Every song, every memory, everything we were—I kept it all half-done because they told us that's how you keep them safe, that's how you keep *her* safe, and tonight I finished it, just once, just to feel like a married man again for five minutes, and the well drank it down like it was owed."

"It was owed," said a voice from the crowd.

They turned. Old Mrs. Callow, ninety if she was a day, wrapped in oilcloth, her face under her hood as calm as a stone in a stream. "It was always owed, Tomas. We've just been very good, for a hundred years, at not paying. You paid. The water rose. That's not wickedness. That's arithmetic." She looked at the grate, and something in her ancient face flickered—pity, or memory, or fear. "The question isn't what you did. The question is what happens when the rest of us start remembering how good it felt."

No one answered her. The rain filled the silence.

And then Ida said, in a small clear voice that cut through the rain like the bell used to:

"Papa. It knows your name now."

Tomas looked down at his daughter. Her face was pale and wet and terribly awake, the face of a child who has been hearing something for a long time and has finally decided the adults need to hear it too.

"It's been saying it since the churchyard," Ida said. "Under the rain. It says it the way Mama used to." Her hand tightened in his coat. "Twice."

Down the lane, behind the grate, far below the reach of any lantern, the water in the old well went on rising—past eleven, unhurried, patient as a debt—and somewhere in the dark beneath Hollowmere, a number scratched into stone grew by one, and waited to grow by more.
