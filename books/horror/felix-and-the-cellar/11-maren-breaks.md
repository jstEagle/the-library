## 11. Maren Breaks

The kitchen at midnight is the only honest room in the house.

Maren Holloway has thought this for years. The kitchen keeps its dimensions. The kitchen doesn't padlock itself or breathe through its walls or add a step to the staircase when it thinks no one is watching. The kitchen is where she does the math—the terrible, nightly arithmetic of her life—and the kitchen never argues with the total.

Tonight she is not alone in it.

She stands in the doorway with two cups of tea she won't drink and watches Coral Eriksen hunched over his laptop at her table, surrounded by the small white architecture of his profession: printed floor plans, a laser measure lying on the wood like a surgical instrument, a stack of photographs fanned out and weighted down with salt shakers because the house's drafts keep moving them. He has been working since ten o'clock. He has not looked up in an hour. His lips move as he types—she can see him dictating to himself, that low mutter of numbers and timestamps—and every few minutes he glances at the cellar door as if checking whether it's still shut.

It is still shut. It is always still shut until it isn't.

She should let him work. She should let him finish his report, take his check, and drive away tomorrow morning with his tidy conclusions about deferred maintenance and market comparables, and never think about this house again except as a line item in someone else's ledger. That was the plan. That had always been the plan with the surveyors, the inspectors, the appraisers who came before him: be boring, be brief, be forgettable. Get them out before the house gets interested.

But the recorder is sitting on her counter, charging beside the toaster, and she saw his face when he played it back this afternoon. She heard the second voice through the thin wall of the mudroom—dry, patient, unhurried as water finding a level—and she watched Coral Eriksen go white in a way that no appraisal discrepancy could account for.

He knows now. Or he knows enough. And a man who knows enough will either run or start asking questions, and Maren Holloway has spent six years learning which questions she cannot survive answering.

She sets the tea down across from him. He startles—actually flinches, hand going flat against his chest—and she realizes he didn't hear her come in. Of course he didn't. She has gotten very good at not being heard. It turns out it's a skill you can practice in your own home.

"Sorry," she says. "I didn't mean to sneak up."

"You didn't." He closes the laptop halfway, then all the way, as if the screen might read over his shoulder. "I was just—" He gestures at nothing. "Deep in it."

"The numbers again."

"The numbers." He laughs, but there's no humor in it, just air escaping. "Mrs. Holloway, can I ask you something? And I want you to know you don't have to answer. I've been doing this long enough to know when a client's silence is an answer."

Maren sits down across from him. The chair legs scrape, and somewhere below the floor—or inside the floor; she has stopped pretending there's a difference—something shifts its weight.

"Ask," she says.

"The hallway." He taps the closed laptop. "I measured it eleven times today. Eleven passes, same instrument, same starting point, calibrated twice. I got nine different lengths. The variance isn't random—it's not instrument error, it's not thermal expansion, it's not settling. It's like the hallway is deciding how long it wants to be." He looks up at her, and his eyes are red-rimmed and steady and terribly, professionally awake. "And when I cross-checked my notes from Monday against today's, the square footage of the whole house has grown. Four hundred and twelve square feet. Since Monday."

He waits. She lets the silence sit between them like a third person at the table.

"Iris draws on her floor," he says quietly. "Chalk marks. Little tally lines around the perimeter of her room. I saw them when I photographed the bedrooms. I assumed it was a kid thing. But they're precise. They're evenly spaced. And there are fresh ones over old ones, worn ones underneath, going back—I'd guess years." His voice drops. "So the room knows where she is. That's what she told me. So the room knows where she is."

The kettle ticks as it cools. Down in the dark under their feet, the house listens with the patience of something that has been listening since before any of them were born.

"Yes," Maren says.

Coral exhales. Not relief—nothing so kind. More like a man setting down a weight he's been carrying alone, only to find the ground beneath him is also giving way.

"How long have you known?"

* * *

How long. She almost laughs. How long have you known the water is wet, Mrs. Holloway? How long have you known that winter follows fall?

"I grew up forty miles from here," she says. "In Corrick. You'd have driven through it to get here—no, you wouldn't have. There's no reason to drive through Corrick anymore. The highway bypassed it in '98 and the town just... folded up behind it." She wraps her hands around the cooling cup. "My grandmother lived on the Holloway road. My mother told me things about this property the way mothers tell children not to swim after eating. Rules without reasons. Never answer after dark. Never count out loud on the stairs. If you hear knocking, let it knock. If it asks your name, it already knows it, and saying it back is a gift."

"And you believed her?"

"I believed her the way you believe weather reports. Abstractly. Right up until the storm." Maren looks at the cellar door. From here it's just a door—white paint, brass knob, the padlock throwing a small shadow in the nightlight's glow. "Jonas grew up in this house. Did you know that? Everyone assumes we bought it. No. It's been his family's since 1911. It came to him the way a debt comes to you. He used to joke about it. *The family homestead,* he'd say, *free and clear.*" Her voice catches on the word *free*. "His grandfather kept the cellar locked. His father kept it locked. Jonas kept it locked. Nobody ever said why, out loud, in words. You just did it. Like you don't walk on ice in November."

Coral has gone very still. She recognizes the stillness—he's been doing it for days, the stillness of a man trying to become furniture—but tonight it looks less like a technique and more like fear wearing professionalism as a coat.

"Your husband," Coral says. "The listing says the sale is part of an estate settlement."

"It says whatever I told the lawyer to make it say." She drinks the cold tea anyway. It tastes like tannin and honesty. "Jonas died four years ago. March. The official cause was a fall down the cellar stairs. Which is true, in the way that a headline is true. He did fall down the stairs. What the coroner couldn't explain was why a sober man—a careful man, a man who had lived with those stairs for thirty-eight years and knew every one of them—would be standing at the top of them at three in the morning with the padlock already open in his hand."

She hears Coral's breath stop.

"He wasn't sober," she says. "That's the part I've never told anyone. Not the lawyer, not Iris, God help me, not anyone. Jonas had been drinking. Not falling-down drunk—worse. Loose drunk. Happy drunk. The kind of drunk where a man forgets every rule his grandmother ever taught him and starts feeling friendly toward the world." Her hands are shaking now. She lets them. "I woke up because I heard him talking. Standing at the top of the cellar stairs in his socks, having a conversation. Just chatting. Warm. Amused. And I heard—" She stops. Six years, and she has never said this sentence aloud. "I heard the other one answer him. In a voice like dry paper. And Jonas laughed, and said, *sure, yeah, I'm Jonas. Jonas Holloway.*"

The nightlight flickers. Neither of them moves.

"And it took him," Coral says. Not a question.

"It collected him." She makes herself say it plainly, because euphemism is a luxury this house does not permit. "He went down those stairs like a man answering a dinner bell. I got to the top of them maybe four seconds too late. Four seconds. I have done the math on those four seconds every single day for six years—if I'd grabbed his arm, if I'd shouted his name—no. Not his name. You don't shout a name in this house. If I'd grabbed his arm." She sets the cup down before she cracks it. "They found him at the bottom. Broken. And smiling. That's the detail that broke me, Mr. Eriksen. He was smiling. Whatever it gave him at the end, it was worth the price of admission, and I have hated him a little bit ever since for that, and I have hated myself more for hating him."

The clock on the stove reads 12:47. It has read 12:47 for what feels like a long time.

* * *

"Why stay?" Coral asks, and his voice is gentle now, the gentleness of a man who has already guessed. "After that. With two kids. Why not leave?"

Maren laughs—a short, awful sound.

"Because I tried."

She tells him about the rental house in Ashford, eighteen months after the funeral. A nice little place near the school, month-to-month, nothing fancy. She tells him how the first week was fine. How the second week, Dov started sleepwalking—not in the new house, but *toward* it, standing at the front door at 3 a.m. in his pajamas with his little hand on the knob, saying *yes, yes, okay* to nobody. How Iris began waking up screaming that the rooms were wrong, that the bedroom was bigger than it should be, that something was counting her through the walls. How Maren herself would find chalk dust on her own fingertips in the mornings and have no memory of drawing anything.

"And then one night," she says, "I heard it. In Ashford. Eighteen miles from this house. I heard the voice in the hallway of a rented duplex, asking Dov—in his sleep, sweet as syrup—asking him to say his name. Just once. Just for the record." She meets Coral's eyes. "You understand? It doesn't live in the house. The house is just where it keeps its books. We're what it owns. And the count follows the counted."

"So you came back."

"So I came back. Because here, at least, there are rules. Here, at least, Iris figured out the system—God knows how, she was seven, eight years old, and she mapped it out like a general. The chalk marks. The name rules. Never answer, never count aloud, never sign anything you don't have to sign. She's kept us alive, Mr. Eriksen. My twelve-year-old daughter has been keeping this family alive while I made dinners and went to work and pretended we were grieving normally." Her voice frays. "Do you know what that does to a mother? To watch your child build fortifications out of sidewalk chalk and know that they're load-bearing?"

Coral doesn't answer. He's looking at her with an expression she can't fully read—pity, yes, but underneath it something harder and more familiar. Recognition. As if she's described something he lived through once, in another house, in another childhood.

"Maren," he says—first time he's used her first name—"the file. The probate file from 1911. It's sealed. Court-ordered. Do you know what's in it?"

"I know what my mother-in-law told me before she died. Five children. The first Holloways. Five kids gone in a single season, and the mother left holding nothing but a voice in the cellar that *kept accounts.*" She recites it flatly, a catechism learned by heart. "And I know what she did about it. Because we've all done versions of it. She gave it a name instead of five. Fed the ledger one entry so it would stop collecting the rest."

Something moves behind Coral's face—she sees it land, sees the idea take root and grow thorns—and she suddenly understands, with a lurch, that this man is not thinking about leaving. This man is thinking about *bargaining.*

No. Absolutely not.

"Listen to me." She leans across the table and takes his wrist—his pulse is hammering, poor man, he's terrified and fascinated in equal measure, and that combination is exactly what killed Jonas. "Whatever you're thinking right now, whatever clever loophole you think you've found in six days of measurements—you put it down. You finish your report. You write *structurally sound, minor settling, fair market value,* and you get in your car and you drive until the mirrors don't show this road anymore."

"Maren—"

"You want to know the terrible math?" Her voice cracks and she lets it. Shame and exhaustion have been waiting six years for this table, this witness, this hour. "Here it is. Every person who stays is a payment. Every person who leaves takes the debt with them. Jonas paid. My mother-in-law paid. Five children paid in 1911 and their mother spent the rest of her life paying interest. I have spent six years doing arithmetic at this table—how many of us, how many names, how many nights until the count comes due—and the number never goes down, Mr. Eriksen. It only goes up. And now—" her voice drops to a whisper, because the house is listening, the house is always listening— "now it counts four again. Me, Iris, Dov. And you."

The word lands between them like a dropped glass.

"Me," Coral repeats.

"You slept here four nights. You talked to it through a recorder. You asked it questions and it answered." She grips his wrist harder. "You think I haven't seen this before? Surveyors, inspectors, buyers—two offers fell through, did I tell you that? Two families signed and then withdrew, and both times the husband called me afterward, whispering, asking me insane questions about square footage. Both times the house had gotten *interested.* And both times they got out fast enough, and the count stayed at three, and we survived the season." She finally lets go of his arm. "You are not a buyer, Mr. Eriksen. Buyers look and leave. You're a measurer. And it called you that, didn't it? On the recording. I heard it. *The measurer.*"

Down in the cellar, soft as a page turning, something agrees.

Coral sits back. For a long moment he says nothing, and Maren watches him do the thing she has watched him do all week: retreat into procedure, into documentation, into the religion of numbers that can be verified. But his hands aren't steady, and when he speaks, the professional calm is a coat two sizes too small.

"I'll finish the report," he says. "Tomorrow. Tonight, I need—there's a clerk in the county office who owes me a favor, and there's a sealed file from 1911, and I need to see it before I write another word."

"That's not finishing the report. That's digging."

"Yes," he agrees mildly. "It is."

"Coral." She uses his name deliberately, a weapon and a plea. "People who dig in this house don't come back up. Ask my husband."

He gathers his papers slowly, squaring the edges, aligning the photographs, and for a moment he is only a tired man tidying a workspace. Then he looks at her, and there is something in his face she hasn't seen before—something older than fear, something that looks almost like recognition of a debt.

"My father used to lose count of me," he says, so quietly she nearly misses it. "When he was angry. I learned how to be uncounted. Unlisted, unphotographed, unremembered. I survived a childhood by making sure no record of me existed anywhere." He slides the laptop into his bag. "So when your voice tried to fix my details this week—my name, my age, my height—it kept getting them wrong. It's been getting them wrong all week. Because I don't stay written down, Mrs. Holloway. I never have."

Maren stares at him. The kettle ticks. The clock holds its impossible minute.

"What are you telling me?" she asks.

He shoulders his bag and heads for the mudroom, and at the door he pauses, half-turned, lit by the nightlight's amber glow.

"I'm telling you that for the first time in my life," Coral Eriksen says, "being unrecorded might be the thing that saves everyone else instead of me. And I need to know what happened in 1911 before I decide what that's worth."

The cellar door rattles once, softly, like a throat clearing.

Then the padlock—locked, bolted, six years unopened—slides open on its own, and the darkness inside begins, patiently, to count.
