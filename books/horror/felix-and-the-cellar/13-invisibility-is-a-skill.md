## 13. Invisibility Is a Skill

The winter Coral turned nine, his father broke his left hand on the kitchen doorframe, and Coral learned the first rule: never flinch where it can be seen.

Not never flinch. That was impossible. The body flinched the way the body breathed. But you could choose the geometry of it. You could flinch into a corner, into shadow, into the blind spot behind the refrigerator's open door. You could make your pain architecturally inconvenient, so that by the time his father's eyes tracked toward the sound of the impact, Coral was already somewhere the light didn't reach, and his father would stand in the kitchen breathing hard, fist throbbing, looking at an empty room with the expression of a man who has lost his place in a book.

His mother called it hiding. Coral came to understand it was something else. Hiding was temporary, deliberate, a game with an end. What Coral did was closer to erasure. He practiced it the way other children practiced piano, thirty minutes a day, scales of absence.

The second rule came in February, when the school photographer came and his father, drunk and expansive in a way that always preceded damage, insisted on being in the frame. Coral stood at the edge of the family portrait and made himself thin. Not physically—he couldn't do anything about his body, all elbows and collarbones—but photographically. He softened his focus the way you soften your gaze at an optometrist's chart, let the camera take a picture of the space he was occupying rather than the boy inside it. When the prints came back, his father's face was a red sun of rage and his mother's smile was a held breath, and Coral was a smear at the margin, a motion blur, as if he had been leaving the frame even as the shutter fell. His father squinted at it, turned it over, squinted again.

"Where's the boy?"

"Right there," his mother said.

His father looked at the smear. He looked at the hallway, where Coral stood with his back against the wallpaper, holding still in the way that made him part of the house. His father looked directly at him for four full seconds—Coral counted, counting was safe, counting was math and math was a room his father couldn't enter—and then his father's eyes slid off him like water off wax, and he said, "Huh," and went to the den, and that was the night Coral understood he had found something.

Not a talent. A skill. Skills could be drilled.

* * *

By eleven he had a curriculum.

Exercise one: the doorway drill. He would stand in a doorway while his father watched television, and he would practice being furniture. Weight on both feet. Shoulders dissolved. Eyes aimed at nothing, at the middle distance where no one keeps their attention. The trick, he discovered, was not stillness—stillness drew the eye the way a dead bird draws crows. The trick was *unremarkability*. He learned to hold his body in the exact posture of a person no one would ever ask a question of. He studied how his mother stood when she wanted his father to pass her by without comment, and he copied it, refined it, made it his own.

Exercise two: the name drill. This was harder. His father would sometimes come home and stand in the living room and call out names—not Coral's name, never Coral's name, that was the point, but names, testing the house the way you tap a wall to find the studs. "Sandra." His mother. "Sandra." Silence. And Coral, upstairs or under the stairs or in the closet with his flashlight and his library book, would practice not existing in response. Because a name was a hook, and the body answered hooks without permission. Someone called your name and your diaphragm jumped, your mouth opened, your whole self leaned toward the sound like a plant toward a window. Coral trained the reflex out of himself. He had his mother call his name from behind him, over and over, evenings, in the kitchen, her voice low and tired, until he could hear *Coral, Coral, Coral* and feel nothing in his body at all. Not a flicker. She would stop sometimes and look at him with an expression he couldn't read then and can read now, thirty years later, in the dark of the Holloway farmhouse, listening to a dry voice in a recorder recite names down a stairwell. The expression was grief. She was watching her son learn to be un-summoned, and she knew what it cost, because she had paid a version of the price herself, and she knew the skill kept him alive and she knew it was killing something else, something with no name, something she would have called his soul if she'd been a woman who used that word.

Exercise three: the paper drill. This was the one that mattered most, though he didn't know why for years.

His father worked for the county. Roads and bridges. A small man with a small title and a large rage, the kind of man who is nobody in every room except his own, and who makes his own room a country with borders patrolled by fists. And because he was county, he understood records. He understood that a person is a file, a person is a line in a ledger, a person is an address where an authority can find you. When Coral was twelve, his father decided the family would have no more photographs—*why give them your face*, he said, *why give them anything*—and Coral, who had been unphotographable for two years by then, felt a strange kinship he would spend decades trying to scrub out. His father's paranoia and Coral's survival had the same architecture. They were both trying to be unrecorded. The difference was that his father wanted to be unrecorded so he could do what he did in the dark, and Coral wanted to be unrecorded so he could survive what was done in the dark, and the difference between those two things is the whole distance between a predator and prey, and yet the skill, the actual skill, was identical. Coral hated that. He hated it the way you hate a family resemblance.

He took the paper drill further than his father ever would have. When the school sent home forms, he lost them. Not carelessly—carelessness was a signature, and signatures could be traced. He lost them with the clean randomness of true entropy. He misspelled his own name on tests, differently each time: Corral, Coral Eriksen, C. Erikson, Cora Elsen. He gave the census taker a wrong birthday and a wrong middle initial and watched, with the detached interest of a young scientist, as the error propagated into the record like a dropped stitch propagating through a sweater. By fifteen, Coral Eriksen existed in the county's files as four or five partial, contradictory boys, none of them whole enough to be a person, none of them findable. He was, in the language he would later use professionally, an unresolvable parcel. A lot with disputed boundaries. A property whose deed did not match its ground.

* * *

The cost arrived on schedule, the way costs do.

You cannot practice erasure for six years without erasing. At sixteen, Coral stood in the school cafeteria and realized he could not remember his own face. Not metaphorically—he had seen mirrors, of course, but he had trained himself out of the mirror the way he'd trained himself out of everything, and now, trying to summon his own features the way you'd summon a phone number, he got static. He knew he was thin. He knew his hair was brown. Everything else was a smear at the margin of a photograph. He had made himself into the thing his father couldn't see, and it turned out his father's eyes had been the only mirror he'd had, because a child knows himself first through being seen, and Coral had spent six years making sure no one saw him, and now no one had, including Coral.

He sat in the cafeteria with his tray and understood, with the cold clarity that would later make him a good surveyor, that he had built a house with no door on the inside. He could keep anything out. He could keep nothing in. He did not know what he liked. He did not know what he wanted. When teachers asked—the rare ones, the kind ones, the young English teacher who looked at him like he was a locked room she wanted to open—*what do you want, Coral?*—he felt the question the way his body had once felt his father's footsteps on the stairs: as pressure, as threat, as a demand to be located. Wanting was a coordinate. Wanting fixed you on a map. He had spent his whole childhood refusing to be fixed, and now the refusal was total, and he was nowhere, and nowhere is not a place a person can live.

His mother found him that spring, sitting in the garage in the dark, not hiding—there was nothing to hide from, his father was at a union meeting—and she sat down next to him on the cold concrete and didn't touch him, because touch was a coordinate too, and she had learned that much from watching him.

"You have to be somebody someday," she said. "The skill is for the storm. You can't live in the storm forever, and you can't live in the skill either."

"I don't know how to stop," Coral said. It was the most honest sentence he had spoken aloud in years, and it came out in his own voice, which startled him, because he'd half forgotten what it sounded like.

"You pick a thing," she said. "One thing. And you let it know you. You let it write your name down. That's how you start."

He picked measurement. He was seventeen, and he picked it because measurement was attention without intimacy, because a tape measure looked at a wall and the wall didn't look back, because numbers were a language that described the world without claiming it. He could devote his life to recording things, and as long as the things were walls and lots and easements, the recording would never turn around and record *him*. He would be the man behind the instrument. The laser finds the corner; the corner does not find the man.

He told himself that for thirty years. It was almost true for most of them.

* * *

Now, in the Holloway house, on the ninth night, Coral lies on the mattress in the back bedroom with the recorder running and understands, at last, why the dry voice has trouble with him.

He has listened to the playback four times now. The voice recites names—Maren, Iris, Dov, and *the measurer*—but when it reaches his, it hesitates. He has the recording. He has played the three seconds of hesitation over and over in the dark, and it sounds like a man reading a ledger and finding a line where the ink has smeared. *The measurer,* it says, and then a pause, a dry, papery pause, and then, softer, almost to itself: *the measurer, the measurer, which one, the one who was not written down.*

It cannot fix his details. That is why the hallway gives him different numbers on every pass—not the house lying to him, not exactly, but the house trying to *measure him back* and getting the same smear at the margin he spent his childhood manufacturing. He is unresolvable. He is the dropped stitch. Every skill he built to survive his father is now the reason a thing in a cellar keeps reaching for him and closing on smoke.

And he understands something else, lying there, something that arrives with the specific dread of a surveyor who finds that his benchmark has been set in moving sand. The skill has a flaw he never noticed, because he never had a reason to test it. Invisibility works against eyes. It works against cameras, against clerks, against fathers on stairs. But the voice does not use eyes. The voice uses *names*. And a name is not a photograph. A name is a hook, and he trained the flinch out of his body, yes, he can hear *Coral* and feel nothing—but the voice does not need his body to flinch. It only needs the name to be *true*. And somewhere in a county file, in a probate record sealed by court order, in a ledger older than any county file, the name is already written. He made himself illegible to the living. He never once thought to check whether he was legible to the dead.

The recorder hisses. Down the hall, a door he does not remember being open stands open, six inches, the width of a listening ear.

And from below—from below, through the floor, through the joists, through the cold—Coral hears it begin, patient as arithmetic: not his name, not yet, but the counting. *One. Two. Three. Four.* A pause, the length of a held breath, the length of a smear at the margin of a photograph.

Then, in a voice like dry paper turning: *Five.*
