**EXTRACT — PROBATE FILE NO. 1911-0447, ASHCOMBE COUNTY COURT**

*Sealed by order of Hon. E. T. Marsh, Judge of Probate, 22 November 1911. Unsealed 1911–present only by court order.*

*Transcription of testimony given 18 October 1911:*

Q: State your name for the record.

A: Sarah Holloway. Widow of Abraham.

Q: Mrs. Holloway, you are the mother of six children. Is that correct?

A: I am the mother of one child.

Q: The court's records indicate five children residing at the property as recently as June of this year. Can you account for their absence?

A: They are not absent. They are counted.

Q: I don't understand your meaning.

A: There is something under the house that keeps accounts. It has kept them since before we came. My husband thought it was rats in the walls, then water in the stones, then he thought it was God, and then he stopped thinking anything at all because it asked him his name on a night when he had been drinking, and he answered.

Q: You are telling this court that your five children have been—what? Taken?

A: Recorded. It is not the same thing. If they were taken, they would be somewhere. These are nowhere. I have looked in every room. The rooms are smaller than they were. I measured.

Q: You measured.

A: With my husband's tape. The house was larger in June. It is exactly large enough now for me and my daughter Ruth and no one else. Do you understand what I am telling you? The house knows how many people it holds. It adjusts itself to hold no more than it holds and no fewer. When there were seven of us it was a big house. Now it is a small one. It will not let me make it bigger by wishing.

Q: Mrs. Holloway, the court is prepared to consider charges of—

A: I know what you are prepared to consider. Write down what I say instead. It wanted five names and I could not give it five names, so I gave it one name instead. I told it that Ruth was all of them. That she was Abraham, and Josiah, and the twins, and little Peter, all folded into one girl. And it considered this. It is still considering. It considers everything slowly, like a clerk with bad light. But Ruth sleeps in her room and her room stays her size, and I believe—I need this court to believe—that as long as no one in that house ever answers when it asks after dark, and never counts aloud on the stairs, we will remain uncollected.

Q: This testimony is not credible.

A: Then seal it. Seal it and sell the house to some family that doesn't read.

*[Note appended in clerk's hand:] File sealed per judicial order. Property listed for sale spring 1912.*

**LETTER, GIDEON MERCER TO HIS BROTHER, THOMAS MERCER, DETROIT**

*Undated fragment, ca. November 1914. Held in Mercer Family Papers, Box 6.*

Tom —

You ask why we don't come home for Christmas. I'll tell you plain, though you'll laugh. The house won't let us plan past the season. Not won't allow — allows everything. But every plan I write down comes back wrong. I wrote a list of what to pack and when I read it back it was a list of our names, each one written twice, and at the bottom a line I don't remember writing that said *and one more*. Ada says burn it. I burned it. The ash was cold before it hit the stove.

The previous owner left a Bible in the pantry shelf with dates written in the front. Births, deaths. Under deaths, in different ink, someone has written *not died — filed*. I have shown it to Reverend Hale and he has asked me not to bring it again.

We are well. The children are well. Tell Mother the house is warm and dry and the land is good. All of this is true. It is also true that last Tuesday I found the hallway four feet longer than it was on Monday, and I did not measure it twice, because the first time I measured it, it grew while the tape was out, patient as a plant, and I heard — Tom, I heard something in the cellar counting along with me. Under its breath. Checking my work.

Don't come for Christmas. Don't write back about this letter. Come in summer, in daylight, and bring nothing with writing on it.

Your brother,
Gideon

**EXTRACT — LAST WILL AND TESTAMENT OF HARLAN VOSS**

*Dated 2 September 1936. Probate 1936-118.*

Item the Third: To whoever takes the house, which I hope is nobody and fear is somebody: it does not want much. It wants to be included. Talk to it the way you'd talk to a hired man who eats alone. Say good morning through the floor if it suits you. Never give it your name whole. A nickname will do. It cannot file what it cannot spell.

I have lived twenty years in that house and it has never taken a thing from me, because I never gave it anything with my name attached. Let my estate pass to the county. Let the county's lawyers deal with it. Lawyers' paper is thick. Maybe it can't read through it.

*[Marginal note, probate clerk's hand:]* Testamentary disposition of real property to the County rejected; County declined conveyance by resolution of the Board, 19 Oct 1936, no reason recorded. Property sold at auction.

**DIARY OF PEARL DANNETT**

*Entries selected. Held in Dannett Family Papers, donated 1989.*

**June 3, 1937.** Moved in. Elmer nailed a horseshoe over the cellar door and I laughed at him but I was glad of it too. The cellar door locks from the outside. Was it built that way? Who builds a door that only locks against the inside? We asked the auction man and he said ask the county and the county said ask the last owner and the last owner is dead, so. Locked it stays.

**September 19, 1937.** Baby due in March. Elmer has painted the nursery yellow. Last night I woke and heard him in the hall saying numbers under his breath. Counting steps, he said. I said you don't count steps at three in the morning, Elmer Dannett, and he looked at me like a man caught stealing. He said the house gets restless if you don't count the stairs right. I said who told you that. He said nobody. He said he just knew, the way you know to swallow. I am going to write to my sister about coming to stay when the baby comes. I find I want witnesses.

**March 8, 1938.** Robert born, seven pounds, healthy. The night he was born the clocks all stopped at the same minute — Elmer swears it, and the kitchen clock and the parlor clock and even my father's pocket watch, which was in a drawer. They all read 3:33 and none of them has run right since. Elmer says the house was marking the occasion. I told him houses don't mark occasions. He said this one counts occasions. He has started leaving a saucer of milk by the cellar door. I have not stopped him. I am ashamed to write why: because the nights he leaves it, nobody in this house says numbers in their sleep.

**October 30, 1944.** Robert went to war today. Sixteen years old in that house and never once sick, never once hurt, and I know now it isn't luck. You raise a child in a house like this and you teach him without teaching him: don't answer after dark, don't count aloud, don't write your full name on your schoolbooks. Robert writes *R. Dannett* on everything. His teacher sent a note home asking why. I sent a note back saying it is our custom. It is our custom. It is the whole of our custom and it has kept him alive.

**April 12, 1961.** Elmer passed in his sleep, peaceful, sixty-eight years old. I sat with him till morning. Around dawn I heard, very faint, from below the floor, a sound like a page turning. Like a great ledger being closed on a finished entry. I have decided to think of it as respect. A man can decide these things. A woman alone in a house decides them or goes mad.

**June 1, 1961.** Listed the farm with Hollis & Son. The agent asked why the price. I told him: the house costs what it costs. Take it or leave it. He took it.

Claimant: June Ostrander

Loss: Water damage, main hall flooring

Adjuster's notes, 14 May 1988:

Odd one. Claim is straightforward — pipe burst, floors buckled — but claimant insisted I walk the house with her and "confirm the measurements" before she'd sign. Confirmed. She then asked me to confirm them again. And again. Four times total. Each time the tape gave the same figure and each time she looked less comforted. On the fourth pass she said, quietly, "It's holding still for you. It likes paper people." I didn't ask. Signed her out same day. Recommend expedited processing. Some claims you just want off your desk.

**FRAGMENT — SCHOOL ASSIGNMENT, GRADE 5, VERRIN ELEMENTARY**

*Retrieved from district archives, misc. folder, undated ca. 2002.*

**My House** by Iris Holloway, age 10 [sic — see correction below]

My house is old. My dad says it is older than it looks and my mom says it is older than that. My house has a cellar with a lock on the outside of the door. My dad says the lock is to keep the cold in. I think it is to keep the counting in. My house is bigger when we have visitors. My grandma visited in July and I measured the hallway with my ruler and it was longer than in June. When she left it got short again. My house keeps track of everybody. I keep track of my house too. Every night I draw chalk lines around my bed so my room knows where I am. If the room knows where I am it doesn't have to guess. Guessing is how people get filed.

*[Teacher's red pen:]* Iris — creative and detailed! But check your facts: you wrote your age as 10 and your birthdate makes you 9. Small errors matter on official work. — Mrs. Pell

*[Second annotation, different pen, small careful letters in the margin:]* She wasn't wrong. She was never once wrong. — M.H.
