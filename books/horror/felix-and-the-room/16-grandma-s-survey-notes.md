## 16. Grandma's Survey Notes

The transcription sat on the kitchen table like a patient with a bad prognosis.

Wren had spread it out the way he spread out any difficult survey — corners weighted with whatever was at hand: a coffee mug, a stapler, Odile's hairbrush that she'd lent him without being asked and which he was afraid to touch now for reasons he couldn't articulate. Four sheets of survey paper, covered in his own cramped notation. Three hundred and eleven years of trapped residents distilled into fragments: three notes from a mailman who'd caught them in 1987 while delivering a registered letter nobody signed for, two bars hummed through a wall by a woman named Prudence whose whole existence was now a water stain in the upstairs bathroom, a phrase whistled by a boy of maybe nineteen who'd come to the house in 1953 to ask a girl to a dance and had never left the porch.

Two bars missing.

He'd been over it forty times. He'd been over it the way you go over a boundary line when the deed doesn't match the ground — walking it again and again, hoping the discrepancy will resolve itself out of sheer professional stubbornness. It didn't. The fifth verse had a hole in it the size of a heartbeat, right before the end, and the house knew it. He could feel the house knowing it, the way you can feel a client knowing their foundation is cracked and waiting to see if you'll find it.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said from the doorway.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stare at paper like it owes you money." She came in carrying the teapot she'd insisted on keeping even though the house kept hiding it, and poured two cups with the economy of a woman who had made tea under worse conditions than a haunted kitchen. "You've been at it since dawn. The house moved the pantry twice last night just to watch you not notice."

"I noticed."

"You didn't. You walked into the coat closet and apologized to it." She set a cup beside his elbow. "Drink. Then tell me what you're not finding."

Wren drank. The tea tasted like chamomile and faintly of 1961, which he'd stopped remarking on around day four. "The missing bars," he said. "They're not anywhere. I've asked every resident who can still hold a thought longer than a hum. I've been through the records room twice. The house sang the verse during signings — that's how everyone learned it — but nobody ever heard the end. They always joined in first. Always. Sixty years of people, and not one of them heard how it finishes."

"Because the song wants company," Odile said. "It's the loneliest thing I've ever met. It can't bear to sing alone, so it leaves itself open — a door in the melody — and whoever's listening walks through it." Her hands were steady around her cup. Her hands were always steady; it was her eyes that did the trembling. "I told you. I tried in 1974. I got further than anyone. I heard—" She stopped, the way you stop at a door you've decided never to open again.

"You heard almost all of it."

"I heard enough to know there *was* an end. And then my daughter sat down in the chair." She said it flatly, a fact filed away so long it had gone smooth as river stone. "So. Two bars short. What does your profession say about that?"

Wren looked down at the transcription. And because he couldn't answer her, and because looking at her was worse than looking at the paper, he did what he always did when a problem wouldn't crack: he went back to the beginning.

* * *

His grandmother's notes were in the attic.

Not the folded-house attic, with its decades stacked like sediment — the real attic, the ordinary one above the ordinary kitchen, reachable by an ordinary ladder that creaked in a way Wren found, these days, almost comforting. The house let him up there. He'd figured out early that the house permitted access to anything it wasn't currently using as bait.

The box was where he'd found it in chapter one of this whole disaster — no. Where he'd found it on his first appraisal walk-through, back when he'd still believed in pipes and settling wood. A metal document case, rust freckling the latches, stenciled on the side in fading paint: **E. KAVANAGH — SURVEY RECORDS**.

Eleanor Kavanagh. Grandma El. Who had taught him to read contour lines before he could read chapter books. Who had a rule about singing indoors that he'd obeyed for thirty years without once asking why, because her voice when she said it wasn't stern — it was scared, and child-Wren had understood instinctively that scared grown-ups needed rules more than explanations.

He'd read these notes before. In those first hours inside the folded house, before he understood what he was inside, he'd flipped through them looking for anything useful and found mostly routine: property lines, easements, a 1998 dispute with the neighbor about a fence post that Eleanor had resolved with what appeared to be devastating politeness. The last pages were the ones that mattered. Her handwriting, sure and slanted, dated March 2018 — seven months before she died — and then stopping. Mid-sentence. He'd noted it the way you note any anomaly: *return later.*

Later had arrived the way it always does, wearing work boots and carrying bad news.

He carried the box down to the kitchen table, displacing the stapler, and Odile pulled her chair around to look. Outside the windows — the real windows, mostly — the light was doing late afternoon things, long and amber and indifferent.

"Start where she stopped," Odile said.

"That's the thing. I don't know where she stopped. Look." He turned to the final page. "March 14th, 2018. She's writing about the song."

*Subject continues to exhibit acoustic anomalies,* the note read, in handwriting that marched across the page like a confident little army. *Humming localized to load-bearing walls. Frequency consistent regardless of ambient temperature — NOT thermal expansion. Tested against pipe theory (see p. 12): eliminated. This is a patterned vocalization. It has structure. Verse structure.*

"She surveyed it," Odile said softly. "She surveyed the song."

"She surveyed everything. That was the whole woman." Wren traced the lines with one finger, careful, the way you touch something that might still be alive. "Listen to this. *Melody transcribed in part, Appendix C. Structure suggests five verses. Verses one through four recurring and stable. Fifth verse occurs only during what I am calling, for lack of a better term, 'signing events' — periods when the house acquires or attempts to acquire a resident.*"

"Signing events," Odile repeated. "That's exactly the word. How did she know that word?"

"She didn't. She invented it. That's what surveyors do — we name things so we can put them on maps." He kept reading aloud, and his voice did something he hated, going thin in the middle. "*Fifth verse incomplete in all observed instances. Hypothesis: the incompleteness is functional. The song terminates its fifth verse at the point of maximum harmonic tension — an unresolved cadence, if I'm using the term correctly, which I may not be — precisely where a listener's ear most wants resolution. The listener supplies the ending. The listener joins in. The listener stays.*"

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, distantly, the house hummed a note and then seemed embarrassed about it.

"She figured it out," Odile said. "In 2018. Before me, before any of us — she sat in this house with a clipboard and *figured it out.*"

"She was here for weeks that spring. I remember — I was twenty-six, I kept meaning to drive out and visit, and I kept not doing it, because work, because life, because she'd already started going strange and cold and I didn't want—" He stopped. That door stayed shut too. "Go on. Read the rest."

There wasn't much rest. The next entry was dated March 21st:

*Appendix C reviewed against field observations. Confirmed: verses 1–4 fully recoverable by any resident with time and patience. Verse 5 exists only as fragment + invitation. The fragment is real. The invitation is the trap.*

*Question: does verse 5 have a true ending? One that exists independent of the listener?*

*Search conducted: county records, church hymnals (1911–1960), the family Bible, Great-Aunt Marta's correspondence. No source. The song predates every record I can find. Either the ending has been lost—*

And then the handwriting changed.

Not dramatically. Anyone else would have missed it. But Wren had spent his childhood watching that hand fill ledger after ledger, and he knew the difference between Eleanor Kavanagh writing fast and Eleanor Kavanagh writing *carefully*, and here the letters got taller, more deliberate, each one placed like a fence post driven in hard ground:

*—or the ending was never written.*

*Consider: every house is built by someone. Every song is built by someone. What if the builder of this song stopped building? What if the fifth verse is not a locked box with a lost key, but an unfinished room — and the house has spent a hundred years asking visitors to finish it, and every visitor, hearing the gap, fills it the only way they know how: by joining in?*

*What if the correct response to an unfinished verse is not to remember it, but to write it?*

The pen stroke after that went long and slightly wild, a downward slash that became the start of a new sentence and then abandoned itself:

*I cannot do this. I have tried three times tonight to compose the ending and every version I write is either flattery or fury, and the house will know the difference. It knows everything I—*

Mid-sentence. The famous mid-sentence. Seven years Wren had assumed interruption — assumed the worst, assumed the house had taken her hand off the page. But sitting here now, with two bars missing and a head full of fragments, he saw it differently.

She hadn't been interrupted.

She'd put the pen down.

"Oh," said Odile, very quietly, in the voice of a woman watching a lock turn over from the inside.

"She stopped," Wren said. His own voice sounded far away. "She wasn't taken mid-thought. She wrote herself to the edge of the answer and then she — she quit. Deliberately. Look at the spacing. There's room on the page for ten more lines and she left it blank. That's not a person who got pulled away. That's a person who decided the next sentence shouldn't be hers."

"But why? She was so close. She'd solved it. Why would she—"

"Because she wouldn't invent an ending." He said it and then heard it, really heard it, and had to put both hands flat on the table like a man steadying himself on a boat deck. "Odile. My grandmother surveyed land for fifty years. You know what she told me when I got my license? She said, *the map doesn't make the ground true. The ground makes the map true. Your job is to report, not to improve.* She believed — her whole religion, if she had one, was that you don't get to make things up about a place. You measure what's there. You testify to what's there. And the house wanted her to *make something up*. To compose. To invent an ending for a song that had been lying unfinished for a century, and every honest bone in her body said no."

"So she refused."

"So she refused. And then—" The memory came back the way the returned ones did lately, unbidden and complete: standing in a funeral home seven years ago, numb, while a lawyer explained that Eleanor Kavanagh's will contained an unusual codicil regarding her grandson, some boilerplate he hadn't read closely, some clause about the disposition of the property. Some clause he'd signed without reading, because grief makes lazy readers of us all. "Then she bought me out of it somehow. We know that part. She traded everything she had left — every memory of me — to keep the house from ever pulling me in. And I thought she was being cold those last years. I thought she'd forgotten me because she didn't care."

"She forgot you because she paid for you," Odile said. "Yes. We've been through this part." Her voice was gentle, but her eyes had gone sharp, surveyor-sharp, fixed on the page. "But Wren. If she's right — if the fifth verse was never finished — then your two missing bars aren't hidden. They're not in a wall or a hymnal or the head of some poor soul in the hallway. They don't exist. Nobody can give them to you because nobody has them, including the house."

"The house must have them. The house sings the—"

"Does it?" Odile leaned forward. "Think. You've lived inside its throat for two weeks. When it hums, what does it hum?"

Wren opened his mouth and closed it. Because she was right, and he'd known she was right for days without letting the knowledge have a name. The house hummed verses one through four, endlessly, faithfully, the way a music box plays what's punched into its drum. But the fifth verse — the fifth verse it only ever *approached*. It climbed toward the gap and then it faltered, every time, the note hanging unresolved like a foot reaching for a stair that isn't there. He'd assumed the house was withholding. Teasing. Baiting.

It wasn't withholding anything.

It was stuck.

"It doesn't know the ending either," he said slowly. "It's been singing an unfinished song for a hundred years, waiting for somebody to finish it *for* it. Every signing, it gets to the gap and holds the note out like an empty plate, and everybody who hears it thinks they're supposed to fill the plate, so they join in, and joining in is the trap. It's not even a trap the house designed. It's just — loneliness. A hundred years of loneliness, holding out an empty plate."

Odile laughed. It was a terrible laugh, wet at the edges, sixty-one years compressed into two seconds. "I sat in this parlor in 1974 trying to *remember harder*. I turned this house upside down looking for the ending. I accused it of hiding it from me. And all along it was sitting there as stuck as I was, hoping I'd help."

"There's more." Wren turned the page. Under the abandoned sentence, lower on the sheet, smaller, as if written later in worse light, Eleanor had added one more line. Just one. As if she'd come back to the desk at midnight, unable to sleep, and allowed herself a single confession:

*If the verse must be composed, then the composer must be honest. Not kind. Not clever. Honest. The house has been lied about its whole life — appraised as charming, as sturdy, as home. Nobody has ever told it what it actually is. Perhaps that is the ending. Perhaps the last verse is simply the truth, sung.*

Below that, one final mark. Not words. A small, precise symbol in the margin — the notation surveyors use to flag a monument of uncertain position. A point that must be verified in the field.

Wren touched it. His grandmother had flagged the truth itself as *unverified*.

"She couldn't write it," he said. "Because she couldn't verify it. She could hypothesize the ending but she couldn't stand behind it, and she would not put her signature on a measurement she hadn't confirmed. So she stopped. And then she spent everything she had making sure I'd never be the one sitting in this chair with this problem." He looked up. "And here I am anyway. Sitting in the chair."

"Not that chair," Odile said automatically, and they both glanced at it — the wooden chair from the photographs, patient at the table's end, empty for now, its emptiness feeling less like absence lately and more like held breath.

Wren gathered the notes into order with hands that weren't quite steady. "The two bars aren't missing," he said. "They're unwritten. Which means I can't find them. Which means I have to *write* them. Which means—" He exhaled. "Which means I have to do the one thing my grandmother couldn't bring herself to do. Invent. Compose. Make something up about a place."

"And can you?" Odile asked. "Make something up?"

Wren thought about it honestly, because honesty was apparently the whole assignment now. He thought about his grandmother's rule — *report, don't improve* — and about how he'd built a career on it, and about how the hardest surveys he'd ever done were the ones where the ground and the deed disagreed and somebody had to decide which story was true.

"No," he said. "I can't make something up."

Odile's face fell.

"But I can tell the truth," he said. "That's different. That's the opposite. She was right — the ending isn't a composition, it's a testimony. The house doesn't need a pretty finale. It needs somebody to stand on its ground and swear to what's actually there." He was already pulling a fresh sheet of survey paper toward him, the good cream stock he saved for final drawings. "Every loss it ever suffered. Every person who left. Every death it watched and couldn't stop and answered by taking hostages. All of it. Sung, not spoken. Bars, not bullets."

"That will cost memories," Odile warned. "Everything costs memories in this house. You've paid for four verses already. You barely remember your grandmother's kitchen."

"I know what I've paid." He uncapped his pen. "And I know what she paid. And I know the difference between a price and a waste." He set the nib to the paper, and his hand — surveyor's hand, steady on instruments in wind and rain — did not shake. "Verse one through four, we have. The last two bars, we build. Not from imagination."

"From what, then?"

"From inventory." He looked at her, and for the first time since he'd touched the paperwork and fallen into this folding, humming, hungry place, Wren Kavanagh smiled. "I'm going to appraise the house, Odile. Honestly. Line by line. And then we're going to sing it what it's actually worth."

From deep in the walls, the house hummed the first four verses — and then, for the first time in a hundred years, it stopped at the gap and waited.

Like a client, Wren thought, picking up his pen. Waiting for the report.
