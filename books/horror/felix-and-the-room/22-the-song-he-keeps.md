## 22. The Song He Keeps

The cemetery in Millbrook had exactly two things going for it: a decent view of the river and a coffee kiosk that opened at seven, run by a man named Gus who believed grief could be substantially managed with a maple scone. Wren bought one every visit. It was not a ritual he'd planned. It was simply that the first time he'd come — three weeks after everything, hands still unsteady on the wheel — he'd needed something to do with his mouth other than cry, and a scone had been available.

Now it was October again. A year, nearly to the week.

He parked on the gravel shoulder, tucked the scone under his arm like a newspaper, and walked the familiar path past the Hendersons' granite angel (chipped nose, eternal judgment) to the row of flat markers where his grandmother lay under a slab of gray granite that read:

**ELEANOR KAVANAGH**
*1941 – 2018*
*SHE KEPT WHAT MATTERED*

Wren had chosen the epitaph himself, over the objections of the funeral home's suggestion booklet, which had offered him gems like *AT REST IN THE ARMS OF JESUS* and *A LIFE WELL LIVED*. She kept what mattered was true in ways the stone mason couldn't have imagined, and Wren liked that about it. A secret hiding in plain sight, which was, he'd learned, the family specialty.

He sat down on the grass beside her — no folding chair; he'd stopped bringing one months ago, for reasons that would have sounded insane at a hardware store — and unwrapped the scone.

"Morning," he said. "Gus added cardamom. I want you to know I'm eating this on your behalf, because you'd have hated it, and someone should suffer."

The wind moved through the maples along the fence line. Somewhere down the hill, a lawnmower droned its one long note. Ordinary sounds. Explained sounds. He'd spent a year learning to hear them without flinching, and mostly he succeeded.

Mostly.

"I closed the Hollis place last month," he went on, because talking to her had gotten easier once he understood she wasn't there to be embarrassed by it. "The one with the wraparound porch? Forty-year-old roof, gorgeous bones, terrible wiring. I flagged all of it. Honest appraisal." He took a bite of scone. "The new owners are putting in a garden. Tomatoes. You'd have told them tomatoes were a waste of good dirt, and then you'd have grown eleven varieties yourself."

This was how the visits went now. News from the living, delivered to the dead, with pastry. His therapist — yes, he had one now, a patient woman named Dr. Okafor who never once asked him to explain the chair thing because he'd shown her the photographs and she'd gone very quiet and then very professional — called it "continuing bonds." Wren called it Tuesday mornings. Both of them agreed it was working.

He finished the scone, brushed the crumbs from his knees, and did the thing he'd come to do.

He hummed.

Just the first verse. Fourteen notes, soft as breath, pitched low enough that the lawnmower nearly swallowed it. Outdoors, in open air, under a sky with nothing folded inside it — safe. That was the rule he'd written for himself, the only rule left: *never indoors.* He sang in his truck with the windows down. He sang in the shower with the bathroom door open like a coward. He sang here, above her, where the sound could go anywhere it wanted and belong to nothing.

The melody rose out of him the way water finds a crack in stone. He didn't think about it anymore. Thinking about it made his throat close.

And then — the way it always happened, the way it had happened maybe six times now, each one landing like a gift he hadn't ordered —

the memory came back.

Not returned, exactly. *Surfaced.* Like something rising through dark water into light, whole and impossible: a kitchen with yellow curtains and a radio that only got the AM station, and himself — small, so small, two years old maybe, standing on a kitchen chair because his feet wouldn't reach the floor — and Eleanor, young Eleanor, hair still brown, flour on her knuckles, humming this exact melody while she rolled out pie dough. And little Wren humming back, garbled, wrong, delighted. And her laughing and saying, *"That's it, sweetheart. You've got the shape of it. Don't you ever let anyone teach you the words."*

Wren sat very still on the cold grass.

That was the part that undid him every time. Not the yellow curtains, not the flour, not even her laugh — but the words. *Don't you ever let anyone teach you the words.* She'd known. Even then, thirty years before the house took her memory, she'd known what the song was, and she'd planted him on a kitchen chair — a chair, of course it was a chair — and given him the melody like an inoculation. The tune without the trap. The shape of it, harmless, so that if he ever heard the real thing, some deep animal part of him would recognize it and run.

She'd been protecting him since before he could talk.

"You knew," he said to the granite. His voice came out rough. "You absolute menace. You knew the whole time."

The wind stirred the maples. Down the hill, the lawnmower cut off, and in the sudden quiet a bird started up somewhere — three notes, four, nothing like the verse, just a bird being a bird — and Wren laughed, wet-eyed, and wiped his face with his sleeve.

Dr. Okafor had a theory about the returned memories. She thought the house, in accepting his honest appraisal, had released more than it had taken — that truth, once paid into a system, drew interest. Wren had his own theory, less clinical: his grandmother had traded her memories of him to buy his safety, and when the house emptied itself, those memories had nowhere to live except in him. They'd come home the long way around. Through the song. Through the melody she'd hidden in him like a letter sealed in a wall.

Some prices, he understood now, came back as interest.

He'd lost things too — he knew that the way you know a room is missing furniture, by the shape of the absence. The smell of her kitchen was gone; he'd traded it for the first verse and never gotten it back, and sometimes in bakeries he'd stop dead, chasing a ghost of cinnamon and woodsmoke that dissolved before he could name it. Certain summers were thinner than they should be. There was a hole where his tenth birthday should be, and he'd decided, after much deliberation, to leave it empty. Some rooms you don't refurnish. You just keep the door open and let people know someone lived there.

But what had come back outweighed it. Her voice, teaching him. The weight of her hand on his head. The specific way she said his name — *Wren*, two syllables, like a door opening. He carried those now the way you carry a lit match through wind: carefully, cupped, aware of what a miracle fire is.

* * *

He stayed until the sun cleared the tree line, then stood, brushed off his jeans, and patted the granite twice — their old signal, *all done, good job* — and walked back toward the truck.

Gus flagged him down at the kiosk on his way out, holding out a second scone in a wax bag. "On the house. You look like a man who talked to somebody important."

"Gus, you are a scholar and a gentleman."

"I'm a man with excess inventory and a soft heart, but sure." Gus squinted at him. "You get any writing done?"

That was the other new thing. Somewhere around February, Wren had started writing it all down — not the case file version, not the surveyor's inventory with its names and dates and conditions, though that existed too, sealed in a box he intended to leave to whoever needed it — but the story. The house, the song, Odile walking out into daylight holding her daughter's hand while sixty years fell off her face like snow off a branch. He wrote it as fiction, mostly, because fiction was a door you could leave open without anything walking through it. His agent had wept. His editor had asked, gently, if the ending was *too* hopeful, and Wren had said, "You try telling the truth to a haunted house and see how hopeful you feel afterward," and the editor had wisely dropped it.

"A little," Wren said. "It's coming together."

"What's it called?"

"*Felix and the Room.*"

"Felix?"

"The house named itself. Long story."

"Everything with you is a long story." Gus handed him the bag. "Bring me a copy when it's done. Signed. I'll trade you scones for life."

"Deal."

* * *

The drive home took forty minutes along the river road, and Wren drove it with the windows cracked because October air was a finite resource and winter was coming. At the top of Hartley Hill, where the road bent and you could see the whole valley laid out gold and rust, he passed the turnoff he always passed — the gravel lane, half-swallowed by sumac now, that led up to the house.

He didn't avoid it anymore. That was the difference. For seven years he'd driven the long way around, adding twenty minutes to every trip rather than see it, and now he looked right at the turnoff and felt — nothing sharp. Something old and sad, yes. Something that ached in the way a healed break aches before rain. But the dread was gone, and in its place was something almost like tenderness, which he suspected was the strangest thing the house had ever done to anyone.

Because here was the truth he'd sung into its rafters, the accounting it had accepted instead of a soul: the house had never been evil. It had been *lonely*. Built by a man who died before the paint dried, occupied by families who treated it as a waypoint, a starter home, a mistake — loved by no one long enough to be loved back — and it had learned the only language it could: hold on. Keep them. Fill the rooms. A house that captured people was monstrous, yes. But a house that captured people because it had watched everyone it ever held walk away and never once been mourned — that was something else too. Something that deserved, at the end, to be seen clearly. To be appraised honestly, defects noted, fair value assigned.

He'd given it that. And it had let go.

The county had sold it in June to a young couple from the city, the ones who were putting in tomatoes. Wren had done the final walkthrough himself, alone, in full daylight, and found the house exactly what it appeared: old, tired, structurally sound, smelling of dust and cedar and nothing else. No extra chair. No hum. In the parlor, someone — he never learned who, and chose not to investigate — had left fresh flowers on the windowsill.

The photographs had changed too. All of them, everywhere, in albums and attics and probate boxes across three counties: the extra chair simply gone, as if it had never leaned against the wallpaper, as if no one had ever sat in it waiting. Except Wren remembered it. He remembered the creak of it drawing closer across the kitchen floor, and he figured that was fair. Somebody ought to remember. Memory was the whole point, in the end. It was the only currency the house had ever really been trading in.

* * *

Back at his apartment — his and Dev's apartment, technically, though Dev was currently in Lisbon for six weeks and sent daily photos of pastries with captions like *THINKING OF YOU AND ALSO THIS IS BETTER THAN YOUR SCONE GUY* — Wren set the wax bag on the counter, made tea, and opened his laptop to the manuscript.

Chapter twenty-two. The last one. He'd been circling it for a week the way you circle a high dive.

The cursor blinked. Outside, a neighbor's wind chime clinked its random music, and a car door slammed, and someone's television laughed at something unfunny — the ordinary orchestra of a building full of strangers, none of whom were trapped, all of whom could leave whenever they liked. He listened to it for a while with something close to love.

Then he typed:

*Felix visited the grave on Tuesdays, and brought a scone, and told her the news, and hummed the first verse the way she'd taught him — outdoors, where it was safe. And every time, a little of what she'd paid came drifting back to him, like interest on a debt she'd never let him know he owed.*

He stopped. Read it. Deleted *like interest on a debt she'd never let him know he owed* and replaced it with something simpler, because simple was harder and better:

*Some prices come back as interest.*

Good enough. True enough. He saved the file, closed the laptop, and stood at the window with his tea while the afternoon went gold.

Down in the street, a kid was practicing trumpet — badly, gloriously, the same eight bars over and over, each attempt a little closer to right. Wren found himself keeping time with his thumb against the mug, and realized he was smiling, and realized also that he would probably always listen to music this way now: with one ear cocked for the shape underneath, the trap or the gift of it, waiting to learn which.

The kid nailed the phrase — clean, bright, unmistakable — and whooped, and somewhere a dog barked approval, and the ordinary evening rolled on.

The noises, Wren thought, raising his tea to the window, to the boy, to the grave on the hill, to the house with its tomatoes — the noises have explanations.

Some of them are songs.
