## 5. 1911

The parish archive smelled like a church basement that had given up on being a church and settled for being a basement. Wren had been in it for three days now, ever since the bell rang — or rather, ever since she'd admitted to herself she wasn't going back to the chapel, not after what she'd heard in its tone. Her name. *Wrennie.* Nobody had called her that since her grandmother died, and nobody living knew it, which meant either the bell was haunted or it had been listening, and honestly at this point she'd have accepted either explanation with a certain professional detachment.

The archive was kept by Father Ambrose, who was ninety if he was a day and mostly deaf, which made him ideal company. He'd let her in with a nod, pointed at a folding table under the one window that worked, and said something about biscuits that she'd chosen to interpret as an invitation rather than a warning.

"Take your time," he'd added, louder than necessary. "Nobody's been down here since 1987. A man came asking about property lines." He'd snorted, which for Father Ambrose constituted open rebellion against the diocese.

So Wren took her time. She went through boxes of baptismal records, marriage registers, burial ledgers, all of them written in the careful copperplate of clerks who were paid by the legible word. Bell Hollow's population scrolled past her like a slow, sad credits sequence: births tapering off after 1950, deaths continuing at their usual rate, until the ledger entries started arriving from towns forty miles away, where the descendants had scattered like seeds on wind.

And then she found 1911.

It wasn't labeled. That was the first strange thing. Every other year had its neat box, its tidy label in the clerk's hand. But 1911's records were stuffed loose into a crate that someone had, at some point, turned to face the wall. Wren turned it around. It felt like the kind of thing you did in horror movies right before the soundtrack got expensive, but curiosity had always been her worst quality, the one her grandmother used to say would get her into water over her head someday. *Well, Gran,* she thought, elbow-deep in yellowed paper. *You called it.*

The first document was a bill of sale. Dated March 1911. The parish had sold its bell — a fine bronze thing cast in 1843, according to the invoice — to a foundry in the city, for the sum of eleven pounds, four shillings. There was a note appended in different ink: *Buyer to collect by cart. Payment upon removal.*

Except there was no record of payment. And no record of collection. And when Wren cross-checked the foundry's name against the business directories in the next box, the foundry didn't exist. Had never existed. The address on the invoice was a coal yard that, per the tax rolls, had burned down in 1904.

"So the bell was sold to a ghost," she murmured, "and then it melted anyway."

Because melted it had been. That much even Teo's garbled family legends agreed on: the bell of Bell Hollow hadn't rung since 1911 because there was no bell to ring. It had gone soft in its tower one August night, they said, runned like candle wax down the belfry stairs, and the tower had been torn down rather than repaired, and everyone had agreed never to speak of why. Odile spoke of it so rarely that "rarely" was generous. Teo spoke of it constantly and uselessly, the way Teo spoke of everything, a firehose of lore with no valve.

Wren dug deeper. Under the invoice: letters. Dozens of them, from parishioners, from the bishop's office, from a solicitor representing someone named only as *the Founders' Trust.* The letters were angry, frightened, pleading. They wanted to know where the bell money had gone. They wanted to know why the vestry minutes for June through September of 1911 were missing — torn out, the binding still showing the stubs. They wanted to know why seven families had withdrawn their children from the parish school in the same week.

Seven families. Wren counted them twice, then a third time with her finger on the page like a child reading. Halloway. Merrick. Voss. Aldery. Crane. Fenwick. And one more whose name was smudged beyond recovery, as though someone had licked their thumb and scrubbed at it, over and over, for years.

She sat back. The window light had gone from white to honey without her noticing, and dust motes drifted through it like the whole room was underwater. Somewhere upstairs, Father Ambrose was singing to himself, tunelessly, something that might have been a hymn or might have been a song about boats.

"Okay," she told the box quietly. "What did you seven do?"

* * *

The photograph was at the bottom of the crate, wrapped in oilcloth, and it took her breath in a way she wasn't ready for.

Seven people stood in front of the Halloway house — the house, unmistakably, though younger, its stone unweathered, its doorframes dark and bare. Three women, three men, and one figure at the end whose face had been scratched out long ago, deliberately, with something sharp. They were arranged the way groups always were in old photographs: stiff, formal, Sunday-best clothes, hands folded. Except their faces —

Wren held the photo closer to the window. Tilted it. Held it at arm's length.

Their faces blurred. Not damaged-blurred, not faded-blurred. *Identical*-blurred. All six visible faces were the same face — same wide-set eyes, same mouth, same faint suggestion of a smile that sat wrong on each of them, like a mask fitted slightly too large. Different bodies, different ages, different clothes, and every single one wearing the same expression, the same features, as though the photographer had taken one portrait and printed it six times onto six different people.

Or as though, she thought, with the cold sliding feeling she was getting more familiar with lately, they had all been becoming the same person. Or the same *thing*. Or as though whatever they'd been doing together had been wearing them down to a common shape, the way water wears stones round.

The seventh figure, the scratched-out one, had no face at all to share.

"Founders," she whispered. The word felt official. Heavy. Like a key turning somewhere far away.

There was writing on the back, in pencil, faded almost to nothing:

*The seven who kept the water out. God forgive us the price. We are not finished.*

Wren read it four times. *We are not finished.* Not *we failed.* Not *we stopped.* *We are not finished* — present tense, ongoing, a sentence that refused to die even as the pencil faded. She looked at the smiling identical faces and felt, for the first time since the bell rang, something worse than fear.

She felt *invited.*

* * *

The diary was tucked inside a hollowed-out Bible, which was tucked inside a hatbox, which was labeled in someone's shaky hand: *DO NOT OPEN — E. HALLOWAY, 1912.* Wren opened it. Obviously. Her grandmother had also raised her to believe that warnings were just invitations with bad manners.

The handwriting inside belonged to Edith Halloway, and it changed over the course of the diary, which was the second thing that scared her. It began in January 1911 as a fine, confident script, the writing of a woman who kept accounts and ledgers and probably ran her household like a small navy. By June it had loosened. By August it slanted hard to the right, letters crowding each other, as though the hand holding the pen was hurrying, or trembling, or both.

Wren read by the last of the light, and the diary carried her backward through the year like a riptide.

*January.* Ordinary things. Prices of flour. A cousin's wedding. Complaints about the damp. Then, near the end of the month, one line out of nowhere: *Mr. Voss says the well has been singing again. I told him he is a fool. The well has been dry these sixty years and dry it will stay.*

*March.* *The singing is not only Voss's fancy. I heard it myself on Thursday, low, under the ground, like a choir through a wall. Aldery says his grandfather spoke of this. Says there is a naming that must be finished before winter or the water comes home to roost. Grandfather's words. Superstition. And yet I have not slept well.*

*May.* *We seven met at the Halloway house. It was Aldery's idea to make it formal. Seven names for seven plates, he says, as our grandfathers' grandfathers did, and the water will stay in the deep places where it belongs. I confess the ceremony moved me more than I expected. When we spoke the first name aloud, the air itself seemed to lean in and listen.*

*June.* *The vestry wants to know where the bell has gone. Let them wonder. A bell that rings true names calls other things besides congregations, and we cannot have it ringing while the naming is unfinished. We voted. We are seven and we do not require eight opinions.*

So that was it. That was where the bell went — not sold, not stolen. *Melted*, Wren thought, or made to melt, or made to be forgotten, because a bell that rang true names was dangerous to people mid-ritual. Her skin prickled. Because the bell had rung yesterday. Across a valley with no bell in it. Calling names. Calling *hers.*

*July.* *Three names spoken. The plates at the well take the names strangely — not carved, but grown, as though the metal remembers. Merrick has begun to look like Aldery. Or Aldery like Merrick. I try not to stare. The price is memory; we knew this going in; we agreed. But watching Crane forget his own wedding day, watching him laugh about it afterward with that same smile the others wear — I confess I am afraid, and I am ashamed of being afraid, because fear was not part of the bargain.*

*August.* *Fenwick is gone. Not dead — gone. His chair sits empty at the meetings and his plate at the well stays blank and none of the others will say his name, will hardly say anything anymore, they only smile. I wrote to his sister in the city. She writes back that Fenwick has not arrived and never wrote of coming. Six of us, then. The naming must be finished before winter. Six names and a seventh that we have not dared speak of, that we agreed in May never to write down, and I begin to understand why our grandfathers left the ritual unfinished. Some debts are better owed than paid.*

Then, on the final page, dated the last night of August, the handwriting barely legible, the pen pressed so hard it had torn the paper in places:

*I have hidden this where no one will look, in the Lord's own book, which is fitting, for it is His house they are hollowing. Tomorrow we finish it. Tomorrow the seventh name is spoken and the water sleeps another hundred years and we pay what we must pay. I have decided it will not be me. Whatever they smile about, whatever they have become, I will not give them*

The sentence stopped. Mid-word. No period, no dash, no torn page. Just ink trailing off as though the pen had been lifted, or the hand had been taken, or Edith Halloway had simply stopped existing between one letter and the next.

Wren stared at it for a long time. The archive had gone fully dark except for the window, and outside, the valley was doing its dusk thing, shadows pooling in the hollow like water finding its level. Somewhere up the hill, the Halloway house sat in the last light with its doorframe smiles, waiting.

*We are not finished,* the photograph had said. *Tomorrow we finish it,* the diary had said. And Edith's last line, unfinished, hanging: *I will not give them—*

Give them what? A name? Whose? Hers? Someone she loved?

Wren closed the Bible gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping thing, and put it back in the hatbox, and put the hatbox back exactly where it had been, and then stood in the dark archive with her heart going like a trapped bird.

Because here was the thing she couldn't stop thinking, the thing that had crawled under her skin and set up house: the ritual had never been finished. The well had been refilling for a hundred years. The bell — the melted, sold-to-a-ghost, erased-from-the-records bell — had rung yesterday evening across an empty valley, and it had rung her childhood nickname, and it was calling the descendants home, and there were seven nameplates behind the Halloway house and six names spoken a century ago and one plate that had stayed blank ever since.

One plate. Still waiting. Patient as water.

Upstairs, Father Ambrose's tuneless singing stopped. In the silence that followed, Wren heard — clearly, distinctly, from beneath the floorboards, from beneath the foundations, from somewhere down in the deep places where water belongs — a sound like a choir taking a breath.

She grabbed the photograph and the diary and ran.
