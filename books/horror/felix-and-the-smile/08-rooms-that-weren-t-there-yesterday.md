## 8. Rooms That Weren't There Yesterday

Teo Halloway had lived in Bell Hollow for thirty-one years, which by the standards of the village made him practically a tourist. His grandmother Odile had been born in the Halloway house, as had her mother, and her mother's mother, going back so far that when Teo once asked where the family had come from *before*, Odile had looked at him like he'd asked where water came from before it was wet.

So Teo knew the house. He knew which floorboards sang in E-flat, knew the pantry door needed a hip-check on humid days, knew that the third stair from the bottom was loose and would remain loose until the heat death of the universe because every attempt to fix it had ended with the nail working itself back out overnight, politely, like a guest returning a borrowed pen.

He also knew — in the way you know a thing without ever saying it aloud, the way you know not to whistle after dark — that the house changed.

Not dramatically. Nothing so vulgar as walls sliding around like a haunted funhouse. Just small drifts. A hallway that took four steps longer than it should. A window that faced the orchard one winter and the well the next. Odile called it "the house settling," and she said it in the tone of voice other people used for "we don't talk about your uncle at Christmas."

But this morning, standing in the upstairs corridor with a mug of coffee going cold in his hand, Teo was looking at a door that had absolutely not been there yesterday.

It sat between his grandmother's room and the linen closet, flush with the wall, oak, unremarkable except for the fact that its frame bore a carved smile — small, contented, patient — and except for the fact that Teo had walked this corridor roughly eleven thousand times and could have drawn it blindfolded, drunk, and mid-sneeze.

"Gran," he called down the stairs, keeping his voice level the way you keep your voice level around skittish animals and gas leaks. "We've got a new door."

"We've got a new *what*?" Odile's footsteps came up fast, cane and all. She reached the landing, looked at the door, and her face did something complicated — a whole weather system of expressions passing over it in the space of two seconds.

"That wasn't there yesterday," Teo said.

"No," Odile agreed. Her knuckles whitened on the cane. "No, it was not."

"Should we... open it?"

"Should we *open* it," Odile repeated, in the voice she used when Teo suggested microwaving fish. "Boy, in this house, when a door appears overnight, you do not open it. You make tea. You sit with it. You let it know you're not afraid, even if you are, and then—"

The door opened on its own.

"—or that," Odile finished. "That works too."

* * *

Beyond the door was a nursery.

That was the first wrong thing, though it took Teo's brain a moment to flag it, because the room itself was almost aggressively ordinary. Yellow wallpaper with a faded pattern of ducks. A wooden crib. A rocking chair with a crocheted blanket folded over one arm. A toy chest. Afternoon light came through a window that should have opened onto the north slope but instead showed a view of a garden neither of them recognized — a garden with a clothesline, a plastic tricycle, a fence painted the particular exhausted blue of fences that have been repainted by fathers who do not enjoy painting fences.

The second wrong thing arrived when Teo stepped inside and felt the temperature change. Not colder. Warmer. The room held warmth the way a cupped hand holds a moth — carefully, like it might leave.

"Oh," said a voice behind them, and there was Ivo Villalobos, the surveyor, standing in the doorway with his laser measure tucked under one arm like a clipboard-carrying archangel. He'd been measuring the exterior footings all morning; Teo had watched him through the kitchen window, pacing out distances, muttering numbers, radiating the contentment of a man whose entire belief system was functioning correctly.

Now his belief system was looking at a duck wallpaper.

"This is—" Ivo stopped. Started again. "This room doesn't exist."

"It exists now," Odile said grimly. "That's generally how it goes."

"No, I mean—" Ivo raised the laser measure, pointed it into the room, and frowned at the readout. Frowned harder. Lowered it, shook it, raised it again. "The house is forty-two feet wide. Exterior measurement, verified twice. This room alone is nineteen feet across, and it sits in the middle of the footprint." He looked up, and for the first time since Teo had met him, the man's face held something other than professional composure. It held the specific expression of an accountant who has found a number that shouldn't exist. "There is no room for this room."

"There never is," Odile said. "Come in or don't, but stop blocking the light. Houses hate that."

Ivo came in. And that was when Teo saw the third wrong thing, the one that turned the whole scene from strange to unbearable, because Ivo crossed the threshold, took two steps toward the crib, and stopped as though he'd walked into glass.

On the crib rail, hanging by a ribbon, was a mobile. Felt stars and a felt moon, hand-sewn, slightly lopsided, the moon's smile stitched with black thread that had gone grey with age.

Ivo knew it. Anyone could see that he knew it. It moved across his face like a shadow crossing a field.

"Where did you get this?" he asked, and his voice had gone quiet in a way that made Teo's chest hurt.

"Nobody got it anywhere," Odile said gently. "It's yours."

"I've never been here before."

"Not here. But it's yours." She nodded at the mobile. "Isn't it."

Ivo didn't answer. He crossed to the rocking chair instead, moving like a man wading upstream, and lifted the crocheted blanket. Cream yarn, edged in green, with a single crooked row where someone — someone with bad eyes and good intentions — had dropped a stitch and kept going rather than start over.

"My grandmother made this," he said. "She taught me the basic stitch when I was six. I made a square. One square. It took me a month." He laughed, a short, startled sound with nothing funny in it. "I haven't thought about that in thirty years. I haven't thought about any of this in—" He set the blanket down with exaggerated care, the way you put down something sleeping. "How does this house have my grandmother's blanket?"

Teo looked at the toy chest. He shouldn't have. He knew he shouldn't have, in the same way you know not to look at the sun, and he looked anyway.

Inside: a tin rocket ship with a dent in its nose cone. A baseball glove, left-handed, oiled dark with use. A stack of picture books with cracked spines. A jar of marbles. And beneath everything, half-buried, a child's drawing in crayon — a house with a smiling door, rendered with the ferocious confidence of a five-year-old, signed in wobbling letters: *IVO, AGE 5.*

"You drew the smiles," Teo said. His own voice sounded far away. "When you were a kid. You drew houses with smiling doors."

"I drew houses with smiling doors," Ivo agreed faintly. "My mother kept it on the fridge until—" He stopped. Closed his mouth. Opened it again. "Nobody knows about that drawing. Nobody alive. My mother died when I was twenty-six and I never told anyone about the drawing because it seemed like a stupid thing to remember, a stupid thing to *keep*, and now it's sitting in a toy chest in a room that is mathematically impossible in a house I've known for four days."

The room said nothing. Rooms don't. But the light through the impossible window shifted, warm and gold, and somewhere below them — Teo would have sworn to this later, sworn on anything — the floorboards hummed a note that was almost, but not quite, contentment.

Odile lowered herself into the rocking chair. It creaked under her, a familiar complaint, and she laid her cane across her knees and looked at Ivo with an expression Teo had seen maybe three times in his life: the look she wore when she was about to tell the truth and hated it.

"Sit down, Mr. Villalobos."

"I'd rather—"

"Sit. Down."

Ivo sat on the edge of the crib's footboard, which groaned but held. Teo stayed standing by the toy chest, one hand resting on its lid, feeling oddly like a chaperone.

"The house remembers," Odile said. "That's the plain truth of it, and I'm sorry you had to find out this way instead of me telling you over breakfast like a civilized person. It remembers things. Things people forget, things people throw away, things nobody living ever knew. It's been doing it longer than my family's been in it, and my family has been in it since before the bell melted." She tapped the floor with the cane, twice, a punctuation mark. "For most of my life it remembered *our* things. My mother's hair ribbons turned up in a drawer forty years after she lost them. My father's war letters, which he burned, mind you, burned them in the yard and raked the ashes — I found them in the attic, whole, smelling of smoke. We learned to live with it. You don't ask a thing like that why. You just say thank you, and you don't take anything out of the house, and you hope it stays interested in the past."

"And now?" Ivo asked.

"Now it's interested in *you*." Odile said it plainly, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "It started the day you arrived. The smiles on the frames got sharper. The hallways started drifting toward wherever you were standing. And now it's built you a nursery full of your own childhood, which, in eighty years of watching this house work, I have never once seen it do for anybody." She paused. "It's not haunting you, son. Haunting is cheap. This is worse. This is *collecting*."

"Collecting," Ivo repeated.

"Houses collect what they love," Odile said. "Or what they intend to keep."

The word *keep* landed in the room and stayed there, buzzing, like a fly against a window.

Teo watched Ivo's face go through it — the skepticism rallying, the empiricist reaching for explanations: coincidence, prior knowledge, some elaborate con by a family of rural eccentrics with access to archival crayon drawings. Teo could see the man *wanting* the rational answer, wanting it the way a drowning man wants a rope. And Teo watched the want fail, because there was no rope. There was only a dented tin rocket and a blanket with a dropped stitch and sunlight falling through a window that looked out on a garden from a life Ivo had never mentioned to anyone.

"My father built that fence," Ivo said suddenly, nodding at the window. "Blue. He hated painting it. Did it every summer anyway. Said a man keeps up his fence whether or not anyone sees it." His jaw worked. "I was seven. That garden was gone by the time I was eight. We moved. I never went back. I never told anyone about the fence either. Do you understand? It's not that these things are secret. It's that they're *gone*. They're nowhere. They exist in exactly one head, mine, and I haven't opened that drawer in decades, and this house has gone in and taken everything out and laid it out like—" His voice caught. "Like it matters. Like it was worth keeping."

Teo's throat tightened. Because he understood, suddenly, exactly, in his bones: the horror wasn't the impossible room. The horror was that the room was *kind*. That somewhere in the walls of this old place was something that had rummaged through a lonely man's forgotten childhood and arranged it under warm light, and the tenderness of that was more frightening than any amount of blood or shadow. You could fight a monster. What could you do with a house that loved you?

"Mr. Villalobos," Odile said quietly, "I need to ask you something, and I need you to think before you answer, because I think the house is listening to how you answer."

Ivo looked at her.

"When you look at this room," she said, "how does it feel? Honest answer. The house will know if you lie. It always knows."

A long silence. The felt moon turned slowly on its ribbon, though there was no draft.

"It feels," Ivo said at last, slowly, like a man testing ice, "like being measured. All my life I measure things. Square footage, boundaries, easements, setbacks. I walk into places and I reduce them to numbers so they can't surprise me. And this room feels like—" He exhaled. "Like being on the other side of the instrument. Like something walked into *me* and started taking measurements, and it isn't using feet and inches. It's using..." He gestured helplessly at the toy chest, the blanket, the crayon house with its smiling door. "*This*. Everything I forgot. Everything I decided wasn't worth carrying."

The rocking chair creaked as Odile nodded, slow and grave.

"Then hear me," she said. "Because tomorrow I'm going to tell you about the well, and the nameplates, and what my family did and didn't finish in 1911, and none of it will make sense to a man who only trusts numbers. So start practicing now. Start believing things you can't measure." She nodded around the yellow room, at the ducks, at the impossible window, at the small smiling moon. "Start with this. This room is real. Say it."

"That's not evidence, that's assertion—"

"*Say it.*"

Ivo closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, which he clearly wished they weren't.

"This room is real," he said.

Somewhere in the walls, deep down, something sighed — long and satisfied, like a dog circling twice before lying down at the foot of a bed.

And Teo, hand still resting on the toy chest lid, felt the wood shift very slightly under his palm — not opening, not closing, just settling, the way a cat settles against a person it has decided belongs to it.

That night, after supper, after Odile had told them the first part of what happened in 1911 and refused, absolutely refused, to tell the rest until "the light is right, and it won't be right until tomorrow evening," Teo climbed the stairs to check on the nursery one last time.

The door stood open. The nightlight inside — a nightlight that hadn't been there that afternoon, a small ceramic duck glowing amber — was on.

And on the wall above the crib, where the afternoon had shown blank wallpaper, someone had hung a framed photograph. Teo stepped closer, heart tapping quick against his ribs.

It was a picture of the seven founders, the ones from the parish archive, the ones whose faces blurred identically whenever you tried to hold them in your eye. But in this version, standing at the end of the row, taller than the rest, dressed in modern clothes with a laser measure tucked under one arm —

— was Ivo.

The photograph was old. The paper was foxed and brown at the edges. It had been hanging there for a hundred years.

Downstairs, faintly, from the direction of the dry well behind the house, came a sound Teo had never heard it make before.

Water, dripping. Once. Twice.

Like something counting.
