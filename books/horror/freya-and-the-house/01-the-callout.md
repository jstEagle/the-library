## 1. The Callout

The call came in at 7:42 on a Tuesday morning, which Ansel Fairweather would later consider the last ordinary moment of their life, though at the time it was just a phone call and a lukewarm cup of coffee going colder in their hand.

"Fairweather Pest Solutions," Ansel answered, because they'd never gotten around to printing new business cards after the divorce, and "Fairweather Pest Solutions" was what the cards said, and it was easier to say it out loud than to admit the business was one person, one van, and a laminated binder of testimonials from people who mostly spelled Ansel's name wrong.

"Hi, yes—Marisol Vega? On Corliss Street? You did my neighbor's place, the Hendersons, for the carpenter ants?" The voice was warm and slightly rushed, the voice of a woman talking while doing something else, probably wrangling children or a recalcitrant toaster. "They gave me your number. I've got a noise situation."

"A noise situation," Ansel repeated, and wrote it down even though they wouldn't need to. Writing things down made customers feel heard. Ansel had learned that early. People don't actually want you to fix the problem so much as they want you to take the problem seriously, to nod at it, to hold it up to the light and say, *yes, I see this, you are not crazy.*

That was, if Ansel was honest—and Ansel tried to be honest in the way that people who lie to themselves daily are honest, which is to say selectively—ninety percent of the job.

"Scratching," Marisol said. "In the walls. Mostly at night. And something that sounds like—okay, this is going to sound weird."

"Weird is my specialty," Ansel said, which was true, though not in the way Marisol hoped.

"It sounds like humming. Like someone humming in the walls. My husband says it's the pipes. But pipes don't hum, do they?"

Ansel looked at the ceiling of their own kitchen, at the water stain shaped like a hand that they had never gotten around to repainting over, and said, with the easy confidence of seven years of practice, "Pipes can do a lot of things. I'll take a look."

* * *

The van smelled like the inside of the van always smelled: cedar blocks, old coffee, and the faint chemical sweetness of the good stuff Ansel wasn't legally allowed to use anymore. Ansel drove the four minutes to Corliss Street with the window cracked because October was doing that thing October does, where it can't decide between sweater weather and the first rehearsal of winter, and the light came down through the sugar maples in long amber bars that made even the modest houses on Corliss look like paintings of themselves.

Marisol Vega answered the door before Ansel knocked, which Ansel would think about later, much later, in the small hours when thinking about things was unavoidable. At the time, it seemed like nothing. People hear the van. The van is not subtle.

"You must be Fairweather." She was maybe forty-five, quick-eyed, with a pencil through her bun and flour on one forearm, the universal uniform of someone who'd been interrupted mid-something. "Thank you for coming so fast. The Hendersons said you were good."

"The Hendersons are generous," Ansel said. "They also paid their invoice promptly, which in my experience correlates strongly with being called good."

Marisol laughed, and Ansel liked her immediately, in the uncomplicated way you like people who laugh at the right things. She led them through a house that was warm in the particular way houses with children are warm—shoes in a drift by the door, a crayon drawing of what was either a horse or a very optimistic dog taped to the fridge, the ambient percussion of a family living its life. Two kids, Ansel gathered. A husband named Tomás who was at work. A house with good bones and, presumably, something in the walls.

"So." Marisol stopped in the hallway and crossed her arms, and her voice dropped into the register people use when they're about to say something they've been rehearsing. "It started about three weeks ago. Scratching, like I said. But it's the humming that's got me. It's always three notes. Same three notes, over and over. And it's not the pipes, because I had the water shut off last Thursday to fix the outdoor spigot, and it kept going."

Ansel nodded and made a small professional sound, something between *mm* and *huh*, a sound engineered to convey deep diagnostic thought.

"Could be settling," Ansel offered. "Houses make noise. Wood expands, contracts—"

"In a melody," Marisol said. "Three notes. In a melody."

"Could be wind in the chimney."

"It doesn't happen when it's windy."

"Could be—" Ansel stopped, because there was a word sitting at the back of their throat that they had spent seven years never saying, and they swallowed it down with the dregs of the cold coffee still in their travel mug. "Could be a bird. Some birds get into wall cavities. They vocalize. Starlings do it, sometimes. It's a whole thing."

"In October?"

"Migratory confusion is real," Ansel said, and wrote *starling?* on their clipboard, and Marisol looked at them with an expression that said, politely and without malice, that she did not believe a single word, and Ansel respected her enormously for it.

* * *

The inspection itself was normal, blessedly, gorgeously normal. Ansel checked the crawlspace under the porch and found the classic signs of mice—droppings like grains of black rice, a chewed corner of insulation, a sunflower seed cache arranged with an almost architectural ambition behind the water heater. Mice. Actual, ordinary, biological mice, with bones and hearts and no interest whatsoever in melody.

"There's your scratching," Ansel said, emerging from the crawlspace with a smudge of gray dust across one cheekbone that they wouldn't discover until they saw themselves in the rearview mirror that evening and thought, briefly and without understanding, *I look like I've been crying ash.* "Mice. Family of them, by the look of it. I'll bait, seal the entry points—there's a gap at the sill where the cable line comes in, they're using it like a highway."

Marisol's shoulders dropped an inch. "Oh, thank God. I was starting to feel crazy."

"You're not crazy. Houses are just loud." Ansel smiled the smile they gave every customer, the one that said *the world is knowable and I am the knower.* "The humming, though—that one I'd put money on being your pipes. Water hammer. It's when the flow gets shut off suddenly and the pipe—"

"We turned the water off," Marisol said again, patiently. "The whole house. For four hours. It hummed the whole time."

"Air in the lines, then. Trapped air can—"

"Mr. Fairweather." She said it gently. "I'm not asking you to explain it right now. I'm asking you to listen to it. It's better at night, but it does it during the day sometimes, when the house is quiet. Can you just—be quiet with me for a minute?"

And because Ansel Fairweather was, at their core, a person who had built an entire life on the principle of staying small and agreeable and not making anyone uncomfortable, they said, "Sure. Sure. One minute."

They stood together in Marisol Vega's hallway, two near-strangers listening to a wall.

The house was quiet. The kids were at school. The fridge cycled off. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and gave up on it. Ansel counted the seconds the way they counted everything—automatically, a habit from a childhood spent waiting for sounds in walls to either mean something or stop—and at second thirty-one, it came.

Three notes. Rising, then settling. Low and round and unhurried, like someone humming to a child who was almost asleep. Not through the wall exactly. *In* the wall. Under it. In the floor, in the bones of the house, in the places where a house keeps its oldest wood.

Ansel knew the notes.

That was the thing. That was the thing that happened in the space of a single heartbeat, in the warm hallway of a stranger's house, while a woman Ansel had met eleven minutes ago watched their face: Ansel knew the three notes. Not recognized—*knew*, the way you know a phone number you dialed every day as a child, the way you know the smell of a specific kitchen, the way you know a lullaby that someone sang to you before you had words to hold it with. The notes arrived and something in Ansel's chest rose to meet them, an answering hum gathering in the throat like a sneeze, like a sob, like a—

Ansel's hand found the doorframe and gripped it.

"Mr. Fairweather?"

"Pipes," Ansel said. Their voice came out fine. Their voice always came out fine; it was the one part of them that had never once betrayed them. "That's—yes. That's a pipe resonance. It's a harmonic thing. Your water main and the framing of the house, they can share a frequency, and when conditions are right—humidity, temperature—the pipe will carry it. It's called sympathetic vibration. It's very common in older houses."

"Corliss Street was built in the fifties," Marisol said.

"Classic era for it." Ansel was already moving, gathering the clipboard, the flashlight, the professional posture. "I'll write up an estimate for the mice work. For the pipe thing, honestly, you'd want a plumber to balance the pressure, but it's cosmetic. It's nothing. It's the house breathing."

"The house breathing," Marisol repeated.

"That's what old-timers used to call it," Ansel said, and had no idea whether that was true, and heard, as they said it, how much it sounded like something someone had once told them on a porch with a blanket over their knees, in a kitchen that smelled like cardamom, in a house on a street not four miles from this one.

*Grandma's house. The house Ansel had lived in for seven years. The house where, every night at some hour too small to be called late and too large to be called early, the floorboards under the bed hummed three notes—rising, then settling—like someone humming to a child who was almost asleep.*

Seven years of explanations lived in Ansel's kitchen, taped inside the cabinet over the stove where no guest would ever see them. A list, in Ansel's own handwriting, added to and amended like a living document: *Settling. Pipes. Squirrels (ruled out). Wind (ruled out). Water heater (replaced anyway). Old houses talk.* Seven years of staying small and agreeable and never, ever listening long enough for the sound to finish, because some part of Ansel—the part that had gripped Marisol's doorframe just now—understood, without language, that if you listened to the whole thing, you might find yourself answering it.

"Mr. Fairweather? You went somewhere just now."

Ansel blinked. Marisol was watching them with that quick-eyed attention, the pencil still through her bun, and Ansel realized their hand was still on the doorframe, knuckles pale.

"Just mapping the wall cavities," Ansel said. "Mentally. For the sealing work. It's a whole process."

It was a lie. It was such a transparent lie that Ansel was almost proud of it, and Marisol almost certainly knew it was a lie, and neither of them said so, because that is the contract strangers make: *I will let you have your explanation if you let me have mine.*

"The mice," Ansel said briskly, writing numbers that were fair and honest because the mice were real and the mice work would be real, and there was comfort in that, in the one clean corner of the morning. "I can start Thursday. Bait stations, seal the sill gap, follow-up in two weeks. Should quiet the scratching down within the month."

"And the humming?"

Ansel looked at her. Marisol Vega stood in her warm hallway with flour on her arm and worry she was trying to wear lightly, and every professional instinct Ansel had said *give her the plumber line, close the invoice, drive away.*

"You'll want to keep an ear on it," Ansel said instead, which was not an answer, and they both knew it. "If it changes. If it gets—closer, or louder, or if it starts sounding like it's coming from different rooms. Call me."

"From different rooms," Marisol said. "Why would it come from different rooms?"

"Sympathetic vibration," Ansel said. "It travels."

* * *

The van sat in Marisol's driveway while Ansel sat in the van and did not start the engine.

Through the windshield, Corliss Street went about its October afternoon. A leaf blower somewhere. A school bus sighing at the corner. A woman watering mums that were already dead but didn't know it. An ordinary street, painted in amber light, full of ordinary houses with ordinary bones.

Ansel's phone buzzed. A reminder they'd set themselves years ago and never deleted, one of the small bureaucratic rituals of their survival: *Cabinet list — review quarterly.* Ansel looked at it for a long moment. Then they opened the notes app instead, to a file labeled *work*, and under *Vega, Corliss St — mice*, they typed, because their hands needed to do something and the alternative was putting them over their own ears:

*3 notes. Same 3.*

Then, after a moment:

*pipes. it's pipes. it's always pipes.*

The engine started on the second try, the way it always did, and Ansel Fairweather drove the four minutes home to the house they'd inherited, past the sugar maples, past the corner where the school bus sighed, and did not—did not, they were very firm about this—hum.
