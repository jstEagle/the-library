## 1. The Appraisal Order

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday, which Xanthe Eastwick would later consider the last ordinary fact of his life.

It was a heavy cream-colored thing, hand-addressed in a copperplate so fine it looked typeset, and it sat on his doormat between a pizza flyer and a reminder from his dentist that he was, apparently, overdue for everything. He picked it up the way he picked up all mail — by the corners, thumb and forefinger, as though it might be damp — and carried it inside to the kitchen table, where all important documents were received, read, annotated, cross-referenced, scanned, backed up to two drives, and filed.

Xanthe Eastwick documented things. This is the first thing anyone learns about him, usually within ten minutes of meeting him, because he will tell you. Not boastfully — more the way a man with a limp mentions the stairs. *Fair warning,* the telling says. *This is how I am.*

He photographed his food before eating it when the meal was notable. He measured doorframes of rented flats "just to know." His car's glovebox contained a logbook of every fill-up since 2016: date, mileage, litres, price per litre, weather, mood (his own). The mood column had begun as a joke and then quietly stopped being one, because there was something steadying about it — about writing *overcast, 3/10* on a grey morning and feeling the day become a data point instead of a weight.

His sister Priya called it his religion. "You don't have faith, Xan," she'd said once, over Christmas, watching him log the exact time the turkey went in. "You have *records*."

"Records are better than faith," he'd said. "Records can be audited."

He'd meant it lightly. Mostly. But there was a truth under it that he didn't examine often, because examining it required looking at the years before age seven, and those years were — there was no gentler word for it — gone. Not traumatic-amnesia gone, not dramatically gone. Just *thin*. Like a book with the first chapters torn out and the spine re-glued so you wouldn't notice. He knew he'd been born in Ashford. He knew there had been a house involved at some point, because there were two photographs of him as a small boy standing in front of one, and in both photographs he was squinting against a light that seemed too bright for the season. He did not know the house's name. He did not know why there were only two photographs. When he asked his mother, she would go pleasant and vague in a way that was worse than tears, and say, *Oh, we moved around so much back then, love*, and change the subject to her roses.

So he documented. Every certificate, every receipt, every birthday, logged and laminated where lamination was appropriate. If his life before seven could not be recovered, then by God the life after would be *thorough*. A life so well-kept that nothing could ever be lost from it again. It was, if you thought about it honestly — which he tried not to — a kind of prayer. *I was here. I counted. Count me.*

The letter knife came out. The cream envelope opened with a sound like a small sigh.

---

Inside: an appraisal order, printed on paper stock heavier than any firm he contracted for used, and a cheque as retainer that made him sit down.

The orderhead read **EASTWICK PROPERTIES & TRUST**, which gave him a moment's genuine confusion, because that was nearly his own name and he'd never heard of them. Below it, in that same immaculate copperplate:

*Re: Hollowmere House, parish of Hollowmere, Kent. Full structural and valuation survey required prior to transfer of title. Property vacant approximately forty (40) years. Access by arrangement. Completion required before month's end.*

And beneath that, a line that struck him as odd even before he understood how odd:

*The property has been expecting you.*

He read it three times. Estate agents wrote nonsense all the time — *charming period features*, *deceptively spacious*, *a property with real character* — but they did not, as a rule, suggest that derelict houses held expectations regarding specific surveyors. He turned the page over. Nothing. He held it up to the window light, half hoping for a watermark, a hidden second message, anything. Just paper. Good paper, though. Very good paper.

The retainer cleared the next morning. That settled the matter, as retainers do. Xanthe was not rich; surveying paid steadily but without glamour, and forty-year-vacant properties were the sort of job that made a portfolio sing. Listed buildings, probate nightmares, houses with histories — these were the commissions other surveyors dreaded and he quietly loved. A house nobody had touched in four decades was a time capsule. You could read a building like sedimentary rock, if you were patient, if you wrote everything down.

He rang the number on the letterhead. It rang four times and was answered by a woman whose voice was dry and pleasant and slightly too close to the receiver, as though she were leaning in to hear his heartbeat.

"Mr. Eastwick. Good. We hoped you'd ring today."

"You did?"

"The post is slow out here." A pause that went on a beat longer than politeness allowed. "You'll want to come before the month turns. The twenty-eighth, ideally."

"The letter says month's end generally—"

"The twenty-eighth," she said again, gently, the way you'd correct a child's arithmetic, and rang off before he could ask what happened on the twenty-ninth.

He wrote down the date. Underlined it twice. Then, because the conversation had left a faint film on him, like walking through a spiderweb, he made tea and did what he always did with unease: he filed it. Opened the folder — new tab, new label, HOLLOWMERE / APPRAISAL / 2024 — and began, methodically, to build the file that would hold this job. Scanned letter. Retainer record. Call log: *14:22, spoke w/ unidentified female, insisted on 28th, no surname given.* He felt better immediately. Whatever was strange about this job, it was now *documented strangeness*, and documented strangeness was just a category of work.

That was the trick of it, really. That was the whole religion. Fear lived in the unrecorded spaces. Write enough down and there was nowhere left for it to hide.

---

He spent the following week preparing, because preparation was the part of the job he loved best — the part that felt like packing a survival kit against chaos itself.

There was a ritual. There was always a ritual.

First, the kit. Laser measure, calibrated and recalibrated. Two tape measures, thirty-metre and pocket. Spirit level, moisture meter, torch, chalk, camera with a fresh memory card and a spare in his breast pocket like a spare bullet. Graph-paper notebooks, hardbound, in triplicate — one working copy, one clean copy, one that stayed in the car, because Xanthe had learned young that the world ate paperwork and you fed it copies until it choked.

Second, the research. He pulled everything the county archives had on Hollowmere parish, which was less than he expected and stranger. A tithe map from 1841 showing the house and its grounds, drawn with unusual care — whoever had inked it had given the house more detail than the church. A sale notice from 1984, yellowed and brief: *"HOLLOWMERE HOUSE — substantial residence, c. 1780, requiring complete renovation. Sold with contents. Enquiries in person."* No floor plan. No photographs. For a house of that size and age, that was like a biography with no portrait. And then nothing. Forty years of archival silence, broken only by a single line in a parish maintenance ledger, 1997: *Road to Hollowmere cleared of fall. No further action.*

No further action. Xanthe underlined it, then wrote in the margin, in his small upright hand: *Who cleared it? Why?*

Marginalia was the third part of the ritual, and the most sacred. Other people highlighted; Xanthe conversed. Every document he touched came away annotated — questions, contradictions, small jokes to keep himself company (*"sold with contents" — contents being, presumably, forty years of spiders*). His university tutors had despaired of it; his clients had come to rely on it. A report by Xanthe Eastwick didn't just tell you what a building was. It told you what the building was *hiding*, or at least where the hiding places were.

Priya, on their Sunday call, heard the name of the commission and made a noise.

"Hollowmere," she repeated. "Why does that — hm. It's nothing. Sounds like somewhere from a poem."

"It's a real place. Kent. Vacant since the eighties."

"Mm." A pause. He could hear her deciding whether to say the thing, and then saying it, because she was his sister. "You get that look, you know. When a job's got hooks in it."

"What look?"

"The happy-dreadful one. Like a man ordering something off a menu he knows will hurt him." She sighed. "Take your little notebooks. Take your spare batteries. Ring me when you're back, and if you don't ring me I'm sending Mum."

"I always ring."

"You always document," she said. "Sometimes those are different things." And then, lighter, because she wasn't cruel: "Go on then. Go be happy-dreadful. What's the house called again?"

"Hollowmere House."

"Hollowmere," she said once more, tasting it, and he noticed she said it the way you'd repeat a word you suspected you'd dreamed. "Funny. Feels like I should know it."

He laughed and told her that was just her dramatic streak, inherited from their mother along with the roses and the vagueness, and they said their goodnights, and he did not think about it again until much later, when thinking about it was far too late to be useful.

---

The night before departure, he laid everything out on the kitchen table the way a surgeon lays out instruments, and performed the final act of the ritual: the first entry.

Every job began with a fresh notebook, and every fresh notebook began with the same page. Date. Client. Property. Objective. And then, at the bottom, a line he'd been writing since his very first commission, aged nineteen, in a student flat in Canterbury with a damp problem and a landlord who lied:

*Property surveyed in full. Surveyor accounted for throughout.*

He didn't remember when he'd started writing it, exactly, or why that phrasing — *accounted for*, as though he were inventory, as though someone might come looking for him and need the books to balance. But it went in every book. Forty-one jobs. Forty-one books, boxed and dated on the shelf above his desk, a paper spine holding up his whole life.

He uncapped his pen — fountain pen, black ink, no exceptions; pencil smudged and digital could be edited, and ink was a promise — and wrote the date. Wrote *Client: Eastwick Properties & Trust.* Wrote *Property: Hollowmere House, Kent.*

Then he sat for a moment with the pen hovering, because there was one more line he hadn't planned, and his hand wanted to write it anyway. This happened sometimes — rarely, maybe four times in his career — a phrase arriving unbidden, the way a tune arrives, and he'd learned not to fight it. The good marginalia always came from somewhere slightly outside himself.

He wrote:

*Note: the client's letter states the property "has been expecting" the undersigned. Unprofessional phrasing. Flagged for follow-up.*

He looked at it. It was accurate, professional, and completely beside whatever his hand had actually wanted to say, which hovered somewhere underneath the words like a shape under a sheet. He almost crossed it out. Instead he added, after a moment:

*Also: check why the 28th. Also: why does the retainer smell faintly of earth?*

(It did. Faintly. He'd told himself it was the envelope.)

He closed the notebook, squared its edges with the table's edge, and set it atop the packed bag by the door, where it would be the first thing his hand found in the morning. Then he took a photograph of the whole arrangement — bag, notebook, keys, flask, the kitchen clock reading 21:47 — and filed it under HOLLOWMERE / PREPARATION / 001.

Somewhere in the flat, a draught moved through a room with no open window, and the papers on his desk lifted and settled, all together, like something exhaling. Xanthe glanced up, frowned mildly, noted *draught, source unknown, investigate seals* on the whiteboard by the kettle, and went to bed.

He slept well. He almost always slept well before a new job. There was a comfort in beginnings — in the moment when a building was still just a file, still just numbers waiting to be taken, still obedient to measurement. He did not yet know that some buildings kept their own books. He did not yet know that somewhere in Kent, a house with windows arranged like a face mid-blink had already entered him into its ledger, in a hand older than his family's name, under a heading he would spend the rest of his life trying to strike through:

*Expected: one (1) surveyor. Arriving soon. Patiently awaited since 1984 — and, in another sense, rather longer.*

The harvest moon was nine days out.
