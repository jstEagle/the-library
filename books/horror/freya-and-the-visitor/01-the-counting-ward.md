## 1. The Counting Ward

The recorder was already running when Bram Ravensworth made his first round of the night.

He had set it on the windowsill of Room 6 at eleven minutes past midnight, as he did every night in every room, a small black rectangle with a red light like a patient heartbeat. The machine cost forty pounds and had outlived three of its owners. He checked it the way other nurses checked IV drips—without thinking, without ceremony—and then he stood for a moment in the doorway of Harold Finch's room and listened to an old man count backwards from one hundred and forty-four in his sleep.

"Eighty-one," said Harold. "Eighty. Seventy-nine."

His voice was clear and unhurried, pitched low, the voice of a man reading numbers off a list he trusted completely. He lay flat on his back with his hands folded over the blanket, and his breathing was even, and his face—the face of a man whose liver had quietly decided, three months ago, to stop being a liver—was calm. Not the strained calm of the medicated. Something else. Something almost pleased.

Bram wrote the time in the margin of his notebook. *12:11 a.m., Rm 6, counting resumed.* He had learned to write small, because the notebooks filled fast. He was on volume thirty-one.

*Seventy-eight. Seventy-seven.*

There were four residents left at Larkspur Hospice now, down from nine when Bram had started five years ago. The building was a converted Georgian rectory outside Ashcombe, all tall sash windows and radiators that clanked like distant machinery, and the funding came—as it always had—from Marigold House, though Bram had never given that fact much thought. Money arrived; salaries cleared; the morphine cupboard stayed stocked. In the arithmetic of end-of-life care, gratitude was a luxury nobody could afford to itemize.

He crossed the corridor to check on Mrs. Okafor, who slept without counting, thank God, her oxygen cannula hissing softly. Then Mr. Pell, awake at 12:20 as he always was, wanting tea he wouldn't drink and conversation he would.

"You're doing your rounds," Mr. Pell observed, from beneath a mountain of blanket.

"I'm doing my rounds."

"With your little book." The old man's eyes glittered. "Writing us down."

"Somebody should."

"Nobody else does." Mr. Pell turned his face toward the window, where the garden was a black suggestion beyond the glass. "You know what my daughter said when I told her I'd come here? She said, *Dad, they'll take good notes.* Like I was a car going in for service." He laughed, which turned into a cough, which Bram waited out with a hand on his shoulder until it settled. "She wasn't wrong, mind. You do take good notes."

"It's not for the file," Bram said. He said this often, and it was true, and it was also not the whole truth, and he had stopped interrogating the difference years ago.

"No?"

"The file gets bullet points. *Patient resting comfortably.*" He tapped the notebook against his palm. "This is for me."

Mr. Pell studied him with the frankness of the dying, who have earned the right to stare. "And what's it for, then? For you?"

Bram considered the question honestly, because Mr. Pell deserved honesty and because lying to the dying is a special kind of theft. "So it isn't just gone," he said finally. "All of it. The nights. The talking. If nobody writes it down, it's like—" He searched. "Like it happened to no one."

The old man nodded slowly, as if Bram had confirmed something he'd long suspected about the living. "Go on, then," he said. "Do your rounds. Write me down good."

* * *

Harold was still counting when Bram came back through Room 6 at half past one.

"Fifty-two," Harold said. "Fifty-one. Fifty."

He had been at it, by Bram's estimate, for nearly ninety minutes, descending at a rate of roughly one number per minute, which meant he would reach zero sometime around two-thirty in the morning if he kept the pace. He had started at 144 at some point before midnight—Bram had the tape from the previous night to confirm it, and the night before that, and the night before that. Every night this week: 144, counted down in sleep, steady as a metronome, unbroken except by the pauses when Harold seemed to be listening to something.

That was the part Bram didn't write in the notebook. The pauses.

Because between the numbers—between *fifty* and what would surely come next—Harold's mouth would go still, and his brow would crease slightly, the way a man's does when he's being asked a question. And then he would answer. Bram couldn't hear the questions. On the tapes, in the gaps, there was nothing but room-tone: the radiator, the wind at the sash, the hum of the mini-fridge where they kept Harold's anti-nausea medication. But Harold answered, clearly, in words.

Tonight the answers had been: *"Yes, of course."* And: *"I never told anyone that."* And once, very softly, with something like wonder: *"How did you know her name?"*

Bram had told himself, the first night, that Harold was dreaming of his late wife, June, dead eleven years. People at the end talk to their dead; Bram had heard it hundreds of times, had learned to stand quietly in doorways while the living held conversations across the divide, and he recorded those conversations too, because they were the truest things anyone ever said in this building. So: June. A dream of June. That was the reasonable explanation, and Bram Ravensworth was a reasonable man, a professional man, a man who had spent fifteen years in hospice wards and knew every shape grief could take.

Except that Harold hadn't mentioned June. He'd answered questions about a boat—a sailboat, a mistake with a jib sheet, a brother overboard and pulled out coughing, a secret kept for sixty years that Harold had apparently confessed now, in his sleep, to the dark air above his bed. Bram knew this because Harold had told *him*, last Tuesday, in daylight, over tapioca pudding—or rather, Harold had almost told him, had gotten as far as *the summer we took the boat out, my brother and I,* and then had closed his mouth like a drawer and changed the subject to the weather.

And tonight, in his sleep, Harold was giving away the rest.

"Forty-nine," said Harold. Pause. "Forty-eight."

Bram sat down in the visitor's chair by the window, notebook open on his knee, and did what he always did, which was stay.

* * *

Here is what the other nurses said about Bram Ravensworth, when they said anything at all: that he was quiet, that he was reliable, that he was strange about the recordings but harmless, that he'd been at Larkspur longer than anyone and would probably die in the staff wing someday and someone would find his notebooks stacked like sandbags around the body. Sister Adeyemi, the day manager, had once told him gently that he might want to talk to someone, after the thing with the little girl's mother in the spring, and Bram had thanked her and meant it and done nothing, because talking to someone was a door and Bram had spent his whole adult life standing outside doors with a notebook, writing down what went on inside them instead of going in.

He knew this about himself. Knowing it had never once changed it.

It had started with his mother, though he rarely let himself trace the line that far back. His mother, dying of the same slow failure that was currently eating Harold Finch, when Bram was nineteen, in a council flat that smelled of lavender and antiseptic. Four months of it. And Bram—young, terrified, useless with a kettle—had discovered that if he wrote down what she said, the saying of it hurt less. *Mum, 3rd of March: asked about the blue dress. Said her own mother came to the foot of the bed and stood there smiling and didn't say a word, and she wasn't afraid.* Writing it down put it somewhere outside him. It became evidence instead of wound. It became a thing that had happened rather than a thing happening to him, forever, in a flat that smelled of lavender, and there was nothing he could do.

Devotion, he called it. Not science. He had never once published anything or shown anyone the notebooks entire, though he'd transcribed passages for eulogies, for grandchildren, for a widow in 2019 who had wept with gratitude to hear her husband's exact words read back to her six weeks after the funeral. That was the use of it. That was the good of it. Everything else—the compulsion, the volumes, the way his hand actually shook if a resident died on a night the recorder batteries failed—everything else was just the shape his love had worn itself into, the way water wears stone: not a choice, eventually. A channel.

So when Harold began counting backwards from 144, Bram documented it. Of course he documented it. It was what he had.

*Forty-one. Forty.*

But here was the thing he was only now, sitting in the dark of Room 6, allowing himself to notice: Harold wasn't afraid.

Bram had watched a great many people approach the edge of the thing. He had seen terror and denial and bargaining and a ferocious, obscene cheerfulness, and he had seen peace, real peace, maybe a dozen times, and he had learned to tell the difference between peace and sedation the way a jeweler tells paste from diamond. Harold Finch was not sedated. His chart showed 2mg of morphine sulfate at eight p.m., a dose that would soften pain but not produce *this*—this lucid, level, almost ceremonial calm, this man lying at attention in his own body like a soldier awaiting a relief watch.

And he was counting down. Not up. Down. Toward something, not away from it. As if the number mattered. As if he'd been told to keep track.

"Thirty-three," Harold said, and paused, and his lips moved silently for perhaps ten seconds, answering, and then: "No. I'd have remembered. ... Well. Maybe I wouldn't have, would I." A dry little laugh, alone in the dark. "Fair enough."

Bram's pen had stopped moving.

He looked at his own handwriting on the page—*12:11, counting resumed; 1:31, answers continue; subject calm*—and saw how it looked from the outside, how it would look to Sister Adeyemi, to a doctor, to anyone: clinical, contained, a nurse managing an observation. And underneath it, in the space between the lines where he kept everything that mattered: *Who are you talking to, Harold? Who taught you the number?*

Because that was the other thing. The number was never different. One hundred and forty-four. Not 143, not 150, not whatever number a dying brain might randomly seize upon. One hundred and forty-four, every night, seven nights running, from a man who—if Bram pressed his memory back through the ward's recent history—was not the first. There had been Mrs. Calloway in the winter, pancreatic, and she had counted too, he realized now, he had *heard* it through the wall on nights he'd told himself it was mumbling, and before her old Mr. Anselm, and—

He didn't finish the thought. He wrote instead: *Ask Harold tomorrow, waking, re: the number. Gently.*

He would not ask. They both knew he would not ask. Some doors you don't open in daylight.

*Twenty-six. Twenty-five.*

Outside, the wind moved through the yew trees along the drive, and somewhere far off a dog barked twice and gave up. The radiator clanked. Harold breathed. The red light on the recorder burned its small steady eye into the dark, taking all of it down, keeping it, making it real, making it *documented*, so that whatever happened in this room tonight would happen to someone—to Bram, at least, in the morning, with coffee and headphones and the volume turned low—and not merely to an old man alone with the dark and whatever the dark was asking him.

Bram understood, distantly, that this was not health. That a man who needed a machine between himself and other people's deaths was a man carrying something he ought to set down. He understood it the way you understand the price of things you intend to buy anyway.

"Twenty-four," said Harold. "Twenty-three."

Then the pause. Longer than usual. Thirty seconds. Forty. Harold's eyes moved beneath their lids, quick, tracking something. His hands tightened on the blanket—not in fear, Bram noted, in *attention*, the grip of a man leaning forward to hear better.

And then Harold smiled. Actually smiled, in his sleep, a soft helpless smile like a young man's, and said:

"Oh. *Her.* Yes. Tell her I'm sorry about the fence."

And went on counting.

Bram wrote none of that down. His pen hovered over the page and did not move, because some sentences refuse to be evidence, and *tell her I'm sorry about the fence* was one of them. He sat in the chair with his heart going harder than it had any clinical reason to, watching an old man descend a staircase of numbers toward a door Bram could not see, and he felt—for the first time in fifteen years of nights exactly like this one—the specific, cold discomfort of a witness who suspects he has been witnessing more than he knew.

*Twelve. Eleven.*

At 2:37 a.m., Harold reached zero.

"Three. Two. One." A breath. A long, satisfied exhale, like a man setting down a heavy bag after a long walk. And then, clearly, conversationally, in the tone of someone concluding a pleasant evening with a neighbor:

"There. All accounted for."

Silence. The radiator clanked. The red light burned.

Bram sat very still until his shift ended, and then he collected the recorder from the windowsill with hands that were almost steady, and carried it down the corridor to the nurses' station, where he fed the tape into the player and put the headphones on and pressed rewind—all the way back, past midnight, past eleven, to the beginning of the night, to hear again the moment Harold had begun to count.

And in the silence between *one hundred and forty-four* and *one hundred and forty-three*—in the gap where the tape should hold nothing but the radiator and the wind—

something answered him.
