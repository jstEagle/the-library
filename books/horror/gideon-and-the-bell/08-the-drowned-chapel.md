## 8. The Drowned Chapel

The county records office smelled of cold paper and old coffee, and Juniper Vane stood at the map cabinet like a man reading his own autopsy.

He had come on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays the archive clerk was out and the self-service room stayed open. He had learned to schedule his life around other people's absences. It was one of the small dishonesties he permitted himself—the way he permitted himself to say *preliminary* in every report, to say *pending verification*, to let the word *pending* do the work that courage should have done.

The plat maps for the Hollowmere parcel were bound in green cloth gone soft as felt at the corners. He carried them to the long oak table beneath the buzzing fluorescent tube and untied the string with fingers that wanted to hurry and forced themselves not to. Hurrying was how mistakes happened. Hurrying was how a man signed off on eleven doors when there were twelve.

The 1952 survey showed the property whole. He spread it flat and weighted the corners with the brass paperweights chained to the table, and there was Hollowmere House sitting on its rise above the valley like a dark tooth in a pale jaw, and below it, running along the eastern boundary, a structure he had never seen and never been told about.

A chapel.

It was drawn with the loving precision of a surveyor who liked his work. *St. Brendan-in-the-Vale, est. 1841.* The little cross of walls. The tower at the northwest corner, square, with a note in faded ink: *bell, 2 cwt., bronze, cast 1861.* A path led from the chapel down to the vale floor, and the vale floor was drawn in fine contour lines that stepped down and down like the rungs of a ladder into the earth.

Juniper sat very still. The fluorescent tube buzzed. Somewhere in the stacks a cart rolled and stopped, rolled and stopped, like breathing.

He turned to the 1968 survey.

The chapel was still there. The contours were still there. But across the vale floor, in red ink, someone had written: *RESERVOIR PROJECT — PHASE II — SEE HYDROLOGY DIV.*

He turned to the 1971.

The contours of the vale were gone. In their place, a single flat blue wash, and the contour lines of the reservoir's planned surface, and the chapel—St. Brendan-in-the-Vale, est. 1841, bell 2 cwt.—sitting beneath the blue wash like a coin at the bottom of a bowl.

*Submerged structure. Demolition waived. See easement 74-119.*

Demolition waived. Juniper read the phrase four times. They had flooded the valley and left the chapel standing beneath the water, because demolishing it would have cost more than leaving it. He thought of all the bureaucratic sentences he had written in his own career, all the small cruelties dressed in passive voice, and understood that this one—*demolition waived*—had been someone's Tuesday too. Someone's coffee and cold paper and hurry.

Easement 74-119. 1974. The year Aldous Crane walked into Hollowmere House with his ledger and his partner and his intentions, and did not walk out. The year Edith Marsh was declared dead without a body. The year a chapel bell was listed as *recovered* in a file Juniper had read so many times the pages had gone soft at the folds.

The bell was not recovered. The bell was *taken*. He had known it in the way you know a load-bearing wall by the crack above the door. And now he knew where it had been taken *from*.

He copied the plat coordinates onto the back of his hand in ballpoint, the way he'd copied phone numbers as a boy, and the ink sat in the whorls of his skin like something written on him rather than by him.

* * *

The drive back took forty minutes and he did not remember any of it, which frightened him more than the frost had. He had learned, in the last weeks, to be frightened in an orderly fashion—fear itemized, fear filed, fear given a line in the ledger of the day. But the drive had simply been a gap, and gaps were what the house traded in.

The reservoir lay east of the house, a quarter mile of flat water gone the color of old pewter under an overcast sky. In summer it should have been busy—bass boats, swimmers, the tinny radios of teenagers. It was empty. It had been empty every day he'd been at Hollowmere, and he had filed that under *seasonal*, the way he filed everything, and understood now that he had been filing his own burial preparations.

He stood at the water's edge where the old boat ramp slumped into the shallows, concrete cracked and furred with green, and looked out.

The tower's tip broke the surface.

Not much of it—a gray triangle, maybe a yard of masonry above the waterline, crowned with a rotted wooden cap that listed to one side like a hat knocked askew. Weeds grew at its base where the water lapped. It was so ordinary, so *surveyable*, that his professional eye had catalogued it weeks ago as a ruin, an outbuilding, a well house, and moved on. He had appraised the top of a drowned church and called it an outbuilding. He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, and the water did not ripple at the sound, and somehow that was worse.

He walked the shoreline as the light went long and gold and low, counting his paces, because counting was the only prayer he trusted. At two hundred and forty paces from the ramp, the tower stood closest to shore. The water between was dark and still as poured glass. He could see down into it, a little—stones, a suggestion of walls, the pale geometry of a roof ridge running away into the murk like the spine of some enormous sleeping thing.

And on the stones, in the green-black shallows where the light reached, frost.

Frost. In July. Underwater.

It fanned across the submerged masonry in white ferns, the same ferns that bloomed on his bedroom windows each night, and they did not melt, did not thin, did not care for the season or the temperature or any law Juniper had ever relied upon. They grew from the chapel outward, he saw—traced the walls, followed the roofline, climbed the sunken tower in white spirals—and then, where the water deepened and the light failed, they kept going. He could not see where. But he knew where they went. They went to his windows. They went up his walls. They were in the house now, in the corners of the twelfth room, in the margins of his reports.

The pattern was not on the chapel. The pattern came *from* it.

He crouched at the waterline with his camera and photographed the submerged ferns, and when he checked the screen the image showed something the water had not: the frost forming letters. Small, patient, old-fashioned letters, the hand of a schoolmistress, spelling out along the sunken roof ridge a single word:

*COME.*

* * *

He did not sleep that night so much as lie in the dark of Hollowmere House listening to the recorder's red eye and the house's long silences, and at some hour past two, when the frost had finished its nightly work on the windows and the cold stood in the room like a visitor who would not sit, he got up, took the recorder from the nightstand, and set it on the sill beneath the ferned glass.

"I found the chapel," he said.

The silence had a texture. He had learned its textures the way a sailor learns winds. This one was listening.

"The plat maps. 1952. St. Brendan-in-the-Vale. The reservoir went in and they just—" he stopped, started again. "They left it under the water. Demolition waived."

He pressed record. He pressed stop. He pressed record again, because the ritual of it steadied him, the way counting doors steadied him, and then he sat on the edge of the bed in his coat with his breath showing and rewound the tape.

The hiss. The hiss went on a long time. Long enough that he began to compose the apology he would make to himself in the morning—*fatigue, suggestion, the mind filling pauses the way water fills a hull*—and then, in his silence, in one of his pauses, a woman's voice.

"It was never waived, Juniper. It was *protected*."

He sat very still. The frost ferns on the window seemed to lean toward the recorder's small speaker.

"Protected," he said to the empty room, and pressed record.

"They tried, the year the water came. Men with crowbars and a permit and a foreman who'd been promised a bonus. The chapel would not come down. Walls that a sledge could mark but not break. The tower stood while they pulled at it, and the water rose around it, and on the day the vale was sealed the men went home and the foreman wrote in his book that the structure was sound and the cost prohibitive. But that is not what happened, Juniper. What happened is that the chapel was loved, and a loved thing under water is not a ruin. It is a held breath."

Her voice was the same as on the sleep recordings—patient, low, with an accent from a county he couldn't name, worn smooth like a stone in a current. Fifty years of current.

"The bell," Juniper said. His voice cracked on the second syllable and he let it. "The ledger said two hundredweight. Cast 1861. It's listed as recovered in the Crane file."

The pause this time was longer. When Edith spoke again, the patience in her voice had something under it, the way ice has water under it.

"Aldous took it. From the tower, before the water. He said— he told me it was the only thing in that valley worth what we owed. Two hundredweight of bronze, he said, and there are men in the city who pay for bronze like that, for church bells, for the metal alone." A breath that the tape caught and held. "I helped him carry it. I want you to know that first, before anything else I ever ask of you. I helped him. We took it down the tower stairs on a hand truck at four in the morning, and the bell did not ring, and I remember thinking that was mercy. A bell that heavy, swung even once by accident—it would have called every soul in the vale out of their beds. It did not ring. It has not rung since. That is the whole of the trouble, Juniper. That is the entire architecture of it. A bell that cannot ring, and a chapel that cannot be demolished, and a debt that cannot be paid, all of us holding our breath together under the water."

Juniper looked at the window. The frost ferns had changed. He was almost sure they had changed—had shifted, the fronds rearranging themselves while he listened, the way a sleeper shifts.

"The pattern," he said. "On the windows. Every night, more of it. I photographed it and the photographs keep— developing differently. I thought it was a warning."

"It is a map."

The words came without a pause this time, immediate, almost eager, and the eagerness in her voice did something to his chest that he did not have a line item for.

"A map," he repeated.

"To the bell, Juniper. To where it must go back. The frost draws what the chapel remembers. It has been drawing the same drawing for fifty years, every winter, every night, and no one has ever stayed long enough or looked long enough to read it. The spiral is the tower stair. The branching lines are the walls. The small marks along the outer ring—" her voice gentled, the way you gentle a voice for bad news, or for hope, which is worse— "the small marks are the pews. Forty of them. The chapel holds forty souls, Juniper, and the frost has been counting them all this time, every night, because a chapel counts its congregation. That is what it was *for*."

He got up. He could not help it. He went to the window with the recorder held before him like a lamp, and he looked at the ferns, really looked, for the first time not as a phenomenon but as a *text*.

The spiral was there. It had always been there—he had photographs of it going back weeks, spirals nested in spirals, which he had catalogued as *dendritic growth, atypical*. The branching lines ran outward from it, regular as floor plans. And along the outermost ring, where the frost met the warm glass and stopped, small dense clusters of white, evenly spaced, patient as a congregation in their pews.

Forty. He counted them twice. Forty.

"I can't read the rest of it," he said. "The center. It's not finished."

"No," Edith said, and the word came out of the recorder and settled over the room like snowfall. "It is not finished. It finishes at the solstice. It has been finishing for fifty years, a little more each winter, and every winter it has finished *almost*, and every winter the house has had to begin again, because the pattern is not complete until the bell is home. The frost is not the house's hunger, Juniper. It is the chapel's *drawing*. A thing under water, holding its breath, drawing and drawing and drawing the picture of what was taken from it, hoping someone will read it."

"And if it finishes," Juniper said, "and the bell isn't back—"

He let the pause stand. He was learning to let pauses stand, because that was where she lived.

"Then the drawing is a door," Edith said, "and a finished door opens, and what comes through it is not the chapel. The chapel is kind. The chapel is only a chapel. But the house was built over it, and houses take their character from what is under them, and what is under this one has been patient for fifty years, and patience, Juniper—" the recorder hissed, and her voice came through the hiss like a figure coming through fog— "patience curdles."

The frost on the window creaked. A single fern, at the center of the unfinished spiral, extended one white branch the length of a finger, and stopped, waiting.

Juniper stood in the cold room with the tape turning and felt the thing he had been bracing against for weeks finally turn over inside him—not fear, or not only fear, but something with a direction to it. Purpose has a temperature, he thought. It is warmer than fear. It is the only thing he had felt in this house that was warmer than fear.

"The ledger," he said. "Crane's ledger. You said the house took him. Where did it take him, Edith? Where do I find what he wrote?"

The pause was the longest yet. When she answered, her voice had gone quiet in a new way—the quiet of someone standing at the edge of a room they have not entered in fifty years.

"The twelfth door," she said. "The one you counted at midnight. The one that is warm. He is not there, Juniper. But his ledger is, and the ledger is the only map to what he *did*—and you will need to know what he did, all of it, before you touch that bell. Because the bell is not only bronze. The bell is a *witness*. It was cast to hold what is spoken beneath it, and it has been holding fifty years of silence, and when it rings—"

She stopped.

"When it rings what, Edith?"

The frost ferns trembled on the glass. Outside, far off across the pewter water, something that was not wind moved along the drowned roofline, and the small white marks in the outer ring of the pattern—forty of them, patient, counted—seemed, in the moonlight, to lean forward in their pews.

"Everything," Edith said. "It will say everything. So you must decide, before you lift it, whether you can bear to be standing under it when it does. Now go and sleep, Juniper. And tomorrow—"

The tape ran on. Her voice did not. He waited through a full minute of hiss, then another, and in the silence he heard, very faintly, from beneath the floor, from beneath the foundations, from beneath fifty years of held breath, a sound he would spend the rest of this story trying to unhear:

A bell, deep under the earth, shifting on its rope.

Not ringing. *Waiting.*
