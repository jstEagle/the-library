## 2. Seven Years of Explanations

I keep a second journal. Not the survey log with its grid coordinates and passage widths—that one is professional, almost clinical, the kind of thing you could hand to a county engineer without shame. The second journal is the one where I write down the sounds.

I started it the first week, back when I still thought of myself as a tourist in my own childhood. *Entry one: knocking, three times, evenly spaced. Probably water in a standpipe. Tunnels this old have standpipes. Everything has an explanation if you're willing to do the homework.*

Seven years of homework. I could teach a seminar.

Let me catalog, because cataloging is what I do instead of screaming. Dripping water—obviously. A tunnel system under a village that gets forty inches of rain a year is going to drip; it's practically contractual. The drip I heard every Tuesday for the first two years, always from the east, always at the same interval—*plink, plink-pause, plink*—was condensation cycling with the temperature. I checked the weather data. The correlation was good enough. I didn't look too hard at the fact that the correlation was *too* good, that the drip kept its schedule through a drought in the third summer when there was no condensation to be had anywhere in the county.

Settling stone. That was my workhorse explanation. Old mines settle; the Hollow sat on top of a century of abandoned workings, and stone under stress groans the way old houses do, the way ships do, the way anything does when it's carrying more weight than it was built for. The long sighing moan from beneath the churchyard—settling stone. The rhythmic scrape, like something heavy being dragged a few feet and set down, over and over, always just past the reach of my headlamp—settling stone, contracting in the cold. The sound of my own name, spoken low, in a voice I almost recognized—well. That one I classified as *acoustic pareidolia*, which is a real phenomenon, which is the scientific term for your brain finding faces in wallpaper and voices in water pipes, and which I wrote in my journal with the particular firmness of a woman who does not want to examine the fact that pareidolia does not usually know your *childhood nickname*.

I want to be clear that I was good at this. I was excellent at this. I had a system, a taxonomy, a little mental drawer for every sound the Hollow made, and for seven years every sound went obediently into its drawer, and every drawer closed, and I went home—or home-ish, the rented room above the feed store that I told people I kept for the surveying—and I slept fine. Mostly fine. Fine the way you sleep when you've gotten very good at not listening.

Because here is the thing about explanations: they're not really about the sound. They're about you. An explanation is a story you tell so you can keep doing the thing you were doing. My explanations let me keep coming back. And I had to keep coming back, and I told myself for seven years it was for the map.

The map is real. I don't want to diminish the map. Two hundred and eleven kilometers of surveyed passage under a village of nine residents, drawn in my own hand, accurate to within centimeters. Nobody else wanted the work. Nobody else would crawl into a collapsed adit behind the old tannery with a laser rangefinder and a headlamp and a heart rate that said *leave, leave, leave* while the rest of me said *one more passage*. I told myself it was dedication. I told myself it was scholarship. There is a version of this story where I am simply a very stubborn cartographer.

Here is the other version.

* * *

I grew up at the top of Bell Lane, in the house with the crooked chimney, and for the first eighteen years of my life my name was Daniel Galloway.

There. It's in the journal now, which means it's in the world, which is the whole point of the journal and the whole terror of it. I left Kessel's Hollow at eighteen with two duffel bags and a name I'd been rehearsing in private since I was fourteen, and I did not come back for nine years, and when I did come back it was under a survey permit with my new name on it, and I told myself the survey was the reason.

The truth is that the Hollow called me back before I ever filed a permit. The truth is that on my last night in the village, the night before I left, I heard something in the tunnels under Bell Lane say *Daniel*—not shouted, not menacing, just *said*, the way you'd say the name of someone who'd wandered off in a crowd and you'd spotted them—and I ran, and I kept running for nine years, and then I came back, and I have never once been able to explain to myself why.

Explanations. Right. I had one for that too, in the early years: *closure*. I was going back for closure, that wonderful therapeutic word, that word that means *I need to keep returning to the site of the wound and touching it*. I told myself the map was closure. Every passage I surveyed was me putting a pin in the past, naming it, measuring it, making it small. The Hollow had known me as Daniel; the map would know me as Ulyana; between the two of us we'd get the story straight.

Except the Hollow never called me Ulyana. Not once, in seven years. Not in the drip, not in the settling stone, not in the pareidolia. Every sound that might have been a voice was the old voice, the young voice, the voice from the crowd. *Daniel.* Like it had learned the name first and saw no reason to update its records.

I wrote that down once, in the second journal, three years ago: *It doesn't know my name. Or it refuses it. Or names down there are like strata—you get what was laid down first.* Then I underlined it twice and wrote underneath, in letters I can still feel the pressure of in my hand: *Or I never really told it. Or I never really told anyone.*

That's the grief part, in case you were wondering where the grief was in all this dread. Everyone thinks the hard part of a life like mine is the leaving. It's not. The hard part is that you leave and the world keeps your old name on file anyway—the school register, the parish roll, the mouth of every neighbor who watched you grow up—and you build a whole new self in a city where nobody knows, and it works, it actually works, and then you come home and the first thing the dark does is reach for the old file. The tunnels don't misgender me. That would almost be better. The tunnels just use the dead name like it's still current, like the account was never closed, and some nights that feels less like being haunted and more like being *reminded*—gently, patiently, by something that has all the time in the world—that the person I was is still down there in the dark, filed and waiting, and one day I'll have to go collect her or she'll come collect me.

* * *

Last night I sat down with both journals and did what I do: I made a table. Sound, first heard, explanation given, explanation status. It was meant to be reassuring. A professional reviewing her case files. It was not reassuring.

*Dripping, east passage, years 1–2: condensation cycle. Status: collapsed in year 3, drought. Sound continued.*

*Knocking, three evenly spaced, near the old cistern: water in standpipe. Status: cistern dry since 1962 per county records. I checked. I checked in year one and I checked again last month because I am a professional.*

*Scraping, rhythmic, past the tannery adit: settling stone. Status: the adit collapsed in year 5. There is no passage there anymore. The scraping continued. I surveyed the collapse face myself. There is nothing behind it but thirty meters of rubble and then solid rock, and something on the other side of the solid rock is still dragging something heavy a few feet and setting it down.*

*Voice, low, my name: acoustic pareidolia. Status: —*

I left that one blank. I've left it blank for seven years. Last night I sat with the pen hovering over the line and I could not write a single word, and the pen hovered there so long that a drop of ink fell and bloomed on the page like a small dark mouth, and I closed the journal.

Because here is what I actually believe, down under the explanations, in the cellar of myself where I keep the things I don't say: I believe the Hollow has been talking to me the whole time. Not menacing. Not even unfriendly. *Persistent.* The way a creditor is persistent. The way someone is persistent when they believe—reasonably, from their side of the ledger—that you owe them something.

And I believe the sounds have been getting closer. Not louder—closer. The drip was two passages east for two years, then one passage east, then in the wall, then, last spring, in the floor of the passage while I stood on it, directly beneath my boots, at a depth the survey says is solid. The knocking was at the cistern, then at the junction, then at the ladder, then—three weeks ago—three knocks on the inside of my survey case while I ate a sandwich on the surface, in daylight, with the hatch latched.

I explained it as a branch pressing the case in the wind. There was no wind. I wrote it down anyway. *Branch. Wind.* Two words, doing the work of a lifetime.

And then there's the corridor. The new one, the one from yesterday, the one that wasn't on any map and wasn't there last month, its walls scored floor to ceiling with tally marks in groups of five, thousands of them, tens of thousands, going back further than my headlamp could reach. I stood in that corridor for a long time doing what I always do, assembling the explanation—*miner's counting gallery, ore tally, some surveyor before me*—and the explanation assembled itself beautifully, and I want it on the record that it was a good explanation, a professional explanation, and that I did not believe one word of it, and that I surveyed the corridor anyway, and that when I counted the freshest group of marks—four strokes and a diagonal, four strokes and a diagonal, over and over, a count in progress—I noticed that the last group had only four strokes.

Waiting for its fifth.

I got out of there. I climbed the ladder into the cold afternoon and latched the hatch and stood on the surface breathing like I'd run a race, and I did the other thing I always do, which is make it small. *Old tally. Old mine. Old village full of old dead habits.* And it almost worked. It would have worked, probably, the way it's worked for seven years, if the church bell hadn't rung at midnight.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. That's tomorrow's entry. Tonight I want to write down the last thing, the thing I've been circling, the thing the table was really about.

I went back through seven years of journal entries last night, and I noticed something I should have noticed years ago. The dates. I have surveyed in every month of the year, every kind of weather, at every hour I could get away with. But the sounds—the drip, the knocking, the voice, the scraping—every single entry, all seven years, falls in a pattern I never saw because I wasn't looking: they cluster. Around the anniversary of my leaving. Around the last week of October, the week Daniel Galloway walked out of Kessel's Hollow and the week, every year since, that the Hollow starts saying his name again.

It's not haunting me. That's what I finally wrote, at the bottom of the table, in ink. *It's not haunting me. It's counting me. I'm on some list down there, and every October somebody checks whether I've come back, and I always come back, and every year the count gets one mark longer.*

Four strokes and a diagonal. Waiting for its fifth.

The bell rang at midnight. One toll. I counted. I always count.

And the worst part—the part I'll deny writing this if anyone ever reads it—is that when the sound faded, I wasn't surprised. I was *expected*. I lay in the dark above the feed store with my heart going like something trapped, and what I felt, underneath the fear, was the awful familiar relief of a child hearing her name called home for supper.

Tomorrow I'm going to the church. The bell was melted down in 1911. I've read the minutes of the parish meeting myself.

Something in that church is still ringing it.
