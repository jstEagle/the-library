## 4. Tomas Vey Signs Nothing Yet

The papers came on a Tuesday, which I have always considered the most dishonest day of the week. Mondays you expect bad news; it arrives wearing its work clothes. But Tuesdays pretend to be nothing days, and that is when the world likes to slip its knife between your ribs.

I found the envelope wedged in my letterbox at an angle no postman would use — flat against the back, as though someone had stood at my gate and pushed it through with care, with intention, the way you'd slide a note under a door for someone sleeping. No stamp. No postmark. Just my name in copperplate so fine it looked engraved: *Mr. Tomas Vey, Kessel's Hollow.*

Nobody has called me *Mister* since my funeral, and I attended that myself, thank you, forty years ago, when the last of the Vey cousins drove up from the coast to bury my mother and decided I'd be easier to mourn than to visit.

Inside: a folder of heavy cream stock, the kind of paper that costs more per sheet than I spend on bread in a month. HALLOW & VEY ESTATES, said the letterhead, in letters like headstones. And beneath it, smaller: *Founding Partners: A. Hallow, J. Vey, R. Merrick, S. Quill.*

I sat down on my own doorstep, which is not something a man of eighty-one does lightly, because getting up again is a negotiation.

J. Vey.

I know that name. I've known it since I was small enough to hide behind my grandmother's skirts, and she had skirts built for hiding behind, wide as church doors. There's a stone in the old parish yard — third row, leaning east like a tired man against a wall — that reads JONATHAN VEY, 1841–1911. My great-granduncle, or some such relation; the Veys never branched much, we just thickened. He died the same week as the others. All four of them. Hallow, Vey, Merrick, Quill. Four stones, four dates within nine days of each other, and the village being what it was — what it is — nobody ever asked why. You didn't ask questions in Kessel's Hollow. Asking questions was how you ended up with answers, and answers were worse company than silence.

My grandmother had one answer she gave away free, the way you'd give away bread heels to the birds. She told me once, kneeling in her garden in the grey light of a November evening, pulling up carrots that had gone woody, that the founders of everything rotten in this valley died in the same week the bell came down. That they'd sold the bell to buy something. She wouldn't say what. She just wiped her hands on her apron and said, "Debts don't die with the debtor, Tommy. They wait for the estate."

I was seven. I thought she meant money.

I am eighty-one, and I am beginning to suspect she did not mean money.

* * *

I took the papers inside and read them at my kitchen table under the good lamp, the one with the green glass shade that makes everything look like it's underwater, which felt appropriate.

The offer was generous. Insultingly generous. Generous the way a fisherman is generous with the worm. They wanted my house, my land, the cottage by the mill race, the strip of orchard where nothing has grown properly since the war — all of it, every acre I've spent seventy years keeping upright out of pure spite — and in exchange they'd pay me enough to buy a small town somewhere warmer and forget this one entirely.

That was the phrase they used. *Forget.* "An opportunity to move forward unencumbered by the past." Unencumbered. As if the past were a rucksack I could set down at the door. As if I hadn't been carrying this village on my back so long I'd grown into the shape of it.

There were clauses. There are always clauses. Most of them were the usual lawyer porridge — easements, mineral rights, access corridors — but clause fourteen stopped me with my tea halfway to my mouth.

*The Purchaser shall assume custodianship of all subterranean structures, natural or constructed, known or unknown, upon transfer of title, and the Vendor releases all claims, responsibilities, and obligations pertaining thereto, in perpetuity.*

Obligations. Pertaining to what? I have owned this land my whole life and the only obligation it's ever given me is the roof, and the roof and I have an understanding. But the word sat there on the page like a stone in a shoe. *Obligations.* Someone had chosen that word. Lawyers don't choose words; words choose lawyers, and this one had been picked out deliberately, the way you'd pick out a key for a lock you couldn't see.

And then there was the signature line. My name printed above it, full and formal, Thomas Ambrose Vey — which is correct, which is exactly right, down to the middle name my mother chose from a hymnal because my father couldn't be bothered to attend the christening. Nobody living knows my middle name. I haven't written it anywhere in thirty years. The bank knows me as T. A. Vey. The council knows me as T. A. Vey. Even my solicitor, God rest his bureaucratic soul, calls me Tom.

Thomas Ambrose Vey, printed in that same fine copperplate, waiting above a line, waiting for ink.

I put the papers down and looked out the window at the dark coming up the valley the way it always does, from the east first, filling the hollow like water fills a cup. Somewhere out there a girl with a headlamp was probably crawling around under the ground, mapping tunnels that shouldn't exist, and more power to her. Ulyana Galloway. Yes, I know who she is. I've watched her come and go for seven years, thinking herself invisible, poor creature, the way all of us thought ourselves invisible once. The Galloways were always good at disappearing. It ran in the family like blue eyes.

She thinks she's exploring. She doesn't know she's inventorying.

* * *

Here is what I did next, and I tell it plainly because a man my age has no business lying to himself about anything anymore; the lies get heavy and my back is shot.

I took the papers to the stove.

Not immediately. First I made tea I didn't drink and ate bread I didn't taste, and I listened to the house do its evening arithmetic — beams ticking, pipes muttering, the wind trying the chimney like a burglar testing a window. Seven years of noises, the girl says in her journal — yes, I've seen her journal, she leaves it in places she thinks are secret, and this village keeps no secrets from its last two residents, whether they know about each other or not. Seven years of explaining sounds away. I've had eighty-one years of it. Settling stone. Swelling wood. The dead don't walk, Tommy, that's just the frost heaving the ground.

But tonight the house was quiet in the wrong way. Quiet like held breath. Quiet like a room where everyone has stopped talking because you walked in.

So I took the folder to the stove, and I opened the fire door, and I held the papers over the coals, and I will admit to you — to whoever reads this, if anyone does, though I suspect the only reader I'll ever have is myself, going over these pages the way the girl goes over her maps — that my hand shook.

Not from fear. Well. From fear. But also from something underneath the fear, something older and more shameful, and it took me a long moment crouched there in the coal-light to name it.

Relief.

For one black, honest second, burning those papers felt like relief. Sign nothing, burn everything, let the valley keep itself, let whatever waits below knock on doors that aren't mine. Let them take the land over my cold ashes. Let the development company and the thing in the cellar fight over the bones while I'm past caring. Eighty-one years is a long time to stand watch, and no one — no one — has ever once relieved me of the post.

The corner of the folder began to brown. The cream stock curled like a leaf in autumn, and the heat breathed up at my face, and I thought: *this is easy. This is the easiest thing I've ever done.*

Then I saw the letterhead through the browning paper. HALLOW & VEY. J. Vey. Jonathan Vey, 1841–1911, leaning east in the third row. My blood, my name, carved into a firm that wants to buy the ground he's buried in — wants it badly enough to print a dead man's name on living paper — and I understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming across a trough, that burning these papers wouldn't end anything.

You cannot burn a debt by burning the bill. My grandmother told me that too, though she said it about the butcher and the coal merchant, and I laughed at the time. Everything she ever said was about something else. That's the trouble with grandmothers. They speak in parables because the truth is too heavy to lift directly, so they roll it to you in pieces and trust you'll assemble it before you need it.

If I burned the papers, they would send more. If they sent more, they would come themselves. And if they came — and I have a feeling, an old man's feeling, the kind that lives in the teeth and the knuckles — that whoever came would not be carrying a briefcase and a smile. Or rather: they would carry both, and both would be wrong.

Besides. There was the other matter. The matter I have not yet said aloud, even here, even to this page, but which sat beside me at the stove like a second man wearing my coat.

Clause fourteen. *Obligations pertaining thereto, in perpetuity.*

If I sign, the obligations pass to them. To Hallow & Vey Estates. To the names on the stones. Whatever this valley owes — and it owes something, it has owed something since 1911, since the bell came down and four men died in one week and my grandmother started speaking in parables — whatever it owes would become their problem. Their custody. Their perpetuity.

And if I don't sign, the obligation stays where it has always been.

With me. With the last Vey. With the last name left standing in a ledger I have never seen but have felt my whole life the way you feel weather coming in an old scar.

* * *

I closed the stove door on the unburnt papers and sat back down, and my knees complained, and I let them, because complaint is the last honest conversation an old body has.

Here is my position, stated plainly, for the record, for whatever record survives me:

I will not sign. Not tomorrow, not Tuesday, not ever, and not because I'm brave — I want that understood. I am not brave. I have been frightened continuously since 1953 and I see no reason to stop now. I will not sign because signing is a kind of surrender, and surrender requires believing the other side will honor terms, and I do not believe anything honors terms in this valley except gravity and rot.

But I will not burn them either. Because the papers are evidence. Evidence of what, I can't yet say — but a man who has kept a village alive on stubbornness alone learns to keep documents. The girl maps the tunnels. I map the paper. Between us we might assemble the whole shape of the thing before it finishes assembling us.

I put the folder in the biscuit tin, under the tin, beneath the false bottom my mother used for the ration coupons she couldn't bear to throw away, and I set the tin on the high shelf where the mice have never managed to climb, on account of I greased the shelf bracket myself in 1987 and re-greased it every spring since. A man defends what he can defend. The shelf is what I can defend.

Then I sat by the window with the lamp off, watching the dark finish its work in the hollow, and I did what I have done every night for eighty-one years, which is count.

Sheep, when I was young. Bells, now. Don't ask me why bells; I couldn't tell you, except that my grandmother counted them too, lips moving at the window some nights, and when I asked her what she was counting she said, "What's left," and went to bed.

Tonight I got to four in my head before I heard it.

Not imagined. Not settling stone, not swelling wood, not frost-heave, not wind. A single stroke, deep and round and wrong, rolling up out of the ground itself — out of the direction of the old church, out of the direction of the cellar, out of the direction of everything my family spent a century not discussing — and passing through the floor, through the chair, through my ribs, and ringing in the bones of my ears like I was the bell, like I was the metal, like I was the thing being struck.

One toll. At half past ten at night, which is no hour for a bell, especially a bell that was melted into shell casings in 1911 and shipped off to kill strangers.

I sat very still. Across the valley, in the dark, I could just make out the lit window of the Galloway cottage — the sister's place, empty these three years, though there was light in it tonight, moving, a headlamp beam sweeping, and I understood that the girl was home, and awake, and had heard it too.

Good, I thought. Good. Then: *poor creature.* Then, with a shame I'll not soon be rid of: *better her than me.*

No. Not that. Strike that from the record. An old man is allowed his cowardice for the space of one breath, and no longer.

I got up, knees cracking like the stove, and took the biscuit tin down from the shelf, and looked at the papers one more time in the dark, by feel, the way you'd check on a sleeping animal you don't trust.

*Thomas Ambrose Vey,* said the copperplate, patient as a gravestone.

"Not yet," I told it. "You'll have your answer when I'm good and ready, and not one hour sooner. I've outlasted a war, two governments, and every Vey that ever doubted me. I can outlast a letterhead."

The house said nothing. Outside, the dark lay on the valley like a lid.

And far below — below the church, below the cellar, below the mapped and unmapped dark where a girl with a headlamp was surely writing something in a journal she thinks is secret — I heard, faint as memory, faint as my grandmother's voice across a carrot bed fifty years gone, a sound like a pencil moving across paper.

Writing.

Adding a name to some list I have never seen but whose existence I would swear to on any Bible you put in front of me.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would go and find that girl. Tomorrow I would show her the letterhead, and the clause, and the middle name no living person should know. Because if the counting has started — and it has started, God help us, it has started — then somebody in this valley had better finally start asking the questions my grandmother spent her whole life answering in parables.

Starting with the oldest one of all:

What, exactly, did we sell the bell to pay for?
