## 7. The Company of Old Names

The county archive occupied the ground floor of the old courthouse, a building that smelled of wet plaster and the particular despair of microfiche machines. I'd been in and out of it a dozen times over the years, usually chasing property lines, the boring cartography of who owned what above ground. I'd never once needed the business registries. Above-ground things had never interested me much.

Now I sat with a ledger of incorporated companies open in front of me, my coffee gone cold, and I read the name *Hallow & Vey Estates, Ltd.* for the fourth time, as if repetition might soften it.

It didn't. Names don't work that way. Names only ever sharpen.

The registry entry was brief, the way old bureaucratic things are brief, as though the clerk who wrote it had been in a hurry to be done with the whole business. Incorporated 1908. Principal place of business: Kessel's Hollow. Purpose: land acquisition and development. And then the founding partners, listed in that formal, comma-spliced way that makes dead men sound like a grocery order:

*Edmund Hallow. Josiah Vey. Ambrose Kessel. Ruth Hallow.*

Four names. I sat back in the creaking wooden chair and did the arithmetic I already knew the answer to, because I'd spent seven years in the tunnels doing arithmetic with tally marks.

Edmund Hallow, Josiah Vey, Ambrose Kessel, Ruth Hallow. The same four names, in the same order, carved into the cellar wall at the end of the corridor that hadn't existed last month. I'd photographed them. I'd measured the depth of the carving, the way I measured everything, because measurement was how I kept the world from sliding sideways. The grooves were clean. Fresh-looking. As fresh as something could look when it had been cut into limestone sometime before the Great War.

I turned pages. The registry cross-referenced to the probate index, and the probate index was where the pattern stopped being coincidence and started being a schedule.

*Edmund Hallow, d. March 1911.*
*Josiah Vey, d. March 1911.*
*Ambrose Kessel, d. March 1911.*

Same week. I found the newspaper notice in the bound volumes of the *Kessel's Hollow Courier*, tucked between an advertisement for a cure-all tonic and a report on the county fair. The headline was almost apologetic in its smallness: **THREE LOCAL NOTABLES TAKEN IN ONE WEEK.** The article attributed it to "a fever of unusual virulence," which is the sort of phrase newspapers use when they don't want to look too closely at what they're printing. There was no mention of Ruth Hallow's death at all, which struck me as its own kind of data. A woman who dies without a death notice is either unimportant to the paper or important to something else.

I copied everything into my field journal, in pencil, in my cramped surveyor's hand. Dates. Names. The address of the company's registered office, which was the Hallow house on the green, which had burned in 1912, which had never been rebuilt, which I had mapped the foundations of in my second year underground and noted, in a marginal comment I'd since come to regret, *unremarkable.*

Nothing down there was unremarkable. I'd just spent seven years teaching myself not to see.

Here is the thing about paranoia that nobody tells you: it feels exactly like clarity. It arrives with the same clean click, the same sense of pieces fitting, the same relief. *Oh,* you think. *So that's what's been happening.* And for a moment the world is orderly again, and you are grateful, and only later do you notice that the order the pieces have snapped into is the order of a trap.

I sat in the archive until the lights went off over the periodicals section, and the archivist, a young man with a beard he was clearly still negotiating with, told me gently that they closed at five.

"One more question," I said. "Can a company that dissolved in 1911 still make an offer on land?"

He blinked. "Legally? No. It'd have to be reconstituted. New registration, new—" He stopped, because I'd already turned the registry book back around and put my finger on the entry.

There was a second line beneath the dissolution record. Ink that was not old. A re-registration, dated fourteen months ago, in a hand so neat it might have been typeset: *Hallow & Vey Estates, Ltd. — reconstituted. Founding partners as before.*

*As before.*

"Who filed this?" I asked.

The archivist leaned in, squinted, and shrugged with the particular helplessness of someone who processes a thousand documents a week. "The filing address is just a PO box in the county seat. It happens. Old family firms get revived all the time when land comes up for sale. Somebody finds the original charter in a trunk and thinks, why not." He smiled, wanting to be reassuring. "It's mostly a formality."

"Mostly," I said.

Walking back to the Hollow in the last of the light, I did the other arithmetic, the kind I liked less. Fourteen months ago was when I'd started finding the new corridor. Fourteen months ago was when Tomas Vey, the last man left in a village of nine, had first mentioned to me at the post office that someone had written to him about buying his land. Fourteen months. The company hadn't come back because the land was suddenly valuable. The land had become valuable because the company came back. Or—and I turned this over as I walked, testing its weight, hating how well it balanced—the company had never left at all, and the paper trail was just the part of it that bothered to exist above ground.

The tally marks in the corridor had been counting down. I'd assumed they were counting days. Standing on the green with the ruined church behind me and its bell-less tower black against the dusk, I understood that I had been assuming a great many things, and that assumption was a luxury whose bill was coming due.

My phone buzzed. A text from a number I didn't have saved, though I recognized the country-code prefix of the county seat, and the message was four words long:

*Your name is noted.*

I stood there for a long moment, thumb hovering, the way you hover over a wound deciding whether to touch it. Then I deleted the message, blocked the number, and told myself, in the firm tone I used for explaining away noises, that it was probably a wrong number.

It was not a wrong number. But I wasn't ready to know that yet, and the mind protects itself with the tools it has. Mine had always been explanations. I was running low on those.

* * *

Tomas Vey kept his mother's good teacups in the cabinet above the sink, and he took one down on the morning the second set of papers arrived, because he had decided that if he was going to be bullied, he would be bullied like a gentleman.

The envelope was heavy cream stock, heavier than the first one. The first had come in October, polite as a church newsletter: *Hallow & Vey Estates, Ltd. wishes to express interest in your property.* He'd read it twice, laughed once, and put it in the drawer with his mother's recipes, which was where he put things he intended to deal with later and never did. But the second envelope had come by hand—by hand, delivered by a courier who hadn't waited for a signature—and that was a different grammar entirely. Letters come from offices. Hands come from people who want to watch you open them.

He made tea he didn't want and laid the papers out on the kitchen table, in the good light from the window, the way his mother had laid out linens to check for moths.

The terms were better this time. That was what frightened him. The first offer had been insultingly low, the kind of number you offer a man you expect to be stupid or desperate, and Tomas Vey was neither, whatever else he was. This offer was fair. It was more than fair. It was the kind of number that said *we have done our research and we know exactly what this land is worth to you, and to us it is worth considerably more, and we would prefer not to explain why.*

The letterhead bore the names. He'd noticed them the first time and told himself it was coincidence, because Hallow and Vey were old county names, common as dirt, and half the gravestones in the churchyard carried one or the other. But now he sat with the letterhead in front of him and let himself remember the specific gravestones. Edmund Hallow, third row, under the yew. Josiah Vey—his own great-uncle, that one, though the family didn't talk about Josiah, hadn't talked about Josiah since before Tomas's father was born, which in the Vey family meant there was something to talk about. Ambrose Kessel, whose stone was the tallest and the cleanest, as though the weather had agreed to make an exception for him.

Four names on the letterhead. He'd checked the incorporation papers once, years ago, out of the same idle thoroughness that made him read every word of every contract. All four partners had died in the spring of 1911. He'd assumed the modern company had simply taken the old name for its gravitas, the way new banks put up brass plaques with old-sounding names. Companies did that. The world did that. The dead were a branding resource.

He read the new terms twice. Then he read the cover letter, and it was the cover letter that made him take his mother's teacup down from the cabinet.

*Dear Mr. Vey,* it began, in the same immaculate hand, *We note that your solicitor has not yet been engaged. We would be pleased to recommend one. We would further note that time is a factor for all parties, though not, we assure you, equally for all.*

He read that last sentence four times. It was the kind of sentence a lawyer would call unenforceably vague and a grandmother would call a threat, and Tomas Vey was seventy-nine years old and had been both those things in his time, in the sense of having been threatened by grandmothers, and he knew exactly what it was.

*Time is a factor for all parties, though not equally.*

Because they had time. That was the thing, wasn't it. That was the whole architecture of it. Whatever was wearing the names Hallow and Vey and Kessel like a borrowed coat had been down in the dark since 1911 at the latest, and it could wait out anything—wait out Tomas, wait out the village, wait out the slow erosion of everything above ground until only the patient and the buried remained. It didn't need him to sign. It only needed everyone else to leave, and everyone else was leaving, one bell toll at a time, and then the papers would be a formality, and then not even a formality.

He thought about burning them. He actually got up, teacup in hand, and stood at the wood stove with the envelope in his free hand, and the stove was cold because it was a mild morning, and he thought, *I'll light it, I'll burn the lot, and that will be my answer.*

He didn't. He stood there a full minute and then set the envelope back on the table, and the reason he didn't burn them was the same reason he hadn't left when the others left, the same reason he'd stayed in the Hollow through seven winters of thinning company: because running was a thing his family had done once, in 1911, and the family had been paying for it ever since, in ways nobody said aloud but everybody arranged their lives around. The Veys didn't run. The Veys stayed and kept the accounts. His grandmother had made him memorize that, along with the psalms, and he'd thought it was just stubbornness dressed up as duty.

His grandmother had known things. He was beginning to suspect she'd known them the way you know the weight of a stone in your pocket—constantly, and without wanting to.

He put the papers in the drawer with the first set and the recipes, and he wrote a reply in his own hand, in pencil, because pencil was honest: *Mr. Vey thanks the company for its offer and will respond when his solicitor has reviewed the deed.* He had no solicitor. He had not had a solicitor since he sold his sister's house in 1998. But the sentence had a door in it, and a man his age learned to leave doors in his sentences, because you never knew which side of one you'd end up on.

He sealed the envelope and set it on the hall table for posting, and then he stood in his kitchen and listened.

The house was quiet. The village was quiet. The whole Hollow had the held-breath quiet of a place where nine people were each privately pretending they hadn't heard the bell, and the pretense was getting harder to maintain, the way a lie gets heavier every day you carry it.

And then, faint, from the direction of the church—too faint to be anything, he told himself, too faint to be anything at all—he heard the sound of metal remembering its shape.

* * *

I found Tomas on his porch that evening, because after the text message I had made a list of everyone in the Hollow whose name was on something old, and his was the only one I couldn't stop thinking about.

"Vey," I said, from the bottom of his steps. "You got a second letter."

It wasn't a question, and he didn't treat it as one. He just looked at me for a long moment, this old man in a cardigan the color of wet bark, and then he nodded once, toward the chair across from him. "You've been in the tunnels again," he said.

"Every week for seven years."

"And they've been changing."

It was my turn to be still. "You know that."

"I've known that since August." He folded his hands. "My grandmother told me the tunnels were the village's memory. She said a place forgets from the top down and remembers from the bottom up. I thought she was being poetic. She was never poetic in her life." He looked out past me, toward the church tower, and I watched him decide something. "What did you find down there, Miss Galloway?"

The name landed strangely. Not *Daniel*, which no one in this village had said to my face in seven years, and not the careful nothing that most people managed. Just my name, plain, like it was the most obvious thing in the world. I'd forgotten what that felt like. I'd forgotten that it was supposed to feel like anything.

"Tally marks," I said. "A door bound in bell-metal. And a company that died in 1911 trying to buy the village back."

Tomas Vey nodded slowly, the way you nod at a diagnosis you'd already suspected. "My grandmother said the cellar keeps accounts," he said. "I think it's time somebody read them."

Behind us, in the dark, in the tower that had held no bell for a hundred and thirteen years, something waited—patiently, the way the dead wait—for us to finish saying the word *accounts* out loud, so it could begin counting again.
