## 8. Second Toll

### Ulyana

I want to tell you that I slept after the first toll. I want to tell you I did the sensible thing—the thing any rational adult does when a bell that was melted down in 1911 rings once at midnight in a village with four residents and a church roof like a colander. I want to say I packed a bag, got in my car, and drove until Kessel's Hollow was a rumor in my rearview mirror.

What I actually did was make tea.

That's the truth of me, if you want it. Seven years of mapping tunnels under a dying village, and my response to the impossible is not flight but *documentation*. I sat at my kitchen table with a mug going cold and wrote in my journal: *Bell rang once. 12:00 exactly. No wind. No mechanism. Confirmed no bell exists.* As if writing it down made it a data point instead of a wound.

The tea was chamomile, which tells you how seriously I took my own survival.

I told myself: one ring could be anything. A truck on the county road, compression of metal somewhere, an echo with ambition. My whole life has been built on explanations—good ones, load-bearing ones—and one anomalous sound wasn't going to bring the structure down.

Then, at 2:14 in the morning, it rang twice.

Not at midnight. Not at any hour that meant anything to a clock. Twice, close together, the second note slightly flat, slightly *hungry*, the way a voice goes when it's been talking too long and likes it.

And here's the detail I keep circling, the one I've written three times and crossed out twice: the sound didn't come from the church. It came from *underneath* it. From below. From the direction of everything I'd spent seven years pretending was just geology.

I stood at my window with my tea forgotten and watched the village. Four occupied houses. Three lights came on, one by one, like eyes opening. The fourth house stayed dark, because its owner had died in March and nobody had gotten around to the paperwork, which in Kessel's Hollow means nobody ever will.

Three lights. Three neighbors awake and pretending they weren't.

I know that pretense intimately. I invented half its techniques.

### Dora Quill

Dora Quill had lived in Kessel's Hollow for sixty-one years, which she would tell you was fifty-eight years too many, delivered in the tone of a woman who has never once considered leaving.

She heard both rings. She was awake for the first one anyway—her hip saw to that most nights—and the second one went through her like a knitting needle.

Her mother had told her about the bell. Not the melted-down bell; the *other* story, the one you told children so they'd come in before dark. *When the bell calls your name, Dora, you answer or you're taken answering late.* Her mother had died believing nothing, the way sensible people do, and Dora had inherited the belief along with the house, keeping it in a drawer with the good scissors, never used, never thrown out.

At 2:15 she stood in her kitchen in her nightgown and listened to the silence where a third ring should have been.

"Two," she said aloud, to no one. "It said two."

She didn't decide to leave. That's what she'd have told anyone who asked, if anyone had asked, if anyone had stayed. Deciding is a thing you do with reasons, and she had none. What happened was simpler and worse: she found herself moving. Dressing. Pulling the suitcase from under the bed—the one she'd sworn she'd use for holidays, never had—and filling it without looking at what her hands chose. Cardigan. Photographs. The tin of her husband's ashes, because Harold wasn't going to spend eternity in a parish that rang bells it didn't own.

By 3:40 her car was loaded. By 4:00 she was at the end of the lane, and she did something then that she hadn't done in eleven years: she looked at the Fenwick place, where the lights were also on, also moving, also packing.

She raised one hand. Agnes Fenwick, framed in her doorway, raised one back.

Neither of them waved. Neither of them spoke. There was nothing to say that wouldn't require admitting what they'd both heard, and Dora Quill had spent sixty-one years perfecting the art of not admitting things. She was, as it turned out, only perfecting it up to a deadline.

She drove south with her headlights off for the first mile—not out of fear of being seen, though that too, but because some animal part of her understood that light draws attention, and attention, tonight, was currency she couldn't afford.

At the county line she stopped the car and cried for twenty minutes, and could not have told you why, except that it felt less like grief than like a debt being forgiven.

### Agnes Fenwick

Agnes stayed another hour, which she would later understand was the whole difference between them.

Not courage. Curiosity—the bad kind, the kind that keeps you standing in a doorway watching a neighbor's taillights disappear while something in the walls of your house counts.

She'd planned to stay through winter. Everyone said so; she'd said so herself, loudly, at the harvest supper two years ago, the last time anyone bothered with one. *I'll be buried from that churchyard or not at all,* she'd announced, and old Tomas Vey had raised his glass and said, *Here, here,* and everyone had pretended this was a plan rather than a surrender.

But the bell had said two, and Agnes had spent the hour since then doing arithmetic she hated.

One ring for the first. Two for the second. And there were—she counted them the way you count your own fingers, needing them to come out right—four residents left. Herself, Tomas, the Galloway girl who'd come back and thought nobody knew who she was (Agnes had known Daniel Galloway's face the moment she saw it walking around in a stranger's body, and had said nothing, because saying something was a door you couldn't close), and the Hollis boy, twenty-six, who mostly existed as a shape behind lit curtains.

Four residents. A count that had reached two.

She didn't finish the arithmetic. She made herself not finish it. Instead she packed—slower than Dora, telling herself each item into the bag was a choice, a free choice, a woman exercising perfectly ordinary judgment—and at 5:10 she pulled out of her lane past the dark Hollis house, past the Vey farmhouse where a single lamp burned steady and stubborn as its owner, past the church whose tower held nothing, nothing, nothing.

At the crossroads she stopped and looked back once.

The village lay gray and small under the last of the night, and it looked, for the first time in her seventy years, exactly like what it was: a place that had already ended, still going through the motions, waiting for someone to say it out loud.

She drove east. She did not look back again, and the not-looking cost her more than the looking would have.

### Ulyana

I watched both cars leave.

From my window, wrapped in a blanket I refused to admit was for comfort, I tracked Dora Quill's ancient Volvo south and Agnes Fenwick's hatchback east, and I felt the village contract around me like a fist closing.

Here's what nobody tells you about being invisible: it works fine until the counting starts. Then invisibility isn't safety. It's just being uncounted *so far*.

I did the math myself, sitting there, because apparently I'm constitutionally incapable of not doing the math. Four residents at midnight. Two gone by dawn. A bell that rings in sequence. I'm a surveyor; patterns are my trade. This pattern had a shape, and the shape was a countdown, and countdowns end.

I should have left with them. I knew it even then—I stood in my kitchen at 6 a.m. with my car keys in my hand and every rational faculty I owned voting yes.

But Marta's cottage sits empty at the north end of the village, and my sister's name is Galloway too, and the ledger I'd found in the vestry two days ago had *Galloway* struck through in brown ink that was dry before either of us was born.

You can't outrun a name. Believe me. I tried for thirty-one years, under two of them.

So I put the keys down, and I did the only brave cowardly thing available to me: I opened my journal to a fresh page and started a new column.

*Toll 1 — 12:00 — ?*
*Toll 2 & 3 — 2:14 — Quill departs. Fenwick departs.*

Underneath, after a long moment, I wrote: *If it's names, whose?*

And underneath that, in handwriting I barely recognized as mine, smaller, like something whispered:

*Find out what happens to the ones who stay.*

### Tomas Vey

Tomas Vey heard every ring and did not turn on a single light.

He sat in his kitchen with the development company's papers squared neatly in front of him—he kept them squared; disorder was for people with time to waste—and he listened to his village empty itself car by car, and he poured himself two fingers of whisky he'd been saving for a better occasion, on the theory that no better occasion was coming.

His grandmother had a word for nights like this. She'd had a word for everything, his grandmother, most of them in the old dialect, all of them fatalistic. She'd have called this *the thinning*, and she'd have said it the way you'd say *harvest*: not as tragedy, but as season.

He'd promised himself he wouldn't sign. He'd promised Ulyana Galloway, too, when she'd come to his door with her maps and her urgent eyes, speaking of ledgers and bells like a woman reading weather. He liked her. He'd known her grandfather, and the family resemblance was a strange comfort—same stubborn jaw, same habit of looking at you slightly sideways, as if checking whether you were real.

But liking her didn't change the arithmetic, and Tomas Vey had never flinched from arithmetic. Two rings, two departures. The company wanted his signature by Friday. And somewhere beneath the church, in a cellar he'd been carefully not thinking about for eighty years, something patient was keeping books of its own.

He looked at the pen beside the papers. Good pen. His solicitor's gift, ironically enough.

"Not yet," he told it, and turned off the lamp, and sat in the dark listening to the house settle around him like an old dog lying down.

If the bell rang again, he decided, he'd count along. Someone in this village ought to know the score.

### The Vestry

The ledger did not need a witness, but it had one anyway, in the sense that all things in Kessel's Hollow are witnessed eventually.

In the sealed vestry, in the drawer where Ulyana Galloway had found it, the 1911 parish ledger lay open to the page no living hand had turned to. The old column of names ran down in faded brown—struck through, all of them, collected, settled—and beside it the newer column waited in its terrible patience.

Sometime between the second ring and the third, without any hand near it, the pencil moved.

A stroke appeared beside *Quill*.

A stroke appeared beside *Fenwick*.

The graphite gleamed faintly, wet-bright, as if freshly laid, and if you had bent close—you didn't; nobody did—you might have noticed the strokes weren't quite straight. They curved, very slightly, the way marks do when made by something writing with effort, with care, with the deliberate pressure of a hand that wants its work legible.

Below *Fenwick*, two names remained unmarked in the fresh column.

*Vey.*

*Galloway.*

The vestry air moved, though the room was sealed. Somewhere far below, in the dark under the church, something turned a page of its own, and settled in to wait for morning, and the morning after that, and however many mornings the count required.

It had waited since 1911.

It could wait for Thursday.
