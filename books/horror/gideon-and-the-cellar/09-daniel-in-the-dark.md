## 9. Daniel in the Dark

I have never told anyone about the county line.

Not my therapist in Leeds, who got the sanitized version—estrangement, small town, family who couldn't hold the shape of me. Not Marta, obviously. Not the online forums of urban explorers where I post under a handle and never a face. Nobody has ever heard the part where something chased me for eleven miles calling my name in a voice like wet gravel learning to be human.

There's a reason I've kept it. Several reasons, actually, and they all used to fit neatly into the same box: *if you say it out loud, it becomes real, and if it becomes real, then you have to decide what to do about it.* I built a whole life on that principle. A life with excellent load-bearing denial.

But tonight the ledger sits on my kitchen table with *Galloway* written in it twice—once in brown ink gone the color of old blood, once in pencil so fresh the graphite still shines—and denial is no longer load-bearing. It's not even decorative. So I'm going to tell you about the night I left, and I'm going to tell it properly, because I think the thing in the cellar has been waiting seven years for me to say it out loud, and I am done giving it the pleasure of my silence.

* * *

Seven years ago I was Daniel Galloway, and Daniel Galloway was very good at being nobody.

That's a skill, by the way. People don't talk about it enough. Being nobody takes craft. It takes years of practice at lowering your voice half an octave before you speak, at laughing one beat too late, at wearing your sleeves long in summer. It takes learning which hedgerows to walk behind and which doors to enter through, and in Kessel's Hollow—a village of forty-three souls when I was born, down to nineteen by the time I left—the hedgerows are generous and every door has a back way in.

I came out to my mother on a Tuesday in October, because Tuesdays were her bad days and I'd learned that bad days made her softer, or maybe just too tired to fight. It didn't matter. She looked at me across the kitchen table—at the dress I'd finally worn out of my room after three years of wearing it only after midnight, only with the curtains drawn, only in a house where everyone else was asleep—and she said, "Not under my roof."

Four words. Efficient. My mother always did hate waste.

Marta wasn't there. Marta had left for Manchester two years earlier and we'd already begun the slow estrangement that siblings perfect when they inherit the same wound from opposite ends. My father had been dead six years, which meant there was exactly one person whose roof it was, and her verdict was final.

So I packed what fit in a rucksack. Which turned out to be most of what mattered: clothes that were mine now, honestly mine; my grandfather's brass compass, which he'd left to me and not to anyone else, though nobody in the family ever discussed why; and a torch with fresh batteries, because even at nineteen I knew the tunnels better than anyone alive, and I knew the fastest way out of Kessel's Hollow was down.

Here's a thing about dying villages: they hollow from the inside. The shops close first, then the church stops holding services, then the houses go dark one by one until the place is mostly cellar and memory. Kessel's Hollow sat on top of a honeycomb of workings—chalk mines, some said, or smugglers' runs, or plague pits, depending on which pub conversation you eavesdropped on. The truth, as far as my seven years of mapping have established, is that it's older than any of those explanations and less interested in being understood.

But when you're nineteen and your mother has just unmade you at her own kitchen table, a tunnel is just a tunnel, and it goes where you want to go, and nobody down there knows your name.

Or so I believed. Hold onto that word. *Believed.* I'll be taking it back shortly.

* * *

I went in through the culvert behind the old tannery at eleven at night, because the tannery's back wall had crumbled years before and I'd been using that entrance since I was twelve, back when sneaking underground was just a boy's hobby and not a rehearsal for disappearing.

The first hour was ordinary. That's important too—I need you to understand that the horror didn't arrive all at once, waving its arms. It arrived the way cold water does, at the ankles first.

Ordinary: the drip of water through the chalk, which I catalogued in my head as I always did—*north gallery, third junction, active seep*—because naming things is how I keep the dark from naming me. Ordinary: the smell of wet stone and old timber. Ordinary: my own breathing, amplified, slightly wrong-sounding, the way it always is underground.

Then, at the fourth junction, where the passage forks toward the church end, I heard my name.

"Daniel."

Just that. Once. Spoken quietly, almost kindly, the way you'd call someone back who'd walked off without saying goodbye.

And here is the shameful part, the part I have rewritten a hundred times trying to make myself look less foolish: I answered. Not out loud. But in my head, in the reflex of a lifetime, I thought *what?*—and whatever was down there felt the answer land. I know this because the second time it spoke, it was closer.

"Daniel. You've forgotten something."

I want to be fair to my past self. There were reasonable explanations available. Echoes do strange things in chalk; sound travels along galleries for hundreds of metres and arrives stripped of context, so that a voice you heard an hour ago can seem to come from beside you. I'd heard my own footsteps overtake me more than once down there. And I was nineteen, exhausted, grieving, running on adrenaline and half a packet of biscuits. The mind offers you voices the way a drowning man grabs driftwood.

So I did what I had spent my whole life doing. I explained it away. I put my head down and walked faster.

It called again at the flooded gallery. "Daniel." Closer. And again at the ladder shaft near the millstone bed—"Daniel Galloway"—full name now, both barrels, the way teachers used to say it when I was in trouble. And each time, the voice was a little more practiced. A little more *warm*. Like something rehearsing humanity from the outside, working its way in through the syllables.

By the fifth mile I had stopped explaining. That's the moment I mark as the true end of my childhood—not the kitchen table, not the dress, but the moment in the dark when I ran out of rationalizations and had to simply be afraid, with nothing between me and the fear except my own legs.

I ran. I won't pretend otherwise. I ran through passages I'd crawled through calmly a hundred times, banging my shoulder on a rib of rock I could have named in my sleep, and the voice ran with me—or rather, it didn't run, it *kept pace*, unhurried, patient as interest accruing, and it said my name at intervals so regular they might have been measured. Every four hundred paces. I counted. Counting is another thing I do instead of screaming.

"Daniel."

Four hundred paces.

"Daniel."

Somewhere around the eighth mile I realized the horrible geometry of it: the voice was herding me. Not chasing—herding. Every time I drifted toward a side gallery, the name would come from that direction, gentle as a sheepdog's eye, and I'd swerve back to the main run without deciding to. It was steering me along the one passage that led out of the parish. I understood that much even then, in the animal part of my brain. Whatever it was, it wanted me gone.

You'd think that would be comforting. It wasn't. Being expelled is worse than being hunted. Hunted means you're food. Expelled means you're *refuse*—or worse, means you've broken something, defaulted on something, and the expulsion is the collection.

At the tenth mile the tunnels ended. They genuinely end there—I've mapped it since; the workings stop dead at a fault line, beyond which the chalk is solid for a quarter mile to the parish boundary. There's a rusted iron ladder up to a field drain, and above the drain, stars. Real ones. I came up out of the ground like something being born badly, tore my hands on the drain grating, and lay in a turnip field gasping under a sky that had never once said my name.

The voice stopped at the fault line. I know because I stood there, bleeding, listening, and the last thing it said—it said it clearly, no echo, no distortion, right against the back of my teeth—

"Daniel. We'll keep count."

Then nothing. Just wind in the turnips, and eleven miles between me and the county line, which I crossed at dawn on foot, and which felt, absurdly, like a border crossing out of myself. On the far side of the bridge over the Grayle I bought a coffee at a petrol station and the kid behind the counter asked my name for the loyalty card and I said—

Well. That's the other reason I've never told this story. Because the true version is that I stood there with the pen hovering over the card and I heard, faintly, from underneath the floor—from *underneath the floor*, thirty miles from any tunnel I knew of—four hundred paces' worth of patience, waiting to see what I'd write.

And I wrote *Ulyana.*

First time. Ever. In a petrol station at six in the morning with shaking hands, while something below the earth took note.

* * *

Here is what I understood only last week, standing in the vestry with the ledger open in front of me: I thought I had escaped that night. I thought the story was *girl flees village, girl reinvents self, girl builds life elsewhere.* Clean arc. Satisfying. Publishable.

But I came back. Seven times a year, some years. More, lately. For seven years I told myself it was the mapping—the surveyor's itch, the incompleteness of an unmapped system, the pure professional pleasure of putting names on blank space. And all of that is true. I do love it. The tunnels are the first place I ever felt competent, the first place the world made sense as a set of relationships—this passage feeds that one, this collapse blocks that route. Underground, everything is honest about what it connects to.

But honesty compels, as the thing in the cellar keeps reminding me. So let me be honest:

I came back because it called me, and some traitorous chamber of my heart leaned toward the calling like a plant toward a window.

Because for nineteen years I lived in a village that knew me as Daniel, and for seven years I've lived in cities where I'm Ulyana to everyone and no one, and there is exactly one entity in this world that has known me as *both*—that watched me become myself and never once pretended otherwise. It used my dead name like a hook, yes. But a hook only works on something that swims toward it.

God, that's ugly to write. Let me sit with it a moment.

No—I won't soften it, because softening it is how the cellar wins. Here is the ugliest sentence I own: *part of me kept returning because it was the only place where the whole of my life existed in the same room.* The village held Daniel. The city held Ulyana. The tunnels held the seam between them, and the thing in the dark held the thread, and I followed the thread home year after year like a fool following her own blood back to a leech.

Except—

Except that's not quite it either, and I owe myself better than a false confession. Because there's a difference between returning to a captor and returning to a crime scene. And that's what it was, isn't it? That's what Kessel's Hollow is. Something happened down there—in 1911, in the vestry, in the cellar beneath the church—and my family was part of it, and I fled the scene at nineteen without knowing I was fleeing anything but my mother's face. The voice calling *Daniel* wasn't longing. It was bookkeeping. It was a creditor confirming the debtor's address.

And I kept coming back the way people keep driving past the house where something happened to them. Not because they want in. Because they need to see it. To stand outside it and say: *there. That's the place. That's where it began, and I am not in it anymore.*

Except I am in it. My name is in the ledger. The pencil column is filling. And the thing I mistook for a haunting of my past turns out to be a lien on my future.

* * *

Tonight I did something I should have done seven years ago. I sat down with my survey journals—all fourteen volumes, dated, cross-referenced, obsessive—and I read them not as maps but as testimony.

Volume one, seven years ago, three weeks after I left: *"Heard knocking in the north gallery. Probably settling stone."*

Settling stone doesn't knock in groups of three. Settling stone doesn't pause, as if listening, between the third knock and the fourth.

Volume two: *"Voice-like sounds near the flooded gallery. Echo phenomenon. Note: check acoustics with proper equipment."* I never checked. I owned the equipment. I checked the humidity instead.

Volume five, the year I started hormones, the year my voice settled into its true register for the first time: *"Something repeated a word down the east run. Couldn't make it out. Possibly my imagination."* It wasn't a word. I know that now. It was a name, spoken in a register I hadn't earned yet, and it frightened me so badly I logged it as weather.

Every volume, the same pattern. Every anomaly explained, filed, buried—tally marks of my own, struck through in the dried brown ink of rationalization. I have spent seven years doing to the truth exactly what this village did in 1911: writing it down and then striking it through and hoping the ink dries fast enough.

Well. The ink hasn't dried. The pencil column proves that.

I closed volume fourteen and looked at the ledger on my table, at *Galloway* sitting there in two centuries of handwriting, and I said—to the kitchen, to the dark under the floor, to whatever keeps count—

"My name is Ulyana Nystrom-Galloway. I came back because I chose to. Write that down."

The house did not answer. The pipes did not settle. Somewhere far beneath me, so far it might have been my pulse, might have been memory, might have been four hundred paces of patience—something turned a page.

And then, from the direction of the ruined church, thin as wire and clear as a bell:

One toll.

Which is impossible, because the bell was melted down in 1911.

I know because I checked. Twice.
