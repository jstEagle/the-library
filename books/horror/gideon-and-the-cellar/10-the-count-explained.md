## 10. The Count Explained

I have lived in Kessel's Hollow for eighty-one years, which means I have spent eighty-one years being the youngest person in every room, and now that everyone else has gone or died, I am finally also the oldest, and I can tell you that neither distinction is worth the paper it's printed on.

The girl came to my door on a Tuesday. Ulyana Galloway — though she didn't say the surname at first, and I confess I pretended not to know it, because that is what we do in this village. We pretend not to know things until pretending becomes impossible, and then we pretend we always knew it was impossible.

She stood on my step with her maps rolled under one arm like a soldier carrying a rifle she no longer believed in, and she said, "Mr. Vey. The bell rang last night."

"I know," I said.

"It rang in 1911 too. Didn't it? Before they melted it down."

"Come in before the wind gets ideas," I said, because some conversations you cannot have on a doorstep, where the houses lean in to listen. My house listens worst of all. It was built by a man who owed money to everyone, including, if family legend holds, the ground beneath it.

* * *

I made tea because that is what old men do when young women bring them the end of the world. You put the kettle on. You get out the good cups, the ones with the hairline cracks that nobody drinks from anymore, and you set them out like evidence at a trial nobody wants to hold. Ulyana sat at my kitchen table and unrolled her map, and I will admit it took my breath away, seeing seven years of her life inked into the dark under our feet. Every passage. Every crawl. She had drawn the tunnels the way other people draw family trees, and I suppose that's exactly what they were.

"You've been down there since you were what, twenty?"

"Twenty-three." She turned her teacup a quarter-turn, then another quarter-turn, as though trying to find an angle from which the room made sense. "Seven years. I told myself I was mapping them. I think maybe I was checking whether they were still there."

"Were they?"

"That's the thing." She smoothed the map flat. "There's a corridor off the north gallery that wasn't there in September. I've measured it twice. The walls are covered in tally marks, Mr. Vey. Hundreds of them. And they're not old."

I looked at her over the rim of my cup and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the first toll. Eighty-one years old. Grandmother Ede raised me after the fever took my parents, and Ede Vey was a woman who believed in three things: God, the price of wool, and never, ever going into the cellar after dark. She held the third belief most firmly, and she held it the way you hold a door shut against something pushing from the other side.

"My grandmother used to whisper," I said.

Ulyana went very still. People who spend time underground learn stillness; I'd seen it in her before, that quality of a body making itself small even sitting up. "Whisper what?"

I could have lied. At my age lying is mostly habit rather than hope, but it is a comfortable habit, like a coat worn soft at the elbows. Instead I got up, knees announcing their objections, and took the key down from behind the picture of my mother. Not the cellar key. The other one. The vestry key, which Ede pressed into my hand at fourteen and said, *When they start counting, boy, you give this to whoever asks, and pray someone asks.*

Nobody had asked in sixty-seven years.

* * *

We didn't go to the vestry that night. That came later, and it's her story to tell, mostly. What happened in my kitchen was older than that, and quieter, and it belongs to me, so let me tell it properly, the way Ede told it, in the voice she saved for things too heavy to say aloud.

It was 1911. The parish was poor — poorer than poor, poor the way a body is poor when it starts selling blood. The war effort wanted metal, and the church bell was bronze, and bronze was money, so the village voted to take the bell down and sell it. All except six men who voted against, and those six names you'll find carved in the cellar wall under the ruined church, because they were the ones who knew why the bell mattered.

Ede was nine years old that summer. Her father was sexton. She used to sit on the vestry steps while the grown men argued about the bell, and she heard everything, the way children hear everything and are believed in nothing.

Here is what the minutes don't record, because the minutes were written by men who needed to sleep at night: the bell wasn't just the bell. It was the counter. Before 1911, the Hollow had an arrangement — call it a debt, call it a tithe, call it what my grandmother called it, which was *the keeping*. Something lived under the church. Had lived there longer than the church, longer than the parish, back to when this valley was all alder swamp and nobody's name was written anywhere. It kept its side of whatever bargain the first settlers struck — kept the wells sweet, kept the fever down, kept the winters survivable. And once a generation, the village paid.

Not often. Once in a lifetime, if you were lucky. A life promised, delivered down the cellar stair, and the bell would ring once — one clean note, the whole valley hearing it, everybody knowing what it meant and nobody saying it — and the debt was settled for another span of years.

"The bell wasn't melted down because of the war," I told Ulyana. "Or not only. The bell rang the payment. When they sold it, they weren't just selling bronze. They were selling the receipt."

She had both hands flat on the table now, steadying herself or the table, I couldn't tell. "Then how—"

"How does it count without a bell?" I nodded. "That's the clever part, and the damnable part. They thought selling the bell ended the arrangement. Debt discharged by bankruptcy, you might say. But you can't bankrupt a creditor like that one. It doesn't forget. It just waits, and it counts in whatever voice it can reach. In 1911, the year they took the bell down, six men died in one week. All six who voted against the sale. The parish ledger calls it fever. My grandmother called it *collection*."

"And the promise," Ulyana said. Her voice had gone careful, the way you walk on ice. "You said a life was promised. Promised when?"

I looked at her — really looked, at the fear under the composure, at the way she'd arranged her whole self into something presentable, something deniable, and I understood suddenly that she had been doing that her entire life, and that the thing below the church had been counting on it.

"Ede said the last payment was due in 1911," I said. "One soul from the Galloway line. That was the old terms — the Galloways settled the valley first, so the debt ran through their blood. Your great-grandfather Aldous was named in the vestry book. He was supposed to go down the cellar stair the same week they took the bell."

"What happened to him?"

"He ran." I said it gently, because you hand people that kind of news the way you hand them a hot coal — quickly, and with an apology. "Took his wife and son and walked out of the valley at night and never came back. The village breathed out. Thought the debt died with the distance. But debts like that don't die, girl. They hibernate. And now the bell rings again — a bell that's been scrap metal for a hundred years — and it's counting, and the only question left is counting toward what."

* * *

She didn't cry. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in an old man's kitchen. She sat with the whole weight of it — her name in a hundred-year-old promise, her blood marked out like livestock — and she didn't cry, and she didn't run, though running was the family trade. She turned her teacup another quarter-turn and asked the practical questions, one after another, like a surveyor: How many tolls so far. One. What happens at each toll. Names come off the ledger, people leave, or worse. How many tolls altogether. Ede whispered seven. Seven rings and either the debt is paid or the village is empty, and an empty village, Ede said, is not the same as a free one.

"Because it follows the blood," Ulyana said. Not asking.

"Because it follows the blood," I agreed. "And yours is the only Galloway blood left in the parish. Except—" I stopped, because the next part was not mine to say easily.

"Except Marta," she said. "My sister owns Mum's cottage. She's never signed anything, never claimed anything, but ownership isn't the issue, is it. Blood is the issue."

"Blood is always the issue here. It's the only currency the cellar accepts."

She laughed then — one short, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a person finding the trapdoor in the floor of a familiar room. "So the development company. Hallow & Vey Estates. Hallow and Vey. Those are the founders' names in the cellar wall."

"Hallow and Vey," I confirmed. "Josiah Hallow and Ambrose Vey. My great-uncle. Both dead in 1911, both buried in the churchyard, both apparently very active in the property market lately." I pushed the developer's papers across the table toward her — the ones I'd been avoiding for two weeks, the ones with letterhead too fine and signatures dated in a hand I recognized from gravestones. "They want me to sign the parish land over to them. Every acre. Every tunnel. And I'll tell you what my solicitor would say, if I had a solicitor instead of a headstone for a lawyer: never sign anything offered by the dead. They write the terms differently."

"Why haven't you signed already? They're pressing you."

"Because signing is delivering," I said. "That's the trick of it. Ede was clear on that point. The cellar can't take. It can only be *given*. That's why it counts, why it rings, why it sends lawyers with dead men's names — it needs a living hand on the pen. A promise made freely, or a signature given freely, or a soul walking down those stairs on its own two feet. Coercion spoils the payment. So it waits, and it pressures, and it makes staying more expensive than leaving, and it hopes somebody, eventually, signs."

Ulyana was quiet a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the empty street, rattling the shutters of houses whose owners had left mid-toll with their eyes full of nothing, and I watched her listen to it the way you listen to a language you almost speak.

"Tomas," she said finally — first time she'd used my Christian name, and it landed strange and warm, like sun through cellar glass. "Your grandmother whispered all this to you when you were fourteen. Sixty-seven years. Why didn't anyone ever say it out loud? Why did the whole village just... pretend?"

And there it was, the question underneath all the others, the one I'd been turning over like that teacup for most of a century. I gave her the honest answer, because she'd earned it, and because honest answers are the only currency I have left.

"Because pretending is how we survived it," I said. "A village runs on pretending the way a clock runs on springs. We pretended the noises were pipes. We pretended the ledger was moth-eaten coincidence. We pretended Daniel Galloway's grandfather was a coward instead of the man who saved us all by breaking a promise. Pretending kept us small and quiet and alive. It worked for a hundred years." I tapped the developer's papers. "But pretending has a cost too, girl, and the bill's come due. The thing down there doesn't feed on blood, not really. Ede figured that out at the end. It feeds on what's *owed and unsaid*. Every secret this village ever kept went down that cellar stair like a coin in a well. We thought we were hiding from it. We were feeding it."

She gathered her maps slowly, rolling each one with the care of a surgeon closing a patient, and at the door she paused.

"The vestry key," she said. "You said your grandmother told you to give it to whoever asked."

"She did."

"Then I'm asking."

I put it in her hand. It was heavier than a key should be, cold as bell-metal, and I felt something turn over in the ground beneath us — not an earthquake, nothing so honest — just a long, slow attention, like a sleeper adjusting its grip on a pillow.

"One more thing," I said, before she could step off my porch into the gathering dark. "The seventh ring. Ede said the seventh ring is the last. Either the debt is paid, or the count finishes and it takes what it's owed by force — the village, the blood, all of it, contract or no contract. So whatever you find in that vestry, whatever you decide—" I gripped the doorframe, because my hands had decided to shake without consulting me. "Decide fast. Because I counted the tolls, Ulyana. There have been three."

Her face went white in the porch light. "Three? There have only been two. The midnight bell, and the second one Tuesday—"

"No," I said quietly. "There was one last night too. Early. Off-schedule. Just past eleven." I had heard it through the floorboards, through the foundations, through eighty-one years of my grandmother's whispers, and it had sounded wrong in a way I hadn't been able to name until this moment, standing in front of a Galloway with my grandmother's key gone from my hand. "It didn't sound like a bell at all, girl. It sounded like a name. Like something calling a child home."

Behind us, deep under the kitchen, under the cellar, under a hundred years of pretending, something began — very softly, very patiently — to hum.
