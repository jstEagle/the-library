## 21. After the Count

The morning after the end of the world smelled like woodsmoke and wet stone, which I suppose is as good a smell as any for the morning after.

I stood in the churchyard with my hands in my pockets and watched Tomas Vey feed the development company's papers into a rusted oil drum he'd dragged out from behind the vestry. He did it slowly, ceremonially, one page at a time, like a man who had waited his whole life to burn something and intended to savor the wait. The wind kept trying to hurry him along, snatching at the corners of the letterhead — *HALLOW & VEY ESTATES* in that handsome old serif typeface, the names of dead men printed in ink that had probably been mixed by someone who was also now dead — but Tomas held each sheet until it caught properly, then let it go.

"You're going to set your coat on fire," Marta said. She'd been up since before dawn, packing her car with the grim efficiency of a woman who has decided to do a thing and will not be talked out of it or into anything else. She stood with her arms crossed against the cold, watching the drum, and every so often she glanced at me sideways, quick little checks, the way you look at someone who has recently been through something you can't quite picture.

"My coat has survived worse," Tomas said.

"Has it?"

He considered this. "It survived 1974," he allowed. "That was a bad year for coats."

The last page went into the drum. It was the signature page, the one with the line that said *Tomas Vey* above a space where his name would have gone, and above that, in smaller print, all the legal language about transfer of title and assumption of obligations. *Assumption of obligations.* I'd read enough of those papers over the past weeks to know what that phrase meant when the parties involved were a hundred and fourteen years old and lived under a church. Tomas read it too, one more time, holding the page steady while the fire climbed toward his fingers.

"Obligations," he said, to no one in particular. "They always did love that word."

Then he dropped it in, and the paper curled and blackened, and the name *Tomas Vey* burned away before it had ever been written, and I felt something in my chest unclench that I hadn't known was clenched. It had been clenched, I realized, for seven years. Possibly longer. Possibly since I was seventeen years old and running down the county road with a duffel bag and a boy's name in my mouth like a stone I couldn't spit out.

Marta put her hand briefly on my shoulder. She didn't say anything. We'd gotten better at that, she and I, over the past two days — at the silences that weren't wounds anymore, just silences.

* * *

The village emptied the way a theater empties after a play nobody liked: quickly, quietly, with everyone avoiding eye contact.

There were only six of us left to empty, really. Tomas. Marta. Me. The Hollis brothers, who had come back for the final night because — and this still strikes me as the most Kessel's Hollow thing imaginable — they wanted to see whether their grandmother's stories were true, and had brought a cooler of beer to watch the apocalypse. And old Mrs. Adeyemi from the far cottage, who had heard exactly four of the seven bells and decided on the fourth that she'd rather be a coward in a warm flat in Leeds than brave in a cold house here. She'd left before dawn, honking twice at the church as she drove past, which I choose to interpret as a farewell and not a warning.

The gas-leak story had been Marta's idea, and it had worked better than any truth would have. A gas leak gets you sympathy and a hotel voucher. "A bell that melted down in 1911 is counting our names" gets you a referral to a specialist. So we'd made our calls, and the regional authority had sent a man in a high-visibility vest who walked the high street, sniffed theatrically, found nothing, and wrote a report anyway, because the alternative was admitting there was nothing to find, and bureaucracies would rather condemn an innocent pipe than admit ignorance. The village was officially uninhabitable pending repairs. The repairs would never come. The development company's founders being, as they were, dust and old resentment, there was no one left to sue.

By noon, the Hollis brothers were gone, arguing amiably about whether what they'd seen through the cellar door counted as "a presence" or "an atmospheric phenomenon." By two, Marta's car was loaded, and Tomas stood on the vicarage step with two suitcases and a hatbox containing, he informed us solemnly, his grandmother's letters and nothing else of value.

"You could come with us," Marta said to me. Not pleading. Offering. That was new for her, the offering without the plea, and I loved her a little extra for it. "There's room. There's a sofa. It's ugly, but it's a sofa."

"I know," I said. "One more night."

She looked at me for a long moment. My sister, who had spent eleven years not speaking to me, who had signed nothing and owed nothing and said so with her chin up even while the bell rang her childhood nickname off the hillsides — my sister looked at me, and whatever she saw, she nodded once.

"One night," she said. "And then you call me. Every week. Not every other week. Every week."

"Every week," I agreed.

"And you sign things with your own name now." She said it lightly, but it wasn't light. "That's the deal. You don't get to be half-heard anymore. You've paid enough for that."

Tomas snorted from the step. "Listen to her. Bossy as her mother."

"She was bossier," Marta said, and for a second we both smiled at the same memory of the same woman, and the smile didn't hurt, and I understood that this, too, was part of what the cracked bell had given back.

* * *

They left at golden hour, which felt right. Tomas in the passenger seat with the hatbox on his knees, Marta driving with both hands on the wheel like a woman piloting something precious out of a war zone. At the bend where the high street met the county road, the car stopped, and Tomas's arm came out the window, and he raised his hat to me — actually raised it, the whole gesture, like something out of a film — and then they were gone, taillights shrinking down the valley, and Kessel's Hollow belonged to me and the stones and whatever was left of the thing under the church.

Which was, as far as I could tell, nothing much.

I went down to check. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is apparently the currency this place trades in now: I went down into the tunnels one more time partly to make sure, and partly because seven years of habit doesn't dissolve in a single night, and partly — the smallest part, the part I'll admit to only here — because I wanted to say goodbye to the dark that had raised me twice.

The corridors were just corridors. The tally marks on the walls had run backward, as I'd watched them run, stroke by stroke peeling off the stone like frost retreating from a window, until the walls held only the faintest ghosting, the way a wall holds the shape of a picture taken down long ago. The corridor that hadn't existed last month existed now, but it was only a corridor, dry and ordinary, ending in rubble that had plainly been rubble for decades. The cellar beneath the church was cold and smelled of ash and bell-metal, and the door bound in that pale metal hung open on hinges that no longer breathed, and behind it there was nothing but a small round room and a silence so complete it rang.

In the middle of the floor lay the bell itself.

Not the whole bell — the cracked halves, the two great broken pieces of the original, the one they'd supposedly melted down in 1911 and hadn't, or had, or had and then somehow been given back. It lay split open like a mouth that had finally finished its sentence. I crouched beside it and touched the rim, and the metal was cool and dumb and heavy, and it did not hum, and it did not count, and it did not know my name or anyone else's.

"Paid in full," I said aloud, to the room, to the dark, to whatever might still be listening out of habit. My voice sounded strange down there — not half-heard, oddly, but fully heard, the way sound carries in an empty house. "Whatever we owed. Whatever they promised you. It's done. You can stop."

Nothing answered. But the silence felt different than it had seven years ago, or even seven days ago. It felt like the silence of a room after an argument has ended, not the silence of a held breath. An exhale, not a pause.

I took one photograph. Just one, with the old camera I'd stopped trusting weeks ago, when every shot of the cellar door came out showing the door open. I aimed it at the broken bell and pressed the shutter, and later, when I developed it in the vicarage kitchen, the image came out exactly as it should: a cracked bell, split halves, cold floor, nothing else. True. Boring, even. The most beautiful boring photograph I have ever taken.

* * *

I stayed the night in my mother's cottage, because Marta told me to, because she said if I was going to be sentimental enough to sleep one last night in this village I should at least do it somewhere with a working boiler, and because some stubborn part of me wanted to fall asleep under the roof where I'd been Daniel Galloway and wake up — well. As whoever woke up.

The cottage was exactly as Marta had kept it: dust-sheeted furniture, my mother's crockery still in the cupboard, the calendar in the kitchen frozen at a month neither of us ever named. I lit a fire in the front room because the boiler was a liar, and I sat on the floor with my back against the sofa and my maps spread out around me — all seven years of them, every passage I'd ever penciled in, the growing map, the map that had grown back at me. I'd brought them down intending to sort them, decide what was worth keeping, and instead I sat and looked at them and thought about what they were.

They were a record of a woman paying attention. That was all. Seven years of showing up in the dark with a lamp and a notebook and refusing to look away, and telling myself it was cartography when it was also — I could admit it now, cheaply, easily, the way you admit things after a haunting — penance. Return visits. A hundred small attempts to answer a question I'd fled rather than ask: *can I come back here as myself?*

The answer, it turned out, had been yes the entire time. The village had never been the problem. The village had been a debtor, same as me, hiding from a creditor, and we'd both been hiding in the same tunnels, and it had taken a bell made of lies to teach us that hiding was the debt.

Around eleven I made tea I didn't drink and went upstairs to my mother's old room, which still had her curtains, faded roses gone gray at the edges. I lay down in my clothes on top of the quilt and listened.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the whole point of a surveyor: I listened, deliberately, the way I had listened for seven years. For dripping water. For settling stone. For footsteps that matched mine a half-beat late. For the whisper that had followed me to the county line saying *Daniel, Daniel, Daniel* in a voice like a struck bell. For my name — either of my names. For anything.

There was nothing.

No pipes. No wind. No counting. The village around me was silent the way a held note is silent after it ends — not absent, but resolved. Somewhere outside, an owl made its idiotic honest noise, and a branch scraped a gutter, and those sounds were only themselves, only owls and branches, and I lay in my mother's bed in the village that had raised me twice and slept without dreaming, without hearing, without flinching, for nine hours straight. The longest unbroken sleep of my adult life. When I woke, gray light was coming through the rose curtains, and for one full minute I didn't remember where I was, and for the first time in my life that not-knowing felt like peace instead of threat.

* * *

I locked the cottage at noon and put the key under the third flowerpot, the hiding place we'd used since I was six, and walked up the high street one last time. The church stood open to the sky the way it had for decades, roofless, patient, its ruined arch framing nothing but clouds. In the vestry, the parish ledger lay where I'd left it, and I looked at the old brown-inked names, struck through one by one across a century, and at the pencil column beside them, which had stopped growing. Eleven strokes, then blank paper. I closed the book gently, like closing a door on a sleeping room.

At the edge of the churchyard I stopped and turned around and looked at the whole of it — the crooked houses, the leaning fences, the hills beyond with their sheep-shaped shadows. A dying village. An empty village. Mine, in the way that places are yours when they've hurt you and healed you in the same breath.

"Well," I said out loud, to all of it. "See you around."

And I meant it, which surprised me.

My car was parked where I'd left it, under the dead elm, and in the passenger seat sat my field bag with the notebooks and the final draft of the survey — the real one, the clean one, the one I'd been quietly preparing for months without letting myself think about why. On the cover, in the space marked *SURVEYOR*, the line was blank. It had been blank for seven years. *U. Galloway,* I'd written on the early drafts, then scratched out, then *anonymous,* then nothing at all, because a name was a hook and hooks got pulled.

I sat in the driver's seat and clicked a pen and held it over the line for a long moment.

Every week, Marta had said. Sign things with your own name. You've paid enough for half-heard.

I wrote *Ulyana Galloway* in careful block letters, and it looked strange there, and it looked right there, and my hand only shook a little.

Then, because the engine needed a minute in the cold, I flipped idly to the final page of the survey — the appendix, the blank page I always kept at the back for corrections — and stopped.

There was a drawing on it. Small, in the lower corner, in pencil, in a hand that was unmistakably mine and that I had no memory of making: a bell, drawn in three quick strokes, with a single jagged crack running through it top to bottom.

I stared at it for a long time. The engine ticked. The owl, or its cousin, called once from the elm.

Then I closed the notebook, put both hands on the wheel, and drove out of Kessel's Hollow — past the church, past the drum with its ashes, past the bend where Tomas had raised his hat — and I did not look back, because everything I needed to carry was already with me, and everything else could rest.

But that night, in a motorway services outside Leeds, waiting for tea, I opened the notebook again to the last page, just to check.

The bell was still there.

And beneath it, in the same hand, in pencil so fresh it shone, someone had added a single word:

*Thanks.*
