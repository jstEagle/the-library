## 5. Harvest Eve Potluck

The invitation came the way everything in Harrow Bend seemed to come: without anyone actually saying anything.

Oscar found it on Tuesday morning, tucked under the farmhouse's front door like a leaf blown there by accident—a square of cream cardstock, hand-lettered in a careful, old-fashioned script. *Harvest Eve Potluck. Grange Hall. Sundown. All families welcome.* No signature. No phone number. Just that word, *families*, sitting at the bottom of the page with a weight that felt deliberate.

They stood in the doorway holding it for a long time, the morning air cool and smelling of cut hay and something sweeter underneath—apples going soft somewhere out of sight, fermenting where they'd fallen. Behind them, inside the house, the clock in the parlor ticked with the patient rhythm it had kept since before Oscar was born. Somewhere deeper, beneath the floorboards and the foundation and the packed earth below that, the house held its silence the way it always did now. Ever since the knocking. Ever since Oscar had knelt on the cellar stairs at two in the morning and listened to three slow knocks, three fast, three slow, and had not answered.

Gideon hadn't mentioned it. Gideon never mentioned anything he didn't want to mention.

"You should go," he said now, from the porch swing, where he sat with his legs not quite reaching the boards, swinging in a rhythm that didn't match the swing's natural arc. He'd been doing that all week—moving things slightly wrong, slightly off, so that watching him was like watching a film with one frame missing. "Mrs. Aldous will bring her pickled beets. She'll be hurt if you don't try them."

"You've been to these?" Oscar asked. They were still holding the card. The paper was good stock, heavy, the kind of thing people used to buy from the stationer in Corning before it closed.

"Every year." Gideon smiled. His smile was the same smile from twenty years ago, the gap-toothed grin of an eight-year-old boy, but on his unchanged face it landed strangely now, like a song played at the wrong speed. "Everyone's very kind."

*Everyone's very kind.* That was the thing about Harrow Bend that Oscar couldn't shake—the kindness was real, that was what made it awful. It wasn't the fake warmth of small towns in movies, all teeth and suspicion. These people genuinely wanted to feed you, to ask after your grief, to press casseroles into your hands and mean it. And underneath the kindness, threaded through it like a wire through taffy, was something else. Something that watched.

The funeral had been eleven days ago. Grandmother Marchetti, ninety-four years old, buried in the little cemetery behind the Methodist church in a plot that already held four generations of Marchettis—and, Oscar had noticed with a chill they'd told themselves was nothing, space cleared for a fifth. Their plot. The grass over the empty space had been mowed. Someone was maintaining it.

Oscar went to the potluck because refusing felt more dangerous than attending. That was becoming the arithmetic of their whole life here: every choice weighed not against what they wanted but against what would happen if they declined. Stay through harvest or forfeit the estate, the will said. Don't sing past sundown, Gideon said. And now this card, soft as an invitation and structured exactly like a summons.

They dressed carefully—not dressed up, which would mark them as trying, not dressed down, which would mark them as disrespectful. Jeans, a clean shirt. In the hall mirror they caught themselves and thought, absurdly, of their grandmother's hands smoothing their collar before church, twenty-some years ago, saying *you're a Marchetti, act like one*. They'd hated it then. Now the words felt less like scolding and more like instructions.

Gideon did not come with them. When Oscar asked, he only swung harder on the porch swing and said, "I don't go where everyone can see me," and wouldn't explain, and Oscar—coward, they admitted it later, coward—didn't push.

* * *

The Grange Hall sat at the crossroads in the center of town, a broad white building with a tin roof gone dull as old pewter, its paint peeling in long curls that revealed gray wood beneath like skin under a bandage. Trucks lined the road on both sides, dusted with field dirt. Through the open doors spilled yellow light and noise and the smell of a hundred dishes: fried chicken, baked beans, cinnamon, vinegar, woodsmoke from the cookout barrels out back.

Inside, folding tables ran the length of the hall in two long rows, groaning under food. People moved between them in slow orbits, and when Oscar stepped through the door, the orbit paused—all of it, all at once, forty faces turning toward them in a single smooth motion, like iron filings finding north.

Then, just as smoothly, the motion resumed. Smiles. Nods. A chorus of *Oscar, honey, come sit, you look thin, are you eating.*

Dale Hutchins found them first, as Oscar had known he would. Dale had been their closest friend once, back when friendship meant catching crawdads in the creek and daring each other to touch the electric fence. He was heavier now, grayer, with a farmer's squint and a handshake that lasted half a beat too long.

"Glad you came," he said, and meant it, and the meaning of it had layers Oscar didn't want to examine. "You remember Ruthie? My wife. And the girls—" Two teenagers, bored and beautiful, murmured hellos and drifted away toward their friends. "Sit with us. Mrs. Aldous saved you a spot."

Of course she had.

The meal itself was almost enough to make Oscar forget why they'd been afraid. Almost. The food was extraordinary—these were farm people, and they cooked like it, everything rich and seasonal and made by hands that knew what they were doing. Mrs. Aldous's pickled beets were sweet and sharp and stained Oscar's fingers pink. There was a corn pudding someone's grandmother had made from a recipe older than the county. Cider pressed that week, still warm from the press. Around them the talk flowed easy and endless: combines and rain forecasts, who's daughter was marrying who, the price of diesel, the football game.

Belonging. That was the word for it, and Oscar felt its pull like gravity. Twenty years in cities where nobody knew their name, and here was a room full of people who had known them at nine years old, who remembered their grandmother's garden, who asked after them with genuine, unguarded warmth. It would be so easy. It would be so goddamn easy to just stay, to let the current take them, to become one of these faces around these tables until the strange things stopped being strange.

That was the hook in it. Oscar could feel the barb of it even while the line reeled gentle.

Because there were details. Small ones, accumulating. The way no one asked how long Oscar was staying. The way the question was never *will you* stay, always assumed, folded into sentences like *when you're settled in* and *come spring you'll want to see*. The way the teenagers, when the adults' attention lapsed, went quiet and watchful, exchanging glances with the practiced fluency of children who had been taught something without ever being told they'd been taught it. The way the hall's windows, though the evening was mild, had all been shut and latched.

And the way, when Oscar bit into a slice of pie and looked up, three separate people at three separate tables were looking at them—not hostile, not unfriendly, just *attentive*, the way you watch a kettle you know is close to boiling.

"Sundown's coming," Mrs. Aldous said beside them, quiet, and Oscar realized the light through the west windows had gone amber, then copper, then the deep bruised gold that precedes dark in late September. "They'll start soon."

"Start what?"

But the answer was already happening. The talk was dying down table by table, conversations ending not with conclusions but with trailing silences, people setting down forks, turning in their seats toward the front of the hall where an old man Oscar didn't recognize had risen from his chair. He was ancient, bent like a fence post, with a face seamed so deep it looked plowed. He didn't call for attention. He didn't need to. The hall simply emptied of sound around him, the way water empties around a stone.

And then he began to sing.

It was a round. That much Oscar understood immediately, musically—the melody simple and cyclical, built to be taken up by voice after voice, each entering a measure behind the last so the song wove itself into a braid with no beginning and no end. The old man sang alone for one verse, his voice cracked and thin and utterly unselfconscious, and then Mrs. Aldous joined, and then Dale, and then the whole hall, men and women and children, forty voices folding into the pattern.

The words were dialect, old-fashioned, some of them not quite English:

*Low in the loam, low in the loam,*
*Lay down your burden and carry it home.*
*What the field takes, the field keeps,*
*What the field keeps, the field sleeps.*

It was pretty. That was the horror of it—it was genuinely, achingly pretty, a harvest song like a hundred harvest songs, the kind of thing that belonged in a school music book beside *Simple Gifts*. Oscar sat with their hands in their lap and felt the melody reach into them with familiar fingers.

Because they knew it. Not remembered—knew, the way you know your own heartbeat. The tune lived somewhere below memory, below childhood, in whatever cellar of the self holds the things learned before learning. Their lips parted. Their throat tightened around the shape of the second verse, the entrance they'd have taken if—

If they'd been nine years old, sitting on these benches, singing with a friend whose name they hadn't spoken in twenty years.

"No," Oscar whispered, to themselves, and clamped their mouth shut.

But the body is a traitor. Humming isn't singing; humming needs no words, no decision, just resonance, and somewhere in the third round, as the braid of voices thickened and the candles along the walls guttered in air that had stopped moving, Oscar's chest began to hum. Low, involuntary, a vibration more felt than heard, matching the melody the way a tuning fork matches a struck note.

Around them, the hall changed.

It happened in pieces, in peripheries. A woman mid-laugh beside the bean table—her laugh cut off, cleanly, like scissors through thread. Dale, who had been leaning to say something to his wife, straightened slowly and did not finish leaning. A child halfway through asking for more cider closed her mouth and swallowed and stared at her hands. Conversation after conversation stopped mid-sentence, mid-word, mid-breath, until the only sound in the Grange Hall was the song itself, forty voices braided and seamless, and beneath it, faint and rising, one more voice.

Oscar's hum.

They felt every face turn. They didn't need to look; they felt it the way you feel sun on your neck. Forty pairs of eyes, and in none of them was there anger. That was the worst part. What Oscar saw when they finally forced their gaze up from their hands was worse than anger—it was relief. Wide-eyed, trembling, hungry relief, the look of people watching a drought break. Old man at the front, still singing, tears running freely down his seamed cheeks. Dale with his mouth half-open. A young mother clutching her son's shoulder hard enough to whiten her knuckles.

*They thought I wouldn't,* Oscar understood. *They thought the line had run dry.*

Their grandmother's unfinished entry in the ledger rose in their mind—*the last of us, and she won't*—and Oscar shut their throat with an act of pure will, biting down on the hum, killing it in their own chest. The silence they left behind was enormous.

For three full seconds, no one breathed.

Then the song ended—one final circling phrase, *what the field keeps, the field sleeps*, fading like light through closing blinds—and the hall exhaled all at once, and the talk resumed, and the laughter resumed, and someone passed the cider, and it was as if nothing had happened at all. That was the most frightening part of all: how completely, how cheerfully, they all pretended it hadn't. Plates scraped. Children giggled. Dale asked Oscar if they wanted more pie.

* * *

Mrs. Aldous walked them out.

She appeared at their elbow as Oscar was gathering their coat, and she steered them gently, firmly, with a hand light on their arm, through the crowd and out the side door onto the gravel lot, away from the lanterns and the last stragglers. The night had turned cold. Above the tree line, the moon was rising fat and orange, a harvest moon, and the fields beyond the Grange Hall lay silver and still, the corn already cut, the stubble gleaming like a scalp.

Mrs. Aldous was seventy if she was a day, small and dry and neat as a pinned moth, with white hair braided in a crown and eyes the pale blue of skimmed milk. She had brought beets to the funeral reception. She had held Oscar's hand at the graveside and said, *she was the best of us.* Standing beside her now, Oscar noticed for the first time that her hand, resting on their sleeve, was shaking.

"That was a near thing," she said.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Yes, you do." She said it without reproach, almost tenderly, the way you'd tell a child they'd touched a hot stove. She looked out at the fields, at the moon, anywhere but at Oscar. "Your grandmother sang that round at every Harvest Eve for sixty years. Never missed one. Even the year her hip went bad, she came and sat right there on that bench and sang till the candles burned out." Her thumb moved on Oscar's sleeve, a small unconscious stroke, soothing or pleading. "When she stopped coming, three years ago, folks said it was age. But I saw her face that last time. She was singing it like a woman paying a bill she'd disputed her whole life."

The wind moved through the cut corn, a dry whisper, thousands of stalks shifting together. From far off—too far off, miles maybe, or not far at all—came a sound Oscar might have imagined: a low tone, sustained, like a finger circling the rim of a glass.

"Why are you telling me this?" Oscar asked.

Mrs. Aldous finally looked at them. Up close, in the moonlight, her milk-pale eyes were wet.

"Because I liked your grandmother," she said. "And because I buried my own boy thirty years ago, and I have watched this town do what it does every autumn since, and I am tired, Oscar Marchetti. I am so tired." Her grip tightened on their sleeve. "Listen to me now, and listen with whatever part of you your grandmother trusted. Leave. Before the season turns. Before the equinox. Go back to your city and your life and let the house stand empty. Let the estate go. Sign it away, burn it, I don't care. Just don't be standing on that land when the last of the corn comes down."

"The will says—"

"I know what the will says." Something moved behind her face, quick and bitter. "Do you imagine your grandmother wrote that condition to trap you? She wrote it hoping you'd refuse. Hoping you'd forfeit everything and walk away free. It was the only inheritance she had left to give you, child—an easy door out. And instead you stayed, and you went to the potluck, and tonight you hummed."

The word dropped between them like a stone into a well. Oscar heard it fall a long way.

"I didn't mean to."

"No," Mrs. Aldous agreed softly. "You never do. None of us ever do. That's how it works. That's how it has always worked." She released their sleeve at last and stepped back, and the distance she put between them was deliberate, a woman putting down something heavy before it broke her arms. "There's a bus through Corning on Thursday mornings. Nobody would think less of you. Nobody would say a word." A pause, and her voice dropped to nearly nothing, lost under the wind. "Some of us would thank God."

"And if I stay?"

Mrs. Aldous looked at them for a long moment. The harvest moon lit half her face and left the other half dark, and in the dark half her expression was unreadable, ancient, carved.

"Then before the equinox," she said, "someone is going to teach you the rest of the song."

She turned and walked back toward the light of the Grange Hall, small and straight-backed, her shadow long and thin across the gravel, and Oscar stood alone in the cold with the moon climbing and the fields whispering and the taste of beets and cider still on their tongue.

Behind them, faintly, from the direction of the farmhouse a mile down the dark road, Oscar could have sworn they heard the porch swing creaking. Keeping time.

Waiting up.
