## 10. Trespass

The knocking stopped on the fourth night.

That was worse than the knocking itself. For three days the cellar had spoken to Oscar in that patient rhythm—three slow, three fast, the old pattern from games played in the tall grass with a friend who wasn't real—and for three days Oscar had lain awake above it with a kitchen knife under their pillow and their grandmother's rosary wound around their knuckles like brass knuckles, and had not gone down. Had not answered. Rule two: never answer knocking from below. Gideon's voice delivering it, sweet as syrup off a spoon.

But silence was its own kind of invitation. Silence meant whatever knocked had either given up or grown tired of asking politely, and both possibilities sat in Oscar's chest like swallowed river stones.

On the morning of the fifth day, they went down anyway.

Not to answer. To trespass. There was a difference, Oscar told themselves, and the telling was thin as tissue paper over a broken window, but it held long enough to get them down the stairs with the lantern lit and the hatch key—found in grandmother's desk, third drawer, wrapped in a dish towel embroidered with wheat sheaves—cold in their palm.

The cellar smelled of earth and apples gone soft, of the particular mineral sweetness of stone that has been wet for a hundred years. Grandmother's preserves still lined the shelves in ranks, her handwriting on every lid: *peaches '19, tomatoes '21, quince*. The last jar was from this spring. She'd been canning while she was dying. That detail hit Oscar sideways every time—the stubbornness of it, the refusal to let the harvest pass unharvested—and they stood there a moment among her jars, breathing through their mouth, before turning to face the hatch.

It was set into the floor like a door into nowhere: oak planks banded with iron, older than the house around it, older than any house should be. The iron had been polished by hands. Not worn—*polished*, deliberately, generation after generation of Marchetti palms laying themselves flat against those bands, maybe in blessing, maybe in farewell.

Oscar crouched beside it and pressed their ear to the wood.

Nothing. No hum, no knock, no breath of air. Just the sound of their own pulse tapping against the grain, and beneath that—or did they imagine it?—a vastness of quiet, the way a field at midnight is quiet, which is not the absence of sound but the presence of something listening.

"You shouldn't be down here."

Oscar didn't jump. They were past jumping where Gideon was concerned; the fear had settled into something more like weather, always present, always changing form. They looked up. He stood at the top of the stairs, backlit by the kitchen's gray morning light, and he was wearing the same clothes he'd worn on the porch the first day—corduroys, striped shirt, bare feet—and his face wore an expression Oscar had never seen on him before.

He looked afraid.

"For you," Oscar said. "I know. But I'm not you."

"Nobody is me." Gideon came down one step, then another, slowly, as if the stairs might argue. "That's rather the point of me."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," he said, and then stopped, and his mouth worked around words like a man chewing something too large, "it means the rules aren't mine. I only carry them. Like a boy carrying water—he doesn't make the well." His eyes went to the hatch, and something moved behind them, some vast slow grief or hunger, hard to say which. "You'll go in regardless. You have your mother's chin when you've decided something. And your grandmother's everything else."

"I'm going in."

"I know." He sat down on the bottom step, drew his knees up, made himself small in a way that was almost convincing. Almost childlike. But his feet didn't quite touch the floor right, and his shadow fell toward the hatch instead of away from the window, and Oscar had learned in these weeks not to trust the geometry of him. "Then take the lantern low. The air changes. And if you hear singing—"

"If I hear singing past sundown, don't sing along. Rule one."

"No." Gideon's voice came out strange, flattened. "If you hear singing, count the verses. If it stops at four, come home. If it goes to five—" He pressed his lips together. Whatever the rest was, he would not give it up, not even now, not even afraid. "Count, Oscar. Promise me you'll count."

"Fine. I'll count."

"And—" He hesitated so long the lantern flame leaned and recovered. "Come back. Please. It likes you already. Don't let it finish liking you."

Oscar wanted to ask what that meant, wanted it badly enough that their throat ached with the question, but Gideon turned his face to the wall and became, in the space of a blink, the kind of absent that furniture is absent, and Oscar understood the audience was over.

They put the key in the lock.

* * *

The hatch opened on air, not stairs.

That was the first wrongness. Below the door there was simply dark, and a cool updraft smelling of loam and deep water and something faintly sweet, like corn left too long in the field, like honey poured over rot. Oscar lowered the lantern on its handle and watched the light sink, expecting steps, finding none—just a shaft cut clean through earth, walls of packed clay studded with small pale stones, descending farther than the light could follow.

There were handholds carved into the wall. Rungs, really, worn smooth and shallow, spaced for smaller bodies than theirs. Children's rungs. Generations of children climbing down into the dark, and climbing back up, or not.

Oscar thought about Mrs. Aldous's face at the potluck, the gentle terrible kindness of her: *leave before the season turns.* Thought about the ledgers upstairs in the locked trunk, the columns of names, the struck-through ones. Thought about the knocking, three slow, three fast, and how it had matched exactly the game they'd invented at nine years old with a boy no one else could see—a boy who had taught them the rhythm, Oscar realized now with a cold sliding feeling, and not the other way around.

Gideon hadn't been making up childhood games. He'd been rehearsing them.

Oscar climbed down.

The rungs took them perhaps twenty feet, and then their feet found floor—not clay now but flagstone, fitted without mortar, each stone bearing a chiseled mark like a tally or a letter from an alphabet that predated the town. The tunnel ran straight ahead into blackness, tall enough to walk upright, wide enough for two abreast. Someone had built this. Someone had built this *well*, and built it to last, and the someone was not any farmer Oscar's family had ever been.

The humming was everywhere and nowhere. Not a sound so much as a pressure, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the fluid of the eyes. It had melody to it, barely—an interval repeating, rising and settling, rising and settling, like a lullaby remembered by someone who'd only ever heard it through a wall. Oscar's body knew it. That was the horror of it. Their lungs wanted to swell in time with it. Their blood kept its own tempo against it, and lost, and adjusted, and lost again.

*Count the verses,* Gideon had said. Oscar counted their footsteps instead, because counting footsteps was a thing a person did, and a person was what they intended to remain.

Fifty steps. A hundred. The tunnel bent once, gently, following some logic of soil or stone, and the flagstones gave way to raw-hewn rock, and the rock began to change.

At first Oscar took the marks for tooling—chisel work, quarry scars. Then the lantern swung and the shadows reversed and the marks resolved into letters. Names. Carved in rows, in columns, in spirals that climbed toward a ceiling lost in dark, covering every surface the light touched: *names*, thousands upon thousands of them, crowded shoulder to shoulder like a congregation, like a ledger written on the world's own bones.

Oscar raised the lantern higher and walked slower.

HARROW. ALDOUS. VANCE. CREEL. Surnames Oscar knew from the cemetery on the hill, from the mailboxes along the county road, from the potluck tables where women in cardigans passed dishes hand to hand. Founding families. Keeper families. Every name a household, and under every surname, first names, dozens of them, generations deep, some carved in a careful schoolroom hand and some in a shaking one, some so old the letters had softened into suggestion.

And MARCHETTI. Over and over. More times than any other name on the wall, Oscar was almost sure of it, though the wall gave no easy way to be sure of anything. MARCHETTI ran down the stone like a vein runs down a leaf. And the strangest thing—the thing that lifted the hair on Oscar's arms and held it there—was that the carvings changed character as they went deeper. The oldest were crude, desperate, gouged. The middle ones were formal, ceremonial, cut by confident tools. And the newest—

The newest were fresh.

Oscar found them at chest height, near where the tunnel widened into the first hint of a chamber beyond: MARCHETTI, cut into the rock within what looked like weeks. Stone dust still clung in the grooves. And below it, half-finished, the beginning of another word in a hand Oscar knew better than their own—knew from grocery lists and birthday cards and the margins of library books—

Their grandmother's hand.

The carving stopped mid-letter. The M done, the A begun, the chisel stroke trailing off like a sentence interrupted.

Oscar stood very still in the humming dark, holding the lantern up to their grandmother's unfinished name, and understood several things at once, the way ice understands the lake when it goes out all at once. She had come down here. Near the end. She had come down here with a chisel, or the chisel had been here waiting, and she had started to write her own name into this wall of the fed and the feeding, and she had stopped. Stopped mid-stroke. Either she'd been called away, or she'd changed her mind, or—

Or she'd run out of time, and the entry was left open, and an open entry needed a hand to close it.

"Hello, Oscar."

The voice came from ahead—from the chamber whose edge the lantern couldn't reach—and it was Gideon's voice, but sung instead of spoken, stretched into the melody of the hum, four notes rising and settling, rising and settling. Four notes. Oscar counted them twice, heart hammering, lips pressed shut against the urge to echo. Four, and then silence, and then four again.

Not five. Whatever waited in the dark was singing in fours, patiently, the way you'd hum to a child to coax it across a room.

Oscar's legs wanted to walk forward. That was the worst discovery of the night—that the wanting wasn't entirely theirs, that the hum had gotten into the walking muscles somewhere between the fiftieth step and the thousandth name. They gripped the lantern until the wire handle bit their fingers and used the pain as an anchor, and stepped backward instead, one flagstone, two, keeping their eyes on the chamber's darkness because turning around felt like the kind of permission you can never take back.

From the black, softly, almost fondly:

*Three slow. Three fast.*

The knocking rhythm, given in a whisper. An old game. An old invitation. *Your turn.*

Oscar walked back up the children's rungs with their grandmother's unfinished name burning behind their eyes, and did not look down, and did not sing, and did not breathe easily again until the hatch was locked and bolted and they were sitting on the cellar floor among the jars of peaches and quince, shaking, alive, certain of only one thing:

The wall had room on it for one more Marchetti. And something in the dark had already started saving the space.
