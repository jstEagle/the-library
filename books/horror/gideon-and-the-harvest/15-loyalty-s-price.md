## 15. Loyalty's Price

The corn died facing the house.

Oscar noticed it first from the bedroom window on the morning after the equinox chorus—rows of it, every stalk in the eastern field turned like a congregation toward the farmhouse, leaves curled and gray as old paper. The western rows still stood green. It was only the corn that could see the house that had withered, as if the act of looking at them had cost it something vital.

By noon, the hens were dead too.

They lay in a neat line along the coop's north wall, five of them, wings folded, heads tucked, arranged with a care no fox or dog would bother with. Their eyes were open. All of them faced the house.

Oscar stood over them for a long time with their hands buried in their jacket pockets, breath steaming, and did not touch them. Behind them, at the edge of the yard, Gideon sat on the fence rail with his legs swinging, watching the bodies with an expression Oscar couldn't read—hunger, maybe, or homesickness.

"They're not dead," Gideon said.

"They're pretty clearly dead."

"They're *spent*." He hopped down, landed soft as ash. "Everything here is spent. The ground gave what it had early this year because everyone's been singing so hard, trying to make up for you." He smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "You should hear how they sing now. Like people praying with a gun to their heads."

Oscar turned away. The well water had started tasting faintly sweet three days ago, which Mrs. Aldous said was normal before harvest, though her voice had done something complicated when she said it. The dogs up and down the county road had stopped barking altogether. And every morning there was more of the town gathered at the property line—not angry, never angry, just *there*, standing in the frost with their hands in their pockets, watching the house the way the corn watched it.

Today there were eleven of them. Yesterday, seven.

* * *

Dale came at dusk.

He'd been Oscar's friend since fourth grade—the boy who'd dared them to jump off the rail bridge into the Harrow, who'd split his chin open doing it himself to prove it could be survived. He looked older than thirty-one ought to look. Everyone in Harrow Bend did; it was a town that aged its people like fruit in a cellar, slowly, in the dark.

He stood on the porch steps where Gideon used to sit, holding his hat in both hands, turning it, turning it.

"Your porch light's out," he said.

"I know."

"Mrs. Marchetti never let it go out. Not once. Not even the week she was dying." He said it gently, but it landed like a bill presented. "Folks notice things like that."

"Folks can come tell me themselves instead of standing in my field like scarecrows."

"They're not—" Dale stopped. Started again. "Oscar. Can I come in?"

"I'd rather not."

Something flickered across his face—hurt, then resignation, then a tired kind of understanding that was worse than either. He nodded slowly and stayed where he was, and Oscar realized with a cold turn in their stomach that he wasn't going to leave, and that he knew they knew it, and that this whole conversation had been rehearsed somewhere, in some warm kitchen, by people who loved each other, about how to handle the Marchetti who wouldn't sing.

"You remember my dad?" Dale said.

"Everyone remembers your dad."

"He used to say this town was like a body. Every family an organ. The Aldouses are the memory, the Vances are the hands, the Coles are the—whatever, the spleen." A ghost of the old grin. "And the Marchettis are the throat. You're the ones who sing. Two hundred years, Oscar. Your people have sung every harvest since before there was a Harrow Bend, back when it was just a clearing and a name nobody agreed on. That's not superstition. That's *maintenance*. You don't skip maintenance."

"The corn is dead, Dale."

"The corn will come back."

"The hens are dead."

"Hens come back too." His voice cracked on it, just slightly, and he pressed his hat brim against his mouth for a second. When he lowered it, his eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "My daughter drew a picture yesterday. Of our house. She drew all of us inside it and she drew the walls black. She's six. She says the house is hungry. Kids say stuff, I know, kids say all kinds of—except every kid in this valley is saying the same thing right now, Oscar, and none of them talked to each other first."

Inside the house, behind Oscar, a floorboard creaked. Neither of them looked.

"My grandmother kept ledgers," Oscar said quietly. "I've read them. Do you know what's in them? Names. Struck through. Generations of names with lines through them, and next to most of them, in different handwriting, different centuries: *refused.*"

"And next to the others?"

Oscar didn't answer.

"Next to the others," Dale said, "it says *kept*, doesn't it. Kept the town. Kept the fields. Kept the children." He stepped up one stair, close enough now that Oscar could smell woodsmoke and feed store and fear on him. "I'm not asking you to die. Nobody's asking you to die. We're asking you to do what your family has always done, what they *chose*, over and over, for two hundred years—and yeah, sometimes it costs, I know it costs, my own—"

He stopped. His jaw worked.

"Your own what?" Oscar asked. "Dale. Your own what?"

But he shook his head and put his hat back on, and when he spoke again his voice was flat and careful, a man walking on ice he knew was thin. "Harvest night is in nine days. There'll be a place for you in the line. There always is. Nobody wants it to go any other way." He backed down the stairs. "Nobody in this town wants anything bad for you, Oscar. Remember that. Whatever happens, remember we tried to be kind about it."

"Dale—"

"We tried," he said again, and walked out into the dark, and the eleven watchers at the field line parted to let him through and then closed again behind him, faces turned toward the house, patient as planted things.

* * *

That night the knocking came back.

Three slow. Three fast. Three slow.

It rose up through the cellar floor and through the kitchen floorboards and into the soles of Oscar's feet while they sat at their grandmother's table with the ledgers spread out under lamplight, and this time they didn't freeze. This time they pulled the oldest ledger toward them and opened it to the first page and read aloud, deliberately, drowning the rhythm in ink.

"*In the year of our founding, being the third autumn after the clearing,*" they read, "*we did set our voices into the earth as seed is set, and the earth did keep us through the winter, and the price was written down so that no child might say they were not told.*"

The knocking stopped.

For a moment there was only the wind working at the eaves, and then, softly, from the doorway, Gideon's voice: "That's not the whole entry."

Oscar looked up. He stood in the hall shadow with his head tilted, and in the lamplight his face did the thing it had been doing more and more lately—flickering, like a candle guttering, the child-shape sliding for half a heartbeat into something taller and wearier and much, much older, then settling back.

"There's a second paragraph," he said. "On the founding page. Your grandmother could recite it. So could I." He came forward into the light, and his bare feet made no sound at all. "Want me to say it?"

"No."

"It's short."

"*No.*"

Gideon stopped beside the table. He looked down at the ledger—at the columns of names, the struck-through ones, the ones marked *kept*—and his expression was so nakedly sad that Oscar's chest hurt, because it was Gideon's face, it was the face from the tree fort and the creek and the summer everything was still a game, and it was also, unmistakably now, the face of something that had been doing this for two hundred years.

"You think they're threatening you," Gideon said. "Dale. The watchers at the fence. You think it's pressure. Leverage." He touched the page, one fingertip resting beside a name marked *refused*—a name Oscar had looked up in the county records, a Marchetti cousin who'd died in 1931, age nine. "But it's not. It's grief. They've all lost someone to a refusal. Every single one of them. They stand at your fence because they don't know what else to do with their hands."

"And whose fault is that? Whose *rule* is that?"

"Mine," Gideon said simply. "Ours. The bargain's. It's very old and it isn't fair and I have never once claimed it was fair." He lifted his eyes. "But it's the only thing standing between this valley and the thing underneath it, and you've spent three weeks learning verses four deep and refusing to ask about the last one, and everybody can feel you deciding. The ground can feel you deciding. That's why the corn turned. That's why the hens are lined up like offerings. It isn't punishment, Oscar. It's *asking*. Everything down there knows how to do is ask, and ask, and ask."

"Then it can hear me now." Oscar stood, palms flat on the ledger, and spoke toward the floor, toward the dark beneath the floor, loud enough to fill the kitchen. "I hear you. I *know* you. I know you were sung into being by people who were scared and hungry, and I know you've been fed children, and I know the fifth verse, whatever it really does, and I am not going to sing it. Not forward. Not ever. Find another throat."

The silence afterward had texture. It had weight. Somewhere far below the cellar, deeper than the hatch went, something exhaled—a long, slow movement of air through stone, almost like disappointment, almost like relief.

Gideon laughed. It was a small, broken sound.

"Oh, you *idiot*," he whispered, and there was such love in it that it took Oscar a moment to hear the terror underneath. "You beautiful, stubborn idiot. You think refusing is the same as ending it." He backed toward the hallway, and the shadows took him unevenly, his outline fraying like cloth. "Refusing is just choosing who pays. That's all it's ever been. Ask Mrs. Aldous what her son chose. Ask her what came up out of the ground that November wearing his voice."

"Gideon—"

"Nine days," he said from the dark of the hall, and his voice was already fading, already thinning into the walls. "And Oscar—the knocking tonight. Count it again. Three slow, three fast. That's not the pattern from our games."

The house settled around its own silence.

"That's the pattern for when something below is *counting down*."

* * *

Oscar didn't sleep. Near dawn they carried the lamp down the cellar stairs—not to the hatch, not yet, but to the corner where Grandmother Marchetti's trunk sat under a dust sheet—and knelt and opened it and took out the ledgers again, all of them, and stacked them by date on the cold concrete.

The earliest volume began in 1804. The latest ended mid-sentence, in their grandmother's shaking hand, dated eight days before she died:

*The child is coming home. If they refuse, God forgive me, I should have told them about Dale's father. I should have told them what happened to the ones who thought refusal was an ending. The verse doesn't end. The verse only*

Nothing after. The pen stroke trailed off the page like something dragged away.

Oscar sat back on their heels in the freezing cellar, lamp guttering, and above them—faint, rhythmic, patient—came the sound of the whole town beginning to wake: doors, engines, kettles. A valley full of people who believed they were kind, preparing for a harvest, waiting to see whether the Marchetti throat would open.

Three slow. Three fast.

They counted without meaning to. Then they counted again.

Seven days now.
