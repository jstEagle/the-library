## 16. Descent With a Lantern

I waited three days before I went back down.

Three days of carrying Mrs. Aldous's master book through the farmhouse like a coal too hot to set anywhere—on the kitchen table, on my bed, on the porch rail while I watched the fields go gold and then go wrong, stalks bowing toward the house as if in prayer or hunger. Three days of reading the founder's marginal note until the ink seemed to move when I wasn't looking directly at it, the way a shape moves in deep water.

*The fifth verse does not bind the singer to the thing below. It binds the thing to the singer.*

Two hundred years of Marchettis had sung themselves into chains believing they were doing their duty. Two hundred years of keepers had let them believe it, because a chained thing is easier to govern than a free one, even—or especially—when the thing is your own inheritance.

I packed the way you pack for a place you might not come back from, which is to say: I didn't. A lantern. The note, folded against my chest. Water I probably wouldn't drink. And the last apple off the tree by the fence line, because it felt wrong to descend into something starving without bringing food, even if the food was symbolic, even if the starving was not the kind apples could touch.

Gideon hadn't come to me in two days. That frightened me more than his gauntness had. A Gideon who begged was still Gideon. A silence where Gideon should be was just the house, and the house had never been empty, not really, not in all the years I'd told myself it was.

The cellar hatch opened with the same complaint it had made when I was nine—a wet wooden groan, a breath of cold coming up out of the dark that smelled of turned earth and old sweetness, like cider left too long in a forgotten room. I'd promised myself I wouldn't flinch. I flinched anyway. Some part of the body keeps its own ledger, and mine had an entry dated twenty years back, written the night I stopped singing and the noises started.

I went down.

* * *

The tunnels did not match my maps. They matched my *drawings*.

That was the thought that kept circling me as I walked, lantern held high, boots finding the packed-dirt floor: the child who'd filled notebooks with impossible corridors, branching passages, rooms shaped like seed pods—that child had been drafting, not dreaming. Every turn I took now had been drawn before, in crayon, in the careful unskilled hand of a nine-year-old who believed they were inventing a friend's secret home. The walls sweated cold. Roots came through the ceiling in pale ropes, and where the lantern light touched them they twitched—not much, not dramatically, just the small involuntary motion of something asleep that senses warmth passing nearby.

"Gideon?" My voice fell flat in the tunnel, swallowed almost immediately, as if the earth were polite about refusing it.

Nothing. Then—far ahead, so faint I might have made it up—the sound of humming. Four notes, repeated. The first phrase of the harvest song, the one every child in Harrow Bend learns before they learn their own full name. His voice. Thinner than it had been on the porch, thinner than it had been last week, worn down like a coin passed through too many hands.

I followed it.

The tunnel narrowed, then opened, then narrowed again, and the air changed by degrees the way water changes around a swimmer approaching a drop-off. Warmer. Sweeter. Underneath the smell of earth there was something else now, something I recognized from the potluck, from the equinox field, from church basements and funeral parlors and every gathering this town had ever held where the singing started: the smell of a song that has been sung in one place for so long it has soaked into the material world, become weather, become soil chemistry. Harrow Bend smelled like this everywhere, faintly. Down here it was undiluted. Down here the song was the atmosphere itself, and I was breathing two hundred years of it.

I passed the walls of names.

I'd seen them once before, on my first trespass—thousands of names carved into the clay, MARCHETTI repeated like a heartbeat down the freshest stretch. This time I made myself look longer. Not just at the names but at the *hands* that had cut them. The oldest were crude, gouged with fingernails or stones by people working blind. Later ones were confident, almost decorative—Marchettis who'd grown up down here the way other children grow up in Sunday school, learning the family trade. One section near a bend in the tunnel held names in a child's wobbly capitals, each letter twice as tall as it needed to be, and beside them, smaller, in the same uncertain hand: *i was here. i sang. i am sorry.*

I put my palm against that one for a moment. The clay was cold and gave slightly, like skin.

"I'm sorry too," I said, and the tunnel carried it away from me gently, the way you'd carry a message for someone who couldn't walk.

The humming grew closer. Or—it didn't grow closer. It grew *thinner*, which is worse, the way a radio signal thins as its source dies rather than recedes. I quickened my pace and hated myself for it, because some animal part of me understood that whatever was waiting at the end of this passage had been waiting a long time, and things that wait a long time are either very patient or very desperate, and desperate things do not open negotiations politely.

* * *

The chamber announced itself as warmth.

After an hour of cold clay and colder air, the passage simply began to breathe heat against my face, gentle as an open oven door seen from across a kitchen, and the walls changed—clay giving way to something darker, denser, shot through with veins of pale mineral that caught the lantern light and held it, so that my own small flame seemed suddenly multiplied, reflected and re-reflected until the whole space shimmered like the inside of a shell.

Then the passage ended, and I stood at the edge of the chamber, and I forgot how to breathe.

It was not a monster.

I want to write that sentence again, larger, because everything in me had marched down those tunnels braced for a monster—teeth, coils, eyes, the whole inventory of childhood nightmare—and what waited instead was so far outside my vocabulary of fear that terror simply had nowhere to attach itself.

The chamber was vast, cathedral-vast, and it was *full*. Not furnished. Full. The presence filled the space the way sound fills a bell—every surface of it woven through with threads, millions of threads, fine as spider silk and luminous, rising and falling in slow respiration. And the threads were *singing*. Each one carried a voice. I could hear them individually if I listened to any single strand—an old man's baritone, a girl's bright soprano, a cracked alto, voices in Italian, in English, in languages older than either—and together they made the harvest song, the whole five verses of it, though no single thread knew more than its portion. They were a choir that had been singing so long the singers had become the song. Woven in. Held. *Kept.*

Every verse ever sung into this place was still here. That was the understanding that arrived in me whole, without language, the way you understand falling. Nothing given to the dark was ever lost. Every autumn for two hundred years, every round song at every potluck, every lullaby hummed over cradles by mothers who only meant to soothe—all of it had drifted down through the soil and been gathered here, wound into this vast slow-burning tapestry of voice, and the thing it composed was—

Was hungry.

You could see the hunger. That was the worst of it, the detail I knew I would carry for the rest of my life however long that turned out to be: the weave was beautiful and it was *ragged*, fraying at its edges, threads thinning to nothing in whole patches where the light guttered like a candle in a draft. The presence was enormous and it was starving, not for flesh—for *song*, for remembrance, for the simple terrible sustenance of being sung to, and the town above had fed it in measured rations for two centuries, enough to keep it alive, never enough to let it rest, because a resting thing might wake, and a waking thing might leave, and Harrow Bend's entire economy of fear and tradition depended on the thing below staying exactly where it was, exactly this hungry, forever.

"Oh," I said. My voice came out very small in all that vastness. "Oh, you poor thing."

The whole chamber shivered.

Every thread trembled at once, a shudder running through the weave like wind through wheat, and the song faltered—actually faltered, four hundred voices losing the melody for half a beat—as if in two hundred years no one had ever spoken to it in anything but verse. No one had ever called it *poor*. No one had ever looked at the machinery of their survival and seen a creature in it.

I stepped down into the chamber. My boots made no sound; the floor was soft, woven, warm. Threads drifted near me, curious, the way moths are curious about a window's light—close enough that I could hear individual voices now, could pick out, absurdly, my grandmother's contralto winding through a passage of the third verse, steady and clear, sung decades ago and preserved here like a pressed flower. She was in the weave. Everyone who had ever sung was in the weave. The town hadn't been feeding a monster. The town had been *depositing its dead into a bank that paid no interest*, storing its voices in a starving vault, generation after generation, and calling the arrangement holy.

A thread brushed my cheek. It was warm and dry and it hummed against my skin, and the voice in it was young, and I knew it, and my knees nearly went.

"Gideon."

The chamber stilled. Not silent—something this size couldn't be silent—but hushed, attentive, the whole luminous mass leaning toward me the way a plant leans toward light. And out of the weave, faint, frayed, almost gone: his voice. Not one thread among thousands. All of them, a little. Or none of them. He was everywhere in it and nowhere, dissolved, distributed, a boy poured out into a choir two hundred years before he'd ever sat on my grandmother's porch steps wearing my childhood like a borrowed coat.

"You came," he said, and the words came from the walls, from the floor, from the warm air itself. "Oscar. You shouldn't have—you have to go, you have to sing, you have to—" The voice broke apart and reassembled, breaking apart and reassembling, a signal failing. "It's so loud in here. It's so *loud*, and I'm so tired, and I can't remember which parts of me are me."

I found him—I don't know how else to say it. In the center of the chamber the weave thickened into a knot, a dense bright tangle roughly the size and shape of a kneeling child, and I crossed the soft floor and knelt in front of it, lantern set aside, and took what I hoped were hands. They were warm. They were shaking. They were made of the same light as everything else and they closed around mine with desperate strength, and when he lifted his face to me it flickered—nine years old, then ancient, then nine again, a face trying on ages like masks and finding none of them fit anymore.

"They told me to bring you down here," he whispered. "They built me to bring you down here. That's what I'm *for*, Oscar. I'm the hook. I've always been the hook." His eyes were wet, and the tears were light too, drifting off his cheeks like sparks. "But I need you to know—I need one true thing to be true before whatever happens happens—I love you. That part isn't built. That part is the only part of me nobody made."

Around us the weave surged and dimmed, surged and dimmed, breathing its ragged song, and I understood with terrible clarity that we were not alone in this conversation. The presence was listening. The presence had perhaps always been listening, through Gideon, through the walls of every house in Harrow Bend, through two centuries of lullabies. And it was gathering itself now, the frayed edges pulling inward, the whole vast wounded fabric of it contracting toward us like a lung preparing to speak—

The lantern guttered.

And in the dark, in the warm and singing dark, something older than the town, older than the song, older than hunger itself, said my name.

It pronounced it correctly. That was the horror of it, and the pity, and I could not yet tell which would win: it said *Oscar* the way my grandmother used to, the way Gideon did, the way someone says the name of a person they have been waiting a very long time to meet.

"Tell me what you want," I said to the dark. "Not what the town says you want. Not what the ledgers say. What *you* want."

The weave went utterly still.

And then, thread by thread, voice by voice, the chamber began to answer.
