## 7. What Edith Kept

The winter of 1974 came early and came hungry.

Edith Marlowe was twenty-six that year, and she remembered — would always remember, in the way one remembers the exact weight of a stone carried too long — the first morning the frost lay on the inside of the kitchen window. Not the outside. The inside. She had stood at the sink with her tea going cold in her hands and watched the fern-patterns crawl across the glass from within, delicate as lace, patient as anything with time to spare, and she had thought: *that's not right.* And then she had thought: *but the mine's closed, and Father hasn't worked since October,* and she had wiped the frost away with her sleeve and said nothing to anyone.

That was how it began. Not with a decision but with a silence, a small private agreement between herself and the wrongness of things, signed in tea-stained quiet and never once revoked.

* * *

Hollowmere in November of that year was a village holding its breath. The Vane Colliery had shuttered its gates in the spring — *temporary measures*, the notice had said, though everyone knew what temporary meant when the men in suits used it — and by autumn the shops had begun to shorten their hours, then their shelves, then their smiles. The Marlowes were luckier than most; Edith's father still had his allotment, and her mother could stretch a mutton shank across three days like taffy. But luck in Hollowmere was a shared coat, and it was wearing thin at every seam.

And then the traveling family had come up from the south in their green-and-cream van, the one with the painted swallows on the side panel, and everything had changed.

Edith met them first at the Fox and Hare, where she'd gone to collect her father on a wet Tuesday. The family sat near the fire — a broad man with forearms like mooring rope, a thin woman with a laugh that startled the room into warmth, and two children, a boy of perhaps twelve and a girl who couldn't have been more than nine. The girl sat very straight on the bench with her hands folded, watching the room the way a bird watches a garden: not afraid, exactly. Cataloguing.

Her name was Elenora. Edith learned it because the girl introduced herself — unprompted, formally, the way children do when they've been taught manners are armor. "I'm Elenora Voss," she said, looking up at Edith with eyes the color of strong tea held to the light. "We're staying till the roads clear."

"Till the roads clear," Edith repeated, and smiled, and thought nothing of it. That was the thing about guilt, she would learn much later. It never announced itself. It arrived dressed as an ordinary evening, and you only recognized it decades afterward, when it had grown old alongside you and learned your face.

* * *

The strange things started small, as they always did.

Mrs. Tredwell at the post office swore her rheumatism had eased overnight. The Hendersons' well, which had run chalky all summer, ran sweet. And the frost — the frost behaved itself. It lay where frost ought to lie, outside the glass, in the fields, on the roofs, decorative and harmless, and the village, without ever discussing it aloud, began to breathe again.

It was Tom Ashcroft who first said the name Gideon House at the pub, and he said it quietly, the way you mention a rich relation you're not sure is still speaking to you. "House has been warm," he said. "Walked past Tuesday. Windows lit. Smoke from the chimney."

"Nobody's lived there since the war," someone said.

"Somebody lives there now."

Nobody asked who. That was the detail Edith would return to over and over in the long years after, turning it like a coin worn smooth: nobody asked who. The question rose in every throat in that smoke-filled room — she saw it rise, saw the men glance at one another and away — and every throat swallowed it whole. Because asking who meant admitting something was there, and admitting something was there meant deciding what to do about it, and Hollowmere in the winter of 1974 had no strength left for decisions. It only had appetite for relief.

* * *

Her father was summoned to the church on the last Sunday of November. All the households were summoned — a folded note through each door, unsigned, in handwriting so neat it looked printed. *Parish meeting. St. Brannoc's. After evening service. Attendance matters.*

Attendance matters. Edith went with her parents because she was twenty-six and unmarried and still did as she was told, and because some part of her — the part that had watched the frost crawl along the inside of the window and said nothing — already suspected what attendance would cost.

The church was full. She had never seen it full, not even at Christmas. Every pew occupied, bodies pressed shoulder to shoulder, breath rising in the cold air like the congregation itself was smoking. Reverend Calloway stood at the lectern looking, Edith thought, like a man reading his own death warrant to check the spelling. Beside him, uninvited and unhurried, sat a woman none of them knew — tall, grey-eyed, with hair pinned in a style thirty years out of fashion, her hands folded in her lap as if she had all the patience in the world and intended to use every second of it.

She never gave her name. Later, Edith understood that this was because she didn't have one anymore, or rather had too many, or rather had given hers away so long ago that the giving had hollowed her out into something that could only ever speak on behalf of the house. But that night she was simply the stranger, and her voice, when she finally used it, was soft enough that the whole church had to lean in to hear, which Edith would come to understand was the oldest trick in negotiation: make them come to you.

"The house thanks the village for its hospitality," the woman said. "The house remembers hospitality."

Silence. Somewhere in the rafters a roosting pigeon shifted.

"You have suffered," the woman continued. "The pit closed. The winter comes. The house has watched, and the house is not unmoved." Her grey eyes moved across the pews like a lighthouse beam, slow, impartial, touching everyone. "There is a way. There has always been a way. The house asks little. The house gives much. A mild winter. Full stores. Work returned to the mine by spring — I do not promise this lightly; the house does not promise at all. But the covenant must be renewed, as it was renewed before your grandparents' time, and the renewal requires—" here she paused, and the pause was so perfectly weighted that Edith felt it settle on her chest like a hand— "a resident. A child. Given freely, kept gladly."

The words fell into the church and lay there.

"A child," someone repeated. Old Mrs. Penhale, her voice cracked with age and outrage. "You're asking us to give you a child."

"I am asking nothing," the woman said. "The house is offering. You may refuse. The house has watched villages refuse." She unfolded her hands, refolded them. "The frost was on the inside of your windows last week. It will be back. It will be worse. The house is patient either way. Patience is all the house has."

* * *

They did not vote that night. They voted eleven days later, after the frost had come back and taken the water pipes in four houses and old Mr. Curnow's heart in a fifth, and after the woman in the outdated hairstyle had been seen walking the village lane at dusk, unhurried, her shadow pointing toward Gideon House no matter which way she turned. Eleven days of whispered conversations at gates and over fences, of mothers pulling daughters close in shops, of fathers staring into fires doing arithmetic they hated themselves for. Eleven days of the whole village becoming complicit in increments, each small silence another stitch in the shroud.

Edith remembered the vote itself with terrible clarity, the way trauma preserves things like flies in amber. Evening service done, the congregation not dismissed but simply remaining, settling deeper into the pews as though the wood had grown roots. Reverend Calloway announcing, in a voice sanded down to almost nothing, that the parish would decide by show of hands, that the matter was the continued welfare of the village against — and here he stopped, and looked down at his notes, and did not finish the sentence, and no one made him.

"All in favor," he said instead.

And Edith's own father — her kind, tired, allotment-tending father, who had taught her to whistle and to gut a fish and never to lie — raised his hand.

She stared at it. At the knuckles, at the wedding band, at the small white scar across the thumb from the time he'd slipped with the billhook when she was six. It hung in the candlelight like something disembodied, like a hand raised in a crowd where she didn't know anyone, and around it other hands were rising too, dozens of them, scores, a slow pale forest growing up out of the pews, and the sound was the worst part — the creak of joints, the whisper of sleeves, the collective exhale of a village selling something it couldn't bear to name.

Edith did not raise her hand.

She wanted to believe, for years afterward, that this mattered. That her hand staying in her lap was a protest, a witness, a small lit candle of decency in the dark. But she was honest enough, eventually, to admit what actually happened in that pew: she stayed silent because raising her hand would have required meeting Elenora Voss's eyes someday, and keeping it down let her pretend she hadn't voted at all. Silence wasn't innocence. Silence was just guilt with better posture.

Because here was the thing she had known, had known since the frost crawled the wrong way across her window, had known and buried and fed and kept alive: she had seen the girl. She had spoken with her. *Till the roads clear.* And when the show of hands finished and Reverend Calloway counted — she watched his lips move, watched the number land on his face like a blow — when he said, "Carried," Edith had felt something in the church shift, some vast attention leaning closer, satisfied, and she had thought with perfect clarity: *we have just agreed to feed a child to the winter, and the winter is going to say thank you.*

* * *

The Voss family left three days later. Or rather — the van left. Green-and-cream, swallows on the side panel, heading south with the man driving and the woman beside him and the boy asleep in the back under a blanket. Four people leaving. Edith counted them from her bedroom window as the van passed, counted twice, counted a third time with her heart going like a trapped bird, because she had seen the family at the pub often enough to know their faces and the back seat held only one sleeping shape where there should have been two.

The village told itself stories. The girl had gone ahead to relatives. The girl had been left with friends further north. The girl had never existed quite the way Edith remembered — *Voss, was it? I thought it was Voss with an F. Wasn't the van blue?* — and the stories changed by one detail apiece, person to person, until no two villagers held quite the same memory, and Edith came to understand, slowly, over years, that this was not faulty recollection. This was maintenance. Something was tending their memories the way a gardener tends a hedge, trimming here, filling gaps there, keeping the shape neat and the truth pruned back from the light.

Only Edith seemed immune, and she never learned why. Perhaps because she had never raised her hand. Perhaps the house left one witness per covenant, the way a recipe requires salt. Perhaps being unable to forget was simply her portion of the price, levied quietly, collected nightly, in the currency of sleepless hours.

* * *

The mine reopened in March. The men went back down the shaft singing. The winter, which had promised to be brutal, broke mild in February, and the stores lasted, and Mrs. Tredwell's rheumatism vanished entirely, and the village ate well and slept well and did not look at Gideon House, whose windows glowed warmly all through the coldest nights, whose chimney smoked steadily, whose grounds never showed a single footprint in the snow except — once, only once, in January — a line of small bare footprints leading up the path from the road. Leading in. Never out.

Edith walked past those prints on her way to work at the bakery. She stood over them for a long moment in the blue morning cold. They were small. Nine-year-old small. And pressed into the snow beside them, at intervals, as though the child had trailed one hand along an invisible wall, were fingerprints. Too large for a child's hand. Too many joints.

She did not tell anyone. Who would she tell? Everyone had raised their hands. Everyone had counted. Everyone had eaten the mild winter like a meal and licked their plates.

So instead she did the only thing left to her. She kept it. All of it — the frost on the inside of the glass, the stranger's soft voice, her father's scarred thumb rising in the candlelight, the word *carried*, the footprints in the snow, the fingerprints with their terrible extra knuckles. She kept it the way her mother kept specimens: pressed flowers between the pages of the Bible, a wasp in amber resin on the mantel, a lock of her dead sister's hair in an envelope marked with a date. Guilt preserved like a specimen, labeled and dated and stored against the day it might be needed.

She did not know, then, that the day would take fifty years to arrive. She did not know she would be ninety-four years old, the last one living who remembered correctly, when a stranger with a pest controller's van came up the frozen road to Hollowmere and looked at her with questions in his face.

She only knew, standing over those small footprints in January of 1975, that she had helped put them there — that her silence had been a vote too, a coward's aye — and she made herself a promise in the blue cold, alone, unwitnessed:

*I will remember. Whatever it costs me, whatever it makes me forget, however long it takes — I will keep this true. Someone has to. Someone has to remember rightly, or it wins.*

Behind her, up the hill, the windows of Gideon House glowed like eyes opening.

And far off, though the bell had been melted down in 1911 and poured into shells for a war older than the child now living behind those windows, Edith Marlowe heard — or dreamed she heard, or was permitted to hear — a single toll roll across the frost, deep and patient and unmistakably expectant, as if something had just checked its watch and found the hour good.
