## 1. The Wrong Body

The funeral home smelled of lilies and carpet cleaner, and beneath both, faintly, of cold meat.

Freya Vane stood in the doorway of the viewing room for a long moment before she could make her feet move. The room was done in the muted greens and creams that funeral directors believed were soothing—sage walls, cream curtains, a carpet the color of weak tea—and in the center, on a wheeled stand draped in more cream cloth, sat the casket. Half-open. The way they did it when there was a face to show.

Seven years. Seven years since she had seen her brother, and now they wanted her to look at him through a hinged lid, under flattering light, with a card in her lapel reading *Condolences to the Family* as if she were a guest at someone else's grief.

*You are the family,* she reminded herself. *You are all that's left of it.*

She crossed the room the way you cross ice over deep water: evenly, carefully, without looking down.

The mortician had done his work well. That was the first thing she noticed, and it shamed her—that her professional eye went to craft before it went to Gideon. The skin had been given back some warmth, a rose-tinted suggestion of circulation brushed along the cheekbones. The hair was combed in a side part, which was wrong, because Gideon had never in his life worn a part; he'd pushed his hair straight back off his forehead like a man perpetually about to dive into water. But that was a small thing. People changed their hair. Seven years was long enough for a man to change everything.

His face was thinner than she remembered. Older, obviously—he would have been thirty-one now—but thinner in a way that age didn't explain, as if the years had been spent rather than lived. His mouth was set in an expression of mild patience, the face of a man waiting for a bus.

Freya stood at the rail and looked at her brother's body, and felt nothing rise in her except a terrible, clinical attention.

Then she looked at his hands.

They were folded on his chest, visible above the satin lining, arranged with care—the left over the right, fingers relaxed. She knew hands. She was a novelist; she had spent fifteen years learning to see hands, to describe them so precisely that readers felt them without knowing why. Hands told the truth when faces lied. Faces could be composed. Hands remembered.

Gideon's hands had been pianist's hands, long-fingered and narrow-boned, with knuckles that flared like the joints of bamboo. He'd been vain about them in the way boys are vain about things they pretend not to notice. When they were children he would spread them flat on the dinner table and say, *Look, Frey. Spider hands. Grandmother says I got them from Great-Uncle Ambrose.* And he would wiggle the fingers, and grandmother would smile her closed-lip smile and say nothing at all.

The hands in the casket were broad. Square-palmed, thick through the fingers, with short nails cut straight across and calluses ridging the base of each digit like old rope burns. A laborer's hands. A mechanic's hands. Hands that had gripped tools for decades.

And no scar.

When Freya was nine and Gideon was nine—they were nine together, always the same age, always the same everything, that was the whole architecture of their lives—he had climbed the black walnut behind the house and fallen out of it onto the rusted edge of an old garden trellis. It had opened his left palm from the heel of his thumb almost to the wrist, a clean bright line that bled spectacularly and required eleven stitches. Grandmother had driven them to the clinic herself, both hands on the wheel, silent the entire way while Freya cried and Gideon, gray-faced and fascinated, watched his own blood pool in his cupped right hand and said, *Look, Frey, I'm holding myself.*

The scar had healed into a pale seam, slightly puckered where the stitches pulled, running diagonal across his left palm. She had traced it a hundred times over the years—when they held hands crossing parking lots as teenagers, when he pressed his palm to her forehead to check for fever, when they sat knee-to-knee on the porch steps and he talked with his hands the way he talked with everything, hugely, constantly. It was as much a part of him as his laugh. More, maybe. Scars outlast laughs.

The left hand folded on the dead man's chest was unmarked. Smooth. Whole. The palm of a man who had never fallen out of any tree.

Freya's breath went out of her slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door closes somewhere far away.

*That's not my brother.*

The thought arrived without drama. It didn't shout. It simply presented itself, calm and complete, the way you notice a picture hanging crooked—not the painting itself, just its wrongness against the wall. She looked at the face again, really looked, and saw what she should have seen immediately: the resemblance was good but not true. The nose was close. The brow was close. But Gideon's face had been assembled around a crookedness—a left ear that stuck out slightly, a chin with a small cleft you only saw in certain lights—and this face was symmetrical, smoothed, a portrait of her brother painted by someone who had studied photographs but never known him.

A stranger wearing her brother's name.

"Miss Vane?"

She hadn't heard the door. Mr. Aldous, the funeral director, stood a respectful distance behind her, a thin man with kind eyes and the particular soft-footedness of people who spend their lives around the bereaved. He was holding a folder. Of course he was holding a folder. There was always a folder.

"I'm sorry," he said gently. "I can give you as long as you need."

"How did you find me?" Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "After seven years. How did anyone know it was him?"

"The county identified him through dental records." Aldous consulted the folder. "There was a match to records from his childhood. The remains were found during demolition work—an old property outside town. The state had been trying to locate next of kin for some months. You were... difficult to find, Miss Vane."

*I wasn't difficult to find,* she thought. *I was easy not to look for.*

"I see." She turned back to the casket. The stranger's hands lay folded in their borrowed peace. "Was there anything with him? Belongings?"

"A wallet. Some cash. Identification." A pause, delicate as a held note. "Nothing else, I'm afraid. The condition of the remains suggested—" He stopped himself, recalibrated. "It had been a long time."

Seven years, give or take. The math arrived in her head unbidden and unwelcome: if the body had been in the ground—or wherever—for most of those seven years, then it had died around the time Gideon disappeared. Around the time grandmother died. The same night, the coroner's preliminary report said. Or near enough. The file used careful language: *post-mortem interval consistent with late autumn, seven years prior.*

The night the house went quiet. The night the phone calls stopped. The night Freya, twenty-four years old and desperate to be anywhere but that house, had packed a car and driven until the fuel gauge screamed, and had told herself for seven years afterward that her brother had chosen the same road in the opposite direction.

*He ran,* she had told herself. *People run. Twins don't owe each other permanence.*

She had built a whole life on that sentence. Two novels, one marriage, one divorce, an apartment in a city three hundred miles north. All of it load-bearing walls erected around the assumption that Gideon was alive somewhere, choosing his absence, resenting her or forgetting her or both.

And now the county had dug up a stranger with her brother's teeth and laid him in sage-green quietude, and every load-bearing wall in her life was beginning, very softly, to groan.

"Miss Vane?" Aldous again, patient. "There are documents. If you're ready."

Documents. Signatures. The machinery of death required its oil.

She followed him to a small desk in the corner of the viewing room—of course the desk was in the viewing room, so that grief and paperwork could share the same fluorescent hum—and he laid out the forms one by one, squaring their edges, turning each toward her with the practiced choreography of a man who had done this ten thousand times.

Release of remains. Authorization for cremation—the body was too old, too compromised, too *long*, for anything else, he explained, though the family could discuss burial if she preferred, though the cost, though the considerations. Next-of-kin affidavit. A form confirming the identity of the deceased: *I, the undersigned, being the nearest living relative, do hereby confirm that the remains designated Case 24-1187 are those of Gideon Ambrose Vane.*

Her pen hovered.

Here was the moment. She understood that with a strange, floating clarity—the sense of standing at a fork in a dark road where both directions look identical until you've already walked down one. All she had to do was say it. *This isn't him. Look at his hands. My brother has a scar on his left palm and ears that stick out and he parted nothing, ever, and whoever this is, he isn't Gideon Vane, and I'd like to know who is.* Say it, and the machinery would grind to a halt, and there would be questions, and exhumations, and missing-persons files reopened, and detectives asking her where she'd been for seven years and why she'd never reported him, and—

And what? What would they find? A stranger with her brother's dental work. Dental records didn't lie. Somewhere in a county archive sat a chart of Gideon's childhood fillings, and this body matched it, filling for filling. Teeth were supposed to be the honest part of us. Teeth outlasted fire and water and time.

But hands didn't lie either. And scars didn't lie. And she was looking at a corpse that had her brother's teeth and a laborer's palms and no scar at all, and both of those things could not be true, and yet here they were, lying side by side in satin like old friends.

Unless.

Unless something had happened to his hands. An accident, a fire, surgery—she was reaching, and she knew she was reaching, grasping at rational straws the way a drowning man grasps at anything that floats. Eleven stitches healed into a scar that doesn't vanish. Skin grafts left seams worse than the wound. Nothing erased a childhood scar completely. Nothing made pianist's hands into a bricklayer's.

*Say it,* she told herself. *Say this isn't him.*

But a colder voice underneath, a voice she didn't recognize as her own, said: *Say it, and then what? They'll ask you where he's been. They'll ask you why you never looked. They'll open the file, and the file will lead back to the house, and the house will have to be opened too, and do you want that? Do you want to know what's been kept in there?*

She didn't know where the voice came from. That frightened her more than the body did.

"Take your time," Aldous murmured.

Freya signed.

Her signature came out steady, which surprised her—she had expected the letters to shake. *Freya Maren Vane*, in the quick slanted hand she'd used since high school, flowing across the line like water finding its level. Then the next form. Then the next. Four signatures in all, four small betrayals performed in blue ink, and with each one she felt something shift in the room's atmosphere, some pressure redistributing itself, the way a house settles after everyone's gone to bed.

She couldn't explain why she did it. Not then, not later, not in all the nights that followed. The closest she ever came to an explanation was this: the body needed a name to rest under, and if it wasn't going to get its own—if no one was ever going to know whose hands those were, whose mother had counted his fingers at birth—then it could at least borrow her brother's, and keep the grave warm, and hold the door shut on all the questions she wasn't ready to answer. Someone had to lie in the ground. Let it be a kindness. Let it be the first lie of many, told for the best reasons, the way all the worst lies are.

"There," Aldous said softly, gathering the pages. "That's everything settled. Cremation will be completed by Thursday. We can ship the remains anywhere you'd like, or—" he hesitated, delicately, "—given that the family plot is local, many people choose..."

"The house," Freya said. "Grandmother's house. Yes. He should come home."

The words left her mouth and hung in the sage-colored air, and she heard how they sounded—*home*, as if the house were still a home, as if anything about that place had ever let either of them go.

Aldous nodded and made a note. "Of course. And you'll be staying at the property in the meantime? For the estate?"

"For the estate," she agreed. "I'll need a few days. A week, maybe. To sort things."

"Of course." He paused at the door, folder under his arm, and gave her the look—the one funeral directors and nurses and priests learn, the look that carries a question wrapped in courtesy. "Miss Vane. May I ask something? You don't have to answer."

She braced. *Ask me if I'm sure. Ask me about the identification. Ask me anything.*

"When you're ready," he said instead, "the viewing room will be here. Some people find it easier to say goodbye twice."

*Twice,* she thought, as he slipped out and the door clicked shut. *If only you knew.*

* * *

She stayed after all. She didn't mean to; her car was in the lot and the afternoon was dying into that bruised gold that October does so well, and every sensible instinct said *drive, drive now, drive until dark catches you*. But her feet took her back to the rail, and she stood there while the light through the cream curtains went from gold to copper to ash, looking down at the stranger with her brother's name.

Talking to the dead was a writer's habit. She did it now, silently, lips barely moving.

*Who are you?*

The painted-on warmth of the cheeks. The patient, bus-stop mouth. The broad hands with their rope-burn calluses, folded like a man who had worked all his life and was finally allowed to stop.

*Who are you, and how did you get his teeth?*

No answer. Of course no answer. Dead men were the one audience that never wrote back.

But as the last light failed and the room began to fill with shadows that pooled in the corners like spilled ink, Freya noticed something she had missed before. Something small. The kind of detail her editor would circle in red and write *why?* beside it in sharp pencil.

The dead man's right hand—the lower hand, tucked beneath the left—was not quite relaxed. The fingers were curled, very slightly, inward. Not a fist. Not rigor. Just a gentle closing, the way a sleeping child's hand closes around a parent's finger.

Or the way a hand closes around something it is holding. Something small. Something it carried a long way and refused, even in death, to put down.

The mortician had missed it. Or the mortician had seen it and decided, professionally, that the dead were entitled to their secrets.

Freya leaned closer over the rail, close enough that her breath fogged the polished wood of the casket's edge, and looked at that half-closed hand, and felt the dread arrive—not the sharp fear of the viewing room, not the guilt of the signatures, but a slow, cold, patient dread, the kind that seeps up through floorboards. Because she recognized that curl of the fingers. She had seen it before, once, a lifetime ago, on her brother's hand, the night of grandmother's funeral-that-never-was, when twelve-year-old Gideon had stood at the top of the stairs in the dark and wouldn't come down, and when she'd asked him what was wrong he had looked at his own clenched fist and whispered:

*Something gave it to me, Frey. Something gave it to me and I can't put it down.*

She had laughed at him then. She had been twelve too, and frightened, and laughing was the only tool she had.

She wasn't laughing now.

Outside, the funeral home's sign buzzed and flickered—once, twice—and steadied. In the parking lot, her car waited with its tank full and its engine cold, three hundred miles of city and sanity pointing north.

Tomorrow, she would drive to the house. Tomorrow, she would sort the estate, open the closets, pack the dishes, do all the ordinary arithmetic of a dead woman's belongings.

Tonight, standing over a stranger's body in a room full of lilies, Freya Vane signed one last thing in her heart, though no paper asked for it: a promise to her brother, wherever he was, whatever had happened to him.

*I'm coming home, Gid. Hold on. Tell me what you're holding.*

The extra chair at the kitchen table—she hadn't thought of it in years, hadn't let herself think of it—rose in her memory suddenly and completely: grandmother's kitchen, the morning after the funeral-that-never-was, a wooden chair pulled up to the table where no chair had ever sat, facing the door.

Waiting.

Freya turned off the viewing room light herself, plunging the stranger into a darkness that seemed, for just a moment, relieved.
