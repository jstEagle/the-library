## 4. What the Neighbors Saw

Marisol Reyes had lived across the road from the Vane house for eleven years, and for the first four of them she had believed herself to be a good neighbor. She kept her walkway swept. She waved at old Mrs. Vane when the woman came out to collect her mail, though the old woman never waved back, only watched her with those pale, patient eyes, the way a heron watches water it has no intention of fishing in. Marisol brought over a plate of tamales at Christmas the first year, and Mrs. Vane took the plate with both hands, said *thank you, dear* in a voice like a drawer sliding shut, and closed the door before Marisol had made it down the three steps.

That should have been the first sign. Kindness refused is a kind of confession.

But Marisol had grown up in a house where the doors were always open and the neighbors were always in and out, and she had never learned to read a closed door as anything other than shyness. So she kept waving. She kept sweeping her walkway. And on the night the ambulances came — the night the old woman died and the twins' mother arrived from two counties over with her face already folded into grief, the night a boy of seventeen walked out of the Vane house and was never seen again — Marisol stood at her own window with the lights off and watched, and did nothing, and told no one, for seven years.

* * *

It began, properly, three weeks after the funeral.

Marisol was up late — she was always up late in those years; her husband had left in the spring and the bed was too wide and the quiet too loud, and she had developed the habit of sitting in her kitchen at midnight with the radio on low, listening to stations in languages she didn't speak, just to hear a human voice. She was doing exactly that when she glanced up and saw the light in the upstairs window of the Vane house.

The house was supposed to be empty. Everyone knew it was empty. The mother had signed papers, taken what she wanted, and gone back to her life two counties over, leaving the house to sit and wait for the twins to come of age, or for the roof to fall in, whichever came first. The boy had run away — that was the story, and the story had a hole in it the exact shape of a seventeen-year-old, and the town had poured that hole full of assumptions and left it to set.

So the light was wrong. A lit window in an empty house at midnight is a sentence with no subject, and Marisol sat very still with her mug of chamomile going cold and looked at it the way you look at a word you've written a hundred times that has suddenly stopped looking like a word.

Then the figure crossed the window.

It moved the way a reflection moves — smooth, unhurried, slightly out of step with the world it moved through. It paused at the glass. And it raised its hand and waved at her.

Marisol did not wave back. She told herself, later, that she hadn't been sure it was waving at her, but that was a lie she told in the daylight, in the safety of her own kitchen, with the radio murmuring its gentle foreign syllables. She had known. The window was forty yards away and the night was clear and the figure was looking directly at her, and it waved, and she did not wave back, because the wave was wrong.

It waved with the left hand.

Now — here is the thing Marisol could not explain to anyone, the thing that kept her silent long after fear alone would have worn thin. She had never met the boy. Not really. He was seventeen and she was forty-three and he had moved through the town like weather, noticed but not known. She could not have picked his face out of a lineup. But she had seen him from her window a hundred times over four years, leaving for school, shooting hoops alone in the driveway in the failing light, and a hundred times she had seen him raise a hand to a passing car, and it was always, *always*, the right hand. She knew this the way you know the color of a door you pass every day — not as information but as fact, worn smooth by repetition.

The figure in the window waved with the left hand, and the wave was slow, and courteous, and it held a moment too long, like a bow.

Marisol turned off her kitchen light and went to bed with her clothes on. In the morning the window was dark, and she made herself a strong cup of coffee and told herself she had dreamed it.

* * *

She did not dream it. That was the trouble. The figure came back.

It came back the way weather comes back, the way birds come back to a feeder — with the regularity of something that has found a source. Always after dark. Always the same window, the upstairs one at the front of the house, the one that had been the boy's room. Always the same slow, left-handed wave, courteous as a doorman, patient as a debt.

Marisol started keeping a notebook. She didn't decide to; she simply found, one morning, that she had written the date and a time — *11:40 p.m., left window, waved* — in the margin of her grocery list, and after that the notebook lived on the kitchen table and filled, week by week, with her small terrible census. *Tuesday. 12:15. Stood at window 40 minutes. Did not wave. Wednesday. 11:05. Waved. Left hand.* She recorded the times the way other people record rainfall. On some nights the figure did not come, and those nights she noted too, with a relief that shamed her, because what did it say about her that the absence of a haunting could feel like a gift?

She never told her sister in Phoenix. She never told the priest, though Father Anselm had a way of asking after her sleep that made her certain he could smell a sleepless woman through the phone. She never told the police, though once — one time, in the second winter — she picked up the phone and dialed the first three digits and then set it down in its cradle as gently as if it were a sleeping child.

Why not? She turned that question over for years, the way you turn a stone in a pocket. The easy answer was fear: fear of not being believed, fear of being the crazy widow on Delgado Road, fear of the town's small merciless arithmetic that adds up a woman alone and a strange story and produces, always, the same sum. That answer was true as far as it went.

But there was another answer underneath it, and it took her seven years to say it out loud, even to herself, and she would only ever say it to the dark, in her kitchen, with the radio murmuring: the figure had never done anything wrong.

That was the horror of it, once you sat with it. It stood in the window. It waved. It kept its hours like a monk keeping prayers. It never pressed its face to the glass. It never tapped. It never, in seven years, did a single thing that a person could report to a person. If Marisol had called the police — *there's someone standing in the window of the empty Vane house* — what then? They would have found nothing, or they would have found a squatter, or they would have found the boy, alive, and asked him why he'd let the town believe he was gone, and the story would have cracked open into something worse than a ghost. Or they would have found nothing at all, and the nothing would have moved in next door to Marisol, permanent, official, and she would have had to live the rest of her life knowing that the official version of her window was *nothing*.

So she watched. And the figure watched her watching, and waved its slow left-handed wave, and the two of them kept each other's vigil across forty yards of road and seven years of silence, and the town slept around them both, choosing, in its collective and unspoken way, not to know. Because that's the thing about a town — a town is an animal with one eye, and it sees what it has agreed to see. The Vane house was *empty*. Everyone knew it was empty. And everyone, Marisol came to understand, knew it the way you know a thing you have all decided together, at some meeting no one remembers calling, and the lights in the upstairs window after dark were simply not seen, the way the poor and the strange and the inconvenient are not seen, and the town drove past with its headlights on and its eyes forward and its agreement intact.

Once, only once, someone else said something. The Vasquez boy — nine years old, gap-toothed, delivering the paper — came to Marisol's door in the blue hour of a summer evening and asked her, with the terrible directness of children, *do you know who lives in that house? My brother says nobody lives there but I seen the man in the window and he waved at me.* Marisol looked at the boy's open face and felt the whole weight of her seven years of silence settle onto her shoulders like a wet coat, and she said, *nobody lives there, mijo. It's probably a trick of the light.* And the boy looked at her for a long moment — children can tell when an adult is handing them a lie, they just don't yet know they're allowed to refuse it — and he said, *okay,* and took his paper, and rode off, and Marisol stood on her porch and watched him go and felt something in her chest that had no name in English and only a heavy, apologetic one in Spanish.

She had done it too, then. She was the town now. She had handed the boy the agreement and watched him sign it.

* * *

The years gave the figure detail, the way years give a face to a fetus or a character to a rumor. In the first year it had been a shape. By the third it had shoulders she recognized, the particular set of them, slightly uneven, one higher than the other — she had seen that posture a hundred times on the boy in the driveway. By the fifth year, on nights when the moon cooperated, she could see the pale oval of a face, and it was a young man's face, and it was *almost* right, the way a portrait is almost right, everything in its place and nothing quite where a living face keeps its features. And the hand — the left hand — she came to think of it as a signature. Whatever stood up there signing itself *Gideon Vane* in the window every night had never quite learned to hold the pen.

She wondered, sometimes, in the way you wonder about things you don't let yourself wonder about, whether it was the boy. Whether the boy had never left at all. Whether the story with the hole in it had a boy-shaped boy still inside it, kept somehow, kept the way you keep a thing you can't throw away and can't look at. She wondered what Mrs. Vane had died *of*. She wondered why the mother had not once, in seven years, come back to check on the house she still owned. She wondered why the grass grew slow around the Vane house, why the mail never accumulated, why in seven years the paint had not peeled and the gutters had not sagged while every other empty house on the road went softly to ruin. The house was *maintained*. She had noticed that in the second year and then, deliberately, with an effort she could feel in her jaw like a clenched tooth, she had *unnoticed* it.

That was the complicity, in the end. Not the silence — silence is just the shape fear takes. The complicity was the noticing and the un-noticing, the notebook she kept and never showed anyone, the three digits she dialed and the four she didn't, the tamales she never brought again, the Vasquez boy and his *trick of the light.* Every night she saw the wave and every morning she un-saw it, and the un-seeing was a labor, and she performed it faithfully, and the house across the road accepted her labor the way it accepted everything — patiently, courteously, with a wave of the wrong hand.

* * *

On the seventh autumn, a car came up the road that Marisol did not recognize, and she stood at her kitchen window with her notebook open and watched a woman get out of it.

She knew the face before she knew the name. It was the girl — the twin, the one who had left, the one whose leaving had been the other half of the story with the hole in it. She was a woman now, mid-twenties, with her hair pulled back and the particular posture of someone arriving at a place they have been avoiding for a long time. She stood in the driveway a while, looking up at the house, and Marisol watched her, and the woman never looked at the window, and the window never lit, and Marisol found herself gripping the edge of the sink.

*Don't go in,* she thought, and was surprised by the ferocity of it. *Don't go in there alone.*

The woman went in.

The door closed behind her with a sound Marisol heard clearly across the road, though it was a small sound, a latch sound, the sound of any door in any house. She stood at her sink until dark, and when dark came she did not turn on her radio, and when the upstairs window lit — and it lit, at 11:52 by the kitchen clock, earlier than it had ever come on before, the way a dog comes to the door early when it smells a storm — she did not write it down.

She stood in her dark kitchen and watched the figure take its place at the glass, and she watched it raise the left hand, and this time the wave was different. Shorter. Eager. Almost — she pressed her knuckles to her mouth — almost *hungry*. The way you wave at someone when the person you've been waiting for is finally, finally coming up the walk, and you can't quite keep the wanting out of your arm.

The figure was waving at the woman inside the house. Marisol was sure of it. Seven years of being waved at, and now she was watching the wave pass through her, aimed past her, at the girl who had come home, and Marisol understood with a cold, total certainty that settled into her bones like November — the way you understand a thing you have spent seven years refusing to understand — that the figure had not been keeping a vigil all this time.

It had been *waiting*. And now the waiting was over.

Marisol looked down at her notebook, at seven years of dates and times in her small careful handwriting — her census, her confession, her cowardice in ledger form — and she picked up her pen, and under the last entry she wrote: *She's home. I have to tell her what I saw.*

Then she turned off the kitchen light, and got her coat, and dug to the back of the pantry shelf where she had kept, all these years without ever once asking herself why, a box of rock salt.
