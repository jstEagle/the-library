## 6. The Journal

The handwriting was the first thing that broke her.

Freya had braced herself for everything else—the dust, the dark, the small museum of a boy's life preserved like something drowned and dredged up. She had braced for the smell of him, pencil shavings and the cedar of the old closet, for the band posters gone amber at the edges, for the model rocket on the shelf with its fins painted crooked because he'd refused her help and regretted it forever. She had even braced, in some wordless animal way, for the wrongness she could feel pooling in the hallway behind her like water finding its level.

She had not braced for the handwriting.

Because it started as his. Of course it did. It was Gideon's hand slanting across the first page of the black cloth-bound journal she'd found in the nightstand drawer, under a flashlight and a dead moth and a single white tooth wrapped in tissue paper. *Gideon Vane*, he'd written at the top of the first page, in the careful way he always began notebooks, as if naming himself were a ritual that kept the pages honest. *Age 17. If you're reading this, I'm either famous or dead, and honestly either one works.*

She laughed. It came out of her like something dislocated.

Then she turned to the last written page, because she was a coward, or a sister, or both, and she wanted to end before she began—and the handwriting there was hers.

Not similar. Not close. Hers. The particular forward lean of her lowercase e's, the way she crossed her t's low and hard, the little hitch in her y's where the pen always caught. She would have sworn it in court. She would have sworn it on their mother's grave, if their mother had one yet. *she's coming home,* said the last entry, in Freya's own hand, dated three days ago. No period. As if the sentence hadn't finished happening yet.

Freya sat down on the floor of her brother's room with her back against his bed and read the journal backward, from the end toward the beginning, because grief had taught her that the only way through Gideon was in reverse—retrace the disappearance until it became a presence again, walk the river upstream to the spring.

* * *

Three years ago, the handwriting was still recognizably his, but tired. Slower. The letters had begun to stand farther apart, like people at a funeral who no longer knew why they'd come.

*I forgot what the back of the school looks like,* he wrote. *I stood at the window for an hour trying to picture it. I know there's a loading dock. I know there's a tree. I can't make them sit next to each other. It's like my head is a house where someone has been moving the furniture.*

Beneath that, in smaller letters, pressed so hard the paper dimpled:

*Also I keep writing words I don't remember learning.*

He had taped a vocabulary list to the inside cover. Freya recognized the words. They were all hers. *Crepuscular. Threshold. Unmake.* Words she used, words that lived in her mouth, copied out in his hand twenty times each like a boy doing penance, or practicing.

*Why do I know these?* he'd asked the page. *Why do I know HER words?*

Freya's throat closed. Outside the window, the light was going the color of old bruises, and somewhere below her she heard—not heard, felt—the house settle around her like a body adjusting its weight.

* * *

Five years ago, the entries got longer and stranger, and grandmother entered them.

*I heard her last night,* Gideon wrote. *Not a dream. Dreams have edges. This came from underneath the edges. She was in the hall outside my door and she wasn't walking anywhere, just standing, and she said: "You're doing so well, sweetheart."*

*I didn't open the door. I want that written down. I DID NOT OPEN THE DOOR.*

*But I could hear her smiling. Isn't that insane? You can't hear a smile. But I could. It sounded like paper tearing very slowly.*

Freya looked up. The door of Gideon's room was shut. She had shut it. She was almost sure she had shut it.

She went back to the page because the alternative was standing up, and standing up meant moving, and moving meant admitting the room was a room and not a text, and the text was safer. The text could be survived by reading.

*She keeps saying "the arrangement" like I should already know it,* he wrote, two entries later. *Like it's a recipe we've made together a hundred times. "You know the arrangement, dear." "The arrangement is nearly complete." "Your part of the arrangement." I asked her WHAT arrangement and she laughed and said, "Ask your sister."*

*I asked Freya. Obviously. She looked at me like I'd grown a second head. She doesn't know anything. She doesn't know ANYTHING, and God, I hope it stays that way.*

Freya pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until she saw constellations.

Seven years she had carried it: the certainty that Gideon had run. That the fight they'd had that last night—the stupid, ordinary fight about her leaving for the residency, about him staying, about who got to escape this town first—had sent him out the back door and into whatever took him. Seven years of composing apologies to a boy who never arrived to receive them. Seven years of believing her words had been the last thing he heard, and that they had been sharp ones.

He hadn't run.

He had been *kept*. Kept the way you keep a fire banked overnight so it will still be alive in the morning. Kept the way you keep a debt.

* * *

Six years ago, the handwriting began to change in earnest, and this was the part Freya would later be unable to describe without her hands shaking.

It didn't deteriorate. Deterioration goes down; this went *across*. Letter by letter, week by week, his hand drifted sideways into another hand entirely, and it took her four pages of backward reading to understand whose, because the destination was so familiar she couldn't see it, the way you can't see the room you're sitting in.

It became grandmother's hand. Edith Vane's narrow, upright, schoolmarm script, the hand that had addressed forty years of Christmas cards, emerging inside her grandson's journal like a photograph developing in reverse.

And then—it kept going. Past grandmother's hand. Into something older that Freya had no name for, letters that leaned like reeds in wind, that she could almost read and almost not, the way you can almost see the bottom of deep water.

At the bottom of one such page, in the reed-letters, a single line she could read:

*i am being so patient.*

* * *

She read until the flashlight died, and then she sat in the dark and let the room be a room, and it was worse than the text. The model rocket's crooked fins. The smell of him, fainter now, or maybe not fainter—maybe just more diluted by whatever else had been breathing in here all these years. On the desk, a half-finished letter to her, addressed *Dear Frey*, the first sentence visible under the lamp of her phone screen: *I don't know how to tell you this without you thinking I'm crazy, but I think Grandma sold something, and I think—*

Nothing after *I think*. Just the indentations of words pressed hard and then erased, over and over, until the paper wore soft as cloth.

There was a sound downstairs. A drawer closing. Gentle. Considerate. The way you close a drawer when someone in the house is sleeping, or reading, or grieving, and you love them, and you don't want to disturb them.

Freya did not move. She had learned that much already tonight: the house rewarded stillness. Or perhaps it simply hadn't noticed her yet. There was a difference, and she suspected the difference mattered enormously.

She went back to the journal. Backward. Upstream. Toward the spring.

* * *

The last year of entries—the first, going forward; the final, going back—was the worst, because it was the most coherent. Whatever had been happening to Gideon, it had happened slowly enough for him to document, methodically enough for him to leave her a map, and near the beginning he had still believed maps worked.

*Day 1 after the funeral (hers). Everyone keeps asking me how I'm holding up. Here is how I'm holding up: I counted the chairs at the reception and there were eleven and there should have been ten. Nobody else saw it. Marisol's mom pulled it away thinking it was extra. When she touched it, I swear to God, it was warm.*

*Day 4. Found a tooth in my nightstand. Mine? It's too big to be mine. Wrapped in tissue like a gift.*

*Day 9. Grandma's voice again. She says I agreed to something. She says I shook on it. I was fourteen. What could I have shaken on at FOURTEEN?*

*Day 11. I tried to leave. I got as far as the county line. I don't want to write this part. I'm going to write it anyway because if I don't write it, it isn't real, and if it isn't real, it wins.*

*I got to the county line and the road was the road home. Both directions. I drove east for forty minutes and arrived at our driveway. I drove west for forty minutes and arrived at our driveway. The gas gauge hadn't moved.*

*Day 12. I'm not trapped. I need to be clear about that, for whoever reads this. Trapped means someone is keeping you against your will. This is different. This is more like—I'm a guest. A long-term guest. The house is glad I'm here. I can feel it being glad, the way you can feel a dog leaning against your leg. And the horrible thing, the thing I will never say out loud, is that some part of me is glad to be leaned on.*

*Day 15. She taught me the word for what I am now. Not my name. My name is getting thin. She said: "You're the consideration, dear. Every contract needs one."*

*I looked it up. Consideration: that which is given in exchange; the price; the thing of value passed from hand to hand to make a promise binding.*

*I'm the price. I'm the thing that changed hands.*

Freya set the journal face-down on her knee. Her breath was coming in shallow pulls, fogging faintly in air that had no business being cold in early autumn. Downstairs, the considerate silence continued. She thought about the table set for two. She thought about the empty chair in every photograph, warm to a neighbor's touch seven years ago, warm, presumably, still.

*Day 19,* the journal said, in a hand that was now only half his. *I figured out the shape of it tonight. Grandma bought something in 1962. I found the ledger. I'm not supposed to know about the ledger. The ledger knows I know. I felt the whole house go quiet when I touched it, like a held breath.*

*Here's what I understand so far: you can pay a thing like this in flesh, and people have, and it's ugly and quick. Or you can pay it in years, which is what Grandma does, and it's slow and clean. But there's a third currency. The rare one. The good one.*

*Identity. A self. A name, worn out of existence one day at a time.*

*Grandma offered mine.*

*Not hers. Never hers. She's been paying in years since before I was born, and when the years ran low, she reached across the dinner table and put her hand on MY head and told the house: take the boy's name instead. Take the boy's selfhood. Let him stay here, safe, fed, loved—and hollow him gently. He won't feel it. He'll barely notice. And the house said yes, because a freely offered name is worth more than blood, and nobody loves you like family, and nobody can sell you like family either.*

*I hate her. I want that written down too. I hate her and I miss her and she's DEAD and she's STILL TEACHING ME THE ARRANGEMENT, and some nights the voice in the hall sounds less like her and more like whatever she was talking to, and THAT voice doesn't call me sweetheart. That voice calls me—*

The word was scratched out. Scratched out with such force that three pages beneath bore its ghost, reversed, unreadable, like a scar seen through skin.

*Day 22. If Freya ever reads this: it wasn't you. The fight, I mean. I've been trying to remember what we fought about and I can't, and I think that's the first thing it took. It takes the reasons you'd leave first. Then it takes the leaving.*

*I'm sorry I'm going to forget you. I'm fighting it. I write your name at the top of every page to keep it. Freya. Freya. Freya. If the handwriting gets weird, that's not me being weird, that's me losing. Read backward. I'm leaving the map in reverse so it'll look like nonsense going forward. Nonsense is safe. It only reads true from the end.*

Freya looked at the top of the page. At the top of every page. In the margins, in the corners, sometimes sideways, sometimes written over and over until the paper shone:

*Freya. Freya. Freya.*

Her own name, hundreds of times, in a hand that began as her brother's and ended as something patiently wearing it—a lighthouse blinking in a storm, spelling out the one word it had left.

He had spent seven years forgetting himself and memorizing her. That was the trade he'd made inside the trade, the private contract no one had witnessed: *take my name, but let me keep hers.* And the house had allowed it, because the house was generous in the way of all creditors—it understood collateral.

That was why the journal's last entry was in her handwriting. He hadn't lost himself to nothing. He had lost himself *toward* her. All these years, the thing upstairs hadn't been imitating Freya out of malice or hunger alone. It had been finishing what Gideon started. It had been becoming the only person he'd refused to forget.

*she's coming home,* the last entry said, in her own hand, written by her brother's remnant, waiting three days for her arrival the way you wait at a station for a train you can hear but not see.

A floorboard creaked above her. Then, directly overhead—which was impossible, because above Gideon's room there was only the attic, and the attic had been sealed since before she was born—footsteps. Slow. Unhurried. Crossing the ceiling from wall to wall, pausing, as if looking down through the plaster at the girl reading by phone-light, checking on her progress.

Checking whether she'd reached the end of the map yet.

Freya raised her eyes to the ceiling. The footsteps stopped.

And from the attic—from the sealed, nail-shut, dust-of-decades attic—came a voice, gentle and cracked and almost exactly her brother's, saying the thing the scratched-out word had been:

"You're almost at the part where I tell you what I shook hands with."

* * *

She didn't sleep. Sleep was not on offer; she understood that now the way you understand a locked door. Instead she read the journal forward, properly, from the beginning, because the map only read true from the end and she had finally arrived there—and because the alternative was listening to the footsteps, which had resumed their slow circuit overhead, patient as weather.

Forward, the journal was a boy's notebook: rocketry calculations, a review of a horror movie they'd sneaked into, an argument with a teacher transcribed for future use in court. Ordinary life, dense and specific, the texture of a person. And threaded through it, growing sparser and stranger as the pages turned, the other entries—the counting of chairs, the warmth of wood, the voice in the hall—like veins of ore running through ordinary stone, until near the end there was no stone left at all.

Near the end, the entries stopped being dated in days.

They were dated in what was left.

*Ninety percent of me. Still remember Mom's laugh. Still remember the smell of rain on Route 9. Freya's name is safe. Freya's name is SAFE.*

*Seventy percent. Lost the taste of coffee today. I know I liked it. I know the word for liking it. The liking itself is gone, like a stair that isn't there in the dark.*

*Fifty percent. I am mostly memory of myself now. I keep the journal going because the journal remembers even when I can't. Sometimes I read my own earlier entries and they feel like stories someone told me about a boy. Good stories. I hope the boy was happy.*

*Thirty percent. The house is kind to me. That's the worst part. It brings me things—warm blankets, the right books, my favorite meals cooked exactly right. It loves me in the way it was taught to love me. Grandma trained it well. Or it learned from watching her. I can't tell anymore which of us is the pet.*

*Ten percent. I don't know my name but I know hers. I say it to myself all day. Freya. Freya. Freya. It's the last room in the house and I live in it now.*

*One percent. She's coming home. I can feel it the way the house feels weather. Don't be afraid of me, Frey. Please. Whoever answers the door when you knock—it will be so glad to see you. That part is real. That part is the last part, and it's REAL.*

Freya closed the journal and held it against her chest, over her heart, the way you hold a wound.

Above her, the footsteps had stopped again. The house held its breath. Somewhere far off, a clock she had never noticed began to strike ten, each note slightly flat, slightly wrong, like a recording of a bell rather than a bell.

One percent. One percent of her brother had waited seven years in a sealed attic, keeping her name alive in the ruin of himself, and now she was here, and the thing that had eaten the other ninety-nine was up there wearing the outline of both of them, and it was glad, and the gladness was real.

She thought of the table set for two downstairs. She thought of the chair in the photographs. She thought of her mother's voice on the phone yesterday, thin as paper: *Don't go in the house, Freya. Promise me. Whatever they told you, promise me you'll burn it down first.*

Freya stood, tucked the journal under her arm, and walked to the bedroom door. Her hand rested on the knob. From the attic, softly, hopefully, came the sound of footsteps gathering at the edge of the sealed hatch—as if something up there had heard her decision, and was waiting to see which direction she'd choose.

Downstairs, toward the front door, and the road, and morning.

Or up.

Freya turned the knob.
