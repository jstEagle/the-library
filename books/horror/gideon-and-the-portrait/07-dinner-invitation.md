## 7. Dinner Invitation

The kitchen had been waiting for me.

That was the thought that arrived first, before fear, before sense—the plain animal certainty that the room knew I was coming. The overhead light was on. I had not turned it on. I had walked through this house for hours with the gray afternoon dying at the windows behind me, and I had not touched a single switch, and yet the bulb above the table burned with the steady amber warmth of a light that had been on for years, that had been left on *for someone*, the way you leave a porch light burning when you know your people are driving home late.

And beneath it, the table was set.

Two places. Two of grandmother's good plates, the white ones with the blue rim she only brought out when the bishop came or when my grandfather's memory needed honoring. Two forks, two knives, two spoons laid in their proper order—she'd have rapped my knuckles with a butter knife for getting that wrong, fork outside in, blades facing the plate. Two water glasses catching the lamplight. A small vase between them holding three stems of dried lavender, the kind that used to grow along the south fence until it didn't anymore.

Two chairs, pushed in neatly, as if the diners had simply stepped away to wash their hands and would return any moment, any year, any decade now.

I stood in the doorway with the journal pressed against my chest like a shield, which was ridiculous, because paper stops nothing, but my arms had decided on their own what to do with the thing and I let them. My legs had gone strange. Not weak exactly—*hollow*, as if the marrow had been quietly removed while I read upstairs and replaced with something lighter, something that hummed.

The last entry. *She's coming home.*

I looked at the two place settings and understood, with a clarity that felt less like thinking than like falling, who they were for.

"No," I said aloud, because saying things aloud makes them smaller. It's a trick I learned as a child, naming the monster under the bed so it would be a word instead of a dark. "No, absolutely not."

The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe ticked as it cooled, the ordinary percussion of an ordinary house. If I hadn't spent the whole day learning how wrong this house could look while behaving perfectly normally, I might almost have believed it.

I should have left. Let me be honest about that, here in the privacy of my own skull where no one can hear me lie: every instinct I owned was screaming the same simple instruction. *Car. Keys. Road. Never.* I am not brave. I want that on the record somewhere, in some ledger of my own—I have never been brave, I am a woman who writes about courage for a living precisely because I have to invent it, manufacture it from nothing, and then live inside the invention for a year at a time because I cannot bear the person I actually am.

But I was also so tired. That's the part no one tells you about terror—it is exhausting, it burns fuel like a furnace, and by seven o'clock of your first day in a haunted house you are running on fumes and grief and the cold dregs of gas-station coffee. My body had been clenched since the funeral home, since the wrong hands folded on the wrong chest, since I signed my name—my real name, the one thing I still owned—to a stranger's death certificate. Seven hours of sustained dread does something to you. It grinds you smooth. And underneath the fear, rising up through it like water through limestone, was something hotter.

Anger.

This was *my grandmother's kitchen*. These were *my childhood dinners*. That chair on the left, the one with the wobble she never fixed because Grandpa always sat there and Grandpa liked the wobble—that chair had held me through burnt casseroles and birthday cakes and the long slow silence after Gideon vanished, when I sat in it night after night eating food I couldn't taste while my mother cried into her hands and everyone waited for me to say something true about where my brother went.

Someone had set this table. Someone had been setting it, by the look of the lavender, for a very long time.

"Who did this?" My voice came out cracked and too loud in the warm light. "Who set this table?"

The house said nothing, in the way houses do—but its silence had texture now, had weight, had the quality of held breath. I knew—I knew the way you know things in dreams—that if I stood there long enough, the silence would answer.

Upstairs, a floorboard creaked.

Not the settling kind of creak. Not the thermal groan of old wood contracting in the evening chill. This was the creak of weight, placed deliberately, the way a careful person walks in a house where someone else is sleeping. It came from the hallway above the kitchen—from the stretch between my old room and Gideon's—and it moved. One step. A pause, long enough for me to count my own heartbeats against it. Another step. Moving toward the top of the stairs.

Toward me.

Every hair on my body lifted. My mouth flooded with that pre-vomit sweetness, and my vision narrowed to a bright trembling tunnel with the staircase door at the end of it, and I stood absolutely still in the manner of prey animals everywhere, doing the ancient stupid math: *if I don't move, maybe it can't see me.*

Another step. Closer. The third stair from the top—the one that sang, the one Gideon and I used to jump over in the dark during our dare games—sang its long cracked note into the quiet.

It was coming down.

And here is the thing I will never fully explain, not to Marisol, not to any therapist, not to the page itself: instead of running, I got angry. It rose in me all at once, total and clarifying, like a struck match in a black room. Seven years. Seven years of not knowing, of my mother's face going carefully blank whenever his name came up, of packing and repacking his bedroom into boxes and unpacking them again because putting them in storage felt like burying him alive. Seven years of half my own face missing from every mirror, because that's what a twin is—the other half of your reflection—and today I had stood over a corpse with a stranger's hands and been asked to grieve a lie.

I was done being afraid in this house. Or rather—I was still afraid, completely, down to the wet animal core of me—but the anger had gotten there first and taken the best seat, and fear had to stand.

I pulled out the chair on the right. My chair. The legs shrieked against the linoleum, a sound so violently ordinary that I nearly laughed. I sat down. I picked up the water glass—cool, full, beaded with condensation as if poured minutes ago—and I drank from it, because I was thirsty, because defiance is mostly made of small stubborn physical acts, and because if whatever came down those stairs wanted to poison me it had gone to remarkably elaborate lengths.

Then I put both hands flat on the table, looked at the empty place across from me—at the plate, the silverware, the lavender, the chair pushed in neat as a held breath—and I said, out loud, in the steadiest voice I could find:

"I'm not leaving."

The words dropped into the room like stones into a well. I heard them land somewhere far below the floorboards, below the cellar, below anything with a bottom.

"I know you're there." My heart was slamming so hard I could feel it in my teeth, in my wrists, in the bruise the journal's spine had left against my sternum. "I know you've been here. Seven years. Maybe longer. Maybe my whole life." The floorboards overhead were silent now, and somehow that was worse than the footsteps. "You set this table. You washed these dishes. You kept his room. You—" My voice caught. I pushed through it. "You wrote in his journal. In *my* handwriting. So let's stop pretending we're strangers."

The lights flickered.

Once—a stutter, the whole kitchen dropping into darkness for a single frame, long enough for my hindbrain to shriek and my hand to jerk toward the table's edge. Then again, longer, the amber bulb browning to ember, the refrigerator's hum faltering, and in that brown half-dark the two place settings seemed suddenly arranged around a third presence, an absence sitting in the empty chair with its hands folded, patient as arithmetic.

*I'm not leaving,* I said again, inside my head this time, gripping the edge of the table hard enough to whiten my knuckles. *Whatever you are. Whatever he was to you. I'm not leaving.*

The lights steadied.

They didn't surge back—they *settled*, the way a spooked animal settles, the filament warming from ember back to gold, and the refrigerator resumed its hum mid-note as if it had never stopped, and the kitchen was bright and warm and ordinary again except that everything in it now felt like it was looking at me. The light stayed on. That was the detail I fixated on, kneeling there in my chair with my pulse hammering, unable to look away from the bulb: it had flickered, and it had come back, and it *stayed*.

As if my answer had been received. As if it had been—accepted.

"Okay," I whispered. My breath fogged faintly in air that had gone cool, though the stove wasn't near and the window was shut. "Okay. Then we're having dinner."

I don't know why I said that. I don't know why I reached across the table and straightened the fork at the empty place setting, aligning it with a hostess's fussiness, except that my hands needed something to do and my mouth needed to keep talking because silence had become a substance I could drown in. "I haven't eaten since yesterday. So. We're having dinner, you and I, and you're going to sit there, and I'm going to sit here, and neither of us is going to do anything horrible. Agreed?"

The lavender stirred in its vase. There was no draft. The window was shut, the door was shut, the house was sealed tighter than a jar, and the three dried stems bent gently toward the empty chair and back, like a nod.

My skin tried to crawl off my body. I made myself smile instead—thin, shaking, more bared teeth than warmth—and pulled the pot of coffee I'd abandoned that afternoon off the counter. Cold. I poured it anyway, into both cups I found hanging on the rack, grandmother's cups, the ones with the faded roses, and set one at each place. Steam should have risen from mine; none did. From the cup at the empty setting, thin and impossible, a curl of vapor rose anyway, delicate as a question mark.

"Right," I said. "Good talk."

I drank the cold coffee. It tasted like pennies and exhaustion. Across from me, the chair remained empty, the cup steamed, the light burned steady and gold, and the house held its breath around us like a cupped pair of hands.

We sat like that for—I don't know. Minutes. An hour. Time had gone soft at the edges, stretching and pooling. I talked, because I couldn't stop, because the alternative was listening. I told the empty chair about the funeral home, about the wrong hands, about signing the papers. I told it about the photographs, the extra chair in pictures from 1974, from 1989, from my christening, the chair that had no business being there and had been there all along, waiting in silver gelatin and faded Kodacolor for someone to finally notice. I told it I wasn't stupid. I told it I knew it wasn't Gideon—not entirely, not anymore, not if it ever had been—and I watched the steam from its cup bend slightly, attentively, at each sentence, and I hated myself for finding that companionable.

Somewhere in the middle of it, my anger guttered and something sadder moved in underneath. Because the terrible thing, the thing I'll admit only here, was that it was nice. Sitting at that table. The light was warm. The chair fit me the way it always had. Some deep-buried animal part of me, the part that had been orphaned twice—once by death, once by disappearance—was *relieved*, was purring like a cat returned to a familiar lap, and I could feel that part of me wanting to stay, wanting to be kept, wanting to be fed and remembered and set a place for, forever, in this warm gold kitchen where someone had waited seven years for me to come home.

That's how it gets you, I thought. Not with claws. With dinner.

I set my cup down harder than necessary. "What did my grandmother promise you?"

The steam stopped.

All of it, both cups, mid-curl, cut off as cleanly as a held note. The light dimmed—not flickering this time, just lowering, the way a person lowers their voice—and the temperature slid another degree, and somewhere below the kitchen, below the floor, below the cellar stairs I hadn't yet found the nerve to descend, something shifted. A sound like a heavy book closing. Like a ledger being opened.

Because there was a ledger. I didn't know it yet, not consciously—but I felt it then, the way you feel weather coming in a healed bone. Grandmother had been a woman of accounts. She recorded births and deaths in the family Bible in her copperplate hand; she kept grocery lists going back decades, filed by year; she once showed me, laughing, a notebook where she'd tracked every egg the hens laid from 1968 to 1980. A woman like that doesn't make a bargain without writing it down. A woman like that keeps receipts.

The light came back up. The steam resumed, curling politely from the empty cup. Whatever lived in this house had heard my question, and had declined to answer it in words—

—but it had told me where to look.

I stood up. My knees cracked like kindling. At the empty place setting, the lavender bowed once, low, toward the floor. Toward the cellar.

"Dinner's over," I said, and my voice only shook a little. "Show me the books."

* * *

The cellar door was behind the pantry, where it had always been, but I had never seen it locked before, and it was locked now—no padlock, no keyhole I could find, just a door that had simply decided to be closed, that resisted my pull with the calm immovability of stone. I braced my foot against the frame and hauled, and the door gave all at once with a sigh of cold air that smelled of earth and paper and, faintly, impossibly, of lavender.

A single bulb burned at the bottom of stairs I hadn't known were lit.

On the workbench beneath it, centered with terrible precision, lay a book bound in green leather, its corners worn soft, its cover embossed with a family crest I'd only ever seen on grandmother's good stationery. Beside it, a pen. Beside the pen, a space cleared, exactly the width of a page, as if the bench had been tidied in anticipation of a reader.

I took the stairs down one at a time, my hand on the cold stone wall, telling myself the whole way that I was choosing this, that I was walking toward answers and not into a trap, knowing even as I said it that the distinction had probably stopped mattering the moment I sat down at that table and refused to leave.

The ledger fell open to the last written page on its own.

Seven years ago. Two names.

**Gideon Vane** — paid in full.

And beneath it, a second line, ruled and waiting, blank as an open mouth.

The pen rolled a quarter inch toward me across the workbench, all by itself, and stopped.

*Your turn,* said the silence in the cellar, courteous as a host. *Whenever you're ready.*
