## 12. Phone Call

The cellar light was still burning behind her when Freya climbed back into the kitchen, and she left it on. She didn't want to think about what it cost to keep a bulb lit in that house, or who paid the bill, or in what currency. She wanted her mother's voice. She wanted something that existed outside these walls, outside this town, outside the ledger with its patient columns of teeth.

The kitchen phone was a landline, cream-colored, its cord coiled like something asleep. Of course there was no cell signal here; she'd known that since the driveway, since the little bar-less icon at the top of her screen had seemed less like a technical limitation and more like a house rule. But the landline had a dial tone. Warm. Steady. A sound like a held breath, waiting to be spent.

She dialed from memory. Her mother hadn't changed her number in thirty years, not since the divorce, not since the move, not since anything. Ines Vane was a woman who believed that changing your number was an invitation for the world to lose track of you, and Freya had always read it as a kind of fear dressed up as stubbornness. Now, standing in her grandmother's kitchen with the ledger's contents pressed flat against the inside of her eyelids—*1962, one name, four teeth, paid in full*—she understood that her mother's stubbornness had a different architecture entirely. Ines Vane did not want to be findable. There was a difference between hiding and being hidden, and Freya was only now learning which one her mother had chosen.

It rang four times. On the fifth, her mother picked up without saying hello.

"I saw the area code," Ines said. Her voice was flat, careful, the voice of a woman setting down something fragile before she turned around. "I told you not to go back there, Freya."

"You told me a lot of things." Freya leaned against the counter. The laminate was cold through her shirt. "You told me Gideon ran away. You told me Grandma died in her sleep. You told me the house was just a house."

A silence on the line. Not the empty kind. The kind that has weight, that has been sitting somewhere for years waiting for someone to call and collect it. Freya could hear her mother breathing, could hear the particular rhythm of it—in through the nose, slow, controlled—the breathing of someone who has practiced staying calm in exactly this kind of moment.

"Freya." Her mother said her name the way you'd set a glass down on ice. "Where are you in the house?"

The question landed wrong. Not *why are you there*, not *have you found anything*, not even *are you safe*. *Where are you in the house.* As if the answer mattered. As if the house had rooms the way a body had organs, and some of them you did not want to be standing inside of.

"The kitchen," Freya said.

Another breath. "Okay. Okay. Kitchen's fine. Kitchen was always—" Her mother stopped herself. Freya heard her swallow. "Is the phone on the wall by the pantry? The cream one?"

"Yes."

"Don't unplug it. Whatever you do, don't unplug it while you're talking. And Freya—" the breathing hitched, just once, "—don't look at the window over the sink while we talk. Not until I tell you it's time."

Freya's eyes went to the window over the sink.

It was full dark outside now, and the glass had gone the deep black of a mirror, holding the kitchen in it—the hanging lamp, the counter, her own pale face floating there like something drowned. She looked away immediately, heart tapping at the base of her throat. *Not until I tell you it's time.* Time for what? Time for what to be there?

"Mom. What did you just—"

"I need you to listen to me." And now the flatness cracked, and underneath it Freya heard something that frightened her more than the ledger had: her mother was terrified, and had been terrified for so long that the terror had gone smooth, worn down like a river stone, something she carried in her pocket and touched without thinking. "I need you to listen, and I need you not to interrupt, because if I stop I won't start again. Do you understand? I have been not-starting-again for seven years. I can't do it twice."

Freya said nothing. Outside, wind moved through the hedges along the side of the house, and the sound it made was almost like whispering, almost like a crowd of people all saying the same word over and over, and she made herself not try to hear what the word was.

"Your grandmother didn't die in her sleep," Ines said. "I mean, she did, eventually, that night, the way the death certificate says. But that's not what I meant when I told you she died. I told you she died in her sleep because it was easier than telling you the truth, and the truth is that your grandmother died a long time before her heart stopped. She died in pieces. She sold herself off a piece at a time, and what was left at the end—" a breath, "—what was left at the end was mostly not her."

"Mom, I found the ledger."

The silence this time was different. Sharper. Freya could practically hear her mother go still in her apartment four hundred miles away, could see her—small, gray-streaked, hands pressed flat against the kitchen table there, the table with the wobbly leg she'd never fixed because fixing things meant committing to staying.

"You found the ledger," Ines repeated.

"The payments. The teeth. The names." Freya's voice came out steadier than she felt. "1962. It starts in 1962."

"It starts before that." Her mother's voice had dropped to almost nothing. "The ledger starts in 1962 because that's when your grandmother started writing it down. The arrangement started the winter before. She told me once—once, when I was seventeen and she thought I was asleep, or she thought I'd forget, or she thought it didn't matter because I was leaving in the spring and girls who leave don't come back—she told me that your grandfather died in February. And that in March, she was so lonely she did a stupid thing. She set a place for him at the table. She lit a candle. She said his name three times and she said *you are welcome here*."

The wind outside pressed against the glass. Freya kept her eyes on the pantry door, on the cream phone, on the coiled cord.

"And something answered," Freya said.

"Something answered." Her mother laughed, and it was the worst sound Freya had ever heard her make, a laugh with no floor under it. "Something answered. That's the whole of it, isn't it? That's the entire horror of it, that it's so small. She was lonely. She left a door open. Something walked through it that was not her husband, wearing whatever it could find of her husband, and by the time she understood what she'd done, it had already—"

Her mother stopped. Freya waited. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs, in the house above her, a floorboard settled with a sound like a sigh.

"It had already eaten a year of her," Ines said finally. "That's how she explained it. Not years. A year. Singular. It ate 1963, all of it, and when she got herself back she found the ledger on her kitchen table with an entry in her own handwriting that she didn't remember writing. *Paid: one year, received: kept.* And she understood that if she didn't pay again, it would take the whole rest of her. All at once. Everything. And she was a practical woman, Freya. She was a woman who canned her own tomatoes and hemmed her own curtains and buried two children before she was forty. She was not a woman who could fight something she couldn't see. So she paid. Every year. A piece of herself, and when she ran low on herself—"

"The teeth," Freya said. "The names. There are names in the ledger, Mom. Names I don't recognize. Neighbors, maybe. People from town."

"People from town," her mother agreed, in a voice like ash. "People who were old and alone. People who were dying anyway and didn't have family to notice. She found them. She'd invite them for Sunday dinner and they'd sign the ledger and they'd live another year, comfortable, cared for, and the thing in the house would have its meal and leave everyone else alone. She told herself it was mercy. She told herself that right up until the year she ran out of strangers."

Freya closed her eyes. The cellar ledger had floated up behind them, the columns, the careful hand, the way the entries got shorter as the decades went on, as if the writer were running out of something to write with. "And then there was just us," Freya said. "Me and Gideon."

"And me." Her mother's voice had gone very quiet. "And me, Freya. You keep saying *us* like I wasn't there. I was there. I was there the year she came to me with the ledger open and a pen in her hand, and I was there when she told me what she needed."

Freya's stomach dropped, slow and cold, like an elevator with its cables cut. "What did she need?"

"She needed a name." The words came out of her mother one at a time, each one handled like something hot. "Not hers—she was nearly paid out, she didn't have enough left of herself to cover the interest, that's what she said, *interest*, like it was a bank. She needed a young name. A strong name. A name with years still in it. And there were only two in the house that qualified."

The kitchen was very still. The lamp above the table buzzed at the edge of hearing, a sound Freya had grown up under and never once noticed until now, when it seemed suddenly like the sound of something chewing.

"She made you choose," Freya said. Her own voice sounded far away to her, like it was coming through the window glass from outside. "Between me and Gideon."

"I said no." Her mother's voice cracked clean through. "I want you to know that first. I want you to know that I stood in her kitchen with that ledger in front of me and I said *no, absolutely not, you will not touch my children*, and she said, *then it takes all three of us, Ines, it takes you and the babies and everything, and it will not be quick*—and I said no again. I said no for six days. I counted them. Six days I said no."

"And on the seventh day," Freya said. Her mouth had gone dry as paper.

"On the seventh day your grandmother sat me down and she showed me something. She showed me the ledger, and she showed me the page, and she showed me that the blank space—the space waiting for the next name—it wasn't blank. Not really. She said if you looked at it long enough, in the right light, you could see what was already written there, faint, like a watermark. And Freya, I looked. God help me, I looked. And I saw a name in that space. I saw one of your names. And I couldn't tell which one."

Freya's hand had gone white around the phone. "So you picked."

"So I picked." Ines Vane was crying now, silently, the way she'd cried at funerals, the way she'd cried at the graveside seven years ago with one hand pressed over her mouth, and Freya had thought then that it was grief for a runaway son, and it had been that, but it had been so much more. "She put both names on a piece of paper and she folded them and she put them under the candle—the same candle, Freya, the one from the invitation, it was still in the drawer by the stove, I can still see the drawer—and she told me to draw one. And I stood there and I thought, *I know my children. I know which one of them can survive this. I know which one of them is strong enough to lose something and keep walking.* And so I didn't draw blind. I looked at the folds. I looked at the creases. I chose."

The lamp buzzed. The refrigerator hummed. In the black window over the sink, in the corner of her vision where she was absolutely not looking, something seemed to shift its weight.

"Mom." Freya's voice came out very small. "Which one did you choose?"

Her mother was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Freya checked the little LCD screen to see if the call had dropped. It hadn't. The timer kept climbing, patient as everything else in that house.

"I chose the name that wasn't yours," Ines whispered. "I chose to give it Gideon. I thought—God, I thought I was saving you. I thought I was picking the stronger one to lose. He was the brave one, Freya. He was the one who wasn't afraid of the dark. I thought the dark would have a harder time with him." A wet, terrible breath. "And then he vanished, and I let everyone believe he ran away, because the alternative was to say out loud what I'd done, and I couldn't. I couldn't. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Your brother has been in that house for seven years because I folded the paper wrong. Because I thought I was smarter than a candle."

Freya slid down the cabinets until she was sitting on the kitchen floor, the cord stretched taut, the receiver pressed so hard against her ear it ached. She thought of the journal upstairs, the handwriting drifting from Gideon's to her own, the last entry—*she's coming home*—and she thought of Gideon documenting his own dissolution, forgetting his own name in careful, shrinking letters, and she understood now that the handwriting hadn't drifted. It had been *transferred*. Every year. A piece of him traded away, and what filled in the gaps was her—her loops, her slants, her hand—because the thing in the house didn't invent what it took. It only rearranged. It was wearing her brother, and under her brother, in the hollow where a self used to be, it had packed the space with her.

"Mom," she said. "There's still a blank line in the ledger. The last entry. It has Gideon's name and then a space, and the space is waiting."

"I know." Her mother's voice had gone flat again, but it was the flatness of a woman standing at the edge of something. "That's you, Freya. That's always been you. The arrangement doesn't end when one name is used up. It ends when the family line is. Grandma's dead and I'm paid out—there's nothing left in me to take, I've felt it for years, like a tooth with the nerve gone. You're the last name in the book. It's been keeping him alive all this time—half of him, the half I left it—because it's *saving you*. That's how it works. It keeps one to harvest the other. It's been keeping your brother on the shelf like a jar of preserves so that when you came home, the meal would be fresh."

The word *fresh* went through Freya like a nail.

"Listen to me." Her mother's voice changed completely, all the confession burned off it now, all the grief set aside, and what was left was urgent and hard and shaking. "You need to leave. Tonight. Leave the phone on the hook, leave the door open behind you, don't pack anything, don't take anything with your name on it—do you hear me? Nothing with your name on it. Get in the car and drive and don't stop until the sun's up, and then keep driving, and never—"

"Mom."

"—never come back, never call this number again, I'll change it, I'll finally change it, and you'll be free, it only has what you give it, it can only keep what's—"

"Mom. I found his journal." Freya's voice cracked on *journal*. "He's still in there. Whatever's left of him. He's been writing to me for seven years. He knew I'd come. And I'm not—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I spent seven years thinking my brother walked out on me. I'm not walking out on him. Not again. Not when I finally know the truth."

The silence on the line stretched so long that the house's sounds rose up to fill it: the lamp's buzz, the refrigerator's hum, the wind working at the eaves, and underneath it all, so faint she might have imagined it, a rhythm from the floor above. Footsteps. Slow. Unhurried. The pace of someone who has all night and knows it.

"Freya." Her mother's whisper was barely a voice at all. "You can't save him. Whatever is wearing him, whatever's left—I looked into that blank space in the ledger and I saw what's at the bottom of that house, and there is no saving anything that it has held for seven years. There's only what it lets go and what it keeps." A breath. "Burn it."

"Mom—"

"Burn the house. Burn it with the ledger in it, burn it with the journal, burn it with everything, because fire is the only thing she ever said it feared—she wrote that in the margin of her Bible, I found it after the funeral, *fire and true names*, and I didn't understand it then but I understand it now. Burn it down, Freya. Burn my mother's sin down to the foundation and salt the ground and never—"

Freya looked up.

She hadn't meant to. Her mother was still talking, the words coming faster now, fraying, and Freya's eyes had simply drifted—exhaustion, grief, the body's treachery—and found the window over the sink. The black mirror of it. And in the black mirror, floating above the ghost of the hanging lamp, was the upstairs hall light. Reflected from the ceiling above her, from the hallway fixture at the top of the stairs.

It was on.

She hadn't turned it on. She was certain—she would swear it on anything, on Gideon's grave that didn't exist, on her own name—that she had left the upstairs dark.

And moving across the reflected ceiling, slow and patient, casting a soft and wobbling shadow, was the silhouette of a figure walking the length of the upstairs hall. It stopped, directly above her, where her mother's voice was still saying *burn it, burn it, burn it*, and the shadow of it settled, like someone sitting down on the floor to listen.

"Mom," Freya said. Her voice did not shake, and she would remember that later, would hold onto it like a rail in the dark. "I have to go. Someone's waiting for me."

"Freya—"

"I'm going to fix what you broke, Mom." She said it without cruelty, and that was the strange thing; she felt no cruelty in it at all, only a clarity like cold water. "You chose wrong. I get to choose right. That's all that's left to do."

She hung up the receiver before her mother could answer, and the dial tone hummed in the kitchen like a held breath finally released, and above her, through the floor, through the old bones of the house, she heard the footsteps start again—crossing the hall, unhurried, toward the top of the stairs.

Toward her.

Freya stood up, took the ledger from where she'd left it on the table, and looked at the blank line one more time. In the lamplight, faint as a watermark, she could almost see something already written there.

She picked up the phone's cordless twin from the counter—the one her grandmother had bought in the nineties and never liked—and carried it with her as she walked toward the stairs, because if she was going to climb up and face what was left of her brother, she wanted her mother's voice within reach.

Halfway to the stairwell, the cordless phone in her hand rang.

The display said: **GIDEON**.
