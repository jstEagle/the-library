## 13. Burning Question

The cellar smelled of cold earth and old paper, and Freya stood at the bottom of the stairs with the ledger in both hands and a box of long matches in her coat pocket, and she thought: *this is how it ends. Not with running. With fire.*

She had found the lock on the cellar door an hour after the knocking stopped—midnight exactly, as if some patient visitor had checked its watch and gone home. The padlock was brass, green with age, and it had opened at her touch without complaint, which should have frightened her more than resistance would have. The house was not keeping her out. The house had simply been waiting for her to come down.

Now she stood among shelves of preserves gone dark as amber, jars of plums and quinces her grandmother had put up before Freya was born, their labels faded to ghosts of handwriting. A workbench. A trunk. And on a small table beneath the single bare bulb, open as though someone had been reading it only moments ago, the ledger.

It was not an impressive book. That was the first obscenity of it. She had expected something bound in leather tooled with sigils, pages that whispered, ink that moved. Instead it was a school exercise book, the kind sold in packs of ten at the stationer's in town, its cover soft with handling, its corners rounded like river stones. Someone had written *Accounts* on the front in pencil, in her grandmother's careful hand, the letters leaning slightly rightward the way they always had.

Freya set it on the workbench under the light and opened it to the beginning.

*1962,* said the first entry, in ink gone brown. *Paid in full. One year of warmth. Received: three teeth, freely given.*

Below that, another year. Another payment. She read them the way you'd read a bank statement, because that was what they were—a statement of account, kept faithfully for sixty years. Some years the currency was teeth. Some years it was hair, coiled and pressed flat between the pages like flowers from a funeral. Once, in 1974, a name she didn't recognize, struck through in red when the year turned. In 1989, the year Grandpa Aldous died, there was no entry at all—only a pressed sprig of rosemary and a note in her grandmother's hand: *He is home. No payment due.*

And then, seven years ago, the last complete entry:

*Received: Gideon Vane. Whole. Willing by proxy. Paid: his name, his face, his place at the table. Balance outstanding: one vacancy, to be filled.*

Beneath those words, a blank line. Ruled, patient, empty.

Freya's hands were shaking. Not with fear—or not only fear. With the particular rage of a person who has just discovered that her entire childhood was a transaction, that the woman who taught her to make plum jam and hummed to her during thunderstorms had been running a tab against her family's souls since before her mother was born.

*Willing by proxy.* Her mother had signed something. Her mother had chosen.

Freya took out the matches.

She told herself it would be simple. Paper burns; that was the whole nature of paper. Sixty years of bargains reduced to ash, the debt unrecorded, the contract void. Whatever held this house in its teeth would find itself holding nothing. She struck the first match against the box and the sulfur flared, honest and orange, the most ordinary light in the world.

She touched it to the corner of the ledger's first page.

The flame bent toward the paper like a curious animal, licked at the edge—and went out.

Freya frowned. Struck a second match. Held it until the heat stung her fingers, then pressed the burning wood directly against the page. The paper did not scorch. It did not even warm. When she pulled the match away, the corner where the flame had been was cool to the touch, and faintly damp, as though the ledger had been breathing on it.

"Come on," she whispered.

Third match. Fourth. She held two at once, cupping them together so the flames merged into one strong tongue, and pressed them into the heart of the book, into the middle of the 1962 entries, into the record of the very first payment. The flames stood up straight and burned beautifully and transferred nothing. Heat washed over her knuckles. The pages stayed white. It was like trying to burn water. It was like trying to burn a fact.

The matches guttered one after another, and in the last second of the fourth one's life, Freya saw the smoke do something impossible.

It rose from the dead matches in a thin gray ribbon—the only smoke the fire had produced—and instead of dispersing into the dark of the cellar, it gathered. It pooled upward, curling into itself, thickening, and drew a shape in the air above the workbench: four legs, a high back, arms at either side. A chair. A small wooden chair, drawn in smoke, hanging in the space between the bulb and the ceiling joists, and it stayed there, patient, while the last ember died and the darkness came back.

The smoke chair did not dissipate. It hung there in the blackness, faintly lighter than the dark around it, and Freya understood that she was being shown something. Not threatened. Shown. *This is what waits at your table. This is what you are feeding. You cannot burn an invitation. You can only take it back.*

"No," Freya said aloud, and her voice cracked on it. "No. You don't get to keep him."

She grabbed the ledger off the bench and ran for the stairs.

* * *

The house met her at the top.

Not with violence. That was the thing she would try, later, to explain to Marisol—that the house never once hurt her, never raised so much as a floorboard against her. It simply rearranged itself, gently, the way a parent rearranges a room to make it safe for a child who keeps trying to climb out the window.

The cellar door was gone.

Not locked. Not barred. Gone. Where the door had been there was now smooth plaster, painted the same cream as the hallway walls, with a small framed picture of a lighthouse hanging where the hinges used to be. Freya stood staring at it, the ledger clutched to her chest, and pressed her palm flat against the wall. Solid. Cool. Slightly warm, actually, like skin over muscle, like something alive and calm beneath the paint.

"You don't get to do this," she said. "You don't get to pretend."

The hallway light flickered once—an apology, or a shrug—and steadied.

She walked the length of the hall to the front door, meaning to leave, meaning to stand in the yard and breathe air that hadn't been breathed for her, and the front door opened onto morning.

Sunlight poured across the threshold. Real sunlight, gold and slanting, laying long shadows from the porch posts across the boards. Somewhere beyond it birds were singing, a whole dawn chorus of them, and the air that came through the doorway smelled like cut grass and warming stone. Freya stopped with one foot raised and looked at her watch.

12:47 a.m.

She stepped closer to the doorway without crossing it. Beyond the porch, the yard lay bright and dew-wet, and the maple tree threw its shadow east, the way it would at six in the morning, and the sky above the roofline was the pale washed blue of earliest day. Everything correct. Everything beautiful. Everything arranged with the loving precision of a stage set, and completely, catastrophically false.

"It's midnight," she told the house. "I know what time it is. I've been awake through every minute of it."

The sunlight did not waver. A bird sang directly overhead, sweet and insistent, and Freya thought, with a lurch, that she could not identify a single one of the calls—not the robin, not the thrush, none of them. They were bird-shaped sounds. Bird-flavored sounds. Sounds made by something that had heard birds described.

She slammed the front door shut. When she opened it again, the night was back—black yard, black sky, the neighbor's porch light a far orange star. The change cost the house nothing. It wasn't even a trick, she realized. It was hospitality. *You seemed tired. We thought you might want morning. Whenever you're ready.*

Freya leaned her forehead against the doorframe and laughed, a short ugly sound with no humor in it anywhere.

* * *

She tried the kitchen window next, because the kitchen window had a latch she'd been undoing since she was six years old, and because she needed to believe there was still one way out of this house that belonged to her and not to it. The latch slid. The sash rose. Cool night air moved against her face, real air, smelling of the river two fields over, and for one breath she let herself believe.

Then she looked down.

The kitchen sink was directly below the window. It had always been directly below the window—she remembered the drop, remembered her grandmother telling her never to climb out because she'd fall into the roses. But the roses were gone, and the ground was gone, and where the yard should have been there was a drop of perhaps thirty feet onto flagstones, clean and dry and lit by moonlight that fell from a sky in which the stars were arranged in patterns no astronomer had ever named. The house had not moved. The world outside the window had been redrawn.

Freya closed the window slowly, the way you close a door on a sleeping animal, and latched it, and stood with her back against the cabinets.

"That's cheating," she said quietly. "That's just cheating."

From upstairs, faintly, came the sound of a chair being drawn back from a table.

* * *

She sat in the parlor afterward with the ledger open on her knees, because if she couldn't destroy it, she would damn well read all of it, every year, every tooth, every name, and know the whole shape of the thing she was fighting. The lamp made its small yellow circle. Outside, the false night went on being night, obedient and total.

The early years were almost tender. That was what unsettled her most, turning those brown-inked pages. There were notes in the margins, little domestic annotations in her grandmother's hand: *It likes the radio left on. It does not care for mirrors. It asked, politely, whether we were happy, and seemed pleased when I said yes.* In 1968: *Taught it gin rummy. It cheats, but only a little, and looks guilty after.* In 1971, pressed between the pages, a child's drawing—her mother's, surely, age six or seven—a crayon house with a stick-figure family in the windows, and beside the family, drawn in a color that wasn't any color, a tall smudge with a smile.

Her grandmother had not summoned a demon. Her grandmother had *kept* something. Fed it, housed it, played cards with it. And the payments—the teeth, the names, the years—hadn't been extortion. Reading them in sequence, Freya began to understand they were something worse: rent. A tenant's agreement. Something had answered a door opened in grief, and instead of turning it away, the Vane women had given it a room and a seat and a standing arrangement, renewed annually, witnessed and signed.

*A bargain freely made,* the ledger said, in every entry, in sixty years of handwriting. *Freely made. Freely made. Freely made.*

That was why the fire wouldn't catch. You couldn't burn a signature. You couldn't torch consent. The contract wasn't in the paper—it was in the agreeing, and the agreeing lived in her grandmother's bones now, in her mother's, maybe in her own, woven into the family the way a name is woven into a child. Force was a language this thing didn't speak. It spoke invitation. It spoke welcome. It spoke *stay.*

Freya closed the ledger and held it against her chest and felt, for the first time since the funeral home, something other than dread.

Because she had read the final entry again. *Balance outstanding: one vacancy, to be filled.* And vacancies, she thought—vacancies were things that got filled. Debts were things that got paid. Accounts were things that got settled, closed, reconciled. A ledger was not a cage. A ledger was arithmetic, and arithmetic ran in both directions.

If a name could be taken and recorded—

Her hand drifted to the blank line, and rested there, on the empty ruled space that had waited seven years for someone to write on it.

Upstairs, the floorboards creaked. Once. Twice. The slow, deliberate tread of someone pacing near Gideon's door, wearing the rhythm of a walk she'd known her whole life.

Freya looked at the ceiling, and then down at the ledger, and then at the cold fireplace where her matches lay spent in their little white row, like candles at a vigil.

"All right," she said to the house, to the smoke, to the patient thing that had loved her family for sixty years and eaten her brother by increments. "You won't burn. Fine. But ledgers get amended."

She picked up the pencil that lay in the spine's groove—her grandmother's pencil, worn to a stub, kept handy for sixty years of annual renewals—and turned to the last page.

The blank line waited.

Above her, the footsteps stopped.
