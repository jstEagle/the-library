## 21. Two Coffins

The second funeral was smaller than the first, and truer.

The first had been held in the county chapel, all beige and fluorescent hum, with a pastor who had never met Gideon and a congregation of strangers who had come for the free sandwiches. Freya had sat in the front row with her hands folded like a good mourner and listened to a man describe her brother as *a quiet soul taken too soon*, and she had thought: *you have no idea how taken, or how soon, or how quiet.* She had signed the paperwork at the funeral home because some part of her—some deep, animal part that understood bargains better than she did then—knew that the body needed claiming. That an unclaimed body was an open door. That grief, even false grief, was a kind of invitation.

She had not known that yet, three weeks ago. She knew it now the way you know a scar: by touch, by weather, by the way it ached before rain.

* * *

The stranger's body had waited in the funeral home's cold drawer while everything else happened—the house, the ledger, the long night that ended at 5:47 in the morning with Marisol standing barefoot in the frost-silvered yard, shouting a dead boy's name into the dark until the windows rattled. The coroner had been patient about it, in the way of small-town officials who have learned not to ask questions they don't want answered. When Freya finally called to say she was ready, he asked only one question.

"You're sure it's him?"

Freya had looked out the kitchen window of the house on Corben Street—at the garden her grandmother had kept, at the fence Marisol had helped her mend, at the photograph hanging by the door—and said, "It's someone. And someone deserves to go home."

That was the truth of it, or as close to truth as language could carry. The body in the drawer had never been Gideon. It had never been anyone, really—not anymore, not since whatever had worn it last set it down like an empty coat. The dental records didn't match anything. The fingerprints came back to a man reported missing in 1998 from a town two states over, a drifter, no family, no one to file the paperwork of missing him. The universe had provided a hollow man to fill a hollow grave, and Freya found she could not hate it for that. The world was full of people nobody claimed. She had spent seven years being one of them, in a way—claimed by nothing, belonging nowhere, her name sitting in a ledger like a debt unpaid.

So she would claim this one. Not under his own name, which no one remembered, but under the only name she had to give.

Gideon Vane would have a grave. Someone would rest beneath the stone. The arithmetic of it was ugly, but it balanced, and Freya had learned—she had learned so much, that autumn—that balance mattered more than cleanliness.

* * *

Marisol drove her to the cemetery in the old blue pickup, because Freya's hands were not steady enough for the wheel and both of them knew it without saying. The morning was the kind that October saves for its best lies: gold light, thin mist burning off the fields, air that smelled like woodsmoke and wet leaves and the particular sweetness of apples going soft on the ground. A beautiful day. A day that had no business being beautiful, and was anyway.

"He'd have hated this," Marisol said, nodding at the small gathering ahead—Father Anselm from St. Brigid's, who had agreed to say words over a stranger without asking whose; the coroner, who had come off the clock; old Tom Pell, who had mowed the Vane lawn for thirty years and had apparently decided, in the way of old men, that he was family now whether anyone liked it or not. "A crowd."

"Gideon loved crowds," Freya said. "He just pretended he didn't. He'd stand at the edge of them doing that thing with his eyebrows, like he was grading everyone." Her voice caught on something—not a sob, just a snag, a thread pulling tight. "I haven't done the eyebrows in three weeks. I keep waiting to remember them right and I can't."

Marisol reached across the seat and put her hand over Freya's where it lay on her knee. Her palm was rough from the salt bags she'd hauled, from the garden she'd started unasked behind the house, from twenty years of work Freya was only beginning to learn the shape of. "You will," she said. "Or you won't, and you'll make up new ones. Either way he's not going anywhere. You made sure of that."

*What we remember wrong, we keep alive wrong,* their grandmother used to say, back when she was still a woman who baked bread and not a woman who kept ledgers. Freya was beginning to suspect the old lady had meant it as a warning.

* * *

The service was short. Father Anselm read the Twenty-Third Psalm because it was the one everyone knew, and then he said the committal words over the coffin—the plain pine one, because Freya had refused the mahogany display model with a vehemence that had startled the funeral director—and the gravediggers lowered it down, and the ropes hissed through their gloved hands, and that was that.

Except it wasn't. Because when the coffin settled at the bottom of the grave, Freya stepped forward before anyone could stop her, and knelt at the edge, and put her hand flat on the raw earth.

"I don't know your name," she said quietly. The others pretended not to hear, which was a kindness, or a cowardice, or both. "I don't know what you were called, or what you loved, or what you were afraid of. But you died alone and somebody wore your face into my family's story, and that's not fair to either of us. So here's what I can do. I can give you a stone with a name on it. I can bring flowers every year whether I feel like it or not. I can tell people you were loved, and after a while it'll be true, because I'll be the one saying it." Her throat worked. "You rested in our house longer than anyone should have to rest anywhere. I'm sorry we took so long getting you home."

She stood. Her knees were wet with grave-dirt and she did not brush them off.

Tom Pell cleared his throat and said, "Your grandmother would've wanted hymns," and began, off-key and unembarrassed, to sing *Abide With Me*, and the coroner joined in on the second line, and by the fourth even Marisol was humming, and the sound went up thin and cracked into the gold morning like smoke through a keyhole, and Freya stood at the graveside of a man whose name she would never know and let herself cry for the first time since the night the lights stayed on.

Not for the stranger. Or not only. For Gideon, who had no body to bury, who had gone into the ground of the house itself, into its beams and its plaster and its stubborn old heart, and who had squeezed her hand once—*once*, hard enough to mean it—before the light took the shape of him away. For the seven years. For the chair. For the child she had been who signed the paperwork without knowing why, and the woman she was now who understood exactly why and forgave her anyway.

Mourning, she was learning, was not a feeling. It was a labor. It had stages like a fire has stages—you feed it, you tend it, you wait for it to burn down to coals you can hold in your bare hands. This was the tending. This was the feeding. The crying was just the heat coming off it.

* * *

They went back to the house afterward. Of course they did. That was the strange grace note of the whole terrible season: the house on Corben Street had become, against every law of horror and common sense, a place you could come home to.

It had exhaled, that dawn. Everyone who'd been there said so, though none of them quite knew what they meant. The walls seemed to sit easier on their foundations. The rooms held warmth instead of holding their breath. The upstairs hallway, where the figure had waved with the wrong hand for seven years, was just a hallway now, sunlit and scuffed, needing paint. Even the cellar smelled like potatoes and earth instead of paper and teeth.

But there were changes, small ones, that Freya catalogued the way her grandmother had once catalogued debts. The kitchen clock ran four minutes fast and always would. The third stair creaked in a new key. And the table—the big oak table where the extra chair had waited through decades of photographs—stood in the dining room with only its proper four chairs around it, and somehow looked lonelier than it ever had with five.

Freya hung the photograph by the front door on her first week back.

It was the one from Christmas 1989, the oldest picture the extra chair appeared in—her grandmother young and sharp-faced at the head of the table, her grandfather beside her, a gap-toothed aunt mid-laugh, and there at the far end, pulled up to the oak as naturally as if it had always lived there, the empty chair. No one in the photo looked at it. No one ever had. That was the horror of it, she'd thought once: not that the chair was there, but that everyone had agreed not to see it.

Now she saw it every time she left the house and every time she came in. She'd framed it in plain black glass and hung it at eye level, and Marisol had watched her do it and asked, gently, "Do you want it facing out or facing in?"

"Out," Freya had said. "Toward the door. Let it watch me leave. Let it know I'm coming back on purpose."

Some reminders you hang to forget. Some you hang to remember *rightly*. The photograph was the latter. It said: *this happened. It happened here, to these people, and you survived it, and survival is a thing you do daily, not once.* Every morning she touched the frame on her way out, the way other people touch mezuzahs or horseshoes or the noses of painted dogs, and every evening she touched it again coming in, and the ritual was small and private and it held.

* * *

The stone went up in November, when the ground had begun to think about freezing.

GIDEON VANE
1979 – 2018
BELOVED BROTHER. KEPT AND KEEPING.
AT REST.

Marisol had argued for *beloved son* and Freya had argued for nothing at all, and in the end they'd split the difference the way families do, which is to say one of them won while pretending to lose. *Kept and keeping.* It was the truest sentence Freya knew how to carve. He had been kept—held, fed on, worn, loved in the way a house loves, which is to say possessively and without understanding. And he had kept—kept her name safe somewhere in the hollow of himself all those years, kept the hunger fed and the bargain honored, kept faith with a sister he couldn't remember well enough to miss but missed anyway. The words would confuse anyone who read them. Good. Let them wonder. The truth was not for passersby. The truth was for the ground.

She visited every Sunday. She brought apples sometimes, because Gideon had eaten apples the way other boys ate candy, core and all, and because the stranger under the stone deserved offerings too, and apples covered both accounts. She talked to him—to them, to whoever was listening, which might have been no one, which might have been everyone, which was the whole mystery of graves in a nutshell. She told him about the roof repair. About Marisol's tomatoes, absurdly late-season, still ripening on the sill. About the letter she was drafting to her mother and had not yet sent, because some letters take longer than others to become possible.

Her mother had called twice since the dawn. Both times Freya had let it ring. There was forgiveness in her somewhere—she could feel it, distant, like weather coming—but it hadn't arrived yet, and she was not going to open the door to it half-formed. Her mother had chosen, thirty-eight years ago, which twin to register, and the choosing had echoed forward through everything, and Freya would speak to her again when she could do it as a woman who owned her own name rather than as a daughter begging for one. The ledger was ash-adjacent now, dissolved, unreadable, its blank line finally filled and closed. But ledgers teach you things. One of them is: *never sign anything you don't understand.*

* * *

On the first Sunday of December, snow came early and soft, and Freya walked the cemetery path with her collar up and an apple in her pocket, and stopped at the stone, and stood a long time without speaking.

The snow filled the letters of the inscription slowly, *KEPT* and *KEEPING* whiting out word by word, and Freya found she didn't mind. The stone was for the living. The ground already knew.

"I'm going to tell you a secret," she said to the grave, breath fogging. "I almost didn't come back here. After the funeral home, that first day—I almost got in my car and drove until the gas ran out. Signed the papers and vanished. Let the house keep whatever it was keeping." She turned the apple over in her mittened hand. "And I think some version of me does that, in some other life. Drives forever. Ends up a photograph with a chair in it somewhere, waiting to be seen." She crouched, awkward in her coat, and pressed the apple into the snow at the stone's base. "But I stayed. And staying was the whole trick. Nobody tells you that. All the stories make it about running, or fighting, or knowing the magic word. It's not. It's about staying in the room with the thing that scares you until it stops being a thing and starts being a grief."

She stood. Brushed off her knees. The snow kept falling, indifferent and gentle, covering the whole cemetery like a tablecloth laid for a meal that would never be eaten, or maybe—one of those thoughts that arrived unbidden now, in her new post-haunted vocabulary—like a tablecloth laid for a meal that was finally, finally over.

"Rest," she told the stranger under her brother's name. "Both of you. I've got it from here."

* * *

She walked home through the falling snow, past Marisol's house with its lit kitchen window, past the fence they'd mended, up the walk to the house on Corben Street, and paused at the door as she always did, and touched the frame of the photograph hanging there.

In the black glass, her reflection overlapped the Christmas table of 1989: a woman with snow in her hair, standing where the empty chair stood, filling it, for one flickering instant, with herself.

Then she opened the door, and went inside, and hung up her coat, and began setting the table for supper—for two, tonight, though her hands moved toward the third plate out of habit, and she smiled, and put it back in the cupboard.

Some chairs stay empty. Some names get given away. And some houses, if you love them honestly enough, and grieve them thoroughly enough, and refuse—refuse with your whole stubborn heart—to leave, will finally, quietly, let you belong to them.

Outside, the snow fell on. Inside, the kettle began to sing.
