## 22. Open House

*Freya*

The house smelled of paint and cut pine when we carried the first boxes through the front door in October, and I stood in the hallway with a lamp cradled against my hip and cried for no reason anyone could have named.

Marisol didn't ask. She just set her box down on the floorboards—books, mostly, and a chipped blue mug she'd had since college—and put her hand flat between my shoulder blades the way you'd steady something that might tip. We stood there while the radiator ticked itself awake and the light came slantwise through the stairwell window, gold and unhurried, the way light is supposed to come into a house. The way it never quite managed to come into this one before.

"It's just paint fumes," I said finally.

"Sure," Marisol said. "Paint fumes." She picked her box back up. "Which room's mine?"

"The one at the end of the hall. The good light."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she'd learned to look at me over those weeks—the look that checked whether I was actually fine or only performing it—and whatever she found must have been true enough, because she nodded and went up the stairs two at a time like she'd always lived here, like the house had been waiting for her specifically, which maybe it had.

Because that was the thing nobody tells you about surviving something: the house doesn't care who survived it. But sometimes the house gets a vote anyway.

The restoration had taken most of the summer. We'd hired people from town—people who remembered my grandmother, who remembered the lights in the upstairs window, who remembered and said nothing, because this was Hollow Creek and silence was the local dialect—but by August even the contractors stopped crossing themselves when they came up the drive. The cellar got new joists and a dehumidifier and a lock that was just a lock. The back door, the one that had knocked politely at midnight all those years ago, we replaced entirely; Marisol insisted, and I let her have it, because everyone needs one unreasonable thing in a renovation and that was hers. The new door was painted a deep, stubborn red. She called it *the line*. I pretended not to know what she meant.

Gideon's room we left alone the longest. Six weeks, maybe seven. Then one morning in September I went up with a roll of trash bags and a thermos of coffee and I took down the curtains and washed the windows and sat on the bare mattress until noon, and when I came down Marisol asked if I wanted help carrying anything out, and I said no, and she said okay, and that was the whole conversation. Some rooms you have to empty yourself or not at all.

We kept his bed frame. It's in the attic now, wrapped in a sheet, along with the journal and the ledger and every photograph except one.

You know which one.

* * *

*Marisol*

People in town still talk about us. I hear it at the hardware store, at the diner where I've started eating breakfast because Freya sleeps late and someone has to bring home the bacon, literally, from Delgado's butcher counter. *The Vane girl and the neighbor woman*, they say, in tones that can't decide between disapproval and envy. *Living out at the old Aldous place.* As if the house were contagious. As if grief were.

Let them talk. I spent seven years not talking, and I know exactly what that costs.

I moved in officially on the last Saturday of October, though honestly I'd been half-living there since June, since the night I stood in the yard with salt burning a line through my fist and a school photograph of a boy I barely knew held up against the dark like a lantern, screaming his name into a house that screamed back. My apartment above the pharmacy never felt like mine again after that. It felt like a place where I had once chosen silence, and I couldn't afford the rent on that anymore.

So: boxes. Eleven of them, and a cat named Turnip who belonged to no one and appointed himself ours within a week of the funeral-that-was-honest. He walks the halls at night like a small gray inspector, tail up, checking the corners for anything that shouldn't be there. So far his report has been nothing. Nothing in the corners, nothing under the beds, nothing in the walls. Just house sounds. Just the furnace and the wind and, once, memorably, a raccoon in the chimney that scared us both half to death and made Freya laugh so hard she had to sit down on the kitchen floor.

I hadn't heard her laugh like that before. Not once, all those months. I sat down next to her on the cold tile and laughed too, until we were both wheezing, until Turnip fled in disgust, until the laughter turned into the other thing, the crying, which it does, which it will, which is allowed now. That's what I understand about this house that I didn't understand standing in my own yard with my heart going like a drum: the difference between a haunting and a home isn't the absence of ghosts. It's whether you're allowed to cry in them.

Here, we're allowed.

* * *

*Freya*

Winter came early that year, the first real snow arriving the week before Thanksgiving, fat slow flakes that stacked themselves on the fence posts and hushed the whole street. Marisol built a fire in the living room—the good fireplace, the one that draws properly now—and we ate takeout noodles out of the cartons with our feet on the coffee table, and I watched the flames do ordinary fire things, catch and gutter and warm, and thought about smoke shaped like a chair, and decided, deliberately, to think about something else instead. That's another thing survival teaches you: you don't get to choose what happened, but you get some say in what you rehearse.

"I signed the papers today," I told her. "On the house. It's ours. Well—" I corrected myself, because accuracy mattered in this house more than anywhere else on earth. "Mine. Ours, if you're staying."

Marisol looked at me over her noodles with an expression I can only describe as *affronted affection*. "Freya. I have a toothbrush in your bathroom. I have a *cat*. Where exactly did you think I was going?"

"I don't know. People leave."

"People leave," she agreed, and reached over and took my hand, and her grip was warm and slightly greasy and completely unromantic and the truest thing I'd felt in years. "Not this one. I stood in your yard in the dark and yelled at a haunted house, Vane. You don't walk away from an investment like that."

I laughed, and the fire popped, and outside the snow kept coming down like the sky was settling in for the long, kind work of covering everything over.

* * *

*Marisol*

Some nights I still check the upstairs window. Old habit. I'll be locking up, or letting Turnip in, and my eyes will go up to that square of glass on the second floor—the one I watched for seven years, the one where the figure used to stand and wave with the wrong hand—and I'll look, and there'll be nothing there but lamplight and, lately, the shadow of the bookshelf Freya put in Gideon's old room, because she turned it into a library, which I think is the bravest thing anyone has ever done with a haunted space. Fill it with other voices.

The wrong hand. I still dream about the wrong hand sometimes. In the dreams it waves, and I wake up with my heart slamming, and then I hear the real sounds of the real house—Freya's floorboards overhead, the furnace, Turnip's ridiculous galloping—and I remember: the hand was wrong because it wasn't a hand at all. It was a shape trying its best. It was a guest wearing a borrowed outline, waving at the only neighbor who ever bothered to look, for seven years, saying *I'm here, I'm here, please see me,* in the only language it had.

I didn't see it, not really, not until the end. But I looked. And looking turned out to be the whole job.

Some mornings I make breakfast and set the table without thinking, and I catch myself reaching for a third plate—not the extra chair, never the chair, we retired the chair—but a third plate, out of habit, out of some muscle memory of the household we became, and I stop, and I smile, and I put the plate back. Because three is a number we chose. Three is me and Freya and Turnip, who eats at the table like a small furry uncle and no one stops him.

And four—

Well. Four is a different story. Four is the story Freya tells better than I do.

* * *

*Freya*

On the last page of the ledger—the real last page, past all the years and names and teeth, past the blank space that I filled with my brother's name in ink that cost me blood and a hand that cramped for a week afterward—there is one more entry, written the morning after the sunrise, in my handwriting, because I am the keeper of records now. That's how inheritance works when you survive it: somebody has to hold the truth, and write it down straight, so the next person doesn't have to dig.

It says:

*Household of this address as of November: Freya Vane, who claims her own name. Marisol Reyes, who chose to stay. One cat, self-appointed.*

*One chair, kept empty on purpose.*

*Not for fear. For welcome.*

That's the whole entry. Grandmother's ledger recorded debts; mine records what we're owed, which is each other, which turns out to be the same accounting done right-side-up.

* * *

*Marisol*

Thanksgiving was the first holiday, and we did it wrong in every possible way and it was perfect. Freya burned the rolls. I made gravy with lumps you could skip stones across. Turnip got into the stuffing when we weren't looking and we served it anyway, on the grounds that he'd improved it. We pulled the table away from the wall so we could sit across from each other like actual humans, and the afternoon light came through the dining room windows all amber and forgiving, and halfway through the meal Freya went quiet, and I knew—I *knew*—what she was doing, because I'd caught her doing it before.

She was counting chairs.

There are four chairs at our table. Two we use. One is Turnip's, technically, though he prefers laps. And the fourth stands at the end nearest the window, polished, pushed in neatly, empty.

"What are you thinking?" I asked her, because with Freya you have to ask; she'll carry a thing alone for years if you let her, and I have personally witnessed what happens when the Vanes carry things alone.

She turned her wineglass slowly by the stem. "My grandmother set a table for a ghost," she said. "Out of hunger. Out of loneliness so big it opened a door." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and clear at the same time, which is a trick I've never learned. "I want to keep a chair too. But I want mine to mean the opposite thing."

"And what's the opposite thing?"

"That love doesn't have to be a trap." She said it simply, the way you say a thing you've paid for. "That you can leave a place open at the table and it's not a summons. It's just... room. Room for whoever shows up. Room for whoever we were. Room for whoever's coming."

Outside, the first snow of the season had started again, soft against the dark glass, and the fire ticked, and Turnip snored on the sideboard with criminal contentment, and I understood that we were doing it—that we were already doing it, had been doing it for months, this impossible arithmetic where an empty chair adds to a family instead of subtracting.

"Then keep it," I said. "Keep it forever. And when company comes, they'll wonder why there's a chair nobody sits in, and we'll tell them the truth, and they'll think we're strange."

"We are strange."

"Completely. Pass the terrible gravy."

* * *

*Freya*

Later—much later, after the dishes, after Marisol went up with the cat and the stairs announced her the way honest stairs do—I stood in the dining room alone with the lamp low and the snow coming down beyond the windows, and I set the table for three.

Out of habit, yes. But also out of choice, which is what habit becomes when you examine it and decide to keep it.

Three plates. Three forks. The good napkins, because why save the good napkins for a day that isn't guaranteed. And at the end of the table, the fourth chair, tucked in, gleaming faintly in the lamplight, holding nothing but its own patient shape.

I stood there a long time, one hand resting on the chairback, feeling the smooth wood under my palm the way you'd touch a headstone or a shoulder—both, at once, depending on the minute.

"Gideon," I said quietly. Not a summons. Not a prayer, exactly. Just a name, spoken in a warm room, by someone who owned it now—both names, his and mine, sorted at last, each one back where it belonged. "We're okay. Eat well, wherever you are. And visit anytime."

The fire settled in the other room. The snow whispered against the glass. Somewhere upstairs, Marisol's floorboard creaked, and Turnip answered it with his small thundering trot, and the house held all of it—the noise, the warmth, the ordinary miraculous clutter of the living—and gave none of it back.

I smiled. I turned off the lamp.

And as I climbed the stairs toward sleep, toward the household, toward the long ordinary years, I could have sworn the house exhaled behind me—soft, satisfied, the way a home does when everyone who belongs to it is finally inside.

The third plate waited on the table below, gleaming in the dark.

Not for fear.

For whoever comes next.
