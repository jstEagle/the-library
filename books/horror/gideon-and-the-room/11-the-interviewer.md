## 11. The Interviewer

The voice on the tapes had a name. I was sure of it the way you're sure of a face in a crowd — not because you've catalogued its features, but because your body recognizes it before your mind agrees to.

I'd been listening for three days straight. I'd stopped sleeping much, which felt appropriate; Gideon hadn't slept properly since he was nine, and if I was going to carry his half of things, insomnia seemed like a reasonable inheritance. I sat on his bed with the recorder balanced on my knee and played the interviews back to back to back, building a taxonomy of the man behind the microphone.

He never gave his name. Of course he didn't. Men who spend forty years interrogating a room full of hungry dark don't introduce themselves; they simply begin, mid-sentence, as though you've walked into a conversation that started before your grandparents were born.

But voices have fingerprints.

He said *hollow* with a long O — *HOLL-ow Creek* — the old way, the way the founding families would have said it before the town got embarrassed about itself. He pronounced *Villalobos* correctly, which almost nobody outside the family managed; most people landed on *villa-LOH-bos*, but he said *vee-ya-LOH-bos*, soft on the double-l, the way our grandmother had. And when Gideon's flattened voice answered him, the interviewer made a small sound — a click of the tongue, twice — whenever an answer pleased him. *Tck-tck.* Like a man approving a horse at auction.

I knew that click.

Everyone in Hollow Creek knew that click, if they'd ever been to a town meeting or a funeral or the pharmacy where Elias Thorn still counted out pills with hands that had never once trembled. Thorn. The name was on half the buildings downtown, carved into lintels and foundation stones like a brand on livestock. THORN HARDWARE. THORN & SONS, FUNERAL DIRECTORS — EST. 1891. The year the ledger began. The year the payments began.

I sat very still on my brother's bed while the tape hissed, and I thought: *of course*. Of course it was a founder. Who else would have the stamina? Who else would have the stake?

The final tape — the one that said my name — ended with thirty seconds of room tone after the interviewer signed off. Room tone is never empty. Play it loud enough and a house confesses. In those thirty seconds I could hear the recorder being set down on wood, footsteps crossing a floor, and then — faintly, from somewhere beyond the microphone's reach — a door closing. Not slamming. Closing with care. The way you close a door when what's behind it is asleep and you are very, very invested in it staying that way.

I rewound it four times. On the fourth pass I caught something else, buried under the hiss: humming. A few bars of a hymn, low and unhurried. "Abide With Me." My grandfather used to hum it while shaving. It was the kind of thing a man learns in church and never unlearns, and Elias Thorn, according to every obituary and anniversary notice in the *Hollow Creek Courier*'s yellowed pages, had been singing in the First Congregational choir since 1962.

I turned off the recorder. The house ticked around me, settling, counting.

"Well," I said aloud, to no one, to everyone. "Let's go meet the interviewer."

* * *

Thorn Pharmacy smelled like camphor and cardboard and the particular dustiness of institutions that have decided change is a rumor. The fluorescent lights buzzed at a frequency just shy of headache. Behind the counter, sorting pills into little amber cells with the patience of a monk, stood a tall man in his seventies with a long face, white hair combed with geometric precision, and eyes the color of dishwater — pale, unremarkable, and absolutely still. Eyes that didn't flick toward the door when the bell rang. Eyes that had already noted me coming two blocks away, I'd have bet money.

"Miss Villalobos." He didn't look up. "You'll want the aisle on the left. Cough syrup's on sale."

"I'm not here for cough syrup, Mr. Thorn."

"Tck-tck." There it was. The click, live and in person, aimed at me like a small dry kiss. "No," he agreed, setting down his tray at last. "You're not." He studied me the way I imagined he studied prescriptions — checking the dosage, confirming the patient. "You have your mother's jaw. You have your brother's habit of standing in doorways like you're deciding whether the room is worth entering." A pause. "It usually isn't."

"You've been to the house."

"I've been recording in the house for longer than you've been alive." He said it the way another man might say *I've fished this river all my life* — proprietary, unbothered, faintly bored by the novelty of being asked. "Your grandmother let me use the parlor. Your mother let me use the kitchen. Gideon let me use his bedroom, which was either trust or surrender, and with that boy it was always hard to tell the difference."

My hand found the counter's edge. "Those tapes. The questions you ask it. What are you doing?"

"Interviewing." He came around the counter, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who had never once hurried anywhere, and flipped the sign on the door to CLOSED at eleven-forty in the morning, which told me something about how much the town questioned Elias Thorn's schedule. "Sit down, Miss Villalobos. This will take longer than you want it to and shorter than it deserves."

There were two stools behind the counter, red vinyl cracked and mended with black tape. I took one. He took the other, folded his long hands in his lap, and looked at me with those still gray eyes, and I understood suddenly why the room answered him. It wasn't authority. It was that he had nothing left to be afraid of, and hunger respects nothing so much as a man without fear left to take.

"The founders bound something under your house in 1891," he began. "You know this already, or you wouldn't be sitting there looking at me like I'm a specimen. Mrs. Adeyemi has been feeding you archive paper. Good. Paper is honest in a way people aren't." He tilted his head. "What the paper won't tell you is that bindings need maintenance. A knot doesn't stay tied because it was tied well. It stays tied because someone checks it. Every week. For a hundred and thirty years, someone has sat in a chair beside that wall and talked to the thing under your house, asking it questions, making it answer, keeping its mind occupied the way you keep a dangerous dog's mind occupied — with work."

"The recordings aren't documentation," I said slowly. "They're maintenance."

"They're conversation. Documentation is a side effect." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I'd seen in Hollow Creek, worse than the funeral director's, because it was genuinely warm. "Gideon figured it out before anyone told him. That's why we chose him, in the end. Well. That and the twin thing. But mostly the attention. The room likes a mind that pays attention. It eats inattention, Miss Villalobos. It eats forgetting. Every question I ask it is a meal it can't digest — it has to hold the answer, hold the shape of being *asked*, and a thing that's holding can't be taking."

"And when you die?"

His smile didn't move but something behind it did, some tectonic shift far down. "Yes," he said. "That is the question, isn't it. I'm seventy-eight. My heart keeps a schedule I don't approve of. And the room knows it. It's gotten restless lately — you've noticed. Doors multiplying. Lights failing. Neighbors dreaming of your brother calling their names." He watched me flinch and catalogued the flinch without mercy. "It's testing the fence. It does that when it scents a transition. Which brings us to you."

"I'm not taking his place."

"No," Thorn agreed mildly. "You're going to do something much more interesting, and much stupider, and I say that with admiration. You're going to try to finish it. Completion. The voluntary witness. I read the ledger's last page sixty years ago, Miss Villalobos; I've had a lifetime to think about it." He leaned forward, and the fluorescents caught the topography of his face, every line a year of sitting in that chair. "Here's what your mother won't tell you and Adeyemi can't: completion isn't destruction. Done right, done *fully*, the debt is paid in full and the binding dissolves clean. No more payments. No more twins. No more room. Done wrong—" he spread his hands, "—and the room takes the witness, the subject, and whoever's standing closest to the wall, and it starts the whole ledger over with interest. Your mother tried once. Ask her sometime how her attempt went. Actually, don't. She'll lie, and you'll both feel bad."

The stool was cold under me. "You're offering to help."

"I'm offering to train you. Help implies charity, and I've never given anything away in my life. I sell things. Even now." He rose, went to the ancient safe behind the counter — the kind with a dial you could hear thinking — and came back with a slim black notebook, cloth-bound, its corners worn round. He set it on the counter between us but kept two fingers on it, the way a dealer keeps fingers on the cards. "Mnemonic rites. The founders' methods for holding a whole ledger in one skull without going mad. Palace-building. Chain-linking. The old Catholic memory theater stuff, plus some techniques the founders invented that the Church would have burned them for. Forty years of drills, refined on one subject." His eyes flicked toward the back of the store, toward, absurdly, the direction of my house, six blocks away. "And access. When the time comes, you'll need someone who can keep the room calm while you work. Someone it trusts. That's me. That's worth more than everything in this safe."

"What's the price?"

"There it is. Gideon haggled the same way. It's uncanny." He took his fingers off the notebook. "Three things. First: when this is done — however it's done — the recordings come to me. All of them. Every tape, every transcript. The archive of the room belongs to the founder's line. It's the only inheritance my family has that isn't shameful, and I intend to keep it."

"Fine."

"Second: you tell no one I trained you. Not Adeyemi, not your mother, especially not your mother. Odile and I have an arrangement of long standing, and your training falls outside it. If she knows, she'll interfere, and interference is how rituals fail."

"That's not a price, that's a secrecy clause."

"Everything's a price if it costs you something. Secrecy will cost you plenty." He held up a third finger. "And third." He paused, and for the first time the stillness in his face showed effort, like a man holding a door shut against weather. "Third: whatever the room offers you in there — and it will offer, it always offers — you bring the offer to me before you answer. Whatever bargain it puts on the table, whatever shape it wears, whatever name it says with your brother's mouth. You walk out, or you call through the wall, and you tell me exactly what was said. Before you decide."

"Why?" The word came out sharper than I intended. "So you can veto it? So you can take control of the negotiation? What do you get out of my refusal, Mr. Thorn?"

Something moved behind the dishwater eyes. Not guilt. Regret's distant cousin, maybe — the emotion of a man reading an old letter from himself.

"Because thirty years ago," he said quietly, "a woman sat in that chair where you're sitting, and I gave her the same three terms, and she accepted the first two and laughed at the third. She said no one would manage her choices. She said she'd know the right answer when she heard it." He looked at his own long hands. "She went in alone. She came out alone. And the room has been charging interest ever since — on her, on your family, on this whole drowsy town. I have spent thirty years paying down a debt that exists because I respected a frightened girl's independence." He met my eyes again, and the stillness in them finally resolved into what it had been all along: not calm. Exhaustion. The specific exhaustion of a lighthouse keeper. "I am not offering to manage your choices, Miss Villalobos. I'm offering to make sure that whatever you choose, you choose it with the whole board visible. That's mentorship. Manipulation is cheaper, and I'd have started with it if I thought you'd fall for it."

The fluorescents buzzed. Somewhere in the back, a refrigerator cycled on with a sound like a held breath released.

I picked up the notebook. It was heavier than it looked, the way true things usually are. "When do we start?"

"Tonight." He was already moving, pulling down bottles, resuming his sorting as though I were a customer who'd bought something and should now vacate the premises. "Your house, eight o'clock. Bring the tapes. Bring the ledger. Eat a real dinner first — memory work burns sugar, and fainting witnesses are useless." He glanced at me once, sidelong, and added, almost gently, "And Miss Villalobos. Tell your brother I'm coming. Through the wall, tonight, before I arrive. He'll want to warn you about me, and I'd rather he warned you accurately."

I stopped in the doorway, one hand on the frame — Gideon's habit, apparently, and I couldn't even be annoyed about it anymore.

"Warned how?"

Thorn clicked his tongue, twice, softly. "That I've been talking to the thing under his floor for forty years," he said, "and that somewhere in all that talk, I stopped doing it for the town." He turned back to his pills. "Eight o'clock. Don't be late. The room hates lateness. It counts."

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the creek, because I wanted the noise. The water in Hollow Creek doesn't babble so much as mutter — a low continuous complaint, like a resident who's been petitioning the town council for decades and has stopped expecting results. I held the notebook against my ribs and turned Thorn's terms over, looking for the trap.

There was a trap. There's always a trap; my mother raised me on that principle alongside table manners. But the strange thing, walking home in the failing light with the muttering water beside me, was that I couldn't decide whether the trap was for me — or whether Elias Thorn had spent forty years building a cage around himself, rung by careful rung, and was now handing me the keys along with the blueprint, hoping I'd build a better one before the thing under my house noticed the keeper had grown old.

At the corner of Maple, Mrs. Okonkwo was watering her mums at dusk and called out that I looked thin, and did I know her son drove a delivery route past the county seat and could bring real bread, and wasn't it a shame about poor Gideon, such a quiet boy, such a *helpful* boy —

— and then she paused, watering can tilted, and frowned at the middle distance like a woman reaching for a word on a high shelf.

"Helpful," she repeated, slower. "Wasn't he. I can't quite — honey, what was it your brother did, again? For the town, I mean. I have it in my head that he did something for all of us, and I can't for the life of me think what."

The lights along Maple Street flickered, all together, once — like a page turning.

"He listened," I said. "He was a good listener."

"Oh," said Mrs. Okonkwo, satisfied, and went back to her mums. "Yes. That sounds right."

I stood on the corner until my heart remembered its job. Then I went home, ate the eggs Odile had left in the fridge without asking whether they were mine, and set the notebook on the kitchen table next to the ledger, next to the tapes, next to the salt I'd started keeping in a cereal bowl like a snack for a guest I dreaded.

Through the wall, faint as breath, I heard Gideon's voice start to speak — and stop. Waiting.

"It's okay," I told the wall. "He told me to expect it. Tell me everything, Gid. Especially the parts about him."

A pause. Then, in my brother's cadence, flattened at the edges but unmistakably him:

*"He's the only one who ever asked it what it wanted instead of what it knew. Be careful of him. He's kind. That's the part Mom never forgave."*

I opened the notebook to the first page. At eight o'clock precisely, there was a knock at the front door — measured, patient, the knock of a man who had counted doors for decades and knew exactly which one he was knocking on.

I let him in.
