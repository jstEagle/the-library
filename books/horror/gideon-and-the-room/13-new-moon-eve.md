## 13. New Moon Eve

*Tamsin*

The moon was a fingernail paring over Hollow Creek, thin enough that you could see the dark part of it, the part that was always there and only showed itself when the lit part got out of the way. I stood at the kitchen window with a mug of tea I had no intention of drinking and watched it, because if you're going to spend the last night before you possibly stop existing, you might as well do it looking at something.

The house behind me looked like a siege kitchen. Salt in canisters, salt in bowls, salt in a line I'd poured along every threshold until the floors looked like they'd been dusted with frost. The ledger lay open on the table, weighted down at both ends with Gideon's old baseball trophies — he'd won exactly two, both for participation, and had displayed them with the irony of a man who knew what he was. Mrs. Adeyemi's handwriting marched down the pages in columns: names, dates, memories taken. A hundred and thirty-three years of bookkeeping for a debt nobody had ever intended to pay off.

"You've been standing there an hour," my mother said from the doorway.

"Forty minutes. I counted."

"Of course you did." Odile came into the kitchen wearing her coat, which meant she was going outside, which meant she was going to stand guard on the porch like she'd promised, which meant she was going to be terrified about it, visibly, all night, and refuse to come in. My mother's courage had always been the kind that made you want to look away.

"You don't have to do this," I said. "The salt holds better with the door shut."

"The salt holds nothing. The salt is theater." She picked up one of the canisters and turned it in her hands like she was checking its expiration date. "I know that. You know that. But when your grandmother did this night — and she did, once, before she decided running was easier — she said the salt isn't for the room. It's for the person inside. So they have a line they drew themselves."

"That's either very wise or very cruel."

"Welcome to being a Villalobos mother." She set the canister down and looked at me, and for a moment I saw her not as the woman who'd lied to me for a month but as a girl of nineteen who had run from a room and been paying interest ever since. "I could drive us out tonight. Both of us. Right now, before the moon sets. We could be in Ohio by morning."

"And leave Gideon."

"And keep you." Her voice cracked on *keep*, and I understood that in her private arithmetic those were the only two entries left. Everything else had already been spent.

"I'm not doing the thing you did, Mom."

"No," she said quietly. "You're doing something worse. You're doing it on purpose." She pulled me against her coat — wool and cold air and the ghost of some perfume she'd stopped wearing years ago but apparently kept for emergencies — and held on hard enough to hurt. Then she let go, squared herself, and walked out onto the porch to stand guard against a thing that lived in the walls, carrying a flashlight and a thermos and thirty years of guilt like ammunition.

I turned back to the ledger. And through the wall, faint as breath on glass, Gideon started humming.

* * *

*Gideon*

She thinks I can't tell time anymore. That's fair. In here, time is a rumor the walls pass around. But I know the moon — the room shows it to me sometimes, in the window of a copied kitchen, a fake moon in a fake sky, and even the fakes are getting thinner. New moon. Tonight or tomorrow, depending on whose calendar you believe, and the room believes whichever one frightens me more.

So I hummed. It used to be our thing, before everything — Tamsin humming half a song and me finishing it wrong on purpose, just to watch her face do the thing it does. Through the wall, I couldn't see her face. But I heard her stop moving in the kitchen, and then I heard her finish the phrase, and correct my key, because she has never once let me be wrong in peace.

"Hey," she said to the wall. Her palm came flat against the plaster on her side. I put mine where hers would be. There was no warmth through it. There was never any warmth through it. But there was pressure, sort of, the way memory presses.

"Hey yourself," I said. My voice comes out of me flat these days, like a recording of a recording. I hate that she has to hear it. I hate more that she doesn't flinch anymore. "Big night."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking. I'm narrating. Somebody should get the facts down." I leaned my forehead against the wall. On the other side, faintly, I could feel the vibration of her doing the same. "Tamsin. Listen to me now, because I'm going to say this while I still remember why it matters. When you go in there — and you're going to go in, because you have never once taken the exit when the entrance was more interesting —"

"Gideon —"

"When you go in, it will make you an offer. Not money, not power, nothing so cheap. It'll offer you the truest thing it can find in you and then hold it just out of reach. For me, it was you. It showed me you drowning once, when I was new here, and said it could undo that day. All I had to do was forget which twin I was. Just stop knowing I was the one who stayed." I laughed, or produced the sound that passes for laughing in here. "I said no. It seemed rude to accept, given the circumstances."

"What did it show you after?"

"After? After it showed me Dad's face, and asked if I wanted to keep it. That's how it works. It doesn't threaten. It itemizes." I pressed harder against the wall, as if pressure could carry meaning. "Whatever it offers you, Tamsin — however real it looks, however much it hurts to refuse — you refuse. Promise me. Swear it on something small. Big oaths are easy to break. Swear on the dog."

"Biscuit," she said immediately, and I felt something in my chest that wasn't static. "Swear on Biscuit."

"On Biscuit. Who I can't —" The word snagged. I reached for the shape of him, brown and loud and afraid of the vacuum cleaner, and found a hole where he'd been, edges still raw. They take things in order here, oldest first, unless you fight them. I'd fought for Biscuit. I'd lost. "Who I apparently can't picture anymore. Which is exactly why you swear on him. Small things are load-bearing, Tam. Nobody tells you that."

Through the wall, very softly, she said, "I swear on Biscuit."

"Good." I closed my eyes. "Now tell me something I haven't lost yet. Anything. Read me the ledger if you have to. Your voice keeps the walls honest."

* * *

*Tamsin*

So I read him the ledger. It sounds like a strange lullaby, and it was. Names and dates and stolen things: *Merritt Hale, 1911, gave the memory of his wedding day. Ada Villalobos, 1934, gave the name of her stillborn daughter. Thomas Okafor, 1958, gave his father's voice.* Page after page, and Gideon listening on his side of the plaster, occasionally saying *keep going* in that flattened voice, like a man holding onto a rope by reading its label.

Somewhere around 1962 I realized I was crying, and kept reading anyway, because he'd asked, and because the tears didn't seem to count as stopping.

Outside, my mother's flashlight beam swept the yard in slow arcs. Once an hour she knocked on the wall twice — their old signal, *still here, still us* — and Gideon knocked back, and the whole house felt less like a trap and more like a ship everyone was keeping afloat by taking turns bailing.

At midnight the lights flickered. Not failed — flickered, politely, like a throat being cleared. The salt lines held. The ledger's pages stirred without wind, and the last page, the blank one beneath the words COMPLETION REQUIRES A VOLUNTARY WITNESS WHO REMEMBERS ALL, seemed darker than paper has any right to be.

"Tamsin." Gideon again, and something in his tone had changed — sharpened, urgent. "It's awake early. I can feel it rearranging. The doors are — Tam, the hallway on your side, does it still have four doors?"

I went and looked. Five. One of them hadn't been there an hour ago, and it was painted the exact yellow of our childhood bathroom door, and it was very slightly ajar.

"Five," I said.

"Then it's making room for you." He exhaled. "Okay. Okay. Last things, then, while I'm me. If it goes wrong — if I'm gone tomorrow and you're not — plant tomatoes in the side bed. Mom will pretend not to care and then care enormously. And tell Mrs. Adeyemi she was right, and I hated it. And —"

"And?"

A long pause. Long enough that I pressed my hand flat to the wall to check he was still there.

"And thank you," he said finally, "for counting the doors. Every night, since you got home. I could hear you. Four, five, six. It was the only arithmetic in here that wasn't theirs." His voice did something almost like warmth. "You counted me home, Tam. Whatever happens next — that part already worked."

I put my forehead against the wall and told him I'd see him on the other side, and neither of us asked which other side.

The fifth door, down the hall, opened the rest of the way by itself. Beyond it there was no bathroom, no tile, no yellow. There was a threshold, and cold air coming over it like a tide, and somewhere far inside, a voice that sounded like every interviewer on every tape asking, gently, patiently, the first question:

*"Are you ready to be remembered?"*

I picked up the ledger. I checked the salt at my feet one last time — my line, drawn by my own hand — and stepped over it.
