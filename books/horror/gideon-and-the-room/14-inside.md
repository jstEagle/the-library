## 14. Inside

The salt line was the last thing I checked, which is funny, because salt lines are for keeping things out and I was about to walk in the other direction. I crouched at the closet wall anyway, running my thumb along the white grit like it might say something encouraging. It didn't. Salt is not a talker. Salt is a bouncer with one rule.

Odile stood in Gideon's bedroom doorway with her arms crossed so tight she looked like she was holding herself together by main force. She'd been standing there for an hour. She hadn't said much since midnight, when the lights flickered for the fourth time and the hallway outside grew a door that had never been there before — a plain pine door, unremarkable, numbered 14 in brass numerals that matched nothing else in the house. We don't have numbered doors. We have never had numbered doors.

"You could still run," she said. "I did."

"I know." I zipped my jacket. The ledger was inside it, against my ribs, warm as a second heartbeat. "And look how well that went for everyone."

"That's cruel."

"That's true. They're related." I kissed her cheek. Her skin was cold and smelled of the church — lilies and old stone, grief worn as perfume. "Stand guard. If I'm not out by dawn, burn the house down. Don't negotiate. Don't listen to anything wearing my face."

"Nothing wears your face better than you do," Odile said, and almost smiled, and that almost-smile is going to live in me longer than most things.

The wall opened the way it always opens in dreams: not swinging, not sliding, just deciding, suddenly, that it had never been a wall at all. Cold air came through, air with weight to it, air that tasted like the inside of a freezer and the inside of a church and, underneath both, like the hallway of my childhood — pencil shavings and dish soap and rain on hot asphalt.

I stepped through.

* * *

The room was our house.

Not a copy of it. Not a memory of it. It *was* it — every hallway, every room, repeated without end, folded over itself like a reflection between two mirrors. I stood in our kitchen, the one from before the renovation, with the yellow wallpaper Mom always hated and the dent in the fridge door from Gideon's tricycle. Through the kitchen window there was another kitchen, identical, and beyond that another, receding into a yellow-walled infinity. Somewhere far down the corridor of kitchens, a light was on. Or maybe that was just the nearest one, reflected forever.

"Okay," I said aloud, because saying things aloud is how you stay a person. "Okay. This is fine. This is a haunted funhouse version of my childhood home. Totally normal Tuesday."

My voice didn't echo. It was *answered*. A half-second behind me, faintly, something repeated the shape of my words without the sound of them — a mouth moving in silence, somewhere in the yellow distance.

I walked. There wasn't really anywhere else to go.

The hallways were all our hallways. The living room had the water stain on the ceiling shaped like Portugal. The bathroom still had the height marks penciled on the doorframe — *GIDEON age 6, TAMSIN age 6*, the same height, always the same height, twins even in inches. But when I touched the marks, the wood was warm. Warm like skin. And under my fingers I felt a pulse, slow and vast, like pressing an ear to the flank of something enormous and patient.

That was when I understood the room wasn't decorated with our house. The room was *made* of it. Every memory anyone had ever fed it — four generations of payments, names and faces and dog breeds and fathers' voices — it had built itself out of the wreckage. Our house wasn't in here. Our house was the bricks.

"Hello," I said to the pulse under the doorframe. "I'm here about the ledger."

The lights changed.

They didn't turn off. They turned *attentive*. Every bulb in every repeated kitchen warmed to the color of lamplight in old photographs, and the shadows rearranged themselves, and in the shadows — walking toward me down the infinite hallway, unhurried, in no particular rush after a hundred and thirty years — came the founders.

There were nine of them. I knew the number before I counted, the way you know your own phone number. Nine founding families of Hollow Creek, 1891, their names all through Mrs. Adeyemi's ledger in ink gone brown as dried blood. And they wore those names' faces — or rather, they wore the faces the town had forgotten, which amounts to the same thing. A woman with a jaw like a shovel blade and eyes the color of pond ice. A man whose smile was too wide by exactly one tooth. A child, impossibly, a girl of maybe ten, whose face was the oldest in the room.

None of them were quite solid. They had edges like photographs left too long in the sun, bleached at the borders, and where their bodies should have cast shadows they cast instead small rectangles of darkness, sharp-cornered, like frames waiting to be filled.

"Tamsin Villalobos," said the woman with the shovel-blade jaw, and her voice was every interviewer on every tape, Elias Thorn's flat cadence stretched over something older and hungrier. "Witness. Late."

"I'm early," I said. "New moon's tomorrow."

"We measure differently." She gestured, and the hallway unfolded — kitchens peeling back like pages, rooms opening into rooms opening into the same room, until we stood in what I can only call the center, though the room had no geometry I trusted. In the center was a table. On the table was a contract.

It was written on skin.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left: it was written on skin that was still, in some sense, alive, the letters rising and falling with a breath too slow to belong to anything human. The handwriting changed every few lines — different hands, different centuries, the same terms.

"Read it," the founder-child said. Her voice was kind. That was the worst part. Kindness from a thing like this isn't mercy; it's baiting a hook with something soft.

I read.

*Let the town sleep safe. Let the floods pass over, let the fever break at the border, let the mine stay shut and the dead stay quiet. For this, one house pays. One blood pays. One twin remembers alone.*

"The original bargain," I said.

"The only bargain," said the wide-smiling man. "Everything since has been bookkeeping."

"And the remembering? Why does someone have to remember?"

The founders looked at each other, and for a moment — one flickering moment — their composure slipped, and what was underneath was not malice. It was *tiredness*. Ancient, bottomless tiredness, the exhaustion of things that have been holding a door shut since before electricity.

"Because the thing below does not sleep," the child said. "It waits. Waiting is easier if no one watches. But a watch must be kept, or it forgets it is being held, and forgetting, for a thing like that, means *leaving*." She tilted her head. "So one twin remembers. Holds the whole debt in one mind. One mind is small enough to hold it. Two minds would let it slip through the seam between them."

"One twin remembers alone," I repeated. "That's why it's always twins. Not for the sacrifice. For the *solitude*."

"You understand quickly," said the shovel-jawed woman. "Your brother understood slowly, then all at once. He has been very good. He has been very alone."

Something in my chest went sideways. "Where is he?"

"Here." She spread her hands, and the room rippled. "He is the hallway you walked. He is the warmth in the doorframe. He gave us his memories to build with, and what he built, he became. You have been walking through your brother since you arrived."

I thought of the height marks. The dent in the fridge. The water stain shaped like Portugal, which Gideon used to swear looked like a dog lying down, and I would swear it looked like a shoe, and we'd argue about it every road trip home until the year we stopped coming home.

"You're showing me this," I said slowly, "to soften me up."

"We are showing you the truth," the child said. "You asked for the ledger. Here is the first page."

The contract on its breathing skin turned itself, gently, to a fresh leaf. And the room leaned in — all of it, the infinite kitchens, the attentive lights, the nine bleached-edged founders — and waited, with the patience of a thing that has eaten decades, for me to see what it wanted me to see.

At the top of the new page, in handwriting I recognized because it was my own, were the words:

*One witness may leave. One witness must remain. Choose.*

Behind me, from a hallway that had not existed a moment ago, I heard my brother's voice — flattened, static-threaded, but his — calling my name.

* * *

I want to record what happened next exactly, because I know — I knew even then, standing in that breathing architecture of stolen memory — that exactness was going to matter. That whatever the room offered next, the offer would come dressed as arithmetic, and arithmetic is how you trick grieving people. Grief makes terrible math feel generous.

"Choose," the founders said, all nine, in unison, and their unison had the texture of the second voice on Gideon's tapes.

"Before I choose anything," I said, "I want to see him."

"He is everywhere here."

"I want to see *him*. His face. Not his drywall."

The room considered this. I felt it considering — a pressure change, like weather deciding. Then the yellow kitchens folded away like a fan closing, and there he was.

Gideon sat at the table with the skin-contract, in the chair across from where I now stood, and he looked — God, he looked *good*, better than the casket, better than the tapes. Whole. Solid. His scarred knuckles, the ones the wrong body had lacked, rested on the table's edge. He was wearing the flannel shirt he'd owned for eleven years and complained about for ten.

"Tamsin." He smiled, and it was his smile, the asymmetric one, the one that meant he was about to say something wry to keep from saying something true. "You always did hate being told to wait outside."

"Gideon." My voice cracked on the second syllable and I let it. Some cracks you earn. "Are you real?"

"Define real." He laughed, and the laugh was right, and that was how I knew — not that he was fake, but that the room had built him out of my memories of him, and my memories flatter him, the way love does. The real Gideon was more annoying than this. The real Gideon chewed with his mouth open and sang wrong lyrics with total confidence. This Gideon was the highlight reel.

But he was also, I realized, watching his eyes, *in there*. Somewhere under the reconstruction, my brother was present, the way a person is present under anesthesia — aware, unable to move, listening.

"It shows you what you need," he said quietly. "That's the trap. Not the teeth. The comfort."

"I know."

"Do you?" He leaned forward. "Because it's about to offer you me, Tam. It's going to offer you a trade, and the trade will be fair — that's the horrible part, it keeps its books perfectly — and every cell in your body is going to scream yes. So decide now. Decide before it asks. Decide while you're still angry enough to think straight."

"Decide what?"

But the room didn't wait for him to answer, because the room had heard enough, and the lights dimmed to a color like the inside of a closed eyelid, and the founders drew back into their photograph-frames, and the contract turned its own page one final time.

The new page was blank except for two lines, and the two lines wrote themselves as I watched, in ink that rose from the skin like bruises surfacing:

*THE WITNESS MAY GO FREE.*
*THE DEBTOR MAY TAKE HER PLACE — WHOLE, REMEMBERING NOTHING, OWING NOTHING.*
*ONE MEMORY MUST PAY FOR BOTH: THE MEMORY OF EACH OTHER.*

And the room showed me what that meant. It didn't tell; it *showed*, pouring it directly into my head the way it pours everything, generously, horribly:

Gideon walking out of this house tomorrow morning, alive, whole, forty-one years old and healthy, blinking in the sun. Gideon at Thanksgiving, at Christmas, growing old, complaining about his knees. And me, beside him or near him or across the country from him — a stranger. A woman he'd nod to at the gas station. Every birthday party, every road trip argument about the Portugal stain, every 3 a.m. recording, every time he'd held my hair back or I'd held his hand in the hospital when Dad died — gone, paid, spent. He would live. I would live. We would just never have lived *together*. Two full lives with a Gideon-shaped hole in mine and a Tamsin-shaped hole in his, and neither of us ever knowing there had been a hole at all.

The town would keep sleeping safe. The debt would be current. Everyone eats, everyone sleeps, nobody remembers.

"Take it," Gideon said.

His voice was ragged. The flattening was gone; this was my brother at full volume, my brother with his fists on the table, and behind his eyes I could see the whole weight of what he'd been holding alone — 1974, and the first Villalobos twins, and every payment since 1891, a century of other people's forgotten dead stacked in one skull.

"Tam. *Take it.*" He was crying now, and the room let him, because the room understands that tears are persuasive. "I've done thirty years in here. I can't — you can't do this. You have a life. You have Mom. Take the deal, walk out, and let me forget you. Please. Let me forget you. It'll be *kind*. I won't even miss—"

"Don't." My voice came out strange and steady. "Don't finish that sentence in front of me."

"Someone has to remember alone," he whispered. "That's the whole design. Let it be me. I'm already halfway gone. You're still whole. Spend the whole one. Keep the broken one."

And God help me, every cell in my body screamed yes, exactly the way he'd predicted. Because that's the thing about the people who love us: they know precisely which lever to pull, and they pull it *for* us, and that's what makes it unbearable.

I put my hand on the contract. The skin beneath it was warm and pulsed once, like a heart that had been waiting a hundred and thirty years for someone to finally touch it without flinching.

"No," I said.

The room went very, very still.

"No deal," I said, and reached inside my jacket, and pulled out the ledger, and laid it open on the table beside the contract — two books, side by side, the debt and the account of the debt. "You want a witness who remembers everything? Fine. But you don't get to choose what I remember. Nobody chooses that. Not you, not Thorn, not the founders, not Gideon, not even me."

The founders stirred in their frames. The child's kind face flickered with something that might have been fear.

"What are you doing?" Gideon breathed.

"What the last page says," I told him. "Completion requires a voluntary witness who remembers all. All, Gideon. Not my half. Not your half. *All.*" I looked around at the attentive dark, at the nine bleached faces, at the breathing walls made of everyone's surrendered pasts, and I felt — not brave. Brave is a word for people with options. I felt *certain*, which is rarer and costs more.

"So hold on to something," I said to the room. "I'm going to read you the whole ledger. Every name. Every memory you took. Out loud. All at once. And you're going to remember it — *all of it* — whether you like it or not."

The lights began, very softly, to scream.
