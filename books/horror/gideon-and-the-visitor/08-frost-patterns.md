## 8. Frost Patterns

The morning the frost learned to spell, Mirabel woke up already dressed.

Not in a dramatic way — she hadn't slept in her boots or anything cinematic like that. She'd simply gone to bed at two a.m. still wearing yesterday's sweater, and when she opened her eyes at six-forty-five, the sweater was on and her boots were by the door laced and ready, as if some efficient night-shift version of herself had clocked in while she slept. She stood in her kitchen holding a mug of coffee she didn't remember pouring and thought, with the particular calm of a person who has been running on obsession for three weeks straight: *I should probably eat something green.*

She ate half a granola bar instead. The other half went into her pack for Gideon, if he showed up again, which he would, because he always did, hovering at the edge of the site like a man waiting for a bus that had already left without him a hundred years ago.

The site had changed overnight.

Mirabel noticed it from thirty feet away, before she'd even badged through the temporary fencing that the company had put up and that everyone treated as decorative. The door — the black slab with its ring handle, hauled up out of the void the bore machine had found — had been propped upright against a concrete barrier for study, and it wore a new coat of white.

Frost. But not the kind of frost that happens to things.

The kind of frost that *does* things.

"Morning, Nystrom," called Denny Okafor, the night watchman, wrapped in a parka that made him roughly spherical. He had a thermos and the resigned expression of a man who'd spent eight hours alone with something he didn't want to describe. "You're gonna want to see this. Or maybe you're not. Honestly I've been going back and forth."

"What am I looking at?"

"That." He pointed with his chin, unwilling to commit a hand to it. "Started around midnight. I figured condensation. Then I figured, you know, cold snap. Then around three I stopped figuring and started drinking coffee."

Mirabel approached the door the way she approached all her best discoveries: slowly, camera up, heart doing its familiar traitor's drumroll of *this is it, this is the thing, this is what you were for.*

Up close, the frost wasn't frost at all. Or rather, it was frost the way writing is ink. It covered the slab in fine white lines, dense as lace, and the lines weren't random. They weren't even geometric, not anymore, not the way they'd been in the tunnel — those careful hexagonal blooms she'd photographed until her memory card filled. These lines had resolved. They had become, unmistakably, letterforms.

Words. Columns of words. Hundreds of them, running top to bottom, margin to margin, in a hand so regular it looked typeset — except no typesetter alive could have made letters out of ice crystals, each stroke composed of feathered fractal branches too small to see without a loupe.

Names.

Mirabel read the first one aloud before she could stop herself, because reading is a reflex and horror is a latecomer.

"*Aldous Wren.*"

Below it, a single clean line struck through it, drawn with the same frost, precise as a signature.

"*Marguerite Wren.*" Crossed out.

"*Tobias Fenn.*" Crossed out.

"*Constance Averill.*" Crossed out.

They went on. Column after column after column, every name followed by its strike-through, the whole surface of the door organized with the patient thoroughness of a census taker who had all the time in the world, which, Mirabel suspected, he did.

"Okay," said Denny behind her. "So you're seeing it too. Good. Great. I was worried about my brain there for a second."

"It's a ledger," Mirabel said. Her breath fogged and hung in the air longer than fog should hang, drifting toward the door like it wanted to be included. "It's a list. And every name has been—"

"Crossed off. Yeah. Like a grocery list. Like somebody's been shopping." Denny shifted his weight. "How many you figure?"

Mirabel stepped back and did the math she was built to do, the math that lived in her hands and eyes. Roughly forty names per column. Roughly twenty columns visible. The frost had begun creeping around the edges of the slab, onto the barrier, onto the ground itself, where it thinned but didn't stop.

"Eight hundred," she said. "Give or take."

"Eight hundred *people?*"

"Or eight hundred *somethings*." She raised her camera. Through the viewfinder, the names sharpened, and she felt the old thrill — the collector's joy, the archivist's hunger — arrive right on schedule and then curdle, mid-beat, into something colder. Because here was the thing about ledgers. Ledgers get written *in*. And this one was still being written in.

At the bottom of the last column, the most recent column, the frost-script trailed off mid-word. Half a name. She leaned close, careful not to touch, though touching suddenly seemed like the worst idea available in Harrow County.

The half-name read:

*Mirab*

Her own name, unfinished. Uncrossed. Waiting.

The camera shutter clicked. Somewhere under the click, faint as a pulse through a wall, she heard the humming — the song, the one from her recordings, the one from her floorboards at 3 a.m., the one she'd caught herself humming in the shower two nights ago and had to sit down on the edge of the tub afterward, wet and shaking, to make herself stop.

She lowered the camera. Her hands were steady. That frightened her more than shaking would have. Three weeks ago her hands shook at everything — good coffee, good light, a new tunnel. Now they were steady as survey equipment, and she understood, with a clarity that arrived the way frost arrives, silently and all at once and everywhere: whatever was writing this ledger had been writing it for a long time, and it wrote in an order, and the order was alphabetical, and *Nystrom* came next.

*Cold clarity*, she thought. That was the phrase. Not panic. Panic was hot. This was the opposite — a crystalline, organized understanding spreading through her the way the script spread across the stone, and in that clarity she could see the whole shape of it: the door sealed from the inside, the keeper who wanted it covered, the song with its missing ending, and now this. A guest list. A roll call. A book of accounts, and someone, somewhere behind the black stone, still keeping it.

"Miz Nystrom?" Denny's voice, carefully casual. "You've gone kinda gray. Also your breath is doing a thing."

She looked down. Her exhalations were hanging in the air between her and the door, and they weren't dissipating. They were *arranging* — curling into thin white threads that drifted toward the slab and settled against it, and where they settled, the frost brightened, just slightly, the way a page brightens when fresh ink goes down.

"Oh," Mirabel said quietly. "It's taking dictation."

* * *

By nine a.m. the site had a crowd, because everything in Harrow Mill eventually got a crowd; the town had lost its mill and most of its young people and none of its appetite for standing around looking at things. Mirabel watched from the fence line as the company men in their hi-vis vests took turns approaching the door, reading a few names, making faces, and retreating to compare notes in the tone of people pretending not to be scared.

She also watched for Gideon.

He came at ten, out of nowhere, the way he always did — no car, no hard hat, just a weathered man in a wool coat walking up the access road like he owned the deed to it. The crowd parted for him without being asked, the way crowds do for certain people, an instinct older than manners.

He stopped in front of the door and looked at it for a long time. His face did something complicated. If Mirabel had to name it, she'd have said it was the look of a man finding his own handwriting on a bill he'd hoped was paid.

"You shouldn't be reading this," he said, without turning around. He knew she was there. He always knew.

"I'm not reading it. I'm documenting it. There's a difference."

"There is not." He turned. Up close, in daylight, his eyes were the color of the frost — pale, layered, old. "Reading is how it gets in. Documenting is how it gets *out*."

"Then explain it to me instead." She held up her notebook, open to a fresh page, pen ready. It was her best weapon and they both knew it. "Eight hundred names. All crossed out. Mine's half-written at the bottom. You're going to tell me what crosses a name off, and you're going to tell me now, because in—" she checked her watch, a gesture she'd stolen from him, she realized, somewhere along the way, "—eleven days, the company demolishes this thing, and I would like to know what we're letting out of it before they do."

Gideon looked at the door. At the columns. His jaw worked once.

"They're keepers," he said finally. "All of them. Every name on that stone."

"Keepers of what?"

"The door. Before me." He said it flatly, the way you'd recite a phone number you'd dialed so many times the numbers had worn smooth. "There's always one. The door doesn't stay shut on its own. It stays shut because someone stands on this side of it and *doesn't sing*. And when a keeper falters — when they hum, when they listen too long, when they get tired, and everybody gets tired, girl, everybody, I don't care how strong your knees are — the door takes their name. Writes it down. Waits."

"And the crossing-out?"

"A crossed name is a finished name." His voice didn't change at all, and that was the worst part. "It means they're inside now. It means the singing took them."

Mirabel looked at the ledger. Eight hundred strikethroughs. Eight hundred people who had stood exactly where Gideon stood, doing exactly what Gideon did, for however long the door had needed keeping — centuries, maybe, if the script's patience was any indication — and then, one by one, had faltered.

"And mine?" Her pen hovered over the page. She made herself write it down anyway, because documentation was her prayer and she wasn't going to stop praying now. "*Mirab.* Half-written. What does that make me?"

Gideon was quiet for a moment. When he answered, he didn't look at her, and somehow that was kinder.

"Incomplete," he said. "Which means there's still time. Which means—" and here he finally turned, and his pale eyes fixed on hers with an urgency that cracked his calm right down the middle, "—you need to destroy your photographs, Mirabel. Today. All of them. The tunnel surveys, the door, the frost patterns, the sound files. Everything. Your pictures are how it learns our side. Every frame you take, it takes back. You're not recording the door. The door is recording *you*."

"That's not how cameras work."

"No," he agreed. "It's worse."

* * *

She didn't destroy anything. Of course she didn't. She was Mirabel Nystrom, and Mirabel Nystrom did not delete data; she backed it up twice and labeled it.

But she did go home that afternoon, and she did spread her prints across her kitchen table — the good ones, the ones she'd been planning to pitch to a journal, the hexagonal frost blooms and the fused hinges and the bolts thrown from within — and she laid them out in chronological order, because order was how she thought, and she looked at them for a long time.

That was when she saw it.

In the earliest photograph, taken the first day, the frost on the door's seams had formed a pattern she'd logged as *decorative, radial, possibly natural*. In the second week's photos, the pattern had grown. In last night's shots, it had become the ledger. And in every photograph, in the corner of the frame, in the frost at the very edge of the stone — so faint she'd never seen it without laying the images side by side — there was a small mark. A symbol. Repeated in every photo, growing fractionally more defined each time, like a signature being practiced.

It was a spiral. Tight, deliberate, coiling inward toward a center it never quite reached.

And beneath the spiral in the final photograph, in frost-script fine as hair, three words she needed a loupe to read:

*almost your turn*

Mirabel sat down at her kitchen table, in her kitchen chair, in her kitchen, eleven miles and a locked door away from the site, and laughed — one short, unamused bark, the laugh of a woman who has just realized the interview subject has been interviewing her.

"Okay," she said out loud, to the empty apartment, to the frost beginning, very faintly, to bloom in the corners of her own windows. "New rule. We talk about this like adults."

The frost did not answer. But it kept writing.

And far across town, at the fenced-off site, the demolition crew received their schedule update: heavy equipment arriving Monday. Seven days.

Six, now.
