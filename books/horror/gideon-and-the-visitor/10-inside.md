## 10. Inside

The crowd had thinned by the time Mirabel came back, which was the only mercy the evening offered. The demolition had been called off — officially "pending structural review," unofficially pending everyone forgetting — and the site was supposed to be empty except for the chain-link, the portable lights, and the door.

Except the door had eaten two men.

Dale Kowalczyk and a kid named Ferro had walked into the tunnel at 4:47 in the afternoon, in front of eleven witnesses and one very expensive camera drone, and had not walked out. The drone had followed them forty feet in and then its feed had gone to snow, and the snow had gone to static, and the static had gone — this was the part Mirabel kept replaying — to a pattern. A lattice. Frost, blooming across the screen in deliberate, geometric whorls, before the operator killed the feed.

She stood at the edge of the site in her good boots and her bad coat, and she did the thing she always did, the thing that had gotten her this far and would presumably be the death of her: she made a list.

One: the door had been cracked, not opened. The first strike of the jackhammer had split the stone along a seam that hadn't existed in any of her photographs, and the seam had breathed.

Two: the humming, once audible to everyone, had not stopped when they stopped. It had simply dropped below the threshold of the ear and back into the floor, the water, the bones of the town.

Three: two men were inside, and the town's official response, as of the 6 o'clock news, was that two men had "wandered off site."

Mirabel had spent nine years documenting the unmapped underside of Harrow Mill, and she had learned one law above all others: the map is not the territory, but the territory is always keeping score. Somewhere in her chest, under the fear, under the guilt with its teeth in her — *your photographs brought the crowd, your crowd brought the jackhammer* — somewhere under all of that was the explorer's oldest and most shameful feeling.

She wanted to go in.

* * *

The fence was a formality. She'd been climbing Harrow Mill's fences since she was nineteen and still believed danger was a thing you could photograph from a safe distance. She went over the corner where the chain-link had been pried back during the afternoon's chaos, dropped into the mud, and stood for a moment in the wash of the portable lights, listening.

The bore shaft opened in the ground like a throat. The crew had cut a proper access ramp down to the door — steel plates, timber shoring, the works — and the lights ran down it in a chain of yellow circles that made the whole thing look almost official, almost safe, like an entrance to a parking garage instead of a wound in the world.

The door sat at the bottom, cracked down its middle, and the frost had grown.

That was the wrong word, she knew. Frost didn't *grow* the way this was growing, in hours, in warm air, against every law of thermodynamics she'd ever pretended to understand in a community college physics class. The patterns had spread from the seams out across the whole face of the stone, and they weren't whorls anymore. They had edges. Stems. The confident strokes of a hand that knew exactly where it was going.

She raised her camera. Her hands were shaking, and she let them — she'd learned long ago that fighting the shake made it worse — and took three frames of the door, one of the shoring, one of the crack itself, where the stone had parted just wide enough for a body if the body were sorry about it.

The humming was there. Faint. Patient. She could feel it in her back teeth.

"Dale?" she called, and her voice went down the ramp and into the crack and did not echo, which was wrong, because everything in the tunnels echoed, she had four hundred hours of recordings to prove it. Her voice simply stopped, like a bird hitting glass. "Ferro?"

Nothing. The lights buzzed. The frost wrote.

She checked the shoring, because checking was what she did, because the list was how she prayed. The timber was sound. The steel plates were sound. There was no collapse, no cave-in, no reason on God's green earth for two able-bodied men to fail to walk forty feet back out.

At the mouth of the crack, the frost had formed a lip, like the rim of a cup.

"Okay," she said to herself, in the tone she used for herself on long solo surveys, the one that was half coach and half hostage negotiator. "Okay. You go in, you look, you come out. Forty feet. In and out. You've done worse in the storm drains off Route 9, and those had actual sewage in them."

She clipped her headlamp on. She marked the threshold with a chalk arrow — her own small ritual, *here is where the map ends* — and she took one photograph of the chalk arrow, because if she was going to do a stupid thing she was going to do it with documentation.

Then she went inside.

* * *

The crack widened three feet in. That was the first surprise — she'd braced for a crawl, for the intimate misery of moving through stone on her elbows, but the split in the door opened into a corridor tall enough to stand in, and the walls were not stone.

They were frost. All of it. Floor, walls, ceiling, a corridor of ice that was not cold, and every inch of it was written on.

Mirabel stopped and stared, and the explorer in her — the true one, the nine-year-old who had first pressed her face to the grating over the culvert behind her grandmother's house and thought *nobody has ever seen this* — that girl woke up all the way, and wept a little, and was terrified.

The script covered everything. Thousands of lines of it, in that same deliberate hand she'd watched bloom across the door, dense as a legal document and old as the ice it was cut into. It caught her headlamp and threw it back in soft blue glints, so the whole corridor seemed lit from inside, like a thing that had swallowed a sky.

She photographed it. Of course she photographed it. Her camera clicked and whirred in the silence, and the silence accepted the clicks the way a lake accepts stones.

"Names," she whispered. It was the only word that fit. She'd seen the ledger on the door — names, hundreds, crossed out — and this was the same script at ten thousand times the scale. A library of it. A cathedral of it. She ran her glove along the wall an inch from the writing, not touching, never touching, and felt the warmth.

That was the second surprise. The corridor was warm. Not heated — *breathed*. The air moved past her in slow, regular pulses, in and out, in and out, and there was a smell to it, mineral and faintly sweet, like water over limestone in a cave no one had named.

Her breath did not fog. The frost did not melt. The writing did not care about the rules.

She walked. She counted her steps — forty, sixty, a hundred — and the corridor did not end, and behind her, when she turned, the entrance had gone dim and far, the chalk arrow a pale smudge she could no longer quite read. She turned back quickly. She did not run. Running was for when you knew what you were running from.

"Dale!" The name fell flat again, that glass-bird stop. "Ferro!"

And then, faintly, from somewhere ahead: humming.

Her whole body went cold, and then went still, because she knew the tune. Three verses, ending mid-phrase, the song under the town, the song through her floorboards, the song she had caught herself humming over the dishes two nights ago and stopped with her lips clamped shut like a child who'd said the bad word. It was coming from ahead, from more than one throat, moving in perfect unison, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard and she wanted to walk toward it more than she had ever wanted anything, and that wanting was exactly the problem.

She bit the inside of her cheek until she tasted copper, and the copper helped, a little, an anchor. She kept her lips sealed. She walked toward the singing with her mouth shut like a locked drawer.

*You are not here to listen,* she told herself. *You are here to document. Two men. Forty feet. In and out.*

The corridor curved, gently, the way a throat curves.

* * *

She found the first sign of them at what she estimated to be two hundred feet in — a hard hat, sitting upright on the frost floor, placed rather than dropped, its lamp still burning a little yellow cone into the script on the wall. She photographed it. Her hands had stopped shaking somewhere back around step eighty, which frightened her more than the shaking had. Fear was fuel. Calm, down here, was just the song getting comfortable.

Then she heard footsteps behind her, and turned with her heart in her teeth, and it was Gideon.

He was not calm. That was the first thing she understood, and it undid her more than the corridor had. Every other time she'd seen him — the site, the street outside her building, the county archive steps — he had been weathered and still, a man with his edges sanded down by a century of standing in one place. Now he was moving fast, coat open, and his face was doing two things at once, fury and fear, braided so tight she couldn't tell which was holding which up.

"You crossed," he said. Not a question. He said it the way you'd say *you stepped on the ice.*

"I was looking for—"

"I know what you were looking for." He stopped an arm's length from her, and she watched him make himself be gentle, watched it cost him. "Mirabel. The door was cracked. Cracked is not open, but it is not closed, and you walked through it in the dark, alone, with your little camera, because you thought it was a door and not a mouth."

"It's a corridor."

"It's whatever it needs to be to keep you walking." He looked past her, down the curve, toward the humming, and something in his face went old and tired and terribly kind. "Dale and Ferro are ahead. I can take you to them. But you have to understand what you did when you crossed, and you have to understand it now, while you can still understand things."

"Then tell me," she said. "Tell me instead of—"

"Because there is no undocumented way back." He said it flatly, without theater, the way a doctor says *the test came back.* "That's what this place is. It writes down everything that enters it. Every footstep, every breath, every frame on that camera. You've been writing it down for nine years, Mirabel — the tunnels, the water, the walls. You thought you were the author." He nodded at the frost-script around them, the soft blue glow of ten thousand lines. "You were the rough draft."

The camera hung heavy against her chest. She thought of every photograph. Every coordinate. Every careful, prayerful entry in her notebooks, the ritual precision of it, the comfort of it — *if I map it, it can't hurt me; if I log it, it belongs to me.* She had fed it. Page by page, frame by frame, she had fed it, and now she was standing inside its mouth, in its library, in its cathedral, and it knew her handwriting.

"I can still walk out," she said. "The entrance is right—"

"Where it was," Gideon agreed. "And it will let you reach it. It wants you to reach it. It wants you to come back tomorrow, and the day after, with your camera, because you are the best thing that ever happened to it, and it has been waiting a very long time for someone with your particular faith." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "Documentation is not protection. I tried to tell you at the fence. I tried to tell you with the photographs. You are not recording it, Mirabel. It is recording you."

The humming swelled, ahead, around, in the walls — and she felt the pull again, the sweet gravitational lean toward the melody, and she clenched her jaw and kept her lips shut and Gideon saw her do it, and something in his expression shifted. Softened. Recalculated.

"You haven't sung," he said.

"Not one note."

"Not even in your head?"

She hesitated. "In my head it's just... remembering."

He closed his eyes briefly. "Nothing is just anything, down here." But when he opened them, the fury had drained out of his face and left only the fear, and the fear had a shape she recognized, because she'd seen it in mirrors before bad surveys, before bad decisions: it was the shape of someone who cared whether you came back.

"Show me Dale and Ferro," she said quietly.

"You don't want—"

"I'm a documentarian, Gideon. It's what I do. I go where the thing is and I write it down, and I don't leave people out of the record." She lifted her chin. "You've been keeping this place a hundred years alone. I'm not asking you to like my methods. I'm asking you to walk me to the men who are missing, because nobody else in that town is coming, and you and I both know it."

For a long moment the only sound was the corridor breathing, in and out, in and out, and the humming, patient as water.

Then Gideon sighed — a sound a hundred years deep — and turned, and started walking.

"Stay behind me," he said. "Touch nothing. And Mirabel—" he glanced back, and in the blue glow his eyes were the oldest thing in the tunnel, older than the script, older than the door. "Whatever you hear ahead. Whatever you want to do when you hear it. Keep your lips sealed. The song takes what it teaches, and it is always teaching."

They walked, together, into the curve of the throat, toward the singing — and behind them, in the frost on the wall they had just passed, new letters were quietly blooming, line by line, in a hand that knew exactly where it was going:

*M. Nystrom. Entered willingly.*
