## 16. The Fourth Verse

There is a particular kind of cowardice that looks, from the outside, like patience.

I have had a hundred years to study it. I invented several of its variations. The one I practiced longest was this: tell the truth in pieces small enough to swallow, and never tell the piece that matters, because the piece that matters will change everything, and everything is a heavy thing to hand a person who is already carrying your door.

Mirabel Nystrom sat on an overturned crate in the maintenance room beneath Harrow Mill — my maintenance room, though I had never signed anything saying so — with her notebook open on her knees and frost on her eyelashes, and she asked me the question I had been dreading for eleven days.

"How does it end?"

I should explain that she did not mean the town. She did not mean the excavation, or the lawsuit the company was surely drafting against her for trespassing on their catastrophe, or even the slow white script climbing her apartment windows like ivy with an agenda. She meant the song. Three verses, ending mid-phrase, humming up through the floorboards of every building in Harrow Mill since the bore machine cracked the world open. She wanted to know how the song ended, the way other people want to know how books end — badly, urgently, at two in the morning, with a flashlight under the blanket.

And I, who had answered every question she'd thrown at me with the honesty of a man who has nothing left to lose except his post, heard myself say:

"It doesn't."

Which was true. And also the most generous lie I had told in a century.

* * *

Let me back up, because Gideons are not naturally forthcoming, and if I am going to confess, I intend to do it properly, in order, the way you'd fold a map.

The thing behind the door trades in songs. That much I had already told her, crouched in the corridor while the warm air moved past us like something breathing through its teeth. Every verse sung aloud gives it a voice. The first verse gives it a whisper. The second, a murmur. The third — the verse the workers sang with their frosted eyes and their perfect mouths — gives it a throat. I did not tell her what the fourth verse does because, until that night on the crate, I had not decided whether I would ever let it be sung at all.

Here is what I knew, alone, in the dark, for a hundred years:

The song has four verses. The first three are a conversation. They are the entity speaking and the keeper answering, call and response, question and deflection — the oldest trick there is, older than doors. You keep it talking. A thing that is talking is a thing that has not yet finished arriving. I sang the first verse into the dark in 1911 and I have been singing my half of the second and third ever since, night after night, a duet with a mouth in the wall, and the arrangement held because the song never ended. A song that doesn't end is a lock that doesn't rust.

But every song wants to end. That's the cruelty built into music; it's why lullabies work. And the fourth verse — the last verse, the true one, the one I found scratched into the inside of the door in letters so fine you'd mistake them for scratches made by a trapped animal, which, in fairness, is what I was — the fourth verse does not continue the conversation.

It closes it.

Sung correctly, by the right number of voices, it ends the song. And a song that ends, ends *everything it was holding open.* The corridor. The frost-script. The voice wearing dead men's tones. All of it folds shut like a book whose last page has finally been turned.

There was only one problem, and it was shaped exactly like me.

The fourth verse cannot be sung alone.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only dignity left to a man my age. It isn't that the verse is difficult, though it is — it climbs intervals no human throat finds comfortable, notes that sit between notes, the way frost sits between the molecules of glass. It isn't that the entity forbids it; the entity would love nothing more than to hear it attempted solo, because a fourth verse sung by one voice is just a third verse with ambitions, and it would tear the singer open and pour itself through the hole.

No. The reason is simpler and worse. The fourth verse is written in harmony. Two lines braided together, each one incomplete without the other, dissonant apart and resolved only in combination. One voice singing both parts produces neither. I know because I tried. In 1913, and 1919, and once memorably in 1954 when I was feeling optimistic about whistling. The song simply waited, patient as water, for me to stop embarrassing myself.

That is why I was always alone. Not because the post required solitude. Because the ending required company, and company required me to ask someone to stand next to me at the edge of a door and sing themselves shut inside it, and I could not — in a hundred years, with all my practice at cowardice — bring myself to ask.

So I kept the first three verses going instead. Forever. A man humming in a basement so the world above can keep misplacing its keys and complaining about the price of coffee. It felt like duty. Some nights it even felt like virtue.

Most nights it felt like what it was: a very long way of not asking.

* * *

Mirabel was watching me not answer her, and she was good at it. That's the thing nobody tells you about documentarians — they don't just record, they *notice*. She had noticed that I flinched at the word "verse." She had noticed that when the frost on the walls wrote itself, I read it faster than she did. She had noticed, God help me, that I hummed in my sleep, and she had the decency not to mention it, which is more courtesy than I've been shown by any living soul including, especially, the ones behind the door.

"Gideon," she said. "It ends mid-phrase. Songs don't do that. People don't write songs that just — stop. Unless the stopping is the point." She turned her notebook toward me. On the page, in her tidy surveyor's hand, were the three verses transcribed from her recordings, and after the last line, a gap, and after the gap, a single word with a circle around it: *unfinished?*

"You've been sitting on something," she said. "Since the corridor. Since before the corridor. You get this look, like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like."

"I don't have looks," I said. "I'm a hundred and thirty years old. My face gave up having expressions during the Coolidge administration."

"See, that's a look." She tapped the crate beside her. "Sit down. You hover like a bat."

I sat. This is another thing about Mirabel: she issues small commands the way other people offer tea, and you find yourself obeying before you've decided to, and then you're sitting on a crate in a freezing basement being looked at kindly by a woman with frost on her eyelashes, and the arithmetic you've been avoiding comes due.

"There are four verses," I said.

Her pen stopped moving.

"The song you recorded — the one under the water noise, the one in your floorboards — it has a fourth verse. I've known it since 1912. It's written on the inside of the door. I've read it every day for a hundred years the way you'd read a letter you can't answer."

"And?" Her voice was very level. Level voices are how brave people scream. "What does it do?"

"It ends it. Ends the song, ends the opening, ends—" I gestured at the walls, the frost, the whole white grammar of the place. "All of it. Folded shut. Finished. The thing behind the door goes back to being nothing, because nothing is all it gets to be if no one ever sings it a voice."

For a long moment she didn't say anything, and the maintenance room ticked and settled around us, and somewhere far off the pipes knocked their usual morse code of a dying building.

"That's good," she said slowly. "That's — Gideon, that's the best news anyone's said down here in weeks. We sing it, it ends. Why are you telling me like you're reading me a diagnosis?"

"Because it takes two voices." I looked at my hands. They're good hands. Carpenter's hands, keeper's hands, hands that had bolted a door ten thousand times and never once unbolted it. "It's written in harmony. Two lines. Each one meaningless without the other. One voice can't carry it — I've tried, more times than I'll admit to sober, and I was never sober in 1978, so draw your own conclusions."

"So we need a second singer." She said it lightly. Too lightly. I watched her arrive at the rest of it in stages, the way frost arrives across a window — corner first, then creeping. "Someone who knows the verse. Someone willing to sing it. Someone standing..." She stopped. Looked at the door at the end of the corridor, at the seam of light that wasn't light. "Standing where, exactly, when it's sung?"

"On the threshold," I said. "Both of us. That's the design of it. The verse is a closing argument, and you have to deliver it from inside the courtroom."

The silence that followed had texture. I let her sit in it. I owed her that much — I'd had a century to rehearse mine.

"And if no one sings it," she said at last, "you keep doing what you're doing. Forever. Holding the first three verses in a loop until the sun burns out or the mill falls on your head."

"Until the sun burns out," I confirmed. "The mill falling on my head would be a mercy, frankly. I've thought about requesting it."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking. I'm extremely serious about the mill."

She laughed — one short, startled syllable, gone as fast as it came, the laugh of a person furious at herself for laughing. Then she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes and said, quietly, "You've had this for a hundred years. The way out. The actual way out. And you never—"

"Never what? Put an advertisement in the paper? *Wanted: one soprano, must be willing to be sealed inside an interdimensional door, harmonizing required, pay in gratitude.*" I shook my head. "Mirabel. I met people. Over the years. Surveyors, priests, one very determined mail carrier in 1963. I could have taught them the verse. I could have asked. And every time I got as far as opening my mouth, I thought: this person has a mother, or a dog, or a favorite chair. This person expects to see spring. And I closed my mouth and went back to humming."

"That's not cowardice," she said. "That's—"

"It's both. It's usually both. That's the trick of it — the worst things wear the costume of the decent things. Duty. Protection. Patience." I finally looked at her straight on. "Hope arrived for me about a hundred years late and dressed as a sacrifice, and I kept sending it away because I couldn't bear the postage."

She was quiet again. But it was a different quiet. I've learned the difference the way you learn weather. There is the quiet of someone deciding to leave, and the quiet of someone deciding to stay, and they sound identical right up until they don't.

Then she opened her notebook to a fresh page, clicked her pen, and said, in the brisk tone she used for logging coordinates:

"Teach it to me."

"No."

"Gideon—"

"*No.*" It came out harder than I meant. Harder than I'd spoken to anyone since 1911. "You don't understand what you're volunteering for. The seal needs a keeper. When the song ends and the door shuts, it doesn't shut on an empty room. Someone holds it from the inside. That's the bargain underneath the bargain — the entity gets silence, but the silence has to be *manned.* I have manned it for a hundred years. If we sing the fourth verse, there will be two of us in there. Indefinitely. Possibly forever. There is no version of this where you sing with me and then walk home and make coffee and complain about the price of it."

She didn't flinch. I have seen soldiers flinch less than she didn't flinch just then.

"My windows are writing on themselves," she said. "My archive is filming me sleeping. The town poured concrete over two living men and called it Tuesday. Everything I loved about my life — the mapping, the documenting, the finding of unmapped things — I fed all of it to that thing through a camera lens." She turned the notebook around. On the fresh page she had written, already, in her tidy hand: *Verse 4 — lesson one.* "You spent a hundred years not asking because you couldn't stand the cost. I'm telling you the cost up front, and I'm asking anyway. Teach me the verse, Gideon. Note by note. And I promise you—" and here she smiled, and it was the smile of a woman standing on a threshold she'd already crossed in her head, "—I will not sing a single note out loud until you tell me it's time. Lips sealed. Scout's honor. I was never a scout, but I documented enough of them to know the oath."

I looked at her for a long time.

A hundred years is a long time to be the only voice in a song. You forget that harmony isn't a musical technique. It's a form of trust — two lines agreeing to be wrong alone and right together. I had forgotten it so thoroughly that when hope finally walked into my basement wearing a field jacket and carrying a notebook, I nearly sent it away again out of habit.

Nearly.

"All right," I said, and my voice did something unfamiliar, something that hadn't happened since the Taft administration. It shook. "Lesson one. But we start tonight, and we start with the rule: you may think the notes. You may *hear* them in your head as clearly as church bells. But your lips stay sealed until the frost stops listening — and Mirabel, the frost is always listening."

She wrote that down too. Of course she did.

Above us, faint through a hundred feet of concrete and dying mill, the floorboards of Harrow Mill began, very softly, to hum the third verse — the one that ends mid-phrase, the question hanging in the air like breath on glass.

This time, for the first time in a hundred years, I knew the answer.

And so, God help me, did she.
