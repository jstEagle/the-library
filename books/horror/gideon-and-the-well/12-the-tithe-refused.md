## 12. The Tithe Refused

Quentin Eriksen had a theory, and like most of his theories it arrived at two in the morning while he was eating cold noodles over a monitoring laptop in the Hollowmere kitchen.

The theory was this: the well was polite.

Not kind. Not merciful. Polite, in the way that a debt collector is polite, or a landlord, or the county when they send you a notice about your water bill instead of just shutting it off. Everything the thing beneath the farm did followed a procedure. It counted before it took. It left lights on like a courtesy call. It put its handprints where people would find them — pressed neatly into plaster, five-fingered, patient, *presented*. It had taken Petra only after the crew cut the seal, and even then it had lured her down with her brother's voice rather than simply reaching up and pulling, which Quentin was increasingly certain it could do.

It wanted to be given something. That was the whole architecture of the place. A door sealed from the inside. Lead poured through the keyhole by hands that must have been shaking and had done it anyway. Somebody, sometime in 1847 or long before, had decided that the proper way to deal with what lived under Hollowmere was not to fight it but to *pay* it, on schedule, in full, and Quentin's third-generation pest controller's brain — the part of him that understood you never actually exterminate vermin, you only negotiate terms with them — recognized the shape of the arrangement immediately.

A tithe. Ilse had used the word without flinching, the way old women say words that would flatten anyone younger. One name per generation, freely entered into the ledger, and the household kept. Refuse the tithe, and the well stopped being polite. The motel hallway doors were the first draft of impoliteness. Tomas sleepwalking toward stairwells, whispering numbers downward, was the second.

So: offer it someone willingly, and it reaches for no one else.

Quentin looked across the kitchen table at the ledger, which sat there now the way a loaded gun sits on a table, except worse, because nobody had ever invented a safe for books. The last page still said D-A-L-E in handwriting that wasn't Dale's, wasn't anybody's, in ink that dried matte and dark like dried blood if you let yourself think about it too long, which Quentin was trying very hard not to.

"Okay," he said out loud, to the ledger, to the house, to whatever was listening through the walls with more attention than the walls strictly required. "Here's my problem with you."

The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled. The porch light, visible through the window over the sink, burned its 3:11 a.m. vigil against nothing.

"My problem," Quentin continued, because he'd learned years ago that talking to empty houses was better than letting them talk first, "is that I don't know your rules. I hate not knowing rules. You want a name? Fine. Names are cheap. I've got one. But you're going to take it properly, on the record, with witnesses, or I swear I will spend every night from now until the harvest moon reading names at you until you wish you'd never been dug up."

It was, he reflected later, not the most dignified opening negotiation in history. But the thing about talking to an eldritch presence at two in the morning is that dignity left the building around the time you started bringing chalk to a farmhouse.

* * *

He told the family at breakfast, in the way he'd learned to tell them things: plainly, with coffee, and with Maren present as his translator, since Maren had a gift for hearing what he meant underneath what he said.

"I'm going down to the shaft tonight," he said. "Alone."

"No," said Ruth Hollowmere, who was Maren and Tomas's aunt and had inherited guardianship along with the farm and had opinions about both.

"Yes," Quentin said. "Hear me out. It has to be offered. That's the pattern. Every generation, somebody walks to the edge and gives a name, and the rest of you get to keep living here. If nobody offers, it starts collecting on its own, and we've all seen how it collects." He turned his mug a quarter-turn on the table, a nervous habit he'd never managed to kill. "I'm not offering my life. I want to be clear about that. I'm offering to *negotiate*. There's a difference."

"There isn't," said Ruth, "when the other party eats people."

"Aunt Ruth," said Maren, who was eleven going on forty, "he's right about the pattern. Grandma Ilse said the same thing. It takes what's given. It only takes everything when nothing's given."

"And what exactly are you planning to give it, Mr. Eriksen?"

Quentin had prepared an answer for this. He'd prepared it in the shower, and in the truck, and during the eleven minutes he'd spent staring at the ceiling of his rented room waiting for the fourteenth door to exhale at him. The answer was: himself, conditionally. His name, provisionally. A down payment, with terms.

What came out of his mouth was: "I'll figure that out at the bottom."

Ruth set down her fork with the deliberate care of a woman deciding not to throw it. "You'll figure it out at the—"

"He's a professional," Maren offered.

"He's a *pest controller*."

"Third-generation," Quentin said. "There are certificates."

Tomas, six years old and currently the most closely supervised child in three counties, looked up from his cereal. "You should bring the chalk," he said seriously. "In case you need to count your way back."

And that, Quentin thought, was the single best tactical advice anyone had given him all week.

* * *

The excavation site behind the farmhouse had been abandoned for nine days now, and it showed. The county's orange barriers had been knocked flat by wind. Someone — Quentin suspected Maren, who had been conducting her own quiet investigations with the methodical patience of a child who had grown up counting handprints on ceilings — had laid a line of salt around the shaft mouth in a wobbly, sincere circle that wouldn't have stopped anything with a door, but which Quentin found himself stepping over carefully anyway, out of respect.

The trench ran twelve feet down to dressed stone. The iron-banded door stood open at the end of it, the cut lead seal lying beside it in curled ribbons like the shed skin of something metal. Beyond the door, the shaft went down into the dark, breathing.

That was the word for it. Breathing. Cold air rose out of the ground in slow tides, maybe one exhalation every forty seconds, and standing at the lip of it felt like standing next to a sleeping animal whose dreams you couldn't afford to disturb.

Quentin checked his watch. 11:47 p.m. He'd wanted to arrive after midnight, because midnight felt like the correct business hours for this sort of thing, and also because he'd spent the evening doing something he hadn't told the family about: practicing.

He stood at the shaft mouth with the ledger open on his knee, a flashlight clipped to his collar, a coil of rope over one shoulder, a stick of chalk in his breast pocket like a pen in a salesman's pocket, and he read the names aloud.

"Henrik Ostrander. Given, 1847." The ledger's entries were terse, formal, almost liturgical. "Given freely. Household kept."

His voice echoed down the stone and came back changed. He tried not to listen to how it came back changed.

"Margit Ostrander. Given, 1871. Given freely. Household kept."

The cold air breathed upward against his face, carrying the smell of deep water and old stone and, faintly, unbearably, something sweet, like apples in a cellar.

"Johann Voss. Given, 1894."

He worked through the century and a half of it, name by name, and with each one he felt the shaft listening more attentively, the way a room listens when you mention money. By the time he reached the modern entries — Petra Voss, mid-name, the letters after P-E-T-R-A scratched into absence — his mouth had gone dry and his hands were steady in the specific way hands get steady right before they do something stupid.

"Petra," he said. "Given. Not freely. Taken. Which—" he raised his voice slightly, addressing the dark the way he'd address a client who'd tried to pay in expired coupons, "—which I want to note is a breach of your own stated terms. You're supposed to take what's *given*. She didn't give. Her idiot coworkers cut your seal. If anything, you owe *them*, and they're idiots, so good luck collecting."

The shaft exhaled. Forty seconds. The breath smelled like his grandmother's kitchen.

Quentin's stomach dropped.

It didn't smell *like* his grandmother's kitchen. It smelled like cardamom buns and dish soap and the particular dusty warmth of a radiator in a small apartment above a bakery on Södergatan, and it was coming up out of the ground twelve feet below a farmstead ninety miles from anywhere, and it was his childhood, offered back to him like a sample at a market stall.

"Okay," he said quietly. "Okay. That's — fine. That's a trick. You dig around in heads, everybody knows that. Petra heard her brother. You heard my—"

"*Quentin Arne Eriksen,*" said the shaft, in his own voice.

Not near his own voice. Not an impression. His voice, exact, down to the slight flattening of his vowels he'd inherited from his father and hated and could never shake, down to the little catch he got in his throat when he was frightened, which he was now, enormously, and which the voice reproduced faithfully, so that he stood at the edge of a hole in the earth listening to himself be afraid in stereo.

"*Fourteen Lindqvist Street,*" his own mouth said from underground, warm and conversational, a man chatting on a porch. "*Third floor. The apartment with the blue door. You counted the stairs every day. Seventeen. You counted them because your father taught you to count doors and stairs and streetlights, because a man who counts is a man who notices, and a man who notices is a man who's safe.*"

"That's mine," Quentin said. His voice cracked on *mine*, and the echo cracked identically, and somewhere below him something seemed pleased about it. "That's my memory. Give it back."

"*You were seven,*" said the well, gently, horribly, with the tenderness of a thing that had already swallowed this memory whole and was only showing him the shape of the bulge. "*Your father knelt down in the hallway and put your hand flat against the front door and said: feel that? A door always tells you what it is, if you ask politely.*"

Quentin sat down. Not gracefully — his knees simply resigned, and he ended up on the cold dirt of the trench floor with the ledger splayed across his thighs, staring into the breathing dark, doing arithmetic he didn't want to do.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he'd been circling for days without landing on. He remembered that afternoon. He remembered it vividly — the smell of his father's wool coat, the brass of the doorknob cold under his small palm, the light through the frosted glass making his father's face soft and featureless as an egg.

Except.

Except his father had died when Quentin was five.

He knew that the way you know your own birthday, flatly, without narrative. Car accident. November. He had no pictures of that afternoon in the hallway. He had no *room* in his head for that afternoon; it fit nowhere against the actual furniture of his childhood, and yet it was there, complete, warm, load-bearing, and it had been there for days now, quietly holding up other memories, and he could not for the life of him remember whether it had been there before he came to Hollowmere.

"Oh," he said. Very small. To the ledger, to the dark, to himself. "Oh, you've been *furnishing*."

The shaft breathed. Sweet apples. Cardamom. Home.

Quentin looked down at the ledger on his lap, at the column of names each ending in scratched-out letters, and he understood — finally, fully, in the place where understanding lives when it stops being information and starts being weather — that the well did not want his life. His life was a rounding error. What it collected was *record*. Name, address, the seventeen stairs, the blue door, the father kneeling in the hall. It harvested households one identity at a time, and it had started harvesting his the moment he'd signed his name on the work order, and possibly — he thought of the porch light burning outside his apartment, night after night, faithful as a dog — considerably earlier than that.

"New offer," he said. He was surprised to hear his voice come out level. Professional. Third-generation steady. "You keep whatever you've already taken. Call it a retainer. But the family in the motel — Ruth, Maren, Tomas, the crew — they come off your books. All of them. And you deal with *me* exclusively. I'll come down here every night between now and moonrise and we'll do this properly. Terms. Witnesses. In writing." He held up the ledger. "You clearly like writing."

The silence that followed lasted three full breathing-cycles, which he was beginning to understand was the sound of it thinking.

Then, from far down the shaft, in a voice that was his voice but younger — seven years old, standing in a hallway, palm flat against a blue door — it said:

"*Seventeen stairs.*"

And began, patiently, warmly, in the voice of a child counting his way home, to count.

Quentin gripped the ledger, backed up the trench ladder rung by rung with his eyes on the dark until the dark was out of sight, and did not stop walking until he reached the salt circle, where he turned, and called down into the ground, in a voice that only shook a little:

"Moonrise. You and me. We'll settle accounts."

From the bottom of the world, faint and contented, his own seven-year-old voice finished counting the stairs, opened an imaginary blue door, and said:

"*Welcome home.*"

Up at the farmhouse, behind him, every light in every window switched on at once — and stayed on, all night, like a house holding its breath.

And in the morning, when Maren brought him coffee and asked how it went, Quentin opened his mouth to answer and discovered, with a calm that frightened him far more than panic would have, that he had to think for a moment — really think, search, dig — before he could remember his mother's maiden name.

He drank the coffee instead.

Maren watched him over the rim of her own cup, the way she watched doors, and said nothing, and went and got the chalk.
