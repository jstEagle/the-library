## 13. Maren's Bargain

Maren Hollowmere had learned to sleep with one ear open the way other children learned to ride a bike—badly at first, with a lot of falling, and then all at once, like it had always been in her.

The motel room she shared with Tomas had two beds, a television bolted to a dresser, and a door that hadn't been there when they checked in. It stood in what should have been blank wall beside the bathroom, thin as a drawn line, and it breathed cold. Maren had chalked it herself: **14**. Quentin had come by that evening, looked at it for a long time, and said nothing, which was how Maren knew it was bad. Quentin talked when things were fine. He counted when they weren't.

Tomas slept the way he always did now—curled toward the door, whispering. Numbers, mostly. Sometimes letters. Tonight it was *"four... three..."* in a voice pitched low and even, like he was reading off a list someone else was holding up for him.

Maren lay in the dark and listened to her brother count down and thought about her mother.

Not the way adults thought about her mother, all grief-shaped and heavy. The way Maren thought about her: the smell of bread and bleach, the way she'd hummed while hanging laundry, the exact sound of her boots on the kitchen floor—*clack, clack, pause*—because she always stopped at the pantry to check something before going out to the porch. Maren had been six. Everyone said her mother had left, run off, gone to the city or a man or whatever story grown-ups told children when the truth had too many teeth.

But Maren had heard the well her whole life. She'd heard it before the crew ever broke the stone door, before Petra walked down into the dark with her flashlight and never came back up. She'd heard it through the floor of the farmhouse at night, a slow breathing, in and out, patient as winter. And once—only once, the week after her mother vanished—she'd heard it say a name.

*Elke.*

Her mother's name, spoken in her mother's voice, from underneath the kitchen.

So Maren knew. Knew the way you know where your own hands are in the dark. Her mother wasn't in the city. Her mother was down there, behind one of the doors, counting, waiting to be finished—or waiting to be found. And every night the ledger on Quentin's table grew another letter, and every night the harvest moon got fatter over the water tower, and every night Tomas counted lower, and nobody—not Quentin, not Grandma Ilse, not the police who'd given up and gone home—nobody was going to fix it in time.

Unless somebody paid.

She'd heard them talking through the motel wall. Quentin's voice, tired and flat: *the well must be offered someone willingly or it reaches for everyone.* Grandma Ilse's voice, older and rustier: *that's how it's always worked, boy. A tithe freely given. That's the covenant.* And then a long silence, the kind of silence that has a plan inside it.

Quentin was going to offer himself. Maren had watched him all week—the way he wrote his own name at the top of every page of his logs, pressing hard, like he was making sure the paper remembered him. He was getting ready to be erased. He'd already started saying goodbye without saying it, leaving little instructions everywhere, teaching Maren the chalk system, showing Tomas how to check the porch light. He was wrapping up his life like a man packing a suitcase.

It wasn't fair. It also wasn't necessary.

Because Maren had done the arithmetic too, and her math was better. Quentin had a whole life—a name people knew, a business with a sign on the truck, a third-generation reputation. Maren had eleven years, half a shoebox of drawings, and a mother down a hole. If the well wanted a name freely given, it could have hers. It had been wanting hers since she was six years old anyway. She was just finally going to stop making it wait.

She waited until Tomas's whispering slowed to *"...two..."* and then stopped altogether. She waited until the parking lot light outside buzzed its long buzz and settled. She waited until 3:07 by the clock radio, because she'd learned the schedule the way she'd learned everything else about this place: by watching.

At 3:11, the porch light would come on. Not the motel's porch light—they didn't have one—but the Hollowmere farmhouse's, four miles away, and lately, impossibly, the lamp post at the edge of the motel lot too, burning for something unseen, keeping watch, keeping count. 3:11 was when the well opened its eye. If you were going to knock, you knocked when it was listening.

Maren slid out of bed. Her sneakers were under the chair where she'd put them on purpose, hours ago, so she wouldn't have to grope around in the dark and wake anyone. She took the room key off the dresser. She took, after a moment's hesitation, the stub of red chalk from her jacket pocket, because you didn't go anywhere near the count without chalk. That was rule one, and Quentin had made her write it out.

She looked back at Tomas once. His face was slack and young in the streetlight glow, his lips still moving faintly around numbers. *I'm doing this so you get to grow up,* she told him silently. *So you get to be ordinary. So you never have to hear it breathing under your floor.* Then she eased the door open—past the thin cold door marked 14, which exhaled against her cheek like something sniffing—and slipped out.

* * *

The drive back to Hollowmere took eleven minutes in Quentin's borrowed pickup, which Maren was not licensed to drive and which she handled with the grim competence of a farm kid who'd been steering things since she could see over a dashboard. The headlights carved a tunnel out of the dark. Behind her, in the mirror, the motel shrank to a smudge of yellow light—one lamp burning in an empty parking lot, staying lit, keeping watch over a family that had fled it.

The moon was wrong tonight. She'd heard Grandma Ilse say it: *too big, too soon, sitting low like it's leaning in to listen.* It hung over the fields fat and orange, eight nights early and already swollen, dragging the tide of something up out of the ground.

The farmhouse rose out of the dark the way it always did now—windows dark except one, the porch light blazing at 3:11 exactly, though no hand touched any switch. Maren parked behind the excavation trench, where the county's orange fencing sagged around the hole in the earth like a bandage that had given up. The generator still hummed, feeding the floodlights strung down into the shaft. Somebody had meant to come back and shut it all down. Nobody had. The lights burned all the way down into the stone throat of the property, and at the bottom, the well mouth waited, open, breathing.

Maren stood at the trench edge and felt it feel her.

That was the only way to describe it. Not hearing, not seeing—*feeling being felt*, the way you feel a hand hover just above the hairs of your arm. The cold air came up in slow tides, in and out, and each exhale carried the smell of deep water and old stone and, underneath, faintly, bread and bleach.

Her eyes stung. She wiped them with her sleeve and got out the chalk.

"I know you're there," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected, and she was grateful, because she'd rehearsed this in the truck and in the rehearsal her voice had wobbled like a wet rope. "You've been there my whole life. Under the house. I used to put my ear to the kitchen floor and listen to you breathe."

The exhale came again, longer this time. Attentive.

"You took my mom." The wobble arrived after all, right on schedule. She let it. "Elke Hollowmere. You wrote her down and scratched out the end of her name and you kept her. I want—I want to make a deal."

She knelt at the shaft mouth, where the dressed stone gave way to the older, rougher cut of the original well, and she began to write. In careful block letters, in red chalk, on the stone lip:

**MAREN**

One name. Freely given. No scratching required.

"I give it," she said aloud. "My name. Maren. I give it willing. You can have me instead of—" instead of everyone, instead of Tomas, instead of the man who'd spent a week teaching a child to number doors so she could survive without him. "Instead of the rest. That's the covenant, right? That's what Grandma said. A tithe, freely given. Well." She swallowed. "Here I am. Freely."

The cold breath stopped.

For three full seconds the well held its air, and in that held silence Maren heard—not her mother, not yet, but something turning over, vast and slow, considering. Like a page being smoothed flat. Like a pen being uncapped.

Then a voice came up the shaft, and it was her own voice, aged maybe ten seconds into the future, saying: *"Freely?"*

"Freely," Maren said.

*"And gladly?"*

And here Maren made her mistake, small and human and fatal in the way all small mistakes are fatal near open wells: she hesitated. Because the truth was no, not gladly, she was scared down to the marrow, she wanted her mom but she wanted her mom *up here*, alive, humming over laundry, and the well heard the hesitation the way it heard everything, and it leaned closer—

"—and *gladly*, I think not."

The voice came from behind her. Human, hoarse, furious, and terrified.

Maren spun. Quentin Eriksen stood at the fence line in his coat over his pajamas, work boots unlaced, chest heaving. His hair stuck up on one side. In his fist he clutched the ledger, and in his other hand, absurdly, a travel mug of coffee sloshing with every step.

"How—" Maren started.

"The lamp in the lot came on at 3:11," Quentin said, walking fast now, gravel crunching, "and your bed was empty, and your shoes were gone, and there is exactly one place on God's green earth a Hollowmere child goes at 3:11 alone." He reached the trench edge, saw the red chalk letters, and went white. "Oh, kid. Oh, you didn't."

"It's done," Maren said, chin up. "I gave it. It's mine to give."

"It is *not*—" Quentin crouched, scrubbing at the chalk with his thumb, but the letters had sunk into the stone, red going brown going black, drinking in. He stared at them, and Maren watched a man do terrible arithmetic in real time. "A freely given name. It doesn't matter if you take the chalk away. It heard the intent. It heard the—" He stopped. Pressed both hands over his face. When he lowered them, his eyes were wet and his voice was very quiet. "Maren. Why would you do this?"

"Because it has my mom." It burst out of her, eleven years of it. "Because you were going to do it yourself and that's stupid, you have a whole life, you have a *truck* with your *name on it*, and all I have is a shoebox, and she's down there, Mr. Eriksen, she's been down there since I was six and everybody said she ran away and I *heard her*, I heard her say her own name through the kitchen floor, and if there's a door down there with her behind it then I want—" Her voice cracked clean through. "I just want to see her. Is that so much? Everybody else gets to keep their people. Why does our family have to pay forever?"

Quentin sat down on the trench edge next to her. Not grabbing, not scolding. Just sitting, the way you sit with a frightened animal, and after a moment Maren sat too, legs dangling over the dark.

"Your grandma told me about your mother," he said at last. "Ilse. She told me Elke heard it too, as a girl. Same as you. Same as probably every Hollowmere daughter back to 1847." He turned the ledger over in his hands. "You know what's in here? Names. Every household. Every tithe. And every entry ends mid-name, letters scratched out, because the well takes the end of you first—it takes the part that finishes sentences, the part that signs things. Your mother's entry is in here. I've read it a hundred times looking for a loophole." He paused. "There isn't one. There's just the price, and who pays it."

"Then let me pay it."

"No." Gentle and absolute, like a door closing. "Here's the thing about prices, Maren, the thing nobody tells you until it's too late: survival's cost is never your own name. It's always somebody else's. Every person who's ever gotten free of this thing got free because another name went down instead. That's the whole rotten engine. Your grandma lived because her sister didn't. I'd live because you didn't. And you'd go down there and find your mother, and you know what you'd find? A voice behind a door, counting, that *sounds* like her. That remembers being her the way a coat remembers shoulders." His jaw worked. "Petra's down there right now. Alive. Translucent. Counting backward and begging me not to say her name. That's the inheritance. That's what 'seeing her' means."

Maren stared down into the floodlit dark. The well breathed. Somewhere below, faintly, unmistakably, a voice was counting. *"...seven... six..."*

"That's not her," Quentin said softly. "Or it's not *only* her. And the thing that took her wants you to believe it is, because grief is the best bait there is. It used Petra's brother. It's using yours—" he nodded at the direction of the motel, at Tomas whispering numbers in his sleep, "—and it tried to use yours. It almost worked."

"I wrote my name," Maren whispered. "It heard me."

"Yeah." Quentin rubbed the back of his neck. "Yeah, it did. And we're going to deal with that. But you're coming back to the motel first, and you're going to drink the worst hot chocolate of your life, and tomorrow you're going to help me chalk doors, because that's the job. That's the whole job now. We keep the count until moonrise, and then—" He stopped himself. Maren noticed. Eleven years old, and she noticed.

"And then what?"

"And then I handle it."

They climbed out of the trench together. Quentin kept a hand on her shoulder the whole walk to the truck, and Maren let him, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left to say that the well wasn't listening to.

It was only later—at the motel, hot chocolate steaming, Tomas asleep, the thin door marked 14 breathing cold in the corner—that Quentin opened the ledger to check the damage. To see whether intent, uncompleted, had been enough.

The last page had changed.

Below D-A-L-E, in fresh ink that glistened like it was still wet, a new entry had begun. One letter only, tall and careful:

**M**

Quentin closed the book slowly. Across the room, Maren was asleep at last, and the lamp in the empty parking lot burned on, and down four miles of dark earth, something patient began, very softly, to count.
