## 8. The First Verse

I've always worked better alone. This isn't a personality quirk so much as a professional philosophy—pests don't care how you talk to them, and clients only want to hear two sentences: what it is, and when it'll be gone. Everything else is noise. I built a whole life out of minimizing noise.

So when Marisol left me alone in the east wing that morning with a thermos of coffee and a warning—"don't sing"—I filed it under *eccentric client* and got on with my work.

*Don't sing.* Like I was going to break into show tunes over a crawlspace. Like I was going to stand on a ladder in a dead woman's bedroom and belt something from the radio. Marisol Vane had a way of saying things that sounded like superstition and sat in your chest like instruction. I'd been ignoring them for a week. I was getting good at it.

The east wing was the part of Gideon House nobody had bothered to renovate since roughly the invention of electricity. Wallpaper the color of weak tea, water stains shaped like countries that no longer existed, and a smell I'd come to think of as *old house*—dust and plaster and something underneath, faintly sweet, like flowers kept too long in a closed room. My job that day was the ceiling voids. Something had been moving up there at night, according to Dee, who had rewired half the wing and did not appreciate things moving above her head while she worked. Dee expressed this the way she expressed everything: "If it's in my ceiling again, I'm electrifying the whole grid and calling it pest control."

I'd told her that was a health code violation. She'd told me so was the ceiling.

So there I was, at seven in the morning, head through a hatch in the upstairs hallway, headlamp on, flashlight in my teeth, running my inspection camera along joists that hadn't seen daylight—or inspection—since before either. Dust everywhere. Old wiring, the cloth-insulated kind. A few desiccated mouse nests, long abandoned. And no sign of the thing that had been making the noise. No droppings. No gnaw marks. No tracks in the dust except, when I checked the joists with the camera, a line of small smudges that could have been anything and that I was choosing, professionally, to believe were anything.

I was worming the camera around a corner when I started humming.

It wasn't a song I knew. That's the thing I want to be clear about, because later—well. Later. It was just a tune, five or six notes, going up and coming back down, the kind of sound a person makes when their hands are busy and their mouth is empty and nobody's listening. I hum when I work the way other people crack their knuckles. It fills the silence without inviting anyone in. I've hummed in a hundred crawlspaces and attics and dropped ceilings, and in a hundred of them, nothing has ever answered.

The house hummed back.

I stopped. The camera's little light glowed green on a joist. The hum continued—my five or six notes, up and back down, but lower, resonant, like it was coming up through the floor, through the walls, through the soles of my boots and the bones of my knees. Not an echo. Echoes are thinner than the original. This was *fuller*. This was my tune played on an instrument the size of the building.

"Very funny," I said, to the ceiling, to Dee somewhere below, to whoever.

The house did not laugh. The house held the last note a moment longer than I had, and let it go, gently, the way you set down something you don't want to wake.

I told myself: pipes. Old houses have pipes, and pipes carry sound from the boiler, and boilers hum, and a hum through a pipe can pick up harmonics from— I don't know. From anything. From my own voice vibrating the plaster. There's always an explanation. That's the whole job. Every haunting is a raccoon until proven otherwise, and even then, it's usually a raccoon.

I went back to work. I did not hum.

But here's the thing about a tune: once it's in you, it wants out. Twenty minutes later, crouched in the nursery hallway checking a vent cover, I caught myself doing it again. Same five notes. Up and back down. I clamped my mouth shut on the fourth note, and the house—patient, indulgent—sang the fifth for me.

I sat down on the hallway floor. The floorboards were cold through my coveralls. Down at the far end of the hall, the tall mirror outside the nursery door showed me a small figure in white sitting on the floor of a dim hallway, and I didn't look at it directly, because I'd made a rule about the mirrors, the way I'd made rules about the doors and the prints and the sound of scratching that came from inside walls with no cavity behind them. My rules were simple: don't check, don't count twice, don't look directly. A person can live a long time inside rules like that. I had. It was practically a career.

The house hummed the tune again. All six notes this time, complete, unhurried. And then it did something worse.

It harmonized.

A third above me—or below, I couldn't tell—warm and close, the way a second voice sounds in a small room when the other person is *near*. Not in the walls. Not in the pipes. Near. The kind of harmony you learn by singing with someone across a kitchen table for years, the kind that means somebody knows the song the same way you do.

"Stop it," I said. My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "I'm working."

The harmony softened, but it didn't stop. It just made room for me, the way a duet makes room, the way a hand makes room for another hand. And I want to tell you I got up and walked out of that wing and called Marisol and said the job's bigger than me, call a priest, call a demolitions expert, call anyone. That's what a smarter person would have done.

What I did was hum the next line.

I didn't decide to. That's important too. My mouth opened and the tune went on, a phrase I had never heard and knew completely, the way you know the shape of your own name, and the house came in underneath me like a floor, and for about eight bars—eight bars, maybe ten seconds—the loneliest building I've ever worked in felt like the only place in the world with anyone in it.

Then the pests went quiet.

I'd been hearing them all week, faintly, at the edges: the scratching in the walls, the skitter in the ceiling voids, the soft persistent *presence* of many small things living where they shouldn't. Dee said the noise was worst at night. I'd heard it at dusk, at dawn, in the gaps between Dee's radio and Tomas's trowel. It was the one thing about Gideon House that behaved like a normal job. Pests make noise. That's what pests do.

They stopped. All of them. Everywhere, all at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in.

I stood in the hallway with my flashlight and my camera and my heart going like a trapped bird, and I listened to a silence so complete I could hear the dust settling, and I understood—I understood the way you understand things at three in the morning, totally and uselessly—that they weren't gone.

They were listening.

* * *

I found Marisol in the kitchen, which had become the household's base camp the way kitchens do. Dee was at the table rewiring a lamp with the calm of a bomb technician, and Tomas was at the sink washing soil off his hands even though he'd just come in from the greenhouse and would go back out in ten minutes and get dirty again. It was, I'd noticed, a ritual. Everything in this house that looked like a habit was a ritual, and everything that looked like a ritual was a defense.

Marisol was reading a letter with the particular stillness of someone reading bad news. She looked up when I came in, and whatever was on my face made her put the letter down.

"You sang," she said.

"I hummed. There's a difference."

"Not in this house." She said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. Dee's soldering iron paused mid-air. Tomas turned off the tap.

"It's not a big deal," I said. "I hum when I work. It's a— it's a thing. It doesn't mean anything."

"Did it hum back?"

The kitchen was very quiet. The old fridge cycled on, thought better of it, and cycled off again, as if it too had decided to listen.

"It harmonized," I said, because lying to Marisol Vane in her own kitchen seemed suddenly like a bad survival strategy. "Then it—kept going. It knew the next part. I didn't know the next part. It knew it *for* me." I pulled out a chair but didn't sit. "And then the pests stopped. All of them. At once. Like someone put a finger to their lips."

Marisol looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide something. I was getting familiar with that look. She did it every time she had to choose between protecting me and informing me, and I was starting to suspect the information was always going to win, because she'd learned the hard way what happened to people she didn't inform.

"That's the song," she said. "Or the start of it. The house has been trying to teach it to you since the day you walked in. The humming is just—it's how it starts. It starts with something small. Something you don't notice yourself doing."

"I noticed."

"You noticed after." She folded her hands on the table. "My mother used to hum it. She said she couldn't help it. She said it felt like remembering a song rather than learning one. She said—" Marisol stopped. Tomas, drying his hands, made a small sound that wasn't quite a word, and she took a breath and finished: "She said it felt like belonging. That was the word she used. She wrote it in a letter I have upstairs. *It feels like belonging, and that's how you know.*"

"Know what?"

"That it's working."

Dee set down the soldering iron. "Okay, so we're saying it out loud now," she said. "Good. Great. Casper. The house isn't haunted. Haunted houses want you gone. This house wants you *to stay*. There's a difference, and the difference is that a haunted house you can run from."

"Run where?" Tomas said mildly, to the window. "It's been finding people for two hundred years."

"Thank you, Tomas, very comforting."

I stood in that warm kitchen with its lamp under repair and its kettle and its three people who had, over a week of coffee and door-counting, become something I didn't have a word for yet, and I felt the thing I'd felt in the hallway. The harmony. The made-room-for. And underneath it, cold, the other thing: the silence of every pest in the building, listening to me sing.

"Why does it want me?" I asked. "I'm nobody. I'm the exterminator. I'm supposed to leave when the job's done."

Marisol's face did something complicated. "Casper—"

"Don't." I put up a hand. "Don't do the gentle voice. Just tell me later, when you've decided I can take it. That's your system, right? You ration the truth. Feed the house just enough, feed me just enough." I heard how it sounded as soon as I said it, and I didn't take it back, because it was true, and because I was scared, and because scared people say true things in ugly voices.

Marisol took it without flinching. That was the thing about her; she'd been absorbing worse for longer. "Fair," she said. "But I'll tell you this much now, because you hummed and the house answered, and you deserve to know what answered. It's not a haunting. It's a *feeding*. And it doesn't pick the loud ones or the brave ones. It picks—" she hesitated, and I watched her decide again, and this time she didn't ration— "it picks the ones nobody would miss. The quiet ones. The ones who've practiced being small. It can teach anyone the song. But it can only *keep* someone who's already halfway to being no one at all."

The kettle clicked off. Nobody moved to pour it.

I thought about my rules. Don't check, don't count twice, don't look directly. I thought about a lifetime of entering rooms sideways, of making my voice a tool and nothing more, of being so easy to overlook that I'd started to wonder, some nights, whether I'd still show up in mirrors if I stopped checking.

The house had been humming to me all week. And I had been humming back.

"Okay," I said. "So I don't sing. I don't hum. I work in silence. I wear earplugs. I mouth the words to nothing."

"That'll help," Marisol said.

I heard the *but* she didn't say. So did she. She looked at the table.

"It'll help," she said again, "for a while."

* * *

That night I stayed late—overtime, I told myself, though the invoice was going to be creative—and I worked the west wing in strict silence, earplugs in, mouth shut, humming suppressed with the discipline of a monk. The house let me. That was the unnerving part. No hum in the pipes, no harmony in the plaster, no fifth note offered when I clamped down on the fourth. It was almost respectful. It was the silence of something that had made its point and could afford to wait.

Around eleven, packing up, I passed the tall mirror at the end of the nursery hallway. I kept my rule and didn't look directly. But in the edge of my vision, the way you see a thing without seeing it, I registered the figure in the glass—my figure, my posture, my headlamp still on—and I registered that its mouth was moving.

Not lagging. Not a beat behind. *Singing.*

I stopped. The figure in the mirror stopped. We stood there, the two of us, in a hallway that smelled faintly of flowers kept too long, and I broke my own rule and looked directly, and the reflection looked directly back, and it wasn't doing anything strange at all. Just standing. Just me. Mouth closed. Headlamp off—I flicked it off, and the reflection flicked its off a half-second later, which was correct, which was *late*, the way a reflection is supposed to be.

I told myself: fatigue. I told myself: earplugs, eleven o'clock, an old house, a long day. I told myself the story pest controllers tell themselves in old houses, the one that gets them home and paid and back again next week.

And then, from somewhere above me—up in the ceiling voids, up where the nests were, up where all those small listening things had gone silent at noon—something began, very softly, to hum the first verse.

And every small thing in the walls began, very softly, to sing along.
