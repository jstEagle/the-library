## 11. Lights On

People think electricians deal in power. We don't. We deal in the absence of it, mostly, and in the small ceremonies we perform to keep that absence at bay. I've been keeping the lights on at Gideon House for eleven years, and if you want to know the truth of the job, it's this: I am not an electrician. I am a night watchman whose weapon happens to be a voltage tester.

My name is Dee Okonkwo, and I have rewired this house every single night for eleven years, and no, that is not a figure of speech, and yes, I do mean every night, including the Christmas my mother was in hospital, including the night my truck slid into the ditch on the ridge road and I walked the last two miles with my tool bag over my shoulder like some kind of demented postman. You don't miss nights here. You can miss a lot of things at Gideon House. You can miss a payment, a birthday, a funeral. You don't miss a night.

Because the dark isn't empty here. The dark is a medium. And the lag — the thing in the mirrors, the thing in the walls, the thing we don't name at the table because names are how it gets a grip — travels through darkness the way damp travels through plaster. Room to room. Slow, if you're diligent. Fast, if you get lazy. I have seen it take a whole wing in one bad night, back in '19, when the storm knocked the ridge road out and I couldn't get through until morning. I walked in at six a.m. to find the east corridor mirrors showing a hallway that didn't exist, lagging three seconds behind, and the wallpaper breathing. Not metaphorically. I put my hand flat against it and it rose and fell like the flank of something sleeping.

I spent that whole day replacing fixtures. By dusk the wing was down to one and a half seconds and the wallpaper had stopped breathing, and Marisol made me soup, and neither of us mentioned it, because that's the other thing about this house. We don't talk about the war. We just fight it.

* * *

Casper came to dinner on a Thursday, which I remember because Thursdays are when Tomas does the braised thing with the cheap cut of beef that he pretends is a family recipe but is actually just what was on special in 1987 and never left the rotation. They sat at the table like a cat that's been invited onto a sofa — present, alert, deeply suspicious of the arrangement, one paw ready to retreat at any moment.

I've met a lot of their kind over the years. Not pest controllers — I mean people who've made themselves into a keyhole. Narrow enough that the world can't quite get a purchase on them. They sit at the edge of rooms. They answer questions with questions. They have a way of being helpful that is actually a form of hiding, because if you're useful enough, nobody looks at you closely enough to see that there's not much of you left to look at.

I know because I was one, once. Before the house. Before Marisol found me sleeping in my van in the estate's back lot in February and didn't call the police, just knocked on the window and said, "Can you do breaker panels?" and that was that. Love, in this house, doesn't look like love. It looks like triage. You see someone bleeding out and you don't ask about their childhood. You apply pressure.

So when Casper said, "I should probably head out, I've got an early call tomorrow," I did what I always do with the new ones. I said, "Help me with the fixtures first."

They blinked. "It's — what time is it?"

"Seven-ish. I do my rounds at seven. Every night." I tossed them the spare headlamp off the hook by the pantry door. "You want to understand this house, you walk the rounds with me. Marisol can tell you the history. Tomas can tell you the gossip. I'll tell you the truth."

They put on the headlamp. That, more than anything, told me they were already halfway in. People who are planning to leave don't put on the headlamp.

* * *

Here's how the rounds work, and I'll write it down here even though we've never written it down, because if you're reading this, Casper, then you're one of us now and you need it in your bones and not just your notebook.

Every room with a mirror gets checked first. Not the mirror — the light. The lag feeds on dimness the way mold feeds on damp. A room at full brightness holds it at one second, maybe a hair under. Let the room go twilight and the lag stretches. Let it go full dark and the lag doesn't just stretch — it *spreads*, out through the doorframe, down the hall, into the next room's fixtures, so that when you flip that switch the next morning, the light comes on and the room comes back a half-beat late, like a dancer who's lost the count.

So we run the house bright. Garishly, wastefully, environmentally irresponsibly bright. I've got the whole place on a grid I built myself, independent of the mains, because the mains go down in every storm and I don't trust anything I can't fix with my own hands. Forty-one fixtures on my personal circuit, plus the emergency strips in the corridors, plus the candle protocol for when everything fails — and it fails, maybe twice a year, and those are the nights we all sleep in the kitchen together, all four of us and whatever guests we've got, in one bright room, like survivors of something, which is what we are.

"Forty-one fixtures," Casper said, as we climbed toward the east bedrooms. They'd been counting. Of course they'd been counting. I'd watched them do it at dinner — the doors, the windows, the exits, that little tick of the eyes that people like us perform the way other people check their phones. "That's a lot of electricity for an empty house."

"It's not empty."

"No," they said, and didn't elaborate, and I liked them a little for not elaborating. People who elaborate are usually lying. People who don't are usually paying attention.

I stopped at the first mirror — the long cheval glass in the upstairs landing, the one with the crack running through it like a river delta. "Watch this." I hit the dimmer and took the room down to candle-glow. "Count with me. Look at my reflection. I'm going to raise my hand."

I raised my hand. The reflection raised its hand a beat later. One second. Maybe one and a quarter. In the dimness, the gap had a texture to it — not empty, exactly. *Occupied.* The second between me and my reflection is not vacant space. It's a hallway, and something walks down it every time, checking.

Casper's jaw did the thing jaws do. "Okay," they said, in the voice of a professional cataloguing a symptom. "That's a — that's a delayed reflection. Could be a trick of the low light, perceptual lag, the brain fills in—"

"Raise your hand again and then stop. Fast."

They did. And I watched them watch their reflection keep moving after they'd stopped — the hand still rising, the head still turning, finishing the gesture they'd abandoned, like an echo that hadn't got the memo. The headlamp lit the side of their face and I saw them go very still, the stillness of prey, and then very deliberately relax, the stillness of prey that has decided to be a professional instead.

"Fatigue," they said.

"Sure," I said. I turned the dimmer back up. The reflection snapped into sync so fast it was almost worse, like a hand letting go. "Fatigue. And the wallpaper in the east wing breathes. And the twelfth door—"

I stopped. I shouldn't have said that. Marisol would've told me off. You don't give the new ones the door count on night one. You let them find it themselves, or better, you hope they leave before they have to.

But Casper's head came around like a compass finding north. "Twelve?"

"Forget it."

"There were eleven doors," they said slowly. "I counted. Yesterday. Eleven doors on my walkthrough, and I count everything, it's — I count things." They said it the way people confess things in the dark, which is to say, to the dark, not to me. "This morning there were twelve."

The landing was very bright and very quiet. Downstairs, faintly, Tomas was running water over dishes, and the sound of it was the most human thing in the world and I held onto it like a rail.

"Yeah," I said. "There were."

* * *

We finished the rounds. I showed them the junction box in the cellar that hums when the lag gets hungry — not electrical hum, a hum with a pitch to it, and if you're ever here when the hum finds a melody, you run. I showed them the corridor strips on their separate battery loop. I showed them the candle protocol box in the kitchen: two hundred candles, five lighters, one laminated card in Marisol's handwriting that says *STAY TOGETHER, STAY LIT, DO NOT HUM.*

Casper read that last line twice. "Do not hum."

"It's a long story."

"I've got time."

Everyone's got time, right up until they don't. But there's a thing that happens in this house after midnight, when the rounds are done and the house is bright and the two of you are standing in a kitchen that smells like Tomas's coffee, and the truth comes up out of you like water out of a drilled well. Marisol calls it the house's honesty hour. I call it what it is: you can't carry it alone and you stop pretending you can.

"His name was Rufus," I said.

Casper didn't ask who. They knew. That's the thing about people who count doors. They already know what's missing.

"Rufus Okafor. Came on in 2016, same way most of us do — Marisol found him, put a tool in his hand, gave him a room. He was a plumber. Good one. Funny — you know, the funny that's load-bearing, the kind that keeps a whole crew's roof up. He thought the mirrors were a laugh. Thought the lag was a laugh. He'd wave at his reflection and go, 'Evening, handsome,' and the reflection would wave back a second late and he'd crack himself up every time. Every time. Eleven years I've done this and I never once found it funny, and he found it funny for eight months."

I turned my mug a quarter turn on the counter. The kitchen lights were all on. All of them. I checked them without looking, the way you check a sleeping child.

"January. Cold snap, transformer on the ridge road went, whole valley dark, and the backup generator — my generator — threw a belt. I was forty minutes away getting the spare. Forty minutes. I told everyone: stay in the kitchen, keep the candles lit, do not go upstairs. And Rufus — " I made myself say it plainly, because we don't pretty it up here, prettying it up is how it gets in. "Rufus went upstairs. Not to do anything. He said, after — well, he didn't say, because there was no after. But the candles in the kitchen told the story. They'd burned down to nothing, all of them, and nobody had replaced them, which means nobody was in the kitchen to replace them, which means the whole household had drifted, one by one, the way you do, following a sound. And the sound was humming."

Casper had gone the color of the dish towels. "The song."

"The first verse. Just the first verse. It's a lullaby, you know. That's the filthy genius of it. It's *pretty.* You'd hum it. I'd hum it. It gets in your head like any earworm and you don't even know you're doing it until the walls are doing it with you." I drank the coffee, which had gone bitter and cold, and drank it anyway, because you don't waste anything in this house, not coffee, not light, not grief. "By the time I got back with the belt, the house was dark top to bottom. Every mirror in the place lagging four, five seconds. And Rufus was standing in the nursery — the nursery, Casper, the one with the score on the walls — and he was singing. Not humming. Singing. Full voice. Like he'd been doing it for hours. And the house was singing with him. Every pipe, every floorboard, every mirror. A whole choir of nothing."

"What happened to him?"

I looked at my hands. They're good hands. They've kept the lights on for eleven years and they couldn't keep them on for forty minutes in January of 2017, and I have done the arithmetic on that every night since, and the answer never comes out different.

"He's in the ductwork," I said. "Or he's in the mirrors. Or he's in the walls with the others, the ones Marisol calls the pests, the ones you were hired to clear out. He's whatever this house keeps of the people who sing. And here's the part I need you to hear, Casper, because you've got the look, you've got exactly the look, and I'd rather say it ugly now than say it over your empty bed later."

They waited.

"The house doesn't take you by force. That's the thing nobody believes until it's too late. It *courts.* It gives you what you're missing. For Rufus it was an audience — man had been funny his whole life for rooms that didn't laugh. For Tomas it was purpose, back when his hands were going and his garden was all he had. For me it was — " I shrugged. "Being needed. Being *essential.* You flip a switch and a thousand souls don't get eaten, how's that for mattering? And for you — " I looked at them straight, no kindness in it, because kindness would've been a lie and this house punishes lies. "I don't know you yet. But I know the type, because I was the type. People who make themselves small. People nobody sees. And I think this house has been looking for someone exactly like you for a very long time, and I think it's not haunting you, Casper. I think it's courting you. And I think the humming you're going to catch yourself doing — because you will, everyone does, it's a lullaby, it's *pretty* — I think when that happens, you come find me. You don't finish the tune. You don't see how it goes. You come find me, and I will drop whatever wire I'm holding, and we will turn on every light in this godforsaken house, and we will be loud and ugly and off-key until it lets go of you. That's the deal. That's the whole deal. That's triage."

The kitchen clock ticked. Down the hall, one of the corridor strips flickered — a stutter, half a second, nothing, everything — and I was moving before I'd decided to move, screwdriver out of the bag, and Casper followed me at a jog, and I noted that too. They followed. People who are planning to leave don't follow.

It was nothing. Loose ballast, ten minutes' work, Casper holding the light steady without being asked, their shadow and mine overlapping on the wall into one shape. But as I tightened the last screw, Casper said, quietly, in the voice of someone reading their own sentence:

"Dee. The humming. When did it start for you? Before Rufus, I mean. When did you first catch yourself doing it?"

I sat back on my heels. And I'll be honest, because the honesty hour doesn't end just because the work does.

"Night one," I said. "Before I even knew there was a song. I caught myself humming in the van on the drive home, some tune I didn't know, and it felt — " I turned my mug a quarter turn. "It felt like being sung to. It felt like someone had finally noticed me."

Casper nodded slowly. And then, because they're one of us now, whether they've signed anything or not, they said the thing that turned my blood to well water:

"It started for me on day one. And Dee — last night, when I was doing the walkthrough — I counted twelve doors." A breath. "But that's not what's bothering me. What's bothering me is that when I checked the new one, it was unlocked. And it was warm. Like something had just gone through it. Or like something was waiting on the other side for me to stop being afraid and open it all the way."

I stood up. I looked down the corridor, at all forty-one fixtures burning their stubborn, wasteful, life-saving bright, and at the dark pressing in from every window like a tide against glass.

"Then we'd better go look at it," I said, "before it gets tired of waiting."
