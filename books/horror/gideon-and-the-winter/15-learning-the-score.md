## 15. Learning the Score

The nursery had been locked for eleven years, which in Vane House time was roughly a long weekend.

I stood at the door with the key in my fist and my heart doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing. Casper waited beside me with their toolbox, because apparently that was how they processed everything—by carrying tools toward it. I'd seen them do it with the mirrors, with the ductwork, with the moment Dee told them about the caretaker who sang along. Toolbox first, feelings later. Or never, if the schedule was tight.

"You don't have to open it," Casper said.

"I know." I turned the key. "That's why I'm opening it."

The hinges made a sound like an old man clearing his throat before a difficult conversation. Cold air came out—not the dead-air cold of a closed room but something with intention behind it, the way a freezer breathes when you open it too fast. Inside, dust hung in the lamplight like it was waiting for instructions.

The nursery itself was almost disappointingly ordinary. A crib with its rail down, as if someone had just lifted a child out. A rocking chair worn pale on one armrest where a hand had gripped it for years. A window with frost ferns on the inside of the glass, even though it wasn't cold enough for frost anywhere else in the house. And then the walls.

The score ran all the way around the room at shoulder height, inked directly into the plaster in a brown-black hand that had gone thin and desperate by the end of each line. Musical notation, yes—but between the staves, words. Lullaby-simple words. *Hush now, hush now, winter's come to stay.* The kind of thing you'd sing without thinking, the kind of thing that gets into your mouth before you know you've opened it.

"It starts there," I said, pointing to the corner nearest the door. "Gideon wrote it. My great-great-whatever-times-a-lot. The story is his wife was dying in this room and he couldn't stand the quiet."

"And so he wrote her a song," Casper said flatly.

"So he wrote her a song, and she sang it, and she didn't die. Not exactly. Not in any way the doctor could put on paper." I walked the wall slowly, following the line of notes the way you'd follow a scar. "The house stayed warm that winter. The crops came in early. And every winter after, someone in the family sang it, and Gideon House stayed... fed."

"Fed," Casper repeated. They were looking at the wall the way they looked at infestations—cataloguing, assessing, refusing to flinch. "The song isn't a ward. It's not keeping him out."

"No."

"It's setting the table."

I laughed, and hated myself for it—a short, ugly sound, the laugh of someone who has been sitting with a truth so long it's grown comfortable. "Welcome to the family business."

* * *

We worked by lamplight because the overhead fixture in the nursery had been dead since 1987 and Dee refused to rewire a room that hummed back at her. Casper set up camp the way they did everything: efficiently, quietly, taking up as little space as possible. They unrolled their tools on a drop cloth, produced a notebook, a pencil sharpened to a needle point, and a thermos they offered me from without being asked.

I took the thermos. This was, I would later understand, a significant event in the history of Casper Zhou's emotional landscape, though at the time it just seemed like tea.

"The last verse," I said. "It's here." I led them to the far wall, past the crib, to the section where the handwriting changed. The earlier verses flowed in that careful brown ink, patient as embroidery. But the final verse had been scratched out—scored through so violently the plaster had gouged, deep parallel lines like claw marks, and beneath the gouging, fragments. Half-words. The ghost of letters showing through where the pen had pressed hard enough to emboss the wall before anyone thought to destroy what it wrote.

Casper crouched in front of it and went very still, the way they did when something was exactly as bad as they'd feared.

"Who scratched it out?" they asked.

"My grandmother, we think. Maybe her mother. Whoever got far enough into learning it to realize what it was." I crouched beside them. Our shoulders almost touched. I noticed that I didn't move away, and that Casper didn't either, and that both of these facts felt enormous and I decided to deal with neither of them. "Everyone who learned the whole song stopped singing. That's the pattern. You get to the last verse and you understand, and then you can't do it anymore, and the walls start filling up, and eventually someone younger and more frightened takes over."

"Generational whack-a-mole," Casper murmured. "With extra steps."

"Everything in this family is extra steps. We're very thorough about our doom."

They started copying the fragments into their notebook, and I watched them work, and I will be honest with you: part of me wanted them to fail. Part of me—the part that had kept this house limping along for eleven years on half-measures and guilt—wanted the verse to stay buried, because buried things can't ask anything of you. If we deciphered it, we'd know. And knowing, in this house, had always been the beginning of the bill coming due.

But Casper's pencil moved steadily, and their face held that particular expression I was starting to recognize—the one that meant they'd decided a problem could be solved if only they refused to leave the room—and I found my own hand rising to trace the embossed letters, reading them by touch the way my grandmother must have, the way grief gets passed down like a recipe nobody wants.

* * *

By midnight we had most of it.

We worked it out in pieces, cross-referencing the embossing against a copy of the score Dee had photographed years ago for insurance purposes—an act of bureaucratic paranoia that had accidentally preserved evidence. Casper transcribed; I filled in the gaps from memory, from the shape of the melody my mother used to hum when she thought no one was listening, from the way certain syllables fit certain notes the way a key fits a lock.

And the more we recovered, the quieter the room got.

Not silent—the nursery was never silent. There was always a faint pressure of sound behind the plaster, like a crowd holding its breath. But as the verse assembled itself under Casper's needle-point pencil, that held breath seemed to lean closer. The frost on the window crept a centimeter inward. The lamp dimmed twice, apologetically, and steadied.

"Read it," I said finally. "Out loud. I need—I can't read it first. I'll chicken out. Read it and I'll listen."

Casper looked at me for a long moment. Then they nodded, once, and picked up the notebook, and read:

"*Winter knows you, winter keeps you.*
*Winter says your name complete:*
*Bone and breath and all you bury,*
*all you love and all you eat—*
*Gideon gathers, Gideon gladdens,*
*Gideon calls you by your name,*
*and what he names, he holds forever,*
*and what he holds goes out like flame.*"

Silence. The real kind, for once.

"That's it," I said. My voice sounded like it belonged to someone much younger. "That's the whole—"

"Marisol." Casper's voice was gentle in a way that scared me more than the verse had. "There's more. Underneath. Look."

They turned the notebook around. Below the transcription, in smaller letters, they'd written the second layer—the words hidden inside the words, the ones the melody carried but the surface meaning concealed. The naming. The actual mechanism.

Because that was the thing. The verse didn't banish Gideon. It didn't bind him or ward him or send him back wherever he'd come from. It *completed* him. Every winter, the singer gave the song their name—not just the word, but the whole of it, the entire architecture of a self, every memory and habit and private grief, handed over note by note until nothing remained that was only theirs. The singer didn't die. Dying would have been merciful and simple. The singer was *absorbed*. Folded into the house the way flour folds into dough, indistinguishable, nourishing, gone.

The echoes in the ductwork. The nests of photographs and baby teeth. Those weren't the people who'd refused.

Those were the people who'd sung.

"Oh," I said. It was a small word for the size of the hole opening up under me. "Oh, God. My mother didn't run off. She didn't leave us. She's—" I gestured helplessly at the walls, at the whole rotten edifice of Gideon House, at the scratching sounds I'd been hearing since I was six years old and had been told were pipes. "She's in the *plaster*."

Casper reached over and put their hand on mine. Just that. No speech, no comfort-shaped platitudes. Their hand was cool and steady and slightly calloused from tool handles, and it was the most honest thing anyone had done in this house in a century.

"I'm sorry," they said.

"She knew," I whispered. "She must have known, near the end. She sang anyway. She sang to buy time for—for me, probably. For the household. She traded herself for another year of lights on and doors counted and everyone else alive." I laughed again, that same ugly laugh. "And I've spent eleven years resenting her for abandoning us."

"You didn't know."

"I *chose* not to know. There's a difference, and the difference is cowardice, and I've had eleven years to notice." I pulled my hand free—not because I didn't want theirs there, but because I needed to press both palms against the wall, against the gouged-out verse, against the place where my grandmother had tried to kill these words and failed. The plaster was cold. Behind it, faintly, something shifted its weight.

* * *

Here is what despair looks like in practice, in case you've wondered: it looks like sitting on the floor of a haunted nursery at one in the morning, drinking lukewarm tea, doing arithmetic.

"One singer per winter," I said. "That's the terms. One voice gives the verse its name, and the house eats well, and everyone else gets through till spring. Eleven years I've been feeding it scraps—humming, half-verses, enough to keep the walls quiet but never enough to satisfy it. That's why it's getting worse. That's why the twelfth door. That's why it's courting you." I looked at Casper across the lamplight. "It's starving, and it's decided you're the meal that lasts."

"Charming," Casper said. "I haven't been courted since high school, and that ended worse than this probably will."

"Casper."

"They're going to offer it to me, aren't they. Eventually. In whatever form offers take around here." They weren't joking anymore. They were looking at the wall with an expression I couldn't fully read—something past fear, something almost like recognition. "Permanence. Belonging. Never invisible again. All I have to do is stop being me." A pause. "Which, honestly, some days feels like a small price. That's the part that scares me. Not the trap. How reasonable the trap sounds."

And that—that was the moment the germ of it arrived.

I wish I could tell you it came as revelation, as lightning, as a choir of ancestors singing wisdom through the frost-furred glass. It didn't. It came the way ideas actually come: sideways, mid-sentence, while I was staring at the embossed letters trying to figure out why the naming worked at all.

"Why does it need a name?" I said.

Casper blinked. "What?"

"The verse. Why does naming matter? Gideon's not a demon with a contract clause. He's—he's a hunger wearing a house. What does he care about names?"

Casper sat forward slowly. I watched the same thought arrive in their face that had arrived in mine, two travelers meeting at a border neither expected to cross.

"Specificity," they said. "It's—it's like pest control. You don't bait a trap for 'a rodent.' You bait it for *this* rodent, *this* nest, *this* pattern of movement. The verse works because it's precise. It doesn't say *someone*. It says *you*, all the way down, every particular—" They stopped. Their eyes had gone wide and fixed on the middle distance, on some blueprint only they could see.

"If it named many," I breathed.

"If the verse was split—if every voice in the house carried a fragment—then the naming wouldn't hold anyone. It'd be diluted. Like—" Casper snapped their fingers, searching. "Like a current divided across too many wires. No single path carries enough load. He'd reach for the name and find it scattered across five, six, ten people, none of whom are giving him the whole of themselves. He'd starve reaching."

"He'd starve *trying*," I corrected, and my voice cracked on it, because hope in this house was a dangerous substance and I could feel it burning on the way down. "Or—or he'd unravel. Whatever happens when you feed a thing a name that belongs to no one alone."

The lamp flickered. The frost on the window had stopped creeping. For a moment—one suspended, impossible moment—the whole nursery felt like it was listening differently. Not leaning in to savor. Leaning in to *worry*.

Then the temperature dropped hard, fast, the way it does when the house remembers what it is, and somewhere below us a pipe knocked three times, deliberate, like a knock at a door. Or a warning. Or a landlord reminding tenants that the lease had terms.

"We'd need everyone," I said. "Not just us. Dee. Tomas. Everyone who counts the doors, everyone who keeps the lights on. They'd each have to carry a piece of the naming. Each of them would be handing Gideon a fragment of themselves—small, maybe survivable, but real. I can't ask that. I have no right to ask that."

Casper capped their pen. They looked tired and terrified and more present than I had ever seen them, this person who had built a whole life out of being overlooked, and when they spoke, their voice was quiet and absolutely level.

"You're not asking. I'm telling you it might work. Whether we ask them is a different problem." They stood, shouldering their bag, and for a second the lamplight caught their face and I saw how afraid they were and how they'd decided to carry it anyway. "Besides. Dee rewires a haunted house every night for free. Tomas talks to the ivy. These are not people who require a rational sales pitch."

I laughed—really laughed this time, wet and startled—and blew out the lamp.

Behind us, in the dark, the nursery began very softly to hum the first verse. Patient. Certain. Sure, still, that it would get its solo.

It had no idea we were planning to make it share.
