## 19. The Last Verse

Midnight came the way a debt collector comes: punctually, and with no intention of being polite about it.

We had gathered in the nursery, because it seemed right to end the song where it had been written. Dee had taped candles to every surface that would hold them—she'd run out of candleholders somewhere around the fourth mantelpiece and had started improvising with jar lids and, at one point, a hubcap. Tomas had brought a folding chair for Marisol and then, after a long look at the rest of us, three more. Nobody had asked him to. He'd just carried them up from the kitchen like a man setting a table for a family that had only just remembered it was one.

The score on the wall caught the candlelight and threw it back wrong. Ink that old shouldn't shine. But it did, faintly, the way a cat's eyes do when you've caught it watching you from a doorway.

"Before we start," Dee said, checking her watch, "I want it on the record that I hate this plan."

"It's on the record," Marisol said. "The record being you saying it every twenty minutes since six o'clock."

"Good. As long as it's on the record." Dee flexed her hands. Her knuckles cracked like small gunshots. "Because if this goes wrong, I want future archaeologists to know the electrician objected."

"Future archaeologists," Tomas said mildly, "will find four skeletons in a nursery holding hands, and they will write a very confused paper."

I laughed. It came out of me sideways, startled, and for a second everyone looked at me like I'd done a magic trick. Maybe I had. I'd spent thirty-odd years training myself out of laughing in rooms like this—rooms where you're the outsider, where laughter is a thing you earn or don't. But this room had stopped being like that somewhere around the second rehearsal, and I hadn't noticed the exact moment, which is how the important things always happen.

The house, meanwhile, had stopped pretending.

You could feel it in the walls—not scratching now, not the small busy sounds of the echoes, but a kind of pressure, like the whole building was inhaling and couldn't find the exhale. The frost on the windows had grown into shapes I'd stopped trying to name. Lungs, mostly. Lungs and ribcages and, in one corner of the glass, something that might have been a mouth if mouths were drawn by someone who had only ever had them described.

The pipes had been humming since sundown. Not the song yet—just the space where the song would go, the way an orchestra tunes before the conductor arrives. Every few minutes the pitch shifted, searching, and I understood, with the particular clarity that terror provides free of charge, that it was searching for me.

"Positions," Marisol said.

We stood in a rough crescent beneath the score. Marisol at the center, because it was her name in the plaster and her family's debt. Dee to her left, Tomas to her right. Me at the end of the crescent, where I could see the door.

Old habits. Even now, especially now, some part of me had mapped every exit, had already rehearsed the leaving. I put a hand on that part of myself the way you'd quiet a dog during a storm. *Not tonight,* I told it. *Tonight we stay.*

The candles guttered in unison.

"Oh, that's not ominous at all," Dee muttered.

And then the house began to sing.

* * *

I want to be accurate about this, because accuracy is all I have left by way of armor, and I'm told I should stop wearing armor, but old habits, etc.

It did not come through the pipes. The pipes had been the rehearsal. It came through everything—the plaster, the floorboards, the frost, the air itself, as if the house had finally admitted that its walls were only a throat it had built around a mouth. The first verse poured out in Gideon Vane's voice, and his voice was—this is the part I wasn't prepared for—*lovely*. Warm. A baritone like a banked fire, like a hand on your shoulder, like every kind voice that had ever made you drop your guard right before it asked you for something.

It sang the invitation verse. The one about coming in from the cold.

I felt it land in my chest and begin, gently, to rearrange the furniture. My mouth wanted to open. Not because I was possessed—I want to be clear, it wasn't a takeover, it was an *offer*, which is so much worse—because the song made staying quiet feel like standing outside a lit window in the snow, watching other people be warm. The first verse didn't command you to sing. It just made everything that wasn't singing feel like cold.

"Don't," Marisol said, through her teeth. She had her fists clenched at her sides, nails in her palms. "It starts with the invitation. It always starts with the invitation. That's how it got my great-aunt. That's how it got all of them. Nobody was dragged in. Everybody *came in*."

The second verse followed, seamless, the melody folding into itself like a hand closing. This one was about belonging. About how a voice alone is just noise, but a voice in a song is a home. I watched Dee sway, watched her catch herself, watched her plant her feet like she was grounding an electrical panel with her whole stubborn body.

"It's going for the third verse," Marisol said. "That's the lead. That's where it takes a singer. Casper—"

"I know." I did know. I could feel the third verse approaching the way you feel a wave approaching when you're standing in the surf—pressure first, then the lift, then the moment where the water decides what happens to you. The song was building a space in its own harmony, a shaped absence, a Casper-sized hole, and it was going to pour the third verse into me and my voice would go into it and that would be that. The house wouldn't have taken me. I'd have *joined*.

The candles all bent sideways at once, though there was no draft.

The third verse began.

* * *

Here is the thing about the third verse: it's a duet.

Nobody had told me that. Maybe nobody knew. But as the melody rose, I heard it clearly—the lead line reaching out, and underneath it a second line, waiting, empty, patient. A harmony part with my name on it, except it didn't even need my name. That was the horror of it. It didn't need to name me. It had spent weeks learning my shape. Every time I'd hummed at the ductwork. Every time the house had hummed back. It had been taking measurements this whole time, the way I used to take measurements of mouse holes, and now it had built a Casper-shaped rest in the music and all it had to do was wait.

My jaw loosened. My lungs filled.

And in the mirror over the nursery mantel—because of course there was a mirror, the house had made sure of that—I saw myself standing at the end of the crescent, and I saw the reflection open its mouth half a second before I did.

*Casper.* Gideon's voice, underneath the melody now, warm as a banked fire, patient as winter. *Casper, you don't have to be invisible. You only have to be the song.*

And God help me, some part of me—the part that had spent a lifetime being called "hey, you" and "the pest person" and "who?"—that part *leaned in*.

What stopped me wasn't courage. I want to be honest about that too. What stopped me was Dee's hand, closing around mine, rough and electrician-calloused and absolutely certain.

"Hey," she said, not loudly, over the rising song. "You're not the harmony. You're the whole chord."

Tomas took my other hand. Marisol took Dee's. And the crescent closed.

"NOW," Marisol shouted, "the LAST verse—ALL of us—"

We sang.

* * *

I cannot tell you we sang it well.

Tomas was flat. Dee came in late on every fourth line because she kept losing count. Marisol's voice cracked in the middle and she pushed through it sounding, for two bars, like a teenager again, like a girl who'd learned this song at her mother's knee and hated every note. And me—I sang like a person who had spent their whole life being quiet and had never once practiced the alternative. My voice came out rusty and thin and it shook, and none of that mattered.

Because here is what we had figured out in the lamplight, Marisol and I, hunched over the scratched-out verse like burglars cracking a safe: the final verse doesn't banish. It *names*. It takes a single voice and it names that voice so completely—every fear, every hiding place, every small surrender—that when the naming is done, there is no one left over to be anything else. Gideon hadn't built a prison. He'd built a description, and descriptions, once complete, leave no room for the thing described to be anything but described.

One voice, and the verse eats it whole.

But we were not one voice.

The naming began, and it scattered. Each line of the verse landed in a different mouth. Dee sang the fragment about *the one who keeps the lights against the dark*—and it named her, named her exactly, named the nights she'd rewired this house alone rather than let the lag spread—but she was only holding a fragment, and a fragment of a name is not a name, it's a nickname, and you can survive a nickname. Tomas carried *the one who tends what grows over the graves*, and it was true and it was his and it was not the whole of him, because the whole of him was also the folding chairs and the terrible jokes and the way he'd stayed forty years out of love rather than duty and had never once said so. Marisol took *the last of the line, the one who fed the house to spare it grief*—and I watched that line go into her and I watched her hold it the way you hold something hot, and she did not burn, because it was not all of her, because she was also the person who had invited a stranger to dinner and meant it.

And me. The verse handed me *the one who made themselves small enough to be taken*.

I felt it try to complete me. I felt the description reach for all my edges—the counting, the exits, the lifetime of "hey, you"—and I felt it start to close around me like a hand around a candle flame, and then—

—then Dee's voice came in under mine, carrying the fragment about the lights, and Tomas's under hers, and Marisol's under his, and the naming, which had been built for one throat, for one life, for one complete and total consumption, *stuttered*. It tried to gather me into a single name and found four voices, and then it tried to gather the four of us and found the echoes.

Because that was the last piece. That was the piece we hadn't planned, or maybe we had, maybe Marisol had known it all along and hadn't dared say it: the nursery door was open, and the walls were full of everyone who had ever refused, and they had been listening to us rehearse for three nights, and when the final verse began they did not wait to be invited.

They came in.

Not through the door. Through the *song*. The harmony that Gideon had built to hold one singer filled instead with dozens—hundreds—the small child-voiced ones whose fingerprints climbed the staircase, the great-aunt, the cousins, the ones from before the photographs, all of them carrying pieces of the naming, none of them carrying all of it. The verse that had been designed as a single devouring sentence became, in all those mouths, a chorus. A name so distributed that it belonged to no one. A description with no subject.

Gideon's voice tore.

* * *

I will try to describe what unraveling looks like, though I suspect I'll fail.

He came up out of the mirror first—the real him, the winter guest, the courteous farmer of souls—and he was exactly as Marisol had described: tall, patient, fond. And then the song reached him, the final verse in a hundred fragmentary mouths, and his fondness was the first thing to go. I watched it leave his face the way frost leaves a window when you breathe on it. Underneath the fondness was hunger. Underneath the hunger was something older and smaller and terribly tired, and it screamed.

It screamed a name.

I heard it—I think we all heard it—and the name was long, and it was complete, and it was *his*, every fear and every hiding place and every small surrender, the whole devouring description turned around and pointed at the describer, and no one voice was singing it, and that was the unbearable thing, because he had written it to be sung by one, and it was being sung by everyone, and a name that everyone owns is a name that no one owns, and he was coming apart at every seam the song touched.

The mirrors went off like lightbulbs, one after another, down the corridor behind the twelfth door, a receding line of white flashes, and in each one I glimpsed him—less each time, a coat, a collar, a voice, a hunger—and then the twelfth door itself slammed somewhere deep in the house with a sound like a book closing, and the cold went out of the air so fast my ears popped.

The song stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, mid-phrase, the way a machine stops when the power's cut, and the silence afterward was so complete I could hear candle wax dripping, could hear Tomas breathing, could hear my own heart doing something I'd never heard it do, which was just beating, calmly, like it belonged to someone who wasn't afraid.

Dee sat down on the floor. Not gracefully. She just—sat, right where she stood, like a puppet with a cut string, and started laughing, the unhinged cackling of a woman who has just sung the impossible and is going to need a minute.

"We were flat," Tomas said. "All of us. Terrible."

"The house was in tune," Marisol said. Her voice was wrecked, scraped raw, and she was crying, and she was smiling, and I didn't know a face could do both at once until hers did. "For two hundred years the house was the only thing in tune in this family. And we just sang it flat and off the count and it *lost*."

I looked down at my hands. They were shaking. I looked at the mirror over the mantel, half-expecting the lag, the delay, the other Casper still moving after I'd stopped.

The mirror showed the nursery. Showed it now. Showed it *first*—showed me my own face, tear-streaked and soot-smudged and visible, visible, visible, looking back at me in real time like a person, like a person standing in a room with people, like someone who had been named in fragments and therefore could not be erased.

"Hey," Dee said from the floor, looking up at me. "You okay, whole chord?"

I opened my mouth to say something wry, something deflecting, something small.

What came out instead was: "My name is Casper Zhou."

It echoed in the nursery. Just normally. Just the way sound echoes in an ordinary room.

And from somewhere below us—from the foyer, from the walls, from the eleven ordinary doors of an ordinary house—came a sound I had heard on my first day and never identified until now: not scratching, not climbing, not counting.

Applause. Small, child-sized hands, clapping, once, twice—and then, one by one, going quiet, the way a crowd goes quiet when the show is over and it's time to go home.

Marisol stood, wiped her face with her sleeve, and looked at the score on the wall, where the ink had stopped shining and was just ink again.

"Dawn's in a few hours," she said. "I don't know about any of you, but I'd like to see what the mirrors show us in the morning."

I would too. God, I would too. But as we stood there in the candlelight, four people and a nursery full of going-home echoes, I noticed something that kept the cold from entirely leaving my chest: the frost on the window, the frost shaped like lungs, was melting—and as it melted, it was running down the glass in rivulets that pooled, briefly, into letters.

Not a threat. Not a curse.

An address.

Somewhere far off, another house was still singing—and it had just lost its audience.
