## 1. The Client on Corliss Street

The Aldous house had been waiting for someone to notice it for a long time, and on the morning I came to kill its bedbugs, I was the last person in Harrow Glen who intended to.

I want to be honest about that, at least. Honesty is the one professional habit I've kept. Five years in this town and I could drive its grid with my eyes shut—could tell you which gutters bred mosquitoes in August, which restaurants had roaches behind the ice machines, which of the old Victorians on Delmar Street had carpenter ants eating their sills from the inside out, patient as bookkeepers. I knew Harrow Glen the way a coroner knows a body. Thoroughly, and without love.

The house at 14 Corliss Street sat at the end of its block like a tooth gone gray. Three stories, clapboard gone silver with weather, a porch roof that sagged in the middle the way a tired animal's back does. The other houses on Corliss had been painted within the last decade—cheerful blues, a yellow, one aggressive teal—but the Aldous house wore its original color, if it had ever had one, and its windows had that particular blankness of glass that hasn't been cleaned since the Nixon administration. It didn't look haunted. I want to be clear about that too. It looked *unwatched*, which is a different thing and, in my experience, worse.

The work order said VANE, M. — BEDBUGS — CHILD BITTEN. Two words in the notes field: *please hurry.*

I parked my truck at the curb, killed the engine, and sat for a moment the way I always do, listening to the tick of the cooling motor and telling myself the same lie I'd been telling myself since I took over Delgado's route: *it's just a house, it's just bugs, it's just a job.* In Harrow Glen, that lie was load-bearing. I propped it up every morning and it held every night, and in between I did my work and didn't ask the town anything it didn't want to answer, and the town did the same for me. We had an arrangement, Harrow Glen and I. It ignored me; I ignored it. It was the closest thing to peace I'd found since my mother died, and I guarded it like a woman guards the last unbroken thing she owns.

The woman who opened the door was maybe my age, dark-haired, with the particular hollowed look of a parent who hasn't slept in weeks. She had a coffee mug clutched in both hands like a handrail.

"You're the pest person," she said. Not a question. There was something in her voice—relief, but a thin, strained relief, like a rope that had already taken more weight than it was rated for.

"Paloma Nakamura. Glen Pest Solutions." I held up my license out of habit. She barely glanced at it. "You've got bites?"

"My daughter does. Come in. Please." She stepped back, and I noticed she didn't say the girl's name, and I noticed I noticed, and I told myself to stop noticing things. Noticing was not in my job description. Noticing was how you ended up knowing things about Harrow Glen, and knowing things was how you ended up like old Delgado, who'd retired at fifty with a tremor in his hands and a rule he repeated like a rosary: *Spray what you're paid to spray, Reyes. The rest of it isn't yours.*

The inside of the house was clean in a frantic way. Floors vacuumed to bare nap, surfaces bare, the furniture pulled away from the walls by six inches or more—all around every wall, in every room I could see, a clean channel of exposed baseboard. I'd seen that before. It's what bedbug prep looks like when a homeowner has done their homework and then some.

"You've been through this before," I said.

"Once. An apartment, years ago." She led me toward the stairs. "It's the girl's room. She won't sleep in it anymore. She sleeps on the couch now, with every light on."

"How old is she?"

"Nine."

"Name?"

Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice. "Lucia," she said, after a beat too long. "Her name is Lucia."

The bedroom at the top of the stairs was a little girl's room stripped for inspection: mattress standing on its side, dresser drawers pulled out and bagged, stuffed animals quarantined in a black plastic sack knotted like a throat. The bites, when the mother—Vane, Marisol Vane, according to the work order—turned back the sheet, were textbook. Three in a row. Breakfast, lunch, and dinner, the old techs called it. Linear welts along the inside of the girl's forearm, some older and browning, some new and angry.

I put on my nitrile gloves and did my work. I checked the mattress seams, the box spring's dust cover, the screw holes in the frame. I checked behind the outlet plates with my flashlight and a flat blade, checked the curtain rods, the closet's door track, the underside of the rocking chair. I checked the places nobody checks because nobody thinks a bug can think: inside the hollows of the bed's tubular frame, behind the headboard where the wall meets the wood, in the seam where the carpet met the tack strip.

Nothing. Not a casing, not a fecal spot, not a shed skin, not one living insect in a room that had been bitten like this for weeks.

"That's strange," I said, before I could stop myself.

"What's strange?" Marisol stood in the doorway, arms crossed, mug abandoned somewhere downstairs.

"Bedbugs don't do this." I gestured at the sheet, the welts it had held. "They feed where the host sleeps, but they leave evidence. They're not subtle. A room with bites like these has bugs in it, or had them, or the bites aren't bedbugs."

"Then what are they?"

I'd have liked to say *mosquitoes* or *fleas* or *an allergy*, something with a Latin name and a treatment schedule, something I could invoice. But I'd been doing this long enough to know what bites like those meant when the room was clean, and I said the true thing because it was cheaper than the lie: "Could be a lot of things. Could be nothing I can treat."

She looked at me then, really looked, and I saw that the strain in her face wasn't only sleeplessness. There was a question behind her eyes that she'd been carrying around the way you carry a stone in your shoe—too far now to take it out, too painful to keep walking on.

"Can I ask you something," she said, "and you answer me like a normal person and not like a company?"

"I'm not really a company. It's me and a truck."

"The answering man," she said. "Have you heard of him?"

The word landed in the room and just—stayed there. I made myself shrug. "No."

"Lucia says there's a man in the walls. Not a *man*, exactly, she says, a voice. And when she talks—when she talks to herself, the way kids do, when she's playing or she can't sleep—it answers her." Marisol's voice had gone flat, rehearsed. She'd said this before, to someone, and been given the look I was carefully not giving her now. "She asks questions. It answers. It gets them right. It knew—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again from a different angle, the way you'd reroute around a washed-out bridge. "I put my phone in her room to record, to prove to myself it was her talking in her sleep. Two voices on the recording. Hers, and one I've never heard before."

"Kids make up companions," I said. "It's developmentally normal. And phones pick up all kinds of—"

"It knew her grandmother's maiden name," Marisol said. "Lucia's never heard it. Neither have I, actually. It was on the recording, and I called my mother to check, and my mother cried."

The refrigerator in the kitchen downstairs cycled on with a clunk, and in the silence after it, I heard the house. That's the only way I can put it. Houses have sounds—settling wood, expanding pipes, the small tectonic business of a structure adjusting to the day's heat—and I've heard ten thousand of them, and I file them all under *nothing*. But this house's sounds were arranged. There was a rhythm to them, a spacing, like a sentence spoken very slowly by something with all the time in the world.

I told myself it was the furnace.

"I'll treat the room," I said. "Precautionary. And I'll dust the wall voids. If there's anything in the walls—mice, wasps, whatever—I'll find the entry points." I was already unpacking my gear as I said it, because work is a door and you can walk through it and be somewhere else for a while. Diatomaceous earth, the puffer duster, my inspection mirror. Professional detachment, my whole career in three tools.

I worked the baseboards first. That's when I found the scratches.

They were behind the baseboard in the northeast corner, where the board had pulled a quarter inch from the plaster—a gap wide enough to slide a business card into, and I did, and when I drew it out and angled my flashlight into the dark seam, I saw them. Fine scratches in the plaster itself, behind the board, where no hand could reasonably reach and no mouse could plausibly manage. Not gnaw marks. Not the random scoring of a trapped animal. They were *arranged*. Short strokes, grouped. Some crossed with a single deliberate line.

I've seen writing before, in this line of work. Kids scratch things into walls; so do angry tenants and bored teenagers and, once, a hoarder in a house on Pell Street who'd covered every surface with the same sentence about the county assessor. I know what human writing looks like.

This looked like human writing that had been done by someone with no room to move their hand. The strokes were tiny—smaller than my pinky nail—and dense, packed edge to edge, column after column of them climbing up inside the wall gap as far as my flashlight's beam could reach. And they were layered. Older marks beneath newer ones, generations of them, the plaster worn soft and gray where decades of scratching had gone on and on.

And every column, every single one, was crossed out. One clean horizontal stroke through each group, like a list being checked off. Like names.

Except one.

Near the bottom, at the height where a child crouching would scratch, one group of strokes sat clean and uncrossed, the plaster around it worn smooth as if it had been touched often. I got my inspection mirror into the gap and turned it until the light caught the marks full on, and I read them—or I almost read them, because they weren't letters, not quite, they were more like the *idea* of letters, a word written by someone who had learned writing by listening to it through a wall.

Four strokes. The first one doubled, like a small loop.

My chest did something strange. Not fear, exactly. Recognition is its own kind of fear, and it arrived in my body before my mind could refuse it, the way a smell arrives before you remember what it's from. My mother used to call me a name. Not Paloma—something smaller, something from before, something only she used, in the kitchen, with her back turned, humming. I haven't said it aloud in five years. I have not let myself think it in longer than that, because thinking it was like pressing on a bruise to prove the bruise was real.

The scratches had four strokes. The first one doubled.

I am not going to write the name here. I couldn't then and I can't now. But I stood in a nine-year-old's bedroom in the Aldous house with a mirror in one hand and a flashlight in the other, and I read my own childhood nickname scratched into a stranger's plaster by something with no hands, uncrossed, waiting, and every load-bearing lie I had built my five quiet years on shifted at once, just a few millimeters, the way a house settles.

"Ms. Nakamura?"

Marisol's voice, from the doorway. I straightened too fast. The mirror clinked against the flashlight.

"Find something?"

"No." The word came out level. I was almost proud of it. "Dust and old plaster. This house has been through a lot of decades." I packed the mirror away and dusted the wall voids with diatomaceous earth, thorough and unhurried, a woman doing her job, and all the while my eyes kept going back to that baseboard, to the quarter-inch of dark where the writing lived.

At the front door, Marisol paid me in cash and asked, "Will it come back? Whatever it is?"

"It's not an it," I said, meaning the bedbugs, meaning the nothing I hadn't found. "There's no infestation. Call me if the bites continue." I gave her my card. She held it a moment too long before taking it, and I saw her decide something, saw the stone-in-the-shoe question come back up her throat, and then she swallowed it and said thank you and closed the door.

I sat in the truck and wrote the invoice. BEDBUG TREATMENT — PRECAUTIONARY — NO ACTIVITY FOUND. I wrote it in my neatest handwriting, because neat handwriting is a kind of armor, and I told myself the story I'd been telling for five years: old house, old plaster, coincidence, four strokes is nothing, a loop is nothing, names are scratched into walls by children and children grow up and move away.

The engine ticked as it cooled. The house looked at me with its blank windows. Somewhere inside it—upstairs, behind a wall, in the dark seam where no hand could reach—something waited with one name still uncrossed on its list, and it was mine.

I drove three blocks before I admitted I was shaking. I pulled over on Corliss Street, in front of the teal house, and sat with my hands flat on the wheel, and did the exercise my mother taught me when I was small and scared of the dark under her bed: name five things you can see. A mailbox. A sprinkler head. A jay on a wire. My own knuckles, white. The rearview mirror, and my own eyes in it, wide and too bright.

Name five things you can hear, she'd say then, her hand in my hair. And I'd listen, and the dark would turn out to be empty, and she'd hum, and I'd sleep.

I sat there and listened. The jay. A lawnmower two yards over. Traffic on Route 9, faint. The tick of my engine.

And underneath all of it, so far below hearing that I could have spent the rest of my life calling it imagination—four notes, spaced like a sentence, patient as bookkeeping. Not from the house behind me. From everywhere. From the ground itself.

I drove back to the office and pulled the file on 14 Corliss Street, and that's when I learned the house had a name older than Vane, older than the work order, older than the paint it had never worn: the county still listed it under *Aldous*.

I'd been in that house for two hours. I'd been in Harrow Glen for five years. I had never once heard anyone say that name out loud.
