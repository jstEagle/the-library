## 2. Five Years of Explanations

The thing about explanations is that they are a kind of housekeeping. You sweep the strange into a dustpan, you carry it out to the curb, and by morning the street looks ordinary again. I had been sweeping Harrow Glen for five years, and I was very good at it. Better than I was at pest control, honestly. Pests are honest. They want warmth and crumbs and the dark seams of a house, and they leave evidence you can name. It was everything else in this town that required the finer tools—the rationalizations, the small mercies of not looking twice.

I kept a mental ledger of them. Not the covenant ledger—I wouldn't learn about that for another few weeks, back when this chapter of my life was still only a faint mildew smell I'd learned to ignore. No, my ledger was the other kind, the kind a person keeps without knowing she's keeping it, the kind that grows fat and quiet in the back of the skull like something nesting.

*The Aldous house, March, first year.* A knocking in the east wall, three beats, pause, three beats. Old water pipes. Hammering in the expansion tank. I bled the line, replaced a washer, and told Mrs. Aldous—Edith, then, still alive, still answering her own door—that the pipes in these old houses talk when the temperature drops. She'd looked at me with an expression I couldn't read and said, *Yes. They do talk.* I wrote it off as an old woman's poetry.

*The Ketterling place, that autumn.* A sound like a name being called from inside the chimney flue. Draft. Thermal expansion. Brick settling on brick. I swept the flue, found a bird nest, charged for the sweep, and did not mention that the nest was empty, or that the sound had stopped the moment I put my ear to the damper, as if whatever made it had been satisfied just to be heard.

*The Vane house—no, that one came later, that one belongs to this story. Start earlier.*

* * *

I came to Harrow Glen in the wet spring after my mother died, driving a borrowed truck with everything I owned rattling loose in the bed: a toolbox, two boxes of books, a shoebox of cassette tapes I could not bring myself to play or throw away. I was twenty-six and I had learned the year before that grief has a sound, and that the sound is other people saying your name in that softened, careful voice—*Paloma, I'm so sorry, Paloma, let me know if you need anything*—and how each repetition carved a little more of you away, until you were mostly absence, mostly a shape people spoke gently around.

Invisibility felt like safety. That's the plain truth of it, though it took me years to say it that plainly. My mother had been a woman the world noticed—loud laugh, opinions at dinner parties, a voice on the answering machine so vivid that after she died I would call our old landline from a payphone just to hear the machine pick up, just to hear her say *Nakamura residence, leave it after the beep* with that lilt at the end, like the message itself was a small joke between us. When she was gone, everyone who said my name said it with her absence folded inside it. Every *Paloma* was a small funeral.

So I found a town where nobody knew me, where my name would be just a name on an invoice. Harrow Glen had a population of about four thousand, a dying mill, a main street with more vacant storefronts than open ones, and a pest control business whose owner was retiring to somewhere with actual winters. I bought his truck, his chemical license, his client list, and a small house on Delacroix Street with a porch that sagged in the middle like a tired back. I told myself I'd stay two years. Save money. Move on.

Five years later I was still there, and I want to be honest about why, because the honest reason matters to everything that came after.

It wasn't only the hiding. It was that Harrow Glen hid with me.

There is a particular quality to a town that has decided not to notice things. I felt it the first week, though I didn't have words for it then. The neighbors were polite the way walls are polite. They nodded. They let their dogs sniff my truck. But nobody asked where I was from, nobody asked about the family photos I never put up, nobody asked why a young woman would move alone to a town that was quietly dying. I was grateful. I thought I'd found a town of fellow hiders. I thought the whole place had agreed, the way I had, that looking away was a form of kindness.

I know now that we were all keeping the same silence, and that a shared silence is not emptiness. It's a structure. It has load-bearing walls. And somewhere under all of it, something was leaning on those walls, and had been for a very long time.

* * *

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Back to the ledger.

I cataloged five years of noises the way a skeptic catalogs miracles—thoroughly, and with the conclusion decided in advance. It was professional habit as much as self-protection. Rats in a wall sound like whispering; that's just the truth of it, their claws on lath and their bodies dragging through insulation, a dry susurrus that the human ear, hungry for meaning, turns into syllables. Raccoons in a chimney sound like a child crying. Squirrels in an attic at three a.m. sound like furniture being rearranged by someone who hates you. Starlings in a vent sound like a crowd murmuring behind a closed door. I could have taught a seminar. *The Acoustics of Infestation: Why Your House Sounds Haunted and Isn't.* I gave that speech to clients maybe twice a month, and I was good at it, good at the calm voice and the flashlight and the confident diagnosis, and every time I gave it I felt the small warm satisfaction of a world put back in order.

That satisfaction was the comfort. Here was the cost.

The cost was that I got so practiced at explaining that I stopped hearing. There's a difference, I understand now, between not noticing and refusing to notice, and the second one takes work. It takes a kind of daily labor, the way a garden takes weeding. Every odd sound, every flicker at the edge of vision, every dog in Harrow Glen that wouldn't walk past a particular fence line—I pulled them out like weeds and laid them down in rows of rational causes, and the rows were neat, and the rows were dead.

There was the winter the church bell rang at 3 a.m. and Deacon's son, who ran the hardware store, told me the timer mechanism was old and stuck. I said, sure, timers stick. I didn't ask why the whole congregation showed up the next morning at three, standing in the snow in their coats, not going inside, just standing there facing the bell tower until dawn.

There was the summer the mill pond went still—not calm, *still*, surface like poured glass for nine days, no insects landing on it, and old Tom Brannigan told me it was a temperature inversion. I said, sure, inversions. I didn't ask why nobody fished it that summer, or why the kids who'd swum there every June suddenly swam at the quarry instead, all of them at once, without discussion, the way birds all go quiet at the same moment.

There was the Aldous house itself, which I'd serviced twice a year for five years, first for Edith and then—after—empty, on a retainer from the county, walking its rooms with my flashlight while the house breathed around me. The house breathed. That's not a metaphor. Old houses breathe; they take on heat through the day and let it go at night, and the wood swells and shrinks, and the whole structure ticks and groans like a body settling into sleep. I knew that. I'd told a hundred clients that. And twice a year, in the Aldous house, I would stand in the front hall and hear the breathing come from *below*—not the attic, where a house's breath should come from, but down, under the floors, under the cellar, a slow tidal sound like lungs the size of the foundation—and I would write *no activity* on my service sheet and leave.

Once—only once, the second year—I stood in that hall and the breathing stopped. All at once, mid-exhale, the way a person stops when they realize someone is listening. The silence after it was so complete, so *attentive*, that my ears rang. And I did the thing I was best at. I told myself the wind had shifted. I packed my sprayer, I locked the door, I got in my truck, and I did not look in the rearview mirror until I was three streets away.

That's the cost. I want to say it clearly, because I've had years to think about it and I owe the truth at least this much: deliberate blindness is not free. Every explanation you give is a small payment, and the thing you're paying collects, and it compounds, and one day you look up and realize you've spent five years purchasing your own ignorance at retail price, and the bill has come due, and it's itemized, and every line on it is a sound you heard and named wrong on purpose.

* * *

There were nights, in those five years, when the explanations thinned.

I want to be fair to myself here. I wasn't a coward, or not only a coward. There were nights when the town got so quiet—the mill had cut to three shifts, then two, then one, and the population drained away like water finding a crack—and the quiet had a texture to it, a listening quality, and my rational ledger would lie there in the dark with me and feel flimsy as tissue.

On those nights I would take out the shoebox.

The tapes. My mother's voice, a dozen cassettes, mostly her talking to herself while she cooked, a habit she had, narrating her own life—*okay, onions first, the garlic burns, you know this, you always know this and you never listen*—and once, on the last tape, the only one I ever played, her voice saying my name in the ordinary way, the unguarded way, the way that had nothing to do with sympathy or funerals. *Paloma, the phone's for you.* Four words. I played those four words so many times the tape thinned, and the thinning changed her voice by degrees, stretched it, made her sound like she was speaking up from the bottom of a well, and I told myself the machine was just old.

I never played the others. I couldn't. Her voice was the one thing in the world I couldn't explain, couldn't file, couldn't sweep out to the curb. Grief was the one room in my house I never cleaned. I kept it dark and I kept the door shut and I told myself that was the same as healing.

So understand what I was, when this story finds me. A woman who had made an art of not hearing, guarding the one voice she couldn't bear to hear, in a town that had made a covenant of not seeing, above a thing that had spent fifty years collecting every voice it could get.

We were all of us, in our ways, hoarders of silence. And silence, I would learn, is never empty. It's storage. Something is keeping it for you. Something is keeping it *in* you, the way the walls of the Aldous house kept the scratches, the way the cellar kept its breathing, the way the town kept its bargain, and the interest accrues, and the interest is collected in voices.

* * *

The last entry in the old ledger—the last rational explanation I ever wrote, though I didn't know it—came on an ordinary Tuesday in October, five years and two weeks after I first crossed the town line.

Marisol Vane called about bedbugs. Corliss Street, the yellow house with the iron fence, a rental she'd moved into in the spring with her daughter. I remember the call because her voice had that specific frayed quality I'd learned to hear through—embarrassment braided with exhaustion. Bedbugs carry a shame that has nothing to do with cleanliness, and I always tried to take that weight off people in the first thirty seconds. *It's not about you. They come in on luggage, on furniture, through wall voids from the next unit. It's a building problem, not a you problem.*

She said something odd, though, before she gave me the address. She said, *And maybe you could look at—never mind. It's probably nothing. It's probably the house settling.*

Probably the house settling. The universal solvent of Harrow Glen. I'd have said it myself. I'd said it a hundred times, for a hundred sounds, in a hundred houses, and each time I'd felt that small warm click of the world going back in order.

*It's probably nothing,* she said, and her daughter was in the background, and I could hear the girl—not crying, not exactly. Asking something. Asking it over and over in the patient, terrible way children ask things, the way that says *you haven't answered me yet and I'm going to keep going.*

I wrote the address on my service sheet. Corliss Street. I got my sprayer, my flashlight, my inspection kit, my five years of explanations, and I drove across town in the gray afternoon light, past the shuttered mill, past the still pond, past the Aldous house with its boarded windows and its held breath.

Behind me, the town settled into its long silence. Ahead of me, on Corliss Street, a little girl had been asking a question for eleven nights, and something behind her bedroom wall had finally decided to answer.

I just didn't know yet that it would answer in my mother's voice.
