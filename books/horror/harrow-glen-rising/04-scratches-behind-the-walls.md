## 4. Scratches Behind the Walls

Paloma told herself she was going back for professional reasons.

That was the story she assembled on the drive over, fitting the pieces together the way she'd fit a bait station into a wall void: routine follow-up, client satisfaction, documentation of activity sites. Marisol Vane had called twice since Tuesday—once about the daughter's nightmares, once just to say *thank you again* in a voice that made it clear the thanks wasn't the point—and Paloma had a business to run, and businesses ran on callbacks. That was all.

The Aldous house sat at the end of Corliss Street like a tooth that hadn't fallen out yet. Three stories of gray clapboard gone silver at the edges, a porch roof sagging in the middle as if something heavy had been resting there a long time and had only recently gotten up. The yard was mostly dirt. Nothing grew near the foundation—not weeds, not moss, not even the stubborn crabgrass that colonized every other abandoned lot in Harrow Glen. Paloma had noticed it on her first visit and filed it away under *soil chemistry* without looking too hard at the file.

She noticed it now. She was starting to notice things now, whether she wanted to or not.

Marisol met her at the door with dark crescents under her eyes and a mug of coffee she never seemed to drink from, just carried, like a prop in a play about a woman who was fine.

"She slept last night," Marisol said, which was not a greeting. "Finally. But she woke up asking why the walls were whispering in Spanish."

"Spanish?"

"Her grandmother's lullabies." Marisol stepped back to let her in. The hallway smelled of dust and, beneath it, something faintly sweet and wrong, like fruit going bad inside a sealed box. "We don't speak Spanish in this house. I barely speak it myself. My mother did."

Paloma set down her kit. "Mrs. Vane—Marisol—I didn't find any evidence of infestation. No bedbugs, no rodents, no carpenter ants. I checked the attic and the crawlspace. Whatever your daughter is hearing, it isn't pests."

"I know what it isn't." Marisol's hands were steady around the mug; her eyes weren't. "I want to know what it *is*. You're the one who goes into walls for a living. So go into my walls."

* * *

Paloma had opened a lot of walls in five years.

She knew their anatomy the way a doctor knows skin: the plaster and lath of the old houses, the drywall of the newer ones, the insulation slumped like fat in the cavities between studs. She knew what lived in them—mice threading their highways through fiberglass, wasps papering their nests against warm siding, squirrels gnawing the sweet spots around wiring. She knew the sounds they made and, more importantly, she knew how to explain sounds away. A house settling was a house talking to itself. Pipes knocked because water hammered. Wind found gaps and whistled through them like a child with a blade of grass.

Five years of explanations. She'd gotten good at them. Good enough that the explanations had become load-bearing, holding up more than her career.

The baseboard in the upstairs hallway came off with a pry bar and three screws that turned like they'd been oiled recently. Behind it, the plaster was scratched.

Not chewed. Not gouged by claws seeking passage. *Scratched*—fine, deliberate lines, shallow as fingernail work, arranged in rows. Paloma crouched with her flashlight angled low so the light raked across the surface, the way you raked light across a floor to find footprints. The rows resolved into characters. Some were letters. Some were older than letters, angular marks like runes or tally strokes. They covered the plaster from the sole plate up past knee height, dense as braille, and every few inches a horizontal line struck through a cluster of them—the way you cross out a name on a list.

"Jesus," she breathed, and then, because the word felt loud in a way words shouldn't feel loud, she said nothing else.

She worked along the hallway wall stud by stud, pulling baseboard, peeling back a strip of wallpaper that came away like old skin. The scratches continued. In the corner behind the radiator, where the plaster bulged slightly, the writing went vertical, climbing toward the ceiling in columns, and here the crossings-out thickened until whole sections looked like redacted documents, line after line struck through, struck through, struck through.

Dates. She saw dates worked in among the marks. 1951. 1963. 1974—written larger than the others, pressed deep enough that the plaster had crazed around it like ice about to crack. And below each date, names. She could read some of them: *Eleanor.* *Thomas R.* *Beatrix.* *Aldous* appearing again and again, sometimes alone, sometimes paired with other surnames she half-recognized from the faded signs of Harrow Glen's better days—Deacon, Merrick, Hale, Vane.

Vane. Her flashlight hand trembled, and she steadied it against the wall, palm flat against the scratches, and immediately wished she hadn't. The plaster was cold. Not cool—cold, the specific cold of a root cellar in August, of ground that never fully thaws. It seeped through her glove and up her wrist bones.

Every name was crossed out. Every one. Decades of them, generations of them, struck through in that same deliberate horizontal stroke, as if someone—or something—had been keeping a list and checking entries off. Or closing accounts.

Paloma sat back on her heels in the empty hallway of an empty house and let herself do the thing she'd spent five years refusing to do: she stopped explaining.

If it wasn't pests, it wasn't pests. If the marks weren't claw-work, they weren't claw-work. Something had written on the inside of these walls, patiently, over seventy years or more, and had crossed out every name it wrote except—

Except.

Near the baseboard, low, almost hidden in the shadow of the sole plate, was a cluster of fresh scratches. Fresh—the plaster dust still lay in the little grooves, unvacuumed, undisturbed, bright as new scars. Recent. Days old, maybe less. And the name they spelled was small, four letters, in a child's uneven hand or an adult imitating one:

*PALA.*

Her childhood nickname. The one only her mother had ever used, dead five years, buried two states away. *Pala,* which meant shovel in another language, which her mother had thought hilarious for a girl who grew up to dig into other people's walls. *Pala, mi palita, my little spade.*

It was not crossed out.

Paloma knelt there a long time. The house breathed around her—that faint, rhythmic pressure change she'd catalogued on her first visit as *stack effect, thermal differential*, and which now felt exactly like what it was: breathing. Slow. Patient. The breath of something that had been underground a very long time and had learned to wait.

She thought: *it knows my name.*

Then she corrected herself, because the truth was worse: *it knew my name before I ever came here.*

* * *

She photographed everything. Forty-one shots, flash and no-flash, wide and macro. Her hands were professional and precise the entire time, and she was grateful for that—for the small tyranny of procedure, the checklist that kept her moving when the alternative was to sit down in the dust of a dead woman's hallway and scream, or worse, answer.

Because part of her wanted to answer. That was the thing she couldn't put in the report. Part of her, the part that had been hollow since her mother's voice stopped existing anywhere in the world, heard those crossed-out names and thought: *someone has been keeping track. Someone remembers everyone who was ever here.* And the hungry, shameful animal under her sternum leaned toward that like a plant toward a window.

She pried off one more section of baseboard—at the top of the stairs, where the wallpaper hung in a long peeled curl—and found the oldest layer.

Underneath the visible scratches, the plaster changed texture. Older plaster, grayer, harder. And on it, cut deep enough to have been made with a tool rather than a nail, was a single line of writing, large and careful:

**SEVEN GAVE ONE. ONE KEEPS THE COUNT.**

Below it, seven names in a column, each followed by a year—1974—and each name struck through except the first, which was circled instead. Circled, the way you circle something you intend to return to.

The first name was *ALDOUS.*

And beside it, smaller, in scratches so fine they might have been made by a child's fingernail, was another word. One word, repeated down the margin of the wall maybe thirty times, getting smaller and smaller as it descended, the way handwriting shrinks when the writer is running out of room, or hope:

*Mama. Mama. Mama. Mama. Mama.*

Paloma stood up so fast she cracked her head on a picture rail and barely felt it. She repacked her tools with hands that only shook a little. She carried her pry bar out to the truck like a talisman.

Marisol was waiting at the bottom of the stairs with her untouched coffee. "Well?"

"You should take your daughter and stay somewhere else," Paloma said. "Tonight. Tonight if you can."

"It's a rental, Paloma. I can't afford somewhere else, and I signed a lease, and—" Marisol stopped. Looked at her own hands. "You saw something."

Paloma thought about the photographs on her camera. Thought about the uncrossed name. *Pala.* Thought about how, in five years, she had built a life out of not-looking, and how the not-looking had worked right up until the moment something decided to be seen.

"There's writing in your walls," she said finally. "Old writing. Names. Dates going back decades. Every name is crossed out except—" She stopped.

"Except?"

"Except mine."

Marisol stared at her. Somewhere in the house—a house with no other occupants, no water running, no wind outside—a door closed, softly, the way doors close when someone is being considerate.

"And the thing I keep hearing," Marisol said quietly, "the thing answering my daughter in the dark. It knows your name too."

It wasn't a question. Paloma didn't answer it anyway. She walked out into the gray Harrow Glen afternoon, and as she loaded her kit into the truck bed, she became aware—gradually, then completely—of Mrs. Hollis next door, standing on her porch, watching her. Not with the polite blankness Paloma had gotten used to for five years, the town's practiced blindness.

Mrs. Hollis was smiling. And she said, clearly, across the dead yard and the sagging fence:

"Pala Nakamura. Your mama's girl. We've been wondering when you'd come see us."

Paloma froze with the tailgate half-lowered. Five years in this town, and nobody had used that name. Nobody had known that name.

Behind her, inside the sealed house, faintly, through the walls, something laughed—a small sound, high and delighted, like a child who has just been told a secret is finally working.

She drove home with the windows down and the radio on loud, and neither helped. Because underneath the wind and the music, in the quiet spaces between stations, she kept hearing the scratch of a fingernail adding a line to a list.

And she understood, with a certainty that settled into her bones colder than the plaster had, that whatever kept the count in the walls of the Aldous house had just written a date beside her name.

Tomorrow, she was going to the county archive.
