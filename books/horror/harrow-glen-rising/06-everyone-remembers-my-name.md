## 6. Everyone Remembers My Name

The knock came at six-forty in the morning, and Paloma answered it with the canister of wasp spray she kept by the door because Harrow Glen had taught her that early visitors were rarely friendly.

Dottie Marsh stood on her step holding a pie.

Paloma knew Dottie the way she knew most people in town—by sight, by the particular way they looked through her. Dottie ran the register at the hardware store and had spent five years ringing up Paloma's purchases without once meeting her eyes, without once saying anything beyond the total. Now Dottie was smiling at her with her whole face, all the lines around her mouth engaged, holding out a pie whose lattice crust was still warm enough to fog the October air.

"Peach," Dottie said. "From the tree out back. I thought—I don't know, I thought you might like some."

Paloma did not take the pie. She looked at it the way she'd look at a wasp nest in a wall cavity: noting its position, its activity level, the possible points of entry.

"That's generous," she said carefully. "Is there a problem with your house? Pests?"

"No, no." Dottie laughed, and the laugh had a strange, rehearsed lightness to it, like a bell rung by someone who'd been told where bells belonged. "No pests. I just—well. We never really got to know you, did we? Five years, and here you are, and I don't know the first thing about you. That's a shame, isn't it? That's a shame on us, really."

The morning was cold and gold, the kind of light that made even Harrow Glen's sagging storefronts look picturesque. Somewhere a dog barked twice and stopped, as if corrected. Dottie stood there with her pie extended, and Paloma felt the strangest sensation, like standing in a doorway with wind at her back and warmth at her face, unable to tell which direction the weather was coming from.

"Thank you," Paloma said finally, and took it. The pie plate was heavy ceramic, homemade, the kind of thing you'd want returned. "I'll bring the plate back."

"No rush," Dottie said. "No rush at all. You take your time, honey."

She went down the steps and across the street, and halfway to her own door she turned and waved—not a small wave, not a neighborly flick of the fingers, but a full-armed wave, the kind you give someone at a departure gate.

Paloma closed the door and stood with the pie in her hands and listened to her own house. The refrigerator hummed. The baseboard radiator ticked. Nothing scratched. Nothing answered. And still she stood there for a long moment, holding the warm ceramic, feeling like a woman who had just been handed a receipt for something she hadn't purchased yet.

* * *

By nine, her phone had buzzed eleven times.

She sat at her kitchen table with the recorder from the Aldous house in front of her—she'd taken to keeping it near, the way you keep a wound where you can watch it—and counted the messages. Mrs. Okonkwo from the pharmacy, wanting to know if she'd had her flu shot, and if not, they had walk-ins, and wouldn't it be nice. The Fennimore brothers, offering her first pick of firewood off their lot this year, no charge, they had plenty. A woman named Carol whose name Paloma genuinely did not know, inviting her to a supper club that met Thursdays at the grange hall, "we'd love to have a fresh face, we've been meaning to ask you forever."

Forever. The word sat wrong. Five years of forever, and the asking had waited until the morning after she'd stood in Marisol Vane's kitchen listening to her dead mother's cadence come out of a wall.

She played the messages again, listening this time not to the words but to the music underneath them. All of the voices had it—the same lightness, the same rehearsed bell-tone Dottie had carried. It was warmth, but it was warmth laid over something like a held breath. She'd heard that sound before, in houses. It was the sound a family made when they were being nice to each other in the hours after a fight nobody would name.

At ten, she drove to the hardware store for rodent bait she didn't need, just to see.

Dottie wasn't there. A young man named Teddy worked the register, and Teddy—Teddy who had once told her, when she'd asked about a key blank, that they were "in the back somewhere" and gone back to his phone for four full minutes—Teddy looked up when the door chimed and said, "Miss Nakamura! Morning!" with such unguarded delight that she nearly checked over her shoulder to see who had walked in behind her.

"Morning," she said. "Two rat stations, the black ones."

"Sure thing." He moved to get them, then paused, and leaned on the counter with the ease of an old friend. "You know, my granddad always said you were the only person in this town who really understood the houses. What they're telling you. I bet you hear all kinds of things, huh?"

The question was casual. The question was a hook dropped into still water.

"I hear mice," Paloma said. "Mostly."

Teddy laughed. Everyone was laughing easily today. "Mostly," he repeated, like the word was a small treasure. He rang her up and refused her money—"on the house, seriously, it's nothing"—and when she left, she saw through the front window three separate people pause on the sidewalk to watch her load the truck.

* * *

The pattern held through the afternoon, and by evening Paloma had stopped pretending she was cataloguing coincidences.

The barber on Delmore Street, who had cut her hair exactly once in her first year and never asked her back because he'd spent the appointment talking to the mirror, stepped out of his shop as she passed and told her she looked tired, and that a man shouldn't work as hard as she did, and that his chair was always open. A boy on a bike—she didn't know his name, some Vane cousin maybe, the town was threaded with Vanes the way old walls were threaded with wiring—rode a slow circle around her truck and called out, "Hey! You're the one who fixed the Okonkwos' bats, right? That was awesome." She had fixed the Okonkwos' bats in her second year. Nobody had said one word about it at the time, not even the Okonkwos, who had paid her in cash and closed the door quickly, as though the bats were a shameful condition.

Now it was *awesome*. Now she was *the one who*.

By four o'clock she had three supper invitations, an offer of free snow tires, and a casserole. The casserole had been left on her porch by a woman she had never seen in her life, in a dish with a name written on masking tape on the bottom: *RUTH FENNIMORE — RETURN PLEASE*. Even the dishware wanted to come back. Even the dishes had names and expectations.

Paloma sat on her porch steps in the failing light and ate a bite of the casserole because it seemed rude not to, and it was good, it was honestly good, chicken and wild rice and that particular depth that only came from a recipe handed down. She ate it and felt the warmth go into her and hated how much she wanted the rest of it.

Because here was the thing she could not say out loud, the thing that sat in her chest like a coal: she had been hungry for this for five years.

She had come to Harrow Glen after her mother died because her mother's death had made her invisible in the worst way—grieving people are ghosts, people step around them, they lower their voices when you enter a room, and after a while you start to believe you actually are gone. Harrow Glen had offered a different kind of invisibility, a chosen one. Nobody knew her here. Nobody asked about her mother or gave her that look, the tilted-head pity-look, the one that said *poor thing, orphaned at thirty-two*. In Harrow Glen she could be a function instead of a person. *The pest lady.* She could knock on doors and be let in and treated as equipment, and she had told herself that was peace.

It had not been peace. It had been a fast she'd called fasting.

And now the town had set a table for her, all at once, overnight, the way a trap sets, and the cruelest part—the part she kept turning over like a stone looking for what was under it—was that the kindness didn't feel fake. That was what frightened her. Dottie's pie had been a real pie. Teddy's delight had been real delight. The warmth coming off these people was genuine warmth, the way heat off a radiator is genuine heat. But she kept thinking about the Aldous house, about the fine scratches behind the baseboards arranged like writing, about the way a house can be warm and still be a mouth.

*They're being kind to you,* she thought, *the way you feed something you don't want wandering off.*

* * *

Night came down over Harrow Glen like a lid.

Paloma sat in her truck outside the Aldous house—she'd told herself she was doing a follow-up inspection, and she let herself keep telling herself that—and watched the street. Corliss Street was quiet in the way the whole town was quiet now, a listening quiet. Porch lights burned. Curtains were not quite closed. She had the distinct, skin-crawling sense that she was not surveilled so much as *attended*, the way you attend a patient.

Her phone buzzed. A text from a number she didn't have saved.

*Miss Nakamura — Ellery Deacon. I understand you've been doing some reading at the county archive. I'd be honored if you'd join my family for supper Sunday. There are things about this town it would be better for you to hear from a friend than to find out alone. — E.D.*

She read it three times. The archive visit had been two days ago. The librarian—old, gray-braided, a Vane by marriage, her nameplate said *HETTIE VANE*—had gone pale at the word *annex*, had closed the ledger under her hands like she was covering something sleeping, had said *I think you should go, dear, I think you've found what you were looking for* in a voice that was not unkind and was not kind at all. Paloma had assumed the librarian's fear was private.

Apparently the town's nervous system ran deeper than its streets.

She typed *Thank you, I'll consider it* and deleted it. Typed *What kind of things?* and deleted that too. In the end she sent nothing, because every reply she could imagine was a move in a game whose rules she hadn't learned yet, and the first rule of her trade, the one thing five years of crawling through other people's walls had taught her, was: *when you don't know what's in the dark, don't put your hand in.*

Across the street, the Aldous house stood with its windows dark. But on the second floor, in the room that must be the girl's—Marisol's daughter, the one who complained of the answering man—a light came on. A small shape appeared at the glass, palms flat against the pane, looking out. Looking, Paloma was almost sure, not at the street, but at the truck. At her.

The girl raised one hand. Not a wave. She pressed her palm to the window and held it there, the way you'd press a hand to a wall to feel what was moving on the other side of it.

Paloma's phone buzzed again. She looked down, expecting Deacon, expecting another casserole, another invitation, another warm hand extended across the dark.

It was Dottie Marsh. The pie lady. The message was four words, and the rehearsed bell-tone was gone from it entirely; she could hear the flatness even in text, the held breath showing through:

*Whatever you found—stop looking.*

Paloma looked up. The girl was gone from the window. The light was off. And down the length of Corliss Street, one by one, unhurried, like a tide going out, the porch lights of Harrow Glen began to turn themselves off—until hers was the only truck, and the only headlights, and the only lit face, in a town that had spent all day learning her name.
