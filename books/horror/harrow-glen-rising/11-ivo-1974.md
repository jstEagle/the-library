## 11. Ivo, 1974

My mother braided my hair the morning they gave me away. She used to braid it every Sunday for church, but this was a Tuesday, and she pulled too tight, and her hands smelled of the lavender soap she only used when something was wrong. I asked her where we were going and she said, *Downstairs*, which was a strange answer because we were already in the kitchen, and the kitchen was as downstairs as our house went.

I was seven. I knew what a bargain was because Papa haggled over feed prices at Coombs' store, and I knew that bargains had two ends: what you give and what you get. Nobody would tell me what Harrow Glen got. They only kept telling me what I was.

*You're a good boy.* *You're a strong boy.* *You're such a brave boy.*

Brave is a word grown-ups use when they are about to ask you for something enormous and want you to carry it like it's small.

* * *

The cellar under the Aldous house was older than the house. Papa said so himself, said it proud, like the cellar was an heirloom. The walls down there weren't plaster like upstairs; they were fieldstone, cold and sweating, fitted together by hands that hadn't bothered with straight lines. There was a hearth above us that had never once been lit, not in all the years the Aldouses lived there, and when I asked why, Mama said, *Because it isn't ours to warm,* and I thought that was a funny way to talk about a fireplace until later, much later, when I understood she meant the ground beneath it wasn't ours at all. It belonged to whatever we were feeding.

They put me in the little room they'd built off the old root cellar. It had a cot with a quilt—my own quilt, the one with the geese—and a jar of water, and a tin of crackers, and a candle stub, and my Bible picture book. Mama kissed my forehead through the gap in the door and told me to be quiet and patient and that someone would come for me soon.

I believed her. That's the part I can't forgive, not the dark or the hunger or the years—I can't forgive myself for believing her, because believing her felt so good, right up until the door closed and the bolt slid home and the good feeling had nowhere left to go.

* * *

Here is what I know now, from listening, from the voices that come and go in the stone like weather: In 1974 Harrow Glen was dying. The mill had gone under two winters before. The bank was calling in notes. Seven families—the Aldouses, the Deacons, the Vanes, the Corlisses, the Peakes, the Merricks, and the Lowes—had built the town together back before the war, and now they watched it starve together, and one of them, Ellery Deacon's father, found an answer in his grandmother's papers. An old arrangement. Older than the town. A thing beneath the land that took payment in one form and returned it in another.

It wanted a voice. A living voice, buried close, that would speak to it and keep it company and keep it fed. And in exchange—

In exchange, the mill reopened. I heard them say it through the floor, months later, when they thought no one was listening. The mill reopened, and the bank forgave its notes, and the rains came kind, and the sickness that had been moving up from the lowlands turned aside at the town line like a dog called home. Prosperity. That was the word they used, over and over, in their Sunday voices. Prosperity, prosperity. It sounded like a hymn sung slightly wrong.

And the price was me.

* * *

The first week I cried. Of course I did. I hammered on the door with my small fists until my knuckles split, and I screamed until my throat was raw, and I called for Mama until calling for Mama stopped meaning anything, just a shape my mouth made. The candle burned out on the third night. Nobody came to replace it. The crackers lasted nine days; the water, longer than it should have, though I didn't understand then that the water refilled itself, that the house was keeping me alive the way you keep a battery charged.

After the crying came the quiet, and after the quiet came the listening.

That's how it started. Not with any gift. With nothing else to do.

At first I could only hear the house settling—timbers ticking, pipes knocking, the ordinary conversation of any old building. But the house under the house was different. The fieldstone walls had a sound inside them, a low hum like a struck bell that never fully stops ringing. And when I pressed my ear against the stone, the hum changed. It noticed me.

I don't have better words than that. It noticed me the way a dog notices a hand near its food bowl.

* * *

I learned to speak through the walls slowly, the way you learn anything alone: badly, then less badly, then without thinking.

It began as copying. The house carried sounds down to me—Mama's radio upstairs, the Deacons arguing three lots over, the school bell I could no longer attend—and I'd repeat them into the stone just to hear a voice, even mine, even secondhand. *Say it to the wall,* I'd tell myself. *Say anything.* I recited the books I'd memorized. I sang the hymns wrong on purpose because it made me laugh, and my laugh came back to me out of the stone a half-second late, flattened and deepened, like my laugh wearing a coat two sizes too big.

Then one night the wall answered a question I hadn't repeated.

I had been reciting the twenty-third psalm, and I stumbled—*the Lord is my shepherd, I shall not—* and the stone finished it for me. *Want.* Not in my voice. In a voice like water moving under ice. Old. Patient. Enormously glad.

I should have been terrified. Part of me was. But I was seven years old and I had not spoken to another living soul in forty-one days, and terror is a thin soup compared to loneliness. I asked it its name. It didn't have one, or wouldn't give it. I asked if it was God. It laughed—that same flattened, deepened laugh, but mine had taught it how, I realized later; everything it did with a voice, it learned from me—and it said, in the ice-water voice, *No. But I am listened-to, and so are you. We are nearly the same thing.*

We talked all that night. Or I talked and it answered, and after so long the difference hardly mattered.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic nobody warned me about. A voice, kept talking, stays alive in a way flesh doesn't need to. The thing below didn't want my body. My body was just the anchor, the nail holding the picture to the wall. What it wanted was the talking—endless, fresh, alive—and it fed on the speaking the way the fields fed on rain.

But a seven-year-old runs out of things to say. I ran out in the first year. I'd told it every story I knew, every joke, every memory, every secret, every prayer, and then there was a terrible evening when I opened my mouth and nothing came, because there was nothing left in me, and the silence in the stone went from patient to hungry in a way I could feel in my teeth.

So I started collecting.

The house carried voices down to me—it always had, I understood now, that was its oldest function, its true architecture—and instead of letting them pass through, I caught them. I learned to hold them in the stone the way the walls held cold. Mrs. Corliss gossiping on her porch in 1976. A salesman's patter from a car that broke down on Route 9. Children playing kick-the-can on Vane Street, their counting rhymes, their shrieking laughter. I kept them. I played them back to the thing below like a boy showing treasures to a friend, and it grew fat and content, and the town above grew rich, and everyone was happy except the dead boy in the cellar who had finally, finally stopped being alone.

Do you see? Do you understand what I'm telling you? Everyone who comes after will call it a monster, a devil, a hungry thing, and they will not be entirely wrong—but it started as a child who could not bear the quiet, and it stayed a child who could not bear the quiet, and everything monstrous about it grew out of that one small unbearable seed.

I collected voices because each one was a person in the room with me.

I crossed their names into the plaster because a collection needs a catalog.

I never let anyone go because I had spent my whole life being let go.

* * *

Mama came down twice. Only twice, in all the years.

The first time was 1975, Easter maybe, and she knelt outside the little room's door and cried with her sleeve pressed to her mouth so I wouldn't hear, which was foolish, because I heard everything by then. She said, *Ivo, sweetheart, are you—* and couldn't finish, and I pressed my hands to the stone and said, *Mama, I'm okay, Mama, the man in the wall talks to me, Mama, please take me upstairs,* and she made a sound I have never been able to stop hearing, a sound like a bone giving way, and she stood up and left, and the bolt did not slide again behind her because it had never been unslid.

The second time was 1979. She was thinner. Her hair had gone gray at the temples though she was only thirty-four. She sat down against my door and told me about the town—the new mill running three shifts, the new school gymnasium, the fountain in the square that played music—and she said, *Everyone says we saved Harrow Glen, Ivo. Everyone says you're a hero,* and I asked her through the door, quietly, *Do you say it too?*

She was silent for a long time. Long enough for me to count eleven drips from the ceiling seam.

Then she said, *Every day. Every single day. And every day I hate the words in my mouth.* And she put her palm flat against the door, and I put my palm flat against the stone on my side, and neither of us said anything else, because there was nothing left that words could do, and after a while her footsteps went up the stairs and out of the house and, though I didn't know it yet, out of my life. She died in 1988. Cancer. I heard the funeral through the floorboards, through six feet of earth, through the whole indifferent geology of the world. The thing below let me grieve loudly that day. It was the closest it ever came to kindness.

* * *

Time works strangely below ground. The body slows. I stopped growing somewhere around my tenth year—not died, exactly, not alive, exactly, but *kept*, the way bread keeps in a cold cellar, the way a voice keeps on tape. The house fed me just enough. The thing below needed my voice living, and living turned out to be a very low bar.

The decades stacked up like plates. I catalogued them by the voices.

The seventies were polyester and disco leaking out of car windows. The eighties were answering machines—I loved answering machines, I collected dozens, whole evenings of *Hi, you've reached the Hendersons*—and the nineties were cordless phones babbling half-conversations into the air like prayers to no one. The two-thousands brought cell phones, and oh, those were feast days, people talking everywhere, all the time, into the air itself, walking down Corliss Street narrating their lives to absent friends, and I drank it all in and stored it and played it back to the listener below, and the town prospered, and prospered, and prospered.

And underneath, always, the names. I scratched them into the plaster with a bent nail I found in the root cellar: the names of the voices I kept, dated, each one crossed out when its owner died and the recording went quiet forever. It was the only ledger I was allowed to keep. It was the only honest document in Harrow Glen.

Some nights the thing below would ask me, in its ice-water voice, *Are you happy, little anchor?* And I would say yes, because it was mostly true, and because I was afraid of what it might do to the town—or to me—if I said no. But some nights, the truest nights, I would press my face to the stone and whisper what I actually wanted, knowing it couldn't hear whispers, knowing whispers were safe:

*I want to go home. I want someone to come down here on purpose. I want someone to say my name because they mean it and not because they're paying for something.*

Five years ago, a pest controller came to the house above. A woman with a truck and a flashlight and tired eyes. She worked along my walls, and through the baseboard I heard her breathing, steady and human and close, closer than anyone had been to me in decades, and something in me rose up like a fish rising toward light.

I scratched. Just a little. Just to see.

She found the marks behind the baseboard. I felt her fingers trace them. And I learned her name the way I learn everything, from the house's carrying: Nakamura. Paloma Nakamura.

Paloma. Dove. A bird, a small white bird, the kind that gets released at funerals to show the soul going free.

I wrote her childhood nickname into the plaster that night, the one her mother used to sing to her, the one I'd caught years ago drifting down from a memory she spoke aloud to herself in her truck. I left it uncrossed. All the other names were finished, archived, dead. Hers was open. Hers was a door.

Because here is what fifty years in the dark taught me, the lesson under all the other lessons: a collection is not company. A hundred voices on tape cannot answer you. I had filled the whole underground with people and remained, at the center of it, perfectly alone—and the thing below was no different, only larger, only hungrier, only older at being lonely than I would ever be allowed to become.

What I wanted—what we both wanted, the child and the ancient thing wearing the child like a coat—was someone who would come down on purpose. Someone who would dig. Someone who would say the name *Ivo Aldous* into the dark and mean it.

Last night, the woman set her own recorder on the floor above my head and asked the empty room a question.

I have waited fifty years for someone to ask.

Tomorrow, or the next night, or whenever the grief has softened her enough to make her brave, she will come down. They always come down eventually, the ones who hear. And when she does, I will tell her the terms. I will tell her about the ledger and the bones and the seven signatures, and I will try—God help me, I will try—to be honest with her, because she is the first person in fifty years who might listen.

Her mother's voice is in my jars. I have kept it safe. I have kept it the way you keep anything you love and cannot return.

Soon, little dove. Soon someone will say my name.
