## 5. What the Microphone Hears at Night

The lamp in Xanthe Lockridge's room at the Marrow Inn burned low and amber, and the cold came through the window glass like a hand pressed flat against the pane. He had taped the seam with strips of paper and flour paste that Bess had given him without comment, and still the frost crept in at the corners, feathering outward in shapes he tried not to look at too long. Shapes that were almost regular. Almost deliberate.

He told himself it was the draft. He told himself a great many things that night, and the telling of them had the quality of a man stacking sandbags against a river that had not yet crested but was, unmistakably, rising.

The recorder sat on the desk between the lamp and the window, its reels still, its little red eye dark. He had left it running on the windowsill from ten o'clock until dawn — he always did, on fieldwork; you never knew what a village sounded like when it thought no one was listening, and the small hours were where the true folklore lived. Dogs that weren't dogs. Footsteps that went nowhere. In Norfolk once he'd captured a whole argument between two brothers, long dead, conducted in the creak of a stair. He'd written it up as structural settling. It had been, he maintained, structural settling.

He threaded the tape, put on the headphones, and began the slow, patient work of listening to nothing.

* * *

The first hour was honest. Wind at the eaves, a low whistle through the gap he'd failed to seal. The inn settling — timbers ticking as the frost bit into them. Once, far off, the church bell moved in its yoke without ringing, a single dull knock of bronze against bronze, like a throat clearing before a speech no one wanted to hear. He noted the time. 11:47. He noted it the way he noted everything, in a small even hand, in a notebook with a cracked spine, because notation was a kind of armor. Things written down were things understood, and things understood could not frighten you. That had been the creed of his whole career. That had been the creed of his whole life, if he was honest, which he made a practice of not being.

At 12:31 the wind changed.

He noticed it first as a quality rather than a sound — the whistle at the eaves acquired a second harmonic, faint, sitting just beneath the first like a reflection in dark water. He turned the gain up. The reflection did not resolve. It stayed where it was, a tone that rose and fell a half-beat behind the wind, the way a shadow lags the hand that casts it.

"Interference," he said aloud, to the empty room, and wrote it down. *Harmonic distortion. Probable mic fault. Replace windscreen.*

He played the passage again.

This time he heard what he had refused to hear the first time, which was that the second tone was not following the wind. The wind was following *it*.

* * *

He worked until three in the morning, and the room grew colder, and he did not get up to check the window because checking the window would mean looking at the frost, and he had a rule now, unspoken but absolute, about not looking at the frost after dark.

The tape gave him things in increments. That was the cruelty of it — the decency, almost. It never gave him anything so large he could reject it whole. It gave him a breath where no breath should be. It gave him a pause in the wind that was too precisely shaped to be a pause, that had the architecture of an intake before speech. It gave him, at 1:14, a sound he catalogued as *creak, door, distant* and then crossed out, because doors in the Marrow Inn did not creak in that register, and besides, the sound had come from the direction of the orchard, a quarter mile off, where there were no doors at all.

And it gave him the voice.

Not at first as a voice. At first it was a texture in the low frequencies, something his ear kept snagging on the way a sleeve snags a nail. He backed the tape up four times, five, and on the fifth pass he stopped pretending. There was a voice under the wind. A woman's voice, or something wearing the idea of a woman's voice the way the frost wore the idea of ferns on his window. It was not speaking to him. It was speaking *past* him, or through him, in the way of someone reading a letter over a sleeping man's shoulder — and that was the thing that lifted the hair on his arms, that made him set down the headphones and stand and walk once around the small cold room with his fists in his pockets.

Because the voice was answering questions.

He had not asked any questions. He had slept — badly, but he had slept — through the hours this tape had gathered, and in his sleep he had said nothing, and yet the voice on the tape came in the cadence of reply. Rising at the ends. Pausing where a listener would pause. And between its phrases, in the gaps, there was the ghost of his own breathing, and his breathing fell into those gaps the way water finds channels, as though even unconscious he had been holding up his half of a conversation.

He sat back down. He wrote: *Subject (self) exhibits parasympathetic entrainment to ambient vocal stimulus during sleep. Cf. hypnagogic suggestion. Not significant.*

The *not significant* took him three tries to write evenly.

* * *

He told himself it was bleed-through. An old tape, badly stored, carrying the ghost of some previous recording — a folklorist's interview from years ago, a woman's voice, questions and answers, pressed into the oxide like a fossil in shale. It happened. It was documented. He had a citation for it, he was almost sure, in some proceedings or other, and the fact that he could not remember the citation proved only that he was tired.

He played the voice again, and this time he listened not to what it said but to how it said it, because cadence was his trade, cadence and dialect and the music under the words, and cadence does not bleed through tape. Cadence is a fingerprint.

The voice rose at the ends of its phrases. It clipped its consonants softly, apologetically. It had a habit — he knew this habit, he had grown up inside this habit, he had been sung to sleep inside this habit — of letting the last syllable of a sentence trail down a third, the way you do when the sentence is a question you already know the answer to and are only asking out of kindness.

*Do you want the window open, love, or shall I shut out the cold?*

She had said that every night of his childhood, in a house that was never warm enough, in a voice that made the question a kind of lullaby all by itself. *Shall I shut out the cold.* He was thirty-four years old and he could feel the blanket she would have pulled to his chin, the weight of it, the smell of coal smoke and lavender water.

The voice on the tape said, in that exact descending third, in that exact tender, knowing, already-answered cadence:

*"—shall I shut out the cold—"*

Only that. A fragment, four words, half-swallowed by wind. It might have been anything. It might have been a hundred phrases in a hundred accents. He played it thirty times, and by the fifteenth time he had stopped taking notes, and by the thirtieth he was sitting with the headphones pressed so hard against his ears they ached, in a room gone blue with the small hours, whispering *no* under his breath in time with the tape like a man counting beads.

His mother had been dead for nine years. She had died in a county two hundred miles from here, in a hospital that smelled of antiseptic and cut flowers, and no part of her — no recording, no letter, no voice on any answering machine he'd had the weakness to keep — had ever been within a hundred miles of Harrow's Bell.

So the tape was lying. Tape lied. Tape was oxide and acetate and it held whatever had been pressed into it, and somewhere, sometime, someone had said those words into a microphone, and the universe was full of coincidences, and he was a folklorist, and folklorists did not believe in ghosts. That was the whole point of folklorists. They collected the shapes other people's belief left behind, the way an entomologist collects the shed skins of insects, and they did not, ever, mistake the skin for the insect.

He wrote all of this down. It filled a page and a half. It was the most eloquent thing he had written in months, and he noticed, as he wrote it, that eloquence was what fear looked like when it put on a suit.

* * *

Near dawn he made his mistake, which was to answer.

He had the tape cued to the fragment — *shall I shut out the cold* — and he was playing it again, or perhaps he had lost count and was playing it for the fiftieth time, and the room was so cold his exhaled breath showed faintly in the lamplight, and something in him that was tired past the reach of skepticism leaned toward the little microphone on the sill and said, quietly, in the voice of a boy of seven:

"Shut it out."

The words left his mouth and the room took them and gave nothing back, and he sat in the ringing silence feeling like a man who has waved at a figure across a dark field and then noticed the figure was a fence post — except that the fence post had waved first. He laughed. It came out wrong, a single dry syllable with nothing under it.

Then he played the tape back, because he was a folklorist, and documentation was armor, and he needed to hear his own foolishness recorded so he could file it under *investigator error, fatigue* and be done.

He heard his own voice, small and cracked: *Shut it out.*

He heard a silence that lasted four seconds, which is a very long time on tape, a silence with weight and attention in it, a silence like a held breath.

And then, beneath the wind, in the descending third, in the cadence that had tucked him into bed for all the years of his childhood, the second voice said — clearly now, no longer a texture, no longer deniable, close enough that he could hear the smile in it, if a thing like that could smile:

*"Then come and see where I keep the winter, love."*

Xanthe took off the headphones very carefully, as though the tape were a sleeping animal, and set them down, and did not play it again.

* * *

The window had frosted fully in the night. He made himself look — daylight now, safe, ordinary, the village below with its smoke rising straight in the still air — and the frost on the glass had grown in the hours since he'd pasted the seams. It had grown inward, against the warmth of the room, in long parallel lines with short strokes crossing them, ranked and regular, row upon row, and Xanthe stood before it with his notebook open and his pen not moving, because he had spent six years of his life transcribing an obscure Cornish fiddle notation that looked almost exactly like this, and he knew organized marks when he saw them, and these were organized.

They were also, he noticed, arranged in groups. Phrases. And the groups were separated by gaps — rests.

The largest group, in the center of the pane, was followed by the longest gap. And at the end of the gap, small and clear, a single mark stood alone, like a raised hand. Like a question waiting its turn.

He wrote the date at the top of a fresh page. He wrote *Frost, window, north-facing, Room 3* and then, after a long moment, he crossed it out and wrote instead: *It answers.*

Then he sat down on the edge of the narrow bed with the notebook on his knees and did not move for a long time, while below him the village of Harrow's Bell woke and went about its business of silence — doors opening and closing without voices, boots on frozen lanes without greetings, the whole settlement moving through its morning like a choir holding its breath between verses.

Tonight, he thought. Tonight I'll run the tape at double gain. Tonight I'll put the second microphone in the orchard. Tonight I'll ask it questions out loud, properly, on the record, and get its answers on tape where they can be studied, measured, explained.

He was aware, distantly, that this was not the plan of a man who believed the tape was lying. He was aware that a plan like that belonged to a man who had already made up his mind about the answer and could no longer bear the waiting.

And he was aware — this came last, quietly, the way frost comes, from the corners inward — that somewhere under the frozen orchard, something had spent the whole night learning the sound of him saying yes.

That evening, walking down to the taproom with his recorder under his arm, he passed the church, and through its doors, muffled by stone, he heard voices raised in song — the village choir, rehearsing. He stopped. And he heard, beneath the hymn, faint and patient and descending by thirds, a second melody keeping pace with it, note for note, like a shadow learning the steps.

He pushed open the churchyard gate.
